Tuberculosis incidence and treatment outcomes in the Tarmaber District, Ethiopia: a five-year retrospective study
Authors: Seble Teklesilassie, Tegenu Gelana, Tilahun Alelign
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains a significant public health challenge in Ethiopia, despite ongoing efforts to enhance detection and treatment. Understanding the local TB incidence and treatment outcomes is essential for developing targeted control strategies. This study aimed to assess TB incidence and treatment outcomes over a five-year period in the Tarmaber district of the North Shewa Zone, Ethiopia.MethodsA five-year retrospective data were collected from TB registry books at Debre-Sina and Armanya Health Centers between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2018. The study included 221 individuals diagnosed and treated under Directly Observed Treatment Short-course program. The study used passive case finding at the health centers, consistent with routine DOTS service delivery. Data on demographic characteristics, TB classification, HIV status, and treatment outcomes were extracted and analyzed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics and chi-square tests were conducted to assess TB trends and associations with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAmong a total of 1,291 presumptive TB screened over five-years study period, 221 cases (17.1%) were confirmed as active tuberculosis. Of the 221 individuals suffering from TB, 79.2% had pulmonary TB (35.7% smear-positive and 43.4% smear-negative), and 19.9% had extra-pulmonary TB. It was more prevalent among males (52.5%) and individuals aged from 15 to 44 years (62%). Urban residents accounted for 56.6% of cases. The overall TB/HIV co-infection rate was 19%, with the majority (11.3%) being smear-negative. The average case detection rate for smear-positive TB was 35.8%, and the treatment success rate across the study period was 86.4%. A slight increase in TB incidence was observed in 2017.ConclusionThe study revealed a declining trend in TB incidence alongside relatively high treatment success rates. However, the low detection rate for smear-positive TB and the rising trend of TB/HIV co-infection highlights the need for strengthened diagnostic capacity, improved screening, and integrated TB/HIV services in the district.Keywords: Tuberculosis, Treatment outcome, TB trends, TB/HIV co-infection, Ziehl-Neelsen, Retrospective study, Ethiopia

Background

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a significant global health threat, especially in low- and middle-income countries where it continues to be a major cause of morbidity and mortality. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), TB affected an estimated 10.6 million people worldwide in 2022, resulting in approximately 1.3 million deaths, making it the second leading cause of death following COVID-19 [1]. TB is primarily caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), though other species in the MTB complex such as M. bovis, M. africanum, and M. canetti are occasionally implicated in human infection [2]. The disease commonly presents in two main clinical forms: pulmonary TB (PTB), which constitutes about 80% of all cases, and extra-pulmonary TB (EPTB), accounting for the remaining 20% [3, 4].

The transmission of TB primarily occurs through airborne droplets, typically from individuals with active, untreated PTB who cough, sneeze, or even speak, dispersing bacilli into the environment [5]. Nearly 90% of all TB cases occur among adults aged 15 years and older, and men are disproportionately affected [6]. Furthermore, the TB epidemic is exacerbated by its strong association with HIV/AIDS, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where co-infection rates are among the highest globally [7]. Multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) and extensively drug-resistant TB (XDR-TB) have emerged as significant challenges, further complicating TB control efforts, particularly in countries with limited healthcare infrastructure [6, 7]. In Ethiopia, the clinical management of MDR-TB faces multiple challenges including limited diagnostic capacity, prolonged treatment duration, adverse drug reactions, and inadequate patient adherence due to socio-economic and psychosocial barriers [8].

Developing nations bear the brunt of the TB burden, with an estimated annual infection rate of 2% or more, compared to just 0.5% in developed countries [9]. In 2017, TB incidence was highest in countries such as India, China, Indonesia, the Philippines, and several African nations including Nigeria and South Africa [6]. These patterns reflect global inequities in health system capacities and social determinants such as poverty, malnutrition, overcrowding, and lack of access to diagnostic and treatment services.

Ethiopia is one of the 30 high TB burden countries globally and ranks third in Africa and eighth in the world in terms of TB prevalence [2, 10]. As of 2022, Ethiopia’s estimated TB incidence was 132 per 100,000 population, and the mortality rate, excluding HIV-positive individuals, stood at 19 per 100,000. When accounting for TB/HIV co-infections, the mortality rate increased to 27 per 100,000 [2, 11]. The estimated incidence rate for smear-positive TB specifically is approximately 108 per 100,000. Despite the implementation of the Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course strategy, TB remains a major public health concern, compounded by variable case detection rates, underdiagnosis, and treatment gaps across regions [10, 12].

Ethiopia’s national TB control program has reported a case detection rate of 74%, a treatment success rate (TSR) of 82.5%, and a cure rate of 67% [6]. However, these national-level indicators mask significant regional disparities. In the 2009/10 fiscal year alone, the country reported over 146,000 TB cases, with 33.1% being smear-positive, 35.2% smear-negative, and 31.6% extra-pulmonary [12]. These figures highlight both the scale and complexity of TB control in Ethiopia, emphasizing the urgent need for tailored, locally relevant interventions.

Amhara National Regional State is one of the hardest hit by TB in Ethiopia. National surveillance data and community-based studies have consistently identified the region, particularly the North Shewa Zone, as having high TB prevalence [7, 13]. Factors contributing to this regional burden include limited access to healthcare services in rural and remote areas, socioeconomic challenges such as poverty and malnutrition, and persistent delays in TB diagnosis and treatment initiation [13]. Although the expansion of diagnostic tools such as GeneXpert and the involvement of health extension workers have improved TB detection in some areas, underdiagnosis and late presentations continue to hinder effective disease control.

Several studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia confirm the heterogeneous nature of TB prevalence across the country. For instance, TB prevalence has been reported as 7.3% in Arsi Zone [14], 9.2% in South East Ethiopia [15], 19.4% in Gamo-Gofa [16], 14.2% in Metehara Hospital [17], and as high as 21% in Eastern Ethiopia [18]. Within the Amhara Region itself, reported prevalence includes 2.6% in Dessie and Debre Berhan [19], 4.5% in Dabat [20], and 6.2% in Dessie Hospital [21]. A study at Debre Berhan Zonal Hospital also highlighted a substantial burden of TB among suspected cases [22]. These studies underscore not only the high burden but also the significant variation in TB prevalence within and across regions, which points to differing contextual risk factors and healthcare service gaps.

Despite these findings, limited data exist at the district level in many parts of Ethiopia including rural zones like the Tarmaber district. It is located within the North Shewa Zone of the Amhara region, is predominantly rural and characterized by geographic and socioeconomic barriers that hinder access to timely TB care. All public health centers in the district implement the DOTS strategy for TB diagnosis and treatment. As far as we are concerned, there has been no study specifically describing the prevalence and treatment outcomes of TB at Debre Sina and Armanya health centers. The lack of localized data restricts the development of effective, evidence-based public health interventions.

This study is guided by the hypothesis that TB incidence has declined over the five-year period, accompanied by improved treatment outcomes. Generating such evidence is essential for monitoring progress toward national TB control targets and ensuring alignment with global TB elimination strategies. In this context, the present study aims to assess TB trends and treatment outcomes using retrospective data collected between 2014 and 2018 from Debre Sina and Armanya Health Centers in the Tarmaber district. The study specifically examines the distribution of TB types including pulmonary TB (PTB), extrapulmonary TB (EPTB), and multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) as well as TB/HIV co-infection rates, annual case detection rates, and treatment outcomes among patients managed under the Directly Observed Treatment Short-course (DOTS) program. The findings are intended to provide evidence-based insights to inform local TB control strategies, evaluate the effectiveness of the DOTS program in a rural Ethiopian context, and support future policy development and resource allocation at both district and regional levels.

Methods

Description of the study area

The study was conducted in the Tarmaber district, which is one of the 27 districts within the North Shewa Zone of Amhara National Regional State, Ethiopia. This district is located in the central part of the country, and it lies approximately 190 km northeast of Addis Ababa and 60 km from Debre Berhan, the zonal capital. The district shares borders with Kewet to the northeast, Ankober to the south, Menz Mama Midir to the northwest, Bassona Werena to the southwest, Mojana Wedera to the west, and the Afar Regional State to the southeast.

Tarmaber district covers a total area of 609.25 square kilometers. As per the 2018 data from the district’s administrative office, the population was estimated at 106,731, consisting of 54,884 males and 51,847 females. There are four health centers in this district. Study design and period

A five-year retrospective study design was conducted using TB registry data, covering individuals suffering from TB undergoing Directly Observed Treatment, Short-course (DOTS) services from January 1, 2014, to December 31, 2018. The study used passive case finding at the health centers, which is consistent with routine DOTS service delivery.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria required the presence of complete demographic and laboratory data (medical records) of all age groups. Those who completed the full course of treatment and those transferred out during the designated timeframe were included from the analysis of treatment outcomes. Records lacking critical information such as age, sex, acid-fast bacilli (AFB) results or those involving patients with known chronic comorbidities like diabetes were excluded because of incomplete and inconsistent recording of data in the retrospective registers, which could bias the accuracy and reliability of analysis.

Ziehl-Neelsen smear microscopy techniques

Tuberculosis diagnosis at both health centers primarily relied on Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) smear microscopy, a conventional method for detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis in sputum samples. The ZN technique involves staining sputum smears with Carbol fuchsin, decolorizing with acid-alcohol, and counterstaining with methylene blue; acid-fast bacilli appear as bright red rods against a blue background under light microscopy [23]. Despite its widespread use in resource-limited settings, ZN smear microscopy has limited sensitivity, detecting only about 50–60% of active pulmonary TB cases, particularly in patients with low bacillary loads such as children, HIV-positive individuals, or those with extrapulmonary TB. Moreover, the method lacks specificity in distinguishing M. tuberculosis from non-tuberculous mycobacteria and cannot identify drug resistance [24].​.

The smear-positive pulmonary TB (SPPTB) refers to pulmonary tuberculosis in which a sputum sample tests positive for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) using Ziehl-Neelsen (ZN) staining and microscopy, indicating that the patient is likely highly infectious [25]. Diagnosis is typically confirmed when at least two sputum smears are positive for AFB, or when one positive smear is accompanied by a positive culture or radiographic evidence consistent with TB [26]. In contrast, smear-negative pulmonary TB (SNPTB) refers to cases where sputum smears are negative for AFB, but the patient exhibits clinical and radiological features suggestive of TB [27]. Although these patients test negative on smear microscopy, they may still have active TB and can respond to anti-TB treatment. Smear-negative TB is more commonly observed in individuals with HIV, the elderly, and children, due to their lower bacterial load and atypical disease presentation.

Data sources

Among the four health centers in the Tarmaber district, Debre-Sina (the district’s administrative center) and Armanya Health Centers were selected for this study due to their relatively high patient flow and the availability of tuberculosis diagnostic and treatment services. These two centers handle the majority of TB cases in the district, as government health facilities are the primary providers of TB care. Diagnostic and treatment services are delivered through health center-based TB clinics equipped with laboratory facilities, primarily using the Ziehl-Neelsen smear microscopy technique.

Data collection

Data for this study were collected from the TB registry units at Debre-Sina and Armanya Health Centers in the Tarmaber district. The dataset included a total of 1,291 presumptive TB screened for TB over the five-year study period between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2018. Prior to data collection, official approval was obtained from the administrative offices of both health centers. Then, coordination with the designated TB focal persons then facilitated access to the necessary records.

Data were manually extracted from the TB registry books using a structured data abstraction form designed to capture all relevant variables. That is, a comprehensive review of medical records and TB registration books from selected health centers was conducted to extract socio-demographic data, TB classification, and treatment outcomes for all individuals diagnosed with tuberculosis and initiated on anti-TB treatment during the study period. The key variables included patient type (new or relapsed), TB classification (pulmonary or extrapulmonary), HIV status, presence of drug-resistant TB, and treatment outcomes (cured, treatment completed, lost to follow-up, or deceased). According to the WHO definition, “cured” refers to smear-positive patients who complete treatment with documented negative smear results, whereas “treatment completed” refers to patients who finish treatment without a final smear result. Both categories are classified under treatment success [28]. Additional data on sex, age, and place of residence were also collected. Each form was checked for completeness and consistency before data entry. The dataset included both confirmed TB cases and suspects and was thoroughly checked for completeness, and clarity to ensure data quality for robust analysis.

Data analysis

The collected data were entered into a digital database using Epi Data version 3.1, which allowed for double data entry to minimize errors, and for data consistency checks. Once data entry was complete, the dataset was exported to SPSS version 20 for data cleaning, and analysis. This included identifying and correcting inconsistencies, checking for missing values, and validating entries by comparing a random subset of digital records with the original registry forms. Descriptive statistics were employed to portray the prevalence and treatment outcomes of TB patients. Additionally, the association between risk factors and forms of TB was assessed using chi-square analysis, with significant p values < 0.05.

Results

Overview of presumptive tb and confirmed cases (2014–2018)

A total of 1,291 individuals were screened for TB over the five-year study period, out of which 221 cases (17.1%) were confirmed as active tuberculosis. The annual number of confirmed TB cases ranged from 53 in 2014 (24%) to 39 in 2018 (17.6%), indicating a general decline over the years. The mean number of TB cases per year was 44, with a standard deviation (SD) of 5.5 and a range of 14 (39 to 53).

Table 1 presents the annual distribution of presumptive TB and confirmed cases, along with sex and TB classification. Pulmonary TB accounted for 79.2% of all TB cases (n = 175), of which 35.7% were smear-positive and 43.4% smear-negative. The extra-pulmonary TB has comprised 19.9%. Moreover, only 0.9% of TB cases were multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) (Table 1).

Distribution of TB by year and TB forms

Figure 2 shows the year-specific distribution of TB forms between 2014 and 2018. The mean annual PTB prevalence was 82.6% (SD = 7.1), while EPTB cases had a mean of 20.0% (SD = 3.8).

Trends in Year-Specific Prevalence of TB Forms at the Study Sites (2014–2018)

Pulmonary TB remained the predominant form throughout the study period, accounting for the majority of cases with prevalence consistently above 75%. Smear-positive PTB (SPPTB) displayed marked fluctuations with a decline in 2016 followed by a sharp increase in 2018. Smear-negative PTB (SNPTB) showed variable trends peaking in 2016 before decreasing in 2017 and rising again in 2018. Extrapulmonary TB (EPTB) contributed a relatively stable proportion (10–25%) across the years, while multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) remained very low (< 5%) throughout the period (Fig. 2).

TB prevalence by sex and residence

Of the 221 individuals suffering from TB, 116 (52.5%) were males and 105 (47.5%) were females. The proportion of smear-positive PTB was slightly higher in males (38.8%) than females (32.4%), while EPTB was more common in females (25.7%) than males (14.7%). Smear-negative PTB was the most frequent TB form overall and across both sexes. Urban dwellers accounted for 125 (56.6%) of cases, with 39.2% smear-positive, while rural residents comprised 96 (43.4%), of whom 31.3% were smear-positive. Notably, EPTB was more prevalent in rural areas (25%) than in urban areas (16%) (Table 2).

Trends in TB/HIV co-infection

Over the five years, 42 patients (19%) were co-infected with HIV. The highest prevalence was in 2017 (4.9%), followed by 2014 to 2016 (4.1%). Among HIV-positive TB patients, 11.3% were smear-negative. The average annual HIV co-infection rate was 3.8%, with a range from 1.8% (2018) to 4.9% (2017) (Table 4).

Discussion

This five-year retrospective study provides important insights into tuberculosis (TB) trends, demographics, co-infection patterns, case detection rates, and treatment outcomes in two health centers within Tarmaber district, Ethiopia. The findings reinforce national and regional patterns while offering local-level evidence essential for tailoring TB control strategies. The overall TB prevalence of 17.1% among presumptive TB individuals aligns with prior reports in Ethiopia, where TB prevalence ranged from 7.3% in Arsi Zone [14] to 21% in Eastern Ethiopia [18]. The decreasing trend in annual TB cases from 2014 to 2018 may suggest improvements in public health outreach, diagnostic services, or successful implementation of the Directly Observed Treatment Short-course (DOTS) strategy. This is consistent with previous studies from Yirga Chafe [29] and Nekemte [30], which also reported a declining TB incidence. However, a brief spike in 2017 in EPTB cases and TB/HIV co-infection suggests localized factors such as service interruptions, population mobility, or detection improvements.

Pulmonary TB (PTB) was the dominant form in this study (79.2%), echoing WHO estimates that PTB accounts for about 80% of all TB cases globally [4]. This finding was comparable to other Ethiopian studies conducted in North Shewa Zone hospitals (80.9%) [30] and Gondar [31]. Conversely, extra-pulmonary TB (EPTB) cases accounted for 19.9%, which is lower than the 45.2% reported in Nekemte [30]. Such differences may stem from diagnostic capacity variations (lacking advanced imaging and pathology services) or referral system limitations in rural health centers. The low detection of MDR-TB (0.9%) highlights either effective treatment or underreporting due to limited diagnostic infrastructure.

Gender-wise analysis revealed a a slightly higher proportion of TB cases in males (52.5%), although not statistically significant. This is consistent with national findings reported in Gambella [32], Sidama [33], and WHO reports [7]. The factors contributing to male predominance may include occupational exposure, higher smoking and alcohol use, and greater social mobility [34]. Biologically, sex hormones may also influence immune responses to TB infection. The higher EPTB rate in females may be linked to immunological and biological factors as seen in a study from Sidama [33].

TB was most prevalent among individuals aged 15–44 years who comprised 62% of cases. This is consistent with reports from Bahir Dar [35], Gondar [31], and Metehara [17], and highlights that the most economically productive segment of the population bears the highest disease burden. This age group is often more mobile for work and have social interactions, sexually active, and at higher risk for HIV co-infection, contributing to TB vulnerability [36]. In contrast, the low TB prevalence in children under 15 years (4.1%) suggests the protective effects of BCG vaccination and even the challenges in pediatric diagnosis which is consistent with reports from Nekemte [37] and Rwanda [38].

Though not statistically significant, a higher proportion of TB was observed in urban dwellers (56.6%) than rural (43.4%), which is in line with the previous findings from Gambella [32] and Debre Berhan [22]. The urban environments often pose risks such as overcrowding, limited ventilation, and increased HIV prevalence, which enhance TB transmission [39, 40]. However, the rural residents showed a slightly higher rate of EPTB. Similar urban-rural disparities have been documented in Gambella [32] and Debre Berhan [22] studies suggesting that urbanization increases the risk of transmission, and the rural areas face diagnostic access challenges.

HIV co-infection was observed in 19% of TB cases, peaking at 4.9% in 2017, which is higher than Ethiopia’s national average (11%) [10]. However, it is close with findings reported from Gambella (24%) [32], and Oromia (21%) [41], but lower than WHO regional estimates (36%) [6]. The high proportion of smear-negative TB among HIV-positive patients (11.3%) underscores diagnostic challenges, as immunosuppression lowers bacterial load in sputum [42]. This stresses the need for integrating TB and HIV services to improve co-infection management.

The average case detection rate (CDR) for smear-positive PTB was 35.8%, below the 70% WHO target. While this figure aligns with national findings [10], it surpasses CDRs reported in Tigray (26%), Amhara (23%), and Benishangul (19%) but remains lower than Harar (95%), Dire Dawa (81%), and Addis Ababa (63%) [2]. Factors such as misapplication of diagnostic algorithms, suboptimal sputum quality, and underutilization of GeneXpert contribute to underdiagnosis [43, 44]. The smear-positive PTB case detection rate averaged from 6.8% in 2014 to 9.5% in 2018, which shows gradual improvement and this may reflect DOTS program efficiency. Thus, strengthening diagnostic protocols and capacity is essential for improving CDR.

Treatment success rate was 90.1% of individuals suffering from TB achieving favorable outcomes, which is closely align with WHO targets (85%) and regional studies such as Oromia (85.5%) [30], Sidama (89%) [33], West Gojjam Zone (90.5%) [45], and higher than in Debre Berhan (79.4%) [22]. The success likely reflects a strict DOTS adherence, community engagement through Health Extension Workers, Health education, and improved ART initiation for TB/HIV co-infected individuals (integrated HIV treatment). However, this study’s death rate of 7.5% is nearly similar to Nekemte (8.1%) [30] and Gondar (10.1%) [46] and exceeds those reported in Addis Ababa (3.7%) [47] and Debre Berhan (1.8%) [22]. These mortality variations may reflect delays in diagnosis, co-morbid conditions, or treatment default. Thus, enhancing early detection and managing co-morbidities could help reduce TB-related deaths.

The study found a high rate of new TB cases (92.8%) and an increasing trend of relapsed cases toward 2018. Relapsed TB cases, though few (7.2%) increased slightly in 2018, warranting concern. This pattern suggests rising treatment challenges and possibly increased TB/HIV co-infection. A rise in relapses as observed in Nekemte [37] and North Western Shewa [48] may indicate incomplete treatment or TB/HIV complications. Preventing relapse requires reinforcing adherence counseling (education), follow-up mechanisms, and ensuring individuals suffering from TB complete full treatment regimens.

The chi-square analysis found significant associations between TB forms and age, HIV status, and patient type (new cases versus relapse history). The association between age group and TB form may be explained by immunological and exposure-related factors. Pulmonary TB is more common among individuals aged 15–44 years, likely due to higher levels of social interaction, mobility, and occupational exposure, which increase opportunities for airborne transmission. In contrast, extrapulmonary TB tends to occur more frequently in children and older adults, reflecting their relatively weaker or waning immune responses, which facilitate dissemination of Mycobacterium tuberculosis beyond the lungs. The strong association between HIV status and TB form aligns with established evidence that immunosuppression impairs local containment of infection within the lungs, thereby predisposing HIV-positive patients to extrapulmonary involvement. The observed association with patient type may be linked to the biology of recurrence: relapsed cases often represent incomplete clearance or reactivation of prior pulmonary infections, whereas new cases may present with a wider clinical spectrum, including extrapulmonary manifestations.

The absence of significant associations between TB form and sex or residency could reflect uniform exposure patterns and healthcare-seeking behaviors in this rural Ethiopian setting, where both men and women, and residents from urban and rural areas, share similar environmental and socioeconomic risk factors. Moreover, widespread implementation of the DOTS strategy and TB/HIV integration programs may have reduced sex- or residency-related disparities in diagnosis and treatment access.

Strengths of the study

The key strength of this study lies in its use of a five-year comprehensive dataset, enabling the analysis of long-term trends in TB incidence, treatment outcomes, and TB/HIV co-infection patterns. Data were collected from government health centers operating under routine TB program conditions, enhancing the real-world relevance and applicability of the findings to public health practice. The study offers detailed stratification by TB type (pulmonary, extra-pulmonary, and MDR), age, sex, and HIV status, providing valuable insights into high-risk subgroups. Additionally, the use of standardized documentation from the DOTS TB registration unit ensures consistency and reliability across the study years. Collectively, these strengths position the study as a meaningful contribution to local TB control planning and evaluation of diagnostic and treatment service performance in the Tarmaber district.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective cohort design. The use of pre-existing health records may have affected the accuracy and completeness of the data, and the exclusion of patients with incomplete records could have introduced selection bias, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings. The analysis was confined to descriptive statistics and chi-square tests, which do not fully account for potential confounding factors or complex interactions among variables. While the identification of the factors supports epidemiological relevance, the use of chi-square alone limits interpretability. Future analyses using multinomial logistic regression would better estimate adjusted odds ratios (adjustment of confounders) and identify true predictors of TB form. The in-depth understanding of the associations of factor is essential for tailoring TB control strategies. Although Debre Sina and Armanya health centers are major TB care providers in the Tarmaber district, the results may not be representative of other healthcare facilities in the region. Additionally, the study period (2014–2018) reflects historical TB program performance and may not capture more recent programmatic changes. While these findings cannot be extrapolated to the national level, they offer valuable insights at the district level.

The key determinants of TB, including income, education, housing conditions, smoking, and substance use, were not available due to the limitations of the registry data. Moreover, the very low number of reported MDR-TB cases may reflect underdiagnosis resulting from limited access to culture or molecular diagnostic tools, rather than an inherently low prevalence.

Conclusion

This study underscores that tuberculosis remains a considerable public health concern in the Tarmaber district, despite a declining trend in case numbers and generally favorable treatment outcomes findings that align with national and regional patterns. However, persistent challenges such as TB/HIV co-infection, low detection rates of smear-positive cases, and disease relapse highlight the need for targeted interventions. Priorities moving forward include the application of advanced analytical tools to identify key risk factors, strengthening diagnostic capacity, improving adherence to the DOTS strategy, and enhancing the integration of TB and HIV services to advance TB control efforts in rural Ethiopian settings.
