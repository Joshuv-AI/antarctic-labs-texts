Primary schoolteachers’ prospective acceptability of a multi-component school-based oral health intervention in rural Malaysia: a qualitative study
Authors: Patricia Ponniah, Zamros Yuzadi Mohd Yusof
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health inequalities among rural primary schoolchildren remain a persistent problem, and teacher-led interventions could be a sustainable way to address them. However, their success depends on teachers’ acceptance of such initiatives before they are introduced into the school system. This study explored the prospective acceptability of SPARKLE (School Program for Achieving Radiant Smiles in Kids from Rural and Low Socioeconomic Backgrounds) as a school-based oral health intervention among rural primary schoolteachers in Malaysia using the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA).MethodsIn-depth interviews were conducted with 8 teachers and 4 school administrators from four rural primary schools in Negeri Sembilan, Malaysia. Schools were selected through multistage sampling, while purposive maximum variation sampling was used to recruit participants who were likely to implement SPARKLE in the future. The interviews were analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the TFA.ResultsAcceptability varied across SPARKLE’s components. Sharing oral health infographics with parents was the most acceptable activity because it aligned with routine parent–teacher communication and required minimal additional effort. Teachers valued the Oral Health Report Card for improving parental awareness but were cautious about explaining clinical findings due to limited confidence and concerns about professional boundaries. Daily supervised toothbrushing was the least acceptable component because its daily frequency increased workload and raised concerns that it was a parental responsibility. Teachers appreciated the interactive Healthy Food Project but doubted it could be effective, as unhealthy food remains widely available in and around schools.ConclusionRural Malaysian teachers broadly supported SPARKLE’s aims but its acceptability was conditional and component-specific. Components were more acceptable when they aligned with existing school routines and professional roles, and less acceptable when they added substantial workload or required teachers to assume responsibilities they viewed as belonging to parents or dental personnel. Our findings highlight the importance of flexibility and contextual adaptation in the future implementation of SPARKLE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09233-2.

Introduction

Schools are widely recognized as an effective and cost-efficient platform for promoting children’s oral health, particularly at the age when first permanent molars erupt, and preventive behaviors can be established early in life [1–3]. In Malaysia, this platform offers substantial reach with over three million children enrolled in public primary schools [4].

Oral health services for schoolchildren are primarily delivered through the School Dental Service (SDS), a national program established in 1948 that provides free oral health education (OHE) and clinical care through scheduled school visits by dentists and dental therapists [5, 6]. While the SDS has achieved high population coverage, it faces increasing difficulty in addressing complex and evolving oral health needs, particularly in rural and low-income settings [7–9]. Its reliance on periodic, clinician-led visits limits opportunities for sustained behavior reinforcement, which could better be achieved through parental engagement and integration of oral health practices into daily school routines.

The consequences of these structural constraints are reflected in persistent oral health inequalities among rural schoolchildren. Over the past two decades, surveillance data have highlighted a marked urban–rural divide in caries experience and gingival health, with disease severity inversely related to parental education and household income [10, 11]. Indigenous Orang Asli children, the majority of whom reside in rural areas [12], experience a high burden of dental caries and gingivitis, alongside early uptake of risk behaviors such as smoking and betel nut chewing [13, 14]. Fewer than one-third of this population access oral healthcare services from the age of two years onwards [15]. In such contexts, SDS visits may represent one of the few stable points of contact between children, their families, and formal health services.

To address these limitations, the School Program for Achieving Radiant Smiles in Kids from Rural and Low Socioeconomic Backgrounds (SPARKLE) was developed as a school-based oral health intervention guided by the UK Medical Research Council framework for complex interventions [16]. Its development was informed by a systematic review to identify effective interventions to improve disadvantaged children’s oral health [17], followed by stakeholder engagement with senior dental administrators, public health managers, and frontline SDS implementers in Pahang, Malaysia [18]. Rather than replacing the SDS, SPARKLE is designed to complement it by positioning teachers as co-implementers alongside the SDS, with active parental engagement to support sustained behavior change among children from low-income rural families.

Unlike the visiting SDS, teachers are permanently embedded within the school system and interact with children daily. This makes them well-placed to reinforce oral health messages, embed oral health–promoting practices into routine school life, and sustain behavior change between clinical visits [19, 20]. Evidence from diverse settings suggests that teacher-led initiatives can enhance health outcomes [21–24]. Within SPARKLE, teachers will be delivering multiple activities that vary substantially in duration, frequency, and intensity. These roles include: (i) daily supervision of toothbrushing sessions after recess, (ii) facilitating a monthly Healthy Food Project over four months, (iii) distributing a one-off annual oral health report card prepared by the SDS during parent–teacher meetings, and (iv) disseminating monthly health education infographics over a six-month period via class WhatsApp groups. As shown in Fig. 1, while teachers are central to SPARKLE’s delivery, they operate within a broader coordinated system: the SDS continues its clinical and educational functions, parents are engaged to reinforce oral health practices at home, and the school food environment is addressed through engagement with school management and food service staff.

Expanding teachers’ roles in oral health promotion raises important questions regarding feasibility and sustainability, particularly in under-resourced rural schools. The literature has identified high workload, limited resources, insufficient training, and concerns regarding professional boundaries as common barriers to teachers’ participation in oral health promotion [25–28]. Whether these factors influence teachers’ willingness and capacity to deliver a multi-component intervention such as SPARKLE within the socio-cultural and administrative context of rural Malaysian primary schools remains unclear. The implementer’s perspective is often the “make or break” factor for success, and an intervention of this scope and complexity will only achieve its intended outcomes if those expected to deliver it find it genuinely acceptable.

Acceptability, defined as the extent to which an intervention is perceived as appropriate by its intended deliverers or recipients, is a key determinant of implementation fidelity, uptake, and sustainability [29–31]. Assessing it prospectively allows intervention developers to identify potential barriers and address them before large-scale implementation, supporting the efficient use of resources and informed intervention refinement [32, 33]. The Theoretical Framework of Acceptability (TFA) was selected here precisely because it offers a structured approach to examine how teachers perceive each component of SPARKLE before implementation [29]. The framework is particularly suited to the development and feasibility phases of complex interventions and enables acceptability to be assessed before implementation to inform targeted adaptation rather than post-hoc problem solving [30, 34]. This study, therefore, aimed to explore the prospective acceptability of the SPARKLE among rural primary schoolteachers in Negeri Sembilan, Malaysia, using the TFA to inform further refinement of the intervention prior to broader implementation.

Methods

Study design & ethics

This descriptive qualitative study employed semi-structured in-depth interviews (IDIs) as the data collection method. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Medical Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, Universiti Malaya, Malaysia [Ref: DF CO2406/0016 (P)]. The study was registered with the National Medical Research Register [NMRR ID-24-00906-LLI].

Study setting

The study was conducted in the state of Negeri Sembilan, where nearly one-third of the population resides in rural areas. Data collection took place in Malay-medium national primary schools located within the districts of Jempol, Jelebu, and Kuala Pilah. These districts were selected because of their high rates of absolute poverty and low levels of urbanization [35, 36].

Development and piloting of the interview topic guide

An interview topic guide was developed for this study in both English and Malay and was explicitly structured to prompt discussion at both the intervention and the individual SPARKLE component levels. It was informed by a literature review and expert input. The guide was pilot-tested with two primary schoolteachers in the Rembau district, whose student demographics closely mirrored those of SPARKLE’s target population. Both interviews were conducted in closed rooms at the school, each lasting approximately 30 min. The pilot IDIs served two purposes: to assess the guide’s utility in field conditions and to train the moderator to conduct interviews. No substantive changes were required after the pilot. Sampling and participant recruitment

A multistage sampling approach was used to select both schools and participants. From the 92 rural national primary schools across the three districts, an initial sampling frame was developed using routine SDS surveillance data. In consultation with the State Oral Health Division, schools were screened to reflect variations in oral disease burden, socioeconomic context, and school enrolment size, with particular attention given to schools serving higher proportions of children from low-income households and Orang Asli communities, consistent with SPARKLE’s target population. Three schools per district were shortlisted as potential study sites. To minimize investigator-driven site selection within the pre-identified pool and to manage fieldwork pragmatically, districts and schools were randomly sequenced for approach. Data collection proceeded in that order, moving to the next district/school after completion at each site until data collection was completed.

Following institutional approvals, the moderator (PP) visited each selected school to have an initial meeting with the principal to explain the study’s purpose and sampling criteria. Direct researcher access to teachers without principal involvement was not feasible within the Malaysian public school system. To identify eligible participants, the moderator explained the study’s purposive maximum variation sampling criteria to the principal, specifying that the selected staff should represent variation in their professional roles (health teachers, classroom teachers, and school administrators) [37] as well as demographic characteristics. Priority would be given to those most likely to have a direct role in the future implementation of the SPARKLE. The principal was also informed that participation in the research was voluntary. Information packets containing infographics describing SPARKLE and a Participant Information Sheet were given to the principal. A second date was scheduled for the researcher to return to the school to conduct the interviews.

In the interim, the principal identified the staff members who met the sampling criteria and distributed the information packet for their review. The selected teachers were given a week to review the packet’s contents before the researcher returned. At each school, three participants (two teachers and one administrator) who provided consent were recruited into the study.

Data collection

On the scheduled date, teachers who had provided informed consent were interviewed in a closed room at their respective schools to ensure privacy. Following a brief informal conversation to build rapport, the moderator explained the study’s objectives and the interview process. Participants were explicitly informed that participation was voluntary, that they could withdraw at any time without consequence, and that no personally identifiable information would be reported. Anonymity and confidentiality were assured throughout data collection, analysis, and reporting. Written informed consent was obtained prior to the interview, after which participants completed a brief sociodemographic questionnaire.

Interviews were guided by the semi-structured topic guide described above. Questions were asked and the teachers were encouraged to share their opinions freely until no further points were raised. The moderator used probes to clarify responses and explore issues in depth, allowing each interview to continue until participants had no further points to add on the topics discussed. The topic guide was kept flexible to allow discussion to flow organically and to inform subsequent interviews.

The interviews were conducted in a mix of Malay and English languages, reflecting the code-switching common in Malaysian communication and accommodating participants’ preferences. Before concluding, the moderator summarized the discussion points and asked two final questions: “Is there anything else you would like to add?” and “Do you have any questions for me?” Interviews lasted up to 45 min and were audio-recorded for transcription. Field notes were taken during and after the interviews. Each interview participant received a token of appreciation for their participation in the IDI (approximately USD 10).

In this study, an a priori saturation criterion was specified under which data collection would cease when two successive interviews no longer contributed new insights relevant to the TFA constructs or SPARKLE components [38]. After every three interviews, PP reviewed and coded the transcripts, updated the codebook, and prepared a brief summary of emerging findings. These summaries were discussed with ZYMY during peer debriefing sessions to assess whether the data from the new interviews were adding to the analysis or primarily repeating earlier findings.

Upon determining that two successive interviews had yielded no new codes, the remaining interviews at the current school were completed and a further set of interviews at the next school was conducted to confirm that saturation had been reached. Data collection continued until the research team judged that the data were sufficiently rich to address the study objectives with no new codes identified.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using directed content analysis guided by the TFA [29, 39, 40]. This approach enabled deductive coding of data to the TFA constructs. Directed content analysis was selected to examine how the TFA could be applied to assess the prospective acceptability of SPARKLE as a school-based oral health intervention among rural Malaysian teachers. Prior to analysis, the research team clarified the meaning of each construct within the study context and agreed on adapted definitions (Table 2).

The interview data were transcribed verbatim in the source language (English, Malay, or both). All transcripts were de-identified prior to analysis. An initial draft of each transcript was generated using a free AI transcription service (Riverside.fm). The primary researcher (PP) verified each transcript against the original audio recording and corrected any discrepancies. The finalized transcripts were then reviewed by an independent party to ensure accuracy.

Data analysis began with familiarization through repeated reading of transcripts and listening to audio files. To minimize loss of meaning, the transcripts were analyzed in their original language. Initial coding was undertaken in Atlas.ti (version 23.1.1.30813) by deductively mapping text segments to the relevant TFA constructs. An initial codebook was developed by PP based on the agreed-upon TFA definitions and reviewed by a second researcher (ZYMY). The refined codebook was then applied to the full dataset by PP. Throughout the analysis, ZYMY reviewed the coded transcripts to check the application of the codes. Both researchers then discussed interpretations and challenged each other’s assumptions to ensure that the coding remained consistent with the data. This detailed approach ensures the credibility and dependability of the analysis.

Although coding was guided by the TFA constructs, findings were organized and presented by SPARKLE components to reflect the structure of the intervention. Within each component, themes were mapped to the relevant TFA constructs. Where participants expressed views about SPARKLE as a whole rather than specific components, these were analyzed as overarching perceptions of the intervention and presented separately.

Reflexivity was addressed by considering how the researchers’ professional backgrounds and institutions might influence data generation and interpretation [41]. The interviews were conducted by PP, a female dentist and specialist trainee in dental public health with a prior professional relationship with the State Oral Health Division which oversees the SDS. The research team also included ZYMY, an experienced male dental public health specialist with expertise in oral health inequalities and health promotion. Member checking was conducted by offering participants the opportunity to review their transcripts for factual accuracy [42].

During the analysis, it became apparent that, across several TFA constructs, teachers’ views were not unidirectional. Rather, perspectives could be bipolar within the same construct, reflecting variations across individuals, schools, or SPARKLE components. This was addressed analytically by capturing both positive and negative appraisals within each relevant domain, rather than imposing a single interpretive stance. The illustrative quotations presented below were translated into English by a certified translator.

Results

Participant characteristics

Data saturation was determined to have been reached after the ninth interview (at the third school). The three interviews at the fourth school were conducted to confirm saturation and to complete the school’s planned sample of two teachers and one administrator, and no new codes or themes emerged.

The final sample comprised 12 participants interviewed across four rural primary schools in three districts, including eight teachers and four school administrators. Two of the schools enrolled a sizable number of Orang Asli children. The mean age of the participants was 38.9 years (SD = 6.4); nine were female (75%) (Table 3).

General sentiments expressed by teachers

Overall, teachers’ acceptance of SPARKLE as a school-based oral health intervention was shaped by their direct observations of the children’s impoverished and disadvantaged backgrounds.“There was a student living in a house without electricity, lights, and clean water. He has had to fetch water from the river to cook and bathe. So, what more should we expect him to have? Can we expect him to have—a toothbrush, soap…?” (T07, Jempol).

“There was a student living in a house without electricity, lights, and clean water. He has had to fetch water from the river to cook and bathe. So, what more should we expect him to have? Can we expect him to have—a toothbrush, soap…?” (T07, Jempol).

Within the context of their students’ living conditions, teachers commonly emphasized the importance of continuity in school-based interventions, particularly compared with short-term or one-off initiatives.“School programs should be continuous. If we only do it for a while, it will not have an impact.” (T09, Jempol).“At school, since it is continuous, the student will constantly be monitored. As a result, students will always be focused on their dental care.” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

“School programs should be continuous. If we only do it for a while, it will not have an impact.” (T09, Jempol).

Despite the emphasis on SPARKLE’s continuity, reservations were expressed about the proposed year-long duration of the intervention and the extent of teacher involvement required.“We cannot concentrate on SPARKLE all year round. Otherwise, our syllabus will never be finished. We only have a little time to teach.” (T09, Jempol).“For teachers to conduct it, I don’t think it is very effective, because I’m afraid what we deliver is not the same as what a dentist would.” (T06, Kuala Pilah).

If SPARKLE gets approval from him, we will support any activity that is suggested.” (A10, Kuala Pilah).“If the management says they support it [SPARKLE], the teachers will also support it, for the good of the students.” (T03, Jelebu).

Additional perspectives on the acceptability of each teacher-led component of SPARKLE

Daily supervised toothbrushing

Affective attitude and perceived effectiveness

Teachers expressed positive attitudes toward toothbrushing in schools. They highlighted that many children, particularly those from Orang Asli communities, began their day extremely early to travel long distances to school. Under these circumstances, teachers noted that toothbrushing was sometimes skipped because of time constraints or chilly weather.“Some of them don’t shower in the morning because it’s too cold. Therefore, I think it is especially important to give them time for this (toothbrushing). We assign a specific time for them to do it at school.” (T02, Jelebu).

“Some of them don’t shower in the morning because it’s too cold. Therefore, I think it is especially important to give them time for this (toothbrushing). We assign a specific time for them to do it at school.” (T02, Jelebu).

In these cases, brushing at school was regarded as a valuable alternative, with a novelty factor for children. More importantly, teachers believed that what they did in school could function as a behavioral priming mechanism and help reinforce similar practices at home.“At least on that day, they know they must brush their teeth at school. They will then be reminded that they need to brush their teeth when at home too.” (T02, Jelebu).“What we think of as something simple is actually important. For example, toothbrushing programs may look trivial, but they need to be emphasized even up to Year Six.” (T12, Kuala Pilah).

“At least on that day, they know they must brush their teeth at school. They will then be reminded that they need to brush their teeth when at home too.” (T02, Jelebu).

“What we think of as something simple is actually important. For example, toothbrushing programs may look trivial, but they need to be emphasized even up to Year Six.” (T12, Kuala Pilah).

Providing a complimentary oral hygiene kit was viewed as a practical enabler for implementing this component. Teachers noted that having toothbrushes and toothpaste readily available made the activity easier to implement within the school routine.“When these things (free toothbrush and toothpaste) are readily available for the student, it makes it easy (for them to brush).” (T08, Jempol).

“When these things (free toothbrush and toothpaste) are readily available for the student, it makes it easy (for them to brush).” (T08, Jempol).

Affective attitudes can evolve with familiarity. One teacher with prior experience implementing similar activities described how initial concerns about workload could decrease as the practice became routine.“At the initial stage of implementation, it might seem like more work, right? But when it becomes routine, we might feel, ‘Hey, this is quite fun!’” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

“At the initial stage of implementation, it might seem like more work, right? But when it becomes routine, we might feel, ‘Hey, this is quite fun!’” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

Ethicality

Even if teachers were supportive of promoting toothbrushing, daily supervision appeared to fall outside what they considered to be their role. While teachers were comfortable with reminding the children, they viewed daily oversight of this activity as a parental role rather than their own. This reflects a subtle distinction between a teacher’s willingness to support, rather than lead, a child’s health-related activities.“Because it’s every day, it’s like we are taking over the parents’ duties.” (T03, Jelebu).

“Because it’s every day, it’s like we are taking over the parents’ duties.” (T03, Jelebu).

This concern was also reflected among school administrators who framed daily toothbrushing supervision as extending beyond the school’s professional remit.“The school has other responsibilities other than overseeing the health and welfare of students.” (A10, Kuala Pilah).

“The school has other responsibilities other than overseeing the health and welfare of students.” (A10, Kuala Pilah).

Burden

Several logistical concerns seemed to burden teachers. They cited practical concerns, including pressure to find time each day to complete the task, an insufficient number of teachers to supervise the activity, the need for secure storage for toothbrushes, and variable access to sinks.“In theory, it’s good. However, it’s time-consuming for us at school. We already have limited time.” (T02, Jelebu).“We don’t even have enough taps for them to rinse their mouths or brush their teeth.” (T11, Kuala Pilah).“If we want to do it every day, it is quite difficult. The recess time is not long. Children need to buy food and eat.” (T04, Kuala Pilah).

“In theory, it’s good. However, it’s time-consuming for us at school. We already have limited time.” (T02, Jelebu).

“We don’t even have enough taps for them to rinse their mouths or brush their teeth.” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

School administrators interpreted burden through an operational and management lens. For example, one administrator suggested that toothbrushing could be more feasible if supervision was rotated among teachers, while acknowledging that it would still add another task to existing duties.“If it is implemented on a rotation basis, I do not think it would become a major burden for teachers… usually here it would be the teacher on duty… if this is added, then it becomes one additional task, for toothbrushing.” (A01, Jelebu).

Sharing digital infographics

Intervention coherence

Many Malaysian schools use WhatsApp as a communication channel between parents and classroom teachers. Since teachers already moderate the group and often disseminate information to parents, they felt that this component was a straightforward extension of their current routines.“This does not seem to be a burden. We are already doing this in school, so we just keep going.” (A08, Jempol).“Even now, if we get any information, the teachers will spread it through the WhatsApp group.” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

“This does not seem to be a burden. We are already doing this in school, so we just keep going.” (A08, Jempol).

“Even now, if we get any information, the teachers will spread it through the WhatsApp group.” (T11, Kuala Pilah).

Teachers also suggested ways to adapt infographics to better suit local contexts. Those working with indigenous communities highlighted the need for flexibility in format and language, noting that a ‘one size fits all’ approach may not work for every parent or caregiver.“I suggest that the infographics can be made in the native indigenous language, or we can use voice messages… it’ll be useful.” (T07, Jempol).“We can print it out and give it as flyers or maybe small cards for students to take home.” (T02, Jelebu).

“I suggest that the infographics can be made in the native indigenous language, or we can use voice messages… it’ll be useful.” (T07, Jempol).

“We can print it out and give it as flyers or maybe small cards for students to take home.” (T02, Jelebu).

These suggestions reflect teachers’ awareness that digital dissemination alone could risk excluding some parents, particularly in Orang Asli communities, where language barriers, limited smartphone access, patchy Wi-Fi coverage in remote areas, low literacy, and lower digital literacy among some older caregivers may affect engagement.

Opportunity cost and burden

Teachers also noted that no additional time or effort would be required, as the SDS would provide the infographics. They did not need to create or curate content, which could divert their time from their current responsibilities. Hence, a teacher’s willingness to share these infographics seemed contingent on the fact that the SDS would undertake the content-generation burden entirely.“It’s better if you can provide us with the information. Then, the teachers don’t have to search for it. If it’s readily available, we’ll just share it.” (A01, Jelebu).“For us, sharing is no big deal. However, if we can standardize the materials and get them from the same source, that would be very good.” (T06, Kuala Pilah).

“It’s better if you can provide us with the information. Then, the teachers don’t have to search for it. If it’s readily available, we’ll just share it.” (A01, Jelebu).

“For us, sharing is no big deal. However, if we can standardize the materials and get them from the same source, that would be very good.” (T06, Kuala Pilah).

Delivery of an oral health report card

Perceived effectiveness

Teachers perceived the oral health report card as an effective tool for alerting parents to their child’s oral health status. Currently, such information is not proactively communicated to parents unless parents themselves initiate contact with the SDS.“When we give it (the oral health report card) to the parents, they will know the status of their child. Teachers noted that the oral health report card functioned similarly to a child’s own academic report card. Thus, it could be embedded within preexisting school programs. Communicating a child’s oral health status could be an extension of current practice, since teachers already share information on children’s academic progress and basic health indicators, such as height and weight, with their parents.

Teachers also raised ethical concerns about role congruence. They acknowledged that they could explain clinical findings to parents, but still pulled back when questioning whether they should. There was an overall positive response to the Healthy Food Project, which highlights healthy foods (e.g., fruit, vegetables, grains, milk) monthly through taste tests, public speaking sessions, and educational posters in the canteen. There were even those who described a personal resonance with the initiative.“This activity is good because children often don’t know which foods are healthy; they just eat to feel full. Discussion

This study explored the prospective acceptability of SPARKLE from the perspective of its key implementers: rural Malaysian primary schoolteachers. Across domains of the TFA, affective attitude and perceived effectiveness were generally high, whereas burden, opportunity cost, and ethicality emerged as key sources of concern. Overall, SPARKLE components were viewed as more acceptable when they fitted with teachers’ existing roles, daily school routines, and available school resources. Conversely, acceptability was lower when the components imposed additional burden or crossed perceived professional boundaries.

Malaysian primary school teachers have some familiarity with oral health promotion. Some received training through the Ministry of Health’s bGEMS program during pre-service training [43], while in service, others serve as designated “health teachers” responsible for coordinating school health activities. Teachers also play hands-on roles in community-based oral health initiatives, including the national fluoride mouth rinse program [44, 45]. This background positions them as informed stakeholders capable of offering meaningful insight into SPARKLE’s acceptability and provides early signals about which components are likely to be implemented with fidelity versus adapted, reduced, or resisted.

Implementation research has shown that even evidence-based interventions struggle without contextual fit and implementer buy-in [46]. This was reflected in the present study. Despite a general endorsement of SPARKLE’s overarching goals, this did not automatically translate into teachers’ acceptance of all delivery roles. Mapping the findings to the TFA showed that program acceptability was shaped by interactions among TFA constructs within each SPARKLE component, rather than by any single construct alone.

The interplay between burden and ethicality constructs was illustrated clearly by teachers’ input on SPARKLE’s daily supervised toothbrushing component. Under the existing SDS model, toothbrushing activities are typically delivered as one-off events led by visiting dental personnel. SPARKLE shifts this responsibility onto teachers and requires their sustained involvement. For teachers, the issue centered on whether daily toothbrushing supervision crossed into parental responsibility while the burden was experienced through classroom-level constraints. For administrators, the ethical concern related more to the school’s institutional remit, while the burden was viewed as an operational issue requiring duty allocation and management. In rural school contexts, where staffing, facilities, and educational resources are already constrained, this additional responsibility may be even harder to absorb [47], mirroring constraints reported in other supervised toothbrushing programs [26, 27].

Rather than rejecting this component outright, teachers proposed reducing its frequency. This adaptation may address both constructs simultaneously, i.e., easing the logistical pressure (burden) while also reducing the sense that daily oversight had crossed into parental territory (ethicality), while still preserving the core purpose of the activity. This may be a necessary process of modifying the intervention to fit local contexts without compromising core functions [28] and is consistent with evidence that other school-based toothbrushing initiatives designed for daily implementation operate at lower frequencies in practice. The ‘Smiles’ program, for instance, averaged three sessions per week despite its design intent [48]. These findings suggest that flexibility in delivery intensity may be necessary if teacher-supervised toothbrushing is to remain acceptable and sustainable in resource-constrained school settings.

Professional boundary concerns were equally prominent in teachers’ responses to the delivery of the oral health report card and illustrate how ethicality can function as a ceiling on self-efficacy. Teachers recognized its strong potential to improve parental awareness of children’s oral health, representing a significant improvement over the current SDS, in which parental involvement is limited to providing consent for a child’s dental treatment. However, their reluctance to explain clinical findings revealed deep-seated concerns about the ethicality of conveying clinical information, even if teachers perceived themselves as capable of performing the task. Despite recognizing its value, teachers may not find it acceptable enough to implement this component willingly. Teachers may be highly receptive to managing behavioral health interventions (such as supervising toothbrushing or promoting healthy nutrition) because these align with their existing pastoral care and pedagogical roles. However, explaining clinical findings marks a shift from behavioral reinforcement to clinical interpretation, as teachers feel professionally vulnerable due to their limited clinical knowledge.

While broader teacher health roles have been described in other settings, for example, teachers conducting oral examinations and communicating students’ health findings to parents in Uganda [49], locally, such tasks represent not only an added responsibility but also a departure from established teacher roles. Although capacity building has been shown to improve teachers’ oral health knowledge and skills [49–51], training may significantly improve self-efficacy, but it cannot resolve ethical concerns operating at the level of role identity. Practically, the implementation framework should prioritize tiered delivery designs where teachers could distribute the card and provide basic information, while dental personnel remain available to address clinical questions. This preserves teachers’ role as trusted intermediaries without requiring them to assume clinical responsibilities. thus safeguarding both the integrity of the information and teachers’ professional identities.

The Healthy Food Project highlights an important limitation in interpreting acceptability; i.e. positive affective attitude does not necessarily imply confidence in effectiveness. Teachers doubted the ability of school-based activities alone to counter entrenched eating habits at home or the influence of the wider school food environment. This suggests that perceived effectiveness is not only a judgment about the intervention itself, but also about the fit between the intervention and its wider implementation context. Although the national school feeding program was tightly regulated [52], their concerns were well-founded as other foods sold in school canteens are often less strictly regulated, potentially undermining healthy eating efforts. Similar tensions have been reported in other contexts, including the United States, where canteen operators have justified the sale of unhealthy food [53].

Teachers’ awareness of these macro-level constraints helps explain their skepticism. Without parallel changes to the school food environment, teachers perceived a mismatch between what they were expected to teach and what children were able to practice. This reflects evidence that upstream measures, such as regulating the school environment, may be more effective in reducing oral health inequalities than relying solely on educational strategies, which have shown limited and sometimes inequitable impacts [54–56]. Teachers valuing SPARKLE may be insufficient; they must also perceive that the components can work within the constraints of the school and home environment.

Among all components, the bimonthly dissemination of digital infographics was the most acceptable. This was driven by the convergence of low perceived burden and low opportunity cost, as the activity aligned seamlessly with existing parent–teacher communication practices. Teachers did not need to generate content, allocate additional time, or assume new responsibilities. Instead, acceptability appears to be shaped primarily by the absence of competing demands on teachers’ time and effort, which creates a highly favorable implementation condition. In this respect, the findings align with recent methodological critiques of the TFA, which note that opportunity cost is often treated as a unipolar construct and analytically separated from burden, despite their close interrelationship in practice [57].

While previous studies have focused on recipients’ experiences of digital health messaging as cues to action [58, 59], this study contributes an implementer perspective by illustrating the feasibility of leveraging established parent–teacher communication channels, particularly WhatsApp. Teachers also proposed context-specific adaptations to improve inclusivity, such as involving the Tok Batin (the Orang Asli headman) to display physical copies of the infographics in the village community hall. High acceptability of the component can translate into active ownership among implementers, particularly when some flexibility in intervention delivery is permitted.

Building on these findings, future work should focus on refining SPARKLE components and progressing to a small-scale feasibility study. Such work would allow examination of the day-to-day realities of delivering SPARKLE under routine school conditions and whether adaptive delivery strategies can support sustained engagement over time [60]. Comparing teachers’ perspectives on acceptability before and after active engagement with SPARKLE may provide insight into how firsthand experience influences their confidence and willingness to sustain the intervention over time.

Strengths and limitations of the study

A key strength of this study lies in its context-sensitive sampling strategy. Schools were selected through a multistage process, and maximum variation sampling was intended to capture variation in professional roles and school-level implementation perspectives, thereby strengthening the breadth and relevance of insights for SPARKLE’s intended context. Engaging teachers at this early stage may have fostered a sense of ownership and autonomy, encouraging them to speak openly about their concerns and preferences. This prospective approach can help refine SPARKLE early and prevent costly problems from emerging later in large-scale trials [34]. Although this study did not explicitly target indigenous Orang Asli teachers, the inclusion of schools with Orang Asli children ensured that participants brought relevant experience in teaching and engaging these communities. Moreover, the deliberate choice of semi-structured IDIs over focus group discussions offered a less intrusive option, enabling individual teacher engagement without disrupting daily teaching and learning.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. While the use of the Theoretical Framework of Acceptability enabled an in-depth exploration of teachers’ anticipated perceptions and responses to their prospective roles in SPARKLE, views expressed prior to implementation may not fully reflect experiences that emerge in practice. In addition, the recruitment process relied on school principals as intermediaries to identify eligible participants. While this was necessary to respect the school’s administrative structure and navigate timetabling constraints, it may have introduced selection bias; principals may have preferentially identified teachers who were already engaged or open to health promotion. Teacher responses may also have been influenced by social desirability bias. Given that both the research team and teachers are employed within government-linked institutions, participants may have perceived SPARKLE as a government-endorsed initiative, and may have felt less comfortable voicing criticism or disapproval as a result.

Consequently, the findings may overrepresent favorable attitudes toward SPARKLE and may not reflect the views of less engaged teachers or those in schools with less receptive leadership, limiting the transferability of findings across the broader rural teacher workforce. Nevertheless, we observed that the participants articulated both supportive and critical perspectives across SPARKLE components, suggesting a degree of candor despite these constraints.

The study was conducted in a single Malaysian state. As such, the findings may not capture contextual variation across regions with higher poverty rates. Although maximum variation sampling was employed with the goal of capturing diverse perspectives, the final sample was relatively homogeneous. Participants were predominantly Malay and female, reflecting the demographic profile of the rural teaching workforce in the study districts. Consequently, perspectives from male teachers and other ethnic groups may be under-represented. Finally, the sample size was deemed appropriate for an exploratory qualitative study [61], and the findings are intended to be interpreted as indicative rather than generalizable.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates how rural Malaysian teachers conceptualize the acceptability of SPARKLE, as a teacher-led oral health intervention through the lens of the TFA. Teachers generally supported the aims of SPARKLE, but acceptability differed across its components. Components that aligned with existing school structures and everyday workflows were viewed more positively, while those that added significant logistical demands or required teachers to absorb responsibilities perceived as belonging to parents, dental personnel, or the wider school food system were less acceptable. These findings highlight the need to design school health interventions that are adapted to local contexts and implementer capacities if they are to be feasible and sustainable.
