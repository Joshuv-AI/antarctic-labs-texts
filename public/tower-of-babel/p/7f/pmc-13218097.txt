Developing middle-range theories for the implementation of clinical practice guideline recommendations for pre-eclampsia prevention and management in eastern, central and southern Africa: a realist synthesis protocol
Authors: Andrea Solnes Miltenburg, Nadege Sandrine Uwamahoro, Joseph R Mwanga, Benedikte V Lindskog, Raghnild Misje, Albert Kihunrwa, Andrea B Pembe, Brynne Gilmore, Ferdinand C Mukumbang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHypertensive disorders of pregnancy, such as pre-eclampsia (PE), are one of the leading causes of maternal and perinatal deaths in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Although clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) for PE prevention and management are available, there is limited information on their implementation in LMIC contexts. This realist synthesis therefore aims to uncover the causal explanations underpinning the implementation of CPG recommendations for PE prevention and management in Eastern, Central and Southern African contexts. By developing and refining initial programme theories (IPTs), we will generate a pragmatic evidence base explaining how contextual factors trigger mechanisms that lead to intended and unintended outcomes and why implementation varies across the different settings.Methods and analysisWe conceptualise the implementation of CPGs for PE prevention and management as complex social interventions operating within complex adaptive systems. The realist synthesis method will be employed to systematically review the literature for evidence synthesis. The review process will be conducted in five phases, each iteratively building on the previous phase to uncover generative causation and refine the IPTs. We will identify articles through iterative purposive searching in six electronic databases and search engines (Google Scholar, PubMed, Cochrane, MEDLINE, EMBASE Global Health) and through screening WHO sources. Advisory group consultations will be held to formulate, prioritise and refine IPTs. To conceptualise our realist theories through generative causation, we will analyse the data using a retroductive approach, an integration of inductive, deductive and abductive reasoning. We will inductively examine theoretical insights related to five established care moments and explore how CPGs operate during these moments, including where and how they fail to work as intended. The five care moments are: (1) Risk assessment/prevention, (2) Diagnosing disease, (3) Management of PE without severe features, (4) With severe features and (5) Decision making around birth. Deductive reasoning will support sense-making of evidence-based theories through the lens of theories that have been used to explain the adoption of guidelines in healthcare settings. Lastly, abductive reasoning, centring researcher hunches, will help to unearth mechanisms that have been insufficiently detailed in the literature. The intervention-context-actor-mechanism-outcome heuristic will be used to configure programme theories and articulate the theories using the if-then statements.Ethics and disseminationThis project is part of the larger PREvention of Severe Hypertensive Adverse events (PRESHA) project, which aims to improve the detection, prevention and management of PE in Tanzania. PRESHA has ethical clearance from the Regional Ethics Board in Norway and the National Health Research Ethics Committee in Tanzania. Findings of the review will support the contextual development of CPGs for the prevention and management of PE, which will be implemented within the context of the PRESHA trial.

Introduction

The global progress since Millennium Development Goal 5, which focused on improving maternal health, has been significant; however, efforts under Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Target 3.1, which aims to reduce the global maternal mortality ratio to <70 per 100 000 live births, are ongoing.1 These goals and targets have helped to place maternal and newborn health on the global agenda. In many countries in Eastern, Central and Southern Africa (ECSA), progress toward achieving the set goals is deemed insufficient, with maternal mortality rates remaining high.2 Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), including pre-eclampsia (PE), are one of the leading causes of maternal and perinatal deaths globally.3 Hypertensive disorders were the second leading cause of death in East and Central Africa (27.2%) and the third leading cause in the South African Region (17.8%).4 Preventing these deaths requires improvements in the continuum of care from antenatal care (ANC), childbirth and the post partum periods. Recommendations for care provision for women at risk of, or with established PE, are stipulated in several global and national clinical guidelines.5 6

The WHO develops and updates clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) intended to guide clinical care globally. CPG recommendations for prevention and management of PE have been available for over a decade, yet implementation remains suboptimal in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).5 7 8 Failure to make significant improvements in LMIC, while few maternal deaths related to PE occur in High Income Countries (HIC), illustrates that despite all the global knowledge available, something is not working as it should.9 “We know what works” to reduce maternal mortality is a claim frequently repeated in high-level publications.10 However, many interventions that work in HICs appear less successful when implemented in LMICs.

CPGs go through rigorous review processes, including grading of the evidence-based and expert consensus, before being recommended globally.11 12 Beyond the WHO, several other institutions and countries have developed high-quality CPGs, such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines in the UK13 and guidelines from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (ACOG).14 CPGs serve as a set of recommendations for clinicians, guiding patient care. Guidelines are based on the best available research evidence and practice experience. Limitations to CPG include that they do not allow for individualised care, are prone to bias (for instance, who is involved in their development and what perspectives are prioritised) and are not context sensitive. Additionally, there is limited evidence on whether CPGs lead directly to improvements in clinical practice.15

Most of the global evidence on which the recommendations in these CPGs are based is generated in high-resource settings and/or through research approaches such as clinical trials that control for contextual conditions. Insufficient recognition of the context in which interventions are implemented may explain why promising findings from well-conducted clinical trials do not always translate into high adherence or improved health outcomes when these interventions are transferred to complex LMIC healthcare systems.16 Even when policy makers or high-level champions advocate for new, evidence-based interventions and translate these into CPGs, these are often tweaked and modified (sometimes subtly) by front-line health providers, who must reconcile higher-level expectations with on-the-ground realities.17 18 When interventions need to be available across different stages of pregnancy and at different levels of care, the management of PE in LMIC becomes highly complex. We argue that many years of persistent implementation failure of known ‘effective’ management strategies for PE require a paradigm shift. Rather than focusing on the ultimate outcomes of care provision (eg, mortality and morbidity), we should recognise that any outcome, intended or unintended, is the effect of a complex interplay between the context in which programmes are intended to work and the multiple stakeholders involved. Understanding why PE prevention and management do not yield the desired outcomes in LMIC, therefore, requires close attention to the reasoning and behaviour of the individuals involved, their interactions with their environment, and the (assigned) role of these CPGs within the health system in which they operate.

Over the years, most countries in ECSA have developed national policies and recommendations for maternal healthcare services that align with indicators for evaluating progress toward the SDGs. For example, countries are striving to increase ANC attendance from 4 to 8 visits and recommend that women give birth at health facilities with skilled birth attendants. This follows WHO recommendations on ‘antenatal care for a positive pregnancy experience’19 and ‘intrapartum care for a positive childbirth experience’.20 While ANC attendance and facility births have increased significantly in most countries in ECSA, mortality rates in these settings remain high, prompting urgent calls to improve the quality of care women receive during facility attendance.21 Quality of care is thus a critical aspect of the maternal and newborn health agenda. Understanding the concept of ‘quality of care’, however, involves acknowledging that it extends beyond measurable indicators to include perspectives from both providers and recipients of care.22

Health systems are described as ‘complex adaptive systems’ (CAS), and so is any intervention that aims to achieve positive health outcomes for women at risk for PE. Delivering high-quality healthcare is always a complex endeavour, no matter where it is delivered or for which clinical conditions.23 24 A CAS is a dynamic system that consists of a wide variety of elements (eg, stakeholders, resources), in which the behaviour of each is responsive to the actions of others within the system (adaptive); interactions are nonlinear and responses or changes are unpredictable (complex).25 A key feature of a CAS is the recognition of interconnections between system elements and the adaptability of those elements in response to changing conditions. Recognising that health systems, especially in LMIC, are highly dynamic, it can be expected that understanding decision-making for the management of patients with PE also requires knowledge of the unexpected challenges and opportunities that health workers and patients will face.17 This can also help us to understand both how some interventions fail to ‘stick’ in everyday practice settings and why ‘de-normalising’ already embedded practices can be difficult.26 CAS has been used as a theoretical lens in other studies on maternity care27 and in realist studies within a healthcare setting.28

In response to the remaining high levels of mortality and morbidity associated with PE across LMIC contexts, the PREvention of Severe Hypertensive Adverse events (PRESHA) project aims to prevent maternal and perinatal death and disability due to PE in LMICs through improved prediction, prevention and clinical management. As described in the PRESHA protocol29, one of the key aspects of the project is to implement early risk screening through measuring of biomarkers for PE prediction (eg, placental growth factor/soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1) in addition to standard practice. These biomarkers are now recommended by the International Federation of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists in settings where this is feasible and have been integrated in NICE and ACOG guidelines.30 Although this has the potential to improve early identification of women at risk for PE, also in LMIC, the effect on mortality and morbidity can only be expected with the availability of good-quality care. PRESHA therefore aims to understand implementation challenges of PE guidelines and co-design interventions to improve PE prevention and management, adapted and relevant to the Tanzanian context.

Aim and questions

A first step in this project is to explore how, why, and for whom the implementation of CPGs for PE works in the ECSA context. Using realist synthesis methodology, this study aims to (a) Develop an initial programme theory (IPT) on how the implementation of CPG recommendations for PE works, for whom, why and in which contexts, (b) Refine IPTs through comprehensive literature review and synthesis and identify gaps in the literature, (c) Work with stakeholders to validate or refute the arising programme theories and (d) Translate findings into actionable insights for the context in Tanzania by providing stakeholders (health providers, patient survivors and policymakers) with a pragmatic, up-to-date evidence base that explains how specific contexts trigger mechanisms through which the implementation of CPG recommendations for PE prevention and management leads to intended or unintended, positive or negative outcomes.

We aim to answer the following research questions:

What causal explanations, derived through abductive reasoning from the existing literature on CPG recommendations for PE management, can explain how, why and for whom these recommendations work in ECSA settings?

Methods and analysis

Realist synthesis is a theory-driven evidence synthesis approach to understand how, for whom and under what circumstances complex health interventions work in complex environments informed by the realist philosophy of science.31 Following Pawson and Tilley’s seminal work, realist synthesis was developed to explore the interplay between contexts and mechanisms of action and the outcomes this interaction produces.32 33 A realist inquiry allows for developing a generative causal pathway to outcomes of interest through recognising the social architecture of interventions, the actors’ assumptions implicit in intervention activity, and how such assumptions play out in diverse contexts.34 Intervention, Context, Actors, Mechanisms and Outcome (ICAMO) configurations support theorising in realist reviews, offering a heuristic for understanding generative causation. We have chosen this heuristic as it allows us to explain how the intervention (I), (in this case CPG recommendations), unfolds in a particular context (C) and through various actors (A), triggered by mechanisms (M), leading to the observed outcomes (O).35 36

For this synthesis, the following descriptions are used:

Intervention: an intervention can be described as “a combination of programme elements or strategies designed to produce behaviour change or improve health status among individuals or a group”.36 It includes any modality or aspect and its characteristics. The architecture of the programme/intervention can include implementation or reorganisation of resources, the opportunities the implementation offers and constraints associated with the intervention.

This realist synthesis will be reported using Realist and Meta-narrative Evidence Syntheses Evolving Standards (RAMESES).40 The study will be conducted between September 2025 and August 2026. The research team will consist of clinicians with experience in obstetrics and gynaecology, an anthropologist/sociologist, health systems and public health specialists and realist synthesis methodologists. Due to resource constraints, the review team will not include representations of global/national policymakers, front-line health workers or patient representatives from all potential contexts of interest, which may pose a limitation for theory development.

Nevertheless, we have incorporated stakeholder engagement through expert consultation processes. These advisory groups are intended to complement the perspectives within the review team and to inform, refine and validate the emerging theory.

In addition, we will adopt a reflexive approach throughout the review process. This will be operationalised through regular team discussions in which emerging interpretations and programme theory components will be critically examined in light of team members’ disciplinary and positional perspectives. Analytic decisions and interpretations will be documented, and differences in interpretation will be discussed and resolved through team consensus. Where relevant, alternative explanations will be considered and tested against the data. This approach will enable us to remain attentive to how our perspectives may shape the synthesis and to enhance the transparency and credibility of the findings.

In accordance with the RAMESES standards, this review will follow certain pre-determined phases (figure 1). By nature of the realist approach, however, the phases are dynamic, flexible and iterative, as new evidence can change the direction and focus as new areas or theories emerge.33

Phases in the realist review.

Phase 1: Protocol development: define the scope of the project. Through an initial targeted search of key global documents, including existing global guidelines and policies on implementing CPG for the prevention and management of PE, we will identify key concepts and operational underpinnings to inform the scope of the review.

Phase 2: Develop initial programme theories: we will perform a more targeted literature search of global, regional and national CPG guidelines for antenatal and childbirth care related to PE, as well as the underlying evidence on which these CPGs are based. Through the findings from the literature and insights held by the review team and advisory members, we will develop IPTs comprising “if–then–leading to” statements that articulate generative causation using the ICAMO configuration heuristic. We anticipate that some of these initial statements will be incomplete, under-specified or partially supported by the available evidence. These IPTs will therefore be treated as working theories, which will be systematically and iteratively refined through engagement with empirical evidence using abductive and retroductive reasoning to strengthen, revise or reject the proposed causal explanations. The ICAMO relationships comprising the IPT will be grouped based on care moments derived from the CPG. We will identify five key care moments, as illustrated in figure 2, that represent critical decision points within the care pathway, each associated with specific activities and interventions aligned with CPG recommendations. Through advisory group consultation, we will gain insight into their experience with CPGs for PE and ask for feedback on the relevance and prioritisation of our IPTs. The IPT will inform the detailed systematic literature search, as the search strategy will be designed to identify evidence to test and refine the IPTs. In this way, the IPTs serve as a guiding framework for identifying relevant concepts, contexts, mechanisms and outcomes of interest, while remaining open to revision as additional evidence emerges.

Clinical practice guideline (CPG) recommendation categories: CPG recommendations for management of pre-eclampsia (PE) can be divided into five care moments: (1) Recommendations for what to do when PE is suspected including follow-up of women at higher risk of developing PE, (2) Recommendations on confirming diagnosis of PE, (3) Recommendations for treatment and follow-up of women with non-severe (4) or severe features of PE and (5) Recommendations for decision making around mode and timing of birth. ANC, antenatal care.

Phase 3: Search for evidence: peer-reviewed articles, programme reports and formal guidelines will be reviewed and considered if contributing to offer an understanding of generative causation. Specifically, we will look for literature that will support theory development (eg, relevance) and offer sufficient descriptive information (eg, with focus on richness and rigour), including reactions and responses by actors to interventions or the described context. Of interest are any theories, assumptions or rationales that provide information on CPG recommendations for PE prevention and management, with a focus on implementation fidelity.

Phase 4: Refine initial programme theories: the sources included from phase 3 will undergo data extraction, analysis and synthesis using realist logic, including abduction and retroductive reasoning, to elicit generative causation in the form of ICAMO statements. These will then be used to confirm, refute or refine the IPTs from phase 3. We expect that the literature will not provide sufficient evidence to ensure that our IPTs fit directly within the Tanzanian context in which the PRESHA project operates. To translate and validate our findings (or refute them) from phases 1–2 in the Tanzanian context, we will also rely on primary sources. This includes additional stakeholder consultation through interviews with health providers, facility managers and patients/survivors.

Phase 5: Developing middle-range theories: building on the refined programme theories from phase 4 and considering feedback from the advisory group and additional input from primary sources in the context of Tanzania, we will, when possible, triangulate the refined PTs with the substantive literature to support middle-range theory development. Aligned to CAS, we will consider a systems view of implementation and allow for broad health system perspectives, while also acknowledging the different actors involved and their interactions. This will aim to take the specific Tanzanian PTs from phase 4 and provide a more global resulting middle-range theory with applicability across contexts.

Literature searches

Multiple literature searches will be performed throughout the different phases, with different search techniques that are highly iterative. We will perform initial searches to get a feeling of the literature, and these will then be elaborated on to further identify programme theories. Following the realist approach, the search strategies and terms used are likely to evolve as understanding grows throughout the phases. Rather than exhaustive, we will purposefully search for literature as our understanding of the programme theories develops.

In phase 1, a background search will be conducted using Google Scholar to understand the available literature on implementing PE interventions. We will also search for recent and official global guidelines for pregnancy and childbirth, referring to recommendations for PE. In phase 2, when developing the IPTs, we will also look for regional and national guidelines, policies and relevant supporting evidence within the included countries. Literature for these two phases will be identified through government websites, Google Scholar, WHO databases and ECSACOG country contacts.

In phase 3, which is identifying literature for phase 4, a search for peer-reviewed literature will be conducted. The initial search strategy (online supplemental file 1) includes MeSH (medical subject headings) and free text words for this component of the review, with the reminder that this is an iterative and flexible process. This search will extend beyond policy and guidelines documents and their development from phase 1 and 2, and look for evidence on the implementation of CPG. The search will be conducted in the following databases: CINAHL, EMBASE, Medline, Scopus, Web of Science and Global Health.

We will perform snowballing of the reference lists of relevant articles and include further grey literature such as reports, guidelines and training curricula of interest from professional organisations, government websites and non-governmental organisations. Search results will be managed in Covidence review manager for screening.41 We will conduct screening throughout the review process in all phases and update searches as relevant. We will save the included full-text articles in the Zotero reference manager42 as they are identified.

Study selection and appraisal

In phase 3, study selection and appraisal will focus on information relevant to the development of programme theory for CPG recommendations on PE prevention and management. This will be done independently by two reviewers, with discussions in the event of disagreements. Inclusion criteria for selected items are (1) Articles that have the potential to inform theory, (2) Contain adequate descriptive information, (3) Are on general terms or specifically related to HDP/PE and (4) Are conducted fully or partially in any of the ECSA countries. We exclude articles that are solely in high-income settings, but may include reviews that, in addition to ECSA countries, also include studies from high-income settings. We will not exclude based on language or study design. We will follow the proposed consideration for relevance, richness and rigour by Dada et al43 to guide our appraisal of the literature, with the criteria for relevance and richness as listed in tables1 2. Rigour will be assessed based on trustworthiness of the source and coherence of the theory, assessed with the support of the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist and through deliberation among the research team. In case of low rigour, additional resources will be needed to refine or refute identified theories, with the option of primary research. Any type of evidence (ie, qualitative and quantitative empirical studies, reports, commentaries, etc) will be included. Sources will be included if they can meaningfully contribute to refining and understanding programme theories and are specific to the ECSA context.

Data extraction

We will develop an extraction sheet in Covidence that enables an eclectic, iterative approach and is accessible to all team members. Two reviewers will extract excerpts from included data sources and code them on the IPT, in other words, to the hypothesised ICAMO relationships. Data extraction will consist of capturing relevant excerpts from each included source. Each excerpt will be analysed in relation to the IPTs and coded to indicate whether it confirms, partially confirms, refutes, or refines an existing IPT, or whether it contributes to the development of a new ICAMO configuration not previously captured. This approach of recording the excerpt and associated statement makes explicit the underlying relationships between ICAMOs as suggested by the data. Once two reviewers have demonstrated consistent screening decisions and agreement on initial extractions from the same set of sources, the remaining included studies will be divided between reviewers for data extraction. Each resource will then be extracted by a single reviewer, with a randomly selected 10% of extractions independently checked by a second reviewer to ensure consistency and quality. All excluded resources, together with the reasons for exclusion, will be systematically recorded and reported to ensure transparency and reproducibility of the review process. We will extract basic bibliographic information about the studies, including authors, country, study design, aim of the study and population of interest. Theory development will be organised with the ICAMO heuristics.

Data analysis and synthesis

To extract generative causation in phase 4, we will analyse the data primarily through abductive and retroductive reasoning, using inductive and deductive logic within these processes to identify patterns and test the implications of proposed mechanisms. In line with RAMESES guidance, qualitative and quantitative studies will be treated as complementary sources of evidence for ICAMO configurations and integrated iteratively to support, refute or refine IPTs.40 Following data extraction, the analysis will proceed through iterative cycles of comparison between extracted ICAMO configurations and the IPTs. Each extracted ‘if–then–because’ statement will be examined against the relevant IPT to assess how far the empirical material supports or refutes the proposed causal explanation. This stage will be primarily abductive, focusing on identifying the most plausible interpretations of the data in relation to the developing programme theories.

Extracts will be systematically compared across studies to identify recurring or divergent patterns in how intervention, contexts, actors and mechanisms interact to produce outcomes within the identified care moments. Where empirical material cannot be adequately explained by existing IPTs, we will develop new ICAMO configurations and integrate them into the evolving theory, using these observations to further theorise what works, for whom, in what way and why. Where inconsistencies or unexpected findings arise, retroductive reasoning will be used to infer and refine the underlying generative mechanisms that may explain these patterns. IPTs will be iteratively revised to better account for the observed variations in outcomes across different contexts, and each IPT will be coded as either confirmed, partially confirmed, refuted or refined based on the accumulating evidence.

Throughout this process, analytic decisions and theoretical refinements will be documented to ensure transparency in how interpretations are developed and how the programme theory evolves over time. The analysis will continue iteratively until a coherent and explanatorily robust set of refined ICAMO configurations is achieved, capable of accounting for the variation observed across care moments and included study contexts. Ultimately, the analysis in a realist synthesis is highly dynamic, iterative and focused on sensemaking. By progressively refining and consolidating ICAMO configurations and IPTs, we aim to develop middle-range theories that explain why and how particular CPG-related interventions or activities work (or fail to work), for whom and under what conditions.

As part of the deductive strand of the analysis, we will draw on existing theories of CAS and health systems to inform and test our ICAMO configurations. The path-dependency of care processes (eg, the cascade from pregnancy to disease progression across multiple levels of care and stakeholders) means that one stage is expected to influence the next. We will therefore attend to the order of events, sequences, breaking points, assumptions, dependencies and processes, and examine interaction patterns (eg, feedback loops, ripple effects) between context and mechanisms that underpin causal explanations of CPG implementation. In doing so, we will use systems thinking and health systems frameworks (eg, governance, information, financing, service delivery, human resources, medicines and technology) to consider how mechanisms may be triggered at micro (individual), meso (institutional) and macro (societal) levels.

In the event of insufficient existing literature, our abductive approach will support the generation of programme theories, based on, for example, surprising or counterintuitive observations, using the intervention-context-actor-mechanism-outcome heuristics. Through retroductive theorising, based on our combined analysis we aim to understand the causal mechanisms refining our IPTs.44 As our geographical context is limited to ECSA countries, we will distinguish the analysis according to the country where this is relevant for understanding of the ICAMOs, in other parts where country setting is not perceived to be critical (eg, multi-country studies, or where extracted data is common across countries) findings will be organised into cross-cutting themes, rather than country-specific analysis. We will explore the use of software programmes for the analysis (eg, NVivo, Vensim), but due to the highly dynamic, multidisciplinary and multi-country nature of the project we expect to also rely on manual tracking through MS Office programmes.

Advisory group and stakeholder consultations

Advisory group and stakeholder consultations will take on different forms during this realist synthesis. In phase 1, we will conduct an online consultation with obstetricians from the ECSA countries included. The countries selected are members of the East, Central and Southern Africa College of Obstetrics and Gynaecology (ECSACOG). ECSACOG serves as a collegiate training institution with a mission striving for clinical excellence in reproductive health in the region and a platform for shared experience and skills transfer. ECSACOG has 10 member countries: Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda, South Sudan, Tanzania, Uganda, Zambia and Zimbabwe. We will also involve patient representatives from the various countries, through contacting patient organisations, if these exist within these countries. Throughout phase 2–4 we will continue to consult them and ask for feedback on the development and prioritisation of programme theories. During phase 1 the project and the purpose of the meetings will be introduced. We will introduce preliminary ICAMOs and share stories and experiences, introducing realist thinking. The following meetings will include reviewing all the IPTs identified through the literature search, discussing potential rival theories and reflecting on insights from the supplementary data. Reporting of advisory group contributions to the review will follow the approach according to Power et al.45

The PRESHA project has ethical clearance from the Regional Ethics Board in Norway (REK ref.: 762028/IRB ref: IRB00006244) and the National Health Research Ethics Committee (ref: NIMR/HQ/R.8a/Vol.IX/4870) in Tanzania. This will cover the primary data collection deemed necessary as part of phase 4. The findings of the research will be disseminated through academic platforms, including conferences in Tanzania, the ECSA region and globally. We anticipate developing two publications, one for the refined programme theories developed (phase 1–3) and a complete realist synthesis with middle-range programme theories, including recommendations of how the findings can have meaning in future CPG implementation studies (phase 4–5). The stakeholder meetings, involvement of the advisors from the different countries, and health providers within Tanzania will ensure dissemination among health personnel within the region and in the study setting of the PRESHA project. Findings of the review will facilitate contextual development of CPGs for prevention and management of PE that will be implemented within the context of the anticipated PRESHA trial.

Conclusion

Through the proposed realist synthesis approach, this research offers opportunities to gain understanding of why and how implementation of CPGs for PE, in a given context, leads to intended or unintended outcomes. The realist methodology allows us to go beyond the evidence of effective interventions and generate theories on the contexts and mechanisms that influence the outcomes of these interventions. Through existing data and primary resources, we are able to validate these theories, informing practitioners and policymakers on how to improve CPG implementation. This synthesis will generate middle-range programme theories that explain how and why the implementation of CPG recommendations for prevention and management of PE is successful, or not, and under what conditions. We expect these middle-range theories to be valuable beyond the thematic and geographical focus of this synthesis.
