Rehabilitation in long COVID: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sneha Chakraverty, Nikolas Chynkiamis, Neha Maru, Dimitris Megaritis, Emily Hume, Gabriel Cucato, Eurico N. Wilhelm, Ioannis Vogiatzis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe long-term effects of rehabilitation in individuals with long COVID remain unclear. This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the short- and long-term impacts of rehabilitation programmes on exercise capacity, health-related quality of life (HRQoL), pulmonary function, chronic dyspnoea, anxiety, depression and fatigue in people with long COVID.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis guidelines and the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, five databases were searched, with the final search updated in August 2025. Eligible studies included adults with long COVID who underwent rehabilitation interventions. Outcomes were synthesised using meta-analyses and narrative synthesis where appropriate. The risk of bias was assessed using appropriate tools for both randomised and nonrandomised studies. The review was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024535365).Results12 studies (n=1403 participants) published between 2020 and 2025 were included. In the short term, rehabilitation compared with usual care significantly increased exercise capacity (6-min walking test; mean difference (MD) 102.34 m; 95% CI 44.48–160.21 m; p<0.0001), reduced dyspnoea (modified Medical Research Council; MD −1.44; 95% CI −2.72– −0.16) and anxiety symptoms (hospital anxiety and depression scale-A; MD −1.64; 95% CI −3.02– −0.26; p=0.02). Studies showed wide variation in educational content and delivery. No significant long-term effects were observed in pooled quantitative analyses for HRQoL or dyspnoea; however, qualitative findings indicated sustained improvements in HRQoL in some longitudinal studies.ConclusionRehabilitation programmes yielded short-term benefits in exercise capacity, dyspnoea and anxiety. Evidence for long-term efficacy remains limited and inconsistent. Further high-quality, follow-up studies are needed to determine the durability of these effects in people with long COVID.

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), since the onset of the pandemic of coronavirus disease in 2019 (COVID-19), a total of 24.9 million cases has been recorded in the UK [1]. Those infected with acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) may develop a multitude of symptoms, including fatigue, breathlessness, post-exertional malaise, brain fog, headache, anxiety, depression and muscle pain [2, 3]. In line with the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), those experiencing ongoing symptoms for 4 weeks or more in the absence of any alternative diagnosis [4] after the acute onset of COVID-19 are considered to have long COVID [5]. As of March 2023, the UK Office for National Statistics estimated that 1.9 million people in private households in the UK were living with self-reported symptoms of long COVID, representing approximately 3% of the private household population [6].

Given the heterogeneous and often complex symptomatology associated with long COVID, its management necessitates a personalised approach tailored to individual patient needs. Despite growing clinical interest, current research on effective management strategies remains limited, largely due to the multifaceted nature of the condition [7]. Nonetheless, a multidisciplinary framework involving the long-term monitoring of ongoing symptoms is essential to ensure comprehensive and coordinated care [8, 9].

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is an effective approach to improve exercise tolerance, dyspnoea and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in patients with respiratory symptoms [10]. It combines structured exercise, primarily aerobic and resistance training, with education on disease knowledge and management [11]. PR is a safe and effective intervention, offering a flexible mode of delivery through both face-to-face and tele-rehabilitation formats [12]. A recent randomised controlled trial (RCT) has demonstrated that adapted rehabilitation programmes for individuals with long COVID can significantly improve exercise tolerance and may exert beneficial immunomodulatory effects [13].

Furthermore, evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses shows that rehabilitation programmes for long COVID improves dyspnoea, physical function, quality of life and psychological symptoms [14, 15]. Multicomponent rehabilitation programmes lasting 4–8 weeks are particularly effective [16], and face-to-face delivery appears superior to tele-rehabilitation for improving physical function and quality of life [17, 18].

While previous systematic reviews [14–18] have primarily examined the short-term outcomes of rehabilitation (4–12 weeks), their focus has largely been limited to the exercise component, with minimal consideration given to the educational aspects of these programmes, which are recommended in PR guidance [10, 11, 19]. Furthermore, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding the long-term effects of rehabilitation interventions for long COVID. This is particularly concerning, as a recent systematic review reported that 42% of COVID-19 survivors continue to experience neurological, physical and psychological sequelae for up to 2 years following infection [20]. These findings underscore the urgent need for sustained intervention strategies to mitigate the long-term burden of long COVID. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review is to update the evidence on the short-term effects and evaluate the long-term effects of rehabilitation programmes (encompassing exercise and education components) on key outcomes, such as exercise capacity, quality of life, pulmonary function, dyspnoea and fatigue, in long COVID.

Methods

This systematic review followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [21] (supplementary table S1) and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024535365).

Search strategy

The following electronic databases were searched from inception to August 2025, to identify studies on exercise-based rehabilitation for long COVID: PubMed, CINAHL, CENTRAL, Web of Science and PsycArticle (figure 1). References were managed using EndNote, with duplicates removed before screening. Title/abstract screening, followed by full-text screening, was performed in CADIMA software. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in the supplementary appendix S1.

Flow chart of the systematic review and meta-analysis screening process (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) diagram). RCT: randomised controlled trial; UC: usual care.

Eligibility criteria

Titles and abstracts of all identified records were screened by two independent reviewers (S.C. and E.H.) followed by full-text screening in duplicate by five independent reviewers (S.C., E.H., G.C., E.N.W. and N.M.). Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with an additional reviewer (I.V.).

Inclusion/exclusion criteria

Studies were included according to PICOS criteria. 1) Population: adults (age ≥18 years) experiencing long COVID symptoms persisting for ≥4 weeks post-infection, as defined by NICE [7]; 2) Intervention: structured exercise training combined with an educational component; 3) Comparator: presence of a comparator group; 4) Outcome: at least one of the following outcomes reported: exercise capacity, quality of life, dyspnoea, fatigue, pulmonary functions or psychological state; 5) Study design: RCTs and non-RCTs.

To ensure inclusivity, studies in all languages were considered, although the initial search was in English. Authors were contacted when information was incomplete or unclear.

Studies were excluded if they lacked a confirmed educational component; lacked a comparator group; did not report relevant outcomes; were reviews, editorials, commentaries or conference abstracts, or included populations outside the age range or without long COVID as defined by NICE [7].

Data extraction

Study data were extracted by two independent reviewers (S.C. and E.H.) and discrepancies were resolved by discussion with a third reviewer (E.N.W.). A standardised extraction form was used to collect bibliographic details (author, year, country and study period), participant characteristics (sample size, sex, age and hospitalisation status), intervention details for exercise and education components (type, intensity, frequency and duration), and all relevant outcomes (exercise capacity, quality of life, dyspnoea and fatigue) including key findings. Mean±sd values for pre- and post-intervention outcomes were extracted for the pooled-effects analysis. For missing data, the corresponding author of the study was contacted for the original data, or data in available graphs were extracted using ImageJ software [22].

Study quality

Risk of bias was independently assessed in duplicate by three reviewers (S.C., E.H. and N.M.) using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for randomised trials (RoB 2) [23] and the “Risk of Bias in Non-randomised Studies of Interventions” (ROBINS-I-V2), for nonrandomised studies [24]. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, in consultation with an additional reviewer (E.N.W.) until consensus was achieved.

Data analysis

A descriptive analysis of the moderating variables was conducted, calculating the number and frequency for qualitative variables and the mean±sd for quantitative variables.

Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted to assess the short and long-term effects of rehabilitation on specific outcomes related to long COVID. Analyses were conducted using MetaAnalysisOnline.com [25], provided that the targeted outcome measures were reported in at least two studies.

A random-effects model was used to compare pre–post-intervention changes between the rehabilitation and control groups. Mean differences (MD) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated for single-scale outcomes, and standardised mean differences (SMDs) with 95% CI were used when multiple scales measured the same construct. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 [26]. For studies in which the mean±sd of post-intervention changes was not reported, these values were calculated using the following formula: Meanchange=Meanfinal−MeanbaselineSDchange=(SD2baseline+SD2final−(2.Corr.SDbaseline⋅SDfinal))Where “Corr” refers to the pre–post-intervention correlation coefficient of measurement. If Corr was not reported, the change of score sd was calculated assuming a coefficient of correlation of 0.5 [27].

Random-effects models were used to pool the effects of rehabilitation. Different study designs, including RCTs and non-RCTs, were analysed separately. Unstandardised MDs were calculated whenever possible; when raw MDs could not be pooled, SMDs were calculated by using Hedges'g statistics [28]. Effect sizes were interpreted as small (<0.2), moderate (0.2–0.8) and large (>0.8). Heterogeneity was evaluated using the Higgins I2 test, with interpretation including low (I2≤25%), moderate (I2>25% and I2≤ 50%), substantial (I2>50% and I2<75%) and high (I2≥75%) [29]. Subgroup analyses were conducted by study designs, rehabilitation duration (≤8 weeks and >8 weeks) and mode of delivery (tele-rehabilitation and face to face).

Results

Database searches identified 2945 records. After screening titles/abstracts and removing duplicates, 226 full texts were reviewed, with 214 excluded (population n=65, intervention n=72, no usual care n=36, outcomes n=5, study design n=36), leaving 12 studies (nine RCTs, three non-RCTs) for inclusion in the review (figure 1).

The present systematic review included 12 studies, comprising 1403 adults with long COVID. The characteristics of the included studies are presented in tables 1 and 2.

Risk-of-bias evaluation

The risk of bias in RCTs is presented in the online supplementary table S2. Out of the nine RCTs, six had a low risk of bias [13, 30–34], two had some concerns [35, 36] and one was rated high risk [37], primarily due to allocation concealment and reporting of results.

The risk of bias in non-RCTs can be found in the online supplementary table S3. Of the three studies included, one had a low risk of bias [38] and two had a moderate risk of bias [39, 40], largely because assessor blinding was not reported.

Participants

All participants were adults with long COVID. Sample sizes ranged from 34 to 585, mean age ranged from 23 to 61 years and time since infection ranged from 4 to 11 months (table 1).

Interventions and controls

Across studies, exercise programmes were multimodal, typically combining aerobic and resistance training with breathing or ventilatory control exercises, and in some cases balance, flexibility, inspiratory muscle training, fatigue management or mindfulness-based activities. Several interventions were supervised and/or individually tailored [13, 30–34, 38–40] (table 2). Out of 12 studies reported, seven studies provided standard education in the form of basic guidelines and instructions to perform exercise [13, 30, 31, 34, 37, 39, 40], two incorporated psychological counselling [32, 35] and three offered educational instruction on symptom management [33, 36, 38]. Educational components were generally brief and focused on self-management, healthy lifestyle behaviours, physical activity and breathing guidance, with some studies also including dietary advice, psychological support or WHO based education delivered face-to-face or online (table 2). Intervention duration ranged from 6 to 16 weeks, with frequencies of 2–5 sessions per week.

Control groups received usual clinical care without structured exercise [30, 32–36, 38, 40]. In seven of these studies, usual care was supplemented with an educational component [30, 32–34, 36, 37, 40], whereas three reported no education for controls [13, 31, 39].

Regarding delivery modes, seven studies implemented tele-rehabilitation [30, 32–36, 38, 40], three delivered interventions face-to-face [36, 38, 39] and two used a hybrid approach [13, 37].

Most studies assessed short-term effects; however, five studies included follow-up evaluations: one at 3 months [31], another at 6 and 12 months [32], and others at 12 [37] and 6 months post-intervention [34, 35].

Outcomes assessments varied across studies. HRQoL was assessed using the EuroQoL five-dimension questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) [13, 32, 39], the short form (SF)-36 [30, 33], the SF-12 [34] and the St George's Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ) [31].

Exercise capacity was evaluated using the 6-min walking test (6MWT) [30, 31, 34, 36, 39, 40], the incremental shuttle walk test (ISWT) [13, 38], the sit-to-stand test [33, 40], the short physical performance battery (SPPB) test [13, 39] and the post-COVID-19 functional status (PCFS) evaluation [33, 40].

Dyspnoea was assessed using the modified Medical Research (mMRC) scale [31, 34–36, 40], the Borg dyspnoea scale [31, 40], the dyspnoea-12 [13], the clinical COPD questionnaire (CCQ) [35] and the London chest activity of daily living (LCADL) scale [40].

Fatigue was assessed using the functional assessment of chronic illness therapy fatigue scale (FACIT-F) scale [13, 40] and the modified fatigue impact scale (MFIS) [36].

Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the hospital anxiety and depression scale (HADS) [32, 38–40], the Beck's depression inventory [39] and the impact of events scale-revised (IES-R) [32, 39].

Two studies used the Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA) for cognitive function [13, 39].

Physical activity was assessed by the international physical activity questionnaire in one study [32].

Five studies recorded pulmonary function by evaluating forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and forced vital capacity (FVC) value using spirometry [30, 33, 34, 38, 39].

Meta-analysis

Efficacy of rehabilitation on long COVID

Exercise capacity

A meta-analysis was conducted using data from six studies [30, 31, 34, 36, 39, 40] that employed the 6MWT to evaluate the effect of rehabilitation on exercise capacity both short term and long term (figure 2a,b). In the short term, results from four RCTs [30, 31, 34, 36] indicated a pooled MD of 102.34 m (95% CI 44.48 to 160.21 m; p<0.0001) in favour of rehabilitation. When non-RCTs [39, 40] were included, the overall effect estimate was 67.22 m (95% CI 10.62–123.83 m), indicating a statistically significant improvement in the intervention group (p<0.02). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2=98.5%).

RCT: randomised controlled trial; CI: confidence interval; df: degrees of freedom.

A pre-specified subgroup analysis based on the study design was performed. Among RCTs, the pooled MD was 102.34 m (95% CI 44.48–160.21 m; p<0.0001), demonstrating a significant benefit for rehabilitation. In contrast, non-RCT studies yielded a pooled MD of −7.54 m (95% CI −13.88– −1.20 m; p=0.48), indicating no statistically significant difference between intervention and control groups.

Dyspnoea

Given the predominance of the mMRC scale, four studies [31, 35, 36, 40] utilising this measure were pooled for meta-analysis to assess the impact of rehabilitation on dyspnoea. One study [34] was excluded due to the absence of baseline mMRC data. The overall random-effects model revealed an MD of −1.36 (95% CI −2.29– −0.44), indicating a benefit of rehabilitation compared with control (p=0.004) (figure 3). Notably, heterogeneity was high (I2=96%).

Change in dyspnoea (assessed via mMRC) in response to rehabilitation compared with usual care. RCT: randomised controlled trial; CI: confidence interval; df: degrees of freedom.

An effect estimate of −1.44 (95% CI −2.72– −0.16) in mMRC was observed in the RCT [31, 35, 36] subgroup analysis, indicating a statistically significant improvement in the intervention group (p<0.0001). The single non-RCT [40] included in the analysis reported a similar effect size (MD of −1.36; 95% CI −2.30– −0.44; p<0.0001), also favouring the intervention compared with the control group.

Pulmonary function

Five studies assessed pulmonary function using spirometry [30, 33, 34, 38, 39]. Of these, four studies reported FVC and FEV1 values in litres, while one study [39] presented results as a fraction of the percentage predicted normal values. Due to this variation in measurement units, SMD were calculated using Cohen's d. No difference between PR and control was observed for FEV1 (SMD=0.11; 95% CI −0.12–0.34; p=0.33) (supplementary figure S1a). However, no heterogeneity was observed (I2=0%). The nonsignificant effect remained when running the subgroup analysis on RCTs (SMD=0.14; 95% CI −0.12–0.41; p=0.90) and non-RCTs (SMD=0.02; 95% CI from −0.43–0.47; p=0.93).

For FVC, the pooled analysis of all five studies yielded an SMD of 0.07 (95% CI −0.16–0.30) (supplementary figure S1b). However, the difference was not statistically significant (p=0.54), and no heterogeneity was observed (I2=0%).

Subgroup analysis by study design included three RCTs [30, 33, 34], which reported a SMD of 0.07 (95% CI −0.20–0.33; p=0.93), indicating no significant difference between intervention and control groups. The two non-RCTs [38, 39] reported an SMD of 0.09 (95% CI −0.36–0.54; p=0.80), also showing no significant difference. The test for subgroup differences was not statistically significant, suggesting consistency in effect estimates across study designs.

Quality of life

Three studies [13, 32, 39], two RCTs [13, 32] and one non-RCT [39], were included in the meta-analysis to evaluate quality of life using the EQ-5D-5L instrument. For the EQ-5D-5L utility index, the pooled analysis using a random-effects model with the inverse variance method revealed no statistically significant difference between the experimental and control groups, with an MD of 0.07 and 95% CI of −0.09–0.22 (supplementary figure S2a). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (I2=89.6%), indicating variability in effect sizes. In addition, analysis of the EQ-5D-5L visual analogue scale scores, based on the same three studies and involving 314 participants in the experimental group and 331 in the control group, also showed no significant difference between cohorts. The pooled MD was −0.93 (95% CI −1.92–0.07), and no significant heterogeneity was detected, suggesting consistency in the direction and magnitude of effects across studies (supplementary figure S2b).

Fatigue

Two studies, one RCT [13] and one non-RCT [40], were included in the meta-analysis. Based on the analysis performed using a random-effects model with the inverse variance method to compare the MD, there was no statistical difference between the two cohorts; the summarised MD was 7.96 with a 95% CI of −2.11–18.02. However, a high heterogeneity was detected (I2=93%) (supplementary figure S3).

Psychological state

Meta-analyses were conducted to evaluate the effects of the experimental intervention on anxiety and depression symptoms, measured using the HADS. For anxiety symptoms (HADS-A), the pooled analysis across four studies (three non-RCTs [38–40] and one RCT [32]) showed a statistically significant reduction in anxiety scores favouring the experimental group (MD of −1.64, 95% CI −3.02– −0.26; p=0.02) (supplementary figure S4). However, moderate-to-high heterogeneity was present (I2=71%; p=0.0158). Subgroup analysis indicated that the observed effect was primarily driven by non-RCTs (MD of −2.33, 95% CI −3.34– −1.31), whereas the single RCT reported a smaller, nonsignificant effect (MD of −0.20, 95% CI −1.14–0.74). A significant difference between subgroups was detected (p=0.0027), suggesting potential bias or overestimation in non-RCTs.

For depression symptoms (HADS-D), the overall effect across the same studies was not significant (MD of −0.79, 95% CI −2.52–0.95; p=0.3747), showing substantial heterogeneity (I2=80.1%; p=0.002) (supplementary figure S5). While non-RCTs showed a trend toward improvement (MD of −1.56, 95% CI −3.30–0.19), this effect was not significant, and the included RCT reported a nonsignificant effect in the opposite direction (MD of 0.80, 95% CI −0.08–1.68). Subgroup analysis revealed a significant difference between study types (p=0.0180), highlighting inconsistencies that may be attributable to study design and methodological quality.

Comparison between face-to-face and tele-rehabilitation programmes

A pre-specified subgroup analysis was conducted across four RCTs to assess the impact of programme delivery mode (face-to-face versus tele-rehabilitation) on clinical outcomes. Of the four RCTs evaluating exercise capacity using the 6MWT, three [30, 31, 34] implemented tele-rehabilitation and one [36] used face-to-face delivery. For dyspnoea outcomes, assessed in three RCTs, two [31, 35] employed tele-rehabilitation and one face-to-face [36]. Significant improvements were observed in the intervention groups for both exercise capacity (p<0.0005) and dyspnoea (p<0.03). However, no significant differences were found between subgroups based on delivery mode (p=0.31), indicating that tele-rehabilitation and face-to-face programmes were comparably effective in enhancing these functional outcomes.

All studies reporting pulmonary function outcomes employed tele-rehabilitation. As a result, subgroup comparisons for these outcomes were not possible. The findings suggest that while the mode of rehabilitation delivery may influence functional outcomes such as exercise capacity and dyspnoea, its effect on spirometric measures remains inconclusive.

In terms of HRQoL, measured by the EQ-5D-5L, two RCTs were included: one was delivered via tele-rehabilitation [32], while the other utilised a hybrid delivery model [13]. For psychological outcomes assessed using HADS, only one RCT was included [32], via tele-rehabilitation. Similarly, for fatigue outcomes measured by the FACIT-fatigue scale, only one RCT was available [13], representing a face-to-face format.

Comparison between ≤8 weeks and >8 weeks rehabilitation programmes

A pre-specified subgroup analysis was planned to examine the impact of programme duration on clinical outcomes. All RCTs reporting the 6MWT and mMRC scale results employed interventions lasting less than or equal to 8 weeks. Therefore, subgroup comparisons for these outcomes could not be performed.

In evaluating pulmonary function outcomes, three RCTs were included. Of these, two RCTs [30, 38] implemented rehabilitation for 8 weeks or less, whereas one was extended beyond 8 weeks [33]. No significant differences were observed between the two intervention durations (p=0.24 and p=0.70, respectively). Furthermore, the test for subgroup differences in FEV1 and FVC yielded a nonsignificant result (p=0.90), indicating no meaningful variation in spirometric outcomes based on the duration of rehabilitation.

In terms of HRQoL, measured by the EQ-5D-5L, two RCTs were included: one <8 weeks [32] and the other for >8 weeks [13]. For psychological outcomes assessed using the HADS, only one RCT was included [32], which applied a duration of <8 weeks. Similarly, for fatigue outcomes measured by the FACIT-fatigue scale, only one RCT was available [13], which implemented a programme >8 weeks.

Long-term effects of rehabilitation

Five studies assessed the long-term effects of rehabilitation interventions [31, 32, 34, 35, 37]. One study was excluded from the meta-analysis due to a high risk of bias identified during quality assessment [37]. The remaining four studies were included in the quantitative synthesis. As one study reported outcomes at both 6 and 12 months [32], we used the 6-month follow-up data to maintain consistency across studies.

HRQoL was evaluated using the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) 29+2 Profile v.2.1 (PROPr) [32], SGRQ [31] and SF-12 [34] The pooled SMD for HRQoL was −0.74 (95% CI −2.54–1.06), indicating a nonsignificant effect (p=0.42) (supplementary figure S6). However, a higher heterogeneity was observed I2=96.6%.

Dyspnoea was measured using the PROMIS dyspnoea severity form [32] and the mMRC scale [31, 35]. The pooled SMD for dyspnoea was −0.56 (95% CI −1.72–0.60), also showing no significant effect (p=0.34) (supplementary figure S7).

Functional capacity was measured by 6MWT in two studies [31, 34]. The pooled SMD for functional capacity was 0.88 (95% CI 0.56–1.19), indicating a significant effect (p<0.0001; figure 2b).

Education

Educational content varied widely across studies. Some interventions provided only brief sessions or general lifestyle advice, such as short physiotherapist-led education with written instructions or basic healthy living guidance [30, 33, 37–39]. Other programmes delivered more structured education, including WHO-aligned self-management modules, psychoeducation on symptom mechanisms or health-related information delivered digitally or face to face [13, 32, 34–36]. Overall, education differed substantially in content, depth and delivery mode, reflecting considerable heterogeneity across rehabilitation interventions.

A subgroup analysis compared studies implementing structured education versus non-structured education for anxiety and depression symptoms. Four studies with structured education reported an SMD of 7.68, while one study with non-structured education reported an SMD of 6.01. The difference between subgroups was not statistically significant (p=0.32). However, heterogeneity was extremely high (I2=98.4%; p<0.0001), indicating substantial variability across studies and limiting the reliability of pooled estimates (supplementary figure S8).

Discussion

Our findings confirm that rehabilitation significantly improves exercise capacity and reduces dyspnoea and anxiety symptoms in the short term in long COVID survivors. These results are consistent with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses that have reported improvements in exercise capacity and symptom burden in both acute and post-acute COVID-19 populations [16, 41, 42]. However, these reviews restricted inclusion to RCTs, whereas our review intentionally incorporated both RCTs and non-RCTs to provide a more comprehensive overview of real-world programme delivery.

The pooled analysis of six studies using the 6MWT showed a significant improvement in exercise capacity (by 102.34 m), with a more pronounced effect observed in RCTs. Notably, this improvement exceeds the minimally important difference (MID) of 23–37 m, typically reported for long-term conditions [43]. Similarly, dyspnoea, primarily measured via the mMRC scale, showed a significant mean reduction of 1.36 points, with consistent effects observed across various study designs. This change exceeds the established MID of 0.5 for COPD populations undergoing PR [44].

Rehabilitation was found to significantly reduce anxiety, but not depression symptoms. This result aligns with findings from a recent systematic review and meta-analysis, which also reported that rehabilitation is effective in alleviating anxiety symptoms, but not necessarily depression [14, 16]. The differential effect may be due to the emphasis on physical activity and breathing techniques, which can directly influence anxiety through improved autonomic regulation and perceived control [45], whereas depression may require more targeted psychological interventions [46]. However, it is important to interpret these findings with caution, as our conclusions are based mainly on non-RCTs. Further studies are needed to confirm the effects on anxiety symptoms.

Our review did not find significant improvements in fatigue, contrasting with recent systematic reviews reporting modest benefits in long COVID populations [47]. This discrepancy may reflect methodological differences, as our analysis included both RCTs and non-RCTs and required interventions to incorporate both exercise and education, whereas previous reviews restricted inclusion to RCTs without emphasising these components. A physiological explanation of non-improvement of fatigue in our review may be the multifactorial nature of fatigue, which involves not only respiratory issues but also autonomic dysfunction, neuroinflammation and post-exertional symptom exacerbation (i.e. factors not adequately addressed by conventional rehabilitation programmes) [48]. Additionally, standardised protocols may lack the flexibility needed to accommodate the diverse symptom profiles and recovery trajectories seen in long COVID [49]. These factors suggest that more individualised, multidisciplinary approaches may be necessary to effectively manage fatigue in this population.

Rehabilitation did not demonstrate significant improvements in pulmonary function. This finding aligns with earlier studies suggesting that while rehabilitation enhances functional outcomes, its impact on spirometric parameters may be limited, particularly in the context of long COVID [50]. Similarly, a previous retrospective analysis found no significant association between pulmonary function test results and the severity of post-COVID symptoms [51]. The predominance of tele-rehabilitation (70% of included studies) may have contributed to these findings, as face-to-face rehabilitation has shown to be more effective in improving FVC in long COVID patients [42]. Another reason can be a shift in long COVID phenotypes over time, noting that earlier cohorts were characterised predominantly by severe pulmonary impairment, whereas more recent cohorts present with fewer lung-related sequelae [52, 53]. Nonetheless, emerging longitudinal evidence demonstrates that respiratory sequelae remain clinically meaningful in a significant proportion of individuals [54].

For HRQoL assessed via EQ-5D-5L, no difference was found between the intervention and usual care groups. This finding contrasts with most of the existing literature [14, 55], which suggests that rehabilitation contributes to improved HRQoL in individuals recovering from long COVID. One possible explanation for this discrepancy lies in the inclusion criteria: our study focused specifically on exercise and education components, whereas many existing studies include a wider range of self-management and mental health support interventions, in the form of multicomponent rehabilitation, which may have a more pronounced impact on HRQoL.

Long-term effects

Evidence on the long-term sustainability of rehabilitation benefits in long COVID is limited, as only a few studies in our review included follow-up assessments. Pooled analyses showed no significant long–term effects on HRQoL or dyspnoea, and although two studies demonstrated a sustained improvement in 6MWT performance [31, 34], the small sample size restricts generalisability. Overall, the limited and inconsistent follow-up data highlight the need for more rigorous longitudinal research to determine whether the effects are maintained over time.

Influence of delivery mode and duration

Subgroup analyses did not demonstrate any significant influence of the mode of delivery or duration on clinical outcomes. This finding contrasts with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses, which have reported face-to-face programmes [17] lasting 4–8 weeks, are effective in managing long COVID [16]. However, more recent evidence indicates no substantial difference in outcomes between face-to-face and tele-rehabilitation [14]. One possible explanation for these conflicting findings is that the effectiveness of rehabilitation may depend on various contextual factors, such as the structure and components of the intervention, as well as the severity of post-COVID symptoms.

Integration of core rehabilitation components

Although rehabilitation is widely recommended for managing long COVID symptoms [50, 56], most contemporary interventions still prioritise exercise while neglecting structured education. Yet education is recognised as a core rehabilitation component and is essential for improving self-management, emotional well-being, and long-term behaviour change. International respiratory societies have long identified education as a foundational element of PR programmes, even though its specific benefits are less frequently evaluated than exercise training [57]. Our review is among the first to examine interventions that deliberately combine exercise with education; however, only a minority of studies provided a standardised educational programme related to long COVID [13, 35, 36, 40], with many offering only brief general instructions that fall short of comprehensive education. The presence of both highly structured modules and very brief informational inputs across studies underscores the absence of a standardised educational framework within long-COVID rehabilitation. This inconsistency likely contributes to variability in patient engagement, self-management outcomes and overall programme effectiveness. As education is considered a core pillar of rehabilitation programmes such as PR, the variable approaches observed in these studies highlight an urgent need for clearer, evidence informed guidance on the design and delivery of education specifically tailored for long COVID.

Limitations

Variability in programme design, delivery and outcome measures limited our ability to identify the most effective intervention components. Second, differences in symptom duration and severity, as well as inconsistent definitions of long COVID across studies (e.g. NICE versus WHO criteria), may have introduced heterogeneity. Thirdly, the limited number of studies with long-term follow-up restricts conclusions about the sustainability of rehabilitation benefits. Additionally, the predominance of small sample sizes and nonrandomised designs in some studies may have affected the robustness of the findings. Finally, a key limitation is the absence of standardised, evidence-based educational recommendations for long COVID within rehabilitation frameworks, resulting in wide variation in the type and depth of education delivered across interventions.

A further limitation is the limited reporting of safety outcomes. None of the included studies systematically monitored or documented post–exertional malaise, and adverse events were generally under-reported, restricting our ability to evaluate the safety profile of rehabilitation in long COVID.

Future directions

Future research should prioritise high-quality RCTs with standardised protocols, longer follow-up periods and larger sample sizes. Studies should also explore the cost-effectiveness of different delivery models and investigate the role of patient education and psychological support as integral components of rehabilitation. Given the evolving nature of long COVID, a flexible, multidisciplinary approach remains essential to address the diverse and persistent symptoms experienced by this population.

Conclusions

Rehabilitation improves short-term exercise capacity, dyspnoea and reduces anxiety symptoms in people with long COVID, with the strongest evidence from RCTs. Long-term outcomes remain unclear. Standardised, multidisciplinary rehabilitation programmes, including consistent, evidence-based education and further high-quality research are needed to optimise and evaluate long-term benefits.
