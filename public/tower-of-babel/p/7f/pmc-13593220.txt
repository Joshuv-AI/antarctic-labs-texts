Is androgenetic alopecia a marker of arrhythmia?: A case-control study
Authors: Nihal Sari, Erhan Yilmaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Androgenetic alopecia (AGA) is one of the most common types of hair loss. Histopathological evidence of chronic inflammation in patients with AGA has prompted investigations into the potential increased cardiovascular risk. This study aimed to investigate the presence of cardiovascular risk factors and potential conduction disorders in male patients diagnosed with AGA using both echocardiographic (ECHO) and electrocardiographic (ECG) assessments. This case -control study included 40 male patients diagnosed with AGA and 40 male controls, aged between 18 and 50 years. All participants in both groups underwent comprehensive cardiological examination by a specialist cardiologist and were evaluated using ECG and ECHO. Examination of ECG parameters showed that P-wave dispersion (Pdis) was significantly higher in the patient group (P = .016). Additionally, the shortest and corrected QT interval (QTc min) was significantly lower in the patient group than in the control group (P = .031). No significant difference was found between the patient and control groups in terms of ECO measurements (all P >.05). There was a moderately positive correlation between disease duration and Pdis (r = .365) and between disease stage and Pmax, Pdis, QTc min, and QTc max (r = .490, r = .499, r = .445, and r = .432, respectively). This study concluded that male patients with AGA exhibited an increased risk of developing arrhythmias, as indicated by elevated Pdis and reduced QTc min values. In this context, ECG may serve as a useful screening tool for conduction abnormalities in male with AGA. We suggest that patients with AGA may benefit from closer cardiac monitoring and cardiology consultations, especially regarding rhythm disorders.

1. Introduction

Among the various types of hair loss, one of the most common forms is androgenetic alopecia (AGA). It is a form of alopecia that is induced by androgens in genetically predisposed individuals. Pathogenetically, free androgens in the bloodstream that can bind to androgen receptors on the scalp lead to miniaturization of hair follicles, resulting in the transformation of terminal hairs into vellus hairs.[1] This miniaturization-related hair loss is particularly observed in the frontotemporal and vertex regions of men. Androgen-mediated receptors responsible for AGA pathogenesis are also located in the endothelial lining of the arterial walls, in addition to the scalp. Elevated serum androgen levels promote the proliferation of smooth muscle cells within the vasculature, thereby increasing predisposition to hypertension.[2] Additionally, studies have indicated an elevated risk for ischemic heart disease among males exhibiting advanced AGA.[3] In fact, 1 review recommended the assessment and appropriate management of cardiovascular risk in patients with male-pattern AGA.[4]

One of the primary factors accelerating subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, – a significant contributor to the overall cardiovascular risk,—is the presence of chronic inflammation.[5] In this regard, AGA is traditionally considered a non-scarring form of alopecia, with inflammation not playing a major role in its pathogenesis. However, recent studies have reported the presence of inflammation at the pilosebaceous unit level in the scalp of AGA patients.[6] Histopathological examinations have demonstrated the presence of chronic inflammation in patients with AGA, with this finding being the focus of numerous studies seeking to determine whether these individuals are at an increased risk for cardiovascular disease. Various studies have evaluated the risk of atherosclerosis in AGA patients through measurements such as carotid intima-media thickness, epicardial fat thickness, and aortic stiffness index, and have demonstrated that patients with AGA exhibit a higher risk of atherosclerosis than control groups.[7] Nevertheless, the existing literature includes only a limited number of studies evaluating echocardiographic (ECHO) and electrocardiographic (ECG) parameters in patients with AGA. In this study, we aimed to investigate the presence of cardiovascular risk and potential conduction disorders in male patients diagnosed with AGA using both ECHO and ECG assessments.

2. Methods

This case-control study was conducted at Giresun Bulancak State Hospital between June 2023 and May 2024. A total of 40 male patients aged between 18 and 50 years with AGA, classified according to the Norwood–Hamilton scale, and 40 age- and sex-matched healthy controls without AGA were enrolled from individuals presenting for cardiology checkup evaluations. Individuals with diabetes mellitus, hypertension, thyroid disorders, hyperlipidemia, renal or hepatic failure, coronary artery disease, medication use, obesity, smoking habits, or electrolyte imbalance were excluded from the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participants. All the procedures in this study were performed in accordance with the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study protocol was received approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Giresun Training and Research Hospital (approval number: 05.06.2023/02; date: June 5, 2023). G Power 3.1 software was used to calculate the required sample size. The effect size was calculated as 0.75 in the power analysis calculated according to another study.[8] Based on a power of 90% and a 5% level of significance, we calculated that the total sample size required was 39.

In the patient group, disease duration, presence of vertex baldness, and degree of hair loss according to the Norwood–Hamilton scale were recorded by a dermatologist.[9] For both the patient and control groups, data were collected on age, sex, family history of AGA, presence of comorbidities, weight (kg), height (m), waist circumference (cm), body mass index (BMI)(kg/m2), and routine biochemical screening parameters including fasting blood glucose (mg/dL), complete blood count, lipid profile (total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol) (mg/dL), and insulin levels (µU/mL). Insulin resistance was assessed using the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) index, calculated from fasting serum insulin and glucose values using the following formula: HOMA-IR = [Fasting Glucose (mg/dL) × Fasting Insulin (µIU/mL)]/ 405. The systolic and diastolic blood pressures and heart rates of individuals in both groups were recorded. All participants in both groups underwent comprehensive cardiological examination by a specialist cardiologist and were evaluated using ECG and ECHO.

3. Electrocardiographic evaluation

Twelve-lead ECGs of all patients were recorded in the supine position following a 10-minute rest period (Nihon Kohden ECG–1250K; Tokyo, Japan). The ECGs were obtained at a paper speed of 50 mm/s and a voltage of 10 mV/mm. Three QRS complexes were recorded for each lead ion. Heart rate, P-wave durations, and QT interval were measured. P-wave durations and QT intervals were evaluated across all the 12 leads of the surface ECG. The beginning and end of the P-wave were identified at the points where it departed and returned to the isoelectric baseline. The maximum P-wave duration (Pmax), which represents the longest atrial conduction time, was determined as the longest Pmax measured in any of the 12 leads. The P-wave dispersion (Pdis) was measured by determining the range between the maximum and minimum P-wave durations (Pdis = Pmax−Pmin). QT intervals were determined by measuring the duration from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T-wave, returning to the baseline. If a U wave appeared after the T-wave, the lowest point of the curve between the T- and U-waves was considered. QT dispersion (QTdis) was defined as the difference between the maximum QT (QT max) and minimum QT (QT min) intervals recorded in any of the twelve ECG leads (QTdis = QTmax−QTmin). The corrected QT interval (QTc) was derived using Bazett formula: QTc = QT/√RR, in milliseconds. For optimal measurement accuracy, a combination of calipers and a magnifying glass was used. 4. Echocardiographic evaluation

Transthoracic ECHO evaluations of all individuals included in the study were performed using a Philips HD 11XE Medical Ultrasound System (Bothell) with an S4–2 transducer (2–4 MHz). None of the patients was under the influence of any medications that affected cardiac function at the time of the ECHO assessment. Following a 5-minute rest period, the patients were evaluated while in the left lateral decubitus position. While in this position, echocardiographic assessments – including B-mode, M-mode, color Doppler, pulsed-wave Doppler, continuous-wave Doppler, and tissue Doppler imaging – were conducted through parasternal long and short axes, and apical 4-, 2-, and 5-chamber views. Simultaneously, ECG recordings at a paper speed of 100 mm/s were used to capture flow velocities.

Interventricular septum thickness (IVST), posterior wall thickness, and left ventricular end-systolic (LVESD) and end-diastolic (LVEDD) diameters were assessed using the M-mode echocardiographic technique. Left atrial diameter was determined using the parasternal long-axis approach. Two- and 4-chamber imaging planes were used to calculate left ventricular volumes, and the left ventricular ejection fraction was derived accordingly. İn addition, the diameters of the aortic root and ascending aorta were measured and recorded. Figures 2 shows the ECHO measurements. Each parameter was measured across 3 consecutive cardiac cycles, and the mean value was calculated. All ECHO evaluations adhered to standard protocols recommended by the American Society of Echocardiography.[10]

5. Statistical analysis

In this study, which compared participants with and without AGA, descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range). The distribution of categorical variables was described using frequency and percentage values. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to evaluate whether continuous variables followed a normal distribution. For comparisons between 2 independent groups, the Mann–Whitney U-test was used for variables that did not conform to a normal distribution, whereas the Student t test was applied for normally distributed variables.

Spearman rho correlation analysis was used to examine the relationships between disease duration and stage and ECG parameters, lipid profiles, and metabolic data. Regarding this relationship, a coefficient value of r ≤.30 was deemed as weak, r >.30 to r ≤.70 was deemed as moderate, and r >.70 was deemed as strong.[11,12] IBM SPSS Statistics (version 21.0, Chicago) was used for data analysis, with statistical significance defined as a P-value of less than .05.

This study included 40 male patients with AGA and 40 male control subjects. The mean age of the patient group was 33.85 ± 9.44 years, whereas the mean age of the control group was 32.20 ± 9.92 years (P = .405). 7. Discussion

In this case-control study evaluating the ECG and ECHO parameters of patients with AGA, Pdis was significantly higher and QTcmin significantly lower in the AGA patient group than in the non-AGA control group (respectively P = .016, P = .031) As a result of our study investigating the relationship between cardiovascular disease and cardiac arrhythmias in men with AGA, significant ECG changes were detected indicating that the risk of arrhythmia may be increased in patients with AGA. These findings show indicate that significant changes can be detected in the cardiac conduction system of patients with AGA and that these may carry a potential risk for arrhythmia development.

AGA, is a common form of hair loss that is predominantly observed in men. AGA is associated with several clinical conditions, including benign prostatic hyperplasia, hypertension, and prostate cancer.[13] AGA has been implicated in a greater predisposition to coronary heart disease, as reported in earlier studies.[14] According to the results of our study, although no significant difference was found between the patient and control groups in terms of ECO measurements, the ECG findings were remarkable. Pdis, an ECG parameter, was significantly higher in the AGA group compared than in the controls. This may reflect increased susceptibility to conduction abnormalities in these patients. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate transmural dispersion of repolarization in patients with AGA. Pdis is an indicator of atrial repolarization heterogeneity, and its increase indicates the risk of developing atrial conduction disorders such as atrial fibrillation (AF).[15] Although there no individuals were diagnosed with AF in our study, the significantly longer Pdis duration in the AGA group suggests that these individuals may have subclinical atrial conduction abnormalities and may be prone to arrhythmia. There are also studies on different dermatological diseases regarding the increased risk of conduction disorders. Çetin et al assessed myocardial repolarization abnormalities in children with psoriasis, focusing on the cardiac conduction system. Significantly higher values of Pdis, QTcmax, QTcdis, Tp-e interval, and Tp-e/QTc max ratio were observed in the patients than in the control group. Moreover, QTcdis, a marker known to predict ventricular arrhythmias, was another significant finding in ECGs of children with psoriasis. Inflammation, a key element in the pathogenesis of psoriasis, has been proposed to be the main mechanism linking the disease to cardiac involvement and myocardial heterogeneity.[16]

Importantly, increases in Pdis and QTcdis associated with arrhythmia risk have also been observed in certain metabolic disorders, such as obesity.[17] However, in our study, neither the patient nor the control group met the criteria for obesity as all participants had a BMI below 30. In addition, there was no statistically significant difference between the patient and control groups in terms of metabolic parameters such as waist circumference, fasting glucose, insulin and HOMA-IR in addition to BMI. There was still a significant difference in Pdis and QTcmin parameters between individuals in both groups who had similar profiles in terms of metabolic syndrome risk factors. In this respect, it indicates that the risk of conduction disorders may be increased in patients with AGA, independent of the risk of metabolic syndrome. These findings suggest that Pdis may be an arrhythmogenic marker independent of metabolic syndrome in patients with AGA.

Another study assessing Pdis in individuals with lichen planus (LP), a chronic inflammatory disorder, demonstrated significantly elevated Pdis values in the LP group compared to healthy controls. This significant difference may serve as a useful indicator for early detection of subclinical cardiac involvement in patients with LP. Based on these findings, the authors recommended that LP patients be evaluated from a cardiovascular perspective owing to their potentially increased susceptibility to AF.[8] However, a critical commentary on this literature noted that elevated Pdis can also be observed in various other conditions, such as hypertension, obesity, diabetes, thyroid disorders, and coronary artery disease.[18] From this perspective, we believe that the inclusion of male participants without comorbidities in both the patient and control groups of our study contributed to a more objective and unbiased assessment. Similarly, Namazi et al reported significantly higher Pdis and Pmax values in patients with pemphigus than those in healthy controls. These results suggest an increased predisposition to cardiovascular disease and AF among individuals with pemphigus.[19] Our study also has similar results with the detection of increased Pdis values, similar to studies conducted in patients with psoriasis, LP, and pemphigus.

İncreased Pdis level has been identified as an independent predictor of AF, which represents the most prevalent sustained arrhythmia in the general population. Moreover, elevated Pdis levels have been shown to contribute to increased morbidity and mortality associated with cardiovascular diseases and negatively impact quality of life.[20] Based on these findings, Batra et al investigated coronary artery disease risk in males diagnosed with AGA by utilizing ECG and ECHO. They did not detect any early signs of coronary artery disease in these patients; however, the lack of a control group in their study is considered a major limitation.[21] Similarly, in a study by Doğramaci et al, although a higher prevalence of diastolic dysfunction and mild mitral regurgitation was observed in the AGA group than in controls via ECHO, the differences were not statistically significant. Additionally, no significant differences were detected in ECG findings between the patient and control groups.[22] Another study in adult men recently diagnosed with essential hypertension reported that more severe, early-onset, and long-standing AGA was associated with compromised coronary microcirculation, increased aortic stiffness, and greater carotid atherosclerosis. A greater cardiovascular risk was identified particularly among hypertensive patients with early-onset and severe AGA.[23] In contrast, our study did not detect any statistically significant differences in systolic and diastolic blood pressure measurements between the AGA and control groups with respect to hypertension. In addition, no statistically significant difference was found in terms of blood cholesterol levels, which are other cardiovascular risk factors, between the patient and control groups. In summary, unlike previous studies conducted on AGA, our study compared 2 groups with similar profiles in terms of both metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease risk factors. Independent of these risk factors, a significant statistical difference was found in the ECG parameters Pdis and QTcmin, which are arrhythmia predictors. In fact, it has been shown that in addition to an increased risk of arrhythmia, increased Pdis is associated with the recurrence of AF after cardioversion and the severity of coronary artery diseases.[20–24] Furthermore, in a study involving patients with cryptogenic stroke who were monitored using an implantable loop recorder, a Pdis of 40 ms was detected as the sole independent predictor of subsequent AF.[25] In this study, the average Pdis time in the AGA group was measured as 41.23 ms, which is above a clinically significant threshold value.

However, it is also noteworthy that QTcmin duration differed significantly in the AGA group. Alterations in the QTc interval reflect the stability of cardiac electrical activity and the homogeneity of ventricular repolarization. A previous review suggested that higher QT dispersion may be associated with myocardial fibrosis, particularly in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.[26] Another study showed that patients with high risk of acute ischemic stroke had higher QTcmin values than low-risk patients.[27] On the other hand, a markedly shortened QTc interval may indicate an increased risk for ventricular arrhythmias and potentially life-threatening conditions.[28] In line with these findings, our study demonstrated a reduced QTcmin value in the patient group. This finding suggests that AGA may be associated with electrical instability not only at the atrial level but also at the ventricular level.

Previous epidemiological studies have shown that the prevalence of AGA increases with age, particularly among White populations.[29] In the current study, we identified a moderate positive correlation between Pdis and both the disease duration and clinical stage. This relationship indicates that arrhythmic risk increases as AGA advances. Importantly, Pdis values were significantly elevated even though none of the participants had traditional pro-arrhythmic comorbidities such as hypertension. Furthermore, patients with AGA exhibited markedly higher Pdis and QTcmin measurements, – established ECG markers of arrhythmia – independent of other cardiovascular risk factors, and these abnormalities intensified in parallel with the disease stage.

Although our study is the first study in the current literature to detect an increased risk of cardiac conduction disturbances in patients with AGA, independent of the metabolic syndrome and cardiovascular disease risk identified in previous studies, it has some limitations. These include the limited sample size, the presence of observer error due to manual ECG measurements, and inability to establish a causal relationship because of the cross-sectional design of the study. Although potential confounding variables such as BMI, lipid levels, and glucose metabolism were similar between groups, multivariate regression analysis could not be performed due to insufficient number of patients. Additionally, the lack of long-term arrhythmia follow-up poses a limitation for future inferences. Further research involving larger cohorts and extended follow-up periods is warranted to confirm and build upon these results.

8. Conclusion

This study concluded that male patients with AGA exhibited an increased risk of developing arrhythmias, as indicated by elevated Pdis and reduced QTcmin values. Collectively, these findings indicate that AGA may be associated with alterations in the cardiac conduction system, potentially predisposing patients to conduction abnormalities. In this context, ECG may serve as a useful screening tool for detecting conduction abnormalities in men with AGA. We suggest that patients with AGA may benefit from closer cardiac monitoring and cardiology consultations, especially with regard to arrhythmias and coronary artery disease.
