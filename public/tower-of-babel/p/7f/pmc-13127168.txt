Antimicrobial resistance in Palestine: Emerging hotspots and trends from a 4-year hospital-based study
Authors: Rana Khanafsa, Mamoun AT Ibaideya, Amirah Azzeri, Nurul Azmawati Mohamed, Muhammad Zarul Hanifah Md Zoqratt, Muhammad Azamuddeen Mohammad Nasir, Mohd Nasharudin Razak, Liyana Azmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Resistant isolates rose by 5.8% at the West Bank’s largest regional hospital.•Carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) increased by 10.2% (2020-2023), with the intensive care unit (ICU) identified as its primary hotspot for CRE infection.•Emergency department, ICU, and surgical unit were identified as critical hubs for resistant isolates.•COVID-19 syndemic drove a surge of co-infection with carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumanii in 2022.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a significant global public health concern, resulting in approximately 1.27 million fatalities in 2019 and projected to cause up to 10 million deaths yearly by 2050 if not adequately addressed [1,2]. In low-resource environments, the AMR burden is exacerbated and underreported due to financial restrictions, ongoing political instability, and deficiencies in surveillance systems [3,4].

Palestine is placed seventh among the 21 countries in the Middle East and North Africa region in terms of AMR-related mortality [5]. Although AMR is recognized as a national health threat, existing surveillance data on resistance trends, pathogen distribution, and hospital-level burden remain fragmented and insufficient [6]. Although previous reports document rising resistance rates, particularly, among extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)–producing Escherichia coli (ESBL-EC) [7], methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) [8], carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales (CRE) [9], and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CR-AB) [10], they fall short of providing a longitudinal analysis of the prevalence and ward-specific AMR burden [11]. Most of these studies are limited in geographical scope, often focusing on a single institution or city, such as Nablus or Hebron [3,12], and, thus, do not reflect the broader epidemiologic landscape across the West Bank of Palestine.

To address this critical gap, this study examines the prevalence and distribution of AMR among clinical bacterial isolates collected from the Palestine Medical Complex (PMC), a tertiary care referral hospital which serves as the primary tertiary care and referral institution for the West Bank, catering to patients from all 11 governorates (Figure 1).Figure 1Map showing 11 governates of West Bank. The PMC consists of five interconnected hospitals, including Ramallah General Hospital, Alsheikh Zayed Hospital, the National Centre for Blood Diseases (Hippocrates), Bahrain Paediatrics Hospital, and Kuwaiti Specialised Surgery Hospital. This integrated facility offers an extensive array of specialized services, encompassing internal medicine, surgery, pediatrics, hematology, obstetrics and gynecology, nephrology, infectious disease management, critical care, and emergency medicine, facilitating coordinated and comprehensive medical services throughout the region.

This study aims to provide an evaluation of resistance patterns categorized by organism, resistance type, hospital ward or patient location, and patient demographics. Findings from this study will be used to provide feedback for existing national AMR mitigation efforts and support the implementation of targeted infection control and stewardship policies in Palestine.

Materials and methods

Bacterial isolation

All patient samples were processed using standard microbiological procedures, following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). Bacterial identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing (AST) were performed using the automated VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France), following the guidelines of the CLSI Standard reference strains, including E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. aureus ATCC 25923, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603, and A. baumannii ATCC 19606.

Data set collection

Our data set comprised retrospective positive bacterial isolation reports from patients admitted to PMC for at least 24 hours under medical care and required to submit samples for diagnosis (herewith referred to as in-patients) to the PMC Microbiology laboratory from January 1, 2020 to December 31, 2023. However, the precise timing of specimen collection relative to the admission was not documented in the data set; therefore, all isolates could not be formally classified as hospital- or community-acquired using the standard >48-hour criteria; ergo, all isolates in this study represent clinical infections identified in hospitalized patients. Demographic data of in-patients (age and gender) were collected, with the ID of patients anonymized.

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the State of Palestine, Ministry of Health, Education in Health and Scientific Research Unit (162/357/2025).

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 was used for data analysis. Correlation between rates of infection by resistant bacterial isolates with gender and age was done using a chi-square analysis. Python’s Matplotlib library (version 1.4.3, released in 2015) was used to create graphs showing ward-specific and time trend analyses.

Data availability and ethical approval

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All samples were anonymized before analysis. Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s) in accordance with the Health Education and Scientific Research Unit from PMC and Palestinian Ministry of Health (Ref: 162/1357/2025). All data generated or analyzed during this study are included in this article and its supplementary files.

Collectively, our patients consisted of 6501 males (42.9%) and 8646 females (57.1%). Patients were classified based on the World Health Organization (WHO) age categorization [2] (Table 1). Temporal profiling of resistant isolates

Resistance profiles were classified according to the 2024 WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogens List organisms, consisting of 15 antibiotic-resistant organisms stratified into critical, high, and medium groups [2]. Cumulatively, the prevalence of all resistant isolates was observed to increase from 31.9% (2020) to 37.7% (2023).

When observed at species level (Table 3), the ESBL-EC accounts for the majority dominating resistant isolates, with 37-43% of annual E. coli isolates. This was followed by ESBL K. pneumoniae (ESBL-KP) and MRSA, accounting for 10-19%, respectively. CRE and CR-AB were observed to increase consistently from 2020 to 2023. Specifically for CR-AB, infections peaked at 9.3% in 2020—suggestive of a respiratory outbreak during the COVID-19 peak period before declining to 4.4% in 2023. Other isolates demonstrated fluctuating isolating trends. Of 15,147 patients, 4003 were identified to be infected with resistant isolates, with 1756 (43.9%) consisting of males and 2247 (56.1%) females. No significant association exists for patient gender (χ² = 1.995, P = 0.158). However, age was significantly associated with AMR infection (χ² = 138.126, P <0.0001). When stratified into groups of children (age 0-12 years), adults (age 12-60 years), and the elderly individuals (age >60 years), the adult group represented the largest proportion of resistant infections, suggestive of a higher exposure or susceptibility within this group.

Proportion of resistant isolates by hospital wards

When the top five resistant isolates were mapped to their respective wards (Figure 2), the distribution of absolute number vs percentage of resistance varied substantially. The absolute burden of all five isolates was highest in the emergency department (ED) (n = 1312), intensive care unit (ICU) (n = 1222), reflecting their high patient turnover and acuity; followed by surgical (n = 713), medical (n = 481), and pediatric wards (n = 479). In contrast, when resistance was expressed as the proportion of resistant isolates across all five organisms, the highest normalized rates were observed from orthopedic (43.5%), surgical (41%), and COVID-19 wards (40.6%); followed by ICU (35.7%), medical (35.2%), and coronary care units (31.1%). This normalization allowed the comparison of resistance pressure across wards regardless of the absolute number of isolates. Discussion

Our study provides a comprehensive description of AMR trends in a referral hospital covering 11 governates in the West Bank. Demographically, women accounted for a higher proportion of resistant isolates; however, no significant gender association was observed. This contrasts with global evidence, which suggests that men are more prone to resistant infections [2,13]. However, age played a much more impactful role, with older and middle-aged adults carrying the highest burden of resistance. This was consistent with other studies [14] showing cumulative exposure to antimicrobials and comorbidities.

Across pathogens, CRE emerged as the most concerning threat, with percentages nearly doubling over the study period (7.5-17.7%). Our data mirrors global reports of increasing prevalence of CREs [15] as a critical priority pathogen with limited therapeutic options.

There was also the transient surge of CR-AB in 2022, likely linked to ICU strains during the COVID-19 pandemic, but then waned in 2023, possibly reflecting the improved infection control measures and/or vaccination taking place. Similarly, national surveillance from Oman confirms this trend, identifying ventilator-dependent patients in the ICU as a primary source of CR-AB outbreaks during the same period [16].

The distribution of resistant isolates across hospital wards revealed important contrasts between absolute isolate counts and relative resistance. The ED and ICU recorded the highest numbers of CRE and CR-AB, reinforcing global evidence that critical care units are hotspots for carbapenem-resistant infections [17]. Furthermore, these isolations reflect severity of illness, invasive interventions, and frequent empirical antibiotic use [[18], [19], [20]]. In contrast, when resistance was normalized by total isolates, the highest proportions were observed from surgical, orthopedic, and COVID-19 wards. This indicates that resistant pressure might be proportioned to several activities, such as surgical prophylaxis [21], device use [22], or concentrated antibiotic exposures [23].

Our study, however, is limited by the inability to distinguish hospital-acquired from community-acquired infections because the specimen collection time stamps relative to admission were not available. Future studies should prospectively capture this information to enable formal nosocomial infection classification. Such classification between nosocomial and community-acquired infection would further support the design of infection control strategies and contain resistant infections. Our findings provide a critical foundation for more regionally tailored, ward-specific, and demographically targeted antimicrobial policies, thus reinforcing the urgency of coordinated national surveillance and stewardship initiatives.

Conclusion

The findings of this study present a comprehensive assessment of AMR trends at a tertiary care hospital in Palestine over 4 years (2020-2023). The high prevalence of ESBL-EC and ESBL-KP; the predominance of E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and S. aureus; and the rising rates of carbapenem resistance in Enterobacterales and A. baumannii highlight the urgent need for strengthened antimicrobial stewardship and infection control measures. Due to the fragility of these settings, AMR was disproportionately concentrated in high-risk areas, such as the ED, ICU, and surgical unit. Further demonstrating the demographic impact on AMR burden are statistically significant correlations between resistance categories and patient age and gender. Seasonal variations and post-pandemic ICU strain lead to temporal swings, underscoring the need for ongoing microbiological surveillance and prompt response plans. Although the AMR trends shown here are similar to those worldwide, unique local issues require specialized, evidence-based solutions supported by national public health efforts. Our longitudinal data can potentially serve as a blueprint for the Palestinian Ministry of Health to implement ward-specific stewardship, particularly targeting CRE in high-turnover hospital wards.
