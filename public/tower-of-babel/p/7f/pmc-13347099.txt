Rethinking routine technical choices in minor surgery: a retrospective cohort study of implantable venous port catheter placement
Authors: Omer Yavuz, Mehlika Iscan, Eren Erdogdu, Muhammet Kertmen, Ali Yeginsu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate whether two commonly overlooked technical choices, preoperative skin marking and electrocautery use, independently affect procedural success and long-term outcomes, using implantable venous port catheter placement as a model procedure within minor surgery.MethodsThis retrospective single-center study analyzed 1,122 consecutive port catheter placements, used as a model minor surgical procedure, performed by a single surgeon between June 2022 and September 2025. Preoperative skin marking and electrocautery use were recorded as primary technical variables. The primary outcome was port catheter revision requiring surgical intervention. Learning curve effects were assessed using cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis. Multivariable logistic regression and Kaplan-Meier analyses were performed to identify independent predictors.ResultsOmission of preoperative skin marking was the sole independent predictor of procedural failure in port catheter placement, increasing failure risk 6.07-fold (odds ratio [OR], 6.07; 95% confidence interval [CI], 2.63–14.01; P < 0.001). Port catheter revision occurred in 4.2% of patients. Electrocautery use was a strong independent predictor of revision (OR, 8.89; 95% CI, 1.77–44.70; P = 0.008) and was associated with significantly lower revision-free survival (hazard ratio, 3.52; P = 0.002). CUSUM analysis demonstrated that the reduction in port catheter revisions after electrocautery discontinuation occurred independently of the overall learning curve.ConclusionPreoperative skin marking improves procedural accuracy, while avoidance of electrocautery markedly reduces revision risk, independent of surgical experience, highlighting the critical impact of routine technical decisions in port catheter placement as a model of minor surgical procedures.

INTRODUCTION

Although the concepts of minor and major surgery have been discussed since the early 20th century, clear and universally accepted boundaries between them remain undefined [1]. Despite this lack of a clear definition, minor surgical procedures constitute one of the most common and highly standardized areas of surgical practice. These procedures are often regarded as low risk, and the technical details involved are routinely repeated without being critically questioned [23]. However, such technical choices, particularly those that involve direct interaction with tissue, may have effects that extend beyond the immediate postoperative period.

In minor surgical procedures, technical choices are often shaped by considerations such as speed, practicality, and technical convenience. In this context, electrocautery has become a widely used tool in daily surgical practice because of its ability to provide intraoperative hemostasis and shorten procedure time. Particularly in minor interventions, the use of electrocautery is frequently adopted as a default technical approach and applied without the need for additional evaluation.

Similarly, preoperative skin marking is another technical detail that is often neglected in minor surgical procedures or varies according to the surgeon’s personal practice. Although it is frequently regarded as a simple preparatory step, it may play a role in clarifying anatomical orientation, standardizing the entry site, and facilitating accurate planning of the surgical field.

In contrast, the clinical effects of routine technical choices used in minor surgical procedures have not been systematically examined in the literature. Specifically, there is a lack of large-scale clinical studies evaluating whether routine technical choices in minor surgical procedures independently influence both immediate procedural success and long-term revision outcomes, particularly when analyzed in relation to the surgical learning curve. One of the main reasons for this is that such procedures are generally considered low risk and technical details are often regarded as secondary.

Adverse effects related to electrocautery use tend to manifest late in the postoperative period and are not easily associated with immediate procedural success [3]. In contrast, preoperative skin marking is primarily used to ensure intraoperative accuracy and correct-site surgery [456].

In this context, procedures that are routinely classified as minor yet allow evaluation of both immediate technical success and long-term follow-up provide a unique opportunity to assess the early and late consequences of routine technical choices used in daily practice. Implantable venous access port catheter placement, a standardized and widely performed intervention, represents a reproducible model procedure in which both technical accuracy and long-term device-related outcomes can be reliably assessed.

Ultrasound guidance is well established as the preferred approach under optimal conditions. Although current guidelines recommend performing central venous access under ultrasound guidance in ideal conditions [78], a substantial proportion of daily surgical practice is carried out under resource-limited settings, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In centers with limited institutional resources, strict routine adherence to all guideline-recommended standards may not always be feasible. Under such circumstances, the development of feasible and reproducible alternative strategies that maintain patient safety becomes increasingly important.

The cases evaluated in the present study were performed in a clinical environment where routine ultrasound guidance was not consistently available. Therefore, preoperative skin marking was adopted as a simple, low-cost, and easily applicable strategy aimed at improving procedural success while preserving patient safety. Through this approach, standardization of the entry site, clarification of anatomical orientation, and improved procedural predictability were targeted.

METHODS

Ethics statement

The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (approval date: September 29, 2025; approval number: KAEK/24.09.2025.292). Informed consent was obtained from all patients prior to the procedure.

Study design and population

This study is based on a retrospective analysis of 1,122 consecutive port catheter placement procedures performed at a single center by a single surgeon between June 2022 and September 2025. All port catheter placement procedures performed in adult patients during this period were included. Of these procedures, 1,095 were completed successfully, while 27 were classified as unsuccessful.

Procedure and technical variables

Port catheter placement procedures were performed using a standard surgical approach. Electrocautery use and preoperative skin marking were defined as the primary technical variables, and their presence or absence was recorded for each procedure.

The surgical technique underwent 2 major refinements during the study period to enhance procedural safety and success. First, preoperative skin marking was standardized on January 16, 2024, to ensure optimal pocket positioning and to facilitate successful initial venous puncture of the jugular or subclavian veins. This practice involved marking the planned puncture sites, the pocket location, and the projection of the cavoatrial junction, which also aided in preoperative estimation of the required catheter length, as shown in Fig. 1. Second, the use of electrocautery during dissection was discontinued on November 11, 2024, in favor of cold dissection. In this context, cold dissection was defined as the creation of the port pocket by separating the skin and subcutaneous adipose tissue from the pectoralis major fascia without the use of electrocautery. Following standard skin incision with a scalpel, pocket dissection was performed using blunt and sharp techniques with scissors, clamps, and digital dissection, while electrocautery was deliberately avoided during this step. Hemostasis during pocket formation was achieved primarily by brief manual compression; in the minority of cases where this was insufficient, a single absorbable suture was placed for targeted bleeding control. Electrocautery was not used at any stage of the procedure, as its use had previously been confined to the dissection phase and was fully abandoned with the adoption of cold dissection. The adoption of this approach represented a technical modification, and this transition was prompted by successful outcomes in consecutive cases where electrocautery was contraindicated due to permanent cardiac pacemakers. The observation of adequate hemostasis in these initial cases led to the formal adoption of cold dissection as the standard institutional protocol to minimize potential collateral thermal injury. These changes were evaluated as part of a continuous quality improvement process.

The case sequence was numbered consecutively according to the chronological order of the procedures. Port catheters were classified according to type. Follow-up duration (dwell time) was calculated as the interval from catheter placement to the date of last follow-up, revision, or catheter removal.

Demographic and clinical data were recorded for all patients. Age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and comorbidities (including organ failure and steroid use) were evaluated. Medication use and oncologic treatments, including anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) therapy, were included in the analysis. The side of catheter placement and the vein used (jugular or subclavian) were also recorded as procedural variables.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of the study was port catheter revision. Revision was defined as any condition requiring a surgical intervention due to a complication that impaired the function or integrity of the port catheter. Secondary outcomes included procedural success and revision-free survival. Procedural success was defined as the technical placement of the port catheter with early postoperative usability. Revision-free survival was calculated as the interval from catheter placement to the first requirement for revision.

Learning curve analysis

The learning curve was evaluated using the cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis. The analysis was initially performed as a single time series across all consecutive cases. Subsequently, to avoid confounding between the learning curve and time-dependent changes in technical practice, the CUSUM analysis was reevaluated across 2 consecutive periods. Procedure failure and port catheter revision events were used as the primary outcome measures in the CUSUM analysis. To account for potential time-dependent confounding arising from protocol modifications implemented during the study period, the case sequence was explicitly incorporated into the learning curve analysis to distinguish learning effects from time-related procedural modifications.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as counts and percentages. Group comparisons were performed using the Student t-test or the Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables.

Factors associated with port catheter revision and procedural failure were assessed using univariable and multivariable binary logistic regression analyses. Odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Model diagnostics were performed for the logistic regression analyses. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values, and overall model fit was evaluated with the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. All VIF values were within acceptable limits, and the goodness-of-fit results indicated adequate model fit, with no major assumption violations detected. Revision-free survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared with the log-rank test.

Learning curve analysis was performed using the CUSUM methodology based on the case sequence. All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics software, version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Two-sided P-values of less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. Time-dependent changes in technical practice were considered in multivariable models to reduce confounding related to chronological case progression.

RESULTS

Electrocautery use and port revision outcomes

Characteristics of the patients

A total of 1,095 patients were included in the study, and baseline demographic, clinical, and malignancy characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Clinical characteristics

Electrocautery was used during port placement in 684 patients (62.5%), while 411 (37.5%) underwent the procedure without electrocautery. Regarding port height, the most frequently used devices had a height of 10.6 mm (n = 640, 58.4%), followed by 12.2 mm ports (n = 267, 24.4%), 13.2 mm ports (n = 129, 11.8%), and 11.6 mm ports (n = 59, 5.4%) (Table 2).

Additional systemic therapies were administered in 266 patients (24.3%), with anti-VEGF therapy being the most frequently used agent (Table 2).

Follow-up duration

Median catheter dwell time and follow-up duration are summarized in Table 2.

Overall revision rate

Port catheter revision was required in 46 patients (4.2%), while 1,049 (95.8%) remained revision-free during follow-up. Revision-free survival analysis demonstrated a significantly lower revision-free survival in patients in whom electrocautery was used, with a hazard ratio (HR) of 3.52 (95% CI, 1.82–6.83; P = 0.002) (Fig. 2).

Factors associated with port revision

There were no significant differences between patients with and without port catheter revision in terms of age, sex, BMI, or comorbidities (Table 3). Technical and treatment-related variables were further compared according to port catheter revision status (Table 4). Electrocautery use was significantly more frequent among patients who required port catheter revision. Port height also differed significantly between groups, with a higher proportion of thicker port types observed in the revision group. Anti-VEGF therapy was also significantly more common among patients who underwent revision. In contrast, the use of fluoropyrimidine-based chemotherapy did not differ significantly between patients with and without port catheter revision.

Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the potential risk factors influencing port catheter revision and to analyze the individual effects of the variables (Table 5). According to the univariate analysis results, the use of electrocautery was identified as the most significant risk factor, increasing the risk of revision nearly 14-fold (odds ratio [OR], 14.06; 95% CI, 3.39–58.31; P < 0.001). Regarding port heights, compared to the reference (12.2 mm), the use of 13.2 mm ports was associated with a significant increase in risk (OR, 6.63; 95% CI, 3.58–12.28; P < 0.001), whereas the use of 10.6 mm ports was found to significantly reduce the likelihood of revision (OR, 0.24; 95% CI, 0.12–0.46; P < 0.001). When treatment-related factors were examined, the use of anti-VEGF as an adjunctive therapy was associated with a higher risk of revision (OR, 12.03; 95% CI, 1.56–92.88; P = 0.02). Regarding treatment-related outcomes, the development of adverse events (thrombosis, infection, bleeding, or malposition) significantly increased the requirement for revision (OR, 4.81; 95% CI, 1.34–17.24; P = 0.02). Other clinical outcome variables did not show a significant association with the risk of revision. Furthermore, continuous variables significantly influenced the outcome; each additional day of catheter dwell time slightly increased the risk of revision (OR, 1.002; 95% CI, 1.001–1.003; P = 0.001). Conversely, an increase in the patient sequence, reflecting the surgical learning curve, was associated with a significant reduction in the likelihood of revision (OR, 0.998; 95% CI, 0.997–0.999; P = 0.001).

To determine the independent predictors of port catheter revision, a multivariate binary logistic regression model was performed. Factors that demonstrated significant or borderline associations in the univariate analysis were included in the model (Table 6). In the multivariate analysis, electrocautery use was identified as a strong independent predictor of port catheter revision. The likelihood of revision was approximately 8.9 times higher in patients where electrocautery was utilized compared to those where it was not (OR, 8.89; 95% CI, 1.77–44.70; P = 0.008). Similarly, adjunctive anti-VEGF therapy remained a significant independent risk factor, associated with a nearly 11.6-fold increase in the risk of revision (OR, 11.62; 95% CI, 1.42–95.24; P = 0.02). Other variables that showed significance in the univariate analysis, such as port height, patient sequence, and catheter dwell time, did not maintain independent statistical significance in the multivariate model. The impact of electrocautery use on revision-free survival was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis and the log-rank test. HRs were calculated to determine the relative risk of revision over time (Table 7). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly lower revision-free survival in the electrocautery group (HR, 3.52; 95% CI, 1.82–6.83; P = 0.002) (Fig. 2).

Preoperative skin marking and immediate procedural outcomes

The technical success of port catheter placement and the impact of preoperative skin marking were evaluated in a cohort of 1,122 patients. The overall technical success rate was 97.6% (n = 1,095), while procedural failure occurred in 2.4% (n = 27) of the cases (Table 8).

No statistically significant differences were observed between the successful and failed procedure groups regarding age (60.8 ± 10.4 years vs. 62.8 ± 9.7 years, P = 0.37), BMI (25.98 ± 4.71 kg/m2 vs. 25.26 ± 5.59 kg/m2, P = 0.43), or sex distribution (P = 0.19). In contrast, a highly significant association was identified between preoperative skin marking and procedural success (P < 0.001). Skin marking was omitted in 70% (n = 19) of the technical failure cases, whereas it was performed in 72% (n = 787) of the successful procedures (Table 9).

In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, omitting preoperative skin marking was identified as the sole independent predictor of procedural failure, increasing the risk by 6.07-fold (OR, 6.07; 95% CI, 2.63–14.01; P < 0.001).

Learning curve analysis and the impact of surgical experience on outcomes

The learning curve was assessed using CUSUM analysis for technical success and revision-free survival in the 1,122-patient cohort. The CUSUM curve for technical success reached its inflection point at the 409th case. For revision-free survival, the milestone was identified at the 560th case (Fig. 3).

Electrocautery use was discontinued on November 11, 2024. To distinguish the impact of this technical change from general surgical experience, separate CUSUM analyses were conducted for the periods before and after this date. Following the cessation of electrocautery, a further decline in the cumulative sum of revisions was observed in the subgroup analysis, which occurred independently of the overall 560-case stabilization point (Fig. 4).

DISCUSSION

The primary finding of this study is that preoperative skin marking and the avoidance of electrocautery are independently associated with success in port catheter placement. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that electrocautery use was associated with an 8.89-fold increase in the revision rate, while preoperative skin marking led to a 6.07-fold increase in procedural success. Furthermore, anti-VEGF therapy was identified as a significant risk factor, increasing the revision rate by 11.62-fold (Table 6).

A biologically plausible explanation for the observed interaction between electrocautery use and anti-VEGF therapy may relate to their combined effects on local tissue injury and impaired wound healing. Electrocautery is known to induce localized thermal damage, resulting in microvascular endothelial injury, tissue ischemia, and delayed reparative responses within the surgical field [3]. Anti-VEGF agents, by inhibiting angiogenesis and endothelial regeneration, further compromise neovascularization and collagen deposition that are essential for durable wound healing and tissue integration, as previously described in the literature on VEGF pathway biology [9]. When electrocautery-related thermal injury occurs in the setting of VEGF pathway inhibition, the capacity for microvascular recovery and soft-tissue remodeling around the port pocket may be substantially reduced. This combined biological vulnerability may predispose patients to delayed wound complications, tissue breakdown, and subsequent port-related revisions. Although causality cannot be established in this retrospective analysis, the convergence of these mechanisms provides a coherent biological framework that may partially explain the markedly increased revision risk observed in patients exposed to both electrocautery and anti-VEGF therapy.

To determine whether these outcomes were merely a function of surgical experience, CUSUM analysis was initially performed to map the overall learning curve (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the impact of technical variables on this curve was investigated by re-running the CUSUM analysis for the periods before and after their implementation (Fig. 4). Specifically, success-based CUSUM analysis suggested a typical learning curve prior to the adoption of skin marking; however, the curve remained stable following its implementation (Fig. 4A, B). A similar trend was observed in the revision-based CUSUM analysis (Fig. 4C, D).

In summary, while CUSUM analysis suggests that the surgeon developed increased proficiency over time, the technical choices appeared to exert a distinct and independent effect on the outcomes. The reduction in late-term revisions was not solely attributable to surgical experience but was consistently observed following the abandonment of electrocautery. Similarly, skin marking was associated with enhanced procedural accuracy across different stages of the learning curve, suggesting a technical advantage beyond experience alone. Taken together, these findings support the importance of electrocautery avoidance and preoperative skin marking as key technical considerations for reducing complications and improving procedural success.

Preoperative skin marking is traditionally recognized in the surgical literature as a safety protocol aimed at preventing wrong-site surgery, as part of the Universal Protocol [456]. However, our findings suggest that marking may serve not only as a safety measure but also as an important planning tool that facilitates anatomical orientation of the target vein, which is central to successful venous puncture, appropriate port pocket placement, and incisional accuracy. In the context of vascular access surgery, such as arteriovenous fistula creation, preoperative mapping has been shown to improve technical success and access longevity [10]. Consistent with this concept, our findings showed that skin marking was associated with improved localization of the target vein, a key step in the procedure. This improved localization was associated with higher procedural success, with multivariate analysis demonstrating a 6.07-fold increase when marking was utilized. Notably, the stabilization of the learning curve observed after the implementation of skin marking in the CUSUM analysis suggests that this technical step may reduce variability related to experience, thereby contributing to procedural consistency across different stages of surgical practice.

Port catheter series presented by Aziret et al. [11] and Keum et al. [12] in the literature regard the use of electrocautery as a routine component of standard surgical practice for both tissue dissection and hemostasis. While these studies reported successful outcomes, the independent impact of electrocautery on revision rates or its interaction with the surgeon’s learning curve was not specifically analyzed. In contrast, our study adds to the existing literature by showing that electrocautery use was independently associated with a significantly higher risk of revision (OR, 8.89) in multivariate analysis.

The foundational scientific evidence supporting our clinical observations has been derived from animal models by Rappaport et al. [13], who demonstrated that electrocautery significantly reduces the tensile strength of healing wounds and leads to more extensive tissue necrosis compared to a scalpel. This loss of tensile strength is particularly relevant in port surgery, where the subcutaneous tissue must withstand constant mechanical pressure exerted by the implant. This structural weakening of the tissue may represent an important mechanical contributor to the higher revision rates observed in the electrocautery group in our study.

The choice between a scalpel and electrocautery for skin incision remains controversial in surgical practice. Numerous studies and meta-analyses have reported no significant differences between the 2 methods in terms of wound infection, healing time, postoperative pain, or cosmetic outcomes [14151617]. However, the majority of these studies focus on general surgical incisions and do not address procedures involving permanent implants such as port catheters, where a permanent pocket is formed, and the use of electrocautery during pocket formation may have distinct implications. In contrast to this general consensus, our findings suggest that electrocautery-assisted dissection is associated with higher revision rates. This association may be related to the lateral thermal damage induced by electrocautery, which can disrupt the microcirculation of the thin subcutaneous tissue within the port pocket. When combined with the mechanical stress exerted by the implant, this disruption may predispose the tissue to aseptic necrosis.

The biochemical evidence suggests that the damage caused by electrocautery is not limited to thermal burns but may also trigger a significant pro-inflammatory response. Özdoğan et al. [18] and Yilmaz et al. [19] reported this association by comparing electrocautery and scalpel dissections in mastectomy flap models, reporting significantly elevated levels of tumor necrosis factor-alpha and interleukin-6 in the wound fluid of the electrocautery groups. These studies also found an association between electrocautery use and higher rates of flap necrosis and wound complications. This increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines may reflect an aggravated inflammatory reaction and a higher potential for tissue damage. Furthermore, the deleterious effects of electrocautery have been associated with relative ischemia caused by the thrombosis of subdermal vessels and thermal damage to subcutaneous fat [1819]. Since the subcutaneous pocket created for port catheter placement is biologically analogous to a flap model, the high inflammatory load and ischemic environment induced by electrocautery, especially when combined with the mechanical stress of the implant, may provide a biological explanation for the increased revision rates observed in our study.

The mechanical stress exerted by the implant may represent an important factor in pocket complications. Yavuz and Iscan [20] reported that increased port catheter thickness is associated with a higher risk of complications, suggesting a millimeter threshold for tissue tolerance. When the thermal injury from electrocautery is combined with the increased mechanical pressure of a high-profile port, the risk of aseptic necrosis and subsequent revision may be substantially increased.

The contemporary emphasis on the preservation of tissue integrity has gained a new perspective with the work of Arredondo Montero [21] in 2024, which recommended avoiding even digital dissection during the creation of the port pocket. In a clinical context where even blunt digital dissection is viewed as a potential risk to tissue preservation, particularly in pediatric populations, the uncontrolled thermal injury and lateral tissue destruction inherent to electrocautery may represent a significantly greater threat. These findings suggest that our strategy of entirely eschewing electrocautery is consistent with the micro-traumatic principles of modern surgical practice.

Our transition from electrocautery to scalpel was initially prompted by the successful outcomes observed in consecutive cases where electrocautery was contraindicated due to permanent cardiac pacemakers. The clinical success of this mandatory technical shift subsequently led to the broader abandonment of electrocautery in our practice. This clinical observation is supported by Eginli et al. [22], who reported that electrosurgical methods cause significantly more collateral tissue damage than scalpel use, which may contribute to delayed healing and potential risks of electromagnetic interference. The 8.89-fold increase in revision rates associated with electrocautery in our multivariate analysis is consistent with both this clinical experience and the biological mechanisms of collateral damage and delayed wound healing described in the literature.

The pivotal role of thermal control in reducing complication rates has been further supported by Dyrbuś et al. [23] in their recent study on cardiac implantable electronic device procedures. The authors reported that the use of low-temperature electrocautery resulted in significantly lower rates of hematoma and tissue damage compared to conventional high-temperature systems. This finding suggests that minimizing the thermal load on the surgical site may contribute to improved clinical outcomes. Our findings build upon this concept, suggesting that rather than merely reducing the thermal impact, the complete elimination of thermal injury through cold dissection may represent a more reliable strategy for reducing late-term complications that necessitate surgical revision.

In conclusion, our study suggests that simple technical applications in minor surgery may represent important factors influencing procedural success. This was observed in the context of port catheter surgery, where preoperative skin marking and avoidance of electrocautery were independently associated with improved outcomes, regardless of the surgeon’s experience level.

The observation that electrocautery use was associated with substantially higher revision rates, while skin marking was associated with improved procedural accuracy, supports the consideration of these technical choices during surgical training. Surgical experience may not fully offset the biological and mechanical tissue effects associated with electrocautery, particularly in high-risk patients receiving anti-VEGF therapy or those receiving high-profile port catheters.

Accordingly, cold dissection and preoperative marking may be considered as supportive technical strategies in port catheter placement, with the potential to reduce revision rates and enhance patient safety in oncology practice. Further research involving larger, prospective cohorts is warranted to validate these findings and to clarify the long-term impacts of these technical refinements across different patient subgroups.

There are several limitations to this study that should be considered. First, the retrospective design of the research may introduce inherent selection biases and limitations in data collection. Second, the procedures were performed by a single surgeon at a single center. While this consistency eliminates inter-surgeon variability, it may limit the generalizability of the findings to different surgical settings or experience levels. However, the use of CUSUM analysis was intended to provide a more objective evaluation of the learning curve and the impact of technical changes despite this limitation. In addition, the study was non-randomized, and technical modifications were implemented sequentially rather than through random allocation. As a result, causal inferences should be made with caution despite multivariable adjustment. Furthermore, external validation of these findings in independent cohorts is lacking. The absence of validation in other centers or by different operators may limit the generalizability of the results beyond the present study setting.

Third, although the multivariate analysis revealed significant risk factors, the sample size may still be considered relatively small for some subgroup analyses. Future prospective studies with larger, multi-center cohorts are necessary to confirm these results. Finally, this study focused primarily on revision rates and procedural success, while other factors such as operative time or detailed cost-effectiveness analyses between scalpel and electrocautery use were not extensively evaluated.
