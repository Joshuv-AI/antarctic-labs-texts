Arbuscular Mycorrhizal Fungi May Account for a Phosphorus‐Facilitation Strategy
Authors: Ling‐Ling Chen, Zhao Zhang, Joanna Kotula, Patrick M. Finnegan, Kosala Ranathunge, Hans Lambers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMycorrhizal phosphorus (P)‐scavenging strategies are inefficient in severely P‐impoverished environments; yet many mycorrhizal species occur here. How these species acquire P and how their acquisition coordinates with root and leaf traits remain unknown. We measured rhizosheath acid‐phosphatases, leaf P‐resorption efficiency and root and leaf economic traits and defence metabolites, such as phenolics and flavonoids, of Hibbertia hypericoides (Dilleniaceae) and H. racemosa in monoculture with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal inoculation and in a mixture with P‐mobilising Banksia attenuata (Proteaceae) with or without arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal inoculation. Phosphorus acquisition of both Hibbertia species was facilitated by B. attenuata, but through different interactions: H. hypericoides, non‐mycorrhizal despite having been inoculated, used its roots, whereas H. racemosa, forming arbuscular mycorrhizal associations following inoculation, relied on mycorrhizal interactions with B. attenuata. This was linked to positions along the ‘collaboration’ dimension of root economic space and coordinated with root defence metabolites. Hibbertia species diverged along the leaf economic spectrum, with higher scores (more conservative strategy) negatively correlated with leaf P‐resorption efficiency. We conclude that arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi play a key role in the P‐acquisition strategy of H. racemosa. Root and leaf traits and rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity efficiency interacted and contributed to the growth of both Hibbertia species.

Introduction

Mycorrhizal fungi play a vital role in plant nutrient acquisition and productivity in most terrestrial ecosystems with moderately low soil phosphorus (P) availability (Parfitt 1979; Li et al. 2006). Species that depend on a mycorrhizal scavenging strategy are ineffective at extremely low soil P concentrations ([P]; Parfitt 1979; Treseder and Allen 2002; Albornoz et al. 2021). Instead, in severely P‐impoverished habitats, species with cluster roots acquire P more effectively by releasing large amounts of carboxylates and phosphatases into the soil, thereby mobilising sorbed and organic P, respectively (Lambers et al. 2015a; Lambers et al. 2022). Yet, many mycorrhizal species coexist with non‐mycorrhizal species in severely P‐impoverished habitats, for example, in southwest Australia (Zemunik et al. 2015; Lambers et al. 2018; Lambers et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2025). To coexist, mycorrhizal species may rely on post‐fire P acquisition to rapidly germinate or resprout, followed by a gradual decline as soil P availability diminishes (Korczynskyj and Lamont 2005; Lambers 2022). Alternatively, mycorrhizal species that persist for many years in severely P‐impoverished soils may rely on the release of carboxylates (Wang et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025) or acid phosphatases (Png et al. 2017). A third possibility is that their P acquisition is facilitated by P‐mobilising neighbouring plants, such as cluster‐rooted Proteaceae that release carboxylates (Shen et al. 2024a; Staudinger et al. 2024). Such facilitation is common in P‐impoverished habitats (Lambers et al. 2022; Shen et al. 2024b); therefore, the role of mycorrhizal fungi warrants further investigation. Might mycorrhizal fungi play a key role in obtaining P when the P acquisition of a mycorrhizal plant is facilitated by neighbouring plants? For instance, could mycorrhizal hyphae transfer P mobilised by a cluster‐rooted neighbour to its host?

Plants exhibit a range of below‐ and aboveground traits that enhance P acquisition and utilisation in P‐impoverished habitats (Lambers 2022; Lambers et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2026). The root economic space (RES) and leaf economic spectrum (LES), which describe variation in resource acquisition, conservation and collaboration, provide frameworks for understanding plant strategies (Wright et al. 2004; Bergmann et al. 2020). Although the RES is partly decoupled from the LES (Kramer‐Walter et al. 2016; Ding et al. 2024; Guilbeault‐Mayers et al. 2026), the root ‘conservation’ dimension is positively associated with LES (Weigelt et al. 2021), indicating partial coordination, particularly in nutrient‐poor ecosystems where integrative strategies enhance whole‐plant nutrient‐use efficiency (Reich 2014; Zhang et al. 2024; Guilbeault‐Mayers et al. 2026).

Apart from root and leaf economic traits, acid phosphatases in rhizosheath soil (Png et al. 2017), defence metabolites, including phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidants, in roots and leaves (Wright et al. 2010; Yu et al. 2021) and leaf P‐resorption efficiency (PRE) (Hayes et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2024), are linked to enhancing plant P acquisition and utilisation. Acid phosphatases in rhizosheath soil hydrolyse organic P (Png et al. 2017), while root‐exuded phenolics act as chelators and/or reductants (Dakora and Phillips 2002; Li et al. 2025) and prevent microbial degradation of organic compounds (Weisskopf et al. 2006), thereby enhancing soil P availability. Root flavonoids are among the key signalling chemicals that facilitate the colonisation of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF; Cesco et al. 2010). Antioxidant activity in roots responds to metabolic activity or oxidative stress (Mittler 2002). Phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidant activity in leaves contribute to UV protection, pathogen defence and redox homeostasis (Grace and Logan 1996; Agati et al. 2012). There may be potentially unrecognised interactions among root and leaf economic traits, defence metabolites, rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf P resorption in native species from the severely P‐impoverished habitats of southwest Australia. This gap in knowledge limits our understanding of whether and how these traits interact to shape plant strategies for acquiring and utilising P in severely P‐impoverished ecosystems.

Hibbertia hypericoides (Dilleniaceae) is widely distributed in southwest Australia (Thiele and Cockerton 2015) and exhibits a variable leaf manganese concentration ([Mn]) (Staudinger et al. 2024). Leaf [Mn] can be a proxy for rhizosheath carboxylate concentrations (Lambers et al. 2015b; Pang et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2023); however, its interpretation may be context‐dependent. High or intermediate leaf [Mn] may indicate that a plant acquires P using either a carboxylate‐releasing strategy or that it is facilitated by carboxylate‐releasing P‐mobilising neighbours (Lambers et al. 2021; Staudinger et al. 2024). Furthermore, the roots of H. hypericoides are surrounded by cluster roots of neighbouring Banksia species (Zhong et al. 2021; Lambers et al. 2022), suggesting its P acquisition can be facilitated by a P‐mobilising Banksia species. Another Dilleniaceae, Hibbertia racemosa, is similarly widely distributed near Banksia species in P‐impoverished environments (Tauss et al. 2019). In rhizoboxes, the growth and root location of H. racemosa are impacted by neighbouring Banksia attenuata (de Britto Costa et al. 2021). Interestingly, some roots of H. racemosa grow within the rhizosphere of compound cluster roots of B. attenuata (de Britto Costa et al. 2021). However, when H. racemosa is grown with B. attenuata, its leaf [Mn] is not significantly higher than when grown alone which might suggest a lack of facilitation in P acquisition (de Britto Costa et al. 2021). Alternatively, facilitation of P acquisition of H. racemosa by Banksia might be obscured by AMF intercepting mobilised soil Mn and thus preventing its uptake by the plants (Bethlenfalvay and Franson 1989; Hashem 1995; Nogueira et al. 2002; Nogueira et al. 2007; Alho et al. 2015). Arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi cannot access insoluble organic P (Smith and Read 2010), since AMF do not release phosphatases (Read and Perez‐Moreno 2003). However, AMF have a superior ability to efficiently absorb P that is available in the soil solution (Read 1991; Neville et al. 2002; Wu et al. 2025), as their mycelia extend into a soil volume beyond the root depletion zone. The P acquired from this enlarged range can be transported back to the plant by a hyphal network (He et al. 2003). Might mycorrhizal fungi associated with H. racemosa, when growing with B. attenuata, play a role in absorbing P mobilised by a neighbouring plant? Alternatively, might H. hypericoides roots directly access P mobilised by neighbours, with limited dependence on mycorrhizal associations?

The present study aimed to investigate how AMF impact P‐facilitation strategies of two Hibbertia species and how plant root and leaf economic traits and defence metabolites, including phenolics, flavonoids and antioxidants, rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf P resorption, interact to determine which traits are critical for growth in severely P‐impoverished habitats. We tested the following hypotheses: (1) P uptake by H. hypericoides is facilitated by co‐occurring B. attenuata independent of the inoculation of AMF through an interaction that is associated with the root defence metabolites, including the root phenolic concentration and antioxidant activity; (2) P uptake by H. racemosa is only facilitated by co‐occurring B. attenuata when inoculated with AMF; and (3) P‐acquisition strategies of H. hypericoides and H. racemosa are linked to the positions along the ‘collaboration’ dimension of the RES, with more ‘outsourcing’ strategies reflecting P‐facilitation strategies and being positively associated with root phenolic and flavonoid concentrations, antioxidant activity and rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity. (4) H. hypericoides and H. racemosa occupy different positions on the LES, while the LES positions are associated with leaf PRE. By studying the role of AMF in P‐facilitation strategies and the coordination of root and leaf functional traits of these two mycorrhizal species, we aimed to enhance our understanding of how belowground symbioses and plant traits integrate to contribute to plant coexistence and nutrient acquisition and utilisation in P‐impoverished ecosystems.

Materials and Methods

Species Selection and Glasshouse Experiments

Hibbertia hypericoides and H. racemosa belong to the Dilleniaceae, a family in which arbuscular mycorrhizal fungal associations have been reported (Brundrett and Abbott 1991), although species‐level data are currently lacking. These two Hibbertia species share a similar distribution in southwest Australia (FloraBase, https://florabase.dbca.wa.gov.au/) with contrasting P‐acquisition strategies indicated by different leaf [Mn] in severely P‐impoverished habitats (de Britto Costa et al. 2021; Zhong et al. 2021; Staudinger et al. 2024).

Seedlings of H. hypericoides, H. racemosa and B. attenuata were purchased (Muchea Tree Farm, Muchea, Western Australia) and cut to the same size and height of about 15 cm. Trimmed seedlings were transferred to 3‐L pots of triple‐pasteurised soil in a glasshouse with min/max temperatures of 17/22°C and a mean relative humidity of 60% (Zhang et al. 2026). The soil was topsoil to a depth of 80 mm collected from dunes of Bassendean sand along the Jurien Bay chronosequence (Laliberté et al. 2012; Zhong et al. 2025). Each pot contained two seedlings: H. hypericoides, or H. racemosa, grown either in monoculture with the inoculation of AMF, or mixed with a B. attenuata seedling with or without the inoculation of AMF (Figure 1). For each Hibbertia seedling in an AMF treatment, 50 mg of inoculum was placed directly beneath the root zone during transplanting. No inoculum was added to non‐AMF‐inoculated treatments. Following growth in a glasshouse at the University of Western Australia, 6 months after transplanting in April 2024, six pots per treatment were used for harvests and analyses. Hence, the monoculture treatment included twelve Hibbertia seedlings as replicates, whereas each mixture treatment comprised six Hibbertia seedlings and six B. attenuata seedlings as replicates.

Experimental design for the glasshouse experiment. Seedlings of Hibbertia hypericoides and Banksia attenuata or H. racemosa and B. attenuata were grown in monoculture with inoculation of arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) or in mixture with or without inoculation of AMF (n = 6).

The AMF inoculum applied during transplanting was prepared using a mycorrhizal Acacia lasiocarpa (Albornoz et al. 2017), which is known to support native AMF communities. Seeds were germinated and seedlings grown for 3 months in non‐pasteurised soil collected along the Jurien Bay chronosequence. Roots were washed, cut into small fragments and homogenised using a Geno/Grinder 2000 (Spex SamplePrep, Metuchen, NJ, USA) to serve as the AMF inoculum. Although the inoculum likely contained other micro‐organisms in addition to AMF, there were no traces of damage by pathogens in the A. lasiocarpa seedlings or their roots.

Harvest and Growth‐Related Measurements

At harvest, the height of each plant was recorded before severing mature and young leaves from the stem and collecting senesced and abscised leaves. Mature leaf area was measured using a scanner (Epson 1680 scanner, 600 dpi, Epson America, Long Beach, CA, USA). Stems and leaves were oven‐dried separately at 70°C to a constant dry weight (DW) and their total weight was recorded to determine the aboveground biomass. Leaf dry mass per area (LMA) was calculated as the DW of mature leaves divided by their area. Leaves were ground to a fine powder in a vertical ball‐mill grinder with plastic vials and yttrium‐stabilised zirconium ceramic beads (GenoGrinder, Spex SamplePrep, Metuchen, NJ, USA). Leaf [P] was determined using approx. 200 mg ground leaf material digested in hot concentrated HNO3: HClO4 (Zarcinas et al. 1987) and measured by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (Optima 5300DV; PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). Leaf P content was calculated by multiplying the leaf [P] by the respective leaf biomass. Total leaf P content was the sum of the individual leaf P content from mature and young leaves. Leaf PRE was calculated as the percentage reduction in leaf [P] from mature to fully senesced and abscised leaves.

Soil was carefully tipped out of the pots, and the root systems were gently shaken to remove loosely adhering soil. The soil tightly bound to the roots was defined as rhizosheath soil and was collected and stored at 4°C (Pang et al. 2017; Mo et al. 2023). Whole root systems were carefully rinsed with deionised water to remove remaining soil and spread out with deionised water in a transparent tray to record root images (Epson 1680 scanner, 600 dpi, Epson America, Long Beach, CA, USA). Images were analysed (WinRhizo v.4.1c, Regent Instruments, Quebec, Canada) to obtain total root length, root surface area, mean root diameter (RD), root volume and specific root length (SRL). Root tissue density (RTD) was calculated as the ratio of root biomass to root volume. A subsample of four or five washed fine lateral roots of each Hibbertia plant, including AMF‐ and non‐AMF‐inoculated treatments, was stored in 70% (v/v) ethanol for AMF‐colonisation analyses. Remaining roots were snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen. All roots, including those preserved in ethanol after AMF‐colonisation analyses and those stored in liquid nitrogen, were freeze‐dried for 7 days (VirTis BenchTop Pro ‘K’ Freeze Dryer; SP Scientific, Warminster, PA, USA), then weighed to determine the root biomass.

AMF Colonisation

A subsample of fine lateral roots was cleared by immersing them in 10% (w/v) KOH at 60°C for 3 h and then at room temperature for 4 days before rinsing in acidified water [pH around 2, adjusted with 1% (v/v) HCl] (Phillips and Hayman 1970). Cleared roots were stained with 5% (v/v) ink (Shaeffer black ink)‐white vinegar solution for 1 h at room temperature. Stained roots were rinsed several times in acidified water to remove excess stain (Vierheilig et al. 1998). Stained 1‐cm length root segments were mounted on slides and examined under ×20 magnification (Axioskop optical microscope, El‐Einsatz; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) and photographed (Axiocam digital camera, Carl Zeiss Microscopy, GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany). AMF colonisation was quantified using the line‐intersect method (Giovannetti and Mosse 1980) by examining 100 intersections per sample and recording the presence of AMF hyphae and arbuscules at each intersection.

Chemical Compounds in Rhizosheath Soil, Roots and Mature Leaves

Acid phosphomonoesterase activity was quantified following a modified protocol based on Tabatabai (1994). About 1 g fresh rhizosheath soil was placed in a 10‐mL tube, followed by adding 0.2 mL toluene, 4 mL universal buffer (MUB; 20.0 mM TRIS hydroxymethyl aminomethane (THAM), 20.0 mM maleic acid, 14.6 mM citric acid, 20.4 mM boric acid, pH 6.5 with 0.1 M NaOH) and 1 mL 50 mM p‐nitrophenyl phosphate. Tubes were gently shaken and incubated at 37°C for 1 h. The enzymatic reaction was terminated by adding 1 mL 0.5 M CaCl2 and 4 mL 0.5 M NaOH. The suspension was filtered through a P‐free 0.45 μm filter and the absorbance of the filtrate was measured at 420 nm using a spectrophotometer. The absorbance was converted to acid monophosphatase activity based on a standard curve prepared with p‐nitrophenol (Tabatabai 1994).

Total phenolic and flavonoid compounds and antioxidant activity of roots and leaves were extracted from approx. 100 and 80 mg of freeze‐dried ground root and mature leaf material, respectively, according to Santas et al. (2008) with some modifications. After adding 1.5 mL 75% (v/v) ethanol, each sample was shaken at 1000 rpm at room temperature for 30 min in a thermomixer (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany), and then incubated in an ultrasonic bath at 40 kHz for 20 min. Extracts were cleared by centrifugation at 845g for 15 min at 20°C. After removing the supernatant, the pellet was extracted twice more with fresh solvent, each time with shaking times of 45 and 90 min, respectively. The supernatants were pooled and stored in the dark at −20°C until analysis.

Total root and leaf phenolic compounds were determined by mixing 200 μL diluted ethanol extract (50 μL extract plus 150 μL 75% (v/v) ethanol) with 1.5 mL 10% (v/v) diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent in Milli‐Q water and 1.5 mL 2% (w/v) sodium carbonate. Mixtures were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 765 nm using a spectrophotometer and converted to be expressed as mg gallic acid g−1 DW based on a calibration curve prepared from a series of gallic‐acid (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) standards ranging from 0 to 250 mg L−1.

Flavonoids were determined by modification of the method used by Chang et al. (2002), mixing 300 μL ethanol extract with 900 μL 95% (v/v) ethanol, 60 μL 10% (w/v) aluminium trichloride and 60 μL 1 M potassium acetate. Mixtures were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 415 nm using a spectrophotometer and converted to mg quercetin g−1 DW according to a calibration curve prepared from a series of quercetin (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) standards ranging from 0 to 200 mg L−1.

Antioxidant activity was measured according to Brand‐Williams et al. (1995), using 200 μL diluted ethanol extracts (50 μL extract and 150 μL 75% (v/v) ethanol) mixed with 2 mL DPPH reagent. The DPPH reagent was prepared by sonication of 0.1 mM DPPH in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 1 h. The absorbance of the reaction mixture was measured at 515 nm using a spectrophotometer and converted to μmol trolox g−1 DW according to a calibration curve prepared from a series of trolox (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) standards ranging from 0 to 300 μM.

Statistical Analyses

All statistical analyses and graphical visualisations were performed in R Studio v.4.3.2 (R Development Core Team 2024) using the ‘ggplot2’ package (Wickham 2016). Treatment effects on Hibbertia species and Banksia attenuata were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models, with treatment as a fixed effect and pot as a random intercept, using the ‘lme4’ package (Bates et al. 2015). Model residuals were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and response variables were log₁₀‐transformed when necessary to meet model assumptions. Treatment differences were evaluated based on response‐scale estimated marginal means, using the ‘emmeans’ package (Lenth 2024).

A principal component analysis (PCA) was used to explore the variation of the two Hibbertia species in root and leaf economic traits, concentrations of defence metabolites, rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf PRE using the ‘FactoMineR’ package, based on log₁₀‐transformed variables (Lê et al. 2008). Correlations among root defence metabolites, rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity and RES or leaf defence metabolite concentrations, leaf PRE and LES were assessed by Pearson correlation and the corresponding heatmap using the ‘corrplot’ package (Wei and Simko 2010).

The effects of root and leaf traits on total biomass and leaf P content were examined separately for the two Hibbertia species using the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) framework (Lundberg and Lee 2017; Lundberg et al. 2020). Random forest regression models were fitted with total biomass or leaf P content as the response variables and root and leaf traits as predictors (Breiman et al. 2018). SHAP values were computed using the ‘fastshap’ package (Greenwell and Greenwell 2020), based on Monte Carlo sampling to estimate local feature attributions. SHAP values were calculated for all observations to quantify each variable's contribution to model predictions, and mean absolute SHAP values were used to rank overall variable importance.

Results

Plant Growth Parameters

The presence of B. attenuata significantly increased the total biomass and belowground biomass of H. hypericoides, regardless of inoculation with AMF (Figure 2a; Table 1). When not inoculated with AMF, H. hypericoides grown in the mixture with B. attenuata showed a higher leaf P content than when grown in monoculture with inoculation of AMF (Figure 2b). The P content, aboveground biomass and height of H. hypericoides in AMF‐inoculated mixture with B. attenuata were indistinguishable from AMF‐inoculated monoculture or mixture without AMF (Figure 2b; Table 1). There were no differences for the other growth parameters measured among any treatments in H. hypericoides, while there were no differences for B. attenuata in any of the growth parameters between the two AMF treatments (Figure 2a,b; Table 1). Roots of H. hypericoides and B. attenuata did not show any evidence of AMF colonisation, including arbuscules, vesicles and hyphae, in any treatment.

Chemical Compounds in Rhizosheath Soil, Fine Roots and Mature Leaves

Phenolic concentrations and antioxidant activity in H. hypericoides roots were higher in the presence of B. attenuata than in its absence, regardless of AMF status, while flavonoid concentrations were indistinguishable among treatments (Figure 3a,b; Table 1). Rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity for H. hypericoides was greater after the inoculation of AMF when grown alongside B. attenuata than when in monoculture (Table 1). When grown in mixture with H. hypericoides, B. attenuata exhibited higher rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity and root antioxidant activity when inoculated with AMF compared with the uninoculated treatment (Table 1). In contrast, we found no significant differences in other root or leaf defence metabolites or leaf PRE in H. hypericoides or B. attenuata among any treatments (Figure 3a,b; Table 1).

Correlations Among Root and Leaf Economic Traits and Concentrations of Defence Metabolites

A PCA conducted for the combined results for both H. hypericoides and H. racemosa recovered 89% of the observed variation among the root economic traits within PC1 and PC2 (Figure 4a; Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Furthermore, PC1, which explained 62% of the variation, captured positive associations with RD and negative associations with SRL which defines the ‘collaboration’ dimension in the RES (Figure 4a; Supporting Information S1: Table S1). PC2 (28% of the variation) mainly captured positive loading with RTD (Figure 4a; Supporting Information S1: Table S1), which partly defines the ‘acquisition‐conservation’ dimension. Along PC1, H. hypericoides grown in mixture with B. attenuata, irrespective of the inoculation of AMF, were positioned more positively than in monoculture, while H. racemosa was positioned more positively when grown in a mixture with B. attenuata with AMF than when grown in monoculture with AMF or with B. attenuata without AMF (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3a). Furthermore, the individual locations of H. hypericoides and H. racemosa were not significantly separated along either PC1 or PC2 (Figure 4a; Supporting Information S1: Figure S3a,b).

Among leaf economic traits, the variation among H. hypericoides and H. racemosa individuals was largely explained by PC1 and PC2, which together accounted for 72% of the variation (Figure 4b; Supporting Information S1: Table S2). PC1 accounted for 38% of the total variance, positively driven by LMA and negatively driven by leaf [P] (LP). Thus, PC1 represents the LES axis (Figure 4b; Supporting Information S1: Table S2). PC2, which explained 34% of the variation, primarily captured positive loading with leaf P content (LPc; Figure 4b; Supporting Information S1: Table S2). Along PC1, the positions of H. hypericoides individuals were more positive than those of H. racemosa (Figure 4b; Supporting Information S1: Figure S3c).

In the PCA of root and leaf concentrations of defence metabolites, rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity (PA) and leaf PRE among H. hypericoides and H. racemosa individuals, about 60% of the variation was explained by PC1 (32%) and PC2 (27%; Figure 4c; Supporting Information S1: Table S3). Notably, H. racemosa individuals were well separated from those of H. hypericoides, mostly due to phenolic concentrations (RPh), root antioxidant activity (RA), PRE and PA (Figure 4c; Supporting Information S1: Figure S4 and Table S3).

For H. hypericoides, PC1 of the PCA of root economic traits (RPC1), representing the collaborative dimension of the RES, was positively correlated with RA and PA, while PC2 (RPC2), representing the acquisition dimension of the RES, was positively correlated with RPh and RA (Figure 4d). There were no significant correlations between concentrations of leaf defence metabolites and PC1 of the PCA representing the leaf economics spectrum (LPC1; Figure 4d). In H. racemosa, like H. hypericoides, RPC1 was positively correlated with PA; however, in contrast with H. hypericoides, RPC1 was positively correlated with RPh instead of RA, while there were no correlations between RPC2 and defence compounds. The H. racemosa LPC1 was negatively correlated with PRE, while the H. hypericoides LPC1 only tended to be negatively correlated with PRE (Figure 4e).

Root and Leaf Economic Traits and Concentrations of Defence Metabolites as Predictors of Total Plant Biomass and Leaf P Content

To assess the relative importance of root and leaf economic traits, concentrations of defence metabolites, rhizosheath‐soil acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf PRE in explaining variation in total plant biomass and leaf P content for the two Hibbertia species, we conducted SHAPs analyses (Figure 5; Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). For H. hypericoides, RPC1, defining the ‘collaboration’ dimension in the RES, showed the highest mean absolute SHAP value and thus emerged as the strongest predictor of total biomass variation (Figure 5a). Root antioxidant activity and leaf flavonoid concentration (LF) ranked next in importance (Figure 5a). Furthermore, the SHAP value distributions showed that RPC1 and RA were generally associated with positive contributions to total biomass (Figure 5a). Similarly, RA and RPC1 were identified as the most influential factors in predicting the leaf P content of H. hypericoides, with LPC1, representing the LES axis, and LF and PA, contributing to a lesser extent (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5a). For predicting total plant biomass for H. racemosa, RPC1 emerged as the most important predictor, as it was for H. hypericoides, with the highest mean absolute SHAP value, followed by PRE and RPh in decreasing order of importance (Figure 5b). The most important factor predicting leaf P content for H. racemosa was PRE, while LPC1, RPC1 and RA were progressively less important (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5b). Overall, the SHAP analyses highlighted a strong coupling between P‐acquisition and P‐utilisation traits in the growth of H. racemosa and H. hypericoides.

Discussion

Facilitation of P Acquisition in Hibbertia Hypericoides via Root Interactions With Banksia Attenuata

The total biomass and belowground biomass of H. hypericoides were significantly greater when grown with B. attenuata than when grown in monoculture, increasing from 3.47 to 5.86 g, showing that its growth was facilitated by B. attenuata (Figure 2a; Table 1). Furthermore, the growth facilitation of H. hypericoides by B. attenuata occurred irrespective of the inoculation of AMF. This pattern demonstrates that the facilitation was not mediated by AMF but rather resulted from direct belowground facilitative interactions between H. hypericoides and B. attenuata. The roots of H. hypericoides were associated with the cluster roots of B. attenuata (Figure 6; Zhong et al. 2021; Lambers et al. 2022), reinforcing the likelihood that H. hypericoides roots directly interacted with B. attenuata cluster roots.

[Color figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

Notably, the facilitative effect of B. attenuata on H. hypericoides was not limited to growth parameters: root phenolic concentrations and antioxidant activity in H. hypericoides, like total biomass, were also higher in the presence of B. attenuata than in its absence (Figure 3a,b). These findings indicate that root defence metabolites may be involved in the facilitation of P acquisition of H. hypericoides by B. attenuata. Phenolics are known to act as chelators and/or reductants, enhancing soil P availability (Dakora and Phillips 2002; Juszczuk et al. 2004; Pontigo et al. 2018) and preventing AMF inoculum degradation of some organic compounds (e.g., phosphatases and carboxylates) (Weisskopf et al. 2006). The antioxidant activity may reflect the response to metabolic activity or oxidative stress associated with intensified nutrient acquisition in P‐impoverished soils (Mittler 2002; Hernández and Munné‐Bosch 2015).

We found no evidence of AMF colonisation in H. hypericoides roots. Although root flavonoids are key signalling chemicals that facilitate AMF colonisation (Perry et al. 2007; Cesco et al. 2010), their concentrations in H. hypericoides were not distinguishable among treatments (Table 1). This supports the conclusion that the facilitation of H. hypericoides growth by B. attenuata was independent of AMF and likely driven by direct root‐cluster root interactions (Figure 7a), associated with defence metabolites, including root phenolic concentrations and antioxidant activity, supporting our first hypothesis.

Facilitation of P Acquisition in Hibbertia Racemosa via Mycorrhizal Fungal Interactions With Banksia Attenuata

Hibbertia racemosa exhibited significantly greater total biomass and aboveground biomass when grown with B. attenuata and AMF than other treatments (Figure 2c; Table 2). Furthermore, H. racemosa was colonised by AMF, as evidenced by the presence of key symbiotic structures, including arbuscules, vesicles and hyphae in its roots (Supporting Information S1: Figures S1 and S2). Consistent with previous studies (de Britto Costa et al. 2021), the roots of H. racemosa were associated with the cluster roots of B. attenuata (Supporting Information S1: Figure S6). These findings indicate that AMF played a pivotal role in facilitating P acquisition of H. racemosa, presumably through interactions with B. attenuata roots, confirming our second hypothesis (Figure 7b). The observed facilitation likely involved two mechanisms: soil P mobilisation by cluster roots of B. attenuata (Lambers et al. 2015b; Lambers et al. 2022) and enhanced P‐uptake capacity through the extraradical mycelia of AMF (He et al. 2003). This mechanism would help explain the low leaf [Mn] of H. racemosa in the glasshouse experimental study of de Britto Costa et al. (2021). Although leaf [Mn] can be a proxy for rhizosheath carboxylate concentrations (Lambers et al. 2015b; Pang et al. 2018; Yu et al. 2023), AMF intercept soil Mn and prevent uptake by host plants (Bethlenfalvay and Franson 1989; Hashem 1995; Nogueira et al. 2002; Nogueira et al. 2007; Alho et al. 2015). Therefore, leaf [Mn] may not invariably be an indicator of facilitation of P acquisition by neighbours with carboxylate‐releasing strategies when AMF participate in P acquisition. Therefore, leaf [Mn] needs to be explained in the context of alternative P‑acquisition strategies and species‑specific responses, rather than as a stand‐alone proxy.

Root and Leaf Economic Traits Correlated With Defence Metabolites

Hibbertia hypericoides in mixture with B. attenuata occupied a more ‘outsourcing’ position on the ‘collaboration’ dimension along the RES than in monoculture, while H. racemosa positioned a more ‘outsourcing’ location when grown with B. attenuata with AMF than when grown in monoculture or with B. attenuata without AMF (Figure 4a; Supporting Information S1: Figure S3a). This supports that the P acquisition of both H. hypericoides and H. racemosa was facilitated by B. attenuata, but in different ways. Hibbertia hypericoides benefited through direct root‐root interaction, whereas H. racemosa benefited through mycorrhizal fungi, interacting with B. attenuata roots. Previous studies on root “outsourcing” strategies have mainly focused on mycorrhizal plants growing in P‐sufficient soils (Bergmann et al. 2020; Ding et al. 2023; Zhu et al. 2025), while in severely P‐impoverished habitats, mycorrhizal scavenging without interactions with P‐mobilising facilitators is ineffective at obtaining P (Treseder and Allen 2002; Albornoz et al. 2021; Lambers et al. 2022). We, therefore, propose a complementary concept: under severely P‐impoverished soil conditions, a root morphological “outsourcing” strategy may instead reflect P facilitation by P‐mobilising neighbours. In the root economic‐traits PCA, both H. hypericoides and H. racemosa showed correlations between PC1(RPC1) or PC2(RPC2) and root metabolites, with RPC1 positively correlated with rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity in both Hibbertia species (Figure 4d,e). These patterns support the notion linking the RES to root exudation for nutrient acquisition and confirm the third hypothesis (Wen et al. 2019; Wen et al. 2022; Williams et al. 2022).

Along the LES, H. racemosa was positioned toward the acquisitive end compared with H. hypericoides (Figure 4b; Supporting Information S1: Figure S3c). These patterns showed divergence in leaf economic traits between congeneric species (Gille et al. 2024). In addition, consistent with Ma et al. (2024), PC1 of the LES PCA (LPC1) showed negative correlations with leaf PRE, indicating conservative traits along the LES were compensated by high leaf PRE. In summary, the congeneric species H. hypericoides and H. racemosa occupied different positions on the LES and these positions were correlated with their leaf PRE in extremely P‐impoverished habitats, supporting our fourth hypothesis.

Root and Leaf Economic Traits and Defence Metabolites Contribute to Achieving Total Biomass and Leaf P Content

Root and leaf economic traits, concentrations of defence metabolites, rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf PRE contributed to explaining variation in total plant biomass and leaf P content (Figure 5; Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). For H. hypericoides, RPC1 and root antioxidant activity emerged as the strongest predictors of both total biomass and leaf P content (Figure 5a), suggesting that root nutrient‐acquisition strategy, along with the ‘collaboration’ dimension of RES, and root antioxidant activity were key determinants of its growth (Wen et al. 2019). By contrast, for H. racemosa, RPC1 was the most important predictor of total plant biomass, whereas leaf PRE and LPC1 were the strongest predictors of leaf P content (Figure 5b). This indicates that the growth of H. racemosa was closely linked to the RES, while its leaf nutrient status was more sensitive to leaf resource‐acquisition economic traits. The strong role of LPC1 suggests that H. racemosa compensated for low soil P availability by investing in conservative leaf traits (Wang et al. 2025).

Conclusions

The present study highlights the different roles of AMF in facilitating P acquisition in severely P‐impoverished environments, showing different strategies among two Hibbertia species, thus providing a deeper understanding of how RES and LES are associated with root and leaf biochemical traits. We demonstrate that facilitation of P acquisition in H. hypericoides is exclusively through its roots, while that in H. racemosa is through the establishment of mycorrhizal fungal associations that interact with roots of neighbouring plants, in this case B. attenuata, which releases carboxylates that mobilise soil P. Furthermore, we confirm that root and leaf traits, including economic traits, defence metabolites, rhizosheath acid‐phosphatase activity and leaf PRE, interact to shape P‐acquisition and P‐utilisation strategies and contribute to growth in both Hibbertia species. Integrating multiple below‐ and aboveground functional traits, this study sheds light on the complexity of P‐acquisition and ‐utilisation strategies in highly biodiverse severely nutrient‐impoverished ecosystems. This knowledge is crucial for understanding how mycorrhizal species survive in P‐impoverished habitats and contribute to their megadiversity.
