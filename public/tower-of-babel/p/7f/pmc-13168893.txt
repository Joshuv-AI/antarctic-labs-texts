Whole-genome CRISPR screening identifies genetic modifiers of stem cell–derived islet transplantation
Authors: Marlie M Maestas, Kameron Bradley, Mira Shunkarova, Noyonika Mukherjee, Matthew Ishahak, James Lu, Jeffrey R Millman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGenetically engineering human pluripotent stem cell (hPSC)-derived islets is a promising strategy for improving transplantation for diabetes cell therapy; however, genetic perturbations that modulate transplantation outcomes have yet to be systematically explored.MethodsTo identify potential targets, we performed an unbiased whole-genome CRISPR-activation screen in transplanted stem cell–derived islets (SC-islets). Specifically, we created a stem cell line with CRISPR-activation components (HUES8-VPR) and then transduced these stem cells with a lentiviral guide RNA library targeting the whole human genome. Following transduction, the stem cells were differentiated into SC-islets, which were subsequently transplanted into NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) immunodeficient mice. After transplantation, SC-islets were extracted for next-generation sequencing.ResultsThe screen identified multiple candidates, including the Fc alpha/mu receptor (FCAMR). In vitro characterization revealed that FCAMR overexpression did not negatively affect SC-islet function or transcriptomic identity. Mice subcutaneously transplanted with SC-islets overexpressing FCAMR had reduced blood glucose levels and increased C-peptide compared to controls. Additionally, mice receiving FCAMR-modified grafts into the kidney capsule or hindleg muscle maintained a higher body weight compared to controls in a diabetic setting.ConclusionsIn conclusion, this study demonstrats improved glucose regulation at a subcutaneous transplant site. In addition, we show that FCAMR SC-islets could play a role in systemic metabolism when transplanted into the kidney capsule or hindleg muscle. Overall, our study establishes a functional screening approach to identify gene candidates to improve SC-islet transplantation.Keywords: cell transplantation, developmental biology, diabetes, differentiation, embryonic stem cells, pancreatic differentiation, pluripotent stem cells

This study demonstrates the utility of whole-genome CRISPR activation screening to uncover genetic factors that enhance the survival and function of stem cell-derived islets (SC-islets) following transplantation. By identifying and characterizing candidates, such as FCAMR, the work provides novel insight into how targeted gene modulation can improve graft engraftment, insulin secretion, and systemic outcomes in diabetic models. These findings address a critical barrier in the development of cell replacement therapies for type 1 diabetes and establish a powerful discovery platform that can be extended to other genes, pathways, and transplantation challenges.

Introduction

Currently, many individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D) are reliant on insulin injections to regulate their blood glucose levels due to the destruction of insulin-secreting β-cells within the islets of Langerhans. However, exogenous insulin injections fail to precisely regulate glucose homeostasis, resulting in an increased risk of hyper- and hypoglycemia. In addition, insulin alone cannot prevent the systemic complications associated with T1D.1,2 An alternative treatment would be to replace the lost β-cells with transplanted islets. Many T1D patients have received allogeneic transplantations.3 These islets isolated from deceased donors demonstrate the efficacy of cell replacement therapy;4–6 however, this approach still has significant limitations. The need for lifelong immunosuppression can lead to complications, and multiple donors are needed for a single patient, but an insufficient supply of cadaveric donor islets is available.

To address the current limitations of transplanting cadaveric islets, human SC-islets provide a potentially unlimited source for cell replacement therapy. Through the precise differentiation of these microtissues,7–12 researchers can produce SC-islets that are transcriptionally and functionally similar to their primary counterparts. Recently, a study involved transplanting SC-islets into T1D patients, and after 1 year, 83% of the patients were insulin independent.13 In diabetic mouse models, SC-islets can regulate blood glucose levels and produce significant amounts of human C-peptide.13 However, optimizing SC-islet transplantation remains challenging. Studies have shown significant loss of islet mass14 and low engraftment rates,15 with SC-islets losing almost 50% of their mass when transplanted into the kidney capsule or subcutaneously.16 Furthermore, transplant outcomes can vary substantially depending on the transplantation site, with subcutaneous sites showing particularly poor engraftment.16,17 Despite recent advances, systemic approaches to identify genetic modifications that could enhance SC-islet transplantation outcomes across different contexts remains limited.

This paper explores the use of unbiased functional genomic screening to identify genetic modifications that modulate SC-islet transplantation outcomes.16 We employed a whole-genome CRISPR activation screen to identify genes of interest that were enriched after transplantation into the kidney capsule of mice, reasoning that such genes may play roles in graft survival, function, or adaptation to the transplant microenvironment. Our analysis identified SIX3, FCAMR, and GSMT3 as candidate genes. We overexpressed the gene candidates in SC-islets and investigated their roles in vitro and in vivo. Here, we present the results of this genomic screen and the subsequent in vivo characterization of these candidate genes, with particular focus on FCAMR, which showed a context-dependent effects on graft function and host metabolism. This work establishes proofs of concept for using functional genomic screens to discover candidate genes for SC-islet transplantation and provides insights into how genetic engineering might be optimized for cell therapy outcomes.

Methods

Culture and generation of inducible HUES8-VPR cell line

HUES8 were maintained under feeder-free conditions on plates coated with Matrigel (Corning, 354277) in mTeSR1 medium (Stem Cell, 85850). For passaging and transfection, cells were dissociated into a single-cell suspension using TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco, 12604039). To enhance survival, cells were cultured in media supplemented with 10-µM ROCK inhibitor (BioGems, 1293823) for the first 24 h after plating.

HUES8 cells (1 × 106) were resuspended in 100-µL of supplemented P3 Nucleofector™ solution (from P3 Primary Cell 4D-Nucleofector® X Kit, S; Lonza). The cell suspension was mixed with 5-µg of total plasmid DNA, consisting of a mixture of the pT076 dCas9-VPR-T2A-EGFP AAVS1 targeting plasmid (Addgene #137880) and the two TALEN plasmids: AAVS1 TALEN L (Addgene #59025) and AAVS1 TALEN R (Addgene #59026). Cells were transfected using the Amaxa™ 4D-Nucleofector™ X Unit (Lonza) with program CM-113. Immediately following nucleofection, cells were plated onto Matrigel-coated plates in mTeSR supplemented with CloneR (STEMCELL Technologies, 52847268 01) to enhance single-cell cloning efficiency. Selection with 50-μg/mL of Geneticin (Fisher Scientific; 10131035) was initiated 48 h post-transfection. After 10-14 days, emerging colonies were treated with 2-µg/mL DOX to induce dCas9-VPR-T2A-EGFP expression. The GFP-positive colonies were isolated and expanded.

Generation of lentiviral vectors

Forward and reverse primers targeting our gene of interest were ordered from Millipore Sigma. To assemble the plasmid, the primers were resuspended in ultrapure water to a concentration of 100 μM. Forward and reverse primer (1 μL), T4 ligation buffer (NEB, B0202A), T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (NEB, M0201S), and Ultrapure water were added to a PCR tube and placed in the thermocycler for phosphorylation and annealing reaction. The resulting gRNA oligonucleotide duplex was inserted into a Lentiguide Puro Backbone (Addgene plasmid #73797) by combining Rapid Ligase buffer (Enzymatics, B1010L), Fast Digest Esp31 (Thermo, FD0454), Dithiothreitol (Promega, PRP1171), Bovine Serum Albumin (NEB, B9000S), and T7 DNA ligase (Enzymatics, L6020L). This master mix was placed in the thermocycler for the Golden Gate assembly reaction to create the plasmids.

Plasmids are now transformed into Stbl3 One Shot Chemically Competent Escherichia coli (Invitrogen, C737303) by adding 2-μL of plasmid to the Stbl3 tubes. The mixture is incubated on ice for 5 min and then heat-shocked at 42 °C for 30 s. The mixture was returned to ice for 2 min. S.O.C. media (200 μL) was added to the tube and spread on an agar plate containing carbenicillin (GOLDBIO, C-103-25). After overnight incubation at 37 °C, one colony was selected and cultured in LB-broth (Gibco, 10855021). Plasmid DNA was isolated using Qiagen Maxi Prep Plus kit (Qiagen, 12981). Plasmids were sent off for sequencing to Genewiz to confirm integration of gRNA. The whole-genome plasmid (Cellecta Human Genome-Wide CRISPRa sgRNA Library; Cat. No. KAHGW-106-P) was ordered from Cellecta.

293T (Takara, 632180) cells were plated so that they would be 80%-90% confluent the next day. Confluent cells were transfected with 4.5 μg of psPAX2 (Addgene, 12260), 1.5 μg of pMD.2 (Addgene, 12259), 6 μg plasmid DNA, 1470 Opti-MEM (Gibco, 31985-062), and 117.6 μL of polyethylenimine (Polyscience, 24765-2). This was incubated for 16 h at 37 °C, and then, the media was changed. The supernatant was collected 88 h post-infection and concentrated using the Lenti-X concentrator (Takara, 631232). The resulting lentivirus was titered using Lenti-X GoStix Plus (Takara, 631280).

Fluorescent microscopy

Fluorescent images were all captured using the Nikon A1Rsi confocal microscope. For EGFP images, stem cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. The cells were incubated in 5% donkey serum with 0.1% Triton-X in PBS (ICC solution) for 45 min at room temperature. The cells were stained with DAPI (Thermo, D1306) for 15 min at room temperature.

For staining of stem cells, HUES8 and HUES8-VPR stem cells were seeded at 0.75 × 106 on Matrigel-coated 6-well plates; 24 h after seeding, half the HUES8-VPR cells were treated with DOX for 48 h. The wells were washed with PBS, and 4% paraformaldehyde was added for 30 min at room temperature. The paraformaldehyde was removed, and the cells were incubated in ICC solution to permeabilize and block for 45 min at room temperature. Primary antibody was added to the cells to incubate overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the cells were incubated in the secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. The cells were then stained with DAPI (Thermo, D1306) for 15 min at room temperature.

For histological immunofluorescent images, the mouse kidney was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight at 4 °C. The tissue was then embedded in paraffin and sectioned by HistoWiz (Long Island, NY). The slides were deparaffined using Histoclear (ThermoFisher, C78-2-G). 0.1 M of EDTA (Ambion, AM9261) was used for antigen retrieval in a pressure cooker (ProteoGenix, 2100). Slides were then incubated in ICC solution for 30 min at room temperature. Primary antibodies were added to the slides to incubate overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the slides were incubated in the secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. To mount the slides, DAPI Fluoromount-G (SouthernBiotech, 0100-20) was used.

Primary antibodies were diluted in ICC solution: 1:300 mouse anti-OCT4 (Santa Cruz, Sc-5279), 1:300 goat anti-NANOG (R&D systems, AF1997), 1:300 rat anti-C-peptide (Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank [DSHB], GN-ID4-S), 1:300 mouse anti-SST (Santa Cruz, sc-55565), 1:300 rabbit anti-GCG (Sigma, 259A-18)), 1:1000 rabbit anti-CHGA (Abcam, ab15160), 1:200 rabbit anti-Ki67 (Abcam, ab16667), or 1:100 mouse anti-Synaptophysin (LSBio, LS-C174787). Secondary antibodies were diluted at 1:300 in ICC solution: anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A21203), anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A11001) anti-goat Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A11058), anti-rat Alexa Fluor (Invitrogen, A21208), anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (Invitrogen, A21207), or anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A21206).

Flow cytometry

To detect EGFP, stem cells were treated with DOX for 48 hr. The cells were then single-cell-dispersed using TrypLE and washed with PBS. The cells were centrifuged at 1000 g for three min. The PBS was decanted, and Zombie Violet Viability Kit (BioLegend, 423113) was added to the cells at a 1:2000 dilution. The cells were incubated in the dark for ten min at room temperature. To neutralize the zombie staining, 1 mL of MACS buffer (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-376) was added to the cells. The cells were then resuspended in 300-μL of MACS buffer and filtered through a 5-mL polystyrene round-bottom tube (Falcon, 352235). The cells were run on a Cytek Northern Lights. For the positive zombie control, before staining, we incubated the cells at 65 °C for 10 min to induce cell death. The gating strategy included gating on single cells and live cells, excluding cells that stained positive for Zombie Violet.

Real-time PCR

SC-islets were collected, and RNA was extracted using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74106) and RNase-Free DNase (Qiagen, 79254). cDNA was made using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Applied Biosystems, 4368813) and a thermocycler (Applied Biosystems, A37028). The cDNA, primers, and PowerUP SYBR Green Master Mix for qPCR (Applied Biosystems, A25742) were combined and run on a QuantStudio 6 Pro (Applied Biosystems). TBP was used as the housekeeping gene to normalize the data, and the results were analyzed using ΔΔCt methodology. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

SC-islet differentiation

The differentiation of SC-islets followed a published protocol.7,18 In short, undifferentiated HUES8 (5 × 107) were seeded onto a T-75 flask (TPP, 90076) coated with Matrigel. mTesR1 supplemented with 10-µM Y-27632 was used to maintain the stem cells. The following day, the cells were treated with the differentiation media and factors listed in Tables S2 and S3. On Stage 6, Day 7, the cells were single-cell dispersed using TryPLE; 5 × 106 cells were seeded into a single well of a 6-well plate and placed on an orbi-shaker rotating at 100 RPM in a 5% CO2 37 °C incubator. On Stage 6, Day 9, to activate the genes of interest, DOX was added to the cell media at 5 μg/mL.

Whole-genome CRISPR screen

Viral titers were determined by functional puromycin resistance. The HUES8-VPR cell line was seeded at 500 000 cells/well in a 6-well plate and transduced with serial dilutions of the concentrated viral stock. Forty-eight hours post-transduction, cells were selected with 0.4 µg/mL puromycin for 3 days. The viral titer (TU/mL) was calculated by comparing cell counts in puromycin-treated wells with those in untreated parallel control wells. Over 500 million HUES8-VPR cells were infected at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.2. Following a 48-h incubation, the cell population was selected with 0.4 µg/mL puromycin to generate a stable library-transduced population. The cell pool was then expanded, maintaining a minimum of 100 million cells at all passages to ensure >500-fold library representation.

The cells were then differentiated into SC-islets. At the end of Stage 6, the differentiated cell population was split. A portion was harvested to serve as the day 0 control sample, controlling for any representation changes that may occur during the differentiation process itself. Twenty NSG mice were transplanted with 10 × 106 differentiated SC-islet cells into the kidney capsule. After 10 days, mice were euthanized, the kidney grafts were excised, and DNA was extracted for next-generation sequencing performed by Cellecta. The resulting sgRNA read counts were analyzed using the Model-based Analysis of Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 Knockout (MAGeCK)19 methods to identify sgRNAs that were significantly enriched or depleted in the final harvested grafts relative to the differentiated T = 0 control population.

Mouse transplantation studies

All animal studies followed all Washington University in St. Louis’s Animal Care and Use Committee regulations. Mice were randomly assigned to experimental groups. NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ (NSG) male mice were ordered from Jackson Laboratories (005557). Mice were anesthetized and injected with 5 × 106 cells for kidney capsule transplant and 1 × 107 cells for subcutaneous and muscle transplantations. Kidney capsule transplants were performed using a 25G butterfly needle (TERUMO, SV*25BLK). Intramuscular transplants were injected into the left thigh muscle using a Gastight syringe (Hamilton, 7662-01). Subcutaneous transplants were injected on the left side of the back at the midline with a 27G needle (BD, 309623). For both control and experimental groups, the same batch of SC-islets was used to avoid the influence of batch-to-batch variability. For 2 weeks post-transplantation, the experimental group was provided drinking water that had DOX while the control group did not. To induce diabetes, mice were injected for 5 days with Streptozotocin (STZ) at 45 mg of STZ/kg of mouse.

Blood glucose was measured using a Contour NEXT blood glucose Monitor (Bayer). For the glucose tolerance test, mice were fasted for 6 h and then injected with 3 g glucose/kg of mouse in 0.9% Sterile Saline (Moltox, 51-405022.052). Blood glucose was measured every 30 min for a total of 120 min. In vivo GSIS was measured by fasting mice and injecting them with glucose as previously mentioned, waiting 30 min, and collecting whole blood from the tail into microvettes (Sarstedt, 20.1280.100). The microvettes were centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was removed and stored at −80 °C. C-peptide was measured with the Ultrasensitive Human C-peptide ELISA (Mercodia, 10-1141-01).

Generation of HUES8-VPR FCAMR, SIX3, and GSTM3 cell lines

The HUES8-VPR cell line was seeded onto a Matrigel (Corning, 354277)-coated tissue culture flask (MidSci, TP90076). mTesR (Stem Cell, 85850) supplemented with 10-µM Y-27632 (Abcam, ab120129) was used to maintain the stem cells. When the cells were passaged, lentivirus containing the guide RNA (gRNA) was added to the cells at a multiplicity of infection of five. After the cells reached 80% confluency, the cells were passaged, and puromycin (1 µg/mL) was added to the media for two to three passages to select for cells containing our gRNA of interest. These cells were then used for SC-islet differentiation. The gRNA sequences are listed in Table S4.

Caspase3/7 cell viability

Stage 6, Day 9 SC-islets were treated with DOX until Stage 6, Day 14. On Stage 6, Day 14, SC-islets were dispersed using TryPLE and plated onto a 96-well white with clear bottom plate (Corning, 3903) at 40 000 cells per well. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, Cell Viability was measured using CellTiter-Fluor Cell Viability Assay (Promega, G6080), and Caspase-3/7 was measured using Caspase-Glo 3/7 Assay System (Promega, G8093). In short, the cells were washed with PBS, incubated in the viability assay reagents, and fluorescence was measured using the Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek). The cells were then incubated in the Caspase-Glo 3/7 reagents, and luminescence was measured on the Synergy H1 microplate reader.

Glucose-stimulated insulin secretion

Stage 6, Day 14 SC-islets were placed in Transwell inserts ­(MilliporeSigma, PIXP01250). The cells were then washed three times with Krebs-Ringer Bicarbonate (KRB) buffer, 128 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 2.7 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM MgSO4, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, 5 mM NaHCO3, 10 mM HEPES (Gibco, 15630-080), and 1% bovine serum albumin. The Transwells with the islets were then placed in 2 mM glucose KRB solution for 1 h to acclimate the cells. After 1 h, the islets were transferred to a new 2 mM glucose KRB solution (low glucose) for 1 h, and the supernatant was collected. The cells were then transferred to 20 mM glucose KRB solution (high glucose) for 1 h and the supernatant was collected. Cells were then single-cell-dispersed using TrypLE and counted using Vi-Cell XR (Beckman Coulter). Supernatants from low- and high-glucose samples were measured using the human insulin ELISA kit (ALPCO, 80-INSHU-E10.1). The cell counts were used to normalize the data.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

After differentiation and aggregation, on Stage 6, Day 9, half the cells were treated with DOX to activate the expression of FCAMR. On Stage 6, Day 19, the cells were single-cell-dispersed using TrypLE, suspended in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Fisher, 11-960-044), and submitted to the Genome Technology Access Center at Washington University for Illumina sequencing and library construction. The 10X single-cell RNA sequencing was prepped using the Chromium 3ʹ library and analysis on cellranger-8.0.1 and aligned to GRCh38-2020-A. The samples were sequenced using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. We used Seurat v5.3.020 to perform analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing. Cells with high gene counts (>10 000 total genes for FCAMR OE; >12 500 total genes for CTRL), low gene counts (<2000 for FCAMR OE and <2500 for CTRL), and cells with high mitochondrial genes (>25%) were filtered out. Each dataset was normalized using NormalizeData. The datasets were integrated using SelectIntegrationFeatures, FindIntegrationAnchors, and IntegrateData. To create the UMAPs we conducted ScaleData, RunPCA, RunUMAP (dims = 1:15), FindNeighbors (dims = 1:15), and FindClusters (resolution = 0.3). FindMarkers was used to identify clusters.

Results

Whole-genome CRISPR activation screen of transplanted SC-islets

To identify genes that promote the function of SC-islets after transplantation, we performed a whole-genome CRISPR activation screen (Figure 1A). We created a cell line with CRISPR activation elements by transfecting HUES8 stem cells with doxycycline-inducible TRE3G-dCas9-VPR-T2A-EGFP-CAG-TetON-NeoR-SA (dCas9-VPR) plasmid and 2 TALEN plasmids of AAVS1.21 The dCas9-VPR activating domain was integrated into the AAVS1 safe-harbor locus of HUES8 stem cells (HUES8-VPR). The expression of dCas9-VPR is under a doxycycline-inducible promoter, and when activated, it induces expression of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP). We confirmed HUES8-VPR system integration by the presence of EGFP (Figure 1B and C) and dCas9 (Figure 1D) in the stem cells after doxycycline (DOX) addition. We also confirmed that the integration of the CRISPR-activation elements did not negatively affect stem cell markers NANOG (Figure 1E) and OCT4 (Figure 1F).

To conduct the whole-genome screen, HUES8-VPR stem cells were transduced with a lentiviral library containing 18 915 gene targets (at least 5 single-guide RNAs [sgRNA] per gene target) and 3755 non-targeting control sgRNAs. There were 106 400 sgRNA sequences in total. Following puromycin purification, the resulting cell population was differentiated into SC-islets (Figure 1A). Gene activation was induced by the addition of DOX at the end of the differentiation before the transplantation into the kidney capsule of NSG immunodeficient mice. The SC-islet grafts were recovered 10 days post-transplantation and sent for next-generation sequencing to quantify the representation of each sgRNA.

The sgRNA counts from the in vivo samples were compared against those from a day 0 pre-transplantation control group. The day 0 control group had 99.96% of all guides detectable, with every gene represented, and at least 3 guides targeting each gene. To identify genes that may improve SC-islet transplantation outcomes, we used Robust Rank Aggregation.19 This method identified the top 1% of genes that were significantly enriched in the transplanted population compared to control (Figure 1G). Gene ontology (GO) analysis of the top 1% of enriched genes indicated a significant overrepresentation of genes associated with fundamental biological pathways ­(Figure 1H). These included cellular process (GO: 0009987), metabolic process (GO: 0008152), and catalytic activity (GO: 0003824). Among the top 10 candidate genes identified in the screen, we prioritized 3 candidates for detailed functional assessment based on biological rationale, enrichment strength, and feasibility: SIX3, FCAMR, and GSTM3 (Figure 1I). We selected SIX3, our top-ranking hit, because it has an established role in pancreatic islet maturation and function,22 suggesting it could influence SC-islet adaptation to the transplantation environment, though its role in transplantation has not been investigated. We selected FCAMR because, as a cell surface immune receptor,23 it represents a druggable target with potential translational relevance, despite having no prior connection to islet biology or transplantation. Finally, we chose GSTM3 because it functions in glutathione metabolism and reactive oxygen species detoxification,24 pathways highly relevant to transplant-associated oxidative stress and early graft loss. While other highly ranked genes were identified, many had unclear functions in islet biology, ubiquitous housekeeping roles, or potential to disrupt islet differentiation. Our selection strategy prioritized genes where overexpression would not compromise islet identity and provide transplant-specific benefits.

In vitro characterization of FCAMR, GSTM3, and SIX3

To further investigate the roles of FCAMR, GSTM3, and SIX3 in vitro, we utilized the HUES8-VPR stem cells we previously created. Three distinct lentiviral constructs, each containing a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting one of the genes of interest, were transduced into the HUES8-VPR cell line. This was done to overexpress the corresponding gene. These engineered stem cells were then differentiated into SC-islets (Figure 2A). Upon DOX treatment, qPCR analysis confirmed successful target gene activation in SC-islets. This analysis revealed a 2.64-fold increase in FCAMR expression, a 2.81-fold increase for GSTM3, and a 1.95-fold increase for SIX3 relative to no DOX controls (Figure 2B).

To assess the impact of this overexpression on the SC-islet phenotype, we evaluated gene expression, apoptosis, and function. Transcriptional analysis of FCAMR-overexpressing SC-islets revealed no significant changes in key pancreatic identity or cellular stress genes (Figure 2C). While GSTM3 overexpression resulted in elevated PDX1 expression, other islet markers were unaffected. In contrast, SIX3 overexpression led to a significant reduction in INS (insulin) gene expression. Importantly, no increase in apoptosis was detected in any of the overexpression lines, as determined by a caspase 3/7 assay (Figure 2D). A functional assessment using glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS) showed that FCAMR and GSTM3 overexpression did not affect islet performance. However, SIX3-overexpressing SC-islets exhibited impaired glucose sensing, characterized by elevated insulin secretion in low glucose conditions with no compensatory increase in high glucose conditions (Figure 2E). The reduced INS expression and increased insulin secretion in low glucose could indicate that SIX3 promotes inappropriate secretion or degranulation of stored insulin without maintaining insulin biosynthesis. The in vitro analysis demonstrated that the genes of interest had a minimal effect on the SC-islets, suggesting the genetic modifications are well tolerated without compromising SC-islet phenotype.

FCAMR overexpression reduces blood glucose and increases C-peptide in SC-islet transplanted mice

To evaluate the function of the engineered SC-islets in vivo, we transplanted 10 million SC-islet cells subcutaneously into mice. The subcutaneous space is more clinically relevant compared to mouse kidney capsule transplants. However, this site is typically associated with poor engraftment16 and is insufficient to reverse hyperglycemia.17 We selected this challenging site to set a high threshold for success, reasoning that any significant improvement in glycemic control or graft function would be attributable to the genetically engineered SC-islets. Mice were randomly assigned to two groups for each gene: a control group transplanted with cells not treated with DOX and an experimental group transplanted with cells overexpressing the gene of interest. The mice transplanted with cells treated with DOX were also provided drinking water with DOX for 2 weeks post-transplantation.

Monitoring blood glucose levels revealed that at 9 weeks post-transplantation, FCAMR overexpression led to significantly lower blood glucose levels of 132 ± 3.8 mg/dL compared to control levels of 144.4 ± 3.2 mg/dL (Figure 3A). No significant changes were observed for GSTM3 or SIX3 at this end time point (Figure 3B and C). However, SIX3 did induce transient reductions in blood glucose levels three weeks post-transplantation (110.4 ± 3 mg/dL) compared to control mice (123.6 ± 1.2 mg/dL), but this reduction was not sustained (Figure 3C). Next, we tested the glucose-responsiveness of the grafts by measuring human C-peptide levels, which is indicative of insulin secretion, in the mouse serum after fasting the mice for 6 h and a 30-min glucose challenge. Mice with SC-islet cells that overexpress FCAMR showed increases in C-peptide secretion of 124.3 ± 13.09 pmol/L compared to 84.6 ± 8.6 pmol/L in the control group (Figure 3D). There were no significant differences in C-peptide secretion in mice overexpressing GSTM3 or SIX3 (Figure 3E and F). These results suggest that the overexpression of FCAMR has the potential to improve subcutaneous SC-islet transplantation by increasing insulin secretion, as indicated by lower blood glucose levels and elevated C-peptide levels.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis of FCAMR-overexpressing SC-islets

FCAMR is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein that acts as a receptor for immunoglobulin A (IgA) and immunoglobulin M (IgM).25 This receptor is mostly found on immune cells, such as B-cells and follicular dendritic cells, and is not endogenously expressed in pancreatic islets23; therefore, the ectopic expression of FCAMR could have implications for local inflammatory response. To further investigate FCAMR overexpression in SC-islets, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing to understand the second-order effects this modulation might have on the transcriptome of the SC-islets.

SC-islets engineered to overexpress FCAMR (FCAMR OE) were compared to control islets (CTRL) that do not express FCAMR. Integration of the two datasets, CTRL and FCAMR OE, revealed seven distinct populations (Figure 4A and B). Across conditions, the cell type populations were consistent (Figure 1a). We identified these populations by the expression of canonical marker genes (Figure 4C), which revealed the expected major endocrine cell types: β-cells, characterized by the expression of INS and ERO1B, α-cells identified by GCG and TTR expression, and δ-cells marked by the expression of SST and the transcription factor HHEX. In addition to these major endocrine cells, we identified a population of enterochromaffin (EC) cells expressing TPH1 and SLC18A1, a small cluster of proliferating cells marked by levels of MKI67 and PCNA, which has previously been reported,26–29 and a population of cells expressing markers of UPR stress.

We found slight differences in cell type distributions between the CTRL and FCAMR OE groups (Figure 4D). While β-cells, enterochromaffin cells (EC-cells), stressed cells, and the proliferating population showed very slight variations, we found that α-cells decreased by 1.84% and δ-cells by 2.32% in the FCAMR OE population compared to the control. The polyhormonal population increased by 4.26% in the FCAMR OE population compared to control cells (Figure 4D). Within this population, we found upregulation of BMP3 (Figure 1b), a member of the TGF-beta superfamily that is a known regulator of cellular differentiation. Its upregulation in this population suggests a potential role in altering endocrine cell fate within the FCAMR OE SC-islets.

Our analysis revealed slight differences in gene expression between FCAMR OE islets and CTRL, with specific genes being regulated in various endocrine cell populations ­(Figure 4E–G, Figure 1b-e). A notable and widespread change was the upregulation of metallothionein-1G (MT1G) in β-, α-, EC, and polyhormonal cells. Metallothioneins are cysteine-rich proteins that play a crucial role in zinc homeostasis and can protect against oxidative stress. Reductions in oxidative stress could improve islet survival post-transplantation. In addition, MT1G was found to have higher expression in adult β-cells compared to fetal cells.30 β-cells exhibited an enrichment of genes associated with metal binding by metallothioneins ­(Figure 4E). In α-cells, we observed upregulation of ghrelin (GHRL), a hormone that stimulates appetite,31 and downregulation of HSPA5, a heat shock protein involved in cellular stress response and protein folding (Figure 4F). Furthermore, in EC cells, we identified upregulation of PPP1R3A, known to play a role in glycogen metabolism (Figure 1c). In δ-cells, FOXO1 is upregulated and known to play a role in insulin signaling (Figure 4G). In the stressed cell population, TNFRSF10B is upregulated in the FCAMR OE population and is a receptor involved in apoptosis (Figure 1d). In the proliferating cell population, SYN3 and IGF2 are upregulated ­(Figure 1e). Across all cell types, there are no significant differences in stress-associated genes between CTRL and FCAMR OE (Figure S1f), which reflects the previous real-time PCR data (Figure 2C). This data suggests that FCAMR OE does not have a large negative effect on differentiation, function, or the overall stress levels of the cells.

FCAMR induces weight gain in diabetic mice

To further investigate FCAMR in vivo, we transplanted FCAMR OE SC-islets into NSG mice. The transplantations were performed in 2 distinct locations: the kidney capsule and the intramuscular tissue of the hind leg. The kidney capsule is highly vascularized and is a common site for mouse transplantation. The hind leg muscle is potentially a more translatable location (Figure 5A, E). For comparison, we included a control group (CTRL) that did not receive DOX and was transplanted with SC-islets, as well as a non-transplant control group. The FCAMR OE transplanted mice received DOX in their water for 2 weeks post-transplantation. To understand how the transplanted islets respond to different metabolic environments and to give the islets more time to mature in vivo, we evaluated the mice pre-diabetes and post-diabetes induction. Five weeks post-transplantation, we induced diabetes in the mice through five days of streptozotocin (STZ) injections, which has been shown to have very little toxicity to the human transplanted cells32,33 but is toxic to the mouse β-cells.34 We confirmed diabetes by increases in blood glucose levels in our non-transplanted control mice (Figure 5B, F). For both transplantation sites, we used the same cohort of non-transplanted control mice.

Our initial assessment of graft function, determined by measuring circulating human C-peptide in the mouse serum, revealed no significant difference between FCAMR OE and CTRL at either transplantation site (Figure 5C, G). This indicates that FCAMR OE did not inherently impair the engraftment function of the SC-islets. Weekly blood glucose levels showed transient changes that were location dependent. In the kidney capsule, FCAMR OE mice had significantly lower blood glucose at week 2 post-transplantation, with an average blood glucose of 134.3 ± 4.8 mg/dL compared to the CTRL group’s 156.8 ± 8.4 mg/dL (Figure 5B). The early glycemic advantage observed in kidney-transplanted FCAMR OE group was not sustained. After diabetes induction, there is no significant difference in blood glucose between FCAMR OE and CTRL in the kidney (Figure 5B). When transplanted into the hind leg muscle, the FCAMR OE islets were associated with a higher blood glucose level at week 3, 146 ± 4.4 mg/dL while CTRL levels were 124 ± 8.8 mg/dL (Figure 5F). However, after diabetes induction, there is no significant difference in blood glucose when cells are transplanted into the hind leg muscle (Figure 5F). Overall, throughout the experiment, the blood glucose levels of the mice between FCAMR OE and CTRL mice in either location were transient, suggesting that blood glucose was not meaningfully affected by FCAMR OE.

Interestingly, in both the kidney and muscle, mice with FCAMR OE had greater weight throughout the experiment (Figure 5D, H). Before the transplantations, there was no significant difference in weight among the 3 groups, CTRL, FCAMR OE, and no-transplant, in either transplantation location (Figure 5D, H). However, in muscle transplant mice, FCAMR OE mice were heavier than CTRL mice after the induction of diabetes at week 6 post-transplantation (30 ± 3 g vs 26 ± 1 g) (Figure 5H). Notably, while the weights of FCAMR OE mice remained stable in the muscle transplant between week 0 (29.0 ± 0.7 g) and week 6 (30.3 ± 1.2 g), the weights of the CTRL mice slightly decreased from week 0 (27.3 ± 0.8 g) to week 6 (26.1 ± 0.5 g) post-transplantation. This finding of weight loss in the CTRL group was not observed in the kidney capsule transplants. Interestingly, in the kidney transplants, we found no significant differences in the weights of CTRL mice from week 0 to week 11, whereas FCAMR OE mice experienced significant weight gain, increasing from 28.4 ± 1.1 g at week 0 to 33.5 ± 0.8 g at week 13 (Figure 5D). In kidney transplantation, at week 11, FCAMR OE mice weighed 32.9 ± 0.6 g which is significantly more than the CTRL group at 27.4 ± 0.9 g. This difference in weight is also seen in the muscle transplants with FCAMR OE, weighing 31.3 ± 1.2 g, and CTRL, weighing 28 ± 0.5 g. Both FCAMR OE and CTRL mice grafts showed expression of human C-peptide, Chromogranin A (CHGA), glucagon (GCG), and somatostatin (SST) in the kidney capsule (Figure 5I). In addition, FCAMR OE and CTRL mice had no indication of proliferating cells in the graft (Ki67) (Figure 5I). These results indicate that FCAMR OE has a significant effect on the weight of mice in both kidney and muscle transplantation sites, while not affecting blood glucose or C-peptide measurements.

Discussion

While cell replacement therapy using SC-islets holds immense promise for treating diabetes,35 optimizing graft outcomes remains a critical challenge,36,37 particularly when addressing patient heterogeneity38 and varying transplantation sites. Systematic approaches to identify genetic modifications that could enhance SC-islet transplantation have been limited. Here, we demonstrate the feasibility of using a whole-genome CRISPR activation screening to identify genes that are enriched after transplantation of SC-islets, providing an unbiased platform for discovering genetic modifiers of transplantation outcomes.

Our screen, conducted in kidney capsule transplants, identified multiple candidate genes. We selected 3 for deeper investigation: FCAMR, SIX3, and GSTM3. The kidney capsule, while a standard preclinical transplantation site in mice, is not clinically translatable for human therapy. Therefore, we chose to conduct our initial screen of these three genes using a subcutaneous transplant model. The subcutaneous space is known to be a suboptimal environment for SC-islet function, which provides a key advantage for this study: any functional improvements conferred by our candidate genes would be more readily apparent against this challenging baseline. While acknowledging the environmental differences from the kidney capsule, this model served as a platform to dissect the contributions of our genes of interest.

We further investigated 3 such candidates, FCAMR, SIX3, and GSTM3. We first characterized these three genes in vitro. We found that overexpression of these genes did not induce caspase 3/7 activity, caused minimal changes to glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, and maintained expression of key islet markers. These results were expected given that these candidates emerged from a transplantation screen rather than an in vitro functional screen, and it confirmed that the genetic modifications were well-tolerated.

When we transplanted mice with SC-islets that overexpress our genes of interest, SIX3 and GSTM3 showed no significant differences compared to control mice. FCAMR overexpression, however, produced distinct outcomes depending on the transplantation site and metabolic context. In the subcutaneous space, the most clinically relevant but challenging site, FCAMR overexpressing SC-islets significantly improved graft function by reduced blood glucose levels and increased C-peptide secretion. This data represents improvements at a transplantation site where SC-islets typically perform poorly.

Unexpectedly, when SC-islets overexpressing FCAMR were transplanted into the kidney capsule or intramuscular tissue we observed no significant differences in blood glucose or C-peptide measurements compared to control mice. However, mice receiving FCAMR-modified grafts maintained significantly higher body weight throughout the post-diabetic period compared to control mice. Weight loss if a feature of poorly controlled T1D,39 suggesting that FCAMR-overexpressing grafts may provide metabolic benefits. Of note, we also observed no overgrowths or tumor formations in any of the FCAMR OE or CTRL mice. Furthermore, while injecting STZ after transplantation is unlikely to have harmed the SC-β-cells,32,33 further studies transplanting SC-islets with FCAMR overexpression into mice with pre-existing diabetes should be performed.

The mechanisms underlying FCAMR’s effects remain to be fully elucidated. FCAMR is a type I transmembrane glycoprotein that functions as a receptor for IgA and IgM, predominantly expressed on immune cells23,25 and is not known to be endogenously expressed in pancreatic islets. Our single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that FCAMR overexpression induced upregulation of ghrelin (GHRL) in α-cells. GHRL is known to stimulate food intake40 and promote weight gain.41 Potentially, the observed weight gain is from FCAMR-overexpressing grafts secreting ghrelin systemically, driving increased food intake. The mechanism linking FCAMR to ghrelin upregulation could involve FCAMR-mediated signaling cascades that activate transcriptional programs regulating GHRL expression, or alternatively, FCAMR may influence α-cell differentiation status, which would be consistent with our observation of slight increases in polyhormonal cells and upregulation of BMP3, a TGF-β superfamily member involved in cell fate decisions. In the present study, we did not measure food intake, whole-body metabolic rates, fat and protein mass, or circulating ghrelin levels, which would be needed to confirm this hypothesis. FCAMR overexpression in SC-islets may have a more systemic effect on the mice rather than a localized effect, which would need to be further investigated in future studies. FCAMR endogenous expression is very low, so overexpression could induce a paracrine effect on the surrounding tissue.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, we used exclusively male mice due to the difficulty in inducing diabetes through STZ injection in female mice.42 Second, by transducing the whole-genome screen at the stem cell stage, we risk partial gene silencing during the differentiation, potentially introducing noise into our screen and obscuring the identification of hits. Third, the magnitude of any potential benefit of FCAMR over expression, while statistically significant, was modest and may be difficult to detect against the background of other metabolic changes such as weight gain. Fourth, we did not directly assess graft survival, mass, or vascularization in this study, measurements that would more directly address transplantation efficacy. Therefore, we cannot definitively determine whether the improved glucose control in subcutaneous transplants is reflective of enhanced cell survival, improved per-cell function, or both mechanisms. Finally, the unexpected weight gain phenotype, while potentially beneficial, complicates interpretation and highlights that the consequences of genetic modifications can extend beyond the transplanted cells themselves to influence systemic physiology.

Despite these limitations, our study makes several important contributions. First, we demonstrate that whole-genome CRISPR activation screens can be successfully performed in transplanted SC-islets, establishing a platform for unbiased discovery of gene candidates. This approach is consistent with prior uses of CRISPR screening in stem cell contexts43 and extends it to a complex in vivo transplantation setting. Second, we identified FCAMR as a gene candidate to improve SC-islet function in a challenging subcutaneous transplantation site and can influence host metabolism in other contexts. While the mechanism remains unclear, these findings open new avenues for investigations into how immune receptors might be leveraged to improve cell therapy outcomes. Third, our work illustrates the complexity of transplantation biology. Genetic modifications can have transplantation site-dependent and context-dependent effects that are not always predictable from in vitro studies.

Several other strategies have been developed to improve islet transplantation through various methods. One technique is to encapsulate the SC-islets to protect the transplanted islets from immune rejection.44,45 This method includes both macroencapsulation and microencapsulation to protect the islets from immune rejection.46,47 Another approach involves the co-culturing of endothelial cells with SC-islets to promote vascularization.48,49 In addition, methods to improve islet health in vitro50–56 could potentially be applied to a transplantation context.
