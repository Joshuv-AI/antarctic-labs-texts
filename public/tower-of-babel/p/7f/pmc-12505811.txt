The shift in Mosquito-borne diseases incidence across Asia-Pacific region (1992–2021): insights from an Age-Period-Cohort analysis using the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Shenao Wei, Tao Zhang, Shijie Sun, Yuwei Liang, Wenxiong Zhou, Weidong Li, Shizhu Li, Manman Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeMosquito-borne diseases (MBDs) pose a significant threat in the Asia-Pacific region, encompassing both the Western Pacific and South-East Asia. This study is aimed at improving the understanding of the evolution of incidence patterns of MBDs within the Asia-Pacific region and supporting the development of effective health policies.MethodsCase numbers, incidence, and age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of MBDs were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021. Furthermore, an Age-Period-Cohort model was applied to characterize overall temporal trends in MBDs incidence and to assess the independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort. Decomposition analysis was used to quantify the extent to epidemiological changes, population growth, and aging contributed to the observed trends. Additional stratified analyses were performed by regions and country.ResultsFrom 1992 to 2021, the ASIR of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region displayed regional heterogeneity. Overall, the net drift of ASIR indicated an annual increase of 1.07% per year in the Western Pacific region, rising from 292.26 to 380.84 per 100,000 population. In contrast, the South-East Asia region experienced an annual decrease of 1.88% per year, with ASIR declining from 3,779.81 to 1,856.84 per 100,000 population. Meanwhile, driven by population growth and epidemiological changes, dengue gradually replaced malaria as the predominant MBD in both regions. Dengue cases increased from 1,082,887 to 3,839,091 in the Western Pacific region and from 14,326,313 to 32,627,815 in the South-East Asia region, far exceeding the corresponding malaria cases (2,387,112 and 5,784,641). Age-Period-Cohort analysis further revealed distinct epidemiological patterns: South-East Asia exhibited favorable period and cohort effects, while the Western Pacific showed an unfavorable trend.ConclusionThe intensifying threat of MBDs in the Western Pacific region underscores the need for highly responsive and adaptive public health strategies, supported by ongoing regional collaboration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24765-y.Keywords: Mosquito-borne diseases, Asia-Pacific region, Decomposition analysis, Age-Period-Cohort model

Background

Mosquito-borne diseases (MBDs) constitute a category of infectious diseases transmitted by mosquitoes, which serve as vectors carrying various pathogens from animals to humans or within humans [1]. These diseases are prevalent throughout a multitude of nations worldwide, representing a substantial threat to nearly half of the global population [2]. Furthermore, the expanding geographic reach and rising incidence of MBDs have raised considerable attention. In 2023, the global incidence of malaria reached an estimated 263 million, marking the rise of 11 million cases compared to 2022 and contributing to 597,000 fatalities [3]. Over the past half-century, the incidence of dengue has surged by 30 times, with approximately 390 million people infected annually and a mortality rate of 20% among severe cases [4]. The outbreak of Zika virus disease has further impacted over 100 million people across more than 70 countries [5]. Alarmingly, these diseases have re-emerged in regions that had previously eliminated MBDs, as illustrated by the reported cases of local malaria in the United States in 2023 [6]. These statistics underscore the persistent and alarming threat MBDs pose to global health.

The World Health Organization (WHO) has unequivocally stated that to safeguard global health security, it is imperative to deepen understanding of the mechanisms of MBDs transmission and to implement effective prevention and control measures. The WHO actively advocates for comprehensive surveillance and research globally, particularly tailored to diverse regions, to devise precise and unerring prevention and control strategies [3, 5]. Amid significant global challenges posed by MBDs, the Asia-Pacific region becomes a critical battleground for their control, due to its unique geographical, economic, and social development characteristics. The Asia-Pacific region, a vital contributor to the global economy, is located predominantly in tropical and subtropical zones [7]. Over the past three decades, the rapid transformations in land use, urbanization, and population mobility have potentially intensified the spread of MBDs in this region [4, 8]. Given that solutions to global challenges must be aligned with regional characteristics, focused attention on and in-depth study of the Asia-Pacific region are especially necessary and urgent. Additionally, investigating the epidemiology of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region can also afford valuable insights into the complex relationships between socioeconomic development and infectious disease patterns.

On the other hand, addressing the threat of MBDs also faces numerous challenges, including the scarcity of safe and efficacious pharmaceuticals and vaccines, shifts in public awareness and behavior, and the proliferation of resistance against insecticides [9–11]. To address these complex and formidable challenges, detailed research data is essential. A deep understanding of the incidence patterns and trends of MBDs across regions enables the strategic formulation of plans, accurate allocation of medical resources, and implementation of effective interventions. Consequently, this study used data from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021 to evaluate the incidence trends of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region. These findings aimed to provide critical evidence to enhance preventive and intervention strategies, ultimately reducing the impact of MBDs in the region.

Methods

Data sources

The GBD 2021 is an international collaborative project that assesses the burden of 371 diseases, injuries, and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories [12]. This comprehensive analysis encompasses a wide range of metrics, including incidence, mortality, prevalence, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [13]. GBD 2021 utilizes multiple distinct data sources, including scientific literature, government websites, statistical yearbooks, epidemiological surveillance data, household surveys, disease registries, clinical information and other relevant materials [14]. Detailed information can be found online via the GBD 2021 Sources Tool (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021/sources).

GBD 2021 utilized the DisModMR2.1 to estimate epidemiological parameters. This tool models disease incidence using negative binomial regression and is fitted via Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithm [15]. The modeling process consists of four steps: (1) integrating raw data on epidemiological parameters and adjusting for methodological distinctions; (2) internal consistency checks were conducted on incidence estimates; (3) to derive internally consistent epidemiological estimates, data from all parameters were synthesized with previous outputs, while also propagating the underlying source uncertainties; and (4) estimating values for countries or regions with sparse or missing data based on available information, thereby enabling comprehensive global assessment. All estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty interval (95%UI), derived from the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of 1,000 draws form the posterior distribution generated by the MCMC algorithm [16]. The GBD study adheres to the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER) statement [17].

Disease selection and case definition

In compliance with the International Classification of Diseases-10 (ICD-10), this study focused on four major MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region included in the GBD2021 database, selected for their complete time-series estimates, independently extractable incidence data, and high disease burden. These diseases include malaria (B50–B54), dengue (A90–A91.9), yellow fever (A95–A95.9), and the Zika virus disease (U06-U06.9). GBD2021 adheres strictly to ICD-10 clinical diagnostic criteria for case definitions, and each episode meeting the clinical criteria is considered a distinct event. Disease incidence was calculated based on cause-specific observation periods: if the same patient received multiple diagnoses for the same cause within the observation window, only the first episode was counted; diagnoses beyond this period were treated as new independent events. Incidence estimates were calibrated at the population level using a negative binomial distribution model. ICD-based cause stratification mapping and comorbidity correction (COMO) methods were applied to attribute and separate mixed infections, thereby minimizing overestimation due to recurrent and co-infections and enhancing the accuracy of the estimates [16]. Other MBDs (e.g., Japanese encephalitis, chikungunya, and filariasis) were excluded from this analytic framework due to classification constraints and the absence of continuous incidence data. Based on the above criteria, we extracted data of the four diseases from the Global Health Data Exchange (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), following the methodology outlined in previous studies [18, 19].

Study population

This study focuses on the population affected by MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region. In GBD 2021, the Asia-Pacific region comprises the Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions delineated by WHO. The South-East Asia region comprises 11 countries, including the Democratic People’s Republic of Korea, Bhutan, India, Indonesia, Bangladesh, Maldives, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand and Timor-Leste [20]. The Western Pacific region comprises 31 countries, including American Samoa, Australia, Brunei Darussalam, Cambodia, China, Cook Islands, Fiji, Guam, Japan, Kiribati, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, Malaysia, Marshall Islands, Micronesia (Federated States of), Mongolia, Nauru, New Zealand, Niue, Northern Mariana Islands, Palau, Papua New Guinea, Philippines, Republic of Korea, Samoa, Singapore, Solomon Islands, Tokelau, Tonga, Tuvalu, Vanuatu, Viet Nam [21].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis

To comprehensively characterize the incidence trends of MBDs, this study conducted descriptive analyses at national and regional levels. Drawing on data from GBD2021, we assessed cases, incidence, age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), and 95%UI in both sexes, males, and females across 42 Asia-Pacific countries. These data visually illustrate the trends in MBDs incidence within the region during 1992–2021. Meanwhile, we used ASIR as the basis for cross-disease and cross-region comparisons, thereby eliminating the influence of population age structure differences and improving comparability across diseases and regions.

Trends analysis

The study utilized an Age-Period-Cohort (APC) model to assess the influence of age, period, and birth cohort on the temporal trends of MBDs incidence [22]. Due to the inevitable identification problem caused by the multicollinearity among age, period and cohort, it is difficult to attain the unique estimation set for the three factors effects. To address the issue, an Age-Period-Cohort model incorporating the Intrinsic Estimator (IE) method using principal component regression was applied to distinguish the distinct effects across the three temporal trends and yield relatively efficient parameter estimation [23]. The Age-Period-Cohort model incorporating IE method can be formulated as the follows: , in which represents the incidence within group (); represents the model intercept; , , and refer to the age effect in the -th age group, period effect in the -th period group, and birth cohort effect in the -th birth cohort group, respectively; and indicates the model residual [24]. The primary parameters of the APC model include net drift and local drift. Net drift represents the annual percentage change in the numerical values of incidence after adjusting for period and cohort effects, while local drift denotes the annual percentage change in the numerical values of incidence within specific age groups [25]. Furthermore, the APC model produces the estimated longitudinal age-specific rates within the reference cohort, referred to as the age effect. The effects related to period and cohort are delineated through relative risks, computed as the ratio of the age-specific rates in each period/cohort to those of the reference period/cohort [26]. In this study, incidence along with their 95%UI and corresponding population data from each country in Asia-Pacific region served as the primary inputs for the APC model. Age group was categorized into 5-year intervals, spanning from 0 to 4 to over 95. The study period (1992–2021) was divided into six 5-year intervals, and twenty-five overlapping 10-year birth cohorts to facilitate the estimation of age, period and cohort. The model outputs (net/local drift, age, period, cohort effects and relative risks) were all reported with 95% confidence intervals (CI), which was derived from standard errors estimated under the IE method to quantify the statistical precision of the APC parameter estimates [27]. The APC analysis was conducted using the APC Web Tool, an online application developed by the National Cancer Institute (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/).

Decomposition analysis

To examine the factors influencing changes in the incidence of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region from 1992 to 2021, this study employed the methodology developed by Das Gupta to decompose the effects of population growth, aging, and epidemiological change [28]. This method uses algebraic decomposition techniques to break down the overall change into contributions from these three components, allowing for identification of the specific contribution of each factor. The observed net change is the sum of these three factors. Decomposition analyses employed counterfactual frameworks to calculate the absolute changes and relative contributions attributable to each factor. Here, epidemiological change refers to any residual variation in case numbers after adjusting for population and age structure, including changes driven by unmeasured factors such as vector transmission efficiency, climatic fluctuations, and socioeconomic conditions [29]. All data processing, statistical analyses, and visualizations were conducted in R software (vision 4.3.3), utilizing relevant packages such as dplyr, ggplot2, and others.

Results

Incidence trends in MBDs in regions from 1992 to 2021

In the Western Pacific region, MBDs cases increased from 4,659,534 individuals (95%UI: 2,667,913 to 7,948,574) in 1992 to 6,226,203 individuals (95%UI: 4,282,656 to 10,707,318) in 2021. The ASIR rose from 292.26 to 380.84 per 100,000 population. Conversely, in the South-East Asia region, MBDs cases decreased from 52,020,861 individuals (95%UI: 25,037,577 to 92,194,666) in 1992 to 38,412,456 individuals (95%UI: 7,023,756 to 78,492,115) in 2021. The ASIR decreased from 3779.81 to 1856.84 per 100,000 population. The net drift value revealed an annual increase of 1.07% in Western Pacific region and a 1.88% annual decrease in South-East Asia region between 1992 and 2021 (Table 1). Profile of MBDs incidence across countries in Asia-Pacific region

In 2021, the five countries with the highest ASIR across 42 countries in Asia-Pacific region included Solomon Islands (27,626.90 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 24,020.38 to 32,802.80), Papua New Guinea (19,203.55 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 16,758.33 to 21,900.55), Tonga (14,363.29 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 136.46 to 50,916.52), Marshall Islands (10,324.23 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 1060.09 to 49,396.09), and Singapore (8714.74 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 1927.54 to 20,177.45), all of which are situated within the Western Pacific region. In the South-East Asia region, the three countries with the highest ASIR are Maldives (5095.16 per 100,000 population, 95% UI: 0 to 18,143.76), India (2295.37 per 100,000 population, 95%UI: 213.05 to 5039.90) and Sri Lanka (2106.45 per 100,000 population, 95%UI: 552.08 to 7025.59). From 1992 to 2021, the five countries that experienced the most substantial changes in incidence were Democratic People’s Republic of Korea (net drift: 18.95% per year, 95% CI 2.95–37.43%), Tonga (net drift:17.56% per year, 95% CI 16.90–18.22%), Bhutan (net drift: -17.28% per year, 95% CI −19.50% to −15.00%), Timor-Leste (net drift: −12.29% per year, 95% CI −12.93% to −11.64%), and Maldives (net drift: 10.22% per year, 95% CI 9.67–10.76%). The evolution of diseases spectrum of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region

The primary MBDs still were malaria and dengue in the Asia-Pacific region. Nevertheless, dengue has progressively replaced malaria as the predominant MBD in the region (Fig. 3A, B). A further regional analysis was conducted. In the Western Pacific region, Dengue cases increased from 1,082,887 individuals (95%UI: 297,726 to 2,028,093) in 1992 to 3,839,091individuals (95%UI: 2,195,749 to 7,991,613) in 2021, with the ASIR rising from 69.21 to 221.20 per 100,000 population. Malaria cases decreased from 3,576,647 individuals (95%UI: 2,370,187 to 5,920,480) in 1992 to 2,387,112 individuals (95%UI: 2,086,908 to 2,715,705) in 2021, with ASIR dropping from 223.05 to 159.64 per 100,000 population. In the South-East Asia region, the cases caused by dengue increased from 14,326,313 individuals (95%UI: 690,728 to 29,479,694) in 1992 to 32,627,815 individuals (95%UI: 3,934,496 to 65,804,731), with ASIR rising from 1,055.09 to 1,584.25 per 100,000 population. The cases caused by Malaria decreased form 37,694,548 individuals (95%UI: 24,346,850 to 62,714,972) in 1992 to 5,784,641 individuals (95%UI: 3,089,259 to 12,687,384) in 2021, with the ASIR dropping from 2,724.72 to 272.59 per 100,000 population (Supplementary Table S4).

This research further conducted a decomposition analysis to investigate the driving forces behind the incidence of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region. The results revealed that the increasing trends of MBDs within the Western Pacific region were primarily attributed to epidemiological factors and population growth, whereas aging exerted a mitigating effect. On the other hand, the decline trends of MBDs in South-East Asia was mainly due to epidemiological factors and aging, while population growth had an opposing effect. Regarding major diseases, the reduction in malaria cases was largely attributed to epidemiological factors and aging, while population growth exerted an opposing effect. In contrast, the increase in dengue fever cases was primarily driven by epidemiological factors and population growth, whereas aging mitigated this trend (Fig. 3C, D and Supplementary Table S5).

Local drifts and age-specific incidence of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region

In the Western Pacific region, the incidence of MBDs exhibited an increasing trend across the majority of age groups (from ages 5–9 to 80–84). The most significant rise was observed in the 25–29 age group (local drift: 1.92%, 95% CI 1.70–2.15%). In contrast, in South-East Asia region, most age groups exhibited a declining trend, although this trend diminished as age progresses. The most substantial reduction in incidence was observed in the 0–4 age group (local drift: −2.66%, 95% CI −3.07% to −2.24%) (Fig. 4A, B and Table S6 in the supplementary materials). For specific diseases, malaria was on a downward trend across all age groups, while dengue was on an upward trend, as shown in Supplementary Figures S1 and S2.

Age, period and cohort effects on MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region

Age effects: in the Western Pacific region, the infection risk decreased initially with age, reaching its lowest point at the 55–59 years age group (164.04 per 100,000 population, 95%CI 151.94 to 177.10), and then increased. Contrastingly, in the South-East Asia region, the infection risk initially rose with age, then decreased, and subsequently increased again. The highest incidence occurred in the 5–9 years age group (6044.01 per 100,000 population, 95% CI 5,591.48 to 6,533.17), whereas the lowest incidence was observed in the 80–84 years age group (1,315.21 per 100,000 population, 95% CI 1,073.52 to 1,611.32). Period effects: the reference period was set as 2002–2006. In Western Pacific region, the relative period risk steadily increased after 2007–2011, reaching its peak during 2017–2021 (RR: 1.32, 95%CI 1.25 to 1.38). Conversely, individuals residing in the South-East Asia region exhibited a decreased relative period risk, reaching its lowest point during 2017–2021 (RR: 0.75, 95%CI 0.71 to 0.79). Cohort effect: The birth cohort of 1957–1966 was designated as the reference cohort. Populations of the Western Pacific region experienced an escalating relative risk until the 1997–2006 cohort (RR: 1.77, 95% CI 1.65 to 1.91). Despite a continual decline in relative cohort risk for individuals born post-2002, the risk for the 2012–2021 birth cohort remained higher than that of the reference cohort. Conversely, in the South-East Asia region, individuals born in the 1907–1916 cohort exhibited the highest risk (RR: 2.07, 95% CI 1.39 to 3.08). Subsequently, the relative risk decreased until the 2012–2021 cohort, which showed the lowest risk (RR: 0.24, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.28) (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables S8–S10). Upon in-depth analysis of specific diseases, we observed that the incidence of malaria in the Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions demonstrated favorable age-period-cohort effects. In contrast, the incidence of dengue exhibited detrimental age-period-cohort effects (Supplementary Figures S3, S4 and Tables S8–S10).

Discussion

This study provides novel insights on the evolution on MBDs incidence patterns in Asia-Pacific region from 1992 to 2021. During the study period, the incidence of MBDs demonstrated regional heterogeneity, with an increasing trend in Western Pacific region and a decreasing trend in South-East Asia region. Concurrently, dengue has progressively replaced malaria as the predominant MBD in Asia-Pacific region. This shift was largely attributable to epidemiological change and population growth, with this trend especially pronounced in Western Pacific region. Furthermore, the APC model revealed adverse period and cohort effects in Western Pacific region, indicating intensifying impact of MBDs in the region. Of particular concern is the rising incidence among young adults, necessitating targeted interventions for this demographic.

During the last 30 years, the Asia-Pacific region has made significant progress in reducing the overall MBDs incidence, a reduction largely attributed to the decline of malaria cases. The data reveal a substantial 84.7% reduction in malaria cases across South-East Asia region and a 33.3% decrease in Western Pacific region, collectively driving down the regional MBDs incidence. Through decomposition analysis, the reduction in malaria cases is primarily driven by epidemiological factors. Initially, the collective global efforts to eliminate malaria have yielded significant results [3]. The WHO Global Technical Strategy for Malaria 2016–2030 has provided a technical framework for all malaria-endemic countries in Asia-Pacific region, guiding and supporting them in developing and implementing malaria control and elimination plans at both regional and national levels [31]. Furthermore, the 1–3–7 approach proposed by China has been incorporated into WHO’s technical documents and promoted globally. Meanwhile, through the malaria control project in Greater Mekong Subregion [32], technical training, cross-border joint prevention and control mechanisms, and material support have provided to high-transmission border areas such as Cambodia, Myanmar, Lao People’s Democratic Republic, and Viet Nam, significantly enhancing regional collaborative control efforts and further contributing to the reduction of malaria cases in Asia-Pacific region [33]. Following that, the primary vector for malaria transmission, Anopheles, predominantly breeds in rice paddies and ditches in rural area. The rapid urbanization in Asia-Pacific region has led to a reduction in rural population density, which has, to some extent, mitigated the spread of malaria in rural environments [34]. Ultimately, increased public awareness in malaria prevention has played a significant role in mitigating disease transmission [35].

This study also reveals that dengue has replaced malaria as the primary MBD in the area, with the dengue cases surpassing malaria since 2007 and showing rapid growth. This epidemiological shift has been driven by rapid population increase and the acceleration of urbanization [36, 37]. Over the past three decades, the population in South-East Asia region has increased by approximately 714 million, while the population in Western Pacific region has grown by about 344 million. During the same period, dengue cases have increased by 254.5% in Western Pacific and by 127.7% in South-East Asia. Particularly concerning is the pronounced upward trend, which is especially evident among individuals under the age of 30. This trend illustrates how urbanization-driven migration of young adults to urban employment centers fosters the expansion of high-density vector breeding habitats in peri-urban areas [38]. This specific urban environment, characterized by inadequate sanitation infrastructure and hyper- concentrated populations, creates optimal conditions for vector propagation, ultimately contributing to a notable increase in dengue cases. Additionally, available existing evidence indicates that population mobility has a significant impact on the diffuse of dengue [37]. As a key economic engine of the global economy, the Asia-Pacific region attracts numerous merchants for trade and, with its abundant tourism resources, draws many international tourists [39]. This increase in population mobility may have intensified the risk of human-to-human transmission of dengue.

Additionally, this study employed the Age-Period-Cohort model to better identify the diver of epidemic patterns of MBDs in Asia-Pacific region. The results revealed that the incidence model on MBDs in Western Pacific differs significantly from that in South-East Asia. Despite experiencing population growth and urbanization, the MBDs incidence in South-East Asia region has been steadily declining, showing favorable period and cohort effects. This trend is likely attributable to the region’s robust malaria control measures, which have led to a faster decline in malaria incidence compared to the rise in dengue. Conversely, the incidence of MBDs in Western Pacific region has significantly increased, leading to adverse period and birth cohort effects, which is due to the slow decline in malaria incidence failing to offset the explosive growth of dengue [40]. This asymmetry is driven by region specific structural vulnerabilities: Literature and surveillance data unequivocally identify the Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus mosquitoes as the key vectors of dengue [41], with Aedes aegypti being particularly significant in terms of transmission efficiency. Notably, the Aedes aegypti is widely distributed across Pacific Island nations [42], and under the dual influences of global warming and El Niño phenomenon, significant changes have been observed in the life cycle and reproductive rate of Aedes aegypti [7]. These changes have resulted in an extended life cycle and accelerated reproduction, thereby exacerbating the risk of dengue transmission in Western Pacific region. Moreover, despite urbanization progressing in both Western Pacific and South-East Asia regions, there are differences in the pace of urbanization between regions. According to the latest statistics from the World Bank, the average urbanization rate in South-East Asia countries was approximately 40% in 2021, which is relatively lower compared to the average of about 60% in Western Pacific countries [43]. In the rapid process of urbanization, the development of urban infrastructure often struggles to keep pace with population growth and urban expansion, leading to a lag in infrastructure construction [44]. This lag, coupled with a lack of public awareness about environmental protection, can trigger a cascade of issues such as the deterioration of urban sanitation, poor water resource management, and inadequate vector control measures. The convergence of these interconnected dynamics, including population migration, environmental-infrastructural deficiencies, and escalating vector proliferation, reinforces a self-perpetuating cycle of “population mobility, environmental disruption, and Aedes amplification”. This cycle illustrates a key perspective of “the third epidemiologic transition” [45]: rather than gradually eliminating infectious diseases such as dengue, socioeconomic development and urbanization have created new ecological inches for them, leading to the resurgence of MBDs alongside an increasing burden of chronic diseases. This phenomenon highlights a region-specific epidemiological transition path shaped by anthropogenic drivers and structural vulnerabilities [46]. The above theory is also reflected in the vector ecosystems of specific countries. Taking highly urbanized country Australia as an example, although their public health systems are relatively developed, climate warming has promoted the expansion of habitats for Aedes aegypti, international travel has heightened the risk of virus importation, and the common practice of storing water in residential yards provides suitable conditions for vector breeding [47]. The convergence of these multiple risk factors, coupled with a lack of effective regional collaboration mechanisms, has collectively contributed to the persistent rise in incidence in the country [48]. In contrast, although the countries in the South-East Asia region continues to face threats from malaria and dengue, transnational cooperation and systematic containment policies (e.g., the Southeast Asian International Center of Excellence for Malaria Research (SEA-ICEMR) [49]) have significantly enhanced the region’s capacity to respond to MBDs threats.

Encouragingly, the relative period risk of MBDs in Western Pacific region has started to decline, suggesting a potential reversal of the upward trend in case numbers. However, sustained vigilance through dynamic surveillance systems and robust preventive strategies is imperative to ensure durable maintenance of this declining trend. In the short term, focusing on preventingthe cross-border transmission of MBDs from high prevalence areas. For instance, in Australia and other countries with abundant tourism resources and large numbers of inbound travelers, an MBDs early warning and rapid response systems based on entry screening and symptom surveillance could be established at major international airports or popular tourist attractions to promptly contain imported cases. It is important to note that some countries may face challenges such as limited medical resources, insufficient human resources and funding shortages for surveillance system [47]. They should actively seek regional technical support and financial assistance from international organizations like WHO to bridge resource gaps. Medium-term interventions should prioritize improving infrastructure in countries with scarce freshwater resources and widespread reliance on water storage equipment, such as the Maldives and Viet Nam. By promoting mosquito-proof water storage containers and enhancing community-level water management and waste disposal practices, mosquito breeding environments can be reduced at the source. However, residents’ acceptance of changing existing habits may pose an obstacle, highlighting the need for community engagement and sustained health education to improve the feasibility and sustainability of such policies [50]. Over the long term, a regional coordinated prevention and control mechanism should be established. This can be achieved by creating a regional vector genetic monitoring network and a cross-border information sharing platform among Western Pacific countries to enable early identification and precise responses to vector population distribution, expansion trends, and drug resistance. However, data sharing between countries may face political resistance, disparities in technical resources, and a lack of collaborative coordination mechanisms. A feasible approach is to leverage existing regional cooperation frameworks, such as Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation (APEC), by developing unified data exchange protocols and establishing multilateral cooperation mechanisms to systematically overcome these barriers [48].

Limitations

Although this study reveals the evolution of incidence patterns of MBDs in Asia-Pacific region and their underlying drivers, several limitations remain. Firstly, though malaria, dengue, yellow fever and zika virus disease were included, potentially underestimating risks of MBDs in this region due to low incidence reports of yellow fever and zika virus disease, and the exclusion of other MBDs like Japanese encephalitis and Chikungunya. Future research should include a broader range of diseases and integrate climate, mobility, and vector ecology data to more comprehensively assess regional disease burden. Secondly, despite using GBD2021 data, variations in diagnostics and reporting standards across regions, especially in some pacific island countries and remote areas, may lead to underestimation of disease burden. Standardized metrics like ASIR help the comparison across regions and diseases but require cautious interpretation due to differing transmission modes vectors, and ecological contexts. Future studies should use raw surveillance and field data to improve the accuracy and representativeness of estimates. Thirdly, the analysis’s “epidemiological change” component cannot decompose or quantify the individual contributions from these underlying drivers like vector efficiency and climate. Spatiotemporal models with explicit covariates are recommended to more clearly disentangle and assess the distinct effects of these drivers in future studies. Lastly, while socioeconomic and environmental factors are correlated with MBDs based on cross-sectional data, causality is not proven. Longitudinal studies and multi-source data integration are needed for causal inference in the future

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study underscores the dynamic landscape of MBDs in the Asia-Pacific region and highlights the need for responsive and adaptable public health strategies. By integrating insights from this study with ongoing regional collaboration, it is possible to mitigate the impact of MBDs and promote a healthier future for the population
