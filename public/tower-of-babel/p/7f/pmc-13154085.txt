Novel Use of a Distal Perfusion Catheter With a Removable Side Port in Patients on VA-ECMO
Authors: Raef Fadel, Anas Al-Suraimi, Fares Sukhon, Li Pang, Vikas Aggarwal, Ahmad Jabri, Mir Babar Basir, Brittany Fuller, Asaad Nakhle, Khaldoon Alaswad, Mohammad Alqarqaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Risk of limb ischemia in patients on venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) is minimized with the use of distal perfusion catheters (DPCs). However, conventional sheaths utilize a flow-limiting side port connection, reducing efficacy. We report 5 cases wherein we utilized a commercially available DPC with a removable side port in patients treated with VA-ECMO. The use of a DPC without a side port increased flow by 220% when compared to the use of a DPC with a side port. No patients experienced limb ischemia while on VA-ECMO.

Background

Limb ischemia is a serious and potentially fatal complication of peripheral venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO), driven by occlusive cannula sizes and underlying peripheral artery disease (PAD).1, 2, 3 Distal perfusion catheters (DPCs) are used to provide antegrade flow down the ipsilateral limb to reduce the risk of limb ischemia.2,3 Commonly a 6-F DPC with a coil-wire shaft (Figure 1) is utilized to minimize kinking. Despite a decrease in the incidence of limb ischemia with conventional DPCs,3 flow rate is limited primarily by the diameter of the side port.4,5 In addition, the side port connections, by way of stopcocks and connectors (Figure 1), predispose DPC to thrombosis.6 Compared to conventional sheaths, removing the side port increases overall DPC flow rate by increasing the average diameter across the perfusion circuit and reducing the number of connections, which ultimately reduces the resistance within the circuit. To our knowledge, the use of a DPC with a removable side port has not been previously reported.

We describe 5 patients on VA-ECMO who received a commercially available 6-F × 15 cm (cm) DPC with a removable side port (Catapult, Boston Scientific) in the superficial femoral artery (Figure 2). All patients in the case series had flow measurements before and after removal of the side port, recorded as milliliter per minute (mL/min) using a flow probe over purpose-built male-to-male tubing which was connected to the VA-ECMO (Figure 3), followed by daily recordings for the entire duration of VA-ECMO support (Figure 4).Figure 2Novel Distal Perfusion Circuit Setup Using a 6 French Antegrade Catheter With a Removable Side PortCatapult (Boston Scientific) 6-F × 15-cm sheath with a removable side port (A) allowing direct connection via a male-to-male adapter to the arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation cannula (B).Figure 3Measuring Flow Across the Distal Perfusion CircuitFlow probe (white arrow) placed on a male-to-male adapter, measuring distal limb perfusion across circuit.Figure 4Distal Perfusion Flow Before and After Removal of the Side PortDistal perfusion flow with (red) and 3-day average distal perfusion flow after removal of the side port (dark blue).

Patient #1

A 59-year-old male patient was admitted with ventricular tachycardia storm complicated by cardiogenic shock. Past medical history was significant for coronary artery disease with prior percutaneous coronary intervention to the left anterior descending and right coronary arteries and heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) of 10% to 15%.

The patient was initially treated for cardiogenic shock with a microaxial flow pump (Impella CP, Abiomed). On hospital day #5, the patient was successfully extubated and underwent exchange of the femoral Impella CP to an axillary Impella 5.5 with plans to proceed with cardiac transplant evaluation. However, on hospital day #7, the patient had recurrence of ventricular tachycardia storm, requiring re-intubation and emergent VA-ECMO cannulation with a 17-F outflow cannula in the left common femoral artery, a 24-F inflow cannula in the right common femoral vein, and a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult DPC in the left superficial femoral artery (Figure 3). Distal perfusion flow of 188 mL/min was recorded with the side port attached, followed by an increase to 340 mL/min after removal of the side port (Table 1, Figure 4). Post-cannulation, the 3-day distal perfusion flow average was 435 mL/min. Patient #2

A 69-year-old male patient was admitted with cardiogenic shock secondary to severe aortic stenosis. Past medical history was significant for pulmonary embolism and heavy tobacco use.

The patient presented with progressive dyspnea, fatigue, subjective fevers, and unintentional weight loss of 20 pounds. Initial workup demonstrated leukocytosis, acute kidney injury, and lactic acidosis. A chest computed tomography scan demonstrated significant bilateral pulmonary edema, and blood cultures were positive for streptococcus. A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed severe aortic stenosis with possible vegetation, as well as a left ventricular ejection fraction of 25% to 30%. The patient was transferred to our institution for escalation of care.

On arrival to our hospital, the patient was on inotropic and vasopressor support, and lactic acid levels were elevated to 7.7 mmol/L. The decision was made to proceed with VA-ECMO cannulation with a 17-F outflow cannula in the right common femoral artery, a 24-F inflow cannula in the right common femoral vein, and a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult DPC in the right superficial femoral artery. Distal perfusion flow of 198 mL/min was recorded with the side port attached, followed by an increase to 475 mL/min after removal of the side port (Table 1, Figure 4). Post-cannulation, the 3-day distal perfusion flow average was 537 mL/min. Doppler pulses were present in both lower extremities each day while on VA-ECMO, with no episodes of clotting in the perfusion line or bleeding from the DPC access site.

On hospital day #9, the patient's case was discussed in a multidisciplinary team meeting with cardiac surgery and structural heart disease, and the patient was deemed too high risk for open or transcatheter aortic valve intervention. The patient, along with family members, decided to pursue comfort care measures, and the patient expired shortly after clamping the VA-ECMO circuit.

Patient #3

A 70-year-old female patient was admitted with cardiogenic shock secondary to severe aortic stenosis. Past medical history was significant for HFrEF of 35% to 40%, coronary artery disease, and atrial fibrillation.

On hospital day #6, the patient underwent a right-heart catheterization, and soon after, she developed pulseless electrical activity and required one round of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. The patient then underwent VA-ECMO cannulation with a 17-F outflow cannula in the right common femoral artery, a 24-F inflow cannula in the right common femoral vein, and a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult DPC in the right superficial femoral artery. Distal perfusion flow of 200 mL/min was recorded with the side port attached, followed by an increase to 500 mL/min after removal of the side port (Table 1, Figure 4). Post-cannulation, the 3-day distal perfusion flow average was 617 mL/min. Doppler pulses were present in both lower extremities each day while on VA-ECMO, with no episodes of clotting in the perfusion line or bleeding from the DPC access site.

On hospital day #13, the patient was extubated; however, due to ongoing hemodynamic instability requiring multiple inotropic agents and vasopressors, as well as poor functional status with neurocognitive decline, the patient was deemed a high-risk candidate for surgical or transcatheter aortic valve intervention. A family meeting was held on hospital day #15, and the decision was made to proceed with comfort care measures. The patient died shortly after clamping of the VA-ECMO circuit.

Patient #4

A 56-year-old male patient was admitted with hypoxic respiratory failure. Past medical history was significant for interstitial lung disease.

The patient presented with progressive dyspnea and hypoxia. Chest computed tomography demonstrated diffuse ground glass opacifications worsened compared to prior imaging. The patient underwent evaluation for lung transplant; however, on hospital day #24, the patient developed significant hypoxia refractory to bilevel positive airway pressure ventilation, and the decision was made to undergo veno-venous ECMO cannulation with a 24-F inflow cannula in the right femoral vein and a 17-F outflow cannula in the right internal jugular vein.

On hospital day #27, the patient developed refractory hypoxia despite maximal flow on the veno-venous ECMO circuit. A transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated profound right ventricular dysfunction, so the patient was upgraded to VA-ECMO with insertion of a 19-F outflow cannula in the left common femoral artery, and a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult DPC in the left superficial femoral artery. Distal perfusion flow of 181 mL/min was recorded with the side port attached, followed by an increase to 425 mL/min after removal of the side port (Table 1, Figure 4). Post-cannulation, the 3-day distal perfusion flow average was 444 mL/min. Doppler pulses were present in both lower extremities each day while on VA-ECMO, with no episodes of clotting in the perfusion line or bleeding from the DPC access site.

On hospital day #31, the multidisciplinary transplant team decided that the patient was not a candidate for lung transplant. The decision was made to pursue comfort care measures, and the patient expired shortly after clamping the VA-ECMO circuit on hospital day #32.

Patient #5

A 66-year-old male patient was admitted after suffering pulseless electrical activity at home. Past medical history was significant for coronary artery disease status post 2-vessel coronary artery bypass grafting, HFrEF of 30% to 35%, moderate mitral regurgitation, and diabetes.

The patient underwent right and left heart catheterization with coronary and pulmonary angiogram, which confirmed the presence of a pulmonary embolism. Due to recurrent pulseless electrical activity and severely decreased cardiac output and index, the decision was made to proceed with VA-ECMO cannulation with a 17-F outflow cannula in the right common femoral artery, a 24-F inflow cannula in the right common femoral vein, and a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult DPC in the right superficial femoral artery. Peripheral angiogram prior to VA-ECMO cannulation demonstrated severe PAD and bilateral chronic occlusions in the distal superficial femoral arteries (Figure 5). Distal perfusion flow of 126 mL/min was recorded with the side port attached, followed by an increase to 248 mL/min after removal of the side port (Table 1, Figure 4). Post-cannulation, the 3-day distal perfusion flow average was 250 mL/min. Discussion

We report flow measures for 5 patients on VA-ECMO who required a DPC using a 6-F × 15-cm Catapult sheath with and without removal of the side port (Figure 2). Flow was significantly better without the side port, with an average increase of 220%. We demonstrated a significant improvement in perfusion circuit flow with removal of the side port across all 5 patients, with no reported incidence of DPC occlusion, malfunction, thrombosis, or lower-extremity limb ischemia while on VA-ECMO.

DPC flow is limited by the inner diameter of the side port when using traditional catheters4,5 (Figure 1). The use of DPC with a removable side port to increase distal flow in patients on VA-ECMO has not been previously reported. By utilizing a DPC without the side port, not only is the average luminal area increased across the reperfusion circuit, but the distance across the circuit is also optimized. These factors directly result in increased flow from the VA-ECMO outflow cannula to the distal limb. In addition, by reducing the number of stop-cocks, angled turns, and adapters, flow becomes more laminar, further contributing to increased distal perfusion.

Notably, in patient #5, the Catapult sheath was able to provide adequate distal perfusion flow in a patient with severe PAD. PAD is an independent risk factor for vascular complications among patients on VA-ECMO and is typically considered a contraindication for femoral cannulation in such patients.7,8 Improving DPC functionality to mitigate the risk of ischemia in this patient population is crucial given high rates of vascular complications.9

It is important to consider the anticoagulation strategy while on VA-ECMO, as this has direct implications on the patency and flow across the DPC. Heparin is utilized as the default anticoagulant for patients on VA-ECMO at our institution and is given as an initial bolus of 60 units/kg, followed by an infusion of 12 units/kg/h (maximum 1,800 u/h) and titrated to maintain a therapeutic range of anti-Xa. The patients in this case series were managed with the same anticoagulation protocol, in order to maintain consistency in treatment on circuit.

Purpose-built DPC and reperfusion equipment for the risk of limb ischemia in the mechanical circulatory support space is warranted. Further studies are also needed to support the aforementioned strategy.

Conclusions

In this case series of patients supported with peripheral VA-ECMO, the use of a commercially available DPC with a removable side port resulted in a marked and consistent improvement in distal limb perfusion. Removal of the side port led to an average 220% increase in distal perfusion flow compared with conventional side port configuration, without any observed episodes of limb ischemia, catheter thrombosis, or device malfunction. Further prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings and to support the development of purpose-built distal perfusion systems for mechanical circulatory support.
