Postgraduate medical education in Saudi Arabia: The experience of Riyadh second health cluster
Authors: Khalid Almatham, Sara AlFadil, Ibrahim Al-Homood, Osama Alshaya, Fahad AlGhofaili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPostgraduate medical education (PGME) is central to Saudi Arabia’s efforts to develop a high-performing, self-sustaining healthcare workforce aligned with Vision 2030 and the Human Capability Development Program. As a leading academic and clinical institution, the Riyadh Second Health Cluster (RC2) has pioneered structured and responsive PGME programs under its Executive Administration of Academic Training Affairs (ATA). This review explores the evolution, achievements, and strategic priorities of RC2’s postgraduate education system. It examines the historical development of PGME in the Kingdom, regulatory frameworks, educational methodologies, and emerging challenges such as burnout, resource disparities, and integration of technology. The paper also highlights innovative initiatives launched by RC2, including specialized professional certificates and leadership programs targeting nursing, dental, and interdisciplinary teams. These efforts reflect a deep commitment to capability building, patient-centered care, and system-wide transformation. Through continuous alignment with national goals and stakeholder engagement, RC2 exemplifies a model for educational innovation and excellence. This paper concludes with targeted recommendations for sustaining progress and enhancing impact across other training centers. The RC2 experience provides valuable lessons for the future of medical education in Saudi Arabia and similar healthcare systems undergoing rapid transformation.

Introduction

Postgraduate medical education (PGME) is a pivotal component of modern healthcare systems, especially in countries undergoing rapid transformation in service delivery and workforce development. In Saudi Arabia, the rising demand for localized expertise and the need to transition away from historical reliance on expatriate healthcare workers has necessitated substantial investment in medical education and training infrastructures. This shift is not merely structural but deeply strategic, aligning with the national Vision 2030 and the Human Capability Development Program, which emphasize building a robust healthcare workforce equipped with the competencies to lead and sustain high-quality care systems.[12] The healthcare system revitalization under Vision 2030 requires not only increased capacity but also enhanced quality and strategic alignment with population health needs.[2]

Within this evolving landscape, the Riyadh Second Health Cluster (RC2) has established itself as a vanguard institution in postgraduate medical training. Through its Executive Administration of Academic Training Affairs (ATA), RC2 has developed an integrated, accredited, and responsive educational system tailored to meet both current and future health sector demands. The institutional scope of RC2 includes some of Riyadh’s most prominent hospitals and health centers, and it serves a demographically diverse population. This paper aims to provide a comprehensive overview of the postgraduate medical education initiatives under RC2, detailing its achievements, contextualizing its evolution within the broader national framework, and presenting a set of forward-looking recommendations that may benefit other Academic Training Affairs departments across the Kingdom.

Overview of Postgraduate Medical Education in Saudi Arabia

Saudi Arabia’s journey in postgraduate medical education began in earnest in the late twentieth century. Before the 1980s, specialty training opportunities were limited, and the Kingdom heavily relied on physicians trained abroad. This changed when leading institutions such as King Saud University and King Abdulaziz Hospital initiated residency programs in core specialties like surgery, pediatrics, and internal medicine.[3] The establishment of the Arab Board for Medical Specialization further consolidated regional training standards.

The regulatory framework of PGME in Saudi Arabia has been shaped by institutions such as the Saudi Commission for Health Specialties (SCFHS), which serves as the principal accreditation and oversight body. The SCFHS has introduced competency-based curricula and strategic plans aimed at enhancing quality and ensuring alignment with global standards.[45] Recent implementation of a national framework for evaluation of postgraduate medical training programs has strengthened accountability and standardization across institutions.[5] It continuously monitors key performance indicators (KPIs) related to training effectiveness and workforce responsiveness.[46] Studies evaluating the quality of postgraduate medical training programs have highlighted both achievements and areas for improvement in program structure, resources, and educational outcomes.[6]

The Ministry of Health (MoH) plays an essential complementary role in supporting training infrastructure and facilitating policy implementation, particularly through initiatives embedded in the public hospital system.[7] Together, the SCFHS and MoH form the dual foundation upon which PGME in Saudi Arabia rests. Residency programs in the Kingdom typically range from 4 to 6 years and are complemented by advanced fellowship training. In addition to clinical training, continuous professional development (CPD) courses, certification pathways, and institutional collaborations contribute to the evolving educational architecture.[8]

The Riyadh Second Health Cluster

The Riyadh Second Health Cluster (RC2) is a regional healthcare institution designed to deliver integrated services while also serving as a hub for academic training. It includes King Fahad Medical City (KFMC), Prince Mohammed bin Abdulaziz Hospital, Alyamama Hospital, and a network of affiliated hospitals in Al-Majmaah, Alghat, Zulfi, Tumair, and Hotat Sudair. These facilities provide both primary and tertiary services, serving a diverse population and creating a varied and rich environment for clinical education.

RC2’s structure promotes centralized academic leadership through the Executive Administration of Academic Training Affairs (ATA), which coordinates training strategy, program design, faculty development, and quality assurance. The scope of ATA’s mandate has expanded in recent years, driven by its recognition of the strategic link between workforce development and healthcare system performance. Moreover, RC2 has been formally accredited by the Accreditation Council for Continuing Medical Education (ACCME), which reflects its commitment to international standards of excellence.[8]

Educational Framework and Curriculum

The educational approach within RC2 aligns with the competency-based medical education (CBME) model emphasized by global best practices and endorsed by SCFHS.[910] Core competencies include patient care, medical knowledge, professionalism, practice-based learning, and systems-based practice. Despite significant progress in implementing competency-based frameworks, ongoing challenges in full adoption across all training contexts remain, requiring continued commitment and refinement.[10] RC2 has tailored these competencies to local clinical contexts, ensuring that training programs are not only pedagogically sound but also operationally relevant.

Recent innovations in curriculum development include the introduction of specialized training programs in response to workforce needs. For example, programs in adult stem cell transplantation, male infertility and andrology, movement disorders, and sleep medicine were launched in 2023, reflecting data-driven assessments of national clinical service gaps.[1] The integration of digital technologies has also reshaped the educational environment. Following the COVID-19 pandemic, RC2 expanded its virtual learning platforms, simulation-based education, and online assessments, thereby increasing accessibility and flexibility for trainees.[1112] The pandemic significantly impacted medical education delivery across Saudi Arabia, accelerating the adoption of digital learning modalities and highlighting both opportunities and challenges in remote education.[12]

Emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence and machine learning applications, present new opportunities for personalized learning, adaptive assessment, and clinical decision support in medical education.[13] RC2 is positioned to explore these innovations while addressing implementation challenges related to infrastructure, faculty training, and ethical considerations.[13]

Assessments are both formative and summative, incorporating tools such as direct observation, workplace-based assessments, OSCEs, and logbook reviews. Feedback is delivered regularly and used to inform individualized learning plans and institutional quality improvement cycles.

Challenges in Postgraduate Medical Education

Among trainees, one major issue is burnout associated with demanding clinical schedules, particularly in emergency and intensive care settings.[1415] The pressure to perform in high-stakes environments, combined with limited downtime and complex caseloads, contributes to psychological and emotional stress. A nationwide cross-sectional study revealed significant levels of burnout among postgraduate trainees in Saudi Arabia, with factors including work hours, specialty type, and perceived support affecting both burnout rates and career satisfaction.[15] Mental health challenges were further exacerbated during the COVID-19 pandemic, with residents reporting increased anxiety, depression, and stress related to both clinical duties and educational disruptions.[16]

At the institutional level, disparities in resource distribution between central and peripheral hospitals create inconsistencies in training quality. While institutions such as KFMC are fully equipped with simulation laboratories and specialty faculty, smaller hospitals may lack such infrastructure, thereby limiting trainees’ exposure and skill development.[6]

Culturally, gender disparities remain a concern, particularly regarding access to certain specialties and academic leadership positions. Although female enrollment in medical education has increased, there remains a need for equitable mentorship and advancement pathways.[14]

Another challenge lies in interministerial coordination. PGME in Saudi Arabia spans multiple sectors—including the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Education, National Guard Health Affairs, and private providers—each with distinct policies and operational models. This fragmentation complicates program standardization and workforce planning.[14]

Successes and Innovations

RC2 has achieved several milestones. In 2023 alone, over 1168 residents and fellows participated in structured training programs, alongside 1052 interns and 854 medical students engaged in elective rotations. Seventeen new specialty programs were initiated based on strategic health needs, showcasing the system’s adaptability and forward planning.[1]

As part of a national initiative, RC2 was able to increase capacity to accommodate more trainees while maintaining the training quality standards according to SCFHS and to receive the largest training institute recognition from SCFHS in 2024.[5] The training programs in the different training sites were merged as one program in each specialty or subspecialty, which facilitated the rotation of the trainees across the cluster, giving them exposure to different levels of care from community to tertiary care level.

As part of the transformation, academic and training affairs through headquarters monitored the work centrally, utilizing different digital solutions like the electronic application system, dashboard, and others to supervise all trainees and training activities, which made the process more organized and easier, allowing optimized utilization of resources like staff and others. These digital innovations align with broader technological transformation trends in medical education globally.[13]

The development of specialized professional certification programs has enabled RC2 to address emerging clinical competencies outside traditional residency frameworks. These certifications in areas such as advanced nursing, interdisciplinary care, and clinical communication enhance workforce versatility and support the Kingdom’s broader healthcare transformation.[1]

Additionally, RC2 has rolled out structured leadership programs aimed at dental professionals, nurses, and interdisciplinary teams. These initiatives focus on cultivating leadership competencies such as strategic thinking, team management, and quality improvement. RC2’s emphasis on interprofessional education, social engagement, and community-building through events and retreats fosters an inclusive and collegial learning environment. These nonclinical elements contribute to professional identity formation and long-term retention.

Future Directions and Recommendations

The achievements of the Riyadh Second Health Cluster’s postgraduate medical education system demonstrate the potential of strategically aligned, locally responsive, and innovation-driven training frameworks. However, sustaining and scaling these accomplishments requires a proactive and multidimensional strategy. The following recommendations are proposed to guide RC2 and similar Academic Training Affairs (ATA) departments across the Kingdom in advancing their PGME objectives in alignment with national transformation priorities.

One of the most pressing needs is the physical and technological expansion of training infrastructure. While flagship institutions such as King Fahad Medical City (KFMC) are well-resourced, smaller hospitals within the cluster often face limitations in simulation facilities, academic space, and teaching capacity. Bridging this gap requires the establishment of distributed academic centers supported by standardized simulation labs, virtual learning platforms, and unified digital assessment systems.[167] These should be guided by SCFHS standards and regularly evaluated for impact.[5]

Strategic planning for PGME capacity must also be informed by national workforce projections. By conducting annual training capacity reviews and aligning specialty offerings with disease burden and service utilization data, RC2 can maintain program responsiveness and sustainability.[14] Specialty expansion—such as the recently introduced male infertility and adult stem cell transplantation programs—should continue to be based on structured needs assessments.[1] Budgetary frameworks must earmark stable funding lines for postgraduate training, distinct from service delivery budgets, to ensure long-term viability.

Curricular innovation should remain a core priority. In line with global shifts toward learner-centered education, RC2 is encouraged to enhance its adoption of interprofessional education, problem-based learning, and simulation-based strategies.[610] Integration of flipped classrooms and asynchronous e-learning modules can enhance flexibility, particularly for trainees with heavy clinical loads. Furthermore, educational frameworks should incorporate principles of quality and safety, health informatics, and leadership to align with contemporary healthcare delivery models.

Faculty development is integral to educational quality. Clinical educators at RC2 often assume teaching roles without formal training in pedagogy or assessment.[8] Structured certification programs in medical education, combined with ongoing workshops in feedback delivery, evaluation design, and mentorship skills, are recommended. Linking such development to faculty appraisal and promotion pathways will incentivize participation and embed a culture of academic professionalism.[4]

Trainee well-being must be formally integrated into ATA policies. Reports of burnout and mental fatigue are not uncommon, particularly in high-intensity specialties such as emergency and intensive care medicine.[141516] RC2 should implement wellness programs including psychological counseling, stress management workshops, and confidential reporting mechanisms. Periodic well-being surveys should be used to assess trends and evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions.[16] Given the documented prevalence of mental health challenges among residents, particularly during periods of high stress such as the pandemic, proactive institutional support is essential.[16]

A formal mentorship system—where each trainee is paired with an experienced faculty mentor—should be institutionalized. Effective mentorship not only supports academic development but also enhances retention, satisfaction, and career progression.[1115] Structured onboarding programs and longitudinal coaching models should be introduced to ensure consistency across programs and sites.

RC2 must maintain its strategic alignment with national priorities, particularly those outlined in the Human Capability Development Program and Vision 2030.[12] Each educational program and initiative should be mapped to broader health sector transformation goals, such as increasing Saudi workforce participation, achieving specialty coverage in underserved regions, and enhancing leadership capacity. Participation in national platforms for workforce planning, data sharing, and policy formulation will further ensure that RC2’s programs remain relevant and impactful.

Data-driven decision-making is another frontier of importance. RC2 should expand its use of real-time analytics and dashboard reporting to guide quality improvement. Systems should track trainee progress, faculty performance, examination outcomes, and program impact on clinical services. Establishing a dedicated educational research unit will not only support internal improvement but also contribute to the national and global literature on medical education.[17]

Finally, RC2 must continue to promote diversity, equity, and inclusion in postgraduate training. Gender disparities in specialty selection and leadership representation persist in parts of the Kingdom.[14] Outreach programs targeting female physicians, rural trainees, and underrepresented specialties can help close these gaps. Transparent selection criteria, antibias training for faculty, and routine monitoring of equity indicators are recommended strategies.

These recommendations, if implemented comprehensively, will further solidify RC2’s leadership role in postgraduate medical education and serve as a replicable model for other academic health institutions in Saudi Arabia and beyond.

Limitations and Future Directions

While this review provides a comprehensive overview of RC2’s postgraduate medical education initiatives, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-institution descriptive review, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other healthcare clusters with different resource allocations, governance structures, or demographic characteristics. Second, the paper does not include quantitative outcome measures such as board examination pass rates, trainee satisfaction scores, or longitudinal career trajectories, which would provide more robust evidence of program effectiveness.[6] Third, the review relies primarily on institutional data and published literature without incorporating direct stakeholder perspectives through interviews or surveys with trainees, faculty, or administrative leadership.

Future research should address these gaps through multicenter comparative studies that evaluate educational outcomes across different training institutions in Saudi Arabia. Longitudinal cohort studies tracking graduate performance, retention rates, and career satisfaction would provide valuable insights into the long-term impact of PGME reforms.[15] Additionally, qualitative research exploring trainee experiences, faculty challenges, and organizational culture would enrich understanding of the human dimensions of educational transformation. Implementation science approaches could also be employed to study the adoption and sustainability of innovations such as competency-based curricula, digital learning platforms, and wellness programs.[1012] The role of emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence, in enhancing personalized learning and clinical competency development warrants systematic investigation within the Saudi context.[13] Finally, health economics research examining the cost-effectiveness of various training models would inform resource allocation decisions and policy development at the national level.

Conclusion

The Riyadh Second Health Cluster has demonstrated how a regional health system can lead to transformative efforts in postgraduate medical education. Through its Executive Administration of Academic Training Affairs, RC2 has built a robust, flexible, and strategic educational infrastructure. Its programs are aligned with national priorities, responsive to population health needs, and grounded in international standards. As Saudi Arabia advances toward the goals of Vision 2030, the RC2 model provides a powerful example of how integrated, innovative PGME can shape the future of healthcare in the Kingdom.
