Charting Decodability of Dynamic Facial Expressions in Young and Old Adults: Similarities and Differences
Authors: Dilara Derya, Christian Wallraven
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDynamic facial expressions carry a wide range of signals, encompassing emotional but also more conversational content important for social interaction, for which the dynamic aspect is crucial. Likewise, we know from previous behavioral and neuroimaging studies that processing of emotional stimuli changes across aging - little, however, is known about how age may impact brain activity for dynamic facial expressions. To address this open issue, here we used two cohorts of older and younger adults (total N=77) within a whole-brain MVPA decoding paradigm in fMRI. We used a range of dynamic and conversational expressions as stimuli shown with a foil task in the scanner and had participants rate these post-scanning in terms of their affective content along 12 dimensions (including valence and arousal). The behavioral ratings were used to cluster the facial expressions and the resulting similarity matrix was used in a searchlight decoding paradigm to identify common areas. Using robust bootstrap analyses, we identified the insula as a common brain region able to decode the wide range of emotional and conversational dynamic facial expressions for both participants groups. We also discuss additional brain areas specific to the younger group. Our study adds to the growing literature on neural processing of dynamic expressions in the context of aging.Keywords: Aging, Facial expressions, MVPA, fMRI, Visual and emotional processing

INTRODUCTION

Facial expressions constitute one of the most basic channels of non-verbal social and emotional communication. Given their prevalence and importance, it is no surprise that these have been widely studied not only in their relation to communication, but also as primary or secondary symptoms of neurological disorders. Studies have shown that facial expression processing critically depends on age [1-5], affecting all channels of communication.

In terms of an overarching model of face processing in the brain, Haxby proposed a set of distributed neural systems that include basic face perception, recognition of familiar faces, processing of eye gaze, as well as for interpreting facial emotions [6-8]. The model is divided into two systems with the core system, for example, processing dynamic features in posterior superior temporal sulcus (pSTS), whereas the extended system would add knowledge about the person in medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC).

Building on this foundational work, recent studies have highlighted the need for a better investigation of the dynamic aspects of facial expression processing as well as extending the scope of facial expressions beyond that of the typically-studied, so-called “basic” or “universal” facial expressions. Regarding the former, dynamic facial expressions, for example, have been shown to elicit different behavioral performance [9-12] and in the context of brain imaging result in different activation patterns compared to their static counterparts [13-15]. Regarding the latter, building on early criticism by Russell [16], studies have suggested studying the full range of signals communicated via the face [17-19] beyond the seven “universal” expressions suggested by Ekman and Friesen [20].

Beyond these aspects, we know that facial expression processing is affected at every cognitive level by aging via changes to facial muscles as well as due to ongoing perceptual and neurological aging processes. Concerning recognition of expressions, for example, participants are known to have an own-age bias [21-23], favoring facial expressions of the same age group due to social categorization and motivation (Categorization-Individuation model) [24]. It also has been shown that negative facial expressions are perceived in a more positive manner for older participants [22]. In general, recognition performance of facial expressions has been reported to decline with age as well [1, 25-27]. It has been suggested that these results are connected with aging participants favoring positive emotions more and generalizing expressions into happiness [2, 22, 28-32] - an effect that has been explained, for example, via the socioemotional selectivity theory (SST) [33].

Age-related effects of perceiving facial expressions are not only apparent in behavioral findings but also via large-scale, multi-level neurophysiological changes. Neuroimaging through univariate methods in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), for example, has identified wide-ranging changes with old age in the emotion networks of the brain, including the amygdala, insula, and basal ganglia by both gray matter volume shrinkage or weakened connectivities [34-42]. The seemingly intact recognition of positive expressions is commonly attributed to plasticity [43, 44], seeing increased activation in prefrontal regions and a decrease in the amygdala [3, 36, 45, 46]. At the same time, dorsomedial prefrontal cortex (dmPFC) activity was shown to increase with negative facial expressions and to decrease with positive facial expressions [3].

Recent years have seen the increased use of multivariate approaches for brain imaging analysis, as these offer enhanced sensitivity for decoding patterns of activities in networks of brain regions [47-50]. Concerning aging, these approaches have provided insights to alterations in cognition in terms of neural dedifferentiation driven by functional specificity reduction with regards to motor activity [51], memory functions [51-53], spatial navigation [54], or speech perception [55]. In the context of facial expressions, multi-variate analyses have been applied to decode human faces [56] as well as basic emotions in face selective areas, including the fusiform face area (FFA), the occipital face area (OFA), and temporo-parietal cortices [57-61]. While valence-based decoding (i.e. positive, negative basic, and negative social emotions) [62] has identified a wider range of areas, including anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), precuneus, brainstem, amygdala, thalamus, somatomotor areas, and prefrontal cortex (PFC). Finally, concerning dynamic facial expressions, decoding of basic expressions was shown to be possible in both face selective regions and motion sensitive areas (i.e. superior temporal sulcus, STS) [12, 63, 64].

Importantly, while separate behavioral studies exist on the topics of aging and universal dynamic facial expressions (e.g., Richoz and colleagues [65] reported increased accuracy in recognizing dynamic facial expressions compared to static ones in older adults), no work has yet examined processing of complex, dynamic facial expressions across age via neuroimaging. The present study therefore aims to provide novel insights into this topic using a multivariate decoding approach. As such, our study for the first time sets out to compare brain-wide decodability of a set of dynamic emotional and communicative expressions, comparing a group of younger adults to a group of older adults.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

Given that a power analysis for the type of design and method chosen in our study was not available, we opted for a sample size with reference to recent studies on facial expression processing which was a mean of 25.07 [12, 51, 53-55, 59, 62, 63, 66-72]. As our goal was to compare older and younger participants, we therefore recruited 36 older adults (group OA (old adults); mean age=55 (54.59) years, 6 males, age range=48~67 years) and a larger sample of 70 younger adults (group YA (young adults); mean age=25 (24.68) years, 38 males, age range=18~31 years). The imbalance in the sample sizes of the two age groups was due a requirement from a larger study that this project was part of, in which the focus was on a pre-retirement age range. The choice of the age range for the older group was to ensure they were healthy, cognitively unimpaired, and actively engaged in the workforce. This, however, introduced a constraint in recruiting this group due to their work schedules and the limited opening hours of the MRI center, resulting in a smaller sample size overall - see Section 2.7 for a discussion of the statistical analysis in this context.

Per our previous behavioral experiment [18], we did not find any effect of gender on processing of facial expressions, hence an equal gender division was not enforced here. None of the participants had a history of neurological and psychiatric disorders. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal visual acuity.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Korea University (KUIRB-2019-0005) and all participants provided informed, written consent prior to participation. All procedures and methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki institutional and national guidelines and regulations.

Data availability

Data from the study is available from the authors upon request due to the need to obtain permission from the IRB.

Stimulus selection and rating experiment

The dynamic emotional and conversational facial expressions were taken from the dataset of Korean expressions (the KU Facial Expression Database [73], a database developed with the same protocols of the MPI Facial Expression Database [74]; see there for more detailed information about the background and validation). The KU Facial Expression Database [73] contains more than 50 facial expressions performed by 20 native Korean participants (referred to from hereon as “actors”- note, however, that none of the participants had acting experience, nor were they actual actors). Video clips ranged from 2 s to 6 s (average 4.55 s - note that clips were not normalized in time to preserve the natural dynamics) and were adjusted in terms of average luminance using the SHINE Toolbox [75]. Similarly, to a previous behavioral study of ours [18], we selected 25 facial expressions of the four most consistently-rated actors for the present experiments. To maintain participant engagement without confounding emotion-related neural responses, an orthogonal gender classification task was used [76-78]. Given the broad range of emotional and conversational expressions presented, requiring direct evaluation would have lengthened response times and increased cognitive load, potentially leading to participant fatigue during the 45-minute scan session. Instead, post-scan behavioral ratings were collected to cluster expressions and validate previous findings [18], allowing the study to prioritize neural decoding of expressions while maintaining participant comfort and signal reliability.

After fMRI scanning, participants were scheduled within the same week for a behavioral rating experiment of the expressions (following [18]). In this experiment, the same facial expressions were presented in a pseudorandomized sequence on a 15-inch high-resolution monitor placed at a distance of 60 cm in a quiet room. Participants were then asked to rate each expression on 12 evaluative dimensions using a 7-point Likert-type scale (1=does not convey the property at all – 7=fully conveys the property, except for valence where 1=fully negative – 7=fully positive) with a paper-based, self-paced questionnaire. The dimensions were selected based on our prior research [14] and were: arousal, attractiveness, dynamics, empathy, familiarity, intelligence, naturalness, outgoingness, persuasiveness, politeness, sincerity, valence.

In the context of fMRI analysis, a direct decoding of all 25 expressions was not deemed feasible as the limited amount of repetitions and time constraints in the scanner would not have afforded sufficient power. Therefore, expressions were clustered in a data-driven fashion via k-means clustering. Importantly, this clustering was achieved based on the behavioral data that was acquired from the same participants outside of the scanner (See Supplementary Materials). For this, all 12 rating dimensions were averaged across participants for each of the 25 expressions into one 12-dimensional vector. These vectors were then used as input to a k-means clustering based on Euclidean distances, and the silhouette coefficient was examined for a maximum value for different values of k. The maximum value was obtained for 8 clusters of expressions, which are listed in Supplementary Table 1 (including interpreted names for clusters as well as their contained expressions). We note that the number of expressions contained in each cluster is not equal given this method (See Supplementary Table 1).

Scanning procedures

The main imaging experiment consisted of an fMRI scanning session. After standard familiarization with fMRI scanning and the explanation of the answering equipment, participants went into the scanner and underwent a T1 scan. Following this, the main experiment started in which participants watched the 100 (=4 actors x 25 expressions) video clips twice in randomized order, yielding a total of 200 trials. Each video clip began with a fixation point of 1 second followed by the video sequence - video sequences shorter than 6 seconds were filled with empty frames to ensure proper scan synchronization (analysis, however, used only the data from the original video length). The full experiment was completed in 2 scan sessions of 100 trials each with a break in between. In order to control attention, participants had to complete a gender identification task, in which they were to indicate the gender of the actor/actress in each trial. All stimuli were presented with MR-compatible goggles by NordicNeuroLab (NNL; NordicNeuroLab, Bergen, Norway; refresh rate: 85 Hz; display resolution: 800×600 pixels; viewing angle: 30° horizontal, 23° vertical) using Psychopy software (http://www.psychopy.org/).

Functional MRI data acquisition and processing

High-resolution structural and functional imaging was performed using a 3.0 Tesla Magnetom Trio, A Tim System eco Siemens MRI scanner (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 32-channel head coil.

Structural images were acquired for co-registration to the functional MRI data using a three-dimensional T1-weighted magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo imaging sequence with the following acquisition parameters: repetition time, 1.9 ms; echo time, 2.52 ms; flip angle (FA), 9°; field of view (FOV), 256×256 mm2 matrix size; slice thickness, 1×1×1 mm in-plane resolution; 180 contiguous sagittal slices.

This was followed by T2*-weighted functional images obtained through gradient echo planar images (EPI) with Blood Oxygenation Level-Dependent contrast (TR=2000 ms; TE=30 ms; FA=90°; FOV=220 mm; 64×64 matrix size; 36 slices; voxel size=2.8×2.8×3.0 mm3 with no gap). The stimuli were presented via an MR-compatible goggles mounted on the head coil. Participants used two buttons of a four-button MR-compatible response grip during the experiment.

Preprocessing procedures

FMRI data were preprocessed and analyzed using Statistical Parametric Mapping 12 (SPM12; Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, London, UK). All functional images were corrected for slice timing, re-aligned to the first scan, normalized to the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) 152 echo-planar imaging (EPI) template, resampled at a voxel resolution of 2×2×2 mm3, and spatially smoothed by using a Gaussian filter with 8-mm full-width-half-maximum (FWHM) isotropic kernel to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio. When using a conservative alignment threshold with inter-scan movement less than 2 mm, we had to exclude 9 participants from the older cohort and 13 from the younger cohort - a further 7 participants from the younger cohort due to attention lapses during the experiment. The total number of participants analyzed in our main analyses are, therefore, 27 for the OA and 50 for the YA group, respectively. Given the larger number of excluded participants, additional analysis runs were made based on the full original number of 36 OA and 70 YA participants (see Supplemental Material) - the main results reported below, however, did not change.

Multivariate pattern analysis and statistical analysis

Whole-brain multivariate pattern analysis (MVPA using the Princeton MVPA Toolbox (http://code.google.com/p/princeton-mvpa-toolbox) was used to assess brain activity differences between OA and YA by concatenating voxels to a single feature vector. The decoding was performed at the whole-brain level at a local radius of 3 voxels and for each cluster group separately in a 10-fold cross validation Gaussian naïve bayes (GNB) classifier, training the 9-folds and testing on the 10th-fold. Decoding of clusters was conducted independently for each age group, resulting in a total of 27 accuracy values at each voxel for the OA group and 50 for the YA group, resulting in one decoding accuracy map per subject. The resulting decoding accuracies were then subjected to FDR-corrected t-tests using a q-value of 0.05 and decoding chance level (=12.5% for decoding of 8 clusters) at each voxel in each of the two age groups. These analyses were conducted independently for each age group to identify brain regions where decoding accuracy significantly exceeded chance levels. This design minimized the risk of group differences being driven by sample imbalance, as decoding was performed separately within each group using identical cluster labels and bootstrapping.

A conjunction analysis was then performed to determine the overlapping brain regions in which both younger and older groups exhibited significant differences. Due to the imbalanced sample sizes of the two groups, we performed this analysis using a bootstrapping procedure based on equal-sized subsamples of 24 participants randomly selected from the larger group sample. The choice of 24 subsamples was made to ensure that each subsample in each bootstrap iteration contained a reasonable amount of samples for averaging, while at the same time allowing enough iterations - since we had 27 participants in the older group, n=24 afforded us enough possible combinations.

For each of 100 bootstrap iterations, we took the 24 subsampled participants’ accuracy values, conducted a t-test against chance level (12.5%), and FDR-corrected the results. For this, binary masks were created for each group, labeling voxels with significant decoding accuracy as 1 and non-significant voxels as 0. The intersection of these binary masks identified the voxels that were significant in both groups, highlighting common patterns of neural activity between the younger and older adult group participants.

In addition to the bootstrap-based exploratory conjunction, we performed a voxel-wise minimum-statistic conjunction analysis [79] on the FDR-thresholded decoding maps (q<0.05, cluster extent ≥20 voxels) to identify regions showing above-chance decoding in both groups.

RESULTS

Our main first interest was to establish common areas shared across both age groups. The bootstrapped exploratory conjunction analysis of the FDR-thresholded results uncovered the right insula with an above-chance decoding accuracy of 13.21% (peak voxel at 33 5 -15; 77 voxels - see Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 2).

Shared brain regions showing significant decoding accuracy for OA and YA groups (FDR-corrected (q<0.05) decoding maps shown; thresholded against 12.5% chance level).

To confirm the robustness of this effect, we conducted a voxel-wise minimum-statistic conjunction on the FDR-thresholded maps (q<0.05, cluster extent ≥20 voxels). This analysis again identified the right insula [T=5.03, p<0.001, 51 -7 -4], consistent with the bootstrap results, but also revealed additional peaks in the left superior temporal cortex [T=5.46, p<0.001, -45 -19 -16], left middle temporal gyrus [T=4.30, p<0.001, -60 -52 14], right inferior frontal cortex [T=5.34, p<0.001, 42 17 -16], and bilateral supplementary motor areas [T=4.94, p<0.001, 33 17 26]. The broader spatial distribution in this analysis likely reflects its higher sensitivity relative to the conservative bootstrap-based approach, which was designed to detect only the most robust and spatially stable effects across resamples.

Next, we looked into group-specific areas, excluding the brain areas found in the conjunction analysis. We found significant decoding of the facial expressions in the YA group, which highlighted left thalamus/hippocampus/putamen, caudate and FFA, OFA, as well as left pSTS, middle cingulum, parahippocampal gyrus, bilateral PCC (posterior cingulate cortex), temporal areas, premotor cortex (see Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 3). In the OA group, no area survived the bootstrapped analysis.

Brain regions showing significant decoding accuracy for YA group (FDR-corrected (q<0.05) decoding maps shown; thresholded against 12.5% chance level; regions identified in the conjunction analysis were excluded).

To formally test whether decoding accuracy was higher in younger than older adults, we conducted a voxel-wise two-sample t-test (Welch’s test, assuming unequal variances, FDR-corrected, q<0.05) comparing the two groups’ decoding maps. This analysis revealed widespread regions where YA decoding exceeded OA, including frontal, temporal, and occipital areas (FDR-corrected, q<0.05). The largest between-group difference was observed in the right middle frontal gyrus (t=8.19, [42, 50, 23], cluster size: 78 voxels). Additional significant differences were found in the left inferior frontal gyrus (t=5.98, [-51, 44, 5], cluster size: 9 voxels), occipital cortex (t=5.53, [39, -88, 11], cluster size: 3 voxels), and in the left insula (t=4.72, [-66, -1, 26], cluster size: 3 voxels).

At uncorrected voxel-wise threshold of p<0.001 (≥20 voxels), three large clusters were observed: one in the right middle frontal gyrus ([42, 50, 23]; t=8.19; cluster size at p<0.001: 226 voxels), one in the left caudate/thalamus ([-27, 26, -28]; t=5.58; cluster: 57 voxels), and one in the left inferior parietal cortex ([-57, 29, 11]; t=6.21; cluster: 47 voxels). These uncorrected findings did not survive multiple comparisons correction and should be considered exploratory.

These findings confirm that younger adults in this sample exhibit stronger and more widespread decoding than older adults, and that even the shared right insula region shows higher decoding in YA.

DISCUSSION

Studies on facial expression processing have so far been mostly conducted with static stimuli. However, dynamic representations of facial expressions may potentially convey deeper and more realistic insight into the complex context of facial expression processing. While our study did not include a static condition, prior research suggests that dynamic stimuli can engage broader or different neural mechanisms than static expressions [13-15]. In the present study we therefore aimed at investigating the neural processing of dynamic facial expressions within a decoding approach. Importantly, we sought to reveal age-specific and shared decoding patterns, shedding light on the mechanisms that influence the processing of expressions throughout the lifespan. Our stimuli for this consisted of 25 conversational and emotional expressions that were first clustered into 8 data-driven clusters for decoding.

Shared brain areas for facial expression processing

Our analysis revealed significant common areas within which above-chance decodability was possible for both young and older adults. Specifically, the right insula showed significant decoding results in both age groups as found via our robust bootstrapped conjunction analysis. As discussed in the introduction, prior studies have established the insula as an integral part of emotion processing in the brain - not only for static, but also for dynamic expressions. Importantly, our results show that this region is also able to decode a wider range of dynamic facial expressions (including conversational and emotional expressions) across two age groups, indicating its importance in affective processing in the brain.

Age-related differences

Formal between-group comparisons (voxel-wise two-sample t-test, FDR-corrected) confirmed that decoding accuracy was significantly higher in younger than older adults in these widespread regions, including even the shared right insula, where both groups showed above-chance decoding. These findings point to a preserved core representation and broader engagement in younger participants, though the cross-sectional design precludes attributing these differences solely to aging.

In terms of group specific regions in the brain, we found several areas in the younger group (including pSTS, caudate, thalamus, middle cingulum, parahippocampal gyrus, PCC, motor areas, inferior parietal gyrus, OFA, FFA, and frontal cortex), which did not reach significance in the OA group in terms of decoding accuracy. The bilateral PCC (posterior cingulate cortex) region was implicated - the PCC is known for reward and motivation-related processing [72], emotion regulation [68, 80], as well as for processing of facial identity and familiarity [70, 81]. Previous studies have suggested that OFA exhibits distinct neural processing of facial components situated at precise spatial locations, whereas FFA processes these generally but not specifically [6, 82-84]. Our findings corroborate previous studies of classification and decoding of emotional stimuli and perception of facial expressions in the brain [64, 85], as well as posterior area STS, ventral FFA and OFA [67, 71, 86]. The information regarding facial expressions are processed in ventral regions such as OFA and FFA, while dynamic information is processed in dorsal areas such as dlPFC, STS. These regions represent facial expressions and identities as well as dynamic stimuli [66, 69].

While increasing the searchlight radius could capture more distributed activity, we chose a smaller radius (3 voxels) to prioritize spatial specificity and avoid mixing signals from functionally distinct neighboring regions. Given our focus on local decodability of nuanced facial expressions, maintaining regional interpretability was critical. Future studies could explore varying searchlight sizes to balance spatial specificity and signal robustness.

Limitations and conclusion

As highlighted above, we were not able to identify any additional brain areas in the OA group. One potential reason for this was our project-induced limitation in sample sizes, which necessitated the conservative bootstrapping approach, suggesting that larger sample sizes for the OA group will be needed to assess further common and/or age-specific areas beyond the insula. This sample size limitation partly reflects the challenges of recruiting cognitively healthy, pre-retirement older adults, a specific subgroup selected to focus on early aging effects. Likewise, the facial expressions used in this study were exclusively from younger adults, which may be a potential limitation due to the own-age bias, as older adults might process and recognize faces differently depending on the age of the faces being observed [2, 3], pointing to the need to further experiments using similar-aged expression stimuli. Additionally, we were not able to test decoding of a larger range of expressions due to time constraints while scanning - further studies are therefore necessary to dive deeper into age-related processing differences with a wider range of dynamic facial conversational and emotional expressions.

The clustering of dynamic expressions in the present study was based on combined behavioral ratings from both younger and older participants, which may not fully capture age-specific perceptual structures. Although perceptual differences in dimensions may be influenced by mechanisms like own-age bias or the positivity effect, our design did not include age-specific stimulus sets to directly examine these effects. Because younger participants contributed disproportionately to the ratings, it remains possible that decoding in the older group was influenced by a label structure not fully aligned with their perceptual space. Additionally, while sex differences in emotion perception are well documented (although often small in effect size), the small number of older male participants precluded a reliable sex-stratified analysis. Thus, the possibility that the derived clusters reflect sex-specific perceptual biases cannot be ruled out, and future studies with balanced age-by-sex sampling will be necessary.

Another limitation of our study concerns the lack of significant decoding beyond the insula in the older adult (OA) group. While this could reflect age-related reductions in functional specificity, it may also be partly attributed to the smaller sample size in the OA group, which may have limited statistical power. Despite the use of bootstrapping to mitigate this issue, the possibility remains that additional decodable regions were not detected due to inter-subject variability or insufficient signal strength. Future studies with larger OA cohorts and higher test-retest reliability are needed to more definitively characterize age-related differences in expression decoding. Our participant sample showed a gender imbalance, particularly in the younger adult group. Although prior behavioral work found no significant gender effects, this may still impact neural decoding results in subtle ways. Future studies should aim for gender-balanced samples and examine potential sex-related modulations of facial expression processing.

Related to our analytical approach, we complemented the within-group decoding analyses with a formal between-group comparison (voxel-wise two-sample t-test, FDR-corrected), which confirmed that younger adults exhibit significantly higher decoding accuracy than older adults in widespread regions, including the shared right insula. This strengthens our interpretation that the observed differences are not solely due to thresholding or sample size differences but reflect true group differences in decoding strength and spatial distribution. Nonetheless, the smaller OA sample and cross-sectional design limit the strength of group-level inferences, and residual confounds (e.g., unequal variance, unmeasured covariates) cannot be ruled out.

With respect to conjunction analysis, while we complemented the bootstrap-based overlap with a voxel-wise minimum-statistic conjunction analysis [79], both approaches should still be regarded as exploratory rather than as a formal test of the conjunction null hypothesis. Thus, although they provide convergent evidence for shared effects, these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Another limitation is the use of an orthogonal gender-classification task. Although this task has precedent in prior fMRI studies and is effective for sustaining attention while minimizing top-down emotional effects, we did not include pilot validation within this dataset. This limits the strength of inferences about attentional control.

Additionally, some ROI-level decoding accuracies approached or fell below chance, particularly in smaller or subcortical regions with high variance. These values were reported for transparency but should not be interpreted as evidence of meaningful effects.

Finally, we note that our findings do not stem from a longitudinal design and hence it cannot be excluded that there are additional cohort-specific effects in our sample, which would need to be tested for in a different setup.

In conclusion, our findings shed light on decodability of conversational and emotional dynamic facial expressions in the brain across aging.
