Needle tract seeding of papillary thyroid carcinoma 18 years after initial surgery without presurgical diagnosis of malignancy: a case report
Authors: Hironori Ando, Yayoi Kuwabara-Ohmura, Akira Sumida, Takashi Shibuya, Takeshi Shimada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeedle tract seeding (NTS) is a rare complication of fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC). An 87-year-old man presented with a subcutaneous cervical nodule. Eighteen years prior, he had undergone right thyroid lobectomy owing to tracheal compression. At that time, although preoperative FNAC of the thyroid nodule showed a ‘normal or benign’ finding, postoperative histology revealed papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC). This time, ultrasonography and FNAC of the newly developed cervical subcutaneous nodule indicated PTC, suggesting NTS. Local tumor resection was performed. Using thinner needles and releasing suction before needle withdrawal can reduce, but cannot entirely prevent, NTS. Clinicians should be aware of NTS during the follow-up of patients with PTC.Keywords: needle tract seeding, thyroid fine-needle aspiration cytology, papillary thyroid carcinoma

Introduction

Fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC) is a minimally invasive and valuable diagnostic method for thyroid nodules. Although generally considered safe, complications may occur [1]. Known complications include bleeding, pain, transient vocal cord paralysis, and acute thyroid swelling. However, needle tract seeding (NTS) is a relatively rare complication. NTS occurs when cancer cells aspirated by a needle are disseminated in tissue along the needle tract [2]. NTS is associated with a relatively aggressive tumor behavior. We report a case of papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) in which NTS occurred 18 years after initial FNAC and surgery.

Case report

An 87-year-old man presented with a self-detected nodule in the right neck region. He had undergone a right thyroid lobectomy 18 years previously at another hospital because of a right thyroid tumor compressing his trachea, for which preoperative FNAC did not indicate malignancy. Postoperative histological examination revealed a PTC. Extrathyroid invasion and lymph node metastasis were not observed. He was under observation by his primary care physician and received 50 μg levothyroxine supplementation. Final neck ultrasonography was performed 10 years after surgery. Additionally, he was prescribed 100 mg of irbesartan and 10 mg of amlodipine for hypertension. The patient had no family history of thyroid diseases.

Upon examination, his height and weight were 159 cm and 67.5 kg, respectively. A palpable, elastic, and movable mass of approximately 10 mm was detected in his right neck, superior to the postoperative scar, and no palpable lymphadenopathy was observed. Other examination findings were normal. Laboratory data (Table 1) did not indicate hypothyroidism or elevated Tg levels. However, the evaluation for Tg levels was limited by the presence of the remaining thyroid left lobe.

Other multifocal dissemination, or lymph node metastasis, was not observed in this specimen. The patient was under observation with 50 μg of levothyroxine supplementation, that is, the same dose before surgery. One year after surgery, the patient recovered without any functional impairments such as neck movement disorders. No evidence of local recurrence was observed. The patient’s serum thyroglobulin level was 15.6 ng/dL, which is within the normal range. No thyroid-stimulating hormone suppression therapy was administered.

Discussion

Thyroid FNAC is a simple and valuable procedure for diagnosing thyroid nodules, particularly PTCs, with a high diagnostic accuracy [3]. Complications associated with this procedure, including bleeding, pain, transient vocal cord paralysis, and acute thyroid swelling, and NTS, are rare [1]. According to reports, the frequency of NTS in PTC is approximately 0.14% [1, 4].

A notable feature of the present case is that NTS developed after an exceptionally long interval—18 years following the initial surgery. Ito et al. reported that the duration from FNA to the onset of NTS ranges widely from 2 to 131 months [4]. They further noted early-onset cases (2–68 months) were associated with poorly differentiated carcinoma and the presence of lymph node metastasis at the time of initial surgery [4]. In our case, no lymph node metastasis was observed during the initial surgery, and the prolonged interval before NTS onset is consistent with their findings. Detailed histological information regarding the original tumor was not available.

We summarized previously reported cases of NTS in PTC identified in our literature review in Table 2 [2, 4–10]. The interval from FNA to NTS varies from 2 to 120 months. Among these cases, the 18-year interval in our case is exceptionally long, indicating that clinicians should be aware of NTS, even a long time after surgery.

Spontaneous subcutaneous or intrastrap muscular recurrence (SIMR) could not be excluded in this case. However, we considered our case was more likely to be NTS than SIMR for the following reasons: a) the tumor was located within the presumed needle tract of the original FNA. b) Linear lesions were observed on MRI, and c) lymphoid or other distant metastases were absent [1].

Approximately 45% of SIMR cases have been reported to be associated with distant metastasis [11], suggesting differential diagnosis between NTS and SIMR is important, though often challenging. Differential diagnosis must rely on clinical and radiological features such as those mentioned above.

Salvage surgical resection should be performed for NTS lesions because seeding tumors are reported to be more aggressive than primary tumors [4, 11, 12]. Adjuvant radioactive iodine therapy or suppressive T4 therapy has been reported to be useful in reducing the risk of NTS [6]. However, in many cases, successful removal of lesions does not result in further local seeding or death directly attributable to NTS [12]. Moreover, radioactive iodine therapy would require additional total thyroidectomy, and suppressive T4 therapy carries potential risks such as cardiac events, including atrial fibrillation. Therefore, we did not administer any additional therapies. Nevertheless, we consider careful long-term follow-up to be necessary.

To prevent NTS, the following measures have been recommended [4, 11]: a) using 23-gauge or thinner needles, b) releasing suction before needle withdrawal, c) avoiding piston-like movement and ensuring the needle does not penetrate the posterior capsule. In this present case, details of the FNA procedure at the time of the initial diagnosis were unavailable, making evaluation of these measures impossible.

In Table 2, although some previous reports involved the use of 22G needles, NTS has also been observed with 23G needles, suggesting that we cannot completely prevent NTS. Additionally, some studies have suggested that resecting the omohyoid muscle during surgery may help prevent intramuscular seeding; however, this approach is considered too invasive for routine surgical procedures. In this case, the initial FNAC did not detect malignancy, suggesting that simply contacting the PTC lesion without aspirating malignant cells during the FNA procedure could cause NTS. Generally, during thyroidectomy, the skin and subcutaneous tissues at the fine-needle aspiration (FNA) site are not resected, indicating that NTS can occur regardless of the histopathological stage or whether curative resection is performed.

In summary, this case represents a rare example of NTS from PTC occurring after a prolonged latency of 18 years. Although rare, NTS should be considered during long-term follow-up, even in patients with no malignancy detected on FNAC or no evidence of recurrence for extended periods.
