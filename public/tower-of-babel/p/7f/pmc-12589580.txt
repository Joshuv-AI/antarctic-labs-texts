Mitochondrial proteins contribute to the pathogenesis of myasthenia gravis
Authors: Fangzheng Cao, Houwen Zhang, Bin Xu, Juan Wang, Bin Wu, Yu Zhou, Jialin Yu, Chunrong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder mediated by B-cells, characterized by muscle weakness and fatigue. Mitochondria, essential for energy production and muscle function, have been implicated in MG. Despite their importance, the exact relationship between mitochondrial proteins (MPs) and MG remains unclear. This study utilized two-sample Mendelian Randomization (TSMR) to investigate potential causal associations between MPs and MG. Data from the largest available genome-wide associated study (GWAS), comprising 1873 acetylcholine receptor (AchR) antibody-positive MG patients (1278 late-onset MG [LOMG] and 595 early-onset MG [EOMG]), were analyzed. A total of 66 MPs were selected as exposure variables, with MG and its subtypes as outcomes. Analyses employed inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR-PRESSO, MR-Egger, and weighted median methods, with heterogeneity assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic. Significant causal associations were found between MPs and MG subtypes. For LOMG, GrpE protein homolog 1, oligoribonuclease, protein SCO1 homolog, and rRNA methyltransferase 3 were linked to increased risk. Cytochrome c oxidase subunit 7A1 was associated with higher EOMG risk, while [pyruvate dehydrogenase (acetyl-transferring)] kinase isozyme 1, dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase, and NFU1 iron-sulfur cluster scaffold were linked to reduced EOMG risk. MPs such as dihydrolipoyl dehydrogenase and NAD-dependent protein deacylase sirtuin-5 showed protective effects against MG, while GrpE protein homolog 1, mitochondrial glutamate carrier 2, oligoribonuclease, and protein SCO1 homolog were associated with increased risk. This MR analysis suggests potential causal relationships between MPs and MG, highlighting the need for further research to validate these findings and explore the mechanisms underlying these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22607-w.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder marked by autoantibodies against acetylcholine receptors (AChR) at the neuromuscular junction, causing skeletal muscle weakness. It affects various muscle groups, potentially leading to life-threatening respiratory issues1,2. Driven by B-cell and T-cell interactions, approximately 85% of cases present with AChR autoantibodies3,4. Current treatments offer limited effectiveness, with 15% of patients showing little improvement5. Therefore, understanding the pathogenesis of MG and identifying new therapeutic targets is crucial. In addition to energy production, mitochondria plays a crucial role in biosynthesis, cell signaling, and cell death6,7. They release various signals, including proteins and metabolites, which regulate cellular processes7.

Previous studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial dysfunction plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of several autoimmune diseases8,9 and regard mitochondria as the heart of immunity10, suggesting that mitochondria may serve as potential therapeutic targets for inflammatory disorders10. Current research indicates that the immune system is not the sole factor in the pathogenesis of MG. Mitochondrial dysfunction also plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of the disease11,12. However, no studies have successfully explored the causal relationship between mitochondrial proteins (MPs) and the risk of MG.

Mendelian Randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to estimate causal effects, reducing confounding by eliminating environmental influences. It also minimizes reverse causation and measurement errors, as genetic variants are fixed. MR provides an alternative to randomized controlled trials for studying exposures, helping clarify causality by ensuring that the exposure precedes the outcome13. This study applied two-sample MR (TSMR) to explore the causal relationships between MPs and MG and its subtypes using genome-wide associated study (GWAS) data.

Methods

We selected 66 MPs as exposure variables and chose MG as well as its subtypes (early-onset MG, EOMG, and late-onset MG, LOMG) as outcome variables.

Additionally, our MR study is based on three key assumptions: Firstly, the selected IVs are strongly associated with MPs, ensuring that the IVs can reliably serve as proxies for MPs. Secondly, there is no direct association between the IVs and the onset of MG, EOMG, or LOMG, meaning that the IVs do not influence the outcome independently of their effect on MPs. Thirdly, the IVs influence MG and its subtypes solely through the MPs, without any other pathways or confounding factors. Our research workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data sources of MPs

The MPs data utilized in this study were extracted from the IEU open GWAS database (available at: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/) derived from a comprehensive genomic analysis conducted by Sun et al.14. The study integrated genetic data with proteomic measurements from healthy blood donors enrolled in the INTERVAL study to identify protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs). Utilizing a high-throughput, aptamer-based proteomics technology, SOMAscan, the research measured levels of 3,622 plasma proteins, across 3,301 participants. Genetic associations were subsequently explored through comprehensive genome-wide association analyses, enabling the identification and characterization of pQTLs within this large dataset.

Data sources of MG and its subgroups

We utilized data derived from the most recently released and currently largest GWAS database that included 1,873 patients diagnosed with AchR antibody-positive MG (Table 1, available at: https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/)15. This cohort was compared with a control group comprising 36,370 healthy individuals to identify genetic loci associated with the disease. Distinct analyses were conducted for subgroups categorized by the age of onset: EOMG, defined as diagnosis at age 45 or younger, included 595 cases and 2,718 controls; and LOMG, defined as diagnosis after age 45, included 1,278 cases and 33,652 controls. These subgroups allowed for the exploration of age-specific genetic determinants relevant to the pathogenesis of MG. Additionally, the genetic findings from the initial GWAS were validated using an independent cohort sourced from the UK Biobank, further solidifying the reliability of the identified genetic associations. This detailed stratification and validation process provided a robust dataset for our MR study.

IVs selection

For the TSMR analyses, we selected Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs) significantly associated with MPs, maintaining a genome-wide significance threshold of p < 5 × 10− 616. This selection criteria ensured the avoidance of weak instrument bias while also verifying that these SNPs were not linked to potential confounders, thus adhering to the fundamental MR prerequisites for a robust and uncontaminated genetic association with MPs. To mitigate any biases due to linkage disequilibrium, we established stringent criteria, setting the R2 threshold at 0.001 and the genetic distance at 10,000 kb to guarantee the independence of each IV17. Additionally, we required a minor allele frequency of at least 0.01 and excluded palindromic SNPs and those absent in the outcome dataset. To determine the strength of the association between the instrumental variables and the exposure, we employed the F-statistic, with a threshold greater than 10 indicating a substantial association between the genetic instruments and MPs, thus validating their use in our analyses18.

MR analyses

Three MR methods were employed in our study, including inverse variance weighted (IVW)19, MR-Egger regression20, and weighted median (WM)21, to assess the causal relationships between MPs and MG represented by odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) values. IVW, which aggregates Wald ratios weighted by their inverse variances, was regarded as our primary method; MR Egger Regression, which adjusts for potential pleiotropy by including an intercept term in the regression; and WM, offering a robust median-based estimate that tolerates up to 50% invalid instruments. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q method22, with significance defined at p < 0.05. To explore potential horizontal pleiotropy, the MR-Egger intercept test23 and the MR-PRESSO global test24 were applied. A robustness check through the leave-one-out method25 was performed to confirm the reliability of our findings. Statistical computations were conducted using the Two Sample MR package (Version 0.5.9) within R studio (Version: 4.3.2), with visualization through scatter, forest, and funnel plots to ensure a thorough evaluation of the instrumental variables’ influence and distribution. Given our exploratory study, we chose not to apply multiple testing correction. We established a statistical significance threshold at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first MR study to explore the causal relationships between MPs and MG. Our findings uncovered that MPs could affect LOMG, EOMG, and MG. ORN, SCO1, MRM3, and GrpEL1 were found to be linked with a higher risk of LOMG. COX7A1 was found to be linked with a higher risk of EOMG, whereas NFU1, DLD and PDK1 were discovered to be related with a lower risk of EOMG. In addition, Mitochondrial GC2, ORN, SCO1, and GrpEL1 were related to a higher risk of MG, whereas SIRT5 and DLD were found to be associated with a lower risk of MG. Moreover, MR analysis of these genes was implemented using these MPs-associated SNPs paired on top of the genes, showing a causal connection between these genes and LOMG, EOMG, and MG, which demonstrates that LOMG, EOMG, and MG may affected by MPs through these genes.

MG is an acquired autoimmune disease mediated by autoantibodies, dependent on cellular immunity, and involving complement. It affects the postsynaptic membrane at the NMJ, causing transmission disorders at the neuromuscular junction and leading to skeletal muscle contraction weakness. Most MG patients have autoantibodies against AchR, while a very small number of MG patients are mediated by muscle-specific kinase (MUSK), lipoprotein-related protein 4 (LRP4)26. Recent studies demonstrated the muscle tissue of MG patients display abnormal mitochondrial morphology and function, and the levels of energy are often significantly lower than normal27,28. In MG, abnormally shaped and structured mitochondria on muscle biopsy, ragged-red fibers, loss of mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes-1, and muscle aerobic dysfunction were also observed on muscle biopsy29,30. This finding suggested that MG is associated with mitochondrial abnormalities.

MPs are key components of cellular energy metabolism and life activities, they are mainly responsible for promoting energy production, regulating cell death and apoptosis, maintaining metabolic balance, and dealing with oxidative stress31. These proteins play a central role in ensuring the proper function of cytochrome c oxidase and other respiratory chain enzyme complexes, while also being involved in maintaining the structural integrity of cells and mitochondrial homeostasis31. The occurrence of MG is accompanied by MP dysfunction. However, the relationship between MP dysfunction and MG remains unknown and there are few reports on the direct relationship between MPs and MG.

In our study, we found ORN, SCO1, and GrpEL1 were related to a higher risk of MG and LOMG. ORN is involved in the final steps of RNA metabolism, affects transcription initiation, and is essential for maintaining normal cell metabolism, mitochondrial structure and function, cell growth and morphology32. Our study found a positive correlation between the expression of ORN and the risk of MG. The metabolic disorder of RNA may play a major role in this process, yet the specific mechanism still remains to be further investigated. The SCO1 is a copper-binding protein mainly expressed in adipocytes in mammalian white adipose tissue. It is involved in the assembly of cytochrome c oxidase and the maintenance of cellular copper homeostasis. Overexpression of the protein will lead to disorders of copper metabolism in the organism33. Copper metabolism plays a crucial role in immune response and the body’s antioxidant defense34, and there have been researches suggesting that copper disorders are associated with autoimmune diseases35. Our study revealed that there is a positive correlation between the level of protein SCO1 and the risk of MG, which supports our findings. GrpEL1 has diverse functions. It regulates mitochondrial redox balance, senses intracellular heat shock and redox state changes, and influences cellular physiological activities36,37. Regarding redox balance, GrpEL1 may indirectly modulate mitochondrial redox-related protein functions by regulating mtHsp70 activity, thus maintaining intracellular redox homeostasis37. Our results suggest that the expression of GrpEL1 is positively correlated with the risk of MG. We infer that this may be related to the disorder of intracellular stress signaling pathways caused by abnormal expression of GrpEL1, which remains to be further verified.

Mitochondrial GC2 is a transporter protein located in the inner mitochondrial membrane and belongs to the solute carrier family 2538. Its primary function is to transport glutamate from the cytoplasm into the mitochondrial matrix39. Inside the mitochondria, glutamate can be converted into α-ketoglutarate, which enters the tricarboxylic acid cycle to provide energy for the cell40. In MG, antibody-mediated disruption of synaptic transmission increases the energy demand of muscle cells to maintain function41. Dysfunction of mitochondrial GC2 impairs the transport of glutamate into mitochondria, thereby disrupting the tricarboxylic acid cycle and ATP production, ultimately leading to insufficient energy supply at the neuromuscular junction. Thus, mitochondrial GC2 is associated with a higher risk of MG. The MRM ubiquitously exists among all living organisms. Our study has found that the expression level of MRM3 is positively correlated with the risk of LOMG. Although the specific mechanism remains to be explored, it may be related to the disorder of the functions of MRM, such as its resistance to oxidative stress, influence on the structure and function of ribosomes, and regulation of mRNA translation42. COX is the terminal enzyme of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. It catalyzes the reduction of oxygen to water and pumps protons, driving ATP synthesis, maintaining energy metabolic balance, and regulating the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) to prevent oxidative stress43–45. COX7A1 is one of the core subunits of cytochrome c oxidase. Although there is no direct study on the relationship between COX7A1 and EOMG, previous studies have found that overexpression of COX7A1 may interfere with the normal functions of other subtypes of COX triggering abnormal metabolic adaptation in tissues46. Meanwhile, excessively high COX activity may increase electron leakage, leading to excessive accumulation of ROS and aggravating cell damage47. Our research shows that the expression level of COX7A1 is positively correlated with the risk of MG, which may be related to the accumulation of ROS and abnormal metabolic adaptation.

SIRT5 belongs to one of the three mitochondrial proteins of the sirtuin family of NAD+ dependent deacylases which control several metabolic pathways in the mitochondria48. SIRT5 regulates succinate dehydrogenase through desuccinylation influencing the tricarboxylic acid cycle and ATP production, thereby affecting neuromuscular junction function49. SIRT5 plays a crucial role in eliminating ROS and maintaining glutathione metabolism, while patients with MG often exhibit elevated levels of ROS50,51. In addition, SIRT5 can also regulate the activity and differentiation of innate immune and T cells52. Collectively, SIRT5 may exert a protective role in the development and progression of MG by maintaining mitochondrial metabolism, regulating T cell-mediated immunity, and alleviating oxidative stress. This supports our finding that SIRT5 is associated with a lower risk of MG. We also found that DLD is a protective factor for both MG and EOMG. In addition, NFU1 and PDK1 were identified as protective factors for EOMG in our study. DLD is the E3 component of multiple α-keto acid dehydrogenase complexes, also known as the E3 enzyme. It is a flavoprotein oxidoreductase that plays a crucial role in cellular energy metabolism, contributing to mitochondrial metabolism, energy production, and redox balance53. E3 is involved in different metabolic pathways within mitochondria54. The few reported the deficiency of DLD present with myopathic symptoms with intermittent myoglobinuria, exertion fatigue, myasthenia, and ptosis55. As a scaffold protein for Fe-S clusters, NFU1 is involved in the synthesis of [2Fe-2 S] and [4Fe-4 S] clusters within mitochondria. It facilitates the directional transfer of Fe-S clusters from the assembly core to target proteins by providing intermediate binding sites56,57. Previous studies have found that the loss of NFU1 function is associated with multiple mitochondrial encephalomyopathies58. Moreover, the inhibition of NFU1 expression can lead to abnormalities in the Fe-S cluster-dependent transsulfuration pathway, an imbalance in the ratio of reduced glutathione, exacerbation of oxidative damage, and abnormal immune responses57. Our research suggests that NFU1 iron-sulfur cluster scaffold homolog has a protective effect against MG, which supports our results. PDK1 is a regulator of cellular metabolic homeostasis. It coordinates glucose and lipid metabolism and redox homeostasis by inhibiting the activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex59,60. PDK1 inhibits the activity of pyruvate dehydrogenase through phosphorylation, reducing the production of acetyl-CoA and decreasing the level of mitochondrial reactive ROS. This process regulates cellular metabolic adaptation and enhances protection against oxidative stress, thereby playing a protective role in MG60.

Dysfunction of MPs leading to dysfunction of key enzymes of oxidative phosphorylation will result in reduced ATP production and affect energy supply to muscle cells61. MPs dysfunction can also lead to abnormal protein function in the electron transport chain causing electron leakage and increasing ROS production62. Excessive ROS can cause cell damage and inflammatory response, and aggravate the condition. In addition, abnormalities in mitochondrial membrane proteins may result in decreased membrane potential, affecting mitochondrial function and cell survival63. Mitochondrial antigens can serve as targets of autoimmune attack, promoting immune cells to attack the neuromuscular junction4. Studies have shown that certain proteins on the mitochondrial membrane, such as the outer membrane protein (TOM) and inner membrane protein (TM) complexes, may play a role in immune regulation64. Thus, MPs may contributed to the pathogenesis of MG.

To date, there are limited studies about MPs in MG. Our MR analysis offers a approach to infer a direct causal association between MPs and MG. In this study, SNPs with genome-wide association, independent inheritance, and no LD were carefully selected as IVs to clarify the causal association between the MPs and MG. Additionally, the F statistics in our analysis are substantially higher than 10, reflecting a minimal feasibility of weak IV bias. However, this research does have some limitations.

Firstly, the number of genetic loci identified in the MPs GWAS is limited, which may weaken the IVs, reduce the statistical power of our MR analysis, and restrict the scope of our findings. This could lead to an incomplete representation of mitochondrial protein-related genetic influences on MG. Secondly, the dataset mainly consists of European populations, which may introduce population-specific biases and limit the generalizability of our findings to non-European groups. Genetic heterogeneity across different ethnic backgrounds could influence the associations observed, highlighting the need for validation in diverse populations. Therefore, further research with larger, multi-ethnic cohorts and expanded genetic datasets is essential to comprehensively elucidate the role of MPs in MG pathogenesis.

Conclusion

The current MR analysis thoroughly evaluated the potential causal association between the MPs and MG. Four MPs had a positive causative direction with LOMG and MG, and one MP was discovered to be linked with a higher risk of EOMG, whereas two MPs were found to be linked with a negative causative direction with MG, and three MPs were discovered to be linked with a protective factor of EOMG. Future studies are required to support our current findings and thoroughly investigate the exact mechanism about the MPs in MG to explore potential therapeutic targets for MG.
