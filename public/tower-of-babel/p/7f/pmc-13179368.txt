Alleviatory effect of foliar application of silicon nanoparticles on germination, growth, and photosynthetic pigments of Butterfly pea (Clitoria ternatea) under salt stress
Authors: Ahlam Khalofah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil salinity substantially limits plant establishment and productivity by reducing germination, altering photosynthetic function, and lowering biomass buildup. This study examined the effect of foliar-applied silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) on Clitoria ternatea germination, early seedling development, growth performance, and physiological characteristics under graded salinity stress (0, 50, 100, and 150 mM NaCl). Salinity significantly lowered all evaluated characteristics. Compared with the control, exposure to 150 mM NaCl reduced plant height by approximately 51% and leaf area by nearly 55%. Fresh biomass also declined significantly indicating pronounced growth inhibition under severe salinity stress. Foliar application of silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) markedly alleviated these detrimental effects. Under 150 mM NaCl, SiNP treatment increased plant height by nearly 38% and leaf area by about 24% relative to untreated salt-stressed plants. Likewise, dry biomass improved by approximately 16%, demonstrating the positive regulatory role of SiNPs in mitigating salinity-induced growth suppression. Chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoids were all lowered by salinity but improved after SiNP addition, demonstrating Si’s protective effect on pigment stability. Salinity reduced total chlorophyll, carotenoids, chlorophyll a, and chlorophyll b, but this level increased after SiNP was added, indicating the protective effect of silicon nanoparticles on pigment stability. Biochemical responses validated this mitigation: SiNPs improved salinity-induced proline and soluble sugar accumulations while partially reversing protein, phenolic, and flavonoid decreases. PCA analysis validated these findings by categorizing treatments based on biomass accumulation and structural growth, with SiNP-treated plants clustered in the higher-performance quadrants. SiNP foliar spray increases osmotic adjustment, pigment integrity, and vegetative growth under saline circumstances. Silicon nanoparticles thus constitute an effective, feasible, and environmentally safe method for increasing salt tolerance in C. ternatea.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46349-5.

Introduction

Currently, about one third of the world arable area is suffered by salinization, and this is believed to aggravate further at global level, because of poor irrigation practices, inappropriate agrochemical usage, and industrial pollution. Moreover it is estimated that plant production losses due to salt stress can reach about 50% globally1. Osmotic stress and particular ionic toxicity are the main effects of excessive soluble salt buildup in soils, which disrupts a variety of agricultural plant metabolic processes. Salinity therefore has a negative impact on every stage of plant growth, which eventually lowers crop productivity and yield2. Excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production exacerbates salt-induced phytotoxicity and causes oxidative damage at the cellular level3. The kind and quantity of salts, the genetic potential of the species, the growth stage, and the intensity and duration of stress are only a few of the many variables that affect how plants react to salinity4,5.

Salinity poses serious threats to agriculture and global food security. Therefore, developing strategies to enhance salinity tolerance is essential to meet the dual challenges of food security and sustainable agricultural development. Addressing this issue requires innovative technologies capable of improving resilience, sustainability, and efficiency in agricultural systems6. In this context, nanotechnology has emerged as a rapidly advancing field in agriculture7, offering promising and efficient solutions for sustainable crop production6.

Nanoparticles application in the agriculture sector is contemplated as one of the promising techniques to augment crop productivity under normal8 and harsh environmental conditions including salt stress9. In general, nanoparticles are potential materials for the site-specific delivery of nucleotides, proteins and chemicals under in vitro conditions for achieving vital goals such as improving crop growth and yield as well as resistance against stressful cues. Moreover, nanoparticles are also used in agriculture for the efficient and controlled delivery of fertilizers, herbicides, pesticides and for improving soil physico-chemical properties such as water holding capacity8–10. A nanoparticle is a very fine matter particle with a diameter between 1 and 100 nm. Nanoparticles possess unique physico-chemical properties quite different from those of the same matter with larger size11. Nanoparticles can be synthesized through multiple methods that could be physico-chemical or biological12. The foliar application of NPs gives quicker and better results than soil application of fertilizers13.

The primary premise behind SiNPs use in agriculture is to reduce hazardous. Si can improve the plant’s potential to survive against stresses, along with stimulating physic-mechanical functions in plants14. Si can enhance abiotic stress tolerance, improve activity of photosynthetic, adjust nutrient imbalance, and reduce elements toxicity effect15. SiNPs were also used directly on plants as pesticides, herbicides, and fertilizers16.

SiO2 NPs have been shown to enhance antioxidant enzymatic activity and photosynthetic activity in Aurundianria pigmaea plants under metal stress17. Moreover, improve the development of Triticum aestivum L. plants and reduce the accumulation of cadmium in grains18. Even SiO2 NPs increase phenolic compounds and essential oils of Satureja hortensis L. under cadmium stress19. In addition, SiO2 NPs induce K+ uptake, modulate Na+ content, and decrease the negative impact on the cell wall of Musa acuminata “Gran Nain” plants in abiotic stress conditions20. Even so, they help induce root endodermal silicification and regulate cellular water balance21.

Butterfly pea (Clitoria ternatea L.) is a perennial herbaceous creeping plant that belongs to the Fabaceae family. This plant is mainly distributed in a tropical region that requires high light intensity and is relatively persistent with abiotic stress. As a typical legume plant, its roots form nodules that play a major role in nitrogen fixation in soil and act as a natural fertilizer in agricultural land. Butterfly pea was first cultivated as an ornamental plant and then shifted as fodder for cattle because of its high protein content. The latest research of Butterfly pea is on the exploration of its bioactive compound, such as the antioxidant and protein derived bioactive compounds in Butterfly pea. Cyclotide was an example of a protein-derived bioactive compound that was found almost in every part of Butterfly pea and used as a natural pesticide22,23.

The aim of this study was to look into how foliar application of silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) affected germination, plant growth, photosynthetic pigments, and osmolyte levels in salt-stressed Butterfly pea (Clitoria ternatea L.). The study specifically evaluated plant responses to graded salinity levels (0, 50, 100, and 150 mM NaCl) and assessed whether SiNP application could mitigate salinity-induced reductions in growth attributes, pigment concentration, and osmolyte accumulation, thereby improving physiological performance and stress tolerance in this species.

Materials and methods

Germination experiment

Seeds of Butterfly Pea (Clitoria ternatea) were obtained from the plant nursery, Abha, Saudi Arabia. Homogenized size of C. ternatea seeds were soaked in distilled water for 30 min. 70% ethanol was used for seeds surface sterilization for 30 s. After that the seeds were shaken for 10 min in 5% sodium hypochlorite and finally, they were washed 4 times in sterilized distilled water. After sterilization, 5 seeds were transferred into 12 cm sterile Petri dishes containing filter papers. 5 mL of distilled water was used as control. Different concentrations of treatment solution (50, 100, 150 mM NaCl), and seeds treated with (0, 500, and 1000 mg L⁻¹) of SiO2 NPs solution were sprayed, applied per Butterfly pea plant (stressed or non-stressed) were used. NPs solution was vortex before application to disperse NPs. SiO2 NPs were purchased at Sky Spring Nanomaterials (Houston, TX, USA) with the following characteristics: spherical shape, size (10–20 nm), and surface area of 160 m2/g.

Characterization of silicon nanoparticles

The commercial source of the silicon dioxide nanoparticles (SiO2 NPs) used in this investigation was Sky Spring Nanomaterials, located in Houston, Texas, USA. The manufacturer’s specs state that the nanoparticles had a spherical shape, an average particle size of 10–20 nm, and a specific surface area of roughly 160 m2 g⁻¹. The manufacturer used established analytical procedures to pre-characterize the physicochemical parameters of the supplied SiO2 NPs, including particle size distribution, shape, and purity. As a result, the current study did not conduct any more internal characterization analyses.

Silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) were dispersed in distilled water to prepare a suspension of 500 mg L⁻¹. Since SiNPs are insoluble in water, uniform dispersion was achieved using an ultrasonic processor (e.g., probe sonicator, 20 kHz frequency, 400 W power). The suspension was sonicated for 30 min to prevent aggregation and ensure homogenous distribution of nanoparticles. During preparation, the solution was intermittently stirred to maintain stability. The suspension was freshly prepared before each foliar application to avoid particle sedimentation.

Three replicates were used for each treatment. All previous steps were performed under laminar flow. Petri dishes (control and treated) were numbered and moved to a growth chamber with 23 °C ± 2 and 14/10 h light/dark illumination. When a 2 mm radicle emerged from seed coat and became visible by naked eye, the germinated seeds were counted24,25. Counting process was daily recorded at the same time from the day. Percentage of germination (%), radical length (cm), plumule length (cm), height of seedlings (cm), fresh weight and dry weight of seedlings (mg) were registered 7 days (1 week) post germination. The root and shoot lengths were determined using a ruler. Data representing seedlings shoot and root lengths were based on the randomly selected number of seedlings from replicates of each treatment. Fresh weights were determined directly after harvesting, whereas dry weights were calculated after removal of water content at oven at 70 °C for 48 h. Following treatments with standardized symbols were used during the experiment.

The experiment was designed in a completely randomized factorial design (CRD) consisting of two factors: salinity (0, 50, 100, and 150 mM NaCl) and silicon nanoparticle concentration (0, 500, and 1000 mg L⁻¹), resulting in 12 treatment combinations (4 × 3). Each treatment was replicated three times.

Growth experiment

Butterfly pea seeds were sown in 15 cm diameter plastic pots containing 2.5 kg of sand and pitmoss 1:1. For each pot contained (5) seeds. The seedlings were sufficiently watered with Hoagland nutrient solution and distilled water 3 times in a week. All pots were placed outdoor and sheltered from rain. Temperatures during the experiment were 24–28 °C in the daytime and 17–20 °C at night. The relative humidity was 50–60% and plants were subjected to natural irradiance. Irrigation continued for a week, then the seedlings were treated with salt NaCl concentrations (0, 50, 100, 150 mM) and treated with (0, 500, and 1000 mg L⁻¹) of SiO2 NPs solution was foliar applied, applied per Butterfly pea plant (stressed or non-stressed) 3 times in a week for 3 weeks.

The treatment continued 3 weeks (4 weeks of starting planting) for growth experiment, and at the end of the experiment, the plant samples of each pot were washed with water to remove the suspended soil in them for physiological tests. Then, the growth measurements were taken after 4 weeks of planting include root and shoot length (cm), number of leaves per plant, leaf area (cm2), fresh and dry weight (mg) of plants (the samples were oven-dried at 60 °C to constant weight), and the dry weight was recorded. All growth measurements were taken at three replicates per treatment. The growth experiment followed the same 4 × 3 factorial completely randomized design described above.

Determination of relative water content (RWC)

Three fully expanded young leaves from each treatment were taken. The fresh weight (FW) of each sample was recorded and immediately dipped in test tubes containing distilled water and kept in the dark. After 24 h, the leaves were taken out, wiped with the tissue paper, and the turgid weight (TW) was recorded. The samples were dried at 70 °C for 48 h, and the dry weight (DW) of each sample was determined. Relative water contents were calculated using the formula26.

Determination of photosynthetic pigment contents

For photosynthetic pigment determination, a three fresh leaf sample each of 0.2 g was extracted overnight with 80% acetone (5 mL), then grounded and homogenized. The homogenized mixtures were filtered, and the filtrates were raised to 25 mL by adding 80% acetone in each sample. The absorbance of the filtrates was determined at various wavelengths (663, 645, and 480 nm) using a UV-1900 BMS (Waltham, MA, USA) spectrophotometer. All readings were taken for three samples from each replicate per treatment. The chlorophylls (Chl.a and Chl.b), total chlorophyll (total chls), and carotenoids were calculated to determine their contents and expressed in mg/g FW following (Arnon, 1949) formula. Total anthocyanin content was determined using a previously described method27.

Determination of osmolytes contents

Proline content was determined according to Bates et al.28. Three fresh leaves with approximately 0.5 g of each sample was homogenized in 5 mL of 3% sulfosalicylic acid. Ninhydrin reagent (2 mL) and glacial acetic acid 2 mL were added to the test tube with 2 mL of extract. The mixture was boiled at 90 °C for thirty minutes. The reaction was ended in an ice bath. Then, 5 mL of toluene was added to the reaction mixture after cooling and vortex mixed for 15 s, kept in the dark for 20 min, at room temperature, to allow separation of the toluene layer from the aqueous solution. Each toluene layer was then carefully collected into a clean tube, and absorbance was read at 520 nm using a UV-1900 BMS (Germany) spectrophotometer. Free proline concentration was determined from a standard curve prepared using analytical grade proline and expressed as mg/g FW.

Total soluble sugars (TSS) were extracted and determined, according to Irigoyen et al.29. A 0.2 g sample from each of three fresh leaves was washed with 5 mL 70% (v/v) ethanol and homogenized in 5 mL of 96% (v/v) ethanol and then placed in a boiling water bath at 80 °C for 10 min. After cooling, the extract was centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was stored at 4 °C for measurement. Total soluble sugar concentrations were determined by reacting 0.1 mL of the ethanolic extract with 3 mL of freshly prepared anthrone reagent (150 mg anthrone plus 100 mL of 72% (v/v) sulfuric acid) and placed in a boiling water bath at 80 °C for 15 min. After cooling, the mixture’s absorbance was recorded at 625 nm using a UV-1900 BMS (Waltham, MA USA) spectrophotometer to measure the quantity of total soluble sugars (mg/g FW) using a glucose standard curve.

Determination of protein content

For total soluble protein assay, about 0.5 g of fresh-ground leaves were well homogenized in phosphate buffer (0.05 M-pH 7.8) under cooling, filtered, and centrifuged for 10 min 12,000× g at 4 °C. Uv-Vis spectrum was noted at 595 nm30.

Determination of total phenolics (TPC)

Folin-Ciocalteau method was used for determination of total phenolics content (mg/g FW) according to Singleton et al.31. Briefly, 1 mL of the plant extract or different concentrations of standard (gallic acid) were mixed with 1 mL of Folin reagent. Then 1 mL of 10% (w/v) Na2CO3 solution was added and mixed. The mixture was allowed to stand for 1 h at room temperature and the absorbance was measured at 700 nm using spectrophotometer. Total phenolics content was expressed (mg) as gallic acid equivalent g− 1 dry weight.

Determination of total flavonoid (TFC)

The content of total flavonoids (mg/g FW) was determined calorimetrically using aluminum chloride according to Zhishen et al.32. Briefly, 1 mL extract or different concentrations of quercetin standard solution was mixed with 0.3 mL of 5% (w/v) NaNo2 solution. After 6 min, 0.3 mL of 10% (w/v) Al (Cl)3 solution was added and the mixture was allowed to stand for a further 6 min before 0.4 mL of 1 MNaOH was added. The mixture was mixed well and placed for 12 min and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The results were expressed as mg quercetin equivalent g− 1 dry weight.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted in a completely randomized factorial design (4 × 3), with salinity levels (0, 50, 100, 150 mM NaCl) as factor A and silicon nanoparticle concentrations (0, 500, 1000 mg L⁻¹) as factor B. Data were subjected to two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using SPSS version 16. Prior to ANOVA, normality and homogeneity of variance were tested using Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using standardized data to evaluate multivariate relationships among measured parameters. Means of the main factors and their interaction were compared using the least significant difference (LSD) test at P ≤ 0.0533.

Results

Growth traits of Clitoria ternatea

The growth results presented salinity (0, 50, 100, and 150 mM NaCl) and foliar silicon nanoparticle (SiNP) administration (0 mg L⁻¹ (control) and 500 mg L⁻¹ foliar application) affect the growth characteristics of Clitoria ternatea. Increasing salinity caused a progressive reduction in all measured metrics. Plant height reduced from 22.52 ± 1.05 cm in the control (0 mM NaCl, 0 SiNPs) to 11.03 ± 1.07 cm with 150 mM NaCl and no SiNPs. The leaf area decreased in non-saline circumstances in 150 mM NaCl. Fresh biomass decreased in the control at the maximum salinity level (Fig. 1a,b).

(1a−1b): Growth parameters for the responses of Clitoria ternatea to graded salinity stress and foliar-applied silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs).

The use of SiNPs alleviated these inhibitory effects. Under 150 mM NaCl, SiNP-treated plants showed improvements in all characteristics. Plant height rose from 11.03 ± 1.07 cm (without SiNPs) to 15.19 ± 1.04 cm after SiNP foliar application. After SiNP application, leaf area grew from 3.02 ± 0.52 cm2 to 3.74 ± 0.47 cm2 whereas fresh biomass increased from 16.75 ± 1.32 mg to 17.04 ± 0.74 mg. Dry biomass showed a similar pattern, increasing from 8.79 ± 0.36 mg at 150 mM NaCl to 10.17 ± 0.48 mg with SiNPs (Fig. 1a,b).

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) results showed that salinity had a significant impact on all growth characteristics, including root and shoot length, leaf number, leaf area, and fresh and dry plant weight. Si nanoparticles also greatly increased the growth characteristics in Butterfly pea. Except for a minor influence on dry weight, the interaction between NaCl and Si NPs was largely non-significant, showing that both variables acted primarily separately (Table 1). Salinity significantly suppressed germination percentage and seedling vigor parameters. Radicle length, plumule length, and seedling biomass declined progressively with increasing NaCl concentration. The strongest inhibition was observed at 150 mM NaCl.

Growth traits of Butterfly pea (PCA)

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to summarize the multivariate variance in C. ternatea growth parameters under various salt levels and foliar silicon nanoparticle (SiNP) treatments. The first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) describe the main axes of variation in the six growth parameters: root length, shoot length, number of leaves, leaf area, fresh weight, and dry weight.

PC1 was strongly related with positive loadings for all evaluated growth parameters, with values ranging from 0.3709 (fresh weight) to 0.4383 (root length). This shows that PC1 is a generic growth performance axis in which improvements in any morphological attribute benefit the component. The relatively high and uniform loadings indicate that PC1 represents overall vegetative vigour and biomass accumulation.

PC2 showed a distinct pattern, with negative loadings for root length (−0.1046), number of leaves (−0.2625), shoot length (−0.3445), and leaf area (−0.3575), whereas fresh weight (0.6922) and dry weight (0.4410) showed strong positive relationships. This distinction shows that PC2 differentiates between structural growth traits (root and shoot elongation, leaf development) and biomass-related traits (fresh and dry weight). PC2 was kept to show secondary trends pertaining to biomass allocation even though it explained a lesser percentage of the overall variance. But PC1, which explained the majority variance and represented overall plant growth performance, was the main basis for interpretations. The substantial positive loading of fresh weight on PC2 indicates that biomass buildup accounts for a disproportionate amount of variation along this component (Table 2).

Germination and early seedling growth

Salinity stress resulted in a noticeable and persistent reduction in all early seedling growth characteristics of C. ternatea. Seeds treated to increasing NaCl concentrations (50, 100, and 150 mM) showed a steady decrease in germination %, radicle length, plumule length, seedling height, and fresh and dry seedling biomass. Seedlings remained vigorous in non-saline settings, but the addition of salt significantly reduced their growth, particularly at 150 mM, where the inhibitory effects were most prominent.

The use of silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) increased seedling performance across all salinity levels. Although the quantity of benefit varied by characteristic, SiNP treatment consistently increased germination and seedling development when compared to untreated plants at the same salinity level. In the absence of salt stress, 500 and 1000 mg L⁻¹ SiNPs enhanced radicle and plumule length, seedling height, and biomass accumulation.

SiNPs played a more prominent effect at moderate salinity (50 and 100 mM NaCl), promoting better and healthier seedling growth and maintaining higher biomass than plants without SiNPs. At the greatest salinity level (150 mM NaCl), SiNP administration did not fully restore growth to pre-saline levels, but it did ameliorate stress-induced decreases (Table 3). Seedlings treated with 1000 mg L⁻¹ SiNPs showed significant improvement in radicle length, seedling height, and fresh and dry weight, suggesting a dose-dependent beneficial effect (Fig. 3).

The correlation heatmap reveals high positive connections between all evaluated growth variables. Discussion

Salinity is one of the most harmful abiotic stimuli for leguminous plants, and the significant reductions in germination, seedling vigour, vegetative development, and pigment stability in C. ternatea demonstrate its vulnerability to ionic and osmotic imbalance. In the present study, 150 mM NaCl caused a 51% reduction in plant height, a 54.9% decrease in leaf area, and a 38.5% decline in fresh biomass compared with control plants. Foliar application of SiNPs improved plant height by 37.7%, leaf area by 23.8%, and dry biomass by 15.7% under 150 mM NaCl, indicating partial recovery of growth performance.

Salt stress inhibited both cell division and elongation, as evidenced by decreases in radicle elongation, plumule growth, biomass, leaf expansion, and water content at all NaCl levels4,34,35. Reduced germination at moderate and high salinity levels is due to osmotic restriction of water intake and toxic buildup of Na⁺ and Cl⁻, which limit enzyme activity in the early stages of seed metabolism2–5.

Pigment reductions matched salinity-induced growth inhibition. Chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll decreased by more than 35% at 150 mM NaCl, indicating significant disruption of photosynthetic machinery. Declines in chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids indicate disturbances in chloroplast membrane stability and inhibition of important biosynthetic enzymes due to ionic toxicity36,37. These pigment decreases most likely contributed to poorer biomass results since pigment concentrations are significantly correlated with photosynthetic capacity. The drop in relative water content (RWC) at greater salinity implies severe osmotic stress and impaired water transport, which is a common response in legumes under comparable conditions3,38.

The application of silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) greatly reduced these detrimental effects while reliably improving plant performance across all salinity levels. The significant interaction between salinity and SiNP treatments (P ≤ 0.05) suggests that the protective role of silicon nanoparticles was more pronounced at higher salinity levels. The significant increase in plant height, leaf area, biomass, pigment content, and RWC indicates that foliar SiNPs increased stress resilience by increasing osmotic balance, water retention, and structural integrity. Similar advantages in Si-treated plants have been widely documented, emphasizing Si’s function in cell wall strength, ion transport regulation, and water use efficiency39,40,41. The increased RWC indicates that SiNPs improved root membrane integrity and reduced transpirational water loss, which supports previous findings that Si reduces membrane permeability and promotes tissue hydration42,43.

Metabolite accumulation trends confirm SiNPs’ stress-relieving properties. Salinity caused significant increases in proline and soluble sugars, both of which act as compatible osmolytes, regulating cell turgor and scavenging free radicals28,44. SiNP therapy boosted these results, indicating improved osmotic adjustment. Increases in phenolics and flavonoids under stress, as well as their increase by SiNPs, indicate a robust Si-mediated antioxidant response, as phenolic compounds are critical for reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) during salt toxicity45,46. The enhanced protein concentration with SiNPs indicates improved metabolic stability, most likely due to preserved enzyme function and reduced oxidative degradation.

The broader multivariate patterns from PCA lend support to these physiological explanations. Treatments lacking Si were associated with low biomass, low pigment levels, and poor osmotic behaviour, whereas SiNP-treated plants were associated with higher vigour zones. This apparent distinction suggests that SiNPs improved many, functionally connected qualities rather than acting alone. The coordinated behaviour of root length, shoot growth, leaf expansion, RWC, and pigments supports the notion that Si improves overall plant homeostasis rather than targeting a particular mechanism. This is consistent with integrated models of Si-mediated stress tolerance proposed in recent nanotechnology studies47,48,49.

Notably, the advantages were dose dependent. Higher SiNP concentrations (1000 mg L⁻¹) led to significant improvements in germination, vegetative development, pigments, and metabolites under moderate and severe salinity. This shows that foliar SiNPs may give immediate systemic protection by efficient leaf absorption and subsequent translocation, consistent with studies that nanoparticles penetrate cuticular layers more efficiently than bulk Si fertilizers8,13,50.

Taken together, the findings show that SiNPs are a viable, effective, and environmentally safe method for increasing salinity tolerance in C. ternatea. SiNPs restored plant viability by increasing osmoprotective chemicals, reinforcing pigment integrity, and improving water relations, even at high salinity levels. Given the rising global salinization of agricultural soils, these findings suggest the widespread use of nano-enabled Si fertilizers to boost crop resilience and productivity, particularly in leguminous forage and medicinal plants like Butterfly pea.

Conclusion

Salinity imposed significant physiological and morphological restrictions on C. ternatea, as demonstrated by losses of approximately 50% in plant height, 55% in leaf area, and 40% in biomass at 150 mM NaCl. SiNP foliar spray considerably reduced these inhibitory effects, recovering plant height by 38%, leaf area by 24%, and dry weight by 16% compared to untreated stressed plants. Improvements in chlorophyll pigments, carotenoids, osmolyte accumulation, and protein content provide additional evidence that SiNPs improve photosynthetic capacity, cellular hydration, and metabolic resilience under salt stress. PCA analysis demonstrated that SiNP administration causes stressed plants to move towards clusters associated with improved growth and biomass performance, demonstrating a broad-spectrum favorable effect across various physiological domains. SiNPs significantly improve C. ternatea germination, early seedling vigour, photosynthetic function, osmotic regulation, and overall growth when exposed to salinity. These findings support the use of SiNPs as a promising nanotechnology-based technique for maintaining plant production in saline conditions, as well as its prospective application in sustainable agricultural systems.
