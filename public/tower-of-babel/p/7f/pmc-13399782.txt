Arthroscopically Assisted Dynamic Medial Patellofemoral Ligament Graft Tensioning With Adaptive Length Using Double‐Bundle Technique
Authors: Jacek Walawski, Oliwer Sygacz, Elizabeth A Arendt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis technical note describes a practical, anatomy‐based medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction technique emphasizing individualized, dynamic graft tensioning and independent adjustment of patellar graft arms. By prioritizing femoral fixation first, guided by precise, dynamic anatomical landmarks and arthroscopic verification, the graft achieves optimal isometry and stability throughout the range of motion. The method utilizes a gracilis tendon “V”‐shaped graft configuration with oblique patellar tunnels, ensuring symmetrical, independent tensioning of both graft arms. Additionally, intraoperative graft tension adjustments facilitate dynamic patellar realignment within the trochlear groove, accommodating anatomical variability and trochlear shape differences. This approach aims to reduce complications, enhance patellar tracking, and produce consistent outcomes, especially in cases of severe trochlear dysplasia with variable bony anatomy.

The medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL) plays a crucial biomechanical role in limiting lateral patellar displacement during knee extension and early flexion, which in fact has driven innovations in reconstructive techniques. MPFL reconstruction (MPFL‐R) is a key surgical procedure for addressing patellar instability, particularly lateral dislocation, and restoring joint stability.

The double‐bundle MPFL‐R, using either semitendinosus or gracilis grafts, is a widely adopted technique among various reconstructive methods employing different graft types and fixation modalities. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 Clinical outcomes of this approach are generally favorable. 5 , 6 , 7 However, residual instability, anterior knee pain, and range of motion (ROM) restrictions are reported postoperatively (10%‐26%). 1 , 8 , 9 These suboptimal results are partly attributable to surgical and technical errors. 10 , 11 Essential variations in graft tensioning techniques, applied tension forces, and fixation angles are also noted in the literature. 12 , 13

Proper anatomical placement of the MPFL graft is crucial for achieving better isometry and patellofemoral kinematics. 14 , 15 Numerous studies describe the origin, structure, morphology, attachment sites, and biomechanics of the native MPFL, providing guidance for reconstruction techniques 16 , 17 (Figure 1). However, the precise and consistent identification of femoral and patellar attachment sites remains challenging, complicating accurate graft positioning and graft tensioning in clinical practice. 18 , 19

This technical note describes a practical, adaptive tensioning technique for a double‐bundle gracilis graft, with initial femoral fixation followed by independent patellar fixation supported by arthroscopic repositioning assessment. Instead of applying a predetermined force, graft tension is adjusted based on the shape of the trochlea across the extension‐flexion arc. The objective is to achieve optimal, balanced graft tension on both arms, ensuring the patella remains centered within the trochlear groove during both isolated MPFL‐R and combined procedures.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Examination under anesthesia confirms patellar instability and the pattern of luxation. The patient is positioned supine with the knee flexed at 90°. Arthroscopy is performed to exclude other joint pathologies and to assess lateralization of the patella (Figure 2). Any necessary bony corrections are performed prior to MPFL‐R, as they influence the patellar position relative to the femur.

The authors prefer to utilize the gracilis tendon due to its biomechanical efficiency 20 and to preserve the semitendinosus for potential future procedures. Graft harvesting is performed in the standard manner, with both ends whipstitched using nonabsorbable #2 Braun Premicron sutures (Figure 3) and soaked in vancomycin solution.

Femur and Patella Preparation

The femoral insertion point is identified first by palpation. The adductor tubercle is palpated, and by moving approximately 2 mm distally and downward, the “saddle sulcus” is located 21 (Figure 4). A K‐wire is inserted about 20 mm deep into the bone (Figure 5). It is extremely important to remove all fat tissue over the insertion site down to the medial collateral ligament layer, as the fat pad can hinder accurate anatomical assessment. X‐ray guidance may be used to assist positioning. The medial border of the patella is exposed without violating the synovial layer. A thin strip of medial retinaculum is preserved on the patella for later use as a temporary anchor point (Figure 6).

Femoral graft tunnel is located by anatomic technique using adductor tubercle, medial epicondyle, and gastrocnemius tubercle as the anatomical landmarks. First Anatomical Testing (Femoral Insertion Control)

To verify the isometry of the femoral attachment, a thread loop is placed around the K‐wire and passed between the second and third layers and then temporarily fixed to the medial side of the patella with a clamp. The attachment point is considered correct if the clamp remains locked throughout the full ROM. If it slips, indicating nonisometry, the femoral position is re‐evaluated. This process is repeated until a satisfactory, neutral, and isometric position is achieved. The K‐wire is then overdrilled with a 5 to 6 mm drill to create a femoral socket for the graft and replaced with a 5 mm titanium anchor (Zimmer Biomet Ti‐Screw EasySlide Suture Anchor) (Figures 7 and 8).

The half‐folded graft is fixed into the femoral socket using sliding knots, ensuring it is submerged into the socket (Figure 9). Two oblique tunnels, 4.5 mm in diameter, are drilled in the medial, proximal half of the patellae. Sufficient bony coverage and no damage to the cartilage must be secured. The graft arms are passed through these tunnels and then back to the medial side beneath the patellar retinaculum. No fixation is performed on the patellar side at this stage (Figures 10, 11, 12, 13).

Overmedialization of the Patella and Graft Tensioning

In full extension, the patella should contact the trochlea and begin to move along the trochlear groove as the knee flexes. This applies regardless of whether the trochlea is natural, reshaped by trochleoplasty, or distalized through tibial tubercle osteotomy. The lateral margins of the lateral femoral condyle and the patella serve as reference points to assess medialization. The objective is to achieve a patellofemoral axial engagement index close to 1 (Figure 14). The second step involves overtensioning the graft arms to temporarily overmedialize the patella. The goal is for the patella to “climb medially” onto the initial part of the medial trochlear facet during extension (Figure 15).

Patellofemoral AEI = TL/PL. Practically, lateral border of the patellae and lateral margin of the LFC should be aligned (look for Figure 16, arthroscopy view). (AEI, axial engagement index; LFC, lateral femoral condyle; PL, projected width of the total patellar surface; TL, projected length of the surface of the joint engaged in the trochlea.)

Graft arms pulled medially to overmedialize (“climb”) patella before knee flexion maneuver is performed. The threads are secured manually and tunnel friction only and the graft shift back as exactly directed by the trochlear shape. The back movement between the 2 graph arms is usually not equal in amount. This might be explained by the changes of patella rotation during the flexion of the knee. Patient in supine position, left knee. (Dist, distal; Lat, lateral; Med, medial; Prox, proximal.)

As the knee flexes, the patella naturally “slides down” into the trochlear groove, a movement governed by the shape of the trochlea. This technique assumes that overmedialization will self‐correct as the shape of the trochlea guides the patella into proper alignment during flexion. No specific force is required to medialize the patella; the process relies on the trochlear shape to facilitate correct positioning. If achieving the desired medialization is hindered by severe lateralization, lateral retinaculum lengthening may be necessary. 22 Excessively tight lateral retinaculum can oppose the MPFL‐R. The arthroscope is reintroduced to visually confirm the correction. This allows real‐time assessment of patellar repositioning, and graft tension can be adjusted or released as needed (Figure 16). The lateralization in extension should be less than 25% of the patella's width. Final graft fixation is performed using #2 Braun Premicron nonabsorbable sutures to secure the graft to the patellar retinaculum and/or to itself creating “loop anchorage” (Figure 17, Video 1). The wound closure follows.

The procedure is performed on the left knee. The patient is placed supine with the knee flexed to 90°. Examination under anesthesia confirms patellar instability and luxation pattern. Arthroscopy is carried out to exclude concomitant intra‐articular pathology and to assess the degree of patellar lateralization. The gracilis graft is prepared by whipstitching both arms with nonabsorbable #2 Braun Premicron sutures and soaking the graft in a vancomycin solution. Usually, 1 arm of the graft is thinner than the other. The femoral insertion point is then identified. It is essential to remove the overlying fat tissue, as the fat pad can obscure the anatomical landmarks and make precise localization difficult. The medial border of the patella is exposed without violating the synovial layer. A thin strip of the medial retinaculum is left attached to the patella to serve later as a clamp anchor. The femoral “entry point” is localized using anatomical landmarks. Once the insertion point is established, a K‐wire is introduced to a depth of approximately 2 cm. Using a Kocher clamp, a passage is created between the second and third soft‐tissue layers. The suture thread is looped around the K‐wire and secured to the medialized patella using a clamp. The attachment is considered correct if the clamped thread remains stable throughout the full range of motion. If the clamp slips, the femoral attachment is deemed nonisometric, and the K‐wire position must be re‐evaluated and adjusted until neutral/isometric behavior is achieved. The K‐wire is then overdrilled with a 5 mm drill to create a femoral socket. A 5 mm titanium anchor (Zimmer Biomet Ti‐Screw EasySlide Suture Anchor) is inserted, and the half‐folded graft is fixed by advancing 5 to 6 mm of the graft into the socket using sliding knots. Two parallel, oblique 4.5 mm tunnels are drilled in the proximal patella, angled upward to reach approximately 50% of the dorsal bony width. Care is taken not to perforate the articular cartilage of the medial patellar facet. The graft arms are passed between layers 2 and 3 and then introduced through the patellar bone tunnels. Adequate bony tunnel coverage is confirmed. If the upper graft arm exceeds 4.5 mm in diameter, the upper tunnel may be overdrilled to 5 to 6 mm. The whipstitched graft arms are retrieved beneath the patellar retinaculum and brought back medially, without fixation at this stage. The lateral margin of the lateral femoral condyle and the lateral margin of the patella are used as references to assess medialization. The goal is to achieve a patellofemoral axial engagement index as close to 1 as possible. The graft arms are gently tensioned medially to slightly overmedialize the patella at the medial femoral epicondyle before performing a flexion maneuver. The threads are held by finger tension and tunnel friction only, allowing the graft to shift back naturally according to the trochlear groove contour. Typically, the backward shift of the 2 arms is unequal. At this stage, arthroscopy is reintroduced to verify the in vivo correction and achieved medialization. If needed, graft retensioning or release remains possible. Once the desired alignment and axial engagement index are confirmed, final fixation of the graft is performed. Nonabsorbable #2 sutures are used to secure the graft to the patellar retinaculum. In most cases, the graft arms are long enough to be additionally sutured to their entering point just medial to the patellar border, forming a secure loop anchorage. Finally, the stability and medialization are checked through full extension and flexion. Once satisfactory, the wounds are closed in the usual manner. Rehabilitation

The postoperative brace is recommended for use in a 0° to 30° ROM for 2 weeks primarily for walking. However, many patients opt to keep the brace longer. Full weight‐bearing is permitted as tolerated. Patients are advised to attend rehabilitation sessions twice weekly at a dedicated facility. The goal is to achieve 90° of knee flexion by the fifth week postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

It is well established that nonanatomical graft placement and overtensioning are common causes of complications. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Various graft tensioning techniques exist; however, techniques that secure the graft on the patella first do not replicate the native strain of the MPFL. 23 Currently, only 3 studies describe more kinematic, reverse fixation techniques (femur first), 24 , 25 , 26 none of which are arthroscopically assisted or incorporate lateral border adjustments of the lateral femoral condyle to optimize patellar positioning. There is no consensus on the optimal graft tensioning force nor fixation angle.

The technique described by Su et al. 25 allows for selective tensioning of graft arms. Our understanding of the in vivo behavior of the MPFL graft aligns with this approach, recognizing that under‐ or overtightening can occur if fixation is performed on the patellar side first. However, Su's method lacks detailed guidance on in vivo reference points and tensioning technique. It also does not include arthroscopic control of graft placement or anatomic ROM assessment for the femoral insertion. Conversely, our technique offers greater versatility, reducing the risk of misplacing the femoral graft position and allowing for individualized, independent patellar graft arms tensioning. Overmedialization with initial, temporary tunnel friction graft stability only offers the possibility of graft release directed by the trochlea shape controlled from extension to full knee flexion. Following Beck's recommendation, the patella is positioned relative to the lateral margin of the lateral femoral condyle to reduce overhanging of the subluxated patella. This positioning aligns with anatomical studies and has been confirmed through in vivo observations. Advantages and disadvantages of the technique are summarized in Table 1. The process of graft tensioning and verifying involves detailed steps that are time‐consuming but crucial for ensuring proper medialization and recentering of the patella. It also helps achieve balanced tension in the graft arms and maintains an unrestricted ROM. This procedure is safe and adaptable, allowing for separate tensioning of graft arms and in vivo adjustments based on the relationship between the patella and trochlea. It does not depend on specific knee flexion angles or tensioning forces during fixation and accommodates morphological changes following bony procedures.
