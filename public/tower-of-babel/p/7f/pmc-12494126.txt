Forms of transphobia and their influence on health outcomes among transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse individuals: a global systematic review
Authors: Kristen Ogarrio, Reanna Durbin-Matrone, Breanna De Leon, Laura A Wright, Arjee Restar, Anneliese A Singh, Kristefer Stojanovski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroduction:Transgender people experience health inequities, and stigma often plays an outsize role in shaping the health and lived experiences of transgender persons. We aimed to systematically review the research literature to understand how transphobia is measured and its impacts on the health of trans populations.Methods:We conducted a systematic search using PRISMA guidelines. Titles and abstracts of articles, and full texts, were screened for inclusion. We extracted the study characteristics, populations, methods, transphobia measurement, health outcomes of study, and the relationship between transphobia and health and created evidence gap maps using R.Results:This review found 187 studies that measured transphobia and its associations with mental health, substance intake, and physical health outcomes. Transphobia was predominately studied at the individual, interpersonal, and organizational levels. The Gender Minority Stress and Resilience Measure and the Everyday Discrimination Scale were the most recurring scales used to measure transphobia. Victimization, internalized transphobia, and institutional discrimination were the most reported types of transphobia. Across a majority of studies, transphobias were positively associated with poor health outcomes, with heavy emphasis on mental health outcomes, as well as substance intake. Large gaps existed with regard to physical health.Conclusions:Transphobia negatively impacts the mental health of trans people and often creates elevated substance use. Gaps exist in the measurement of transphobia at the community, organizational and structural levels, and in relation to physical health outcomes.Keywords: Evidence map, health, mapping, stigma, systematic review, transphobia

Introduction

Transgender people commonly experience health disparities. Research posits that transphobia is a leading attributable cause of negative health outcomes for transgender, nonbinary, and gender diverse (henceforth, trans) individuals. For instance, a 2020 study in China found that transgender adolescents were more likely to report anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation than cisgender adolescents (Wang et al., 2020). In addition, a review from the U.S. also observed increased prevalence of addictive behaviors among transgender persons, such as alcohol abuse and illicit drug use (Ruppert et al., 2021). Transgender health inequities are concerning given that trans people disproportionately experience challenges across issues such as housing, finances, mental health and substance use. Studies have identified that transgender persons experience elevated mental health harms such as suicidality, depression, and substance use, as well as elevated experiences of intimate partner violence and challenges with healthcare access (Peitzmeier et al., 2020; Pinna et al., 2022). Moreover, recent global anti-transgender legislation, such as in the United States and the United Kingdom, as well as the ensuing moral panic are creating further health harms, and especially for Black, Indigenous, Latine trans communities, as well as youth and young transgender persons.

Transphobia is expressed across multiple levels of the socioecological model. For example, inter-personally transphobia may be expressed via deadnaming or misgendering, which is the intentional use of incorrect pronouns or a name that a trans person no longer identifies with. At the structural level, policies and legislation hamper the ability of trans persons to live authentically. Transphobia can also occur at the intrapersonal level via internalization of anti-trans attitudes and beliefs (Hendricks & Testa, 2012). Preexisting measures have been designed to capture the spread of transphobia and its various forms. The Gender Minority Stress and Resilience measure (GMSR) constructed in 2015 has been a tool widely implemented or adapted by studies to measure an array of transphobic expressions. The GMSR is a 58-item measure, consisting of nine subscales to reflect the types of transphobias: gender-related discrimination, gender-related rejection, gender-related victimization, non-affirmation of gender identity, internalized transphobia, pride, negative expectations for future events, non-disclosure, and community connectedness (Testa et al., 2015). The Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS), created by Williams and colleagues in 1997, is another tool that measures racially-biased, discriminatory experiences at the interpersonal level (Williams et al., 1997). The EDS is often adapted or modified by the researchers to better reflect trans-specific events. Overall, there is a lack of trans-specific tools to encapsulate transphobia and a lack of standardization in grouping manifestations of transphobia into overarching categories. For example, nonacceptance of trans identity from family members may be labeled as “lack of social support”, “interpersonal stigma”, “social rejection”, or “familial rejection” across studies and scales with no consensus as to an overarching category that this type of discrimination would fall under.

Over the past decade, researchers studying the health of transgender persons have applied the Minority Stress Model as a framework to understand the distal and proximal stressors trans communities experience. This model was originally used to study sexual orientation related stressors and has been used in multiple global studies demonstrating that minority related stress relates to poorer health outcomes (Brooks, 1981). Distal stressors refer to structural or institutional concepts, such as discriminatory laws and social stigma, whereas proximal stressors refer to how an individual manifests distal concepts, such as internalized negative feelings or interpersonal discrimination (Meyer, 2003). For this review, transphobia is understood in the context of upstream and downstream stressors, based on the “Upstream-Downstream Parable” of health equity (Merck, 2018). In this analysis, upstream stressors refer to discrimination and stigma that stem from societal, institutional, or political factors, whereas downstream refers to interpersonal and intrapersonal discriminatory experiences or perceptions.

The impacts of transphobia can have numerous deleterious effects. In the healthcare system, trans patients’ experiences of transphobia create mistrust of healthcare providers, and the healthcare profession, negatively impact the therapeutic relationship, which reduces quality of care, and often results in avoidance in seeking healthcare (Crawford et al., 2024; Lerner et al., 2021). The use of substances among trans individuals is often an outcome of coping with transphobic experiences, thus perpetuating the substance (ab)use inequities (Connolly et al., 2025; Reisner et al., 2015c). Transphobia, in the form of internalized transnegativity, has been shown to be associated with depression, anxiety, non-suicidal self-injury and suicidal tendencies (Inderbinen et al., 2021).

Given the growing body of work on trans-related discrimination and health, we aimed to conduct an exhaustive systematic review to 1) examine how transphobia is defined and ascertained across studies and 2) identify how exposure to transphobia can impact the health and well-being of trans individuals.

Methods

Search strategy

We reviewed studies aligned with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta analysis (PRISMA) criteria (PRISMA 2020 Checklist, n.d.). Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms such as transphobia, discrimination, transgender, gender diverse, and health were employed across five online databases in December 2023. The full search is available in Supplementary Table S1. The databases searched included Scopus (n = 2,529), PubMed (n = 1,887), Web of Science (n = 1,207), Global Health (n = 1,143), and CINAHL Complete (n = 737), yielding 7,503 studies. These studies were imported into Covidence, a web-based software that assists with data screening and extraction (About Us | Covidence—Better Systematic Review Management, n.d.). Covidence identified and subsequently removed 3,680 duplicates in the literature search, and 109 duplicates were removed manually, resulting in 3,714 articles for screening.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) must include transgender and/or gender nonconforming individuals; 2) defined how transphobia was measured; 3) examined how transphobia was associated to a health outcome; 4) results presented were on trans participants; 5) peer-reviewed articles; and 6) written in English or Spanish. Given the language restriction, non-Western search terms were incorporated into our search strategy to capture international papers, such hijra, irahuhua, irawhiti, kathoey, kothi, and mak nyah (see Supplementary File 1). There was no limitation on timeframe or geography. Articles were determined to measure transphobia if they used the term “transphobia”, or related synonyms such as “transgender-related stigma”, “cis heteronormativity”, “violence towards transgender persons”, or used scales that measured external or internal sources of stigma. Health outcomes were limited to physical and mental conditions, as well as substance use.

Screening and data extraction

Four reviewers (RDM/KO/BDL/KS) conducted title and abstract screening (phase one) and the full text review (phase two) independently and in duplicate. Consensus in both phases of screening was achieved through meetings and discussion. Of the 3,714 articles screened, 3,295 were deemed ineligible for inclusion in the title and abstract phase. Reasoning and number of articles excluded at this stage are not required under PRISMA guidelines (PRISMA 2020 Checklist, n.d.). However, to demonstrate, articles were excluded for reasons such as not examining trans persons, measuring health access as opposed to a health outcome, and for being reviews. From the remaining 419 articles, 216 did not meet full-text inclusion criteria, of which 61 were removed due to lack of health outcome, 52 did not explicitly measure transphobia, 50 had no trans-specific results, 40 did not measure the association between transphobia with the outcome, five full texts were not attainable, four were not empirical studies, three did not have explicit trans populations, and one was not in English or Spanish, resulting in 203 articles for extraction. Visualization of the screening process is presented in a PRISMA diagram (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram.

With advances in artificial intelligence, natural language processing, and large number of articles included in our study, data extraction was completed by KO with the assistance of Elicit (Elicit Help Center, n.d.). Elicit is an artificial intelligence tool designed to analyze scientific research articles, which has been evaluated to be useful and rigorous with human verification included in the process (Bernard et al., 2025; Bianchi et al., 2025; Stamm et al., 2025). Each article PDF was uploaded into Elicit for data extraction. Study design, location, sample size, participant demographics, definition of transphobia and its measurement, health outcomes and their measurement, as well as results, were extracted by Elicit through prompts composed by the researchers. An example prompt used to extract the assessment of transphobia was “Provide what scale was used to measure transphobia, if applicable. Include how scores were calculated, such as with a Likert point scale, what a higher score indicates, and cut-off scores. Provide sample questions if available.” The extracted data includes the requested information, shows the location from the article where the data is extracted, and provides reasoning for the response to the queries. The extracted data was downloaded into an Excel file. KO reviewed the extracted information from every study, resolved any conflicts in extraction, and incorporated missing details needed by adding in additional information during human verification and review of articles.

Quality assessment

Articles in the extraction phase underwent quality assessment by Elicit and KO independently. Cross-sectional and cohort studies were assessed with the 22-item Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology checklist (STROBE, n.d.). Example questions from the checklist include: “For each variable of interest, give sources of data and details of methods of assessment (measurement). Describe comparability of assessment methods if there is more than one group”. Qualitative studies were assessed with the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist, consisting of ten questions pertaining to methods and evaluation of qualitative findings (Tell, n.d.). Example scale questions include: “Is a qualitative methodology appropriate?” and “Was the data collected in a way that addressed the research issue?”. The remaining studies were assessed with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT_2018_criteria-Manual_2018-08-01_ENG.Pdf, n.d.). Example scale questions include: “Is there an adequate rationale for using a mixed methods design to address the research question?” and “Are divergences and inconsistencies between quantitative and qualitative results adequately addressed?”. Questions from the appraisal tools were used as prompts in Elicit verbatim. Studies were rated “Poor” (<75%), “Fair” (75–90%), or “Good” (90–100%) based on the percentage of checklist questions answered in the affirmative. Out of the 203 articles extracted, 16 were rated as “Poor” quality and not included in the final analysis, while 121 studies were rated “Fair” quality, and 66 were rated “Good” quality. The extracted data and quality ratings for the 187 articles are in Supplementary Table S2.

Synthesis

To synthesize the data, we categorized the studies based on the type of transphobia that was measured, whether it was proximal, distal, or both, and health outcomes were categorized into mental health, physical health, substance intake, and overall health if studies measured combinations of these outcomes as one variable during data synthesis meetings with the team. We categorized the statistical and qualitative results between the transphobia and health outcomes into positive, negative, or no associations. After we synthesized and categorized the extracted data, we created evidence gap maps using R, version 4.4.3, to visualize the observed associations between transphobia and health outcomes and to identify gaps in the scientific literature. Direction and frequency of associations, as well as corresponding citations, are displayed in the interactive images, whereas citations are unavailable in the noninteractive images. The interactive evidence gap maps are available in online supplementary files. Due to the quantity of studies, citations are presented only in the tables and figures.

Results

Study characteristics

Most of the included studies were of cross-sectional design (n = 153), 14 were qualitative, 12 were prospective cohort, and eight were mixed-methods (Supplementary Table S2). Thirty-three studies only had transgender women as their TGNC population, and seven studies only had transgender men. Approximately 69% of studies were conducted solely in the United States. The total sample across all studies was 860,655 participants, with approximately 44% identifying as TGNC. However, these amounts are not indicative of the number of unique participants. Several studies used the same datasets to conduct analyses, and it is implausible to determine presence of duplicate participants. Two national U.S. studies largely contributed to the low percentage of trans persons in our sample due to the inclusion of cisgender and sexual minorities in their populations alongside trans individuals (Coulter et al., 2018; Miller-Jacobs et al., 2023). To demonstrate, the percentage of trans individuals increases to 84.8% when these studies are not considered in the total population count.

Measures and forms of transphobia

The different forms measured in the studies are presented in Table 1 for downstream stressors and Table 2 for upstream stressors. Downstream stressors were examined in 174 studies and upstream stressors in 77. Scales and questionnaires were often used (n = 149), of which the EDS and the GMSR were the most recurring (n = 28 and n = 28, respectively). Thirty-one of the 149 studies that used questionnaires did not use or modify preexisting measures or employed preexisting scales alongside non-adapted measures. Forty-three studies were analyses of previously conducted survey responses, of which five combined these responses with adapted scales. The 2015 U.S. Transgender Health Survey (USTS) (James et al., 2016) was the most commonly cited national survey, appearing in thirteen studies.

Downstream forms of transphobia

Of the 174 studies that examined downstream stressors, victimization was the most frequently measured form of transphobia (n = 111) (Table 1). Victimization, in a general sense, encompasses verbal or physical abuse, harassment, threats, and sexual assault. Exposure to victimization was often identified with non-adapted questions (n = 16), the GMSR (n = 11), responses to the 2015 USTS (n = 10), adapted versions of the EDS (n = 10), and qualitative interviews (n = 10). Negative interpersonal interactions that fell outside of the scope of victimization (e.g. non-affirmation of gender identity, treated with less courtesy/respect) were also measured across a large portion of studies (n = 76). Negative interactions were commonly measured using the adapted EDS (n = 19) and the GMSR (n = 9). Closely related to these interactions were experiences of rejection or exclusion from family and peers (n = 25). Rejection was predominantly examined using the GMSR (n = 12). Sixteen studies did not specify subtypes of interpersonal enacted stigmas examined in their studies.

Within the intrapersonal sector, internalized transphobia was the most prominent stressor (n = 52), with the GMSR as the most utilized method to measure internalized transphobia (n = 21) (Table 1). Gender dysphoria was mentioned in six studies. Anticipated stigma, including situational avoidance, was measured in 32 studies, with identity concealment examined in 20 studies, both of which were predominantly measured using the GMSR. Lastly, perceived stigma was measured in 23 studies using a variety of tools (Table 1).

Upstream forms of transphobia

Upstream discriminatory experiences were commonly examined in institutional settings. The most common was discrimination in healthcare settings (n = 44), defined as inability to access treatments or services due to trans identity, followed by employment (n = 38), and housing (n = 36). A variety of tools were employed, with ten studies using the GMSR for each of these subtypes (Table 2). Discrimination in public accommodations (e.g., gendered bathrooms and service denial) was explored in 25 studies, with the GMSR as the most cited measure. Discrimination in education was examined across eight studies, which referred to barriers to accessing or continuing education due to trans identity.

Community discrimination toward trans peoples was measured across twelve studies, most often with qualitative interview questions or non-adapted questions. One recurring subtype of this discrimination was exclusion or stigma stemming from religious communities, as examined in five studies (Table 2). The impact of negative media portrayals of trans were expressed in five studies, of which three studies employed qualitative interviews and photo voice methods. The effect of geopolitical environment was examined in two studies, one of which used qualitative interviews and the other used non-adapted quantitative questions.

The lack of national nondiscrimination laws was measured in two studies, one of which was qualitative, and the other used an unspecified, adapted measure. Seven studies examined the lack of state-level nondiscrimination laws, with three using data from the Movement Advancement Project to designate scores to U.S. regions for trans inclusivity in legislation. The remaining four studies used either non-adapted questions or an unspecified, adapted measure. Three of the seven studies that measured state-level laws specifically examined the lack of hate crimes (Table 2). Four studies explored the lack of nondiscriminatory policies, such as workplace environments and schools, at the organizational level. Lastly, policies specific to insurance companies were mentioned in three studies.

Health outcomes

Outcomes, ascertainment, and article allocation are presented in Supplementary Table S3. Most studies measured mental health (n = 136) with depression (n = 63), suicidal ideation (n = 42), anxiety (n = 37), suicide attempt (n = 38), and psychological distress (n = 32) being some of the most prominent (Supplementary Table S3). Depression was commonly measured with the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (n = 26). Data on suicidal ideation and suicide attempt were mostly collected via self-reported questions not derived from a validated scale or tool (n = 27 and n = 27, respectively) (Supplementary Table S3). The Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 was the most frequent method of anxiety diagnosis (n = 10) (Supplementary Table S3). Psychological distress was measured by the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale-6 (n = 14), Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (n = 7), and others (Supplementary Table S3). Other mental health outcomes and scales are presented in Supplementary Table S3.

Studies that measured substance intake (n = 55) were categorized by whether they measured abuse or recreational use. Alcohol abuse was measured in 27 studies, and recreational alcohol use (RAU) was examined in 11. Drug abuse and recreational drug use (RDU) were measured across 22 and 16 studies, respectively. Eight studies specifically measured the effect of transphobia on non-medical use of prescription drugs (NMUPD) as a form of drug abuse. Use of tobacco products, including electronic cigarettes, was an outcome in nineteen studies. All studies of substance intake primarily used self-reported data not from a validated scale or tool.

Thirty-six studies examined physical health outcomes (Supplementary Table S3). Non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI) was the most common physical health outcome, appearing in thirteen studies, of which a majority used self-reported data (n = 7). Physical health in a general context was measured in six studies, with two using non-adapted questions. Overall health, combining mental and physical health into one variable, was the outcome for five studies. Other mental and physical health outcomes, as well as measurement tools, can be found in Supplementary Table S3.

The examined relationships of downstream and upstream stressors on health outcomes can be found in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5, respectively. The findings from these studies are presented as both static and interactive evidence gap maps in Figures 2–4 for mental health, substance intake, and physical and overall health, respectively. Impact of downstream transphobias

Observed associations for downstream stressors and mental health are presented in Figure 2 and Table 3. All downstream transphobias measured in relation to depression, suicidal ideation, and suicide attempt were largely positively associated (range 67–100% for all) with few studies observing no association. Results for anxiety followed a similar trend, except for identity concealment with an even split between positive and no associations observed. All stressors were also largely positively associated with psychological distress (range 83–100%), except for gender dysphoria with inconclusive results. Downstream stressors were positively associated with PTSD (range 80–100%), with the exception of identity concealment. Results for eating disorders were split depending on the subtype of transphobia, however no articles were in disagreement on the results per subtype of transphobia. Results for self-esteem were inconclusive with regard to the impact of victimization and negative interpersonal interactions. Internalized transphobia was negatively associated with self-esteem, whereas perceived stigma was not associated. No associations were observed in relation to emotion dysregulation. Victimization and negative interpersonal interactions were not associated with resilience, and perceived stigma results were in conflict. Negative associations were observed for all stressors measured in relation to post-traumatic growth and general mental health. All stressors measured in relation to stress, gender dysphoria loneliness, dissociative symptoms, and somatization were positively associated with the outcomes with no conflicting evidence.

Social rejection, unspecified enacted stigma, internalized transphobia, and identity concealment were the only stressors not associated with tobacco use. Unspecified enacted stigma and identity concealment were the only stressors not associated with RDU and RAU. Internalized transphobia was the only stressor not associated with NMUPD.

Associations between downstream stressors and physical and overall health are presented in Table 3 and Figure 4. The relationship between stressors and NSSI was dependent on the subtype of transphobia. Victimization, negative interpersonal interactions, and unspecified enacted stigma were mostly positively associated with NSSI (range 86–100%), whereas social rejection, anticipated stigma, and identity concealment results were inconclusive, and internalized transphobia was mostly found to not be associated (67%). Conflicting evidence was presented as to the effect of victimization on non-HIV STIs, and anticipated stigma was positively associated. No stressors were associated with inflammation. Negative associations with general physical health were evident for all stressors measured in this relationship, except for unspecified enacted stigma. Positive associations were observed for stressors measured in relation to HIV, asthma, cardiovascular conditions, sleep disturbance, viral load, likelihood of detransition, likelihood of physical disability, physical inactivity, likelihood of venous thromboembolism, and weight concerns were no conflicting evidence. Anticipated stigma was negatively associated with overall health (100%), victimization was largely negatively associated (67%), whereas negative interpersonal experiences were mostly not associated (67%).

Impact of institutional discrimination

Observed associations between institutional, upstream transphobias and mental health are presented in Table 4 and Figure 2. Institutional forms of transphobia were largely positively associated with depression, suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, anxiety, psychological distress, PTSD, eating disorders, and stress (Table 4, Figure 2). The only exception to this was the relationship between discrimination in public accommodations and anxiety, with equal evidence for positive and no association. Discrimination in public accommodations was not associated with self-esteem, whereas other forms of institutional discrimination were negatively associated. Public accommodation discrimination was the only form of institutional discrimination measured in relation to emotion dysregulation and resilience, in which a positive and negative association was observed, respectively. All forms of institutional discrimination were negatively associated with post-traumatic growth, except for education which was not measured. Institutional discriminations measured in relation to gender dysphoria all revealed positive associations. Lastly, discrimination in healthcare was positively associated with somatization, and negatively associated with general mental health, whereas other forms were not measured (Table 4, Figure 2).

All forms of institutional discrimination demonstrated mostly positive associations with substance intake, with three exceptions (Table 4, Figure 3). Impact of communal and political transphobias

Communal transphobias and lack of policies and laws measured in relation to depression, suicidal ideation, suicide attempt, anxiety, and psychological distress largely demonstrated positive relationships (Table 5, Figure 2). The impact of the lack of state-level nondiscrimination laws on PTSD were inconclusive (50% positive, 50% no association), and lack of state-level hate crimes laws were not associated with PTSD. Negative media portrayals of trans persons and lack of nondiscrimination policies were negatively associated with self-esteem, whereas lack of state-level nondiscrimination laws was not associated. Community discrimination toward trans persons was positively associated with somatization and negative media portrayals of trans persons were positively associated with loneliness (Table 5, Figure 2). Negative medial portrayals and geopolitical environment were negatively associated with general mental health. Lack of state-level nondiscrimination laws was not associated with resilience and positively associated with mood disorders. Lack of nondiscrimination policies was negatively associated with resilience, and lack of state-level hate crimes laws was not associated with mood disorders (Table 5, Figure 2).

Discussion

Our systematic review of 187 studies found that, overwhelmingly, transphobia in its various manifestations was associated with negative mental, substance use, and physical health outcomes. In the literature, there is an overabundance of research examining transphobia’s impact on mental health, with a much smaller proportion of studies focusing on physical health or substance intake. Our review also found that while transphobia operates on multiple levels of the socio-ecological model, research to date predominately focuses on proximal measures of transphobia, with little emphasis on communal- and policy-level influences.

The consistent positive associations between various forms of transphobia and adverse mental health outcomes (particularly depression, anxiety, psychological distress, and suicidality) align with previous research establishing transphobia as a fundamental cause of health inequities (Scheim et al., 2022). Our findings extend this understanding by demonstrating how institutional discrimination in healthcare, employment, and housing systems operates in tandem with interpersonal victimization and internalized stigma to create pervasive barriers to wellbeing for trans individuals. The strong relationship between upstream factors and mental health outcomes provides empirical support for theories that examine multi-level factors of transphobia (e.g., socio-ecological model, Intersectionality Research for Transgender Health Justice) (Gillani et al., 2024; Wesp et al., 2019), suggesting that a complex systems approach to study the totality of factors that produce transphobia and the poor health manifestations are critically needed. Notably, the limited research examining asset-based frameworks, such as resilience and posttraumatic growth, reveals a deficit-focused orientation in the field that neglects the remarkable strength and adaptation demonstrated by trans communities in the face of adversity (Puckett et al., 2019). The inconsistent findings regarding identity concealment’s relationship with mental health outcomes further complicate clinical recommendations, as concealment may function as both a protective and risk factor depending on social context and available support systems (King et al., 2020).

Our systematic review also identifies the predominance of studying downstream stressors in the literature (174 studies) compared to upstream factors (77 studies), which may mirror the theoretical limitations of the Minority Stress Model when applied to trans experiences, which often emphasizes individual or interpersonal-level stressors rather than structural determinants (Testa et al., 2015). This imbalance creates significant gaps in understanding how institutional and policy-level discrimination fundamentally shapes trans health outcomes, particularly for intersectional populations facing compounding marginalization (Diamond & Alley, 2022). The extensive use of adapted measures like the Everyday Discrimination Scale (Williams et al., 1997), which has been adapted to measure gender-based discrimination, and the Gender Minority Stress and Resilience measure (Testa et al., 2015) points to methodological challenges in accurately capturing trans-specific experiences (A. J. Restar et al., 2021), particularly outside the individual level.

The patterns of substance use identified in our review indicate that transphobia contributes significantly to health-harming behaviors, with victimization and discrimination consistently associated with alcohol abuse, drug abuse, and tobacco use. These findings extend previous research documenting elevated rates of substance use in trans communities (DeAtley et al., 2025) by specifying the discriminatory mechanisms driving these disparities. The conflicting results regarding housing discrimination’s relationship with drug abuse highlight how contextual factors may moderate these associations, suggesting the need for more nuanced examinations of how different forms of transphobia interact across settings. The positive association between healthcare discrimination and non-medical use of prescription drugs reveals a particularly concerning pathway through which transphobia in medical settings may inadvertently drive trans individuals toward self-medication strategies in the absence of affirming care. The paucity of research examining the relationship between policy-level factors and substance use represents a critical knowledge gap, as state and national nondiscrimination protections may buffer against the stressors driving substance use behaviors (A. Restar et al., 2024).

Strengths and limitations

This review has several strengths, one of which being the large number of articles included in this analysis. This allowed for examination of a wide array of forms of transphobia on a global scale and their impact on multiple health outcomes. There were also very few articles rated as “Poor” quality after initial data extraction (~8%). Trans individuals are an underrepresented population in research, and this review was able to thoroughly explore manifestations of transphobia and its methods of ascertainment in a variety of countries and identify areas of study that are needed. Our evidence maps illustrate significant gaps in the topics of study in the field of trans health, particularly regarding the impacts of religious discrimination, geopolitical environments (including discriminatory laws and policies), and media representations. These understudied areas represent crucial social contexts that shape both public attitudes and structural landscape affecting trans communities (Earle et al., 2021). Moving forward, the field must prioritize examining how upstream structural interventions can disrupt pathways between transphobia and health outcomes, with particular attention to developing trans-specific measurement tools that capture the full spectrum of discriminatory experiences across cultural contexts and intersectional identities.

Our review is not without limitations, however. A large majority of the included papers were of cross-sectional design. While temporal and causal relationships cannot be determined by those studies, they do suggest a linkage between transphobia and health and provide a foundation for studies that employ more rigorous study designs. The methodological homogeneity across studies highlights the need for more innovative research approaches such as mixed methods, community-based participatory frameworks, and longitudinal designs that can more effectively elucidate the pathways leading to transphobia and its health implications. Secondly, transphobia and outcome ascertainment often involve self-reporting. Despite the use of validated and reliable measures, response bias within the studies cannot be ruled out, given sensitive topics. Generalizability is also limited, due to most of the studies being conducted in the U.S and the paucity of studies among diverse trans identities. The predominance of U.S.-based research (129 studies) limits global understanding of how transphobia manifests across different cultural and political contexts, reinforcing Western-centric frameworks that may not adequately capture the experiences of trans populations worldwide. Additionally, the under-representation of studies examining transgender men exclusively (n = 7) compared to transgender women exclusively (n = 33) perpetuates knowledge gaps about transmasculine experiences, potentially reinforcing harmful assumptions about which trans populations deserve research attention. Similarly, transphobia among nonbinary communities were underexplored; this is critically important given that subgroups of trans communities (e.g., trans women, trans men, nonbinary individuals, etc.) may have different health needs, and experience transphobia differently; as such a gender-specific and gender-inclusive approach to examining transphobia is needed (A. Restar et al., 2021). Although there is a general consistency among findings, these results may not be applicable to trans individuals in different contexts. Next, several findings were observed only in singular articles that measured such relationships, such as mood disorder, post-traumatic growth, likelihood of detransitioning, inflammation, and others. Conclusions drawn from these observations should be interpreted with caution. A lack of standardized language, specificity, and clustering of variables prevented in-depth analysis of subtypes of transphobias, such as the impact of victimization subtypes (i.e., harassment, sexual abuse, physical assault). Furthermore, our review did not examine whether the age of study samples or the year of publication moderated findings, which may affect the generalizability of our results. Age may shape how transphobia is experienced; for example, youth may face heightened mental health risks due to limited bodily autonomy and support systems, while older trans individuals may demonstrate greater resilience. Similarly, the sociopolitical context surrounding trans health continues to shift significantly over time, meaning earlier published studies may not capture the impact of more recent discriminatory policies and structural efforts to erase trans identities. Finally, our review included only published peer-reviewed literature which may have excluded valuable findings from grey literature, affecting the breadth and balance of the evidence base. Overall, these limitations speak to the paucity of research on transphobia and highlight innovative areas for future research and methodological refinement. Despite these limitations, our review demonstrates a tremendous effort to examine transphobia and its impact on the health and well-being of trans individuals. Future work also needs to explore how gender affirming terms are generated and embodied by transgender communities globally. Too often, terms, which include those searched in this review are used as slurs that denigrate trans people and trans research is often completed by cisgender researchers, which serves to reify cis-centric scholarship, and categorizing, of transgender people.

Conclusion

This systematic review highlights the significant impact of transphobia on the health of transgender individuals worldwide. The findings provide critical insights for healthcare workers and public health practitioners who aim to create interventions that ensure equitable access to healthcare. Researchers can utilize our findings to design more rigorous studies that explore the complex, multifaceted effects of transphobia on health and well-being. Theoretically, our review provides a deeper understanding of how transphobia operates across multiple levels of the social ecological model and can serve as the foundation for the development of standardized intersectional measures of transphobia. Future researchers can build on our preliminary findings by utilizing non-cross-sectional study designs, prioritizing intersectional identities in study samples, examining transphobia through life course perspectives, and measuring structural aspects of transphobia. This approach will allow for a more comprehensive understanding of how exposures to transphobia can impact the health and well-being of trans individuals.
