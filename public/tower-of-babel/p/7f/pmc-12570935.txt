Prevalence and dual use of cigarettes, heated tobacco products and electronic cigarettes among young adults in Japan: findings from a 2019 Nationwide Survey
Authors: Yuki Kuwabara, Aya Kinjo, Hongja Kim, Hanako Murase, Hitoshi Maesato, Ruriko Minobe, Susumu Higuchi, Sachio Matsushita, Yoneatsu Osaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aims to estimate the prevalence and dual use of cigarettes, heated tobacco products (HTPs) and electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes) among individuals aged 18–24 in Japan.DesignThis cross-sectional study involved a door-to-door survey administered by trained interviewers using a structured questionnaire.SettingIndividuals aged 18–24 residing in 220 areas within 10 ordinance-designated cities (Sapporo, Sendai, Saitama, Chiba, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Sagamihara, Nagoya, Osaka and Fukuoka) as well as Tokyo’s 23 wards in Japan in 2019 were included.ParticipantsA total of 1738/5500 individuals were included in the analysis, with participants evenly distributed by sex, age and residential area.Primary and secondary outcome measuresCigarette, HTP and e-cigarette use was assessed.ResultsThe weighted prevalence of the current use of cigarettes, HTPs and e-cigarettes accounted for 10.1% (95% CI 8.7, 11.5), 5.1% (95% CI 4.1, 6.1) and 1.8% (95% CI 1.2, 2.4), respectively. Approximately 68.2% of the people who used HTPs at the time were dual users of both HTPs and cigarettes. There was a significant gap in tobacco product use between individuals aged 20–24 and those aged 18–19, with this difference remaining after adjusting for covariates.ConclusionsIn 2019, cigarettes were the most used tobacco-related product among young adults in Japan. Smoking initiation appears to notably increase at age 20 when smoking becomes legal in Japan. The prevalence of HTP use among young adults in Japan is notably higher than in other countries.Keywords: Tobacco Use, PUBLIC HEALTH, EPIDEMIOLOGY

Introduction

Cigarette smoking among youth and young adults not only causes immediate respiratory dysfunction and nicotine addiction but also accelerates the development of chronic diseases over a lifetime.1 Historically, 88% and 99% of daily smokers try their first cigarette by ages 18 and 26, respectively.1 2 Therefore, monitoring cigarette use and preventing initiation in younger generations is essential for effective tobacco control. Recent literature indicates that smoking initiation is increasingly shifting from adolescence to early adulthood (ages 18–23) in the USA.3 In Japan, where smoking under 20 years of age is prohibited, adolescent smoking rates have declined.4 However, the 2019 National Health and Nutrition Survey reported a smoking prevalence of 16.7% among adults (men: 27.1%; women: 7.6%).5 To note, these conventional national surveys covered participants either under 18 or over 20; smoking behaviours among individuals between the ages of 18 and 20 are unknown. Studies on the smoking behaviours of young adults in Japan are limited to surveys of university students, with no data on employed young adults. There is a significant research gap in the smoking prevalence between adolescents and adults, highlighting the need to investigate smoking rates among young adults to adequately allocate tobacco prevention efforts and understand potential generational shifts in smoking behaviours.

The prevalence of heated tobacco products (HTPs) has also increased globally.6 A recent review has reported an increase in lifetime and current HTP use across the European and Western Pacific regions from 2015 to 2020, with Japan and South Korea reporting the highest rates of HTP use. In 2014, IQOS, a novel HTP, was introduced in Japan and Italy. Accordingly, awareness and use of HTPs subsequently rose from 0.3 in 2015 to 3.6% in 2017.7 Japan’s unique regulatory model, which allows for the sale and promotion of HTPs while banning nicotine-containing electronic cigarettes (e-cigarettes), has advantaged HTP use.8 Japan has a unique tobacco product lineup that differs from that of other countries, with HTPs accounting for a significant market share. Considering that the tobacco industry’s marketing strategies often target youth,9 young individuals in Japan may be more attracted to HTPs than e-cigarettes. While the dual use of HTPs and cigarettes is common among adults who smoke, the prevalence of dual use among young adults remains unclear.10 11 Following the expansion of the HTP market, examining HTP prevalence and usage patterns among younger populations is increasingly important.

To date, no population-based nationwide prevalence data for cigarette, HTP or e-cigarette use among individuals aged 18 to 24 have been reported in Japan. Considering the rapid proliferation of novel tobacco products and Japan’s unique regulatory context, obtaining prevalence data is essential for informing tobacco control policies and research. This study aims to estimate the nationwide prevalence and dual use of these products using nationally representative data, exploring generational shifts in smoking initiation by examining prevalence across age groups. Additionally, the study examines the relationship between HTP and cigarette use to determine whether dual use is associated with smoking cessation or a reduction in cigarette consumption.

Methods

Setting and participants

This cross-sectional study involved a door-to-door survey administered by trained interviewers using a structured questionnaire. Data was collected from April to May 2019. The interviewers were from a company specialising in door-to-door surveys. A stratified two-stage random sampling method was used to select 5500 individuals aged 18–24 years from the Basic Resident Registration system. Participants were selected from 220 areas across ten ordinance-designated cities (Sapporo, Sendai, Saitama, Chiba, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Sagamihara, Nagoya, Osaka and Fukuoka) and the 23 wards of Tokyo. We selected 20 areas in each of the 11 cities and randomly sampled 25 individuals from each area. To calculate sample size, we based our estimates on the expected prevalence (P), margin of error (E) and confidence level (Z). Using p=0.18, E=0.01–0.02 and Z=0.95, we calculated the required sample size to range from 1418 to 5670. The assumption was based on the 2017 National Health and Nutrition Survey in Japan.12 Considering the expected response rate and the feasibility of the survey, we determined a final sample size of 5500. These ordinance-designated cities are large metropolitan areas, each with a population exceeding half a million, collectively encompassing over one-fifth of Japan’s national population. As many young people in rural Japan attend colleges or universities in the 11 large cities, we considered that sampling from students and employed youth in the 11 cities was more efficient for recruiting to our door-to-door survey. The interviewers orally explained the study’s purpose, survey content and personal data protection measures to potential participants. Individuals who agreed to participate provided written informed consent before completing the survey questionnaire. Of the 5500 individuals, 1775 (32.3%) consented and responded to the interviews. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the National Hospital Organization Kurihama Medical and Addiction Center (approval number: 338). This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Measurements

Cigarette, HTP and e-cigarette use

To assess cigarette use, participants were asked, ‘Do you currently smoke cigarettes (conventional cigarettes)?’ They could select one of the following responses: ‘I smoke every day’, ‘I smoke sometimes’, ‘I stopped smoking’, ‘I have never smoked’, ‘I don’t want to answer’ or ‘I don’t know’. Participants who answered, ‘I smoke every day’, ‘I smoke sometimes’ or ‘I stopped smoking’ were asked additional questions: ‘On average, how many cigarettes do you smoke per day?’ and ‘How many cigarettes did you used to smoke per day?’ Interviewers recorded the reported number of cigarettes smoked per day. If a participant stated, ‘less than one cigarette’, the response was recorded as ‘one cigarette’. Participants who indicated smoking every day, occasionally, or who had stopped smoking were classified as ever cigarette users (individuals who have tried cigarettes at least once in the past). Participants who indicated smoking every day or sometimes were classified as current cigarette users. And people who had stopped smoking were classified as those who had stopped smoking.

To assess the use of HTPs and e-cigarettes, participants were asked, ‘Have you ever used even one sip of heated tobacco products?’ and ‘Have you ever used even one sip of electronic cigarettes?’ Response options included ‘I have never used’, ‘I have used but not in the past month’ or ‘I currently use’. To clarify product types and avoid confusion between HTPs and e-cigarettes, major brand names, such as ‘iQOS’, ‘glo’ and ‘Ploom TECH’ for HTPs and ‘DR.VAPE’, ‘VITAFUL’, ‘EMILI’, ‘Joytech’, ‘FLEVO’, ‘C-Tec’, ‘VAPE STEEZ’, ‘HoneySmoke’ and ‘VITACI’ for e-cigarettes, were provided as examples. ‘Ever users’ refers to individuals who have tried the tobacco product at least once in the past. Participants who selected either ‘I have used but not in the past month’ or ‘I currently use’ were classified as ever HTP or e-cigarette users. Those who indicated current use were defined as current HTP or e-cigarette users. Additionally, people who reported use of cigarettes and HTPs were defined as dual users (individuals who used both cigarettes and HTPs), and people who reported use of the three products were defined as triple users (individuals who used all three products). Online supplemental file S1 presents detailed questionnaires on cigarette, HTP and e-cigarette use. The questionnaire, although we did not pre-test it before use, was based on the questions in other national surveys.

Demographic variables

Data on demographic variables including sex, age and residential area (Sapporo, Sendai, Saitama, Chiba, Tokyo 23 Wards, Yokohama, Kawasaki, Sagamihara, Nagoya, Osaka or Fukuoka) were collected. Participants were also asked about their occupation, with options including student, full-time employee, contract/temporary/part-time, unemployed, self-employed/freeholder, housekeeping/housewife and ‘I do not know’. Additionally, participants provided their annual personal income, with response options of <1 million yen, 1–2 million yen, 2–3 million yen, 3–4 million yen, 4–6 million yen, 6–8 million yen, 10–12 million yen, no income and ‘I do not know’.

Statistical analysis

The proportions of ever and current use of cigarettes, HTPs and e-cigarettes were calculated according to sex and age groups. Weighted analysis was applied to estimate prevalence. The population size by sex, age group (18–24) and the 11 cities based on the 2020 census data was used for weighting. The weighting was designed to account for imbalances in the participant sample relative to the target population (population size by sex and age group across the 11 cities, based on the 2020 census data). Cross-tabulations were conducted to explore the relationships between ever-use and current-use of cigarettes, HTPs, e-cigarettes, any tobacco product and cigarettes or HTPs with variables such as sex, age, residential area, occupation and annual personal income. Trends in ever-use and current-use proportions across age groups (18–19, 20, 21, 22, 23 and 24 years) were assessed using the Cochran-Armitage trend test. Logistic regression analysis was conducted to examine the associations between each product use pattern and age group, adjusting for sex, residential area and occupation. Additional logistic regression among HTP users was performed to analyse associations between HTP and cigarette/e-cigarette use, adjusting for the same covariates. Among people who currently smoked cigarettes, we used logistic regression to evaluate the association between HTP use and the average number of cigarettes smoked per day. Another regression analysis examined the relationship between HTP use and ex-smoking status among people who ever smoked cigarettes. A significance level of p<0.05 (two-tailed) was applied for all tests. Missing values were observed for occupation (n=1) and annual income (n=4). These cases were excluded from the logistic regression analyses as we assumed that the impact of data exclusion would be minimal on results. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V. 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA) and R software, V. 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were involved in neither the design nor the conduct, reporting and dissemination plans of this research.

Results

Of the 5500 individuals, 1775 (32.3%) consented and responded to the interviews. Data from 1738 participants with complete responses regarding cigarettes, HTPs or e-cigarettes were analysed (see online supplemental file S2). Online supplemental file S3 details participants’ characteristics, with sex, age and residential area evenly distributed across groups, as per the sampling method.

The prevalence of ever cigarette use among those aged 18–24 was 14.2% (95% CI 12.6, 15.8) overall, with 19.8% (95% CI 17.9, 21.7) for men and 8.6% (95% CI 7.3, 9.9) for women (table 1). Overall, 13.1% (95% CI 11.5, 14.7) of the participants reported ever HTP use, with prevalence accounting for 18.6% (95% CI 16.8, 20.4) for men and 7.6% (95% CI 6.4, 8.8) for women. The prevalence of ever e-cigarette use was 9.7% (95% CI 8.3, 11.1) overall, with 14.0% (95% CI 12.4, 15.6) for men and 5.4% (95% CI 4.3, 6.5) for women. Current use prevalence for cigarettes was 10.1% (95% CI 8.7, 11.5) overall, with 16.0% (95% CI 14.3, 17.7) for men and 4.3% (95% CI 3.3, 5.3) for women, and 5.1% (95% CI 4.1, 6.1), 7.1% (95% CI 5.9, 8.3) and 3.1% (95% CI 2.3, 3.9) for HTPs. The prevalence of e-cigarette current use was 1.8% (95% CI 1.2, 2.4) overall, with 2.7% (95% CI 1.9, 3.5) for men and 0.9% (95% CI 0.5, 1.3) for women.

Proportional Venn diagrams (figure 1) illustrate product use patterns across the three products. Substantial overlaps were observed among people who ever used any products, with more than one-third using all products. Regarding people who reported current use of any products, cigarette-only use was the most prevalent (118/212: 55.7%). However, there remained a significant overlap between cigarettes and HTPs. Among people who reported current use of any products, HTP users accounted for 40.1% (85/212) and dual users of HTPs and cigarettes accounted for 27.4% (58/212). Among people who currently used HTPs, 68.2% (58/85) also smoked cigarettes and current use of the three products accounted for 17.6% (15/85). Online supplemental file S4 provides a cross-table of use patterns by individual characteristics, revealing higher proportions of men and older users, with variation across residential areas and income levels. Age group comparisons for each use pattern are presented in line graphs (figure 3 and online supplemental file S5). Regarding ever-use, the prevalence of each use pattern increased with age, regardless of sex. The prevalence of ever-use increased with age across all use patterns, with statistically significant trend tests (p<0.001), except in the employment subgroup. Notable increases in the prevalence of ever-use at 20 years were seen among men and employed subgroups, with peak prevalence at 22 or 23 years. Similar patterns were noted for current use; however, there were gaps in current cigarette use vs HTP or e-cigarette use across age groups. Logistic regression results adjusted for covariates are shown in line graphs (online supplemental file S6). Ever-use of any product was significantly associated with ages 20–24 even after adjusting for covariates. Current use of products, excluding e-cigarettes, was also significantly associated with age >20 years.

Table 2 presents the logistic regression results on the association between HTP use and cigarette/e-cigarette use. Both people who previously smoked (adjusted OR (AOR) 15.09 [95%CI 3.97, 57.34]; P value <0.001) and people who currently smoked (AOR 14.81 [95%CI 4.40, 49.88] ; P value <0.001) were significantly associated with current HTP use among people who ever used HTPs. Current e-cigarette use was also significantly associated with current HTP use (AOR 13.74 [95%CI 3.57, 52.94] ; P value <0.001). However, past e-cigarette use was not significant. Table 3 presents the relationship between current HTP use and daily cigarette consumption among people who currently smoked (n=180). After adjusting for covariates, smoking 6–10 cigarettes per day (AOR 3.94 [95% CI 1.56, 9.97]; p=0.004) and smoking >11 cigarettes per day (AOR 3.21 [95% CI 1.09, 9.44]; p=0.034) were associated with current HTP use, compared with smoking <5 cigarettes per day. Online supplemental file S7 indicates that HTP use was not significantly associated with ex-smoking status among people who ever smoked cigarettes.

Discussion

This study examines the prevalence of tobacco-related product use among young adults (aged 18–24) in Japan, revealing that 14.2% reported ever using cigarettes, 13.1% had tried HTPs and 9.7% had tried e-cigarettes. Current usage rates for these products were 10.1% for cigarettes, 5.1% for HTPs and 1.8% for e-cigarettes, highlighting cigarettes as the most commonly used product among this age group. Notably, 68.2% of people who currently used HTPs reported current use of cigarettes.

A comparison within the 18–24 age range indicated a marked increase in both ever and current cigarette use beginning at age 20, with prevalence across ages 20–24. Studies from Japan reported that the national prevalence of current cigarette use was 1–2% among adolescents from 2017 to 2021 and 16.7% among adults in 2019.13,15 Possible contributing factors for disparities in smoking behaviour include socioeconomic factors, cultural or social norms, access to tobacco products, marketing influence and policy/regulation. Considering these factors, the observed differences in smoking prevalence by age indicate that social norms and legal restrictions on sales to those below 20 years old appear to influence smoking initiation, with substantial initiation of smoking possibly occurring across the age range of 20 to 24 years. Currently, the turning point for smoking initiation appears to be 20 years, the legal smoking age in Japan. This finding indicates the importance of setting the smoking age by law; raising it would also delay smoking initiation. Moreover, in the graphs of the employee/employer subgroup (figure 3), the highest prevalence of smoking was observed in individuals aged 20–21 years with an education level lower than a high school degree. The disparity can be explained by socioeconomic factors. In Japan, educational intention and attainment are significant socioeconomic factors that cause disparities in smoking.4 16 17 In this cross-sectional study, the observed disparities in prevalence from the ages of 21–24 were considered to be affected by disparities in the proportion of people who smoke among individuals with different attained educational levels. Post-adjusting for several covariates, the OR for cigarette use across the age group of 20–24 was statistically significant, with the highest being the age of 20–21. Japan’s prevalence of smoking decreased; however, it remained higher than among global high-income countries.18 Preventive efforts for smoking that focus on the 20–21 age group in educational institutions and workplaces should be prioritised.

We also found that HTP use prevalence among young adults was higher than that of e-cigarettes, with Japan’s HTP use surpassing that reported in other countries6: 1–2% among adults in Europe,19 20 2.4% among US adolescents21 22 and 1.6% among adults aged 18–24 years in England,23 as well as an ever use of 0.9% among individuals aged 15–29 years in Hong Kong.24 Studies from Korea showed close but lower prevalence: 2.0–2.8% current use among adults25 and 3.5% current use among young adults.26 Although a direct comparison is difficult, the prevalence in this study was slightly higher than that in the 20–29 age group in a nationwide survey in Japan in 2018.11 27 Although a rapid increase in HTP use did not occur among young adults in Japan, the prevalence of HTPs is equivalent to that among adults. The proportion of HTP current use among people who reported use of any products at the time (40.1%), observed in this study, was relatively higher than that of the adults in the 20 to 70 age group exhibited in the national survey.15 The increase in HTP use among younger individuals raises concerns that HTP use could increase the risk of cigarette smoking. A consensus regarding the effect of e-cigarettes on smoking initiation was reached.28 29 Our cross-sectional data could not examine the consequences; however, a previous study reported that HTP use was associated with relapse/initiation of combustible cigarette smoking after 1 year.30 Despite Japan’s unique regulatory model, which has advantaged HTPs, different forms of tobacco product use are closely interdependent. Prevention policies need to address all products collectively. Careful monitoring and further longitudinal studies are required to clarify this concern and minimise the harm from tobacco-related products.

Moreover, the proportion of people who reported current dual use of HTPs and cigarettes among current cigarette users (58/180=32.2%) in this study was higher than that reported for adults aged 20–70 years in Japan’s national survey.15 Individuals who initiate tobacco product use after HTPs were introduced in the market may be more likely to become current users of both HTPs and cigarettes. Moreover, the dual use of two products remains controversial in terms of cessation, total cigarette consumption and unknown adverse health risks. A Cochrane review reported insufficient evidence that HTPs are effective for smoking cessation.31 Our results showed no significant association between the use of HTPs and ex-smoking status among people who ever smoked cigarettes. The wide confidence intervals in the logistic regression analysis raise concerns about statistical power, and this cross-sectional study limited the examination of the efficacy of HTPs for smoking cessation. However, at this stage, the use of HTPs for this purpose is not sufficiently justified for young adults who smoke. Philip Morris International has promoted a complete switch to HTPs as a means to reduce harm from tobacco. Several randomised controlled trials funded by tobacco companies found that people who smoked cigarettes and switched to HTPs had lower exposure to the most harmful substances compared with those who continued smoking cigarettes.32 33 Regarding tobacco consumption, this study’s findings suggest that participants who smoked more than six cigarettes per day were more likely to use HTPs than those who smoked less than five cigarettes per day. However, we could not determine whether people who smoke with higher nicotine dependence complementarily use HTPs in various situations or if people who smoke more cigarettes tried to reduce tobacco use by using HTPs.34 A tobacco industry-independent study showed that people who used both cigarettes and HTPs smoked fewer cigarettes in the past 30 days. However, generally, they consumed more tobacco.35 Moreover, a study in Japan reported that people who use both HTPs and cigarettes exhibited an increased risk of hypertension and that overall tobacco consumption of people who used both products did not decrease even though they smoked fewer conventional cigarettes.36 The campaign to completely switch from cigarettes to HTPs seemed too optimistic. Moreover, short- and long-term adverse health effects of HTPs remain uncertain. HTPs contain substances absent in cigarettes;37 hence, dual and triple use of tobacco-related products may have greater health risks compared with exclusive cigarette use. Based on the precautionary health principle, the initiation or use of tobacco-related products among young individuals should be avoided, despite these products being legal. For younger individuals who select legal HTPs, raising awareness that all tobacco-related products, including HTPs, are harmful should take precedence over claims of harm reduction. Policymakers should review current advertising and marketing regulations for HTPs and make efforts to minimise the potentially harmful effects of these novel tobacco-related products.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size and low response rate (1738/5500) reduced the representativeness and statistical power of the study. Although the number of participants met the estimated sample size, it may still be limited to provide the national-level estimates. Second, while our sampling strategy was aimed at minimising selection bias by recruiting participants from 11 major cities across Japan, the exclusion of rural areas may have introduced a selection bias. However, this study is the first nationally representative survey assessing tobacco-related product use prevalence among young adults in Japan. Third, there is a potential for information bias as the study did not involve biological samples and may be subject to social desirability bias.38 39 To mitigate response errors, trained interviewers conducted the survey, potentially reducing inaccuracies. Lastly, the cross-sectional study design does not allow for the causal conclusion between HTP use and cigarette use or consumption.

Conclusions

In 2019, the prevalence of current tobacco product use among young adults (aged 18–24 years) in Japan was 10.1% for cigarettes, 5.1% for HTPs and 1.8% for e-cigarettes. The turning point of smoking initiation appears to be age 20, when smoking becomes legal in Japan. Notably, HTP use among young adults in Japan is significantly higher than that in other countries, highlighting the need for targeted prevention efforts in this demographic.
