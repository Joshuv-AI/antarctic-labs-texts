Toolkit to Support Teachers’ Use of Behavioral Classroom Interventions: Results from a Randomized Pilot Trial
Authors: Gwendolyn Lawson, Julie Sarno Owens, David S Mandell, Sadie Butcher, Aaron R Lyon, Ricardo Eiraldi, Molly Kerstetter, Thomas J Power
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeacher-delivered Tier 1 (i.e., whole class) and Tier 2 (i.e., targeted) behavioral classroom interventions are effective in improving student academic and behavioral functioning, but often not delivered as recommended. Implementation strategies, especially strategies that are both feasible and effective, could support teachers in the delivery of these interventions. This pilot randomized controlled trial evaluated the acceptability, feasibility, and preliminary evidence of effectiveness of the Positive Behavior Management Toolkit (PBMT) in supporting teacher implementation and student outcomes. The PBMT is a modular implementation resource package to support K-5 teachers in delivering Tier 1 and Tier 2 behavioral interventions. Twenty teachers were randomized to receive either the PBMT or implementation support as usual. Quantitative and qualitative results suggested that the PBMT was highly acceptable, feasible and contextually appropriate. We also collected data on teacher fidelity to behavioral interventions and student outcomes for enrolled focal students with symptoms of attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) at baseline and post-intervention. Although the pilot study was underpowered to conduct significance testing, the results suggested the PMBT may be effective in supporting teacher implementation outcomes and were mixed regarding student outcomes.Keywords: ADHD, consultation, intervention, classroom, positive behavior support

Teacher-delivered low-intensity behavioral interventions in the classroom have a strong evidence base for supporting positive student behaviors (Lane et al., 2024), which is important for a range of student social, emotional and academic outcomes (Okano et al., 2020). These interventions include antecedent-based approaches (e.g., reminding students to engage in expected behavior) as well as positive consequence-based approaches (e.g., acknowledging appropriate behavior; Lane et al., 2024). Teacher-delivered behavioral interventions can be applied universally (i.e., Tier 1) and targeted toward students requiring additional support (i.e., Tier 2). They may be particularly important for supporting children with or at-risk for attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), for whom they show strong evidence of success (Evans et al., 2018; Fabiano et al., 2009). Notably, many evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions for ADHD (e.g., behavior-specific praise) can be delivered at Tier 1 for the entire class and intensified for students with or at-risk for ADHD (Fabiano & Pyle, 2019).

Despite their effectiveness, teachers often do not use these interventions with fidelity (Hart et al., 2017; Martinussen et al., 2011; Owens et al., 2018). Fidelity refers to the extent to which a practice or intervention is implemented as designed, and is typically conceptualized with multiple dimensions, including adherence (i.e., extent to which core intervention components are delivered), competence (i.e., quality of delivery), and dosage (i.e., amount of a practice or intervention delivered over a period of time; Sutherland & McLeod, 2022). The literature on teacher implementation of Tier 1 and Tier 2 behavioral classroom management interventions in usual practice suggests that these interventions are often not implemented as designed across these dimensions of fidelity. For example, teacher self-report studies have found that less than 20% of teachers report regularly using a daily report card (Hart et al., 2017; Martinussen et al., 2011), and observational studies tend to find teachers use praise at lower rates than would be recommended (e.g., an average of 1.35 labeled praise statements per target student with or at risk for ADHD per hour; Owens et al., 2018). A range of individual-level (e.g., difficulties remembering to use them; Lawson et al., 2022), school-level (e.g., intervention “fit” with school culture; McLennan et al., 2020), and system-level (e.g., lack of resources; McDaniel et al., 2018) factors may serve as barriers to teacher fidelity to behavioral classroom management interventions. This research-practice implementation gap points to a need for effective approaches to increase the frequency and fidelity with which teachers use behavioral interventions in the classroom (i.e., “implementation strategies;” Proctor et al., 2013).

Several strategies have been tested to improve teachers’ use of Tier 1 or Tier 2 behavioral interventions. At the most intensive end are teacher coaching and consultation, which have generally been found to be effective (Stormont et al., 2015) but require considerable resources, including time from an expert coach (Pas et al., 2020; Stormont et al., 2015). For example, several of the teacher coaching models reviewed by Stormont et al. (2015) include daily coaching interactions. Effective teacher coaching and consultation models (e.g., Cappella et al., 2012; Herman et al., 2022; Reddy et al., 2021) typically include observation and performance feedback as a core component; however, these components may not be acceptable or feasible in some contexts (Fallon et al., 2018). There have therefore been recent efforts to identify less-intensive implementation strategies that are both effective and feasible. Guided interactive technology models (e.g., Mixon et al., 2019; Owens et al., 2019) and emailed implementation prompts (e.g., Fallon et al., 2018) show promise as less-intensive methods of supporting teacher implementation of specific Tier 1 or Tier 2 interventions.

Taken together, the existing literature on approaches to support teacher implementation of evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions suggests that several strategies may be promising, but gaps remain. First, there is a need for packaged approaches that are based in theory and incorporate teacher input while targeting known implementation barriers and facilitators. Second, there is a need for implementation strategies to support teacher delivery of behavioral interventions at both Tiers 1 and 2, as many of the same interventions (e.g., behavior-specific praise) can be applied for the whole class and specifically for students with ADHD or related concerns (Lane et al., 2024; Fabiano & Pyle, 2019). Historically, the research base on strategies at these tiers has been siloed, but supporting implementation at both tiers simultaneously is likely to create efficiencies. Finally, the school psychology literature has conceptualized a multi-tiered framework of professional development for teachers with the goal of school psychologists offering teacher supports of varying degrees of intensity depending on teacher need and preference (Bloomfield et al., 2024; Sanetti & Collier-Meek, 2015; Simonsen et al., 2014). Within this multi-tiered framework, there is a particular need for approaches that are less intensive than traditional coaching models but more intensive than one-time training or asynchronous resources (akin to Tier 2 supports for teachers), as these are less well-represented in the current literature.

Positive Behavior Management Toolkit

We developed the Positive Behavior Management Toolkit (PBMT) to meet this need. The PBMT is designed to support teachers in delivering evidence-based Tier 1 and Tier 2 behavioral classroom interventions to students with elevated symptoms of ADHD. The PBMT is informed by the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB; Ajzen, 1991) and theories of habit formation (Nilsen et al., 2012). The PBMT targets teachers’ intentions to use the intervention (as shaped by their attitudes, perceived norms and self-efficacy related to the intervention), and their ability to act on their intentions, for example through in-the-moment reminders (Nilsen et al., 2012). It includes three core components, designed to target these mechanisms: a library of resources, text-message reminders, and four 15-to-20-minute, bi-weekly meetings with a consultant. These components were selected and refined based on theory, teacher input, and the implementation science literature (e.g., Edmunds et al., 2013; Fallon et al., 2018). The PBMT uses a modular approach (i.e., with standalone components that can be used flexibly) to support the implementation of four evidence-based teacher-delivered behavioral interventions: precorrections (e.g., Ennis et al., 2020; Sherod et al., 2023); behavior-specific praise (e.g., Allday et al., 2012; Cook et al., 2017); calm, behavior-specific corrections (e.g., Abramowitz et al., 1988; Owens et al., 2020); and use of daily report cards regarding behavioral goals for specific students (e.g., Fabiano et al., 2010; Holdaway et al., 2020). We selected these four interventions in partnership with teachers and other educators, with the goal of including interventions that vary along key dimensions, including whether they are designed to be applied to the entire class or to selected students only, and whether they use antecedents, consequences, or both. The resources in the PBMT are organized into six modules: one module focused on each of these four behavioral interventions, as well as two additional modules to support factors that were identified as critical for implementation of the behavioral interventions: student-teacher relationships and teacher wellness (DeShazer et al., 2023; Lawson et al., 2022).

We developed the PBMT using an iterative, community-partnered process with meaningful input from teachers and other school staff member (see Lawson et al., 2024). Specifically, we met monthly with a team of partners (akin to an advisory board) before and during the development process to guide the development of the PBMT. We also conducted a series of tryouts in which teachers used a version of the PBMT and provided quantitative and qualitative feedback on acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility. With input from the advisory board, we used this feedback to iteratively revise the PBMT, making it more appealing, usable, and contextually appropriate. The goal of this work is for the PBMT to help address the critical need for relatively low-intensity approaches of supporting teachers in using evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions, especially for students with elevated symptoms of ADHD for whom behavioral classroom interventions are particularly important. Evaluation is needed to determine the effectiveness of the PBMT regarding teacher implementation outcomes (e.g., fidelity to behavioral classroom intervention) and student outcomes (e.g., ADHD-related impairment).

Purpose of the Current Study

To prepare for potential larger-scale evaluation of the PBMT in the future, we conducted a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) examining the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary implementation and student outcomes of the PBMT. Prior to conducting large-scale evaluations, underpowered pilot RCTs are important to assess the feasibility of randomization, recruitment, and retention procedures, determine readiness for large-scale evaluations, and inform refinements to the theory of change and plans for outcome measurement (Pfledderer et al., 2024). To this end, we addressed two primary research questions: (1) Do teachers perceive the PBMT as acceptable, contextually appropriate and feasible? (2) Does the PBMT show preliminary evidence of effectiveness in improving teacher implementation outcomes (i.e., fidelity to behavioral interventions) and student outcomes (i.e., ADHD-related impairment, student-teacher relationship, academic productivity, observed rule violations)? We examined teacher fidelity to behavioral classroom interventions as applied to the whole class and as applied specifically to focal students with elevated symptoms of ADHD and impairment. We examined student outcomes only for these focal students given particular interest in focal student outcomes. This pilot RCT was designed primarily to prepare for larger-scale evaluation and was not powered to conduct significance testing of the PBMT’s impact on teachers and students.

METHOD

Setting

The pilot randomized controlled trial was conducted from 2022 to 2024 in six K-8 schools in a large urban school district in the Mid-Atlantic United States. The district’s student body is racially and ethnically diverse (about 50% Black/African American, 21% Hispanic/Latino, 14% Non-Hispanic White, 7% Asian, and 5% Multiracial or Other races). Approximately 80% of these students live in households that are income-eligible for free or reduced-price meals. Schools were selected based on principal interest, with an effort to select schools with student demographics representative of the district’s population. Of the six schools, five used the schoolwide PBIS framework at the time of their participation and one was in the process of adopting this framework. There were no other school-related inclusion criteria.

Procedures

The institutional research board at the Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia and the research review committee of the participating school district approved the research protocol. We first recruited teachers via email, flyers, and presentations at staff meetings in participating schools. Teachers were eligible for participation if they taught grades K-5 at a participating school and provided informed consent. After a teacher was enrolled, we attempted to enroll two students from that teacher’s classroom (referred to as focal students). Teachers focused on these two students when they used the PBMT and we collected student outcome data only about focal students.

Teachers were instructed to nominate focal students who “show elevated levels of inattentive, hyperactive or impulsive behaviors;” whom “you think may benefit from increased use of positive behavior management practices,” and “for whom you could use additional support in managing their behaviors.” Teachers completed the Impairment Rating Scale (IRS; Fabiano et al., 2006), a 7-point scale ranging from 0 to 6, for each of these students, without providing identifying information about the student. To be included, a student had to receive a score of 3 or greater on at least one IRS domain, consistent with the literature on the optimal cut-point for this measure (Fabiano et al., 2006). Students were excluded from participating if they had a special education classification of intellectual disability, if their primary presenting concern was psychosis or autism spectrum disorder based on parent or teacher report, or if they presented as in acute risk of harm to self or others. For each student who met criteria, teachers were asked to obtain permission from the student’s parent/legal guardian for the research team to contact them. If permission was granted, the research team attempted to contact the parent to explain the study in more detail and obtain informed consent. If parent consent was not obtained for two focal students, we allowed the teacher to participate with one enrolled student.

Teachers were randomized to condition (Positive Behavior Management Toolkit [PBMT] or Business-As-Usual Control [BAU]) within school and grade level block (i.e., grades K-2 vs. 3–5); students were in the condition to which their teacher was randomized. Randomization occurred after students in the classroom were enrolled and baseline data were collected. Teacher and student enrollment occurred on a rolling basis throughout the two school years, with randomization for most teachers occurring between November and March. Teacher-report, parent-report, and observational measures were collected at baseline and endpoint (i.e., after the 8-week intervention period). Observational measures and some teacher-report measures were also collected at midpoint (between 4 and 7 weeks after intervention start). During the school year following their participation, teachers were invited to participate in a semi-structured qualitative interview regarding their experiences with the PBMT during and after the study period. Parents were compensated for their time completing surveys ($30 per timepoint). Teachers were compensated for their time completing surveys and completing interviews (between $30 and $70 per timepoint, depending on survey length). However, teachers were not compensated for their time using the PBMT or having classroom observations completed.

Intervention Conditions

Positive Behavior Management Toolkit (PBMT)

The PBMT is intended to support teachers in implementing Tier 1 and Tier 2 behavioral classroom interventions, particularly for students with elevated ADHD symptoms. The PBMT has three core components: (1) a library of resources, including written materials, videos, and tangible resources (e.g., custom sticky notes); (2) optional text-message reminders with personalized tips, as well as the option to access these reminders by following an Instagram account; and (3) four 15-to-20-minute, bi-weekly meetings with a consultant (i.e., “guide meetings”) to assist the teacher with goal setting, identifying relevant resources, building motivation, problem solving, and reflecting on progress. These three implementation strategies are designed to target teachers’ intentions to use behavioral classroom interventions (e.g., video testimonials from other teachers about the importance, ease, and helpfulness of these interventions) as well as their abilities to act on intentions (e.g., text messages and tangible resources to serve as reminders). The library of resources, which was made available to teachers physically (via a binder of handouts and tangible resources) and electronically (via a website), is organized into six modules: (1) behavior-specific praise, (2) precorrections, (3) calm behavior-specific corrections, and (4) daily report cards, (5) support teachers in strengthening student-teacher relationships and (6) support teachers’ own wellness.

The intervention period lasted 8 wk, during which teachers in the PBMT condition met with a consultant four times for 15–20 min each. This intervention period length was selected based on the goal of the PBMT being longer than a one-time training but briefer than other intensive coaching models (e.g., Owens et al., 2017). The consultant was a member of the research team (either a Master’s-level clinical research coordinator or a doctoral student in psychology). The consultant was supported by a manual that included a description of process strategies (i.e., rapport building, motivational interviewing, goal setting, problem solving) and session agendas (i.e., a broad outline for the first session, follow-up sessions, and final session). They received initial training in the manual, including role plays and performance feedback, and ongoing supervision (approximately weekly) from the principal investigator. Consultants completed, and the principal investigator reviewed, written notes documenting the consultation sessions and checklists documenting which Toolkit materials each teacher engaged with during and between consultation meetings. Consultation sessions were not recorded for fidelity monitoring due to concerns about the acceptability of recording consultation sessions.

Business-As-Usual (BAU) Control

Teachers in the control condition were not provided with the PBMT, although they were permitted to participate in any training in classroom management or PBIS otherwise occurring within their schools (typically consisting of professional development at the beginning of the school year). Teachers in this condition received the PBMT electronic, written, and tangible resources when the study ended to thank them for their participation. Students in both conditions continued to receive any therapy or medication that they would otherwise receive during the study period.

Measures

Demographics and Characteristics of the Sample

Teachers completed a questionnaire at baseline to provide demographic data. Parents of enrolled students completed a questionnaire at baseline to provide demographic data.

Teacher Engagement with the PBMT

We tracked (1) the number of consultant (“guide”) meetings attended by each teacher in the PBMT condition; (2) the percentage of teachers in the PBMT condition who agreed to receive text message reminders and the number of text messages sent; (3) the percentage of teachers in the PBMT condition who followed the Instagram account, which was offered as an optional way to view reminders; and (4) the specific resources teachers engaged with during and outside of consultant meetings, as reported by consultants on a checklist completed after each meeting.

Teacher Implementation Outcomes: Acceptability, Appropriateness, and Feasibility

Quantitative.

Teachers in the PBMT condition completed at endpoint (i.e., following the 8-week intervention period) quantitative measures of acceptability (i.e., the perception that an intervention is agreeable or satisfactory), appropriateness (i.e., the perceived fit of an intervention for a given setting), and feasibility (i.e., the extent to which an intervention can be used within a given setting). Acceptability of the PBMT was measured by the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM). Appropriateness of the PBMT was measured by Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and feasibility was measured by Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM; Weiner et al., 2017). Each measure consists of four items. The mean item score on each of these measures (scale ranging from 1 to 5) was computed. These measures have shown evidence of acceptable structural validity, internal consistency and test-retest reliability (Weiner et al., 2017).

Qualitative.

We used a semi-structured interview guide that included questions about teachers’ engagement with the PBMT during (for teachers in the PBMT condition) and after (for teachers in both conditions) the study period, as well as acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility and perceived impact. Interviews during the school year following teachers’ participation in the study via virtual, phone, or in-person format, depending on the teachers’ preference. Each interview was audio-recorded, transcribed and deidentified. Ten teachers (7 PMBT group, 3 BAU control group) participated in an interview. The interviews lasted on average 28.6 min (SD = 11.4 mins).

Teacher Implementation Outcomes: Fidelity

A member of the study team conducted one classroom observation per time point (mean length = 28.5 mins, SD = 4.2 mins) at the baseline, midpoint and endpoint of study participation for teachers in both conditions. We attempted to schedule observations at a consistent time of day across time points. These observations used a modified version of the Student Behavior Teacher Response (SBTR) scale (Owens et al., 2017). Observers recorded counts of teacher praise (behavior-specific and unlabeled) directed at enrolled focal student(s) and directed at any student(s) in the class (i.e., “total”), resulting in four categories of coded praise: (a) unlabeled total, (b) behavior-specific total, (c) unlabeled focal student, and (d) behavior-specific focal student. As such, unlabeled focal student and behavior-specific focal student praise counts were a subset of unlabeled total and behavioral-specific total praise counts. In cases where two focal students were present in the observation, focal student praise counts were computed as an average of the count for both focal students. In alignment with the implementation strategy being evaluated, observers also recorded counts of pre-corrections (defined for this purpose as, “The teacher uses statements or prompts (i.e., gestural, verbal, visual, physical) to remind student(s) about behavioral norms before behaviors of concern occur”). To account for variability in observation length, all count variables were converted to a rate per hour. Additionally, observers coded counts of rule violations for focal students (see “Observed rule violations” subsection below); for each student rule violation, observers also coded the teacher response into one of three categories: appropriate, inappropriate, no response (see Owens et al., 2018 for definitions of these categories of teacher responses). Following Owens et al., 2018, we computed the percent of focal student rule violations that received an appropriate teacher response as a teacher-level variable. Finally, the observer rated the teachers’ global competence regarding classroom management and supportive relationships (with the whole class and with each focal student) on a 7-point scale (with the anchors 1 = “poor,” 4 = “adequate/average”, 7 = “excellent”) following each observation. Following established procedures (Sutherland et al., 2023), raters were trained to use a default score of a 4 as the starting point, and increase the rating as teacher performance is better than average, and decrease the rating with performance that is below average.

Observers were trained using a standardized process, involving first studying a coding manual (which included definitions and inclusion/exclusion examples for each code) and practicing with written examples, then practice coding video examples, followed by live practice coding. Approximately 50% of observations were conducted by two observers. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) values were between .76 and .96 for observed count measures, and between .82 and .84 for the global competence ratings reported here.

Student Outcomes

Observed rule violations.

Observers recorded counts of focal student rule violations using the SBTR (see Owens et al., 2018). Violations were counted across five categories (i.e., be respectful, follow adult’s instructions, work quietly, use materials appropriately, stay on task), and the rate of total rule violations per hour was used as an outcome variable at the student level. Approximately 50% of observations were conducted by two observers. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) value was .87 for the total number of observed rule violations.

Student-Teacher Relationship Scale.

Teachers completed the Student-Teacher Relationship Scale—Short Form (Pianta, 2001) about participating students at the baseline and the endpoint. This scale assesses teachers’ perceptions of their relationship with individual students and consists of 15 items rated on a scale from 1 (“definitely does not apply”) to 5 (“definitely applies”) and is sensitive to change over time (Pianta, 2001). Eight items were reverse scored, and then the total score was computed as a mean of all items; higher scores indicate more positive (i.e., higher closeness, lower conflict) relationships, with a possible score between 1 and 5. Internal consistency in this sample was adequate (α = .90 at baseline and endpoint).

ADHD Symptoms and Functional Impairment.

Teachers and parents completed the NICHQ Vanderbilt Scale (Wolraich et al., 2003) about focal students at the baseline and the endpoint considering student behavior during the past four weeks. The inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity scales have acceptable test-retest reliability and convergent validity (Bard et al., 2013). Parent- and teacher-reported inattention and hyperactivity/impulsivity symptoms (i.e., the number of symptom items with a rating of “often” or “very often”) at baseline were used to characterize the sample. Teacher-reported and parent-reported overall functional impairment was measured as the mean score on items related to academic and social performance (e.g., “overall school performance,” “relationship with peers”) rated on a scale from 1 (“excellent”) to 5 (“problematic”), such that higher scores indicate higher impairment. Internal consistency in this sample ranged between α = .85 and α = .89 for teacher report and between α = .70 and α = .73 for parent report.

Academic Performance Rating Scale (APRS).

Teachers completed the APRS (DuPaul et al., 1991) about focal students at baseline and endpoint. The Academic Productivity (i.e., work completion and accuracy) subscale, which consists of 12 items rated on 5-point scales, was used as a student outcome measure. Four items were reverse scored, such that higher Academic Productivity scores indicate greater performance (α = .89 at baseline and endpoint). The APRS Academic Productivity subscale has shown acceptable criterion-related validity and sensitivity to intervention in other samples (Fabiano et al., 2010; Murray et al., 2008).

Data Analysis

We used mixed quantitative and qualitative methods to examine teacher perceptions of acceptability, appropriateness and feasibility of the PBMT. The function of the qualitative data was to expand on the quantitative findings by providing additional depth and richness (Palinkas et al., 2011). We first examined descriptive statistics of the quantitative measures of acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility.

We then analyzed qualitative interview transcripts using an integrated inductive and deductive approach (Bradley et al., 2007). First, two coders coded all transcripts using a broader set of inductive and deductive codes using a structured codebook with definitions, key words and example excerpts. Any discrepancies were reconciled through discussion using a consensus process (Hill et al., 2005). For the current analysis, we focused on content coded under one code that included acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility. Two research team members reviewed all transcript excerpts for this code and independently drafted an analytic memo for each of the constructs of interest (i.e., acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility) containing salient themes within each construct, ID numbers of participants whose comments fit within that theme, and illustrative quotes. We refined these memos used an iterative process of vetting, discussion and revision (Saldaña, 2013).

Finally, to examine preliminary evidence of effectiveness of the PBMT regarding teacher implementation outcomes and student outcomes, we examined baseline and endpoint means and standard deviations (SD) of teacher fidelity outcomes and primary student outcomes for the PBMT and BAU control groups, as well as change scores (i.e., endpoint minus baseline) for each group. We computed Cohen’s d effect sizes for each outcome measure between groups by calculating the standardized difference in change scores for the PBMT versus control group between endpoint and baseline. Specifically, following recommendations from Morris (2008), we used the pooled baseline standard deviation for both conditions in these calculations. For interpretation, we considered an effect size of .2 to be small, .5 to be medium, and .8 to be large (Cohen, 1988). Because this pilot study was underpowered for significance testing, we did not conduct further analyses.

All teachers in this condition agreed to receive text message reminders; teachers were sent on average 12.6 (SD = 4.5) text messages over the 8-week period. Seven (64%) of teachers in the PBMT condition consented to following the Instagram account. Per checklists that consultants completed after each meeting, all teachers in the PBMT condition engaged with resource library materials both within and outside of consultant meetings, although the specific materials used varied between teachers. Custom sticky notes (used to remember to deliver praise and/or precorrections) were the resource most used outside of consultant meetings, with nine (82%) of teachers using them in between consultant meetings per checklists.

Among teachers who were randomized to the PBMT condition, their mean score on the Acceptability of Intervention measure was 4.54 (SD =. 51), on a 5-point scale (5 is highest). The mean score on the Intervention Appropriateness Measure was 4.48 (SD = .56), and mean score on the Feasibility of Intervention Measure was 4.52 (SD = .47).

DISCUSSION

We assessed the acceptability, feasibility, and preliminary effectiveness and implementation outcomes of the Positive Behavior Management Toolkit, a set of resources to support K-5 teachers in implementing evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions. Results indicated that teachers perceived the PBMT as highly acceptable, appropriate, and feasible, and that their engagement with the PBMT was high. Overall, the pilot study results reported here suggest that the PMBT’s may effectively support teachers’ implementation of evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions, both for the class as a whole and for enrolled focal students with elevated ADHD-related impairment, although further evaluation is needed. Evidence regarding primary student outcomes was mixed (revealing benefits in reducing observed classroom rule violations but not changing teacher ratings of student functioning). These preliminary findings have implications for potential future larger-scale evaluation of the PBMT or related implementation strategies.

Quantitative and qualitative results suggested that teachers perceived the PBMT as highly feasible, acceptable, and contextually appropriate. Data about teachers’ engagement with the PBMT (e.g., all teachers attended all four consultant meetings and agreed to receive text message reminders) also point to a high level of acceptability and feasibility. Notably, teachers shared that using the PBMT was not burdensome, and several teachers described it as lowering their workload by preventing behavioral concerns that would require time to manage. Notably, they discussed this both in terms of focal students and the class overall. Although these findings should be interpreted with caution given the small sample size of teachers who participated in a qualitative interview, the possibility that use of behavior management support resources could reduce the perceived workload for some teachers is compelling, given the importance of perceived workload on teacher burnout and attrition (Bettini et al., 2017). In future studies it would be useful to include quantitative measures of teachers’ time spent using resources to support their behavior management, time spent engaging in reactive behavior management, as well as quantitative measures of perceived workload, to investigate this possibility. Teachers specifically cited the PBMT’s alignment with schoolwide PBIS as making it a good fit for their school context. This highlights the potential synergies between schoolwide PBIS and behavioral classroom management interventions (Lawson et al., 2024); however, it also may be important for future research to determine the acceptability and contextual appropriateness of the PBMT in a wider range of schools, including those not using the PBIS framework.

The recruitment, retention, and response rates generally indicate that research procedures were feasible and acceptable to teachers and parents/guardians, although adaptations to procedures for student recruitment may be warranted. Regarding teacher recruitment and retention, 77% of teachers who attended an enrollment meeting ultimately consented to participation, 83% of consented teachers were randomized, and 95% of those completed their participation. Regarding student recruitment, 85% of parents/guardians who agreed to outreach from the study team ultimately consented to their child’s participation. However, it is notable that the study team never received permission to contact nearly 50% of nominated eligible students. Several classrooms moved forward with only one enrolled student due to teachers’ difficulties obtaining permission for study team outreach for a second student. Once enrolled, student retention was high (94%) and over 92% of planned teacher- and parent-report surveys were completed; these retention rates and response rates were well above the benchmark criteria set for the pilot study (Lawson et al., 2023) and are notable given that community-based studies tend to have lower retention rates than university-based studies (Pellecchia et al., 2024). This suggests that similar recruitment and retention procedures could be used in future large-scale evaluations of the PBMT, but that modifications to improve the response rate for teachers’ initial parent outreach may be important. It will be valuable for researchers and school partners to collaborate to decide upon these modifications (Lawson & Owens, 2024).

Notably, the demographic characteristics of enrolled students included many students from minoritized racial/ethnic backgrounds (e.g., over 70% Black, over 25% Hispanic), which is generally in line with the district’s student population. About 70% of the student sample had a primary caregiver whose highest level of education was a high school degree or less. This contrasts with the child and adolescent ADHD treatment literature as a whole, as a recent meta-analysis found that only about 15% of participants in ADHD treatment studies were Black and about 16% were Hispanic/Latino (Merrill et al., 2024). This meta-analysis found that most studies in the ADHD treatment literature did not report caregiver education; of those that did, the average caregiver education was equivalent to a bachelor’s degree (Merrill et al., 2024). The contrast between the demographics of the current sample and the demographics of the youth ADHD treatment literature as a whole is consistent with findings suggesting that schools are an accessible setting for youth from minoritized or low socioeconomic backgrounds to access services (Wilk et al., 2022), and suggest that school-based research is likely important in efforts to increase diversity and representation in youth mental health research.

The current results were promising regarding the PMBT’s potential effectiveness in supporting teachers’ implementation of evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions. First, to place the data in context, the rates observed for teacher’s use of praise at baseline were similar to that found in other studies using the SBTR (Owens et al., 2018). Second, the data suggest that those in the PBMT group increased their use of praise (behavior-specific and unlabeled; directed to focal students and overall) whereas those in the control group did not (effect sizes for the difference ranged from .37 to 1.0). In addition, the percent of praise that was behavior-specific improved from 38% to 61%. Further, changes in observed rate of precorrections, percentage of focal student rule violations receiving an appropriate teacher response, and global competence ratings also all favored the PBMT group. Although this pilot study was not powered to conduct significance testing regarding the PBMT’s impact on improving teachers’ implementation outcomes, it is encouraging that the effect sizes observed here are similar to those observed using similar observational measures of teacher behavior in evaluations of more intensive teacher coaching and consultation models (e.g., Herman et al., 2022; Reddy et al., 2021).

However, the current results were mixed regarding student outcomes. First the observation data suggest that students whose teachers received the PBMT, compared to those in the control group, showed a decrease in observed rate of rule violations. Given that one to two focal students often account for a disproportionate amount of classroom rule violations (Owens et al., 2017), reducing rule violations by nearly half in the PBMT classrooms (from 14 per hour to 8 per hour) is meaningful. The rating scale data suggest a slight reduction in parent-reported impairment, but no difference in other teacher- and parent-report primary outcome measures. There are several potential reasons for these results. First, it is possible that any improvement in teachers’ implementation of behavioral classroom interventions was not sufficient to result in improved student functioning. This possibility suggests that additional resources within the PBMT—such as additional implementation supports for parents and caregivers—may be warranted. It is also possible that improved teacher implementation did lead to improved student outcomes, but the parent- and teacher-report measures used were not sensitive to change in the timeline measured (i.e., outcome measurement immediately after an 8-week intervention period). These outcome measures (i.e., student-teacher relationships, functional impairment, academic productivity) are quite distal to teachers’ implementation outcomes and may be influenced by several factors beyond the teachers’ use of behavioral classroom interventions. It may be important for larger scale evaluation of the PBMT to invest in direct observational measures of student outcomes and include a longer-term measurement timepoint.

Limitations

There are several limitations to note. First, this pilot study had a small sample size of teachers and students; the study was not powered to examine statistical significance or to provide a definitive test of effectiveness. Relatedly, as is common in small-scale RCTs (Eldridge et al., 2016), the intervention and control groups were not fully balanced on baseline characteristics (i.e., teacher-reported inattention symptoms), which further points to the importance of a larger sample size for future evaluation. Second, although the PBMT was designed to support teachers’ implementation of behavioral interventions at both Tiers 1 and 2, student outcome data measurement was limited to enrolled focal students. Additionally, although the rate of parental consent for student participation was high among parents who agreed to study team outreach, the study team never received permission to contact nearly 50% of nominated eligible students. It is not clear how similar enrolled students were to those who were nominated for participation but not enrolled, although it is noteworthy that the enrolled student sample included a relatively high proportion of students from marginalized backgrounds in terms of race, ethnicity and/or caregiver education. Additionally, although the rate of consent among teachers who attended an enrollment meeting was high, we estimate that only approximately 30% of eligible teachers in participating schools enrolled, and it is possible that participating teachers differed from their nonparticipating peers in systematic ways (e.g., openness to receiving behavior management support). Finally, we did not track detailed information about implementation supports in addition to the PBMT received by enrolled teachers in both conditions, which would be important to do in future studies (e.g., via a tracking log, Walsh-Bailey et al., 2021).

Implications for Practice

Despite these limitations, this pilot study has important implications for school psychology practice. First, although further evaluation is needed, these pilot results generally support the acceptability, feasibility, and potential effectiveness of resources that are less intensive than traditional teacher coaching models to support teacher implementation of behavioral interventions. This may ultimately support the goal of offering a continuum of potential implementation supports for teachers of increasing intensity depending on teacher need and preferences (Sanetti & Collier-Meek, 2015, Simonsen et al., 2014). A multi-tiered system of support for teachers is thought to have numerous potential advantages, such as the potential for more efficient allocation of resources (Bloomfield et al., 2024). Resources like the PBMT—that teachers can engage with largely on their own but include brief meetings to support setting goals and identifying relevant resources—may help meet the need for medium-intensity implementation supports (akin to Tier 2) in this continuum. Notably, while performance feedback is a common component of effective teacher coaching models (Stormont et al., 2015), the PBMT does not include this component, which may make it a good fit in situations where observation and performance feedback is not feasible or acceptable.

Moreover, school psychologists and other professionals who consult with teachers might find it useful to use the Theory of Planned Behavior and theories of habit formation as conceptual models to inform their consultation. For example, they might consider a teacher’s level of intention to use an evidence-based intervention and tailor their support accordingly (e.g., motivational interviewing for a teacher with weak intentions vs. helping a teacher with strong intentions develop a reminder system for themselves). This work also points to ways that school psychology practitioners can consider providing teachers with implementation supports that address students’ needs at both the Tier 1 and Tier 2 levels simultaneously, which may lead to efficiencies. Although teachers in this study identified focal students with elevated ADHD-related concern, three of the four behavioral interventions supported by the PBMT can be implemented at Tier 1 and intensified for focus students (e.g., offer praise more frequently to focus students). Indeed, we observed promising teacher outcomes regarding fidelity of delivery of behavioral interventions (e.g., rates of behavior-specific praise) as applied to the whole class, as well as applied specifically to focal students. School psychologists who provide consultation might similarly consider ways to simultaneously support teachers in implementing evidence-based interventions at both the whole-class and targeted levels (e.g., supporting teachers in developing a strong Tier 1 classroom management foundation as part of supporting Tier 2 behavioral intervention delivery).

Conclusion

There is a need for additional effective, feasible, and scalable approaches to support teachers in using evidence-based behavioral classroom interventions at the whole-class and targeted levels. This study adds to the literature on implementation supports for teachers by reporting results of a pilot test of a modular resource package (“PBMT,” which included a resource library, text message reminders, and brief support meetings) to support teacher delivery of behavioral classroom interventions at Tiers 1 and 2. Quantitative and qualitative results suggest that the PBMT was perceived as highly acceptable, feasible, and contextually appropriate. The research procedures were generally feasible and resulted in a diverse sample. Overall, our findings suggest that larger-scale evaluation of the PBMT is warranted, with potential changes to procedures for student recruitment and student outcome measurement. If found to be effective in promoting teacher and student outcomes in a larger-scale trial, the PBMT may be a useful tool in the continuum of implementation supports for teachers.

IMPACT STATEMENT

This pilot randomized controlled trial examined the acceptability, feasibility, preliminary effectiveness and implementation outcomes of a package of resources for teachers to support them in using evidence-based behavior management practices for the whole class and children with symptoms of ADHD. Results suggest that the resource package was highly acceptable and feasible and may help teachers use evidence-based behavior management in the classroom, although larger-scale evaluation is still needed. This study highlights ways that school psychologists may be able to support teachers in using evidence-based classroom practices.
