Analysis of the correlation between work stress and emotional labor in college counselors: the moderating effect of psychological resilience
Authors: Tianlin Chen, Shijian Sun, Lan Luo, Yongfei Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCollege counselors often endure significant work stress, which directly impacts their emotional labor. Although previous studies have described the association between work stress and emotional labor, the critical role of psychological resilience has been overlooked. The goal of this research is to explore the correlation between work stress and emotional labor among college counselors, and further tests the moderating effect of psychological resilience in this relationship. It aims to furnish empirical evidence for higher education institutions to manage stress effectively among this population, promote deeper expressions of emotional labor, minimize superficial displays. Additionally, it provides practical basis for career happiness management at both the institutional level and for individual counselors.MethodsA cluster sampling method was employed, and through online means, 351 full-time counselors were selected as research subjects. Data were collected using the Work Stress Questionnaire, the Psychological Resilience Questionnaire, and the Two-Dimensional Emotional Labour Questionnaire. Normality tests and common method bias tests were conducted using the K-S test and Harman’s single-factor method, respectively. Descriptive statistics were performed using mean z-tests, paired t-tests, and partial correlations. The moderating effect was tested using hierarchical regression analysis and simple slope analysis. The significance level for the analysis was α = 0.05 (two-sided).ResultsThe mean scores (M ± SD ) for work stress, psychological resilience, deep acting, and surface acting were (2.881 ± 0.699), (3.398 ± 0.800), (3.723 ± 0.837), and (3.234 ± 1.014), respectively. Deep acting was significantly higher than surface acting (t = 5.159, p < 0.001). Work stress was significantly negatively correlated with psychological resilience and deep acting (r = − 0.417, r = − 0.816, both p < 0.01), and positively correlated with surface acting (r = 0.812, p < 0.01). Psychological resilience was significantly positively correlated with deep acting (r = 0.679, p < 0.01) and negatively correlated with surface acting (r = − 0.562, p < 0.01). Psychological resilience did not have a significant moderating effect on the relationship between work stress and deep acting (β=−0.053, p > 0.05). However, it had a significant moderating effect on the relationship between work stress and surface acting (β=-0.167, p < 0.001), explaining 2.9% of the variance in surface acting (∆R²= 0.027).ConclusionCollege counselors experience high levels of work stress, with deep acting in emotional labor being higher than surface acting. The relationship between work stress and emotional labor among university counselors reveals a significant positive correlation with surface acting, while exhibiting a significant negative correlation with deep acting. Psychological resilience weakens the positive association between work stress and surface acting. It is recommended to reduce work stress and improve psychological resilience levels to enhance deep acting, reduce surface acting, and improve work efficiency among college counselors.Keywords: Work stress, Emotional labor, Psychological resilience, Moderating effect, College counselors

Introduction

In today’s fast-paced society, the pursuit of happiness has transcended mere personal aspiration, becoming a core component of global consensus and societal development goals [1]. Against this backdrop, happiness management has emerged as a novel organizational management paradigm, fundamentally aimed at creating a workplace ecology that balances efficiency with humanistic care [2]. However, in the realm of current occupational happiness management, primary emphasis is placed on aspects such as mitigating work stress levels and modulating strategies for emotional labor, with less consideration given to the protective functions of intrinsic resources like psychological resilience for individuals [3, 4]. Research [5, 6] validates these inner resources as cornerstones of happiness management, enabling individuals to demonstrate greater tenacity and recuperative strength when confronting occupational challenges and life adversities, facilitating dual personal and professional growth. For university counselors, who are on the frontlines of education, investigating changes in their emotional labor state under work srtess and the underlying mechanisms of intrinsic coping resources can provide empirical foundations for both institutional and personal career happiness management.

College counselors, henceforth referred to as “counselors,” play a crucial role in China’s unique higher education management system, bearing the essential responsibility of guiding student development and nurturing future talent [7]. Their responsibilities encompass not only ideological and political education but also daily administrative tasks, reflecting a high degree of multidimensionality and complexity in their roles [8]. In contrast to “career advisors” or “student affairs officers” in Western universities—who primarily focus on psychological counseling and academic guidance without the added responsibility of ideological education—Chinese counselors exhibit significant institutional differences in their functions. Chinese counselors must simultaneously fulfill multiple roles, including ideological and political educators, psychological counselors, daily life managers, academic supervisors, and safety officers. This functional configuration, which integrates value guidance with administrative management, far exceeds the job boundaries of their Western counterparts [9]. In light of ongoing higher education reforms, as safety management responsibilities for college students are progressively delegated, counselors’ mental workload has systematically increased [10]. This “unlimited liability” work model sharply contrasts with the standardized service processes typical of Western positions, making research on Western “career advisors” or “student affairs officers” inapplicable to this group. Work stress and emotional labor

Work stress refers to the psychological and physiological tension experienced by an individual in the workplace when facing various demands, challenges, or adverse factors that exceed their ability to adapt or cope [11]. Empirical studies [12, 13] have revealed that counselor groups are universally encountering unprecedented pressure challenges. Work stress not only leads to physical and mental distress such as anxiety, depression, insomnia, and headaches [12], but also erodes their sense of occupational happiness [14], triggering alterations in their attitudes towards work [13].

Work stress precipitates changes in counselors’ work attitudes, with emotional labor being the first to be affected [15]. The essence of a counselor’s work is to foster personal development, their emotional expression is not merely a natural outpouring of personal feelings but also a reflection of societal expectations and professional demands [16]. Counselors are expected to present themselves as positive, caring figures. This societal expectation and the demands of their professional role necessitate that counselors manage their emotions in response to work stress, to align with external perceptions of their professional identity [10]. Consequently, the occupational characteristics of counselors dictate that, in addition to intellectual and physical labor, they are also heavily engaged in emotional labor, making them a group highly susceptible to emotional labor overload [10].

Emotional labor, a covert yet ubiquitous phenomenon in the workplace, involves the management of one’s emotional expressions to align with societal expectations and organizational culture, categorized into three strategies: natural acting, a raw and unadorned outpouring of genuine feelings; deep acting, which entails the positive adjustment of internal emotions to achieve harmony between felt emotions and displayed emotions; and surface acting, which modifies only the outward emotional display without altering the underlying emotional state [17]. Within this framework, natural expression is considered by scholars as “non-emotional labor” due to its lack of active emotional management, whereas deep acting and surface acting constitute the core strategies of emotional labor, embodying the subtle interplay and negotiation between the individual and the organization [18]. Diefendorff [17] suggests that surface acting, as a relatively direct and easily implemented strategy, often becomes the preferred choice for individuals when facing intense work stress. The frequent employment of this strategy, while satisfying organizational expectations in the short term, may accumulate emotional exhaustion over time, leading to a disconnection between the individual’s inner feelings and outward expressions, and consequently impacting job satisfaction and occupational happiness [12, 19].

Existing research [10, 12, 18, 20] confirms that there is a reciprocal relationship between work stress and emotional labor: On one hand, work stress predicts emotional labor, showing a significant positive correlation with surface acting and a significant negative correlation with deep acting [18]. counselors’ work stress positively predicts surface acting and negatively predicts deep acting in emotional labor [12]. This implies that under high work stress, counselors are more likely to adopt superficial coping strategies rather than deep emotional adjustments. On the other hand, it has been shown that emotional labor also serves as a predictor of work stress [10]. Emotional labor itself is a source of stress. When individuals need to constantly monitor and adjust their emotional expressions, they feel more tense and anxious, thereby exacerbating their perception of work stress. Increased work stress may then lead to a heavier burden of emotional labor, affecting the quality of emotional labor; whereas a decline in the quality of emotional labor may further intensify the perception of work stress, creating a vicious cycle [18].

However, it is worth noting that even when facing similar levels of work stress, there are significant individual differences in the emotional labor strategies adopted by different counselors [21]. The underlying mechanism behind this phenomenon, that is, When work stress specifically predicts counselors’ choice of emotional labor styles still requires further in-depth exploration and explanation.

The moderating effect of psychological resilience

Psychological resilience refers to an individual’s ability to adapt, recover, and grow positively when faced with adversity and setbacks [22]. As a fundamental competency for navigating challenges, psychological resilience serves as a vital resource for managing work stress, protecting mental health, and achieving occupational well-being. Empirical research indicates that individuals with higher levels of psychological resilience are more likely to employ deep emotional regulation strategies (deep acting) to tackle work challenges, while those with lower resilience tend to rely on surface acting [23, 24].

The distinctive characteristics and mechanisms of psychological resilience are thoroughly validated and explained within the frameworks of the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [25] and Conservation of Resources (COR) theory [26]. The JD-R model suggests that workplace job demands (e.g., work stress) and individual resources (e.g., psychological resilience) collaboratively influence an individual’s work processes and outcomes. From this perspective, psychological resilience serves as a crucial resource that empowers individuals to engage in positive cognitive appraisal, such as interpreting stress as a manageable challenge rather than an overwhelming threat. This resilience enhances their ability to proactively seek and mobilize resources, including emotional and cognitive support, thereby promoting positive work attitudes, such as work engagement, and strengthening their sense of environmental control and adaptability.

COR theory further examines the logic behind individuals’ strategic choices when confronted with threats of resource loss, such as work stress. This theory highlights that the coping strategies individuals employ under pressure—including emotional labor techniques—are heavily influenced by their perceived resource levels, especially their core psychological resilience. A more in-depth analysis of resilience’s moderating role indicates that its impact demonstrates significant directionality.

Under conditions of high psychological resilience, highly resilient individuals possess a more substantial buffer of resources and a greater capacity to acquire additional resources [27]. When faced with significant work stress, they are more likely to perceive the stress as a developmental challenge. This positive appraisal, combined with their superior emotional regulation skills and problem-solving confidence, motivates them to actively mobilize internal resources—such as empathy and value congruence—for deep emotional engagement and adjustment (i.e., deep acting). Although deep acting involves some short-term emotional expenditure, its ability to foster meaningful relationships, generate lasting fulfillment, and achieve resource gains positions it as a valuable and sustainable resource investment strategy for highly resilient individuals. This mechanism illustrates that high psychological resilience in stressful contexts not only promotes the activation of deep acting but also mitigates the tendency for stress to lead to surface acting.

In contrast, individuals with low psychological resilience possess relatively limited resource reserves and exhibit heightened sensitivity to resource loss [28]. Under conditions of high work stress, they are particularly vulnerable to resource depletion and experience intense negative emotions. To mitigate further resource losses, such as emotional exhaustion and identity conflict, individuals with low resilience often prioritize self-protective strategies that conserve resources. In this context, surface acting— which involves managing only outward behavior while requiring lower affective and cognitive involvement and immediate resource expenditure—naturally becomes a more appealing option. As a result, a state of low psychological resilience amplifies the positive correlation between stressors and surface acting: under high stress, low resilience significantly increases individuals’ reliance on surface acting.

Integrating the core tenets of the JD-R model and COR theory clearly demonstrates that psychological resilience is not merely a parallel predictor (antecedent variable) in the relationship between counselors’ work stress and emotional labor strategies; it is fundamentally a critical moderator that profoundly influences the nature of this relationship. Its core moderating effect manifests as a directional transformation: high psychological resilience promotes pressure-driven engagement in deeper positive emotional investment (deep acting) and inhibits stress-induced surface acting. Conversely, low psychological resilience significantly amplifies the impact of work stress in triggering surface acting [26]. Therefore, to thoroughly explore the intrinsic mechanisms by which work stress affects the emotional labor strategy choices of college counselors, it is essential to incorporate the moderating effect of psychological resilience as a fundamental element within the analytical framework and subject it to rigorous examination.

The goal and hypothesis of this study

The goal of this research is to explore the correlation between work stress and emotional labor among college counselors, and further tests the moderating effect of psychological resilience in this relationship. It aims to furnish empirical evidence for higher education institutions to manage stress effectively among this population, promote deeper expressions of emotional labor, minimize superficial displays. Additionally, it provides practical basis for career happiness management at both the institutional level and for individual counselors. Based on the aforementioned literature review, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H1

The relationship between work stress and surface acting demonstrates a significant positive correlation;

H2

The relationship between work stress and deep acting demonstrates a significant negative correlation;

H3.1

psychological resilience weakens the positive association between work stress and surface acting;

H3.2

psychological resilience strengthens the negative association between work stress and deep acting.

Methods

Research design

This study employed a cross-sectional survey design, conducted between November and December 2023. A convenience cluster sampling method was utilized to select full-time counselors from 16 undergraduate universities, specifically 8 in Nanchang, 5 in Guangzhou, and 3 in Fuzhou, as the subjects of the research. Inclusion Criteria: Individuals who are recognized as full-time counselors and are actively involved in frontline student management activities. Exclusion Criteria: Individuals who are classified as full-time counselors but are not currently active in their positions, as well as individuals engaged in frontline student management tasks who do not possess the formal designation of full-time counselor. Prior to completing the questionnaire, all participants provided electronic informed consent, thereby confirming their voluntary participation.

Data collection was conducted utilizing the “Questionnaire Star” platform (https://www.wjx.cn), developed by Changsha Ranxing Information Technology Co., Ltd., located in Hunan, China. This platform is extensively employed in academic research due to its user-friendly interface, comprehensive functionality, and efficiency in the creation, distribution, and analysis of questionnaires. The questionnaire content was disseminated to the target population through unique QR codes provided by faculty members from the Student Affairs Offices of the collaborating universities. All coordinating personnel received standardized operational procedures and training in research ethics, which included guidelines against the use of leading language and a clear communication of participants’ rights to withdraw from the study without any repercussions at any time. The questionnaires were completed anonymously, ensuring confidentiality regarding the specific university affiliation of participants. All items within the questionnaire were mandatory, and only one submission per IP address was permitted to maintain the integrity of the data collected.

The target sample size for this study was established in accordance with Wu’s [29] recommendation, which suggests that the sample size for factor analysis should be between five to ten times the number of items in the questionnaire. Given that the questionnaire utilized in this research comprised 55 items, the target sample size was determined to be between 275 and 550 participants. We aimed to survey approximately 400 eligible individuals from the selected universities. Ultimately, a total of 386 questionnaires were collected. Following the implementation of two quality control measures—excluding questionnaires with completion times shorter than the mean minus three standard deviations (i.e., less than 90 s) and removing patterned responses in which 80% or more of the items received identical answers—351 valid questionnaires were retained for the final analysis. This resulted in an effective response rate of 90.93%.

Participant demographic characteristics

The demographic characteristics of the valid sample (N = 351) were as follows: 148 were male and 203 were female, accounting for 42.17% and 57.83% respectively; 164 were under 30 years old, 145 were between 30 and 34 years old, 36 were between 35 and 39 years old, and 6 were 40 years old or older, representing 46.72%, 41.31%, 10.26%, and 1.71% respectively; 142 had less than 2 years of service, 108 had 2 to 5 years of service, 62 had 6 to 10 years of service, and 39 had over 10 years of service, comprising 40.46%, 30.77%, 17.66%, and 11.11% respectively; 229 were married and 122 were unmarried, making up 65.24% and 34.76% respectively; 58 managed 200 or fewer students, 77 managed between 201 and 300 students, and 216 managed more than 300 students, accounting for 16.52%, 21.94%, and 61.54% respectively; 25 had an average daily working hours of 8 h or less, 169 had between 8 and 10 h, and 157 had more than 10 h, representing 7.12%, 48.15%, and 44.73% respectively.

Survey tools

Demographic questionnaire

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect basic information about the counselors, including gender, age, years of service, marital status, number of students managed, and average daily working hours. Pertinent studies [8, 10, 12, 13] have demonstrated that these demographic variables are significant factors influencing counselors’ work stress, psychological resilience and emotional labor. Given their potential impact on the subjects within the context of this study, we regarded these variables as control variables in our analyses.

Work stress questionnaire

This study uses the College Counselor Work Stress Questionnaire developed by Tang [12] to assess the level of work stress among counselors. The questionnaire comprised 19 items, grouped into five factors: workload, school evaluation stress, interpersonal stress, student-related stress, and personal stress. It was scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating “no stress” and 4 indicating “extreme stress”; higher scores indicated greater stress. This questionnaire has been used by several researchers [30] and demonstrated good reliability and structural validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this study was 0.936. Notably, while broad-spectrum questionnaires such as Karasek’s [31] Job Content Questionnaire and Siegrist’s [32] Effort-Reward Imbalance Questionnaire suit generic occupational contexts, they may overlook the specific conditions and stress elements pertinent to Chinese college counselors. Conversely, Tang’s [12] specially adapted questionnaire adeptly portrays the complexities of their unique work environment and stressors, offering a refined examination of the diverse stresses encountered by counselors in Chinese academic settings.

Emotional labor questionnaire

This study employs a two-dimensional emotional labor questionnaire, which was adapted by Wu [33] from Brotheridge and Lee’s [34] the Emotional Labor Questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of 11 items, with 5 items for surface acting and 6 items for deep acting. It has been widely used among teachers, healthcare professionals, and other groups. Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with values from 1 to 5. Scores for each dimension were calculated separately, with higher scores indicating higher levels of emotional labor. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this questionnaire was 0.899.

Psychological resilience questionnaire

The psychological resilience of counselors was assessed using a resilience questionnaire adapted by Yu & Zhang [35], based on Connor and Davidson’s [36] the Psychological Resilience Questionnaire. The questionnaire included 25 items, scored on a 5-point Likert scale, from 1 for “never” to 5 for “always,” with higher scores indicating higher resilience levels. This questionnaire has been widely adopted by researchers [23, 24] and exhibits good reliability and validity. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this study was 0.917.

Data analysis

This study utilized SPSS version 23.0, including the AMOS plugin, for the analysis of data. A significance level of α = 0.05 (two-tailed) was established to ensure the effectiveness and reliability of the data analysis.

Data Quality Assessment. Prior to conducting formal data analysis, an assessment of data quality was performed. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test was employed to evaluate whether the data distribution adhered to the assumption of normality. Concurrently, Harman’s single-factor exploratory factor analysis was executed to identify the presence of common method bias. The results of these two tests established a robust foundation for the subsequent statistical analyses.

Descriptive Statistics and Correlation Analysis. Descriptive statistical analyses were conducted for key variables, with quantitative data presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). To qualitatively characterize the levels of variables within the study sample, this investigation utilized the theoretical median as an alternative comparative benchmark by calculating mean Z-scores in relation to this value. This methodology facilitates a preliminary interpretation of questionnaire results within a theoretical framework, particularly in the absence of empirical calibration data. Concurrently, to mitigate the influence of spurious correlations introduced by demographic variables, Partial Correlation Analysis was employed to examine the net relationships among variables. This approach allows for a more accurate elucidation of the intrinsic connections between work stress, psychological resilience, and emotional labor.

Moderating effect test. To examine the moderating effect of counselors’ psychological resilience on the relationship between work stress and emotional labor, emotional labor—encompassing both surface acting and deep acting—was designated as the dependent variable. The independent variable, work stress, and the moderator variable, psychological resilience, were mean-centered. An interaction term, “Work Stress × Psychological Resilience, was subsequently created. Hierarchical regression analysis was conducted utilizing Hayes’s PROCESS macro (version 4.0). In Step 1, control variables—including sex, age, tenure, marital status, number of students managed, and average daily working hours—were incorporated into the model. Step 2 involved the addition of the centered independent variable, while Step 3 introduced the interaction term. A 95% confidence interval was computed through bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples, and the change in R² at each step was documented to evaluate variations in the model’s explanatory power. For significant interaction effects, simple slope analyses were performed by categorizing participants based on mean levels of psychological resilience, adjusted by ± 1 standard deviation. Visualization plots were generated to effectively illustrate the differential impact of work stress on emotional labor across varying levels of psychological resilience, thereby facilitating a comprehensive exploration of the moderating mechanism of psychological resilience.

Results

Normality test and common method bias test

The normality of the questionnaire data was tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) test. The results showed that the absolute values of kurtosis for work stress, occupational emotion, deep emotional labor, and surface emotional labor were 0.769, 0.387, 0.644, and 0.984, respectively, while the absolute values of skewness were 0.089, 0.053, 0.593, and 0.053, respectively. According to Drezner [37], stringent requirements for normality are typically unattainable; however, if the absolute values of kurtosis are less than 10 and those of skewness are less than 3, the data can be considered approximately normally distributed. Therefore, the data in this study can be deemed to conform to a normal distribution.

The common method bias of the collected questionnaire data was examined using Harman’s single-factor method. The results indicated that the χ2/df (goodness-of-fit Chi-square test) was 3.947, higher than the criterion (< 3); the GFI (goodness-of-fit index) was 0.521, the CFI (comparative fit index) was 0.671, the NFI (normed fit index) was 0.633, and the NNFI (non-normed fit index) was 0.674, all of which were markedly lower than the criterion (> 0.9); the RMSEA (root mean square error of approximation) was 0.116, higher than the criterion (< 0.10), and the RMR (root mean square residual) was 0.093, significantly deviating from the criterion (< 0.05). Moreover, the factor analysis results revealed that the variance explained by the first common factor was 31.89% (below the critical value of 40%) [38]. These findings indicate that the questionnaire data does not converge into a single factor, suggesting that different variables within the data are not influenced by a systematic bias. Therefore, we conclude that there is no significant common method bias in our data.

Descriptive statistical results for the key variables

The average score for work stress among counselors was 2.881 ± 0.699, which exceeds the theoretical median of 2.500. A z-test was conducted, resulting in the rejection of the null hypothesis that the mean equals the theoretical median at a 95% confidence level. This finding suggests a relatively elevated level of work stress within the counselor population.

In relation to emotional labor, the average score for surface acting was 3.234 ± 1.014, whereas the average score for deep acting was 3.723 ± 0.837. Both scores exceeded the theoretical median of 3.000, as indicated by a z-test that rejected the null hypothesis at a 95% confidence level. Furthermore, a paired t-test revealed that deep acting was significantly higher than surface acting (t = 5.159, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that while counselors engage in emotional labor at a relatively high level, deep acting emerges as the predominant strategy.

The average psychological resilience of counselors was found to be 3.398 ± 0.800, which surpasses the theoretical median of 3.000. A z-test conducted at a 95% confidence level resulted in the rejection of the null hypothesis. These findings indicate that the cohort of counselors exhibits a generally strong level of psychological resilience.

Moderating effect analysis of psychological resilience

Concerns regarding multicollinearity emerged due to the correlations between work stress and both acting strategies exceeding 0.800 [40]. To mitigate this risk in the moderation analyses (Models M1/M2), we orthogonalized the interaction terms, thereby reducing variable interdependencies to improve the validity and interpretability of the model [41].​.

As demonstrated in Table 2, the moderating effect of psychological resilience on the relationship between work stress and deep acting was found to be statistically non-significant (β =-0.053, p > 0.05). In other words, psychological resilience did not significantly strengthen the negative association between these variables. Conversely, psychological resilience exhibited a significant moderating effect on the relationship between work stress and surface acting (β=-0.167, p < 0.001), suggesting that it weakens the positive association between these variables. This moderation explained 2.7% of the variance in surface acting among university counselors (ΔR² = 0.027). According to Wen & Ye [42], a variance explanation exceeding 2% is considered substantively meaningful. Consequently, this study highlights the substantive significance of the moderating effect of psychological resilience in the relationship between work stress and surface acting among counselors.

Discussion

The work stress of counselors is relatively high

The elevated levels of work stress among counselors in this study align with previous findings by Zhao [29], Liu [43], and Mou [44], among others. The counselor’s work stress, stemming from an intricate and complex interplay of factors, involves dealing with a large number of work subjects and a variety of work contents [8,103,12,13], and the relentless intensity of work conditions [43]. In this survey, it was found that more than half of the counselors (61.54%) manage over 300 students, significantly exceeding the guideline of a 1:200 student-to-counselor ratio for full-time counselors in higher education institutions, indicating an overwhelming workload [45]. The complexity of work content compels them to dedicate substantial time and energy to the ceaseless management of student affairs [46]. The survey revealed that 92.88% of counselors work more than eight hours a day, with 44.54% working over ten hours, far exceeding standard working hours. More critically, counselors not only serve as educators but also bear the direct responsibility for campus safety, a dual role that necessitates a constant state of heightened alertness. The prolonged mental strain and the need to be perpetually vigilant contribute to the considerable stress that counselors endure [43].

Counselors tend to engage in deep acting in their emotional labor

In response to work stress, the immediate and relatively simple strategy of surface acting often emerges as an initial reaction [17]. However, our study among counselors reveals a contrasting phenomenon: their emotional labor is predominantly characterized by deep acting, a finding that resonates with Wen, et al.‘s [21] research. This could be attributed to the current selection criteria and policy support for counselors [47]. Institutions employ a rigorous, transparent, and competitive recruitment process, combining written exams and comprehensive interviews, to select those with exceptional political qualities, outstanding abilities, and distinguished academic records [10]. This stringent selection mechanism ensures a cadre of counselors with robust ideological and disciplinary standards, as well as well-rounded competencies. They demonstrate stable personality traits, effectively identifying and managing their emotions through deep acting, adapting to work demands rather than masking their true feelings with surface behaviors [48].

Furthermore, the external policy environment for counselors is gradually improving as well. The issuance of the “Professional Competence Standards for College Counselors (Interim)” [49] not only provides counselors with solid policy support but also elevates their professional development and social recognition. This facilitates the integration of counselors’ professional sentiments with the educational philosophy of higher learning institutions. In this harmonious and supportive setting, deep acting ceases to be an additional burden and becomes a natural and efficient choice for counselors. Additionally, many institutions provide counselors with professional training and psychological support, a gesture of care and investment aimed at developing their emotional management skills and learning to maintain psychological balance under pressure [46].

Psychological resilience exhibits differential moderating effects on the relationship between work stress and emotional labor strategies among counselors

This study reveals that psychological resilience has a significant moderating effect on the relationship between work stress and surface acting among university counselors. Specifically, it acts as a crucial protective factor that mitigates the strength of work stress’s positive influence on surface acting. In contrast, the moderating effect of psychological resilience on the relationship between work stress and deep acting was statistically non-significant, indicating that it did not enhance the negative association between work stress and deep acting. These differential moderating effects can be comprehensively interpreted through the theoretical frameworks of the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) model [25] and COR theory [25], with a specific focus on their explanatory mechanisms regarding resource depletion and protection.

From the combined perspective of the JJD-R model and COR theory, the work stress experienced by counselors represents a typical job demand that continuously depletes individuals’ psychological resources. To cope, individuals may resort to surface acting—suppressing genuine emotions while displaying externally mandated emotional states. Although this strategy may temporarily help maintain a professional demeanor, its prolonged use can lead to emotional exhaustion and perpetuate a vicious cycle of resource depletion. Psychological resilience, a crucial internal resource, aids counselors in allocating their resources more effectively. By employing positive cognitive reframing and emotion regulation strategies, resilience mitigates the negative impact of stress [50]. Individuals with high psychological resilience tend to have more substantial resource reserves. When faced with stress, they are more likely to utilize social support or self-regulation techniques to alleviate emotional strain, thereby diminishing the influence of work stress on surface acting. However, the non-significant moderating effect of psychological resilience on the relationship between work stress and deep acting can be interpreted through both theories. Deep acting requires individuals to actively adjust their internal feelings to align with professional expectations, making it a highly resource-intensive behavior [51]. When work stress exacerbates resource depletion, individuals may lack the necessary resources to engage in deep acting, resulting in the observed negative association. According to the COR theory, individuals under stress prioritize allocating resources to meet immediate the pressing strain caused by the stressor—over pursuing which require resource-intensive deep acting [52]. Even though heightened psychological resilience enhances an individual’s resource reserves, it does not change the fundamental prioritization of resources for immediate survival [53].Consequently, even counselors with high resilience may struggle to employ deep acting as a coping strategy during high-stress conditions where resources are limited, leading to the non-significant moderating effect on this relationship.

Synthesizing JD-R model and COR theory reveals that the variation in the moderating effects of psychological resilience fundamentally reflects an individual’s strategic trade-off between “survival and growth” under conditions of limited resources [54]. This mechanism not only clarifies the complexities of occupational stress management for counselors but also provides a theoretical foundation for developing targeted mental health interventions in the future.

Summary

Conclusion

This study empirically substantiates the correlation between the work stress faced by college counselors and their emotional labor, while also disclosing the regulatory role of psychological resilience in this relationship. This contributes significantly to the enhancement of the Job Demands-Resources model and Emotional Labor Theory, providing robust theoretical grounding and empirical validation for the development of college counseling teams, preservation of mental health, and the execution of happiness management practices. Limitations

All research endeavors have limitations, and this study is no exception. The primary limitations of this study are as follows:

The relatively small sample size, skewed age structure towards younger individuals, and concentration of participants from specific schools and regions may limit the generalizability and external validity of the findings. This underscores the need for future research to broaden the sample base by including a more diverse array of higher education institutions, regions, and counselors of varying ages and backgrounds, thereby enhancing representativeness and ensuring the reliability and applicability of results across wider contexts.

Implications for practice

Based on the findings of this study, to optimize counselors’ emotional labor strategies and enhance their deep acting levels, university administrators and counselors themselves should jointly take responsibility for alleviating work stress and boosting psychological resilience. (1) To alleviate work stress, the Job Demand-Control-Support Model [58] can serve as a guiding framework, advocating for interventions in three dimensions: reducing job demands, increasing job control, and enhancing social support. This can be accomplished by optimizing human resource management, equitably distributing workloads, streamlining work procedures, providing stress management training, offering mental health support, and clarifying career advancement pathways. Counselors, as the captains of their own careers, should also take proactive steps, such as balancing work and leisure, engaging in physical activities, seeking support from their social networks, and continuously upgrading their professional skills, to cultivate resilience in the face of work challenges. (2) In enhancing psychological resilience, Kumpfer [59] posits that its development is a product of the interaction between the environment and the individual. Institutional leaders should assume the roles of facilitators and supporters by providing resilience training, fostering a supportive work environment, strengthening team cohesion, recognizing counselors’ contributions, and offering ongoing professional development opportunities, thereby laying a solid foundation for counselors’ resilience building. As for the individual level, counselors should commit to developing a positive attitude, practicing emotional regulation, acquiring coping strategies, engaging in team-building activities, pursuing personal interests, and sustaining a dedication to lifelong learning. Through this concerted self-enhancement process, they gradually amass a substantial and enduring reservoir of resilience.
