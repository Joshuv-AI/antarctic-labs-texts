Knowledge, attitudes and practices of nurses regarding diabetic foot ulcer care at Sunyani teaching hospital, Ghana: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Clement Naayaara Zuuri, Benedicta Princess Dotse, Joseph Ngmenesegre Suglo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are a major cause of morbidity, lower-limb amputation, and mortality among people with diabetes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Nurses play a key role in DFU prevention and management; however, evidence on their knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) in Ghana remains limited. This study assessed nurses’ KAP regarding DFU care at a tertiary hospital in Ghana.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted among nurses providing direct patient care from September to November 2025. A structured self-administered online questionnaire assessed socio-demographic characteristics and DFU-related knowledge, attitudes, and practices. Descriptive statistics summarized the data, and chi-square tests examined associations between knowledge levels and selected variables (p < 0.05).ResultsOf the 128 nurses invited, 100 participated (response rate: 78.1%). Most respondents were female (77%), and 90% had not received formal DFU training. Overall, 61% demonstrated good knowledge, 30% moderate knowledge, and 9% poor knowledge. Attitudes toward DFU care were generally positive, though workload constraints were noted. Common practices included patient education (81%), foot inspection (74%), and referral of suspected DFU cases (75%), while use of standardized neuropathy screening tools was low (10%) and documentation of foot-care education inconsistent (52%). Knowledge was significantly associated with academic qualification (χ² = 14.876, p = 0.002), years of practice (χ² = 8.219, p = 0.016), and prior DFU training (χ² = 12.604, p < 0.001).ConclusionsDespite positive attitudes toward DFU care, gaps remain in preventive knowledge and evidence-based screening practices. Strengthening structured DFU training and institutional support may improve nursing practice and patient outcomes.

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) represent one of the most serious complications of diabetes mellitus and contribute substantially to disability, amputation, and mortality worldwide. Notably, the five-year mortality rate among individuals with DFUs has been reported to be comparable to that of several major cancers, underscoring the severity of the condition [1]. According to the International Diabetes Federation, 589 million adults worldwide had diabetes in 2025; if current trends continue, that figure is expected to rise to over 850 million by 2050 [2]. It is further estimated that nearly one in four individuals with diabetes will develop a diabetic foot ulcer during their lifetime, with millions of new cases occurring each year [1]. Diabetic foot ulcers account for most diabetes-related lower-limb amputations and are linked to high mortality among patients with diabetes [3, 4].

The development of DFUs is multifactorial and typically results from the interaction of peripheral neuropathy, peripheral arterial disease, and impaired immune function. Neuropathy reduces protective sensation, making individuals less aware of minor trauma to the feet, while vascular impairment compromises tissue perfusion and delays healing. Consequently, small injuries such as friction from footwear, minor cuts, or burns may progress to infection, deep tissue damage, and osteomyelitis if not identified and managed promptly. Without timely interventions, including wound debridement, pressure off-loading, and appropriate antimicrobial therapy, the condition may progress to gangrene or systemic infection, ultimately requiring amputation [5, 6].

Although diabetic foot ulcers occur worldwide, their burden is disproportionately higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where healthcare resources for chronic disease management are often limited. A systematic review estimated the global prevalence of DFU at 6.3%, with Africa reporting the highest prevalence at approximately 7.2% [7]. In addition to physical disability, DFUs often lead to substantial socioeconomic consequences, including loss of income, reduced productivity, and psychosocial distress for affected individuals and their families [8–11]. Preventive strategies such as routine foot examination, patient education, and early identification of high-risk patients are therefore critical components of diabetes management. Current clinical guidance emphasizes regular risk-based foot screening and appropriate off-loading interventions, particularly for individuals identified as having increased ulcer risk [12, 13].

In Ghana, however, available evidence suggests a similarly high burden of diabetic foot ulcers. Hospital-based studies conducted in tertiary facilities have reported DFU prevalence rates ranging from 8% to 12%, with previous ulcer history, peripheral neuropathy, and structural foot deformities identified as major predictors [14]. These findings underscore the need for strengthened preventive care strategies and improved clinical management of diabetic foot complications within the Ghanaian healthcare system.

Effective management of diabetic foot disease generally requires a multidisciplinary approach involving physicians, nurses, diabetes educators, vascular specialists, orthopedic surgeons, and other healthcare professionals [15–17]. Within this team, nurses play a particularly important role because they constitute the largest proportion of the healthcare workforce and maintain continuous contact with patients during clinical care. Their responsibilities commonly include performing routine foot examinations, identifying risk factors such as neuropathy or vascular compromise, providing patient education on foot hygiene and self-care practices, delivering wound care interventions, and coordinating referrals for specialized treatment when necessary [18, 19]. The International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) highlights several core nursing competencies essential for effective DFU prevention and management, including risk assessment, ulcer classification, and the implementation of pressure off-loading strategies [20] .

Evidence suggests that well-implemented nursing interventions can significantly improve patient outcomes. Studies have reported that structured foot-care education and regular risk assessment by nurses may reduce the incidence of diabetic foot ulcers by up to 30% and decrease major amputation rates by nearly 50% [21]. Conversely, inadequate patient education and insufficient preventive counseling are strongly associated with poor foot-care behaviors among individuals with diabetes [22]. Despite the importance of nurses’ involvement in DFU care, research conducted in several countries has documented considerable gaps in nurses’ knowledge and competencies related to diabetic foot management. For example, a large study in northern Vietnam reported that more than 80% of nurses failed to correctly answer key questions related to DFU management, with lack of formal wound-care training identified as a major contributing factor [23]. Similar knowledge gaps have also been documented among nurses in Nigeria, Iraq, Egypt, and South Africa, where substantial proportions of respondents demonstrated limited familiarity with internationally recommended DFU risk-assessment frameworks [24–26].

In Ghana, available research indicates notable challenges in diabetic foot management within the healthcare system. A study conducted in the Bosomtwe District of the Ashanti Region among healthcare professionals reported deficiencies in knowledge and adherence to evidence-based DFU management guidelines, partly attributed to the absence of standardized clinical protocols and limited diagnostic confidence among providers [27]. Furthermore, a qualitative investigation at Sunyani Teaching Hospital highlighted several contextual barriers affecting DFU care, including resource limitations, heavy clinical workloads, and the emotional burden experienced by healthcare professionals when managing patients with severe diabetic complications [28].

While these studies provide valuable insights into systemic and contextual barriers to effective DFU care, they do not specifically focus on nurses nor quantitatively assess their knowledge, attitudes, and routine practices. At the same time, the burden of diabetes and its complications continue to increase in Ghana [29, 30], leading to more patients presenting with advanced diabetic foot ulcers in tertiary healthcare facilities. In routine clinical practice, delayed presentation, inadequate patient education on foot care, and inconsistent foot screening remain common challenges. Standardized risk assessment tools, such as the 10-g monofilament, are not always routinely used, and documentation of preventive foot care education is often inconsistent [31].

Given that nurses constitute the largest group of healthcare providers and are primarily responsible for routine foot examination, patient education, wound care, and referral of high-risk patients, gaps in their knowledge and clinical practices may contribute to delayed detection and progression of diabetic foot complications. This study therefore provides an institution-based quantitative assessment of nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding diabetic foot ulcer care at Sunyani Teaching Hospital. By generating empirical evidence on nurses’ competencies and practice patterns, the study aims to inform targeted training initiatives, strengthen adherence to evidence-based guidelines, and support policy efforts to improve diabetic foot care and reduce preventable complications within the Ghanaian healthcare system.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted at Sunyani Teaching Hospital (STH), a 300-bed urban tertiary facility located in Ghana’s Bono Region. STH functions as a major referral center for the Middle Belt and receives cross-border referrals from neighboring Côte d’Ivoire. The hospital delivers multidisciplinary services through units that include a dedicated diabetic clinic, orthopedics, physiotherapy, and psychiatry [32]. The hospital employs more than 700 nurses across inpatient wards and outpatient services. Their duties include initial assessments, administering medication, wound and peri-operative care, rehabilitation support, and educating patients and families. As a tertiary referral centre, the hospital manages a disproportionately high burden of diabetes-related foot ulcer cases, with lower-limb amputations representing one of the most common severe DFU outcomes, and nurses providing continuous frontline care across inpatient, outpatient, and post-discharge follow-up services.

Considering its referral mandate, service offerings, and nursing workforce size, STH offers a suitable setting to evaluate nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices (KAP) concerning diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care. Data collection focused on clinical areas where DFU prevention and management are regularly performed such as medical and surgical wards, outpatient departments, emergency units, and other relevant sections ensuring that nurses who frequently manage patients at risk of, or with, DFU are well represented.

Study design

A cross-sectional quantitative study design was employed to assess nurses’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) disease and the care of affected patients. This design enabled the collection of data at a single point in time to describe the current state of knowledge and attitudes among nurses in the study setting [33].

Study population and sample size

The study targeted registered nurses and nurse assistants involved in direct patient care at Sunyani Teaching Hospital. Based on the hospital’s human resource records, 128 nurses met the eligibility criteria. Given the relatively small and accessible target population, the study adopted a near-census approach, with efforts made to recruit all eligible participants.

A convenience sampling technique [34] was employed to recruit nurses who were available and willing to participate during the data collection period. This approach was considered appropriate due to the operational constraints of a shift-based hospital environment, where nurses work on rotating schedules, making probability-based sampling difficult to implement within the available study period.

Participants were recruited through professional forums, ward rounds, staff meetings, and online platforms. Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire distributed via Google Forms. Of the 128 eligible nurses, 100 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of 78.1%. The achieved sample represents a substantial proportion of the target population and was considered adequate for descriptive and bivariate analyses.

Participation in the study was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to completion of the questionnaire.

Data collection instrument

The questionnaire was derived from a validated tool on KAP for nurses on the prevention of DFU [35] and a similar study that examined nurses’ knowledge and attitudes towards the diabetic foot [23, 36]. As these instruments are available in the public domain through peer-reviewed publications, no additional permission was required for their academic use. All sources were appropriately cited, and the questionnaire items were adapted to suit the objectives of the present study.

The knowledge section, comprising dichotomous (Yes/No) items, was analyzed using the Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20), while the attitude section, consisting of Likert-scale items, was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. The instrument demonstrated good internal consistency, with a KR-20 coefficient of 0.83 for the knowledge items and a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 for the attitude items, both exceeding the acceptable reliability threshold of 0.70. The pilot test confirmed that the questionnaire was clear, relevant, and appropriate for measuring the targeted constructs, and therefore no modifications were required. The questionnaire comprised four sections:

Section A: sociodemographic and professional characteristics, including age, gender, highest qualification, years of nursing experience, and prior training in diabetic foot care.

Section B: The knowledge section comprised 15 items designed to evaluate nurses’ knowledge of DFU pathophysiology (foe example; neuropathy and ischemia), risk factors, assessment methods (including off-loading and wound assessment), and management principles. Knowledge was assessed using dichotomous (Yes/No) items, where each correct response (“Yes”) was scored as 1 and each incorrect response (“No”) was scored as 0. Individual knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 15. Knowledge levels were categorized using Bloom’s cut-off points. The total knowledge score was converted into percentages and classified as follows: 80–100% (good knowledge), 60–79% (moderate knowledge), and < 60% (poor knowledge). This classification approach is widely used in KAP studies to interpret knowledge levels [37].

Section C: The ‘attitude’ section included 7 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale: strongly agree, agree, neutral, disagree, and strongly disagree, designed to evaluate the following: barriers to providing diabetic foot education during routine consultations and nurses’ attitudes toward diabetic foot examinations in foot care. Attitude toward diabetic foot ulcer care was measured using Likert-scale items ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). For each item, the mean score was calculated to determine the level of agreement among respondents. A decision mean of 3.0 was used as the reference point, where mean scores above 3.0 indicated agreement with the statement and scores below 3.0 indicated disagreement. In addition, a grand mean of all attitude items was computed to determine the overall attitude of nurses toward diabetic foot ulcer care.

Section D: The practice section consisted of 9 items assessed using dichotomous (Yes/No) responses, evaluating nurses’ clinical practices related to diabetic foot care, including patient education and routine foot assessment. Responses were coded as Yes = 1 and No = 0. A total practice score was computed by summing the responses, with higher scores indicating better adherence to recommended DFU care practices. Practice results were presented descriptively using frequencies and score distributions, without categorization into “adequate” or “inadequate” practice due to the absence of validated cut-off points in the literature.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Nurses were eligible to participate if they were registered nurses or nurse assistants working at Sunyani Teaching Hospital, had been employed at the facility for at least one year, were actively involved in direct patient care, had access to a smartphone to complete the online questionnaire, and provided informed consent.

Nurses were excluded if they had worked at the hospital for less than one year, were not involved in direct patient care, were on leave during the data collection period, declined to participate, or submitted incomplete questionnaires.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected over a three-month period (September-November 2025). Following approval from hospital management, trained research assistants disseminated a secure online questionnaire link to eligible nurses during shift handovers and staff meetings. Data collection was conducted exclusively using Google Forms.

To ensure a single response per participant, the survey was configured to allow only one submission per Google account, and participants were instructed to complete the questionnaire once. Additionally, submitted responses were reviewed for potential duplication using response timestamps and demographic characteristics; no duplicate entries were identified.

The survey landing page provided detailed information on the study purpose, confidentiality, and data use, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to access to the questionnaire. Participants completed the survey at their convenience using personal smartphones. All responses were submitted electronically through the online platform.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were used to summarize respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics and reported practices. Knowledge of diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care was assessed by assigning one point for each correct response and calculating a total knowledge score. The scores were converted into percentages and categorized using Bloom’s cut-off points as follows: 80–100% (good knowledge), 60–79% (moderate knowledge), and < 60% (poor knowledge).

Bivariate associations between DFU knowledge levels and selected socio-demographic and professional characteristics were examined using the Pearson chi-square test of independence. Where the assumptions for the chi-square test were not met, that is, where expected cell counts were less than 5, Fisher’s exact test was applied as appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using Epi Info™ version 7.2.6.0.

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance for this study was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE) of Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology, which serves as a recognized Research Ethics Committee (REC) (Approval No. CHRPE/AP/212/26). Administrative permission to conduct the study was subsequently granted by the Ethics and Research Committee of Sunyani Teaching Hospital prior to data collection.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human participants. Participation in the study was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically before participants could proceed to complete the online questionnaire. Respondents were informed about the purpose of the study, their right to decline or withdraw from participation at any time without consequences, and the confidentiality of their responses. No personally identifiable information was collected, and all data were securely stored and used solely for research purposes.

Knowledge of nurses on diabetic foot ulcer care

Table 2 presents respondents’ item-level performance on knowledge of diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care across fifteen items. Overall, the proportion of correct responses varied across the domains assessed.

The highest proportions of correct responses were observed for the statement that the presence of slough is not necessarily an indication of infection in diabetic ulcers, with 83 (83.0%) respondents answering correctly, followed by the item indicating that iodine dressings are effective for wounds with clinical signs of infection, which was correctly answered by 81 (81.0%) respondents. These items had correct response rates within the range corresponding to the “good” category based on Bloom’s cut-off criteria.

Several items recorded correct response rates within the “moderate” range (60–79%). For instance, 79 (79.0%) respondents correctly identified that osteomyelitis impairs healing of diabetic ulcers, while 76 (76.0%) recognized that sensory neuropathy can result in unnoticed skin damage leading to ulcer formation. Similarly, 74 (74.0%) respondents correctly identified that diabetic neuropathic ulcers commonly occur on weight-bearing areas of the foot, and 72 (72.0%) recognized neuropathy as a predominant factor responsible for diabetic ulcers.

Lower proportions of correct responses were observed for some preventive and management-related items. Specifically, 58 (58.0%) respondents correctly identified that mechanical off-loading should be advised to facilitate ulcer healing, while 57 (57.0%) correctly recognized that the risk of amputation increases when DFU is associated with limb ischemia. These items had correct response rates below 60%, corresponding to the “poor” range based on Bloom’s criteria.

Overall, the findings indicate variation in item-level knowledge across different aspects of DFU care, with higher correct response rates observed in some areas of wound assessment and management, and lower response rates in certain preventive and advanced management concepts (Table 2).

Attitude of nurses toward diabetic foot ulcer care

The attitudes of nurses toward diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care were assessed using seven Likert-scale statements. Mean scores were computed for each item using a five-point scale ranging from strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), with a decision mean of 3.0 used as the threshold for interpretation.

The results showed that respondents generally disagreed with most negative statements regarding DFU care. For instance, nurses disagreed that regular assessment of DFUs is unnecessary (mean = 2.49) and that DFU care is a low priority compared to other nursing care (mean = 2.52). Similarly, respondents disagreed with statements indicating that DFU care is too time-consuming (mean = 2.86) and that educating patients about DFU care is not their responsibility (mean = 2.30).

However, respondents agreed with the statement that DFU treatment is more important than prevention (mean = 3.33), suggesting a tendency to place greater emphasis on curative care than preventive strategies.

Overall, the grand mean attitude score was 2.73, which is below the decision mean of 3.00, indicating that nurses generally disagreed with negative attitude statements and therefore demonstrated a positive attitude toward diabetic foot ulcer care (Table 3).

Practices of nurses regarding diabetic foot ulcer care

The table presents respondents’ reported practices related to diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care. A large proportion of respondents indicated that they discuss diet with diabetic patients (85.0%) and educate patients on appropriate footwear and foot hygiene (81.0%). Regular inspection of patients’ feet was reported by 74.0% of respondents, while 70.0% indicated that they ask patients about foot problems at each visit.

More than two-thirds of respondents (69.0%) reported checking patients’ feet for deformities, calluses, or ulcers, and 75.0% indicated that they refer suspected DFU cases when identified. Slightly over half of the respondents (59.0%) reported assessing patients for loss of sensation, and 52.0% indicated that they document foot-care education provided to patients.

In contrast, only 10.0% of respondents reported using a monofilament or other screening tool for foot assessment, while the majority (90.0%) indicated that they do not routinely use such tools (Table 4).

Factors associated with knowledge of diabetic foot ulcer care (N = 100)

Table 5 presents the association between selected socio-demographic and professional characteristics and nurses’ knowledge of diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care.

There was no statistically significant association between gender and knowledge level (χ² = 1.284, p = 0.257). Both female and male nurses demonstrated similar distributions across the knowledge categories.

Academic qualification was significantly associated with knowledge of DFU care (χ² = 14.876, p = 0.002). Nurses with higher academic qualifications tend to have a greater proportion of respondents in the “good” knowledge category. In particular, a higher proportion of nurses with bachelor’s and master’s degrees demonstrated good knowledge compared with those with diploma-level qualifications and nurse assistants.

Years of professional practice was also significantly associated with knowledge level (χ² = 8.219, p = 0.016). Nurses with longer years of practice (≥ 10 years) had a higher proportion of respondents in the “good” knowledge category compared with those with fewer years of experience.

Similarly, prior training in diabetic foot ulcer care showed a strong and statistically significant association with knowledge (χ² = 12.604, p < 0.001). Nurses who had received DFU training were more likely to demonstrate good knowledge compared with those who had not received such training.

Overall, these findings indicate that academic qualification, years of professional experience, and prior DFU training were important factors associated with nurses’ knowledge of diabetic foot ulcer care.

Discussion

This study examined the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of nurses regarding diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care at Sunyani Teaching Hospital in Ghana. The findings indicate that although nurses generally demonstrated positive attitudes toward DFU care, knowledge levels varied across different domains of diabetic foot management, and many respondents had not received formal training in DFU prevention and management. In addition, academic qualification, years of professional experience, and prior DFU training were significantly associated with knowledge levels. These findings provide important insights into the preparedness of nurses to deliver effective DFU prevention and management in a tertiary healthcare setting in Ghana.

A key contribution of this study is the identification of variation in nurses’ knowledge across different domains of DFU care within a tertiary hospital context. While several items related to wound assessment and dressing selection recorded higher correct response rates, falling within the range corresponding to the “good” category based on Bloom’s cut-off criteria, other items, particularly those related to preventive strategies such as mechanical off-loading and ischemia-related amputation risk, recorded lower correct response rates, falling within the “poor” range. These findings indicate that, although overall knowledge levels may be categorized as good or moderate according to the applied classification, important gaps remain in specific areas of DFU prevention and advanced management. This pattern suggests that exposure to routine clinical practice may support knowledge in commonly encountered aspects of care, but may be less sufficient for reinforcing knowledge of guideline-based preventive strategies.

These findings are consistent with reports from other low- and middle-income countries where nurses are frequently involved in DFU care despite limited access to structured training. In many such settings, knowledge of diabetic foot management is often acquired through routine clinical exposure rather than through formal education or specialized wound-care programs [38]. In contrast, studies from high-income countries often report higher levels of DFU competency among nurses due to structured continuing professional development programs and institutionalized guideline-based training systems [39, 40]. This disparity likely reflects systemic factors, including limited training opportunities and the absence of standardized protocols, rather than a lack of professional motivation among nurses. In many healthcare environments, DFU-related learning occurs informally through ward routines or mentorship rather than through structured educational programs.

The pattern of knowledge observed in this study also reflects the influence of experiential learning in clinical practice. Although nurses in this tertiary referral hospital frequently encounter patients with advanced diabetic foot complications, knowledge of preventive management strategies such as off-loading and vascular risk assessment remained limited. Similar findings have been reported in studies conducted in Nigeria, South Africa, and Vietnam, where nurses demonstrated greater familiarity with infection management than with preventive concepts such as off-loading and vascular assessment [41, 42]. Conversely, knowledge related to wound-bed assessment and dressing selection appeared relatively stronger. This may reflect nurses’ routine involvement in wound management, where repeated exposure to infected or chronic wounds reinforces practical understanding of visible wound characteristics and commonly used dressings. Comparable patterns have been documented elsewhere, where nurses demonstrated stronger knowledge of observable wound features than of guideline-based preventive interventions [43]. These findings suggest that clinical exposure strengthens practical wound management skills but may not sufficiently reinforce preventive or guideline-driven concepts without formal training.

Despite variations in knowledge, respondents generally demonstrated positive attitudes toward DFU care, as measured through seven Likert-scale items assessing perceptions regarding the importance of DFU prevention, routine assessment, time constraints, and professional responsibility for patient education. Most nurses disagreed with statements suggesting that diabetic foot care is a low priority or outside the scope of nursing practice, indicating a strong sense of professional responsibility in the prevention and management of diabetic complications. This aligns with previous research showing that nurses often perceive DFU prevention and management as an essential component of their professional role, particularly in settings where they serve as primary caregivers for individuals with diabetes [44].

However, the results also revealed a tendency to prioritize treatment over prevention. More than half of the respondents considered DFU treatment more important than prevention, reflecting a curative orientation in clinical practice. This contrasts with evidence demonstrating that many DFUs can be prevented through early risk identification, routine foot examination, and patient education. Similar prevention–treatment imbalances have been reported in other low-resource settings, where systemic limitations and training gaps often reinforce reactive rather than preventive care approaches [45, 46].

Neutral responses to several attitude statements, particularly those related to time constraints and pain during wound care, also highlight contextual challenges affecting clinical practice. Nurses working in tertiary hospitals in Ghana frequently operate under conditions characterized by heavy workloads, staff shortages, and competing clinical demands. Such constraints may limit the ability to consistently implement preventive interventions despite positive professional attitudes. Comparable findings have been reported in Egypt and Iraq, where nurses expressed willingness to provide DFU care but cited time pressure and limited resources as barriers to implementation [47, 48]. The good internal consistency of the attitude scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.83) further strengthens confidence in these findings.

Reported clinical practices also reveal a noticeable knowledge-practice gap. Although many nurses reported performing basic preventive activities such as dietary counseling, routine foot inspection, and referral of suspected DFU cases, more technical and guideline-recommended procedures were less consistently implemented. In particular, the low use of standardized neuropathy screening tools and incomplete documentation of foot-care education are concerning. Similar studies have shown that nurses frequently perform basic preventive foot-care activities but demonstrate lower adherence to standardized screening and documentation procedures, largely due to limited training, absence of clear protocols, and high workload pressures [49, 50]. In many low-resource settings, essential screening tools such as monofilaments are not consistently available, leading nurses to rely on subjective clinical assessments. Documentation practices may also be affected by time constraints and the absence of structured DFU care pathways within healthcare facilities.

An important observation from this study is the strong desire among nurses for formal training in diabetic foot care. Many participants emphasized the need for structured education in foot care, indicating that the observed knowledge and practice gaps are more likely due to limited access to training rather than lack of interest. Similar findings have been reported in other African studies, where nurses consistently express a desire for formal DFU training to strengthen their competence and confidence in managing diabetic foot complications [51, 52]. These findings suggest that targeted educational interventions would likely be well received.

Consistent with this observation, nurses who had previously received DFU-related training demonstrated higher knowledge levels [53]. This supports evidence that structured education can improve competence in diabetic foot care. However, previous studies in low- and middle-income settings indicate that training programs are sometimes brief and largely theoretical, with limited practical components, which may reduce their impact on clinical practice [54, 55]. Training programs that incorporate practical skills, such as risk assessment, neuropathy screening, and off-loading techniques may therefore be more effective in improving knowledge and practice.

The association between years of clinical practice and DFU knowledge observed in this study also aligns with findings from other settings. Studies across several sub-Saharan African countries have shown that nurses with longer professional experience tend to demonstrate better understanding of DFU risk factors, complications, and preventive measures due to repeated exposure to diabetic foot cases in clinical practice [56, 57]. Similar associations have been reported in other settings, where cumulative clinical experience has been identified as an important predictor of DFU knowledge and management confidence [58]. The findings in this study therefore suggest that experiential learning plays a substantial role in reinforcing DFU knowledge over time.

Academic qualification was also significantly associated with knowledge level. Nurses with higher educational qualifications demonstrated greater knowledge of diabetic foot care, consistent with studies conducted in Tanzania and Uganda [59, 60]. Evidence from high-income countries similarly indicates that higher academic preparation enhances nurses’ ability to interpret clinical guidelines and apply evidence-based practices in DFU management [61]. This may be attributed to the emphasis placed on critical thinking, research appraisal, and guideline-based practice within higher education programs.

Overall, these findings suggest that while clinical experience and higher academic preparation contribute to improved knowledge of diabetic foot care, existing training opportunities remain insufficient to ensure consistent competence across all domains. Strengthening structured, competency-based DFU training programs that integrate practical skills, updated clinical guidelines, and continuous professional development may therefore be essential for improving nursing practice and promoting evidence-based diabetic foot care [27]. Strengthening the structure and content of DFU training may help ensure that formal education yields knowledge gains comparable to those achieved through prolonged clinical exposure and higher academic preparation.

From a practical perspective, these findings suggest that strengthening institutional training programs aligned with the IWGDF guidelines could help bridge the observed knowledge-practice gap. Enhancing nurses’ competencies in preventive screening, patient education, and guideline-based DFU management may improve early detection of high-risk patients and reduce preventable diabetic foot complications and amputations.

Finally, this study provides new evidence on nurses’ preparedness to manage diabetic foot ulcer care in a tertiary healthcare setting in Ghana. Future research should explore the effectiveness of targeted educational interventions and examine diabetic foot care practices across multiple healthcare institutions to improve the generalizability of findings and support the development of national training strategies for DFU prevention and management.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and reliance on self-administered questionnaires may have introduced recall, social desirability, and selection biases; particularly due to convenience sampling and online recruitment; which could overestimate knowledge and positive attitudes. Additionally, the single tertiary hospital setting restricts generalizability, and observed associations, especially regarding training, may reflect variability in prior training quality rather than true effects.

Recommendation

Based on the findings of this study, several recommendations are proposed to strengthen DFU prevention and management in clinical practice.

At the institutional level, hospital management and healthcare facilities should organize regular in-service training and continuing professional development (CPD) programs to enhance nurses’ competencies in diabetic foot care. Such training should incorporate practical components, including risk assessment, neuropathy screening using monofilaments, and appropriate off-loading techniques, to improve evidence-based clinical practice.

In addition, nursing training institutions and professional regulatory bodies, including the Nursing and Midwifery Council of Ghana, should strengthen the integration of diabetic foot care within nursing curricula and professional training programs to ensure that nurses acquire adequate knowledge and skills during their formal education.

Furthermore, hospital administrators and clinical leadership should develop and implement standardized clinical protocols for diabetic foot assessment and management, aligned with the IWGDF guidelines, to promote consistency in screening practices, patient education, and documentation of foot care interventions.

Finally, at the policy level, the Ministry of Health and Ghana Health Service should incorporate structured diabetic foot care training into national continuing professional development frameworks and diabetes management policies to strengthen preventive care and reduce DFU-related complications.

Conclusion

This study found that nurses at Sunyani Teaching Hospital demonstrated moderate knowledge and generally positive attitudes toward diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) care, while reported practices varied, particularly in neuropathy screening, wound assessment, and documentation. Formal DFU training was limited, despite many nurses having substantial clinical experience, and most expressed a need for additional education. Knowledge levels were associated with academic qualification and years of experience.

These findings highlight areas for potential improvement in training and support for DFU care. Further research is needed to explore barriers to evidence-based practice and to assess the impact of targeted educational interventions in similar settings.
