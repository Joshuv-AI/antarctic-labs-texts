Surgical margins in breast conserving surgery for ductal carcinoma in situ of the breast and clinical outcomes: a national audit with long term follow-up
Authors: John FR. Robertson, D Mark Sibbering, Sikhuphukile G. Ndebele Mahati, Ashu Gandhi, Sarah E. Pinder, Karen Clements, Olive Kearins, Judith Bliss, Lucy Kilburn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundOptimal surgical margin width in breast conserving surgery (BCS) for ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is not established. The United Kingdom (UK) Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) recommended a 1 mm margin, whereas a minimum of 2 mm has been recommended in the United States of America (USA). This paper uses precise histological margin width data from UK national datasets to understand the impact of surgical margins on time to recurrence (TTR).MethodsPatients were included if aged ≥45-years with a new diagnosis of DCIS alone, between 2003 and 2014, within the English National Health Service (NHS) Breast Screening Programme. Primary treatment included BCS and a minimum histological excision margin width recorded. Exclusion criteria included: prior history of DCIS; prior history of invasive cancer or its diagnosis within 3-months of initial surgical treatment for DCIS. Data was extracted from NHS England National Disease Registration Service (NDRS), ABS and Sloane Project audits.Findings16,907 patients diagnosed with DCIS having definitive BCS surgery were identified between 2003 and 2014. TTR was found to be significantly shorter for patients with surgical margins <1 mm vs ≥ 1 mm (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) = 1·32; 95% (confidence interval) CI:1·06–1·63; p = 0·012); <2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm (aHR = 1·19; 95% CI:1·05–1·35; p = 0·0062) and ≥1-<2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm (aHR = 1·18; 95% CI:1·01–1·38; p = 0·032). There was no evidence that increasing the surgical margin width beyond 2 mm significantly improved TTR (aHR = 0·96; 95% CI: 0·86–1·08; p = 0·52 for ≥5 mm vs ≥ 2–<5 mm). The rate of recurrence across 14 years following BCS + radiotherapy was 1·2% per annum, 2129 (13%) patients had a recurrence of which 78% were invasive breast cancers.InterpretationPatients with DCIS with histological margins of <2 mm, adjusted for other clinical factors, have significantly worse TTR compared to margins ≥2 mm. These findings may inform optimum treatment of patients with DCIS.FundingAn ABS grant covered the cost of data extraction by NHS England and medical writing assistance. The latter was provided by Edge Health, supervised by the co-authors.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched for the terms: “conserving surgery”, “DCIS”, and “surgical margin width” in PubMed and Google Scholar and found that most studies of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) recurrence report on pre-operative imaging, pathological margin status (but not margin width) or histological features of DCIS. Most of these features can be interrogated retrospectively but histological margin width of individual patients has to be assessed prospectively for it to be analysed. Few studies have looked at actual surgical margin width measurements reported by the histopathologist on the surgically excised specimen and those that do are usually single centre reports with relatively small number of patients and often short follow up. There are few large consecutive reports of breast conserving surgery margins and recurrence and, to our knowledge, no reports of results on surgical margin width for breast conserving surgery (BCS) for DCIS from a national breast screening programme.

Added value of this study

This large-scale (n = 16,907) consecutive, real-life series of pure DCIS diagnosed through the National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) in England has shown that patients having breast conserving surgery with histological margins of <2 mm, adjusted for other clinical factors, have significantly higher risk of recurrence compared to margins ≥2 mm. The annual rate of recurrence in the ipsilateral breast remains constant out to 14 years with no sign of dropping off and 78% of these recurrences were invasive cancers. Finally, having multiple operations to obtain clear margins was associated with an increased risk of recurrence.

Implications of all the available evidence

These findings are important to consider and discuss with patients selecting breast conserving surgery for DCIS. The Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) in the United Kingdom (UK) has already changed its guidance for surgical margin wide to ≥2 mm for BCS in DCIS based on this national dataset. Other countries may also use this data to confirm or change its guidance on surgical margin width.

Introduction

Breast screening programmes have increased the proportion of breast cancers diagnosed as pre-invasive ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS). The United Kingdom (UK) National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP), a national 3-yearly mammographic screening programme for asymptomatic women aged 50 to <71 years since 1993, has resulted in an increased DCIS detection, which now constitutes about one fifth of new screen-detected breast cancer diagnoses.1

Risk of progression of DCIS to invasive breast cancer is uncertain, but non-treatment of DCIS reportedly carries a 10-year cumulative risk of ipsilateral invasive carcinoma of 9% (95% confidence interval (CI) 4–21%), 39% (24–58%) and 36% (24–50%) for low, intermediate and high-grade DCIS respectively in one series,2 with another reporting a 10-year net risk of ipsilateral invasive breast cancer of 17·6% among patients with untreated high-grade DCIS.3 Consequently, most women with DCIS are treated with surgery, which may be breast conserving surgery (BCS) often followed by radiotherapy, or a mastectomy.

Mastectomy provides excellent long-term control, but at the cost of cosmesis and psychological morbidity. One series of 3121 patients treated by mastectomy for DCIS reported a cumulative 10-year local recurrence rate (LRR) of 1·4% with a 10-year distant disease rate of 0·8%.4 The 10-year breast cancer specific mortality was not recorded in that study but from the Surveillance, Epidemiology and End Results Programme (SEER) database was 1·3%, although it was acknowledged that patients undergoing mastectomy for DCIS had cancers with larger mean size and higher grade compared to those treated by BCS.5

Studies have reported that patients with clear margins after BCS for DCIS have lower rates of local recurrence than those with involved (“tumour on ink”) margins,1,6, 7, 8, 9 but these, to date, lack detail on the precise histological margin width achieved. This is important, given a key challenge in defining optimal BCS is the careful balance between multiple factors, including patient demographics, tumour characteristics (e.g. size and grade) and clinical status. Adequate tumour-free surgical margin width is, nevertheless, important to minimise risk of recurrence (local and distant), which could lead to increased breast cancer specific mortality, whilst at the same time also maximising cosmetic outcome.10

There has been limited real-world follow-up data to inform the optimal surgical margin width for BCS in treating DCIS. Despite NHSBSP guidelines for pathology specimen handling and reporting and mandatory participation in relevant quality assurance programmes, and although within the Sloane Project (a UK wide prospective audit of screen detected non-invasive neoplasia) a pathology protocol is applied,11 there are limitations in the available data regarding surgical margin width for BCS for DCIS. This has implications for national guidelines and there is also currently no international consensus.

A minimum margin width of 2 mm has been adopted in the United States of America (USA).12 This followed a collaboration between the Society of Surgical Oncology, the American Society of Clinical Oncology, and the American Society for Radiation Oncology, and was endorsed by the Board of Directors of the American Society of Breast Surgeons. This was informed by a meta-analysis using “threshold margins” (margins the surgical unit set as a minimum), rather than true histological margin measurements.6 The meta-analysis therefore did not use any actual histological assessed margins; as an example, if a unit had adopted a “threshold margin” of 2 mm and one patient had a histological margin with of 3 mm and a second patient had a histological margin width of 8 mm both were counted as 2 mm in the meta-analysis.

In the UK since 2015 the Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) has recommended a minimum 1 mm margin width,13 based on a consensus vote after presentation of the limited evidence available at that time. The National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE) guideline in 2015 recommended a minimum margin width of 2 mm, recognising that the available evidence at the time was of low quality and based on consensus view.14

The collection of detailed surgical margin width data for DCIS only became routine within NHSBSP after 2000 with increasing data quality allowing analysis of large-scale consecutive series of BCS for DCIS from 2003. Using retrospective histological data from the NHS England National Disease Registration Service (NDRS),15 NHSBSP, ABS screening audit16 and Sloane Project,11 we aimed to determine whether there is an association between histologically measured margin width in BCS and time to recurrence (TTR) and overall survival (OS) in patients diagnosed with DCIS from 2003 with a minimum of 5 years follow-up.

Methods

This was an audit of clinical outcomes of breast conserving surgery in patients with ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) detected through the National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP).

The data received from NHS England were a combination of NDRS cancer registration data and from Hospital Episode Statistics (HES), Systemic Anti-Cancer Treatment (SACT) and National Radiotherapy Dataset (RTDS), the Screening Quality Assurance Service (SQAS)/NHSBSP/ABS screening audit and the Sloane Project. There is some overlap between datasets; these were consolidated to enhance completeness of data items.

Inclusion criteria included patients with a new diagnosis of DCIS alone within the English NHSBSP between 1993 and 2018 inclusive, age ≥45 years, primary treatment included breast conserving surgery (BCS) and minimum histological excision margin width recorded. The majority of women are invited to the NHSBSP when they reach 50 years of age but since some have been recruited between 45 and 50 years a lower age of ≥45 years was set as cut-off. Exclusion criteria included invasive cancer diagnosed prior to, or within 3 months of, initial surgical treatment for DCIS, prior history of DCIS in either breast or the presence of microinvasion associated with the DCIS.

The study analysed data from 2003 to 2014, ensuring a minimum 5-year follow-up for patients diagnosed with DCIS and treated by BCS. Data outside this period was excluded due to either inconsistency (pre-2003) or insufficient follow-up time (post-2014).

The primary endpoint was Time to Recurrence (TTR), defined as time from DCIS diagnosis to loco-regional or distant recurrence as defined in the Supplementary Material. The number of distant recurrences as first event is low after BCS for DCIS but given the clinical significance of distant metastases it is important to include distant recurrences. Patients with a new contralateral breast cancer, new primary non-breast cancer or death prior to relapse were censored at this point. Patients alive and event-free were censored at the date last known alive.

Secondary endpoints were time to loco-regional recurrence (TTLR) defined as time from DCIS diagnosis to loco-regional recurrence. Patients with distant recurrence, new contralateral breast cancer, new primary non-breast cancer or death prior to relapse were censored at this point; overall survival (OS) defined as time from DCIS diagnosis to death. Patients alive and event-free were censored at the date last known alive; and breast cancer specific survival (BCSS) defined as time from DCIS diagnosis to breast cancer death. Patients alive and event-free were censored at the date last known alive. Patients who had a non-breast cancer death were censored at the date of death.

The methodology for identifying recurrences and categorising cause of death is included in the Supplementary Material.

Ethics

Access to the NHSBSP/Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) audit and Sloane Project data from external parties is governed by application to NHS England's Research Innovation and Development Advisory Committee (RIDAC) (previously Breast Screening Research Advisory Committee and Public Health England Office for Data Release (PHE ODR)). Access to NHS England National Disease Registration Service (NDRS) data is governed by application to the Data Access Request Service (DARS) (previously the PHE ODR). Data are only released to researchers under approval and in an anonymised or de-personalised format and under a data sharing agreement, with relevant ethics committee approval, if required. The data for this audit was released under approval by NHSBSP Research Advisory Committee of PHE ODR1819_162 and did not require approval by an Ethics Committee. No informed consent forms from individuals were required.

Statistics

The radial surgical margin width variable, as the primary clinical factor of interest, was investigated with different approaches; however, <1 mm vs ≥ 1 mm (cut-off for present UK guidelines), <2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm (cut-off for USA guidelines) and ≥1 but <2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm were pre-defined comparisons. Surgical margin width was a categorical, composite endpoint, as data were recorded either as actual margin width or as a category option within datasets. DCIS with 0 mm margin or categorised as “clear/not stated” were excluded from analyses.

Baseline characteristics, treatments and surgical margin width were analysed using descriptive statistics and changes over time were explored. Associations between key baseline characteristics and surgical margin width were compared using Kendall's tau and chi-square trend tests. Time changes were assessed using Mann Kendall test and other tabulations used the chi-square test.

To assess the association of surgical margin width with outcomes (TTR, TTLR, OS and BCSS), two methods were used: 1. Multivariable Cox regression modelling adjusting for age group, DCIS grade and size, oestrogen receptor (ER) status (positive, negative, unknown), number of BCSs/operations, receipt of radiotherapy (yes, unknown to have had radiotherapy), diagnosis year and cancer registration centre; and 2. as a sensitivity analysis, Inverse probability weighting (IPW) using M-estimation with propensity scores calculated for surgical margin width, to account for the non-randomised nature of the data. The same variables as for the adjusted Cox model were used to calculate the propensity scores. Hazard ratios (HR) were reported for each outcome (unadjusted HR, adjusted HR (aHR) for adjusted Cox models, and IPW HR for the second adjusted method), where HR > 1 favours larger surgical margin width. 10-year estimates of outcome were also reported as a clinically relevant timepoint. Disease outcome data were graphically represented using Kaplan Meier and adjusted survival plots. Analysis was conducted using Stata version 17·0.

This project used de-personalised data (in line with the Information Commissioner's Office) received from NDRS, and SQAS. NDRS data were linked within NHS England (previously Public Health England) to the NHSBSP/ABS audit and Sloane Project screening datasets via an internal Data Sharing Agreement between NDRS and SQAS in NHS England. Details of data access and storage are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Impact of surgical margins on “time to recurrence” (TTR) and time to loco-regional recurrence (TTLR)

Median follow-up time for the cohort was 8·8 years (interquartile range (IQR): 6·6–11·8). In this period, 2129 (13%) patients had a recurrence. There were 1664 (78%) invasive recurrences, of which 1585 (95%) were loco-regional recurrences and 79 (5%) distant recurrences. There were 465 (22%) patients representing with DCIS alone. Therefore, there were 79 distant recurrences out of 2129 (3·7%) total recurrences. The number and proportion of patients by type of recurrence (i.e. DCIS or invasive) was analysed vs age group bands: this was on the basis that if patients diagnosed between 65 and 70 years had fewer subsequent years of mammographic surveillance (as no longer routinely invited for screening in the United Kingdom (UK) National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) after they reached 70 years) then more of their recurrences may have been clinically palpable invasive cancers. However, the data showed no evidence of a significant difference (Table S1 - Supplementary Material).

For the unadjusted estimates, as well as after multivariable adjustment and using inverse probability weighting (IPW), a significantly shorter TTR was observed for patients with surgical margins<1 mm vs ≥ 1 mm, <2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm, ≥1-<2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm (Table 2, Fig. 2A–C and S4A–C - Supplementary Material). Models also showed that risk of recurrence increased as the number of BCSs increased (aHR = 1·25; 95% CI: 1·09–1·43: p = 0·0014 for 2 vs 1 and aHR = 1·97; 95% CI: 1·30–2·99: p = 0·0013 for 3+ vs 1).

The annual event rate of recurrence over 14 years of follow-up was relatively consistent 1·2% pa for margins ≥2 mm versus 1·8% pa for margins <2 mm. For DCIS only recurrences, the average annual event rate was 0·3% pa compared to 1·1% pa for invasive recurrences.

Impact of surgical margins on overall survival (OS)

In total, 1534/16,907 (9%) patients had died. Of these 1534 patients, 162 (11%) died from breast cancer, 599 (39%) from other cancers, 0 (0%) from unknown cancer and 773 (50%) died from other causes. OS was reduced for patients with surgical margins <2 mm and ≥1 mm - <2 mm vs ≥ 2 mm (Table 2 and Figures S8 and S9 - Supplementary Material). Breast cancer specific survival also showed a reduction for those with surgical margins ≥2 mm, though the number of events were small (Table 2 and Figures S10 and 11 - Supplementary Material). OS and Breast Cancer Specific Survival were also analysed for <1 mm vs ≥ 1 mm and the differences were not statistically significant as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

Previous guidance from the Association of Breast Surgery (ABS) recommended that surgical margins for ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) in breast conserving surgery (BCS) should be minimum of 1 mm. We report that patients with DCIS with histological margins of <2 mm, adjusted for other clinical factors, have significantly worse time to recurrence (TTR) and time to loco-regional recurrence (TTLR) rates compared to patients with margins ≥2 mm. Histological margins of <2 mm, adjusted for other clinical factors, had significantly worse overall survival (OS) rates compared to patients with margins ≥2 mm. While 773/1534 (50%) of the deaths were due to cancer, only one in five of the cancer deaths were reported to be breast cancer specific, while four in five were recorded as due to “other cancers”. There is at present no certainty what those ‘other cancers’ were and so the OS data raise a flag for future audits to try and address. Reassuringly, there is no evidence that surgical margins greater than 2 mm improve recurrence rates or OS further and so the 2 mm margin width supported by this dataset, accepted by the ABS and current standard in the United States of America (USA) and a number of other countries, means that ≥2 mm margin width can be accepted as the standard of care pending future substantive data to the contrary.

Several non-randomised trials have reported that wider margins after surgery for DCIS have lower rates of local recurrence than involved margins.17 The Sloane Project18 reported a significantly lower rate of recurrence for lesions with ≥2 mm clear margin (p = 0·01). Further analyses showed that the protective effect of wider margin width was predominantly on ipsilateral invasive, rather than DCIS recurrence, seen both in patients who received or did not receive radiotherapy (RT) (p = 0·03 and p = 0·04, respectively).

Given the significant findings of the present paper, the first national report of actual histological margin width for DCIS with long-term patient follow-up, we proposed that the ABS recommendation should be changed to an optimum width of ≥2 mm clear margins, to minimise recurrence rates and maximise OS. ABS has agreed and recently changed its guidance to a margin of ≥2 mm for BCS for DCIS.19

Other findings in this audit add to the literature on the optimal treatment of patients with DCIS. We have found that an increasing number of BCS excisions is significantly associated with local recurrence. This hazard increases by 30% for one additional BCS to achieve complete excision of DCIS, and by 100% for >2 procedures. There is no definitive explanation for this, but possibilities include: i) the re-excision specimen does not precisely correspond to the involved aspect of the first BCS specimen; or ii) there is more extensive disease than predicted by pre-treatment mammography (e.g. non-calcifying DCIS). Given this risk of re-excision has been identified, it is important patients are informed and given the opportunity to consider whether mastectomy is the better treatment option for them.

The annual ABS National Health Service Breast Screening Programme (NHSBSP) audit report suggests that the size of DCIS being treated with BCS in the UK is increasing.20 During the period of data presented in this study (2003–2014), the proportion of DCIS lesions treated by BCS and >40 mm histologically was 3·6%. This proportion had increased to 9.4% of all tumours by 2021/22 (Table S4 - Supplementary Material – ABS Communication). Modern oncoplastic practice facilitates excision of larger DCIS lesions if a patient does not wish to have a mastectomy. This trend for treating larger DCIS lesions with BCS is also important when assessing the impact of RT after BCS for DCIS. A recent paper reports that women who underwent BCS with or without RT had significantly higher risk of ipsilateral DCIS if they had a larger DCIS lesion (20–49 mm) compared with DCIS <20 mm.17 This was seen after adjusting for age, DCIS grade, margin status, and treatment. In another contemporaneous publication which analysed 2049 women treated with BCS, of which 1073 were with RT, after a median follow-up of 14 years, the authors reported that BCS and RT in women with large DCIS size (>40 mm), ensuring negative margins and the addition of RT boost that high local recurrence free survival (LRFS) and invasive LRFS could be achieved.21 However, it should be noted that the hazard ratio (HR) rose numerically across all the tumour size subgroups receiving RT. Both the 11–25 mm and 26–39 mm groups were statistically significant and the ≥40 mm is borderline significant (p = 0·06) with only 42 patients in the group. Given the small number of patients with DCIS ≥40 mm in size, more data are needed regarding larger lesions treated by BCS oncoplastic techniques to evaluate whether there is a significantly greater risk of recurrence.

In terms of the relationship between use of RT and surgical margin width, Tadros et al. reported from a single centre that their data suggested that if RT was given that there was then no relationship between margin width and local recurrence. This is not the case with the NHSBSP national data. Furthermore, the study meta-analysis supports a minimum margin width of ≥2 mm for patients having breast RT.6 Thompson and colleagues in their publication reported that breast radiotherapy (RT) after BCS was associated with reduction in ipsilateral recurrent DCIS or invasive breast cancer which was “independent of the excision margin width or size of DCIS.”.22

9145/16,907 (54%) of percentage of patients in the current audit had high grade DCIS which is greater compared to other publications.5,23 The United Kingdom (UK) NHSBSP runs an External Quality Assessment (EQA) scheme which is mandatory for all professional groups. The breast screening pathologists have consistently reported a high proportion of high-grade DCIS (e.g. UK DCIS 1 trial, UK NHS screening data, Sloane Project) with no major change and no variation across the years.24 As such it appears there may be a difference from USA and Europe, although other possible reasons include radiologists recall, differences between UK and other countries in terms of different screening programmes (e.g. age start and frequency) or pathologists’ grading. Previously, the Early Breast Cancer Clinical Trialists Group (EBCCTG) individual patient data meta-analysis of the randomised clinical trials of BCS ± RT for DCIS, reported that RT and margin status (involved vs negative) were both significantly associated with the 10-year risk of ipsilateral breast events but had no significant effect on breast cancer specific mortality, mortality from other causes or on all-cause mortality.23 Our audit results regarding RT reducing recurrence are consistent with the EBCTCG data although we note that in the UK RT is not invariably given to patients with even high-grade DCIS.18 We report here a relatively consistent annual event rate at 1·2% per annum out to 14 years, without plateau, but longer follow-up of the more detailed dataset presented in the present paper is required to assess if, and when, there is a plateau in the recurrence risk.

Invasive recurrence carries significant impact even if a patient does not subsequently die of breast cancer – including that most of such patients receive systemic therapies (e.g. chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, immunotherapy &/or bisphosphonate therapy) depending on their prognosis and type of breast cancer. Furthermore, there is psychological impact arising from the uncertainty whether they will in the future develop metastatic disease.

There are a number of key strengths of the analysis presented; this uses real-world, consecutive, national data with measured surgical margin width as collected as part of a national programme, with stringent quality and assurance and long follow-up.

The annual recurrence data are consistent out to 14 years. The data also provides insight regarding type of recurrence following BCS for DCIS. Many previous publications have reported an equal split (50:50) in the proportion of DCIS to invasive recurrences.18 We have found, with long follow-up, that after BCS the proportion of recurrence as invasive disease is 1664/2129 (78%), compared to 465/2129 (22%) of DCIS alone. The Sloane Project also showed that the majority of recurrences following surgery for DCIS were invasive (∼66%) so the data reported in this manuscript are similar.18 Since patients stop being invited for the NHSBSP after reaching 70 years of age, we examined the number and proportion of types of recurrence versus age group bands; the data showed no difference by age group (Supplementary Table S1A-B) and supports that the finding of ∼80% invasive breast cancers is not due to women above 70 years presenting with more invasive cancers than younger age cohorts.

Narod and colleagues did report a higher risk of recurrence in younger age women.5 In their publication they included patients <40 years of age in whom DCIS is uncommon and which in their study accounted for 4·9% of patients. Our audit did not include women <45 years and so it is not possible to compare a similar age group within the NHSBSP but we would note that it is not clear in the Narod publication if the younger women presented clinically with a lump, which other studies have reported carries an increased risk of recurrence, or were undergoing mammographic screening because they were at increased risk of recurrence (e.g. mutation status, family history of bilateral breast cancer). Also, Narod and colleagues appear to have applied adjusted Cox Proportional Hazard models as their only analysis method, but not looked at propensity scoring which would have been statistically appropriate given the non-randomised nature of their data.

Weaknesses of this analysis includes the use of routine data to address questions on surgical margins. These mainly pertain to the type and sources of data currently available. There is no imaging data available on the size of the DCIS. Adequate imaging assessment for DCIS patients before BCS is clearly important, as well as a multidisciplinary discussion of every single case. Historically, cancer registries did not collect data on ipsilateral DCIS recurrences as they were not classed as ‘new’ tumours, whereas invasive recurrences were. However, the data search for this report included any record of subsequent surgery on the ipsilateral breast 6 months or more after the initial definitive BCS and the histology associated with that surgery. Since recurrent DCIS is almost always treated surgically this should have identified virtually all cases of recurrent DCIS. Invasive recurrences have been reported inconsistently as either recurrence or new diagnoses within the cancer registry, but we also identified, via Hospital Episode Statistics (HES), any recurrence following an original DCIS diagnosis; for this analysis any breast surgery 6 months or more after the initial diagnosis and treatment of DCIS where the histology was reported an invasive cancer have been included as an invasive recurrence. We used the Sloane data and the ABS audit to fill in a relatively small number of the gaps in the data from the Cancer Registry. This could contain a natural selection bias but is a pragmatic approach to try to obtain as much data as possible and we tried to account for biases by the methods we used, e.g. propensity scoring.

The length of follow-up while out to 14 years (median 8·8 years) is a limitation in assessing the long-term risk of recurrence, even though this audit is the longest follow-up reported for a consecutive national breast screening programme based on actual surgical margins: longer follow-up will be available and should be a requirement of the NHSBSP in the future.

Whilst results for overall survival (OS) by margin width for DCIS were statistically significant, it cannot be ruled out that this analysis may be confounded. The majority of deaths were identified as ‘non-breast cancer’; for those known to have died from breast cancer, the breast cancer specific deaths were not consistently associated with margin width. Conversely, inconsistent coding within death certificates may also have underestimated the number of deaths impacted by a DCIS diagnosis with subsequent recurrence. Further follow-up is required, along with enlarging the audit dataset to see if OS differences according to margin width can be established. Perhaps the first step should be further follow-up to see if the significant differences between <2 mm versus ≥2 mm can be confirmed.

It is challenging to interpret some key data, due to the method of reporting. For example, RT treatment was either clearly recorded as ‘given’ or the records were ‘silent’, so RT was necessarily classified as unknown in 6810/16,907 (40%) of patients. This may have under-estimated the effect of RT since some of the ‘RT unknown’ group might have received RT. The paucity of data on oestrogen receptor (ER) status is because this has never been within the UK Minimum Pathology Dataset for DCIS and is not routinely assessed in the UK. Indeed, endocrine therapy (ET) is rarely prescribed in the UK for patients with DCIS, possibly because the UK/ANZ randomised trial did not show a significant effect of tamoxifen in the presence of receipt of RT.25 The use of ET for DCIS, particularly in conjunction with RT, is a further area for future audit and research. There is no imaging data available on the size of the DCIS in this audit and we believe that adequate imaging assessment for DCIS patients before BCS is clearly important, as well as a multidisciplinary discussion of every single case.

This audit has identified a number of risk factors for patients diagnosed with DCIS. Patients undergoing BCS with histological margins of <2 mm, adjusted for other clinical factors, have significantly worse time to recurrence (TTR) compared to margins ≥2 mm. The risk of recurrence also increases as the number of BCSs/operations to obtain adequate margins increases from one through to ≥3 operations. The rate of recurrence across 14 years runs at 1·2% per annum without any evidence thus far of plateau. The audit also reports that ∼80% of recurrences were invasive breast cancers which carry a risk to those patients of developing overt metastatic disease at some future timepoint. All of these risks should be explained when discussing surgical options with patients.
