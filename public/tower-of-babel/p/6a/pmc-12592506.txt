A gender comparison based network analysis for social media addiction behaviors in cross-cultural contexts
Authors: Linying Li, Xiaoxiao Yuan, Luzi Lin, Antong Xiao, Yi Chen, Yu Xia, Xiang Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Social media addiction (SMA) has become a prevalent behavioral addiction among university students worldwide, with acculturation stress and social identity threats amplifying this phenomenon among international students. Prior research has focused on causal factors influencing the SMA, such as social norms and cognitive‒behavioral mechanisms, while overlooking the core features and gender differences in SMA manifestations. This gap hinders international students’ recognition of their behavioral pathways and limits educational policymakers’ ability to provide effective support. This study aims to elucidate the gender-mirror core features and differences in the SMA among international students via network analysis, integrating the gender role expectations theory and the cognitive-behavioral model of problematic internet use (PIU) to explain divergent behavioral pathways. The findings reveal that males exhibit an agentic orientation (independence–competition–achievement), leveraging social media for information acquisition and social identity maintenance, with greater self-recognition potentially indicating conscious indulgence. Conversely, females display a communal orientation (caring–cooperation–emotional expression), relying on platforms for emotional network maintenance, with usage concealment likely stemming from low recognition of addictive behaviors. These findings inform tailored interventions—such as time-management training for males and emotional regulation strategies for females—to increase resilience, social integration, and academic success among international students.

Introduction

Background

Social media platforms are essential tools for international students to establish and maintain social connections in a cross-cultural environment. They can enhance their sense of belonging and happiness, promoting mental health1,2. However, excessive use of social media may lead to addictive behavior. Since 2018, Thailand’s educational environment has undergone significant changes, with international academic reforms and stricter regulations impacting students’ learning and living habits3. A study by Waters and Day (2022) on international students in Thailand revealed that Chinese students at University A accounted for as much as 10% of the student body, and even higher proportions of international students may be found in southern Thailand4.

Additionally, nonnationals constitute one quarter of the academic staff at Thai higher education institutions. In 1999, there were only 1,882 international students in Thailand, which increased to 20,309 by 20125. Concurrently, the widespread use of internet platforms has led to new issues. By 2021, student-led protests, influenced by free-thinking disseminated through the internet, had catalyzed significant social changes4. Chinese international students, in particular, must navigate an educational environment where students of diverse nationalities are brought together for study and daily life4. This group commonly faces the dual challenges of cultural adaptation stress and the reconstruction of social networks6.

International students face numerous challenges in cross-cultural contexts due to language barriers, cultural differences, and feelings of loneliness7,8. Establishing effective social networks can alleviate these pressures and promote mental health9. Social media is a convenient social tool that helps international students overcome geographical limitations; stay in touch with family, friends, and new acquaintances; gain social and emotional support; and promote mental health10,11. However, overreliance on social media may lead to social media addiction, which in turn may exacerbate loneliness and anxiety while harming the academic performance and physical and mental health of international students12.

Theoretical foundation

The study is grounded in gender role expectations theory13,14 and the cognitive‒behavioral model of problematic internet use (PIU)15,16 (Fig. 1), which uses acculturation stress and social identity threat as situational triggers and amplifiers to elucidate the differential manifestations of social media addiction across gender and cross-cultural contexts. While prior research on problematic social media use has often focused on measurement tools and scale development17–21, this study emphasizes the construction of an explanatory theoretical framework to uncover the cognitive appraisals and situational mechanisms driving these behaviors.

Gender role expectations theory suggests that sociocultural norms influence males and females differently in terms of emotional expression and identity attachment13,14. When social expectations focus on autonomy and achievement, individuals tend to adopt instrumental, goal-focused online behaviors (male tendency). In contrast, when expectations emphasize relationship maintenance and emotional expression, individuals are more likely to engage in online interactions aimed at emotional regulation and connection (female tendency). These gender-related motivational patterns not only affect preferred online activities and their intensity but also shape how individuals interpret situations and cope with disconnection or threats, creating distinct behavioral pathways at the microlevel13,22.

The cognitive‒behavioral model of problematic internet use (PIU) proposes that problematic internet behavior develops from the interaction of situational cues, psychosocial stressors, and maladaptive cognitive‒behavioral cycles15,16. Negative conditions, such as loneliness, stress from acculturation, academic demands, or social difficulties, lead individuals to seek comfort or information through online interactions. When people rely heavily on online feedback, use digital interactions as main emotional regulation tools, or base their self-worth on online performance, immediate positive responses reinforce these coping mechanisms, potentially fostering problematic behaviors23. The cognitive‒behavioral pathway offers a framework for identifying specific intervention points, including cognitive restructuring and emotional regulation skills training24,25.

By integrating gender role expectations theory and the cognitive‒behavioral model of problematic internet use (PIU), a gender-sensitive process model can be formulated. This model posits that gender role expectations shape the online strategies prioritized by individuals under similar triggering conditions, such as acculturation stress, with males favoring instrumental information-seeking and females prioritizing emotional and relational repair26. The cognitive‒behavioral mechanism elucidates how these strategies are sustained or exacerbated through specific cognitive appraisals, such as anxiety over information loss, perceived threats of relational disconnection, or excessive emphasis on online feedback coupled with reinforcement cycles24. For example, males socialized toward achievement orientation may perceive social media as a tool for maintaining status and accessing resources in contexts of information uncertainty or competition27,28. Their cognitive focus centers on concerns about missing information or status risks, leading to heightened monitoring and repetitive behaviors aimed at restoring informational advantage29–32. Conversely, females socialized toward a relationship orientation tend to use social media to fulfill emotional support and relational connection needs22; when perceiving relational threats or exclusions, they exhibit strong repair motives and repetitive interaction behaviors30.

Within this integrated framework, acculturation stress serves as both a trigger and an amplifier: international students facing language barriers, a lack of social support, or academic pressure are more likely to rely on online channels for information or emotional support, thereby amplifying the influence of gendered motivations on usage behaviors33. Furthermore, social identity threat explains why individuals exhibit intense repair or relapse behaviors when perceiving disconnection or negative evaluation: when online interactions are deemed critical for maintaining significant identities, any threat triggers corresponding repair motives34,35.

Network analysis

Network analysis is a visual model in which nodes represent observed psychological variables, usually psychometric test items, and these nodes are connected by edges representing certain statistical relationships between them. Using network comparison tests, researchers can explore structural differences between subgroups36. Higher global strength may indicate the presence of feedback loops among features, whereas differences in edge strength can aid in analyzing the interactions between various features37.

This study employs network analysis to explore the core behaviors of social media addiction (SMA) among college students and their gender differences. This approach enables an explicit exploration of potential relationships between symptoms and the identification of the network’s most central or influential factors. In addition, the core features and differences in the unique social media addiction behavior patterns of different gender groups were visually identified through the form of a network structure diagram.

Results

Network Estimation

This study estimated two networks by bootstrapping (nBoots = 1,000) resampling techniques, each comprising 20 nodes and 190 potential edges (n*(n − 1)/2, where n is the number of items in the SMA scale). In the male network, there were 104 nonzero edges, accounting for approximately 54.74% of the total 190 edges, with an average edge weight of 0.047, indicating a denser structure with moderate relational strength. In contrast, the female network contained 94 nonzero edges, representing approximately 49.47% of the total edges, with a slightly higher average edge weight of 0.048. This suggests that the female network has fewer connections but marginally stronger edge strength (Fig. 2). Overall, the male and female networks exhibit subtle differences in edge count and average weight, with the male network being denser and the female network showing greater intensity in its relationships.

Male network Estimation

On the basis of the male network (Fig. 3A), S-T1 (Over the past year of studying abroad, I have significantly increased my time on social media), S-T2 (I find myself increasingly reliant on social media while studying abroad), and S-R1 (I wish to cut down on social media use while studying abroad, but I always fail) form a cluster, indicating that the three nodes are highly correlated. This means that male international students may gradually increase their social media use time (S-T1), develop a strong sense of dependence (S-T2), and repeatedly fail when trying to reduce their use (S-R1). These three issues collectively point to a dynamic process in which social media evolves from a coping tool to an indispensable dependency, and males find it difficult to break this pattern through self-regulation. S-T2 is a key node in this cycle, reflecting that excessive social media use by men in cross-cultural environments may be an important way to relieve stress.

Furthermore, the strong correlations between males’ S-C4 (I am confident that my daily activities, such as work, education, and household chores, have not suffered because of social media while studying abroad) and S-S2 (When not on social media, I often think about my next session) nodes indicate that male international students exhibit low recognition of the impact of social media on their social media addiction, revealing a cognitive‒behavioral disconnect: men believe that social media has no impact on their lives (S-C4), yet their minds are constantly preoccupied with it (S-S2). The algorithmic design of social media may further exacerbate this psychological preoccupation, increasing the vulnerability of males to falling into a cycle of social media addiction under the pressure of studying abroad.

Female network Estimation

As shown in the female network diagram (Fig. 3B), females form a cluster at nodes S-C4 (I am confident that my daily activities, such as work, education, and household chores, have not suffered because of social media while studying abroad), S-S2 (When not on social media, I often think about my next session), and S-W2 (I feel down when I cannot access social media). These three nodes are strongly correlated, indicating that female international students exhibit low recognition of the impact of social media, significant psychological salience, and emotional withdrawal symptoms in social media addiction. These features suggest that under the pressure of studying abroad, they may underestimate the impact of social media on their lives and exhibit strong addiction tendencies due to emotional dependence. In particular, S-W2 illustrates the emotional dependence and withdrawal features of females’ social media addiction, highlighting females’ need for emotional connection with social media.

Furthermore, females exhibited a unique correlation between nodes S-C2 (I have lied to my family about how much time I spend on social media while studying abroad) and S-W1 (I become irritable when not using social media), suggesting that female international students exhibit a specific behavioral-emotional pattern in social media addiction: females overuse social media because it satisfies their need for emotional connection but conceal their use to avoid social criticism. Their withdrawal symptoms when they are unable to use social media further highlight the intensity of this dependence. This combination of concealment and emotional withdrawal increases the complexity of addiction, suggesting that individuals may find it emotionally difficult to disconnect from social media. Their strong emotional dependence on social media may lead them to conceal their use in the face of social pressure while also exhibiting significant emotional distress when they are unable to use it.

Edge invariance test

At the global level, the network topologies of the two groups were virtually indistinguishable. The edge invariance test yielded M = 0.20, p = 0.82, failing to reject the null hypothesis of an invariant network structure. Similarly, the global strength test revealed only a trivial difference between males (8.99) and females (9.03), ΔS = 0.04, p = 0.86. These results indicate no statistically significant differences in either the overall pattern of intersymptom associations or the aggregate severity of social-networking addiction between genders.

Edge-level contrasts, however, revealed four connections in which male networks presented significantly stronger edge weights than female networks did. The most pronounced discrepancy emerged between S-C5 (I believe my use of social media is harming important areas of my life while studying abroad) and S-R1 (I wish to cut down on social media use while studying abroad, but I always fail), p = 0.001. Subsequent significant edges were observed between S-C2 (I’ve lied to my family about how much time I spend on social media while studying abroad) and S-S1 (I often sacrifice sleep due to prolonged use of social media), p = 0.025; between S-C3 (I believe my use of social media has affected my relationships while studying abroad) and S-M2 (In the context of my study abroad experience, I use social media to escape from unpleasant feelings), p = 0.034; and between S-C1 (My interest in other hobbies has waned because of my engagement with social media while studying abroad) and S-M3 (While studying abroad, I use social media to forget about my worries), p = 0.045. All results were statistically significant at the 95% confidence interval. All remaining edges showed no significant between-group differences.

Although male and female networks are globally similar, the male network displays tighter coupling within four symptom pairs: conflict-relapse, concealment–salience, interpersonal impairment–mood modification, and diminished interest–mood modification. These localized differences suggest that, for males, a more potent reinforcing cycle interconnects (a) perceived life impairment with repeated relapse, (b) deceptive concealment with physiological costs (e.g., sleep loss), (c) deteriorating relationships with emotion-driven usage, and (d) erosion of alternative interests with worry-alleviating engagement. These pathways merit targeted attention in future prevention and intervention efforts.

Male network centrality

In the male network, betweenness centrality registered a mean of 0.362 (range: 0.285–0.439) (Fig. 5A), denoting a moderate capacity to mediate between otherwise disconnected symptom clusters. This finding suggests that, within the male addiction network, the pathways linking conflict, relapse, and mood-modification symptoms are channeled through a limited set of pivotal nodes-effectively functioning as bridges that maintain the overall structural integrity of the network.

The closeness centrality averaged 0.439 (range: 0.362–0.516), indicating short geodesic distances between any two symptoms. The resulting high efficiency in symptom-to-symptom transmission implies that once a maladaptive behavioral cue (e.g., sleep loss or concealment) is activated, it can rapidly propagate throughout the male network, precipitating a swift and coherent escalation of addictive behaviors.

Strength centrality peaked at 0.671 (range: 0.593–0.748), reflecting robust and stable direct associations among individual symptoms. This pronounced intensity of first-order connections underscores the presence of tightly interlocked symptom pairs—such as relapse and perceived life impairment—thereby endowing the male network with a resilient core that resists fragmentation under perturbation.

Female network centrality

Conversely, the female network exhibited a markedly different centrality profile. Betweenness centrality reached 0.438 (range: 0.361–0.515) (Fig. 5B), surpassing male levels and signifying a more evenly distributed brokerage capacity. Consequently, influence within the female network is not confined to a few central symptoms; rather, multiple nodes share the responsibility of linking disparate symptom communities, yielding a decentralized yet cohesive configuration.

Closeness centrality reflects the male value at 0.438 (range: 0.361–0.515), sustaining equivalently short path lengths. This structural equivalence ensures that emotional distress or behavioral triggers can traverse the female network with comparable rapidity, facilitating rapid access to supportive or compensatory symptoms such as mood modification or interpersonal reassurance.

Most strikingly, strength centrality attained a value of 0.749 (range: 0.672–0.999), with heightened variability. The elevated mean indicates denser and more frequent direct ties among symptoms, whereas the extended upper bound reflects pronounced fluctuations in connection intensity. Together, these features depict a female network characterized by intense, emotionally laden symptom interactions that collectively forge a highly cohesive and adaptable system capable of amplifying both risk and resilience.

Conclusion

Core features

S-C5 (I think that my use of social media is harming important areas of my life while studying abroad.) (Fig. 4) emerged as the single most influential central node for both the male and female networks. Its shared prominence underscores a universal experience among international students; the perception of life-domain impairment functions as the core catalyst that organizes and amplifies all other addiction symptoms. Regardless of gender, once students recognize that academic performance, sleep, finances, or offline relationships are deteriorating because of their social media use, this recognition becomes the gravitational center around which salience, relapse, mood modification, and conflict revolve. In other words, the cross-cultural stressors of language barriers, loneliness, and identity negotiation converge on this single symptom, making perceived life harm the linchpin that drives the entire SMA network in both genders.

Differences between males and females

Agentic orientation: independence–competition–achievement

Diverging slightly from the expectation that male international students overlook social media’s impact on time management and attention due to information seeking, network analysis reveals that this is associated not only with information acquisition but also with heightened self-recognition. Socialized for independence, competition, and achievement, male international students perceive social media as a utilitarian tool for accessing information, entertainment, and skill enhancement rather than a venue for emotional attachment. Their platform engagement is characterized by goal-oriented information scanning23, yielding immediate gratification and measurable progress38,39. Notably, males exhibit recognition of their addictive behaviors in social media use; however, driven by information seeking and confidence in self-regulation, they may consciously persist in patterns conducive to social media addiction40,41, amplifying tolerance. Thus, the relationship between males and social media reflects an agentic orientation characterized by a cognitive pattern rooted in independence, competition, and achievement.

Communal orientation: caring–cooperation–emotional expression

The analysis unexpectedly revealed that hiding social media usage emerges as a significant feature among female international students. This may stem from their use of social media under acculturation stress as a tool for sustaining long-distance intimacy and mutual support through instant messaging, group chats, and emotional disclosure, compensating for deficiencies in offline social networks. In contrast to males, this emotionally driven usage pattern leads females to underestimate their dependency, resulting in a prevalent tendency to conceal actual usage levels, reflecting both insufficient recognition of addictive behaviors and a propensity for concealment42,43. This emotionally charged engagement enhances the platform’s mood regulation function, strengthening the interconnections among mood changes, conflict, and withdrawal symptoms. This indicates an escalating dependence on digital emotional feedback and excessive engagement driven by relationship maintenance anxiety. Consequently, the relationship between females and social media reflects a communal orientation characterized by a cognitive pattern rooted in caring, cooperation, and emotional expression.

Discussion

Social media serves as a digital platform where gender-specific socialization patterns are enacted and reinforced44,45. Male international students, driven by agentic orientations rooted in independence, competition, and achievement, utilize social media as a tool for strategic information seeking, skill enhancement, and status-oriented self-presentation. Conversely, female international students, shaped by communal expectations emphasizing caring, cooperation, and emotional expression, transform social media into a relational stage for sustaining long-distance intimacy and mutual support through instant messaging, group chats, and emotional disclosure. These gender differences offer critical insights for supporting international students in managing social media addiction (SMA).

For male students, interventions should focus on fostering recognition of social media’s impact on time management and academic performance, addressing their overconfidence in self-regulation46,47. Structured time-management workshops and digital literacy programs can help males recognize and mitigate conflict arising from excessive use, whereas goal-setting tools can redirect their agentic drive toward productive offline activities, reducing tolerance and relapse risks. For female students, interventions should target emotional regulation and relational stress, offering alternative avenues for social connection and reducing dependence on digital platforms for emotional support. Recognizing the presence and behavioral features of SMAs can enhance resilience and social integration skills among international students during their study abroad experience, ultimately fostering academic success and psychological well-being.

The findings show that social media addiction patterns differ between male and female international students. First, educators should include topics like emotion regulation, time management, and sleep hygiene in digital well-being workshops, allowing participants to choose between single-gender or mixed-gender groups without labels. Second, education policymakers should integrate these topics into existing credit courses, such as soft skills training, while continuously monitoring students’ emotion regulation, time management, and sleep hygiene to improve course design. Finally, platform governance teams should introduce a ‘well-being mode’ for student accounts verified via university email. This mode would hide public like counts, switch to a grayscale interface at night, and provide easy access to personal screen time and sleep tips. Aggregated data from this mode can be shared with partner universities to improve interventions while maintaining privacy.

Limitations and implications

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although validated scales were used to measure social media addiction (SMA), the reliability of the findings could be improved by developing a more objective assessment method. For example, incorporating behavioral data such as mobile usage logs or app monitoring records may offer a more accurate evaluation of SMA. Second, the sample was limited to Chinese international students in Thailand, which reduces the ability to apply the results broadly. Future research should include other regions and cultural backgrounds to gather more comprehensive evidence that can better inform youth well-being overall. Third, while this study identified gender differences in SMA—where males tend to display more conscious addictive behaviors, whereas females show more unconscious patterns—the design of intervention strategies tailored to these differences remains inadequate.

Given these limitations, several directions for future research can be proposed. Researchers should create more objective and dependable evaluation systems, especially those that utilize digital trace data to observe actual usage patterns. Moreover, future studies should expand beyond a limited focus on SMA to examine related constructs such as meaning in life, inclusivity, and psychological resilience, which may offer a more comprehensive understanding of international students’ adaptation and well-being. Lastly, developing gender-sensitive and culturally appropriate intervention strategies is crucial to help international students improve their happiness, sense of belonging, and mental resilience in cross-cultural settings.

Methodology

This study uses network analysis to explore the structural differences in social media addiction behavior among Chinese international students from a gender-mirror perspective. Gender was determined on the basis of the results of the sample’s self-reports. Network analysis is an emerging statistical method that can identify and quantify the complex relationships between symptoms and reveal the system’s core features and marginal correlations36,48. In comparative studies, network analysis can reveal more intuitive structural differences and help researchers identify key symptoms and their interaction patterns in social media addiction behavior in different gender groups. In addition, network analysis can measure the centrality of symptoms and reveal which nodes play a core role in the addictive behavior network.

Sample and data collection

The target population consisted of Chinese international students studying in Thailand. Participants were recruited from 10 universities (5 public and 5 private) via convenience quota sampling to ensure diversity and representativeness. Following ethical approval and consent from the heads of Chinese student associations at each institution, researchers distributed invitation letters and questionnaire QR codes via WeChat groups. A total of 595 responses were initially collected. After excluding invalid responses due to duplicate submissions, 545 valid responses were retained for analysis, comprising 246 males (45.14%) and 299 females (54.86%), aged 19–38 years.

Data were collected through an online survey from early January to February 2025. Researchers disseminated a QR code with a brief introduction to the study’s objectives and themes. All participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time. Three trained research assistants facilitated the data collection process, guiding participants in understanding the questions and addressing any issues encountered during the survey. The questionnaire included demographic information and scales. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations49. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the study.

Measures

Social media addiction

Social media addiction (SMA) has garnered significant attention in recent years as a potential behavioral addiction. Griffiths (2005)17proposed the six core components model of addiction, comprising salience, mood modification, tolerance, withdrawal, conflict, and relapse, providing a theoretical foundation for SMA scale development. Andreassen et al.18,19. systematically defined SMAs, initially focusing on Facebook. Subsequent research expanded the six core component model, developing scales applicable to multiple platforms21. With the diversification of social media applications50, gender differences may play a critical role in the manifestation and mechanisms of the SMA, rendering the addiction phenomenon increasingly complex.

For Chinese university students, Luo et al.. (2023)20 conducted theoretical and empirical studies, validating the applicability of the SMA scale. The scale contains 20 items in six dimensions: significance, mood changes, tolerance, withdrawal symptoms, conflict, and relapse. In this study, after multiple rounds of expert review, the description of the SMA scale was modified more precisely, and social interaction was explicitly described as social media. Data collection was coded on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The scale showed high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α being 0.954 for the male group, 0.957 for the female group, and 0.957 for the total sample. CFA indicated adequate fit for the one-factor model: χ²/df = 4.83, RMR = 0.058, CFI = 0.935, GFI = 0.880, RMSEA = 0.084. AVE = 0.568 and CR = 0.894 exceeded conventional thresholds, demonstrating good convergent validity and internal consistency.

Data analysis

Network analysis originates from dynamic systems modeling, which constructs network models through nodes and edges36. The botnet package in R provides tools for assessing the accuracy and stability of networks in network analysis. It employs techniques such as bootstrapping to estimate confidence intervals for network structures and the stability of centrality metrics.

Estimate network

The EstimateNetwork function from the bootnet package was used to perform network estimation and construction separately for the male and female groups (Fig. 6). Given that the data in this study are continuous, the “EBICglasso” method is selected as the default network estimation approach. This method, which is based on the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC), applies the graphical lasso (glasso) technique to estimate Gaussian graphical models. Additionally, Spearman’s rank correlation is specified as the association measure for network estimation, as it assesses the monotonic relationship between two variables36. Network analysis related formulas.

The qgraph package provides the qgraph function to estimate and visualize psychological networks. A psychological network is a graphical representation of data in which nodes represent symptoms and edges represent statistical relationships between symptoms.

Network comparison

This study applies the compareCentrality function from the NetworkComparisonTest package (v. 2.2.1) to evaluate node centrality measures in the network model (iterations = 1000). A visual analysis is conducted to compare three datasets on the basis of four centrality metrics: strength, closeness, betweenness, and expected influence. The strength metric quantifies the direct connection intensity of a node; closeness reflects the average distance between a node and all other nodes in the network; betweenness measures the frequency with which a node appears in the shortest paths between all node pairs; and expected influence predicts the extent to which a node impacts the future states of other nodes in the network. Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of the importance and functional roles of nodes within the network36.

Assessing the stability of network centrality metrics

This study used the corStability function in the bootnet package to evaluate the stability of the network centrality indicators. Among them, the CS coefficient (correlation stability coefficient) is a key indicator used to measure the stability of centrality indicators. According to the evaluation criteria, in at least 70% of the cases, the CS coefficient between the original centrality index and the centrality index re-estimated on the basis of subset data should not be less than 0.25, and a value exceeding 0.5 is considered an excellent stability indicator.

Testing for significant differences

This study employs a bootstrap difference test in the network analysis to assess whether group differences in network indices such as node centrality and edge weights are statistically significant36. The test resamples the data many times, calculates the index difference for each resample, and constructs a bootstrap distribution of these differences51. If the confidence interval of the observed difference does not include zero, the group difference is considered statistically significant36. By examining whether the confidence interval of the observed difference contains zero, the analysis will determine whether the network structure of specific symptoms differs significantly across groups.
