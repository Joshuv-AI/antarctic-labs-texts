Contemporary Management and Future Directions of Adults Living With a Fontan Circulation
Authors: Jasmine Grewal, Giulia Pula, Rachel M. Wald, Rachael Cordina, Alexander Van De Bruaene, Luke J. Burchill, Paul Khairy, Jamil Aboulhosn, D. Scott Lim, Nadine A. Kasparian, Yves d’Udekem, Alexander R. Opotowsky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As 30-year survival after Fontan nears 90%, clinicians increasingly encounter Fontan circulatory failure. This panel introduces a pragmatic Fontan circulation function grading framework with red-flag triggers to prompt timely reassessment. Pathophysiology and management of key drivers and consequences of Fontan circulatory failure are outlined, including ventricular dysfunction/heart failure, arrhythmias, pulmonary vascular remodeling, lymphatic dysfunction, thromboembolism, and pathway obstruction. Considerations for pharmacologic, catheter-based, surgical, and transplant strategies are outlined. Comprehensive care is summarized across nutrition, endocrine-metabolic and bone health, mental health and neurocognition, and sexual and reproductive health, including pregnancy risk. Future directions emphasize multicenter registries and learning-health systems, standardized outcome measures, invasive exercise hemodynamics, advanced imaging and biomarker programs. We highlight emerging therapies, such as image-guided lymphatic interventions, cavopulmonary assist devices, optimized heart ± liver transplantation, and targeted/repurposed pharmacotherapy.

Fontan circulation function continuum: grading classification and red flags

The Fontan circulatory function continuum

Fontan circulatory function exists along a continuum from optimal performance to Fontan circulatory failure (FCF), the eventual common outcome for many adults with Fontan physiology. FCF broadly describes dysfunction that limits a patient’s ability to carry out daily activities with severe FCF culminating in death, transplant listing, Fontan takedown, and/or transition to palliative care.1 As 30-year survival now approaches 90%, the long-term complexities of this circulation are increasingly encountered in adulthood.2 FCF reflects diverse mechanisms and presentations, ranging from acute events such as thrombosis to progressive decline driven by elevated Fontan pressures and end-organ dysfunction, including Fontan-associated liver disease (FALD). Even under optimal conditions, the Fontan circulation carries inherent physiologic limitations that predispose to cardiac and noncardiac complications.3 Accordingly, FCF is best conceptualized not as a binary state but as a continuum requiring ongoing reassessment.

We propose a pragmatic grading system to succinctly characterize the continuum and complexity of Fontan circulatory function (Central Illustration). This approach is analogous to the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association physiologic staging of congenital heart disease, while incorporating parameters specific to the Fontan circulation. This working framework integrates cardiac and noncardiac factors that both contribute to and result from FCF, emphasizing management, when possible, to optimize the Fontan circulation. Although Schumacher et al4 recently introduced an excellent ordinal severity-based system for categorizing individual Fontan-related complications to support multicenter collaboration and risk stratification, the proposed classification allows for routine clinical application, offering a unified grading of overall Fontan circulatory function. Fontan circulation function red flags

Worsening Fontan circulation function can be subtle, making vigilance for clinical “red flags” or deviations from baseline essential (Figure 1). Although many abnormalities may be transient or attributable to other causes, they warrant timely evaluation and appropriate investigation. Importantly, a single red flag may reflect multiple pathophysiologic mechanisms, many of which remain incompletely understood (Figure 1). Resulting changes in Fontan circulatory function grade (Central Illustration) should prompt efforts to identify an underlying cardiac/noncardiac cause (eg new arrhythmia). Recognition of such changes may create opportunities for intervention (eg, ablation) to optimize circulatory function. In some cases, however, no clear etiology is found, and the red flag instead reflects progressive worsening and referral for heart ± liver transplantation may become the most appropriate consideration. The proposed grading system is designed to highlight important, often subtle clinical changes, enabling timely surveillance and coordinated care, ideally within a comprehensive Fontan management program (Figure 2). It draws on recognized high-risk features and complications, although it has not been formally validated and should be evaluated in future studies. Ultimately a prospectively validated grading system that considers the complexities of Fontan circulatory function could be used to inform a FCF risk score.

Fontan circulatory function: pathophysiologic factors and related management considerations

Ventricular dysfunction

Ventricular dysfunction is an important etiology of FCF, and the function is never “normal” with varying degrees of systolic and/or diastolic dysfunction among adults with a Fontan circulation. From fetal life through adulthood, the single ventricle is subject to abnormal and changing loading conditions that impact long-term mechanics. In addition, ventricular morphology, ischemic insults, atrioventricular (AV) valvular regurgitation, arrhythmias, systemic inflammation, and genetic predisposition can influence single ventricular function over the lifespan.5 Single right ventricle is generally associated with worse long-term outcomes than a single left ventricle.5 However, ventricular morphology is only one of several interacting risk factors and findings vary across studies.

The role of ventricular dysfunction in FCF is often challenging to delineate due to its diverse and variable clinical manifestations (Figure 1). Certainly systolic/diastolic dysfunction can negatively affect Fontan physiology and function (Central Illustration). Although no validated imaging parameters exist for single-ventricle assessment, a comprehensive approach using conventional and emerging measures can support the diagnosis of systolic ventricular dysfunction (Figure 3).1,6, 7, 8 Diastolic dysfunction is likely more common, and although the normal applied biventricular echocardiographic criteria can be utilized, they have not been validated in this population (Figure 3). Cardiac catheterization remains the reference standard for evaluating single-ventricle diastolic function (end diastolic pressure or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥12 mm Hg) with provocative testing to potentially identify occult diastolic dysfunction (end diastolic or pulmonary capillary wedge pressure ≥15 mm Hg after rapid volume expansion or exercise pulmonary artery wedge pressure >20 mm Hg)1,9, 10, 11 (Figure 3). Evidence suggests that exercise-derived Fontan and pulmonary artery wedge pressures are more sensitive than resting values for predicting outcomes.12 There is also emerging data that noninvasive echocardiographic-derived S/D ratio and resting/stress peripheral venous pressure are associated with resting/exercise pulmonary artery wedge pressures.13 Whether at rest or with exercise, accurate hemodynamic assessment is critical to understanding and guiding management.

Durable mechanical circulatory support has become a potential option for patients with failing Fontan physiology refractory to medical therapy, although it poses unique challenges due to anatomic complexity, abnormal hemodynamics, and multiorgan dysfunction. Consequently, ventricular assist device (VAD) use remains limited; however, among carefully selected patients, over half can be bridged to transplantation with VAD support.15 Outcomes appear most favorable when ventricular dysfunction is the primary driver, with VADs improving cardiac output and relieving venous congestion. In contrast, patients with clinically apparent lymphatic disease (eg, protein-losing enteropathy [PLE]) often fare worse, with VADs exacerbating fluid retention, hepatic congestion, and ascites. Optimal results therefore require careful patient selection, anatomically appropriate implantation, and a nuanced understanding of Fontan physiology to facilitate optimal outcomes after mechanical circulatory support in this population.15,16

Arrhythmias

Arrhythmias both contribute to and result from FCF and represent a major source of morbidity and mortality (Figure 1, Central Illustration). Although modern modifications have reduced early rates, long-term prevalence remains high, with estimates ranging from 20% to 44% overall, and over 75% in those with an older atriopulmonary type of Fontan.2,17,18 The most common arrhythmia is intra-atrial re-entrant tachycardia, although focal atrial tachycardia and atrial fibrillation can become more common with older age. Sinus node dysfunction is the most frequently observed form of bradyarrhythmia evident in 15% to 50%, with the prevalence increasing with time since surgery.19 Intrinsically abnormal or displaced AV nodes (eg, L-looped hearts; unbalanced AV septal defects) and/or malaligned septal structures predispose to AV block. These rhythm disturbances arise from the interplay of structural, hemodynamic, and electrophysiologic abnormalities that evolve over time. Fontan physiology itself fosters an arrhythmogenic milieu, postsurgical scars, chronic atrial stretch, elevated systemic venous pressures, and prolonged abnormal loading conditions promote fibrosis and electrical remodeling that favors triggered activity and re-entry. The Fontan completion itself further heightens risk, as atrial incisions, suture lines, and patches disrupt conduction pathways, predisposing to micro– and macro–re-entrant arrhythmias and bradyarrhythmia. Clinicians must maintain a high index of suspicion for atrial tachycardia and compare with baseline electrocardiograms, as ventricular rates may be deceptively normal and misinterpreted as sinus rhythm.

Arrhythmia management

Timely arrhythmia management with an initial rhythm control strategy is critical as Fontan patients can become hemodynamically unstable or develop tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy in the presence of usually well-tolerated arrhythmias. Atrial arrhythmia management can include AV-node blockers and antiarrhythmic drugs, such as sotalol, dofetilide, and amiodarone, although their long-term efficacy is limited. Catheter ablation has emerged as a key interventional strategy, with acute success rates of eliminating inducible tachycardias from 65% to 95% depending on operator experience, complexity of the atrial substrate, and prior surgical interventions.17,20,21 Onset of new or recurrent postablation arrhythmias, however, remains high (20%% to 50%), particularly among patients with extensive atrial remodeling.17,20,21 Advanced mapping techniques have significantly improved ablation outcomes. Electroanatomic mapping allows for detailed 3D reconstruction of atrial geometry and helps identify low-voltage areas indicative of scar tissue. Access to the pulmonary venous atrium for ablation typically requires transconduit/transcaval puncture or a robotic magnetically-guided retrograde aortic approach.22 These demand advanced imaging, specialized tools, and high procedural expertise. Anticoagulation is generally indicated for atrial arrhythmias in adults with Fontan physiology regardless of CHADS2/CHA2DS2-VASc scores.23 Direct oral anticoagulants may be considered in selected patients with a Fontan circulation; however, vitamin K antagonist therapy is generally preferred in the presence of advanced FALD, PLE, very high thrombotic risk, or concerns regarding drug absorption or drug-drug interactions.

Pacemaker implantation is frequently required for sinus node dysfunction, junctional rhythm, or AV block, with reported implantation rates of 8% to 13%.24 High ventricular pacing ratios, nonapical, and asynchronous ventricular pacing, have been shown to be deleterious, particularly in patients with ventricular dysfunction.24, 25, 26 However, the efficacy of resynchronization therapy by means of multisite pacing is unclear due to limited small studies and technical limitations.26,27 Epicardial pacing is the standard of care given the absence of a subpulmonic ventricle and resulting risk of systemic thromboembolism with systemic single ventricle intracardiac leads. Novel pacing strategies have emerged to address the anatomical limitations of traditional systems. Although long-term data are still limited, recent studies report successful implantation of leadless pacemakers in selected Fontan patients using innovative delivery techniques.28

The role of primary prevention implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (ICDs) in adults with a Fontan circulation also remains uncertain, with research limited by the low absolute incidence of ventricular arrhythmias. ICD can be considered in patients deemed to be at high risk by virtue of systemic ventricular dysfunction, nonsustained ventricular tachycardia, and unexplained syncope. Limited data in mixed adult congenital heart disease cohorts suggest a high rate of appropriate successful shocks but also a high rate of inappropriate shocks and device complications in the range of 15% to 25% over 4 to 5 years of follow-up.29,30 Owing to the lack of transvenous access to the subpulmonary ventricle, subcutaneous ICDs are generally preferred, with a reported eligibility rate that exceeds 90% in 1 small study (21/23 patients).31 Given the high incidence of sinus node dysfunction and atrial arrhythmia, the role of hybrid systems (eg, epicardial bipolar pacing combined with a subcutaneous ICD), should not be underappreciated.

Pulmonary vascular remodeling

Rising pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) or other causes of increase pulmonary impedance both contribute to or result from FCF (Figure 1, Central Illustration). Without a subpulmonary ventricle, pulmonary flow depends on central venous pressure, making even modest PVR increases sufficient to cause venous congestion, elevated Fontan pressures, reduced output, and progressive FCF.32 Autopsy and pathological studies demonstrate pulmonary arterial remodeling in Fontan patients characterized by intimal hyperplasia and altered medial architecture, likely reflecting the effects of chronic nonpulsatile, low-shear flow and dysregulated vasoregulatory pathways.33 Clinical factors associated with pulmonary vascular dysfunction include older age at Fontan completion, longer duration of Fontan circulation, Fontan type, coexisting pulmonary vascular abnormalities, comorbid conditions, and genetic susceptibility. A PVR above 2 indexed Wood units in individuals with normal/near normal body surface area has been to shown to be associated with poor outcomes.1,32

Pulmonary vascular remodeling management

Management has centered on pharmacologic reduction of PVR, but trials of pulmonary vasodilators in those with a Fontan show mixed efficacy, with positive studies demonstrating modest effects on peak oxygen consumption, undefined long-term benefit, and unclear indications (Table 2). Nonpharmacologic interventions also play a crucial role in optimizing hemodynamics in relation to the pulmonary circulation. Fenestration creation may improve functional capacity and relieve symptoms in select patients with progressive, refractory symptoms, but carries risks of thromboembolism and systemic desaturation, requiring careful selection and long-term anticoagulation.34 Surgical revisions may also be warranted in cases where anatomical or functional pulmonary artery abnormalities contribute to increasing PVR. Additional strategies include addressing comorbidities contributing to elevation of PVR, such as sleep-disordered breathing, which is prevalent among Fontan patients.35,36

Lymphatic dysfunction

Lymphatic dysfunction in adult Fontan patients is multifactorial, often subclinical, but can advance with clinical manifestations of PLE, plastic bronchitis, refractory effusions, or ascites in keeping with advanced Fontan circulation dysfunction (Central Illustration, Figure 1, Table 3). Lymphatic assessment is limited by small vessel size and anatomic variability. Despite frequent congenital abnormalities, evaluation is often symptom-driven and has traditionally involved lymphatic flow assessment by lymphangiography. Lymphatic dysfunction management

Initial management of lymphatic complications includes conservative approaches, namely dietary modifications and Fontan circulation optimization (Table 4). Several pharmacologic options exist with limited data on long-term efficacy (Table 4). In patients with persistent or severe lymphatic complications, surgical interventions may be warranted. Thoracic duct ligation, previously used, carries risk of disrupting compensatory lymphatic pathways and worsening congestion and not routinely recommended. Surgical lymphatic decompression is a potential option, although carries the downside of systemic arterial hypoxemia. Although less data exist, transcatheter thoracic duct decompression with a covered stent has provided improvement for a subset, among the small number of patients reported. Embolization, of abnormal lymphatic channels, guided by imaging such as MRL, offers a less invasive alternative by targeting lymphatic leaks or abnormal channels with retrograde flow. Clinical improvement in patients with plastic bronchitis following selective embolization has been demonstrated in a substantial subset; nonresponders lacked retrograde flow or a visible duct.39 Combining embolization with catheter-based thoracic duct decompression may further enhance outcomes.40 There is less evidence for efficacy in the context of PLE as compared with plastic bronchitis. Fontan conversion/cardiac transplantation.

Thromboembolism

Thromboembolism both contributes to and results from FCF, carrying substantial morbidity and mortality (Central Illustration, Figure 1). Reported rates at 25 years post-Fontan are approximately 18%, encompassing stroke and Fontan or pulmonary thromboembolism that disrupt Fontan hemodynamics.41 The prothrombotic milieu is multifactorial, driven by low-flow stasis, endothelial injury from surgery, prosthetic exposure, and chronic inflammation. Dysregulated hemostasis (eg, reduced protein C, protein S, and antithrombin, alongside altered coagulation factors) further amplifies risk. Liver dysfunction, PLE, and abnormal systemic hemodynamics exacerbate this imbalance, underscoring the need for comprehensive management of comorbidities to mitigate thromboembolic risk.

Thromboembolism prophylaxis and management

The optimal thromboprophylaxis strategy for Fontan patients remains a subject of debate, although recent Fontan expert consensus suggests all patients be treated with some type of thromboprophylaxis, low dose aspirin at a minimum.42 A meta-analysis (21 studies, including all ages, with a total of 26,546 patient-years) demonstrated that, when compared to no thromboprophylaxis, aspirin, warfarin, and direct oral anticoagulants were associated with lower rates of thromboembolic events.43 Although aspirin tended to have the most favorable overall profile, there are no definitive data showing superiority of one or another of these approaches. Therapeutic anticoagulation should be considered in those who are at high risk of thromboembolism, including those with a recent or previous thromboembolic event, mechanical valve or recent stent placement, recurrent atrial arrhythmias, pregnancy, prior thrombosis on bioprosthetic valves, and recent thrombosis in the Fontan pathway.42 Clinicians should be aware that adult Fontan-specific evidence for direct oral anticoagulants is still limited. For acute thrombosis, along with the usual thromboembolic strategies, targeted interventions can be considered including catheter-directed thrombolysis which minimizes systemic exposure and reducing bleeding risks.

Fontan pathway obstruction

Surveillance for Fontan pathway obstruction is a part of routine assessment; however, it should be proactively ruled out in the setting of worsening Fontan circulation function grade (Central Illustration). Obstruction can be related to thrombosis, and conduit narrowing or kinking anywhere from the vena cava, within the Fontan pathway and to the branch pulmonary arteries. A high clinical suspicion for this is needed as a cause of advancing FCF (Figure 1). Fontan pathway obstruction can be any anatomical or functional narrowing in the cavopulmonary pathways that is detected by cross-sectional imaging and/or can be associated with flow disturbance and gradients as low as 1 mm Hg on cardiac catheterization.1 Fontan pathway stenting is safe and effective at relieving hemodynamic obstruction, with low complication rates; however, translation to improvements in exercise capacity and outcomes is variable with limited evidence.44

Fontan circulatory failure: other management considerations

Transplantation in Fontan circulation failure

Heart transplantation represents a critical intervention in the management of advanced FCF, with nearly one-third of patients dying or requiring transplantation within 35 years post-Fontan.45 Heart transplantation outcomes have improved significantly, attributable to advancements in surgical techniques, postoperative care, and immunosuppressive management. A meta-analysis of 426 Fontan patients undergoing heart transplantation found a 1-year survival of almost 80% and a 5-year survival of 72.5%.46 Similarly, another study of 66 patients reported survival at 1 year of 83%, at 5 years of 79.0%, at 10 years of 67.2%, and at 15 years of 63.2%; survival was not significantly different from a biventricular congenital heart disease group.47 Referral timing and waitlisting decisions significantly influence transplantation decisions and outcomes in the Fontan population yet there are no clear criteria, resulting in delayed or nonlisting and hence, variable access.48, 49, 50 In a study of 63 Fontan patients, those approved for transplant listing were younger (<18 years old) and had fewer complications (eg, cirrhosis, renal insufficiency and ascites) compared with those deferred/declined.49 Overall survival was significantly lower in patients who were not listed, highlighting that earlier referral to a high-volume congenital heart disease transplant center, before advanced end-organ dysfunction develops, increases the likelihood of listing and is associated with improved survival. Across 15 transplant centers in the United States and Canada, only 131 adults with a Fontan circulation underwent transplantation between 1995 and 2021, underscoring both obstacles to transplantation and resource scarcity among adults with advanced FCF.51 The chronic, often subtle progression of Fontan circulation changes may lead to missed opportunities for timely transplant referral. The Fontan circulation function grading classification provides an objective framework to prompt referral, with transplant evaluation recommended latest by stage C (Central Illustration).

FALD significantly impacts transplant outcomes. In advanced cases, combined heart-liver transplantation (CHLT) may confer a survival advantage. In a multicenter cohort of 131 adults (91 heart transplant and 40 CHLT), CHLT was associated with a trend toward improved 1-year (93% vs 74%; P = 0.09) and 5-year (86% vs 52%; P = 0.05) survival.51 Among patients with FALD scores ≥2 (4 elements: cirrhosis, splenomegaly, varies, and ≥2 paracentesis, 1 point for each), CHLT was associated with significantly better 1-year (85% vs 62%; P = 0.04) and 5-year (77% vs 42%; P = 0.02) survival compared with heart transplantation alone. In highly sensitized patients, a staged liver-first strategy followed by heart transplantation may be considered in selected cases, as the liver’s immunomodulatory effects can reduce allosensitization and mitigate the risk of rejection and other adverse immunologic outcomes.52 However, this approach is typically reserved for specialized centers and carefully selected patients. These findings underscore the importance of comprehensive pretransplant liver assessment and support a tailored approach, including selective use of CHLT in advanced FALD, balancing potential survival benefits against procedural complexity.

Fontan-associated liver disease

FALD is a major noncardiac complication of the Fontan procedure that contributes to significant morbidity and early mortality.51 It is thought to result from chronically elevated central venous pressure, reduced cardiac output, and possibly abnormal lymphatic drainage, leading to hepatic congestion and progressive fibrosis. Liver fibrosis is present in nearly all adults with Fontan circulation, with about two-thirds developing severe fibrosis.53 FALD may progress to cirrhosis, portal hypertension, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Therefore, careful management of modifiable risk factors such as maintaining a healthy weight, avoiding alcohol and hepatotoxic medications, and ensuring appropriate hepatitis vaccination is essential. Because the disease can be silent, regular surveillance with laboratory tests and imaging is recommended, with hepatology referral for abnormal findings.

Exercise as a therapy

Decreasing exercise tolerance is a hallmark feature of and should prompt investigations and possible intervention (Figure 1). It is important to consider that Fontan patients generally demonstrate reduced exercise capacity as compared with age-predicted values. This can stem from multiple factors, including underlying Fontan physiology, reduced cardiac output augmentation, chronotropic incompetence, arrhythmias, autonomic dysfunction, lung disease, hypoxemia, pulmonary vascular abnormalities, skeletal muscle dysfunction, malnutrition, and/or deconditioning.

Exercise training is a safe and effective yet underutilized intervention for adult Fontan patients. Current limited evidence in adults suggests a combination of aerobic and resistance training may be most beneficial. Structured aerobic exercise training has demonstrated improvements in peak VO2 (5%% to 10%).54 Skeletal and inspiratory muscle mass and strength are reduced; targeted exercise to improve these may support venous return, enhance ventricular filling, and boost cardiac output, thereby improving overall Fontan physiology.55,56 Beyond mechanical benefits, increased skeletal muscle mass also improves peripheral oxygen utilization and metabolic efficiency, ultimately improving exercise tolerance and mitigating the systemic effects of chronic low cardiac output. Among adults with Fontan circulation, exercise training and physical activity are associated with improvements in physical functioning and symptom burden, although high-quality data on associations between exercise and quality of life remain limited.54,57

Catheter-based interventions

Diagnostic and therapeutic catheterization plays a critical role in Fontan management, with approximately 50% requiring surgical or catheter-based reintervention within 20 years of Fontan surgery.58,59 In adults, the most common catheter-based interventions include balloon dilation and stent placement to relieve Fontan pathway obstruction or pulmonary artery stenosis, fenestration closure, and occluding collaterals. Coiling/device occluding aortopulmonary collaterals can be performed to decrease volume overload and reduce intraoperative bleeding. Similarly, veno-venous collaterals are all also occluded to reduce intraoperative bleeding particularly before transplantation. Occlusion of veno-venous collaterals in cyanotic patients may improve oxygen saturation, exercise capacity, and potentially reduce thromboembolic risk. However, it may not be the preferred strategy in the presence of elevated Fontan pressures, as these collaterals can serve as a decompressive pathway and their closure may exacerbate systemic venous hypertension and congestion. Aortic coarctation stenting should be performed to relieve afterload on the single ventricle. Several factors are associated with an increased likelihood of requiring catheter intervention, including atriopulmonary Fontan, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, and heterotaxy syndrome.58,59

There are several emerging catheter-based technologies focused on optimizing the Fontan circulation. Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (eg, MitraClip) has shown promise in treating atrioventricular valve regurgitation and offers a less invasive alternative for patients who may be poor surgical candidates, although few patients are appropriate candidates with current technology. Other innovative approaches under investigation include novel stent designs for maintaining Fontan pathway patency, biodegradable devices for temporary support, and hybrid procedures combining catheter-based and minimally invasive surgical techniques. Transcatheter cavopulmonary connections are being developed to address flow obstructions and improve hemodynamics while avoiding open surgery.

As the field progresses, personalized approaches based on individual patient characteristics, anatomy, and hemodynamics are likely to become increasingly important in optimizing outcomes for this complex patient population. The integration of advanced imaging modalities, such as 3-dimensional rotational angiography and fusion imaging, is enhancing the precision of catheter-based interventions. These tools allow for better visualization of complex Fontan anatomy and more accurate device placement, potentially improving procedural outcomes.

Beyond Fontan circulatory failure: provision of comprehensive care

Assessing a given individual’s FCF trajectory is critical to minimize morbidity and mortality. There are, however, many other important considerations, some of which may directly or indirectly alter morbidity and mortality, whereas others are critical to optimizing quality of life (Figure 4).

Nutritional status

Moderate-to-severe malnutrition affects approximately 1 in 5 adults with a Fontan circulation and is associated with worse functional capacity.60 Established nutrition-related complications include PLE, growth impairment, reduced bone mineral density, and malabsorption, with emerging evidence highlighting additional concerns, such as excess adiposity, altered glucose metabolism, and lower skeletal muscle mass.61 Among those with no comorbidities requiring specialized nutrition management, dietary recommendations align with general population guidelines for health promotion and chronic disease prevention. Routine malnutrition screening and comprehensive nutritional assessment, including anthropometry, biochemical markers, clinical data, and dietary intake, are essential to identify nutrition-related complications.61

Metabolic and endocrine

Although data remain limited, thyroid dysfunction appears common in Fontan patients. In 1 cohort of 37 patients, 33% had subclinical hypothyroidism.62 Elevated thyroid stimulating hormone was correlated with markers of congestion, including γ-glutamyltransferase and central venous pressure. Surveillance and management of hypothyroidism is important to optimize Fontan hemodynamics, as low free T3 levels are associated with reduced cardiac index, impaired ventricular relaxation, and elevated natriuretic peptide levels.63 Although specific data on euthyroid sick syndrome in this population are sparse, its presence likely most often reflects chronic illness with hepatic congestion and systemic inflammation.

Impaired glucose tolerance affects approximately one-third of the Fontan population over their lifetime associated with advancing age and liver dysfunction rather than obesity alone, whereas overt diabetes develops in 5% to 10% over time.64,65 Higher fasting plasma glucose and greater post–glucose-spike during oral glucose tolerance testing were independently associated with all-cause mortality. Mechanistically, hepatic congestion, reduced skeletal muscle mass, chronic inflammation, and altered hemodynamics contribute to impaired glucose regulation.

Abnormalities in bone metabolism and vitamin D status are also observed. A pooled analysis estimated about 51% of individuals with a Fontan circulation has vitamin D deficiency (25-hydroxyvitamin D <20 ng/mL) over their lifetime.66 Secondary hyperparathyroidism is also common, implicating disturbances in the calcium-vitamin D–PTH axis in this population.67

Iron deficiency is prevalent and often under-recognized in patients with a Fontan circulation, may occur despite normal hemoglobin, and is associated with reduced exercise capacity, underscoring the importance of routine and repeated assessment.68

Mental health and neurocognition

Mental health conditions are highly prevalent among adults with a Fontan circulation, with a lifetime prevalence of psychiatric diagnosis of up to 65%, particularly anxiety and attention-deficit/hyperactivity-disorder.69, 70, 71 Depressive symptoms are associated with greater cognitive difficulties, reduced exercise capacity, and lower health-related quality of life.72,73 Across life, individuals with Fontan circulation can also experience neurocognitive difficulties, including difficulties in executive function, attention, processing speed, and visuospatial reasoning, often associated with smaller global and white matter brain volumes on neuroimaging.74 These findings likely reflect early and continued neurocognitive vulnerability arising from individual and environmental factors, including hypoxia, peri-operative injury, and chronic low cardiac output intrinsic to single-ventricle physiology. The interplay between neurocognition and mental health can contribute to difficulties in adaptive skills, self-management, psychosocial functioning, and adherence to clinical recommendations, amplifying the burden of Fontan-associated morbidity. Routine screening for neurocognitive and psychological disorders, coupled with early intervention, treatment, and multidisciplinary care, is essential to optimizing long-term outcomes.

Sexual and reproductive health

Sexual health is a complex interplay of physical, psychological, and social factors and can significantly affect quality of life. Despite the young age of the population, in men with a Fontan circulation, erectile dysfunction affects approximately 20% to 25%, typically mild in severity, although many report lower sexual desire and satisfaction compared with controls.75 Among women, overall sexual well-being scores were comparable to norms, but older female patients often reported psychosocial barriers, dyspareunia, low self-esteem, and avoidance of sexual activity.76 It is important to ensure patients are not avoiding intimacy due to addressable concerns about safety or symptoms with exertion.

Fertility outcomes in men with a Fontan circulation appear to be comparable with general population rates, with approximately 30% reporting a prior pregnancy in a partner.75 In contrast, women face significant fertility challenges with fertility rates as low as 16% to 18%.77

Pregnancy in women with Fontan circulation carries high risk relative to many other types of congenital heart disease. Preconception counseling within specialized cardio-obstetric programs is recommended, beginning in adolescence. This counseling should focus on education, realistic expectations, and effective contraception. Long-acting reversible methods, such as the levonorgestrel intrauterine system or subdermal implant are preferred for efficacy and avoidance of estrogen-related thrombotic risk. Given the challenges of delayed menarche, reduced fertility, and high early miscarriage rates, early discussion of fertility preservation and assisted reproductive options (eg, oocyte or embryo cryopreservation and in vitro fertilization) is recommended before clinical deterioration further limits reproductive potential. Risk of assisted reproductive options also needs to be considered including hyperstimulation ovarian syndrome and thromboembolism. Awareness of the high rate of pregnancy loss, with spontaneous abortion rates of approximately 50%, is vital to setting informed expectations.77,78

Pre-pregnancy counseling should address maternal cardiac, obstetric, and fetal risks and optimization; antenatal and delivery planning; and sensitive considerations around longevity and parenting capacity. A systematic review of 255 pregnancies in 133 women with Fontan circulation found maternal cardiovascular complications were relatively infrequent: supraventricular tachycardia = 8.4%; thromboembolism = 5.1%; heart failure = 3.9%; and no maternal deaths.78 Notably, 69% (9/13) of thromboembolic events occurred despite antithrombotic therapy (5 anticoagulation, 4 antiplatelet), underscoring persistent risk. Anticoagulation should be individualized; even if anticoagulation is used, many clinicians maintain low-dose aspirin, particularly when pre-eclampsia risk is elevated. Contemporary U.S. data (n = 509 deliveries) show higher obstetric morbidity vs controls (hypertensive disorders of pregnancy 14% vs 8%, preterm 16% vs 7%, postpartum hemorrhage 13% vs 3%).79 Among live births, adverse fetal outcomes include preterm delivery 59%, small for gestational age 20%, and neonatal death 5%. Hypoxemia is a particularly strong predictor of pregnancy loss, as well as preterm birth and small for gestational age.

Advance care planning

Early proactive advance care planning should be pursued with discussions integrated throughout the care continuum and not delayed until late stages.80 Conversations should focus on patient values, goals of care, prognosis, and potential future interventions, while highlighting shared decision-making and multidisciplinary collaboration. This approach will support patient-centered care, preparedness, and quality of life.

Future directions

Future directions in Fontan care should leverage coordinated, multicenter research and learning-health systems to clarify outcomes, define early failure markers, and drive quality improvement and benchmarking.
