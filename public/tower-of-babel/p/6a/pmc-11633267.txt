Prevalence and Predictors of Depression Among Drug-Sensitive Tuberculosis Patients – A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Deepak Sharma, Naveen K. Goel, Ajeet Sidana, Kawaljeet S. Sandha, Mamta Bishnoi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:According to World Health Organization, India is a high tuberculosis (TB) and depression burden state. The risk of depression is higher among TB patients. Therefore, it is vital to focus on the mental well-being of TB patients.Objective:The present study aimed to determine the prevalence and predictors of depression among drug-sensitive TB patients in Chandigarh city.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 305 TB patients. Trained investigators administered Patient Health Questionnaire 9 to screen for depression. Data analysis was done using Epi info software for windows.Results:The prevalence rate of depression among drug-sensitive TB patients was 7.2%. Current nonworking individuals were more likely to be depressed than their counterparts (OR = 3.7 (1.2–10.9)).Conclusion:Around one-tenth of the TB patients were suffering from depression. This necessitates physicians to regularly screen TB patients for depression and manage accordingly, which will help improve treatment adherence and outcome.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB) is a public health problem worldwide, particularly in developing countries. The WHO South-East Asia (SEA) Region is home to 43% burden of TB incidence. India accounts for 27% of the world’s new TB cases globally. This disease burdens our country’s healthcare system and is responsible for catastrophic out-of-pocket expenditures. However, the last few years have witnessed a decline in TB cases, evidenced by a decrease in India’s TB incidence from 256/lakh population (year 2015) to 210/lakh population (year 2021). The decline is attributed to government policies like mandatory TB case notification, active case-finding drives, and scaled diagnostic capability.[1]

Depression, a common mental health disorder in India, is a frequent comorbidity among TB patients. Existing literature indicates a high prevalence of depression among patients with TB.[23] Koyangi, using the community-based data from the World Health Survey of 48 low- and middle-income countries, reported that the prevalence of depressive episodes among those with and without TB was 23.7% and 6.8%, respectively.[4] A meta-analysis reported the pooled estimated prevalence of depression among TB patients to be as high as 45.1%.[5] The relationship between TB and depression is bidirectional. First, the tuberculous infection may precipitate depression since patients may feel stigmatized and thus alienated from society. Furthermore, an absent caregiver’s support may further bring to the fore the depressive symptoms. Alternatively, depression can also contribute to development of TB. Comorbid depression among TB patients may lead to improper treatment-seeking behavior, low compliance, and poor treatment outcomes, including death.[678] Existing literature reports that drug-resistant TB cases have a higher proportion of depression than drug-sensitive ones.[910] This may be attributed to comparative long duration of MDR-TB treatment, wherein some medicines may cause psychiatric disorders.[11]

Currently, limited studies explore the prevalence of depression among TB patients and its predictors, more so in North India setting. Further, since mostly TB patients in India are drug-sensitive, we must study this study population exclusively. Against this backdrop, the present study was conducted in Chandigarh city of North India to determine the prevalence and predictors of depression among drug-sensitive TB patients.

METHODS

A cross-sectional study was carried out among TB patients currently on treatment in government health facilities of Chandigarh city from December 2022 to February 2023. A sample size of 305 was calculated assuming a prevalence of depression of 23.6%,[2] an absolute precision of 5%, 95% confidence interval, and 10% nonresponse rate. Chandigarh city has four TB units (TU) and 18 Designated Microscopy Centres (DMCs). From each TU, two DMCs were selected randomly. A convenient sampling technique was used to select study participants in the selected DMC. Trained field investigators visited the selected DMCs on a designated day and time and interviewed TB patients after taking their informed written consent. The inclusion criteria were age > 18 years and a drug-sensitive TB diagnosis. Patients already on psychotropic medicines were excluded. For the current study, a “current tobacco smoker” was defined as an adult who reportedly smoked tobacco at least once in the past month preceding the survey. Perceived stigma was assessed by asking if the patient feared disclosing his disease status to others because they may think they will have it too and, therefore, not sit with him. A study participant was considered to suffer from chronic disease based on their available medical records.

Patient Health Questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9) (adapted in Hindi) was used to screen for depression. A cutoff score of ≥ 10 has a good sensitivity and specificity in exhibiting clinically significant depressive symptoms. Also, the PHQ-9 score cutoffs of 5–9, 10–14, 15–19, and 20 or above were used to indicate mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe symptoms.[12] Data were analyzed using Epi info software for windows. Descriptive statistics were computed. Pearson’s Chi-square determined the relationship between the independent variables and depression. The level of statistical significance was decided as 0.05 for the bivariate analyses. The unadjusted odds ratio with 95% confidence interval was reported. Approval for this study was granted by the ethical committee of the Government Medical College, Chandigarh (GMCH/IEC/705R/2022/105).

RESULTS

A total of 305 patients participated in the study. Pulmonary TB was more common (171, 56.1%) among the study participants than extrapulmonary TB (134, 43.9%). Most participants were new cases (275, 90.2%), and mostly, patients were in the continuation phase (215, 70.5%). There were 192 (63.0%) microbiologically confirmed TB cases, while 113 (37.0%) were clinically confirmed. The mean age of the study participants was 34.3 (SD ± 14.4) years. The study sample comprised 49.5% males (n = 151) and 50.5% females (n = 154).

There were 39 (12.8%) illiterates, 26 (8.5%) were currently nonworking, 97 (31.8%) were never married individuals, and 215 (70.5%) lived in a nuclear family. Comorbidity was present in 15.1% (46/305) of study participants, which included hypertension (4.2%;13/305), diabetes (12.1%;37/305), CAD (0.9%;3/305), and CKD (0.9%;1/305). In addition, there were 7.5% current smokers (23/305) and 5.2% used smokeless tobacco (16/305).

The prevalence of depression was 7.2% (22/305). The prevalence of mild, moderate, moderately severe, and severe depression was 17% (52/305), 4.6% (14/305), 2.3% (7/305), and 0.3% (1/305). In the bivariate analysis, depression was not significantly associated with sociodemographic factors, namely, age, gender, type of family, and marital status. Also, tobacco usage, comorbidity, stigma, and duration of illness had no association with depression. However, nonworking individuals had higher odds of depression than their counterparts (OR = 3.7 (1.2-10.9)) [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

In the present study, the prevalence of depression among TB patients was 7.5%. However, Retnakumar (16.1%) in Kerela and Salodia in Delhi (23.6%) have reported a comparative higher prevalence.[913] A study conducted among TB patients in Ethiopia reported that 31% suffered from depression.[14] The comparative higher prevalence in these studies may be attributed to the study population, wherein all cases of TB, drug-sensitive and drug-resistant TB patients, were included, whereas in our study, we studied only drug-sensitive TB patients.

In our study, depression was higher among those currently nonworking individuals. A similar finding has been reported in a study conducted by Kamble.[15] A possible reason for this finding may be that unemployment leads to social isolation and loneliness, which deteriorate mental health.[16] In addition, psychological burden of suffering from this disease may further accentuate depression. It is, therefore, necessary to focus on these individuals by appropriate mental well-being counseling which would in turn improve TB treatment outcomes.

In our study, there was no association of depression with the patient’s age, gender, duration of treatment, and tobacco use. Studies conducted by Abdurahman have reported a contrary finding wherein depression was significantly associated with age, gender, intensive phase of TB treatment, and smoking history.[17] Yet in another study by Amole, smoking tobacco was reported as a risk factor for depression among TB patients.[18] A study by Dasa reported that age, low income, new TB patients, and initial months of treatment were associated with depression among TB patients.[19] In our study, stigma had no association with depression. Contrary to this finding, a study by Zakaria reported a positive correlation between stigma and depression.[20]

The main strength of our study is using a standard validated tool, namely, PHQ-9, to assess depression. Furthermore, enrolling study participants from multiple sites in the city increases the representativeness and generalizability of findings in a similar setting. However, a limitation of this study is the cross-sectional design, and therefore, no conclusion can be made about the temporal relation of depression among TB patients. A second limitation is the risk for selection bias since study participants were selected by convenient sampling. Also, using PHQ-9, a self-report instrument, risks recall bias.

CONCLUSIONS

The present study aimed at assessing the prevalence of depression among TB patients. Around one-tenth of study participants were found to be suffering from depression. This figure is lower than that reported in previous studies, possibly because we exclusively included drug-sensitive TB patients. However, our finding of 7.5% depression among drug-sensitive TB patients is certainly higher than that in the general population (1.7%, National Mental Health Survey).[21] This necessitates that TB patients should be regularly screened for psychiatric disorders including depression and those found suffering should be managed accordingly. In addition, our finding of comparative more depression among currently nonworking necessitates that we focus more on them.
