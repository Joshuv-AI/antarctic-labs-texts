Deep learning for otitis media classification using otoscopic image
Authors: Qingqing Guo, Liangzhen Xie, Ling Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Otitis media (OM) is a globally prevalent health issue that poses significant threats to hearing and overall health. However, traditional diagnostic methods rely heavily on subjective interpretation of otoscopic images, which often leads to inconsistent outcomes and high misdiagnosis rates. This study evaluated 5 deep learning models (ResNet-18, GoogLeNet, AlexNet, MobileNet-V3, and VGGNet-19) for classifying 819 otoscopic images into 4 categories: normal, acute otitis media, otitis media with effusion, and chronic suppurative otitis media. Specifically, the dataset was split into training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%) sets. Subsequently, model performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1 score, and receiver operating characteristic–area under the curve analysis. The results showed that VGGNet-19 demonstrated superior performance, achieving the highest scores in accuracy (94.51%), sensitivity (94.18%), specificity (98.10%), and precision (93.86%), with particularly outstanding performance in chronic suppurative otitis media identification. While other models delivered competitive results, their metrics were consistently lower in comparison. Therefore, the study confirms VGGNet-19’s potential as an accurate, automated diagnostic tool for OM classification. Future research should address dataset imbalances and validate findings across diverse clinical settings to enhance generalizability.

1. Introduction

Otitis media (OM) is a global health issue, particularly prevalent among children.[1] According to statistics from the World Health Organization, approximately 2.5 billion people worldwide suffer from various ear diseases.[2] Acute otitis media (AOM), otitis media with effusion (OME), and chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) are among the most prevalent types of OM.[3] AOM not only leads to hearing loss but may also cause severe intracranial complications, such as meningitis and brain abscesses.[4] OME, characterized by fluid accumulation in the middle ear, can result in persistent hearing loss, adversely affecting children’s language and cognitive development.[5] It is noteworthy that the clinical course and management of OME differ significantly between children and adults. In children, OME is often a sequelae of AOM or related to Eustachian tube dysfunction due to anatomical immaturity, and watchful waiting is frequently employed due to high rates of spontaneous resolution.[6] In contrast, OME in adults is less common and often warrants a more thorough investigation for underlying pathologies, such as nasopharyngeal carcinoma or chronic sinusitis, as it is rarely idiopathic.[7] CSOM is particularly common in developing countries, with approximately 21,000 deaths annually attributed to complications related to OM, especially in regions with limited healthcare resources.[8] The frequent recurrence and long-term effects of OM often necessitate repeated medical visits and even surgical interventions, which can cause significant distress to patients physically and mentally, as well as disrupt their daily lives.[9] Therefore, early diagnosis and timely treatment are essential to avoid delaying the condition.

Otoscope images provide a clear visualization of the pathological changes in the tympanic membrane (TM) and middle ear cavity, serving as a crucial diagnostic tool for OM.[10] However, the interpretation of otoscopic images is highly dependent on the clinician’s experience and subjective judgment, making it vulnerable to variations in expertise and skill levels, which often results in inconsistent diagnostic outcomes and a higher rate of misdiagnosis.[11] The accuracy (Acc) of existing otoscopic methods in diagnosing AOM and OME ranges from 62% to 78% and 43% to 52%.[12,13] Particularly in resource-limited settings, due to the lack of experienced clinicians and advanced diagnostic tools, the average diagnostic Acc for OM is only around 50%.[14] This highlights the urgent need for an automated, objective, and efficient diagnostic method that can be deployed in such settings.

In recent years, deep learning (DL) has made significant strides in the field of medical image analysis, particularly excelling in tasks such as image classification, segmentation, and detection.[15] Convolutional neural networks (CNN), as a core algorithm of DL, has been widely applied in the automated diagnosis of medical images.[16] For instance, network architectures such as ResNet, GoogLeNet, and VGGNet have demonstrated high Acc in image classification tasks for breast cancer, lung cancer, and dermatological diseases.[16–18] These successful cases highlight the immense potential of DL technology in the classification of medical images.[19]

Previous studies have demonstrated that DL-based diagnostic systems for OM can significantly enhance diagnostic Acc and efficiency.[20–23] For instance, Tran et al developed an automatic diagnostic algorithm that achieved an Acc of 91.41% in distinguishing between AOM and OME.[24] However, most of these studies have focused on the classification of AOM and OME, with limited research on CSOM, due to the complexity of its image features and anatomical distortions that complicate feature selection.[20] Additionally, there is still a lack of comprehensive comparisons of different DL models in OM classification tasks. Therefore, this study enriches the research in this field and further validates the potential of DL in OM classification by systematically comparing the performance of 5 widely-used CNN models with distinct architectures in the task of OM image classification.

The main contributions of this study are as follows: we systematically compare the performance of 5 DL models on an otoscopic image dataset, providing quantitative benchmarks and valuable insights for model selection in OM diagnosis. We demonstrate that the VGGNet-19, when combined with pre-trained weights and meticulous parameter tuning, achieves superior performance in OM classification, even without architectural modifications. Our findings have significant clinical implications, offering a reliable and efficient diagnostic tool for OM, particularly in regions with limited healthcare resources.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Dataset description

The dataset utilized in this study was obtained from the outpatient otoscopic image database of the Department of Otolaryngology at the First Affiliated Hospital of Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine. Images collected between January 2023 and December 2023 were selected for retrospective analysis. During or after data collection, the authors did not have access to information that could identify individual participants. All data were anonymized prior to analysis, and the research team was unable to identify individual participants. The otoscopic images were captured using the JuMiao Technology Otoscope (2017 edition). All images were obtained directly from the otoscope device through photography, not from video screenshots. The image format is JPG. After excluding duplicate, unclear, out-of-focus, and incomplete TM images, a total of 819 otoscopic images were included, each with a resolution of 720 × 576 pixels. These images were independently reviewed and unanimously classified by 3 otolaryngology experts. Image annotation strictly adhered to international diagnostic criteria: normal ears were characterized by a translucent, grayish-white TM with clearly visible landmarks and distinct light reflex; AOM required the concurrent presence of acute symptoms (ear pain/fever), erythematous and bulging TM, and signs of middle ear effusion; OME was identified by type B tympanogram, air-fluid levels, and absence of acute inflammation; while CSOM was defined by persistent otorrhea (>2 months) with TM perforation, with or without cholesteatoma.[25,26] Three certified evaluators completed standardized consistency training and performed double-blind independent annotations. Final diagnoses required consensus from at least 2 experts, with discrepant cases resolved through panel review. The final dataset comprised 199 normal ears, 367 AOM cases, 142 OME cases, and 111 CSOM cases. Excellent inter-rater reliability was demonstrated (Fleiss kappa = 0.89, 95%CI: 0.85–0.92), confirming the high reliability of annotation results.[27] The study included subjects with a mean age of 28.4 years (range: 3–74 years), and the gender distribution was 432 (52.7%) males and 387 (47.3%) females. Representative examples of these 4 categories of otoscopic images are illustrated in Figure 1. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Heilongjiang University of Chinese Medicine (HZYLLKT202443101) on December 17, 2024.

2.2. Data preprocessing

To ensure the quality and consistency of input images for the DL model, the dataset underwent a series of preprocessing steps. Initially, all images were resized to a uniform resolution of 224 × 224 pixels to ensure that the input dimensions of the dataset conformed to the requirements of the CNN models. Subsequently, the images were normalized to the range of (0, 1) by dividing each pixel value by 255. This normalization step ensures that pixel values are scaled to a standard range, which is crucial for enhancing the convergence speed and stability of the training process. To further enhance the diversity of the training data and mitigate overfitting, data augmentation techniques were employed. These techniques included random horizontal flipping, random vertical flipping, and random rotation within a range of ± 10 degrees as it aids the model in generalizing to different orientations of the TM. Random vertical flipping introduces additional variability, thereby improving the model’s robustness to diverse image perspectives. Random rotation accounts for potential angle variations that may occur during otoscopic examinations. Finally, the dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets in a 60:20:20 ratio. This stratified division aids in evaluating the model’s performance across different categories.

2.3. CNN models

In 1998, LeCun et al proposed the earliest CNN model, LeNet-5, for handwritten digit recognition tasks.[28] The traditional CNN model, which included fundamental components such as convolutional layers, pooling layers, and fully connected layers (Fig. 2), has been widely applied in computer vision tasks and laid the foundation for subsequent deeper and more complex CNN models.[29] Later developments in CNN models introduced mechanisms such as the ReLU activation function, inception modules, and skip connections, further expanding the applicability of CNN to various image recognition tasks.[30–32] Based on these characteristics, CNN offers several advantages over traditional machine learning methods in medical image analysis: automated extraction of image features, improving the efficiency and comprehensiveness of information processing; incorporation of complex neural networks and nonlinear activation functions, enhancing the ability to learn nonlinear relationships between images and diagnostic labels; and suitability for large-scale datasets, contributing to improved model adaptability.[33] In the task of otoscopic image classification, CNN models can effectively process complex otoscopic image data through automated feature extraction and multi-level feature combination.[22] By learning pathological changes in the TM and middle ear cavity, CNN are capable of automatically distinguishing between normal TM and different types of OM, achieving high-precision (Pre) classification.[10]

This study systematically selected 5 representative CNN architectures in the field of computer vision for comprehensive comparison: the pioneering AlexNet first introduced key concepts such as ReLU activation functions and Dropout; VGGNet-19 demonstrated the effectiveness of very deep networks through stacked 3 × 3 convolutional kernels; ResNet-18 addressed the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks using residual connections; GoogLeNet innovatively proposed the inception module for multi-scale feature extraction; while MobileNet-V3 represents the latest efficient architecture optimized for mobile devices. The selected architectures span key technological milestones in CNN development, meticulously designed to address both the local feature sensitivity (Sen) required for medical image analysis and the computational efficiency demands of clinical settings. This systematic approach enables comprehensive evaluation of different modeling paradigms’ generalization capabilities on otoscopic images, establishing an empirical foundation for subsequent model optimization and deployment. The following subsections provide a detailed exposition of the structural designs of these models.

2.3.1. ResNet-18

ResNet-18, a lightweight variant of the ResNet series, was proposed by He et al.[34] Its core innovation lies in the residual learning framework, which introduces skip connections to address the vanishing gradient and degradation problems in deep networks.[34] By learning residual mappings instead of direct mappings, ResNet-18 significantly reduces training complexity and improves convergence.[35] The architecture consists of 18 layers, including multiple residual blocks with convolutional layers, batch normalization, and ReLU activation functions.[36] Compared to deeper ResNet variants, ResNet-18 offers a more compact structure, making it suitable for efficient image classification tasks.[36] The final layer typically includes a fully connected layer followed by a Softmax activation function for classification. Due to its lightweight design and efficient performance, ResNet-18 excels in medical image classification tasks.[37,38]

2.3.2. GoogLeNet

GoogLeNet, developed by Google, represents a groundbreaking advancement in deep CNN, introducing the innovative inception module, which marks a pivotal milestone in the evolution of DL architectures.[39] The GoogLeNet architecture is specifically engineered to tackle the challenges associated with increasing network depth and width, while simultaneously minimizing computational complexity and mitigating the risk of overfitting. This is achieved through the implementation of a unique inception module, which integrates convolutional filters of varying sizes alongside pooling operations, thereby enabling the extraction of multi-scale features.[40] This architectural design significantly reduces the number of parameters, thereby enhancing computational efficiency.[41] Additionally, the network incorporates auxiliary classifiers to address the issue of gradient vanishing during the training process. Comprising 22 layers, including convolutional layers, pooling layers, and fully connected layers, GoogLeNet employs a final Softmax activation function for classification tasks.[42] The input layer is designed to process images of 224 × 224 pixels, rendering the network highly effective for image classification applications.

2.3.3. AlexNet

AlexNet is a pioneering deep CNN that marked a significant breakthrough in image classification tasks, particularly with its victory in the 2012 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge.[43] The AlexNet architecture consists of 8 layers, including 5 convolutional layers and 3 fully connected layers, making it one of the deeper networks of its time.[44] A key innovation in AlexNet is the use of the ReLU activation function, which addresses the vanishing gradient problem associated with traditional activation functions like Sigmoid and Tanh, thereby accelerating the training process.[45] Additionally, AlexNet introduced dropout regularization in its fully connected layers, randomly deactivating neurons during training to prevent overfitting and enhance generalization.[46] The network also employs Local Response Normalization after convolutional layers to improve feature extraction and model robustness. AlexNet’s input layer processes images of size 227 × 227 × 3 (RGB), and its final layer uses the Softmax activation function to produce a probability distribution over 1000 classes, corresponding to the ImageNet dataset.[47] The architecture’s design, which includes convolutional layers with varying kernel sizes, max-pooling layers, and fully connected layers, significantly reduces computational complexity while maintaining high classification Acc.[45] Overall, AlexNet’s innovative design and techniques laid the foundation for modern DL architectures in computer vision.[48]

2.3.4. MobileNet-V3

MobileNet-V3, a lightweight CNN architecture developed by Google, is optimized for mobile and embedded devices.[49] As the 3rd iteration of the MobileNet series, MobileNet-V3 enhances computational efficiency and reduces parameter counts while maintaining high classification Acc.[50] It employs depthwise separable convolution, decomposing standard convolution into depthwise convolution and pointwise convolution.[51] MobileNet-V3 also introduces width and resolution multipliers to adjust model complexity and performance.[52] The architecture utilizes the ReLU6 activation function to improve numerical stability in low-Pre computations.[53] Comprising multiple depthwise separable convolution blocks, MobileNet-V3 is well-suited for image classification and object detection on resource-constrained devices.[53] In OM otoscopic image classification, MobileNet-V3 demonstrates high computational efficiency and Acc, making it ideal for deployment in mobile or remote healthcare settings.[54]

2.3.5. VGGNet-19

VGGNet-19, a deep CNN proposed by the Visual Geometry Group at the University of Oxford, is one of the deepest variants in the VGG series.[41] It gained prominence during the 2014 ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge for its ability to significantly improve image classification Acc.[41] The architecture is characterized by its depth and uniform convolutional layer design, utilizing multiple stacked 3 × 3 convolutional kernels instead of larger kernels.[55] This design reduces the number of parameters while enhancing the network’s nonlinear expressive power. VGGNet-19 consists of 19 layers, including 16 convolutional layers and 3 fully connected layers.[56] Each convolutional layer is followed by a ReLU activation function, and 2 × 2 max-pooling layers are used after each convolutional block to reduce feature map dimensions, thereby lowering computational complexity and preventing overfitting.[57] The final layers include 2 fully connected layers with 4096 neurons each and a classification layer with 1000 neurons. In OM otoscopic image classification, VGGNet-19 demonstrates superior performance due to its deep architecture and robust feature extraction capabilities, particularly in identifying complex pathological changes.[58]

2.4. Experimental environment configuration

The experiments were conducted in the following hardware and software environments. The hardware configuration included an AMD EPYC 9754 128-Core Processor, NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090D (24 GB) and 60 GB RAM. The software environment was based on Ubuntu 22.04, and the models were developed and trained using Python 3.8 and PyTorch 2.5.1. All code was written and debugged in the Visual Studio Code integrated development environment. Additionally, data visualization was performed using the Matplotlib and Seaborn libraries.

2.5. Model training process

The model training process was conducted using the PyTorch DL framework. To ensure fairness, all models were initialized with pre-trained weights from the ImageNet dataset and modified by replacing the final fully connected layer to accommodate the 4-class classification task. The training process consisted of 400 epochs with a batch size of 32, where all models were optimized using the Stochastic Gradient Descent optimizer (learning rate = 0.001, momentum = 0.9, weight decay = 5e-4) with hyperparameters tuned via grid search on the validation set (20% of training data). To prevent overfitting, early stopping was implemented with a patience value of 10, meaning that training would halt if the validation Acc did not improve for 10 consecutive epochs. Additionally, dropout regularization was applied in the fully connected layers (ResNet excluded) to further enhance the model’s generalization capability. During the training process, the model’s performance on both the training and validation sets was monitored using the Acc metric. After each training epoch, the model’s performance on the validation set was evaluated. If the validation Acc of the current epoch exceeded the previously recorded best Acc, the model’s weights were saved.

After training, the model with the best validation performance was selected and evaluated on an independent test set to assess its generalization capability. This ensured an unbiased evaluation of the model’s ability to classify unseen data.

2.6. Performance metrics

To evaluate the performance of the model in multi-class classification tasks, this study employs a variety of commonly used performance metrics, including accuracy (Acc), sensitivity (Sen), specificity (Spe), precision (Pre), and F1 score. Acc measures the proportion of correctly classified samples relative to the total number of samples, providing an overall assessment of model performance.[59] It is particularly suitable for datasets with balanced class distributions. However, in imbalanced datasets, Acc may be biased as it tends to favor the majority class.[59] Sen, also referred to as recall, evaluates the model’s ability to correctly identify positive instances.[59] In medical diagnostics, Sen is of paramount importance, as false negatives (FN) can lead to severe consequences. High Sen indicates that the model effectively minimizes missed diagnoses.[59] Spe assesses the model’s ability to correctly identify negative instances.[60] High Spe ensures that the model does not misclassify healthy cases as diseased, thereby reducing the likelihood of false positives.[60] Pre measures the proportion of true positive instances among all instances predicted as positive.[61] Pre is particularly critical in scenarios where the cost of false positives is high, such as in medical diagnostics, where misdiagnosis may result in unnecessary treatments.[61] The F1 score, as the harmonic mean of Pre and Sen, balances the 2 metrics and is especially useful in imbalanced datasets, providing a comprehensive evaluation of the model’s ability to identify positive instances.[62]

These metrics are derived from the values of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and FN calculated using the confusion matrix. Given the multi-class nature of this study, TP, TN, FP, and FN values are computed separately for each class. Specifically, TP represents the number of correctly classified samples for a given class, FP denotes the number of samples from other classes misclassified as the given class, TN represents the number of correctly classified samples from other classes, and FN indicates the number of samples from the given class misclassified as other classes.[62] Below are the formulas for each metric and their macro-averaging calculations for multi-class classification. 3. Results

In this study, we collected a dataset of 819 otoscopic images to evaluate the classification performance of 5 CNN models, with 491 images allocated for training, 164 for validation, and 164 for testing. The results revealed that the performance of these models varied across different conditions. As shown in Table 2, the VGGNet-19 model achieved an overall Acc of 94.51%, with particularly notable performance in identifying CSOM. In contrast, the AlexNet model exhibited a lower overall Acc of 88.41%, with an Acc of only 90.24% in the AOM category. The other models demonstrated comparable accuracies, with GoogLeNet achieving an Acc of 99.39% in both the normal and CSOM categories. However, the key challenge in classification problems lies in the model’s predictive ability for samples outside the training set and the evaluation of the Acc of these predictions when the model exhibits uncertainty. Confidence intervals are an effective method for measuring estimation uncertainty.[62] To assess the model’s reliability, we calculate confidence intervals by training the model multiple times and analyzing the distribution of its predictions on test data. In this study, each model was trained independently 5 times, and the mean Acc, standard deviation, as well as the upper and lower limits of the 95% confidence interval were computed. As shown in Table 3, the differences between the upper and lower limits of the confidence intervals for all models remain within a small range, indicating that the uncertainty of all models is at a low level. These findings underscore the importance of selecting an appropriate model architecture for specific diagnostic tasks, with VGGNet-19 proving to be a strong candidate for the classification of otoscopic images.

4. Discussion

Accurate diagnosis of OM is crucial for effective clinical management, especially in resource-limited settings where experienced clinicians and advanced diagnostic tools are scarce.[21] Traditional diagnostic methods, which rely heavily on the experience and subjective judgment of physicians, often result in inconsistent diagnostic outcomes and higher rates of misdiagnosis.[64] The otoscopic images of OM exhibit diverse pathological features: the normal TM typically appears translucent, grayish-white, with clear landmarks and a light cone; AOM often shows a congested and swollen TM, possibly with fluid levels or bubbles; OME is characterized by a retracted TM, potentially with fluid levels or bubbles, but the membrane is usually not red; CSOM may display a perforated TM with purulent discharge.[20,65] These subtle pathological changes pose higher demands for diagnostic Acc.

With the continuous development of DL, various CNN models have been employed for the automated diagnosis of TM images and have demonstrated remarkable performance.[22,62,66,67] However, the performance of these models varies significantly depending on the specific characteristics of the dataset and the task at hand. Particularly, image classification studies on CSOM are rare since CSOM can show variable lesions and anatomical distortion, making feature selection more complicated.[20] While some studies have reported promising results, there is still a lack of comprehensive comparisons between these models in the context of OM diagnosis.[48]

This study supplements research in this area by systematically evaluating the performance of 5 widely-used CNN models with distinct architectures in classifying OM using an otoscopic image dataset. All models are implemented in their original architectures, with pre-trained weights applied to save training time and enhance the classification performance. This study not only provides a robust solution for OM diagnosis but also highlights the potential of fine-tuned DL models in addressing the challenges of medical image classification.

In the present study, VGGNet-19 achieved the highest Acc rate at 94.51%, with particularly notable performance in identifying CSOM. This finding underscores VGGNet-19’s proficiency in recognizing complex pathological alterations, especially when processing images of CSOM. The deep architecture of VGGNet-19, coupled with its uniform 3 × 3 convolutional layer design, enables the extraction of a rich and diverse set of features, which is crucial for its superior performance in the intricate classification tasks of OM.[58,68] The depth of VGGNet-19’s architecture allows for the progressive extraction of features from low level to high level, thereby enhancing its ability to identify complex pathological changes.[68] This structure not only captures obvious pathological features, such as the clear landmarks of a normal TM and the perforations in CSOM, but also incrementally extracts more subtle features, such as the congestion and swelling associated with AOM.[58] In contrast, AlexNet exhibited a lower overall Acc rate of 88.41%, with an Acc of only 90.24% in the AOM category. This discrepancy may highlight limitations in AlexNet’s ability to detect AOM. Its shallower network structure and larger convolutional kernels may lack the flexibility required to effectively handle complex pathological features, especially when distinguishing the subtle changes associated with AOM.[48]

GoogLeNet, leveraging its innovative inception module, demonstrates remarkable proficiency in extracting multi-scale features, achieving an Acc of 99.39% in both normal TM and CSOM categories. This high Acc underscores its capability to effectively capture the distinct landmarks of normal TM and the perforation characteristics associated with CSOM. Nevertheless, in the AOM and OME categories, GoogLeNet shows a modest decrease in performance relative to its performance in other classifications. This discrepancy may stem from the inherent limitations of its complex inception module when confronted with more nuanced pathological changes.[48] The pathological features of AOM and OME are subtle and necessitate the model’s ability to discern finer details, a requirement that GoogLeNet’s multi-scale feature extraction may not fully meet due to its relative inflexibility in such contexts.

ResNet-18 effectively addresses the vanishing gradient problem in deep networks through its residual learning framework, resulting in satisfactory performance in the classification of OM. The residual connections in ResNet-18 facilitate robust gradient propagation, stabilizing the training process and enabling the network to adeptly capture the subtle pathological features of AOM and OME.

MobileNet-V3, with its depthwise separable convolutions and optimized network structure, significantly enhances computational efficiency while maintaining a high classification Acc. It demonstrates balanced performance in terms of Spe (96.91%) and Sen (90.03%). However, MobileNet-V3’s performance in handling complex pathological features is slightly inferior to that of VGGNet-19 and ResNet-18. This may be attributed to the limitations of its lightweight design in terms of feature extraction richness.[54] Although the depthwise separable convolutions in MobileNet-V3 improve computational efficiency, they may not be as flexible as the deep convolutional layers in VGGNet-19 when extracting complex features.

To further elucidate the learning dynamics and generalization capabilities of these models, we constructed curves for training and validation Acc/loss. VGGNet-19 and ResNet-18 demonstrated superior performance, characterized by higher Acc and stability in these curves. This suggests that their architectures, particularly the deep layers and residual connections, facilitate more efficient learning and better generalization to unseen data.[66] In contrast, GoogLeNet and MobileNet-V3, while still performing adequately, exhibited slightly less robust generalization, likely due to the complexity of their respective Inception modules and depthwise separable convolutions, which may introduce some limitations in capturing finer details.[48] AlexNet’s lower validation Acc and higher loss highlight potential challenges in generalizing to new data, possibly attributed to its relatively shallower architecture and larger convolutional kernels that may not capture complex features as effectively.[48]

In terms of discriminative abilities, assessed via ROC curves, VGGNet-19 and GoogLeNet showed strong performance, with VGGNet-19 achieving the highest AUC value. This indicates that VGGNet-19’s deep and uniform convolutional layers are particularly adept at distinguishing between different pathological conditions, thereby minimizing false positives. ResNet-18 and MobileNet-V3 exhibited moderate AUC values, suggesting that while they can effectively classify images, they may face some challenges in identifying more nuanced pathological features. AlexNet’s lower AUC value, coupled with its lower Sen and Pre metrics, underscores potential limitations in its ability to accurately identify positive cases and reduce false positives, likely due to its architectural constraints. Overall, the results from the ROC curves further confirm the reliability and Acc of VGGNet-19 in the automated diagnosis of OM.

The findings of this study hold significant clinical implications. The otoscopic images of OM exhibit a wide range of pathological features that traditional diagnostic methods struggle to accurately identify. DL technology, particularly the VGGNet-19 model, offers a novel approach to automated diagnosis. The high Acc and robust performance of VGGNet-19 suggest that it can serve as a reliable and efficient diagnostic tool, especially in resource-limited settings. However, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the class imbalance in our dataset was not explicitly addressed during model training. Although macro-averaged metrics were reported to provide a balanced view, the inherent bias toward majority classes remains a limitation. Future work should incorporate strategies to handle imbalanced data, such as generative adversarial networks for synthetic sample generation or cost-sensitive learning. Second, all data in this study were obtained from a single medical center. Although strict quality control measures were implemented to ensure image consistency, variations in otoscope device models across different institutions and potential differences in patient population characteristics may exist. This single-center data limitation could affect the model’s generalizability in other clinical settings. Subsequent multicenter studies incorporating more diverse equipment and population data are required to validate the model’s universal applicability. Additionally, while this study compared the performance of various CNN models, it did not explore the potential of combining these models or employing ensemble methods to further enhance diagnostic Acc. Future research could investigate the use of ensemble techniques or hybrid models to improve the performance of automated OM diagnosis systems.

5. Conclusion

OM poses significant challenges to global health, necessitating accurate and timely diagnosis. Traditional diagnostic methods, reliant on subjective interpretation of otoscopic images, often result in inconsistencies and high misdiagnosis rates. This study leverages DL to address these limitations, employing 5 CNN models to classify otoscopic images into 4 categories: normal, AOM, OME, and CSOM. Among the models evaluated, VGGNet-19 demonstrated superior performance, achieving the highest Acc (94.51%) and excelling in Sen, Spe, Pre, and F1 score. These findings underscore the potential of DL, particularly VGGNet-19, as an efficient and objective diagnostic tool for OM, especially in resource-limited settings. However, future research should focus on addressing dataset imbalances, validating models across diverse clinical environments, and exploring ensemble techniques to further enhance diagnostic Acc. This study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the integration of artificial intelligence in medical diagnostics, paving the way for improved patient outcomes.
