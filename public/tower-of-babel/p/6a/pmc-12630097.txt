Atypical presentation of leptospirosis as aseptic meningitis in an immunocompromised patient: Impact of rituximab on recovery and prolonged inflammatory response
Authors: Shifa Karatela, Swechchha Silwal, Bhargav P Patel, Julio C Mendez, Sammer M Elwasila
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeptospirosis is a zoonotic spirochaetal infection with a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from mild anicteric illness to severe systemic disease. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement, particularly aseptic meningitis, is an underrecognized and diagnostically challenging manifestation, especially when isolated from systemic signs. We report a case of a 43-year-old male with a history of CNS demyelinating disease, treated with rituximab, who presented with a one-month history of recurrent fever, headache, and painful lower extremity nodules. He also experienced unintentional weight loss. After a thorough infectious workup, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, including broad-range PCR and the Karius test, confirmed the diagnosis of leptospirosis caused by Leptospira noguchii. He was initially treated with intravenous ceftriaxone and oral doxycycline for 7 days, resulting in symptomatic improvement. However, symptoms recurred two weeks later, with repeat CSF analysis revealing persistent pleocytosis, necessitating a prolonged course of treatment. This case underscores the limitations of conventional testing in immunocompromised hosts and highlights the critical role of advanced molecular diagnostics for early and accurate detection of neuroleptospirosis. Clinicians should maintain high suspicion for leptospiral meningitis in appropriate clinical contexts to improve outcomes through timely intervention.Keywords: Leptospirosis, Neuroleptospirosis, Leptospira noguchii, Aseptic meningitis, Immunocompromised host, Broad-range PCR

Introduction

Leptospirosis, caused by pathogenic Leptospira species, is a globally prevalent zoonosis with highly variable clinical presentations. Although typically characterized by systemic manifestations such as jaundice, renal failure, and hemorrhagic complications, leptospiral infection can also involve the central nervous system (CNS), presenting as aseptic meningitis. This neuroinvasive form is often overlooked due to its nonspecific symptoms and the frequent absence of classic systemic features, particularly in the immune phase of the disease. Diagnosing leptospiral meningitis poses significant challenges given the limitations of culture and serologic tests, especially in immunocompromised individuals who may have impaired antibody production [1]. The advent of molecular diagnostic tools, such as polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays and next-generation sequencing methods, has significantly enhanced detection sensitivity for infectious diseases. We present an atypical case of relapsing leptospiral meningitis in a rituximab-treated patient who lacked classic clinical features, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and the critical role of advanced testing modalities in such complex cases.

Case presentation

A 43-year-old male with a past medical history of CNS demyelinating disease, multiple sclerosis presented with intermittent fevers, persistent headaches, and bilateral painful, erythematous, swollen nodules on his legs that developed approximately one month ago. The nodules appeared two days after fever onset, followed by a constant, non-positional headache that worsened with head movement. He also experienced intermittent vomiting, altered taste sensation, and an unintentional weight loss of 10–15 pounds over the same period.

The patient is maintained on rituximab therapy every six months, with his last dose administered three months before admission. His extensive travel history across East Asia, residential moves within multiple U.S. states, and occupational exposure through the air force increase his risk for diverse infectious exposures. Furthermore, he has been raising chickens since April 2024, adding potential zoonotic exposure to his risk profile.

The patient was previously hospitalized at an outside facility one month prior with a similar presentation. At that time, a lumbar puncture revealed 0 RBCs, 12 WBCs (11 % lymphocytes), glucose of 51 mg/dl, and protein of 39 mg/dl, with a negative meningoencephalitis panel. He was empirically treated with doxycycline for suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever and discharged on methylprednisolone, which led to temporary improvement in symptoms. However, symptoms recurred two days after completing the steroid taper. A repeat lumbar puncture revealed worsening inflammation, with 0 RBCs, 34 WBCs, a glucose level of 50 mg/dL, and a protein level of 53 mg/dL.

Following transfer to our facility, the patient underwent a comprehensive evaluation for infectious diseases. On arrival, he was febrile with a maximum temperature of 38.2 °C and hemodynamically stable but reported a persistent headache, recent dysgeusia, and numbness over the left side of his face. He also noted the appearance of tender nodular lesions on both legs that had developed a few weeks earlier. No meningeal signs were observed. Laboratory studies revealed mild normocytic anemia (hemoglobin 11 g/dL) with a normal leukocyte count. The basic metabolic panel was unremarkable, with preserved renal and hepatic function. Serum HIV and treponemal antibody tests were negative, and CSF cultures remained sterile. Imaging, including CT of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis, as well as brain MRI, showed no acute abnormalities. PET/CT was largely non-diagnostic, except for mild hypermetabolic activity in both lower extremities. MRI of the spine revealed mild enhancement of the L4–L5 cauda equina nerve roots

A third lumbar puncture, performed about a week later, showed a further increase in inflammation with 40 nucleated cells (12 % neutrophils), glucose of 40 mg/dl, protein of 49 mg/dl, and no malignant cells. The progressively rising CSF white blood cell count over the course of a month raised ongoing concern for an underlying infectious or inflammatory etiology.

A comprehensive multidisciplinary team, including infectious disease, neurology, dermatology, rheumatology, and Hematology/oncology specialists, was involved in the patient’s evaluation. Dermatology assessed the bilateral leg nodules and suspected erythema nodosum (EN), likely reactive to a recent infection. As the lesions were in a late, asymptomatic stage, no active treatment was initiated; however, supportive measures such as rest, leg elevation, NSAIDs, and topical corticosteroids were advised for future symptomatic episodes. To rule out an occult malignancy, a bone marrow biopsy was performed, which showed no clonal abnormalities. Further diagnostic workup, including genetic testing, an autoinflammatory syndrome panel, and ongoing surveillance for systemic or rheumatologic causes of his fever of unknown origin, returned negative results. Despite an extensive multidisciplinary evaluation, no definitive evidence of an active infection was identified, and the patient remained clinically stable. In the absence of a clear infectious source and without signs of clinical deterioration, empiric antibiotic therapy was deferred, as there was no strong indication for antimicrobial treatment at that time. Nevertheless, atypical infections remained part of the differential diagnosis as further diagnostic investigations continued

An extensive infectious disease workup was conducted, including PCR testing for non-tuberculous mycobacteria, brucella, bartonella, and an arboviral panel, along with serologic testing for parasitic infections such as strongyloidiasis, Schistosoma, and Toxoplasma (IgG/IgM). Additional evaluations included fungal diagnostics, tuberculosis screening, a venous Doppler ultrasound, and a PET scan. To investigate potential elusive pathogens, advanced testing such as a meningoencephalitis panel, fungal and acid-fast bacilli (AFB) blood cultures, the Karius microbial cell-free DNA test, and broad-range metagenomic sequencing were also pursued.

Broad-range bacterial PCR testing of the CSF detected Leptospira DNA, confirming the diagnosis of leptospirosis. The patient was immediately started on intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g every 12 h and doxycycline 100 mg IV every 12 h. The following day, the Karius™ microbial cell-free DNA test, performed on plasma, corroborated the diagnosis by identifying Leptospira noguchii, a species associated with pretibial skin lesions, findings that aligned with the patient’s bilateral lower extremity nodules. Notably, Leptospira IgM remained negative, and the patient was anicteric, with normal liver function tests and no thrombocytopenia—an atypical presentation for leptospirosis.

The patient remained alert, afebrile, and hemodynamically stable during his initial antimicrobial treatment, with improvement in his skin lesions; a brief episode of fever and fatigue was attributed to a suspected Jarisch-Herxheimer-like reaction. He completed a 7-day course of intravenous ceftriaxone and oral doxycycline without complications, but approximately two weeks later, his symptoms recurred, including fever and headache. A repeat lumbar puncture revealed persistent pleocytosis, and in consultation with a leptospirosis expert at the CDC, an extended 3-week course of intravenous ceftriaxone and oral doxycycline was initiated due to his immunocompromised state from prior rituximab therapy. At follow-up, the patient reported marked clinical improvement with complete resolution of symptoms after completing the extended treatment.

Discussion

Leptospiras are tightly coiled, thin spirochetes (∼0.1 µm by 6–20 µm) with hooked ends and a Gram-negative-like double membrane. They can be visualized by dark-field microscopy or immunofluorescence, and stained with silver stains (e.g., Warthin-Starry) or carbol fuchsin. The organisms are maintained in nature by chronic renal infection in reservoir hosts, most importantly rodents, cattle, pigs, and dogs, leading to urinary shedding and environmental contamination of soil and water, which underlies the zoonotic transmission to humans [2].

Leptospirosis has a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from mild, self-limited febrile illness to severe icteric disease with renal failure, hepatic dysfunction, and CNS involvement. It typically follows two phases: an acute leptospiremic phase, when the pathogen is detectable in blood, and an immune phase, when tissue burden declines but immune–mediated organ damage predominates [3]. The immune phase, often characterized by anicteric leptospirosis, may present with aseptic meningitis, a less commonly recognized manifestation. This form involves meningeal irritation and CSF findings indicative of sterile inflammation, resembling viral meningitis [1]. In our patient, the absence of jaundice, normal liver enzyme levels, and a lack of thrombocytopenia suggested that he was in the immune phase rather than the acute symptomatic phase of the disease.

According to Comia et al., individuals receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) had a higher risk of developing leptospirosis compared to HIV-uninfected individuals, with co-infection prevalence reported between 4.4 % and 33 %. Immunocompromise may contribute to this increased susceptibility. Notably, our patient was also immunosuppressed due to rituximab therapy [4].

Meningeal involvement in leptospiral infection is common, albeit typically mild and frequently underrecognized. However, isolated CNS manifestations, such as aseptic meningitis without systemic features, are rarer and may be overlooked in clinical practice. A case reported by Torre et al. described acute aseptic meningitis caused by Leptospira australis serovar Bratislava, highlighting the organism's capacity to cause isolated CNS disease [5].

Although considered an uncommon manifestation, growing evidence suggests that leptospiral meningitis may be underdiagnosed and underreported, contributing to a paradoxical perception of rarity. For example, a study from Sudan found that 6 % (9 of 153) of CSF samples from children with meningitis tested positive for pathogenic leptospiral DNA, implicating Leptospira as a potential etiologic agent in that population [6].

Similarly, in a Pediatric cohort from Salvador, Bahia, Brazil, enteroviruses were the most frequent etiology of aseptic meningitis syndrome (AMS), yet leptospirosis was also identified as a significant contributor, reinforcing the need to include it in the differential diagnosis [7]. Prasad et al. highlight the diagnostic challenges, noting that only two prior cases of late-onset leptospiral meningitis had been documented in the literature previously, and suggest that this form may be underrecognized due to limitations in routine microbiological diagnostic tools [8].

While viral pathogens remain the most frequently identified causes of aseptic meningitis, leptospirosis represents a clinically relevant and often overlooked alternative, particularly in endemic regions or among individuals with occupational or environmental exposure risks. Increased clinical suspicion and expanded diagnostic testing are crucial for improving recognition and accurately defining the true prevalence of leptospiral meningitis.

Multiple case reports and regional studies have further reinforced leptospirosis as a neglected but important cause of CNS involvement: A British outbreak of benign anicteric leptospirosis among four dairy farm workers featured prominent aseptic meningitis, with serology linking the outbreak to Leptospira interrogans (serogroup Hebdomadis) from cattle [9]. Data from Italy (1973–76) revealed a remarkable frequency of meningeal syndromes in leptospirosis, especially among young people, suggesting a neurotropic tendency of various leptospiral serotypes [10]. In the Philippines, five of one hundred hospitalized patients with aseptic meningoencephalitis tested positive for leptospirosis by MAT, though none were suspected to have the infection upon admission. These cases, mostly presenting during the second week of illness and without typical signs like jaundice or renal dysfunction, point to leptospirosis as a significant but overlooked etiology in tropical settings [11]. Bandar et al. described two cases of neuroleptospirosis confirmed by positive serology, both of whom fully recovered after appropriate management [12].

Confirmation of leptospiral meningitis remains difficult due to the challenges associated with isolating the organism and limitations of serologic testing. Diagnosis primarily relies on clinical suspicion supported by CSF analysis, serology (CSF, blood, and urine IgM), microscopic agglutination test (MAT), culture, and PCR assays [13].

Culture, although definitive, is notoriously insensitive and time-consuming, with a study reporting zero CSF culture positivity in suspected neuroleptospirosis cases. MAT, while considered the reference serological test, is limited by poor sensitivity in the early stage and a delayed antibody response, making it unsuitable for acute diagnosis. Its primary utility lies in epidemiological investigations, where it helps identify infecting serovars and track outbreak patterns [14].

ELISA-based IgM detection is more practical in early disease but carries a risk of false positives and false negatives due to long IgM persistence, which complicates the interpretation in endemic settings or relapsing disease. Furthermore, both MAT and ELISA can be confounded in immunocompromised individuals who may not mount a reliable antibody response [15].

Although the MAT remains valuable for confirmatory and epidemiological purposes, and ELISA assists with early screening, PCR has emerged as the most reliable early diagnostic tool for leptospiral meningitis, particularly when serologic results are inconclusive or in immunocompromised individuals. Ramero et al. demonstrated that PCR achieved an 89.5 % sensitivity in MAT-confirmed cases, outperforming both MAT and ELISA-IgM in early detection [16]. These findings underscore PCR’s superior sensitivity and earlier detection window compared to traditional methods. Therefore, a multimodal diagnostic strategy, integrating clinical suspicion, serial cerebrospinal fluid analyses, serologic testing, and molecular diagnostics, is essential for the timely diagnosis and management of neuroleptospirosis.

In a case described by Bhatt et al., neuroleptospirosis was diagnosed using the modified Faine’s criteria after PCR testing returned negative, possibly due in part to ceftriaxone therapy administered a week earlier, despite PCR’s high sensitivity. While PCR is generally a reliable method for detecting leptospirosis, its accuracy can be reduced by factors such as delayed hospital presentation, prior antibiotic use, and the specific PCR methodology, which may lead to false-negative results [17].

Prasad et al. describe a relapsing aseptic meningitis case in a rituximab-treated patient diagnosed solely via CSF PCR positivity, emphasizing molecular diagnostics' utility [8]. In contrast, our case presented additional complexity: the patient lacked clinical features of leptospirosis, and both leptospiral IgM and PCR from CSF were initially negative, underscoring limitations even of advanced diagnostic modalities.

Rituximab, a chimeric monoclonal antibody targeting CD20-positive B cells, is widely used to treat hematologic malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and transplant rejection. While effective, it causes prolonged B-cell depletion, impairing humoral immunity and altering inflammatory responses. This immunosuppression can result in atypical infection presentations and prolonged inflammatory states, often necessitating extended therapy. Additionally, rituximab-induced lymphocytopenia and hypogammaglobulinemia may explain the negative IgM serology observed in our patient, due to insufficient antibody production.

Conclusion

Leptospiral meningitis remains an underrecognized cause of aseptic meningitis, particularly in immunocompromised patients and those lacking typical systemic signs. Conventional diagnostic approaches, including serology and culture, often fail to detect the infection early or accurately in such contexts. Our case illustrates the pivotal role of advanced molecular diagnostics, broad-range PCR, and microbial cell-free DNA testing in identifying Leptospira species when routine methods yield negative results. Increased clinician awareness and incorporation of these sensitive diagnostic tools are essential to improve early detection, guide appropriate antimicrobial therapy, and prevent potentially serious complications. Further research is warranted to establish standardized diagnostic algorithms for neuroleptospirosis, especially in patients with altered immune status.
