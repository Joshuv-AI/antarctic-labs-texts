Development and preclinical evaluation of KDTV001, an adenovirus 5-vectored trivalent HPV therapeutic vaccine targeting HPV16/18/52
Authors: Yilin Dai, Fanwei Huang, Xiaojiao Li, Wenqing Wu, Ruoyu Duan, Xi Li, Xiaoyuan Huang, Qinglei Gao, Ding Ma, Rui Wei, Fei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundPersistent high-risk HPV infections (e.g., HPV16/18/52) are directly linked to cervical cancer development, thus necessitating the development of effective multi-target therapeutic vaccines.MethodsWe developed KDTV001, a non-replicating adenovirus 5-vectored trivalent vaccine encoding engineered E6/E7 oncoproteins from HPV16/18/52. Its immunogenicity was evaluated in C57BL/6, CD1, HLA-transgenic mice, SD rats, and a human DC-T cell co-culture system. Assessments included IFN-γ ELISpot, flow cytometry, tumour challenge/rechallenge, single-cell RNA/TCR sequencing (scRNA/TCR-seq) of lymphocytes, and immune cell depletion.FindingsKDTV001 demonstrated robust preclinical efficacy across multiple models, eliciting potent immune and cross-reactive responses in immunocompetent and HLA-transgenic mice. scRNA/TCR-seq revealed KDTV001 remodelled the tumour microenvironment, increasing cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and promoting T cell clonal expansion and differentiation. A single dose induced complete TC-1 tumour regression in 60–80% of mice with durable protection (>200 days). Optimising the booster immunisation schedule mitigated the impact of pre-existing vector immunity on the immunogenicity of KDTV001. Human relevance was confirmed through antigen-specific T-cell activation and expansion in HLA-A transgenic mice and PBMC co-cultures.InterpretationKDTV001, the adenovirus 5-vectored trivalent therapeutic vaccine targeting HPV16/18/52, demonstrates potent immunogenicity, significant antitumour efficacy, and long-term protective immunity across diverse preclinical models, paving the way for clinical translation in treating HPV-associated malignancies.FundingThis study was funded by the Noncommunicable Chronic Diseases-National Science and Technology Major Project of China (Nos.2025ZD0544101), the Hubei Provincial Science and Technology Major Project of China (Nos.2023BCA004), and the National Clinical Research Center for Gynaecological Diseases Special Fund of China (Nos.2025LCPT01).

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Cervical cancer is a prevalent gynaecological malignancy that poses a grave threat to women's health and life, arising from persistent infection with high-risk types of human papillomavirus (HPV). Presently, there is an absence of specific therapeutic drugs for HPV infection. The precancerous lesions or cancer it causes may necessitate surgical intervention, which can potentially result in irreversible damage to the patient's fertility. The persistent expression of HPV E6 and E7 proteins in infected cells and associated malignant cells provides clear targets for the immune system. Advancements in the field of immunotherapy have given rise to therapeutic avenues for the treatment of HPV infection, cervical precancerous lesions, and cervical cancer. The development of therapeutic HPV vaccines is a crucial component of this approach. Further research is required to enhance the immunogenicity of these vaccines, broaden their target population, and improve their efficacy.

Added value of this study

The present study successfully developed the trivalent non-replicating human adenovirus type 5 therapeutic vaccine, KDTV001, targeting HPV types 16, 18, and 52. KDTV001 provided coverage of the HPV52 subtype, which is prevalent in Asia. Furthermore, the detection of cross-immunity against other high-risk HPV types suggested that the design of multivalent vaccines broadened coverage against high-risk HPV subtypes. Utilising state-of-the-art technologies such as scRNA/TCR-seq, the mechanism by which it remodelled the tumour immune microenvironment and promoted T-cell clonal expansion and differentiation was elucidated. The study's findings also substantiated the hypothesis that the treatment had the capacity to elicit long-lasting immune memory and impede the reappearance of the condition. Furthermore, the constraints imposed by pre-existing antibodies against adenovirus vectors were overcome by optimising the immunisation schedule. The validation of the drug in human HLA transgenic mouse models and human-derived DC-T cell co-culture systems further reinforced its human applicability and clinical predictive value, comprehensively highlighting KDTV001's clinical translation prospects as a therapeutic HPV vaccine.

Implications of all the available evidence

The present study demonstrated that KDTV001, as the trivalent therapeutic vaccine targeting HPV16/18/52, exhibited exceptional immunogenicity and antitumour potential, possessing clear translational medical value. It demonstrated that KDTV001 efficiently induced antigen-specific cellular immune responses and cross-protective immunity. In addition, it has been shown to remodel the tumour microenvironment, enhance CTL function, and establish long-lasting immune memory. Collectively, these effects resulted in a significant inhibition of tumour progression and recurrence. Furthermore, the study optimised the immunisation strategy to mitigate the adverse effects of pre-existing vector immunity, thereby enhancing the vaccine's immunogenic efficacy. The validation of specific epitopes and responses through multiple human HLA transgenic mouse models and human immune cell co-culture systems indicated that KDTV001 overcame the limitations imposed by MHC polymorphism in human populations, covering a broader range of HPV-infected individuals. This provides robust evidence for clinical translation of KDTV001, supporting its advancement into clinical trials and offering potential to improve treatment outcomes for HPV-related malignant lesions.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is one of the major malignant tumours that pose a threat to women's health worldwide. According to the Global Cancer Observatory data from 185 countries, there were nearly 660,000 new cases of cervical cancer worldwide, accounting for 3.3% of the total global cancer cases in 2022.1 Nearly 95% of cervical cancers are associated with HPV infection. Additionally, HPV also contributes to 88% of anal cancers, 31% of oropharyngeal cancers, 78% of vaginal cancers, 25% of vulvar cancers, and 50% of penile cancers globally.2,3

For HPV-related cervical diseases, the management of high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion (HSIL) is of paramount importance in secondary prevention. The current standard treatment involves cervical excision therapy, which effectively reduces the risk of cervical cancer development. Despite the high effectiveness of HPV prophylactic vaccines in preventing HPV infections, they are not able to treat or clear established HPV infections and HPV-associated lesions.4 Therefore, the development of therapeutic vaccines holds great promise for the treatment of HSIL.

Currently, several therapeutic HPV vaccines are in preclinical and clinical trials, including live vector, protein or peptide, nucleic acid, and cell-based vaccines.5 The majority of these vaccines target HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 with the aim to deliver E6 and E7 antigens to antigen presenting cells (APCs) in order to activate cytotoxic T cell-mediated cellular immune responses to clear infected viruses and block precancerous lesions. VGX-3100 is a combination of two plasmids encoding optimised HPV16 and 18 E6 and E7 antigens that has completed Phase IIb clinical trials.6 China exhibits a high burden of HPV-related diseases. In this nationwide HPV screening dataset, HPV52 (4.7%), HPV16 (3.4%), and HPV58 (2.4%) emerged as the predominant high-risk genotypes, with HPV16 and HPV52 being particularly dominant in HSIL.7,8 Approximately 25% of HPV infections in females involved multiple genotypes, of which HPV-16/52 co-infection was the most prevalent combination.8, 9, 10 Despite this epidemiological profile, current therapeutic HPV vaccines in clinical trials remain largely focused on HPV16 or HPV18, with few targeting other high-risk types, especially HPV52.6,11,12 Furthermore, a systematic evaluation of the immunogenicity of HPV52 E6 and E7 antigens remains critically understudied. It is noteworthy that no therapeutic HPV vaccine candidates incorporating HPV52 have progressed to clinical trials in China to date.13 Therefore, the development of therapeutic HPV vaccines targeting multiple genotypes is equally important for improving the cure rate of HSIL.

Recently, non-replicating adenoviral vectors (Ad) have regained significant attention as carriers for COVID-19 vaccines.14,15 The advantages of non-replicating adenoviral vectors include high-titre growth capacity, ease of manipulation, lack of integration into the human genome, potent immunogenicity, and feasibility for large-scale clinical production.2,16 Extensive clinical data have demonstrated the immense potential and safety of Ad vectors as a platform for vaccines against emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases.17

In this study, we developed KDTV001, a trivalent therapeutic HPV vaccine targeting HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52. A comprehensive preclinical evaluation was conducted to assess its immunogenicity, antitumour efficacy, and immune activation mechanisms. Furthermore, a systematic investigation was conducted into critical clinical parameters, including administration routes, dosage regimens, immunisation schedules, and the impact of pre-existing vector immunity. This investigation yielded comprehensive data that provides the foundation for future clinical trials and offers a non-invasive treatment option for patients with HSIL with single or multiple HPV16/18/52-positive status.

Methods

Ethical approval

All animal experiments were conducted with the approval of the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJH-202212037). The collection of human blood samples was conducted with the informed consent of volunteers and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (TJ-IRB202411098).

Animals

C57BL/6 mice (RRID:IMSR_JAX:000664), CD-1 mice (RRID:IMSR_CRL:022), and Sprague–Dawley (SD) rat (RRID:MGI:5651135) were purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. B-HLA-A2·1/11·1/24·2 mice were purchased from Biocytogen Pharmaceuticals Co., Ltd.18 In these strains, the mouse B2M gene (exon 1 to exon 3) was replaced with a chimeric sequence comprising the human B2M coding sequence (CDS) and the human HLA-A∗0201/1101/2402 gene—including leader sequence, α1 and α2 domains ligated to a fragment of the murine H-2Db gene containing the α3, transmembrane and cytoplasmic domains. All mice were female, 6–8 weeks old, and housed in the specific pathogen free (SPF) environment at the Laboratory Animal Center of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology. All mouse studies were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital.

Cell culture

HEK293 cells (RRID:CVCL_0045), BEAS-2B cells (RRID:CVCL_0168) and TC-1 cells (RRID:CVCL_4699) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS, Siji Green) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Servicebio) at 37 °C, and 5% carbon dioxide (CO2). HEK293 cells and BEAS-2B cells were purchased from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). TC-1 cells were purchased from Warner Bio (Wuhan, China).

Peptide pools

The peptide pools of HPV 16/18/31/33/39/45/51/52/56/58/59/68 E6 and E7 antigens were constructed, and each scanning peptide pool consisted of a series of peptides of 15 amino acids in length with an overlap of 8 amino acids (see in Supplementary Materials, Table S4). The synthesis of these peptides was entrusted to Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co., Ltd., and the resulting peptides exhibited a purity of at least 95%.

Generation and titration of adenovirus

In summary, the HPV16 E6/E7, HPV16/18 E6/E7, and HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 fusion protein genes were introduced into the shuttle vectors pShuttle-CMV (RRID:Addgene_16403). This resulted in the creation of pShuttle-CMV-HPV E6/E7. The four shuttle vectors (pShuttle-CMV and Three types of pShuttle-CMV-HPV E6/E7) were then co-transfected with Padeasy-1 (a replication-defective adenovirus 5 (Ad5) vector genome containing an E1/E3 deletion) plasmid (RRID:Addgene_16400) within BJ5183 (RRID:Addgene_16398) for homologous recombination, respectively, to obtain Pad-CMV (-HPV). The restriction digestion of the recombinant adenoviral DNA with the PacI restriction endonuclease was then performed to yield the linear DNA. This linear DNA was subsequently introduced into HEK293 cells for adenovirus assembly. Subsequent amplification of all adenoviruses was performed in HEK293, and the adenoviral vectors were purified by CsCl density gradient ultracentrifugation. Thereafter, the adenoviruses were exchanged into a 10 mM Tris-Base (PH 8.0), 2 mM MgCl2, 5% sucrose buffer using dialysis. Adenoviral titres were subsequently measured using the Adeno-X Rapid Titre Kit (Clontech, 632250).

Adenovirus transfection and Western blot

BEAS-2B cells were transfected with the indicated adenovirus at multiplicity of infection (MOI) = 20. Cells were harvested 48 h after transfection, and lysed on ice for 40 min by WB (IP) Lysis Buffer (Abbkine, BMP2020). Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration in the supernatant was determined by staining with Coomassie brilliant blue. The supernatant was added loading buffer (Dakewe, 801501X) and boiled for 15 min. Samples containing 25 μg of protein underwent 10% SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis and were subsequently transferred onto the PVDF membranes. The PVDF membranes were blocked with BSA, and incubated with primary antibodys overnight at 4 °C. The primary antibodys included anti-p53 antibody (Santa Cruz, sc126), anti-Rb antibody (Abcam, ab181616), anti-HPV16 E7 antibody (ThermoFisher, 28-0006), and anti-β-actin antibody (Abclonal, AC038). After incubation, PVDF membranes were washed with TBST three times for 5 min each and incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam:anti-rabbit IgG, ab205718; anti-mouse IgG, ab205719) at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, PVDF membranes were washed with TBST three times for 5 min each. The protein expression was analysed by enhanced chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad).

Vaccination procedures

Female C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks were intramuscularly injected with 1 × 108 PFU/50 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) of HPV vaccines or vector control KDTV-Ctrl. For comparing different injection methods, mice were inoculated with KDTV001 at 1 × 108 PFU via subcutaneous, intramuscular, and intravenous routes. For comparing different dosages, mice were intramuscularly inoculated with KDTV001 at 1 × 104 PFU, 1 × 105 PFU, 1 × 106 PFU, 3 × 106 PFU, 1 × 107 PFU, 3 × 107 PFU, and 1 × 108 PFU, respectively. Two weeks after the injection, the splenocytes of mice were isolated for subsequent immunisation analysis. For assessing the immunological changes after vaccination, mice were intramuscularly injected with KDTV001 at 1 × 108 PFU, and splenocytes were isolated for subsequent immunological analyses 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 5 weeks, and 6 weeks after vaccination, respectively. For facilitating a comparison of booster vaccination regimens, mice were injected intramuscularly with KDTV001 at 1 × 108 PFU, and boosted two weeks and four weeks after the first vaccination, respectively. For assessing the impact of pre-stored neutralising antibodies, mice were first intramuscularly injected with vector control KDTV-Ctrl at 1 × 108 PFU, and the same dose of KDTV001 was intramuscularly injected two weeks and four weeks after vaccination with KDTV-Ctrl, respectively. For comparing the effects of different vaccination procedures, one group of mice was given booster injections at weeks four, eight, and twelve after the prime intramuscular injection of KDTV001 with 1 × 108 PFU, and the other group was given booster injections at weeks four and twelve. For evaluating the efficacy of the vaccine in different species of animals (CD-1 mice, B-HLA-A2·1/11·1/24·2 mice, SD rats), the experimental animals were intramuscularly injected with the indicated dose of KDTV001, and a booster vaccination was given after four weeks. Splenocytes were isolated from the mice one week after the booster vaccination for subsequent immunoassays.

Tumour implantation and treatment

Female C57BL/6 mice were used to construct a subcutaneous tumour model. They were subcutaneously inoculated with 2 × 105 TC-1 cells/100 μL PBS in the right glutaeal region. When the tumour volume reached 50 mm3, one vaccine injection was given. In advanced tumour models only (see Fig. 1F and G), 1 × 106 TC-1 cells in 100 μL PBS were injected subcutaneously into the right flank region to establish a subcutaneous tumour model. Vaccination was administered once tumour volume reached 300 mm3. In the vaccine immune memory assessment experiment, 97 days after the initial tumour challenge, mice whose tumour had completely regressed after the first tumour challenge were subcutaneously inoculated a second time with the same quantity of TC-1 cells as the first time, and the tumour growth of the mice was continuously observed. At day 208, HPV16 E7 or HPV18 E6 peptide pool (25 μg of each peptide in 200 μL of PBS) was injected into the tail vein of the mice. Forty-eight hours later, the mice were euthanised, and the splenocytes were extracted to analyse the status of mouse antigenic allogeneic memory cells by flow cytometry. In the in vivo immune cell depletion assay, anti-mouse CD4 (clone GK1.5, Selleck, A2101), anti-mouse CD8 (clone 2.43, Selleck, A2102), and isotype control IgG (clone LTF-2, Selleck, A2116) were injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg the day before vaccination, and then every other day for a total of five injections. The long diameter (D) and short diameter (W) of the tumours were measured with callipers at 2- to 3-day intervals, and the tumour volume was calculated according to the formula W2 × D/2. Tissue samples preparation

Mouse spleens were isolated and immersed in 5 mL Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI)-1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Subsequent to grinding, the spleen samples were filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The erythrocytes in the splenocytes were lysed with lysis buffer (BD Biosciences, 555899). The obtained cells were then washed twice with PBS and resuspended in 5 mL of RPMI-1640 medium. The cell count was subsequently determined in preparation for ELISpot or flow cytometry. Mouse tumour-draining lymph nodes were meticulously isolated, minced, and subsequently filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer. The obtained cells were then subjected to two washes with ice bath PBS and subsequently prepared for flow cytometry. Tumour tissues from mice were separated and minced into small fragments. The tumour tissue fragments were then washed once with ice bath PBS. The Tumour Tissue Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-096-730) was used to digest the tumour tissue fragments for 40 min at 100 rpm in a constant temperature shaker at 37 °C. The enzymatic digestion reaction was terminated with an equal volume of RMPI-1640 medium containing 10% FBS. The tumour samples were subsequently filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and washed twice with ice bath PBS. Finally, tumour-infiltrating immune cells were isolated by percoll density gradient centrifugation for subsequent flow cytometry.

ELISpot assay

Cytokine-releasing cells were quantified after peptide pools stimulation by cytokine-specific ELISpot assay. The pre-coated ELISpot plates should then be washed four times with sterile PBS (200 μL/well). Condition the plates with RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS (200 μL/well) and incubate at room temperature for at least 30 min. Subsequent to erythrocyte lysis, a live cell count should be performed on freshly isolated splenocytes. Remove the medium from the ELISpot plates, add the peptide pool (2 μg/mL per peptide), then add the cell suspension (2.5 × 105 cells/well) to a final volume of 100 μL. The ELISpot plate should then be placed in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for a period of 40 h. It was imperative that the plate remained in situ during this period. The detection of spots assay was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol using the Mouse IFN-γ (3321-4AST-10, Mabtech) or IL-2 (3441-4APW-2, Mabtech) ELISpot Kit. The examination and enumeration of spots was conducted using the ImmunoSpot S6 (Cellular Technology Limited). The reaction was deemed to be positive if the mean number of spotting cells in both replicate wells was a minimum of twice that of the control well, and if a minimum of 50 spotting cells per million splenocytes were detected following background subtraction (DMSO-treated wells only).

Surface molecular staining

Single-cell suspensions containing 2 × 106 cells were stained for dead cells with Fixable Viability Stain 700 (1:100 with PBS, BD Biosciences, 564997) and incubated for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 Fc receptor blocker (BD Biosciences, 553142), diluted at 1:100 with Stain Buffer (BD Biosciences, 554656), and the incubation was carried out at 4 °C for a duration of 15 min. Subsequently, staining was performed with antibodies to surface moleculars, including anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD4, anti-CD8, anti-CD44, anti-CD62L, anti-CD127, anti-KLRG1, anti-CD11b, anti-CD11c, anti-CD86, anti-CD206, anti-F4/80, anti-ly6G/ly6C, anti-CD25, anti-CD107a, anti-PD-L1, and anti-H-2Db. All antibodies were diluted to a concentration of 1:100 with Stain buffer and the cells were incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. For tetramer staining, the relevant antibodies were initially diluted to a ratio of 1:50 in Stain buffer, and the cells were then incubated at a temperature of 4 °C for a period of 60 min prior to undergoing surface molecular staining (such as anti-CD45, anti-CD3, anti-CD4, and anti-CD8). The surface molecular staining was detected by flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, DxFLEX), and the data were processed using Flowjo 10.8.1 (RRID:SCR_008520). The specific information regarding the antibodies utilised is enumerated in Table S1. The staining protocol for each group was illustrated in the Supplementary Fig.S1, S4, and S6.

Intracellular cytokine staining (ICS)

Samples containing 3 × 106 cells were resuspended with 1 mL of RMPI-1640 complete medium and stimulated in vitro with HPV16 E7 or HPV18 E6 peptide pools (2 μg/mL per peptide) at 37 °C, 5% CO2. After 1 h of stimulation, GolgiPlug™ Protein Transport Inhibitor (Containing Brefeldin A) (1:1000, BD Biosciences, 555029) was added and the cells were incubated for an additional 6 h. Intermittent mixing of the cells was required during the incubation period. Subsequent to the incubation, centrifugation was performed, and after washing once with ice bath PBS, live/dead cells staining, Fc receptor blocking, and surface molecular staining were performed. After surface molecular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with the Transcription Factor Buffer Set (BD Biosciences, 562574), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, the cells were subjected to intracellular cytokine antibody staining, encompassing anti–IFN–γ, anti-TNF-α, anti-IL-2, anti-Granzyme B, and anti-Foxp3 antibodies, all of which were diluted with permeabilizing solution at a ratio of 1:50. The cells were then incubated for 1 h at 4 °C. The ICS was detected by flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, DxFLEX), and the data were processed using Flowjo 10.8.1. The specific information regarding the antibodies utilised was enumerated in Table S1. The staining protocol for each group was illustrated in the Supplementary Fig. S1, S4, and S6.

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data processing

scRNA-seq was performed on tumour and inguinal lymph node samples collected from three vector control mice and four KDTV001-treated mice. The “scanpy” (https://scanpy.readthedocs.io/)was used to preprocess individual sample data.19 Cells with fewer than 200 genes and a mitochondrial gene ratio exceeding 15% were excluded, and doublets were removed using “scrublet” (https://github.com/swolock/scrublet).20 The preprocessed data selected the top 2000 highly variable genes as feature genes to better capture differences between cells. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed for dimensionality reduction, followed by clustering analysis using a clustering algorithm. Based on known marker genes, each cell cluster was preliminarily annotated. Then, data from individual samples were merged and batch effects were removed using the scvi tool for further annotation. This process identified various cell populations. For major cell populations like T cells, B cells, and macrophages, specific subtypes were further subdivided to gain a deeper understanding of their dynamic changes in vaccine response.

T-cell receptor (TCR) sequencing and analysis

The “screpertoire” (https://github.com/BorchLab/scRepertoire) was used to process TCR sequencing data and identify T cell clones, which represent clonally expanded T‑cell populations sharing identical TCR sequences.21 This tool efficiently extracts key information such as TCR Complementary Determining Region 3 (CDR3) sequences from large amounts of sequencing data and quantifies and analyzes clones. Functions in screpertoire were used to analyse the expansion of CD4+ T cell and CD8+ T cell clones in the KDTV-Ctrl group and KDTV001-treated group. The clonalOccupy () function was used to display the distribution of clones in different cell types, intuitively showing the patterns and extent of clone expansion. This allows for a comparison of the intensity and activity of antigen-specific T cell responses under different treatment conditions and in different sample locations. The clonalOverlap () function was used to display the shared clones between different cell clusters, analysing the degree of T cell clone overlap between specific T cell subsets in different tissues. This reveals the differentiation and functional connections of T cells between lymph nodes and tumour tissues. Combining TCR clone information with the gene expression characteristics of cell subsets, a T cell clone network was constructed. The output of the clonalNetwork () function was used to further analyse T cell differentiation trajectories, track their possible origins and subsequent differentiation directions, and explore the dynamic evolution of tumour-specific CD8+ T cells after vaccination and their functional remodelling mechanisms in the tumour immune microenvironment.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumours isolated from homozygous mice were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded prior to tissue slicing. Slices were then deparaffinized using dewaxing solvent. For antigen repair, slices were placed in a Tris–EDTA buffer heated to 100 °C, continued to boil for 15 min, and then cooled to room temperature. Following a rinse with PBS for 15 min, the slices were exposed to 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for 20 min at room temperature, under conditions that prevented light exposure. Thereafter, the slices were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Slices were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The primary antibodies utilised included anti-CD4 antibody (Abcam, ab183685), anti-CD8 antibody (Abcam, ab217344), and anti-Granzyme B antibody (Abcam, ab255598). Subsequent immunocytochemical staining steps were performed using a rabbit two-step detection kit (ZSGB-BIO, PV-9001) according to the manufacturer's instructions. For semi-quantitative analysis, the H-score was used.

In vivo CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity assay

Splenocytes from naïve mice were resuspended in RMPI-1640 containing 10% FBS and divided into two portions. One portion was pulsed with HPV16 E7 H-2Db-restricted CD8+ T-cell epitope (5 μg/mL, HPV16 E749–57 RAHYNIVTF, Sangon) or HPV18 E6 H-2Kb-restricted CD8+ T-cell epitope22 (5 μg/mL, HPV18 E667–75 KCIDFYSRI, Sangon) at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 1 h (target cell group), and the other portion was pulsed with an equal volume of DMSO under the same conditions for 1 h (non-target cell group). Subsequent to a single wash with HBSS, the two groups of cells were labelled with varying concentrations of CFSE (ThermoFisher, 65-0850-84). The target cell group was labelled with 2.5 μM CFSE, while the non-target cell group was labelled with 0.25 μM CFSE. Following the termination of the staining and the removal of the free CFSE, the cells from the two groups were enumerated and combined in equal proportions. Subsequently, 2 × 107 of the amalgamated cells were inoculated into the immunised mice via tail vein injection. Twenty hours later, the mice were euthanised, and the spleen single cell suspension was prepared and analysed for CFSE signal by flow cytometry. The percentage of target cells killed was calculated by the ratio of CFSEhigh (target cells) to CFSElow (non-target cells) detected by flow cytometry, and the formula was as follows: 100 – 100 × (CFSEhigh ∼ KDTV001/CFSElow ∼ KDTV001)/(CFSEhigh ∼ KDTV-Ctrl/CFSElow ∼ KDTV-Ctrl).23,24

Human in vitro immunogenicity assay

Venous blood was collected from healthy donors. Subsequently, Human leucocyte antigen (HLA) typing test (Wuhan Kindstar Diagnostics Co., Ltd) was performed on the donors. Monocytes were isolated using EasySepTM Human Monocyte Isolation Kit (STEMCELL, 19359) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and promoted to differentiate into immature DCs in vitro using RMPI1640 complete medium with 500 U/mL IL-4 (R&D Systems, 6507-IL) and 1000 U/mL GM-CSF (R&D Systems, 7954-GM), and infected with KDTV-Ctrl or KDTV001 at MOI = 20 to promote DC maturation. The cells were then co-cultured with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from the same individuals. After in vitro stimulation with HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 peptide pools, the secretion of IFN-γ from the co-culture system was detected by ELISpot (Mabtech, 3420-4APT-10). Subsequent to the analysis of HLA typing results, co-cultured cells of donor 3 and donor 4 underwent tetramer staining (donor3: HLA-A∗1101∼IVCPICSQK and HLA-A∗1101∼SVYGDTLEK, donor4: HLA-A∗2402 ∼AHYNIVTF and HLA-A∗2402∼VFEFAFKDLF) to assess the quantity of HPV-specific CD8+ T cells.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1. Comparisons between the two groups were made using Mann–Whitney U test, one-way ANOVA was used for multiple comparisons, and survival analyses were performed using the Log-rank Mantel–Cox test. P < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Production and identification of KDTV001 adenovirus vaccine

To evaluate the antigen-processing capabilities and specific immune responses elicited by a trivalent vaccine incorporating an additional HPV52 antigen, three non-replicating adenoviral vectors HPV therapeutic vaccine were constructed. The trivalent vaccine KDTV001 incorporates the HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 fusion protein gene into the E1 region of the recombinant non-replicating human adenovirus type 5 vector, while the monovalent and bivalent vaccines (KDTV-HPV16 and KDTV-HPV16/18) share the same platform to incorporate the HPV16 E6/E7 and HPV16/18 E6/E7 fusion protein genes, respectively (Fig. 1A). In order to enhance the safety of the vaccine, we introduced point mutations into the HPV E6/E7 protein genes (Table S2).25, 26, 27, 28 To detect fusion protein expression, we performed in vitro transfection of the human normal lung bronchial epithelial cell line BEAS-2B at MOI of 20, followed by western blot analysis. The results demonstrated that KDTV-HPV16, KDTV-HPV16/18, and KDTV001 all expressed HPV E6/E7 fusion proteins, with protein sizes matching the expected molecular weight (Fig. 1B). p53 and Rb expression levels were measured in BEAS-2B cells that had been transfected. Compared to the vector control KDTV-Ctrl, no substantial alterations in p53 or Rb expression were detected (Fig. 1B). These vaccines were intramuscularly administered into healthy C57BL/6 mice and splenocytes were isolated from animals from all groups for analysis using IFN-γ ELISpot as described in Methods. As depicted in Fig. 1C, after stimulation with HPV16 E6 and E7 peptide pools, all four groups exhibited a relatively weak response against HPV16 E6. A robust immune response against HPV16 E7 was observed in the monovalent vaccine group (KDTV-HPV16), bivalent vaccine group (KDTV-HPV16/18), and trivalent vaccine group (KDTV001). Immune responses in the bivalent and trivalent vaccine groups exhibited slightly superior to the monovalent vaccine group, but these differences did not attain statistical significance.

To assess the therapeutic efficacy of these vaccines, the TC-1 subcutaneous tumour model was employed. Mice bearing subcutaneous TC-1 tumours with an average volume of 50 mm3 were randomly assigned to receive intramuscular injections of 1 × 108 PFU/50 μL PBS of KDTV-Ctrl, KDTV-HPV16, KDTV-HPV16/18, or KDTV001. As shown in Fig. 1D and E, the KDTV-Ctrl group exhibited continuous tumour growth, with all mice surpassing ethical limits by approximately 30 days post-tumour inoculation. Conversely, the other three vaccine groups demonstrated significant tumour suppression. Notably, three out of six mice in the KDTV-HPV16 group and one out of six mice in the KDTV-HPV16/18 and KDTV001 groups experienced tumour regrowth after initial tumour regression. Collectively, these results indicated that one dose of vaccine could markedly inhibit tumour growth and prolong mice survival. To further evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the vaccines against advanced tumours, mice were intramuscularly injected with vaccines when the TC-1 tumour reached a volume of 300 mm3. As illustrated in Fig. 1F and G, the tumours of KDTV-HPV16 group initially were suppressed but later showed regrowth. In contrast, the KDTV-HPV16/18 and KDTV001 groups maintained better tumour suppression, with complete tumour clearance observed in two out of five mice from each group. In summary, the introduction of HPV52 E6 and E7 antigen in KDTV001 did not impair the elicitation of specific immune responses against HPV16 in vivo. In addition, the survival of mice was found to be further prolonged by KDTV-HPV16/18 and KDT001 in comparison with KDTV-HPV16. Consequently, KDTV001, which covers a broader spectrum of antigens, was selected as the primary subject for further investigation in this study.

To further examine the characteristics of the immune response, flow cytometry was performed after stimulation with HPV16 E7 and HPV18 E6 peptide pools to analyse the number of antigen-specific T cells (the tetramer staining without stimulation with peptide pools) and the cytokines. Data processing and analysis were conducted according to the gating strategy outlined in Supplementary Fig. S1. As depicted in Fig. 2D, compared to the control group, an increase in HPV16 E749–57 and HPV18 E667–75 tetramer-positive cells was observed in the KDTV001 group, indicating an expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells capable of specifically recognising MHC I-peptide complexes following KDTV001 immunisation.

CD8+ T cells eliminate pathogens by directly killing infected cells and secreting cytokines, such as IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 which play a central role in anti-viral infection and anti-tumour immunity. After HPV16 E7 peptide pool stimulation, CD8+ T cells were activated, with an increased proportion of CD8+ T cells expressed IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 (Fig. 2E–G). There was also an increase in the proportion of multifunctional CD8+ T cells expressing two or three cytokines, primarily specific IFN-γ+ TNF-α+ CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ+ TNF-α+ IL-2+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, a subset of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells expressed CD107a, a marker of degranulation, thus confirming their cytotoxic potential in mediating target cell killing following vaccination (Fig. 2I). Furthermore, HPV18 E6 peptide pool stimulation also activated CD4+ T cells in splenocytes from KDTV001-immunised mice, with an increased proportion of CD4+ T cells expressing IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-2 (Fig. 2J–L). After stimulation with the HPV16 E7 peptide pool, the expression of the three cytokines and CD107a in CD4+ T cells was slightly higher than in the control group, but most differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 2M). These findings suggested that injection with KDTV001 elicited immune responses and stimulated the generation of activated antigen-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells.

KDTV001 induced strong CTL responses in vivo

Activating robust CD8+ T-cell cytotoxic effect is ultimate goal of vaccines for intracellular infections and cancer treatment. The results above confirmed that KDTV001 was capable of activating specific CD8+ T cells. To further validate the ability of CD8+ T cells to directly kill targeted cells, an in vivo cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) assay was employed. As shown in Fig. 3A, female naïve C57BL/6 mice were intramuscularly injected with two doses of KDTV001. On day 35, a 1:1 mixture of specific peptide-labelled “target” and “non-target” cells was infused and intravenously injected into the vaccinated mice. Twenty hours later, spleens of recipient mice were harvested for flow cytometric analysis of the proportional changes in “target” and “non-target” cells. The peptides used to label target cells were HPV16 E749–57 peptide (RAHYNIVTF) and HPV18 E667–75 peptide (KCIDFYSRI). As illustrated in Fig. 3B, regardless of whether the “target” cells were labelled with HPV16 E7 or HPV18 E6, mice vaccinated with KDTV001 exhibited a significant reduction in the ratio of “target” to “non-target” cells compared to mice vaccinated with KDTV-Ctrl. At a vaccine dose of 106 PFU (G2) per mouse, the efficacy against HPV16 E7 peptide-labelled “target” cells was approximately 62%, with no significant increase at higher doses. For HPV18 E6 peptide-labelled “target” cells, killing rate was approximately 90% at a vaccine dose of 105 PFU per mouse (G1), and this rate increased to 95% at 106 PFU per mouse (G2), with no further increased at higher doses (Fig. 3C). To further investigate the primary T-cell subset responsible for antitumour activity, tumour-bearing mice were administered with CD4-depleting or CD8-depleting monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) to deplete CD4+ or CD8+ T cells in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S2). To characterise the immunomodulatory effect of KDTV001 on the tumour immune microenvironment, we calculated the proportion of each cell cluster in all cells and compared it between the treatment group and the control group, revealing significant changes in immune cell infiltration. Notably, KDTV001 therapy was correlated with an increased abundance of various immune cells in the tumour microenvironment, particularly in cytotoxic CD8+ T cell subsets, including CD8T.prog, CD8T.prog_prolif, and CD8T.Tem. Additionally, CD8T.Tcm, which were associated with long-term immunological memory, exhibited significant enrichment in lymph nodes (Fig. 4B and C, Supplementary Fig. S3G). These findings suggested that KDTV001 effectively enhanced intratumoural immune cell infiltration, thereby exerting antitumour activity while simultaneously promoting the development of long-term protective immune components. Beyond the observed increase in CD8+ T cell proportions, we further evaluated the cytotoxicity of effector and memory CD8+ T cells with cytotoxicity score to assess their functional capacity. Our analysis revealed a significant enhancement in cytotoxicity in the treatment group (Supplementary Fig. S3H and S3I). Collectively, these results highlighted the ability of KDTV001 to modulate the tumour immune microenvironment and enhance antitumour immune responses.

To further investigate the impact of KDTV001 on T cell responses, we analysed TCR-seq data and observed significant differences in the degree of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cell clone expansion between the KDTV-Ctrl and KDTV001 treatment groups. Specifically, the KDTV001 treatment group showed more significant T cell clone expansion in both lymph nodes and tumours, particularly in tumours (Fig. 4D). These findings suggested that KDTV001 treatment activated antigen-specific immune responses, thereby supporting its role as a therapeutic strategy for modulating immune responses. In addition, our analysis of shared clonality between lymph nodes and tumours in the KDTV001 treatment group revealed significant TCR clonal overlap between specific T cell subsets. Specifically, CD4.Th1, CD8T.Prog, CD8T.Prog_Prolif, and CD8T.Tem in the lymph nodes showed notable overlap with CD8T.Tcm, CD8T.Naïve, CD8T.ISG, and CD8T.Prog in the tumours (Fig. 4E). This overlap suggested that aforementioned cells within lymph nodes and tumours might derived from shared antigen stimulus.

Based on the current evidence, we conducted an in-depth analysis and visualisation of the clonal network of various types of cells. Similarly to cell trajectory analysis, CD8T.prog cells within lymph nodes demonstrate multilineage differentiation potential, with their TCR clones sharing with multiple T-cell subsets (Fig. 4F). Among these, CD8T.Tem cells in the tumour microenvironment represented one of the key differentiation pathways.

Further tracking of the differentiation trajectory of CD8T.Tem cells revealed that they can evolve into Tum_CD8T.Prog cells. However, it is important to note that there are significant differences between CD8T.Prog cells in the tumour and their counterparts in lymph nodes. Specifically, Tum_CD8T.Prog cells exhibited remodelling of cytotoxic gene expression profiles, with their cytotoxic function being substantially enhanced compared to Lym_CD8T.Prog cells, despite being clustered as the same cell type based on the surface markers and gene expression profiles. In addition, TCR sharing between Tum_CD4.Th1 and Tum_CD8T.Prog cells has been observed, indicating that they have undergone the same antigen-selection process.

To further evaluate the functionality of CD8+ T cells, specific cytokine secretion of CD8+ T cells was analysed. As shown in Fig. 5G–I, upon in vitro stimulation with HPV16 E7 peptide pool, the experimental group exhibited significantly increased secretion of IFN-γ and TNF-α compared to the control group. Additionally, the proportion of multifunctional CD8+ T cells concurrently secreting two or more cytokines was markedly elevated in the experimental group (Fig. 5J). As depicted in Fig. 5K and L, and Supplementary Fig. S5A within KDTV001 group, there was a significant increase in the CD8+ T cells of positive staining for CD107a (also IFN-γ+) and Granzyme B (also IFN-γ+), indicating an increased infiltration of activated cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) within tumours. As shown in Fig. 5M, compared to the control group, the experimental group demonstrated an increased infiltration of CD4+ Tem cells within tumours. Functional analysis revealed that, following in vitro stimulation with HPV16 E7 peptide pool, there was no significant change in the levels of IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-2 in CD4+ T cells from the experimental group compared to the control group (Fig. 5N–P). These findings align with the notion that CD8+ T cells mainly contribute to the antitumour response. Concurrently, there was a significant reduction in the proportion of Tregs within tumours in the experimental group compared to the control group (Fig. 5Q). IFN-γ ELISpot assays of immune cells in the spleen demonstrated a marked increase in HPV16 E7, HPV18 E6, HPV18 E7 and HPV52 E7 antigen-specific cells secreting IFN-γ in the experimental group (Fig. 5R).

Other immune cell populations, such as myeloid derived suppressor cells (MDSC) and dendritic cells (DCs) were slightly increased in tumour after treatment, whereas the CD86+ dendritic cell (DC) showed a slight increase (P = 0.0601) in the treatment group (Supplementary Fig. S5B and C). scRNA-Seq analysis revealed that KDTV001 treatment significantly increased the abundance of tumour-infiltrating dendritic cells (DCs) (Supplementary Fig. S5H) and potently enhanced their activation state (Supplementary Fig. S5I and Table S5). This activation was particularly pronounced in key functional subsets, namely the antigen-cross-presenting cDCs and type-I-interferon-producing pDCs, within the tumour microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. S5J and Table S5). The ratio of M1/M2 macrophages in the tumour-draining LNs was increased in the treatment group (Supplementary Fig. S5E). Strong upregulation of PD-L1 and MHC I (H-2Db) was detected on TC-1 cells in the regressing tumours of KDTV001-treated mice, in contrast to KDTV-Ctrl-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. S5F and G). Collectively, these results indicated that immune cell infiltration within TC-1 tumours was minimal in the control group and the tumour was insufficient in eliciting antigen-specific immune response. Vaccination with KDTV001 could elicit a powerful antigen-specific cellular immune response to eradicate tumour cells and remodel antitumour microenvironment.

Adenoviral vectors induce an immune response against the vector itself, limiting their transduction efficiency and expression of exogenous genes, ultimately restricting their efficacy with repeated administration.38 Consequently, an investigation was conducted into the impact of pre-existing vector immunity on the antigen-specific immune response induced by KDTV001. The experimental mice were divided into four groups: single-dose KDTV-Ctrl (G0), single-dose KDTV001 (G1), KDTV-Ctrl administered 2 weeks prior to KDTV001 (G2), and KDTV-Ctrl administered 4 weeks prior to KDTV001 (G3) (Fig. 7D). As demonstrated in Fig. 7E, both the G2 and G3 groups exhibited a decline in immune response when compared to the G1 group. The G2 group demonstrated a more pronounced decrease in immune response. This attenuating effect correlates with the pre-existing strength of the vector-mediated immunity, which can be reflected by measuring the titre of anti-adenovirus antibodies in serum to assess the intensity of the anti-adenovirus vector immune.39 We therefore assessed serum adenovirus antibody titres in each group of mice prior to KDTV001 inoculation. Results demonstrated that serum adenovirus neutralising antibody titres in the G3 group were lower than those in the G2 group (Supplementary Fig. S8A). These results suggested that pre-existing anti-vector immunity attenuated the immune response induced by KDTV001. This necessitates further optimisation of the injection schedule to enhance the efficacy of subsequent maintenance dosing. In order to ascertain the most opportune timing for booster inoculations, murine subjects were administered a KDTV001 booster injection either 2 or 4 weeks subsequent to the primary vaccination. The results of the ELISpot assay demonstrated that a four-week interval between primary and booster injections resulted in a more robust immune response in comparison to a two-week interval (Fig. 7F). These findings suggest that optimising the interval between booster doses may mitigate the adverse effects on the immunogenicity of homologous live-attenuated vaccines, thereby providing crucial guidance for clinical application.

Persistent HPV infection is a pivotal factor in the development of cervical cancer, consequently, maintaining potent cellular immune activation is crucial for the successful development of therapeutic vaccines. Current clinical trials for HPV therapeutic vaccines typically employ three-dose or multi-dose vaccination schedules, including 0/4/8/12 weeks, 0/4/12 weeks, or repeated dosing every three weeks.6,40 A comparison was made between two vaccination regimens in C57BL/6 mice: 0/4/8/12 weeks (Program I) and 0/4/12 weeks (Program II). The number of splenocytes specifically secreting IFN-γ after in vitro stimulation with HPV16 E7 peptide pools significantly increased following the second vaccination at week 4 in Regimen I (Fig. 7G). However, the third and fourth vaccinations at weeks 8 and 12 did not further enhance splenocyte IFN-γ secretion levels. In contrast, in Program II, the third vaccination at week 12 led to a significant increase in splenocyte IFN-γ secretion, resulting in a notable peak following the booster dose. These findings provide valuable insights for the long-term and multiple-dose administration of the adenoviral vector vaccine KDTV001 in clinical trials.

In order to evaluate immunogenicity across a range of genetic backgrounds, studies were expanded from inbred C57BL/6 (H-2Kb/Db) mice to outbred CD1 mice and SD rats. The CD1 mice, which possess a diverse MHC profile that mirrors human HLA polymorphism, were immunised with KDTV001. The results of the IFN-γ ELISpot assay demonstrated a dose-dependent response that was specific to the antigen. While exhibiting a reduction in effectiveness at 1 × 105–1 × 106 PFU, a robust response against six antigens was achieved at 1 × 107–1 × 108 PFU (Supplementary Fig. S8B and C). In a similar manner, vaccinated SD rats exhibited dose-dependent responses against HPV16/18 E6 (Supplementary Fig. S8D). The findings of this study lend further support to the translational relevance of KDTV001.

In order to verify the capacity of KDTV001 to stimulate HLA-restricted immune responses in humans, we analysed its specific immune responses to HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 antigens in transgenic mice expressing common HLA-A alleles (A2.1, A24.2, A11.1). The transgenic mice were intramuscularly injected with KDTV001, followed by a booster injection four weeks later. One week after the booster, the mice were euthanised and splenocytes were analysed for antigen-specific immune responses by ELISpot assay (Fig. 7H). In B-HLA-A2.1-mice IFN-γ secreted by splenocytes with the stimulation of HPV16/18/52 E6 peptide pools was detected (Fig. 7I). In B-HLA-A11.1 transgenic mice, HPV16 E7, HPV18 E6, HPV18 E7 and HPV52 E7 specific IFN-γ spots was observed (Fig. 7J), and compared to the control group, the number of HPV18 E6 and HPV52 E7 specific IL-2 spots showed significantly increased (Supplementary Fig. S8G). Similarly, in HLA-A24.2 transgenic mice, KDTV001 immunisation elicited broad, dose-enhanced antigen-specific responses. A significant increase in IFN-γ secretion was detected against HPV16, HPV18, and HPV52 E6, a response that was further augmented by a booster injection (Fig. 7K). Furthermore, vaccination triggered cross-reactive IFN-γ responses to HPV33, HPV58, and HPV68 E6 (Supplementary Fig. S8E). IL-2 ELISpot analysis confirmed the enhanced immunogenicity, showing a significant rise in IL-2 secretion following in vitro stimulation with HPV16/18/52 E6 peptide pools in boosted mice compared to controls (Supplementary Fig. S8H). An HLA-A24.2-restricted CTL epitope antigen peptide for HPV52 E6, VYKFLFTDL was identified (Supplementary Fig. S8F). These results suggested that the three human HLA Class I transgenic mice could simultaneously generate specific immune responses against multiple antigens carried by KDTV001 and a booster injection could maintain or even enhance the immune responses elicited by KDTV001.

Discussion

This study presented KDTV001, a candidate HPV therapeutic vaccine against HPV16/18/52 HSIL and HPV-associated malignancies, which utilised a non-replicating recombinant human Ad5 vector encoding mutated HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 fusion proteins. The vaccine demonstrated robust immunogenicity in multiple animal models including inbred C57BL/6 mice, outbred CD1 mice, HLA-A2.1/11.1/24.2 transgenic mice, and SD rats. In C57BL/6 mice bearing TC-1 tumour, KDTV001 elicited HPV-specific cellular immune responses, suppressed tumour progression, prolonged survival, and established long-term protective immunity against HPV antigens. An in vitro human DC-T cell co-culture system was used to further confirmed its immunogenicity in healthy volunteers and to predict population-level applicability, thereby establishing foundations for clinical trials.

Current HPV vaccine development predominantly focuses on HPV16/18, while HPV52 now represents one of the most prevalent high-risk subtypes in China and world.41,42 KDTV001 is a trivalent therapeutic vaccine targeting HPV16/18/52 E6/E7 antigens. In this study, we identified HPV52 E6-derived 9-mer epitopes in HLA-A24·2 transgenic mice, providing critical insights for future HPV52-targeted vaccine optimisation. In vivo animal studies have demonstrated that KDTV001 elicits cross-immunological reactivity against other high-risk HPV types. However, such cross-reactivity has not yet been detected in human subjects. Current clinical trials of HPV therapeutic vaccines have not observed cross-reactivity against non-targeted high-risk HPV types, which may be attributed to interspecies variations in MHC epitopes. Future clinical investigations will prioritise evaluating the cross-reactive immune responses induced by KDTV001.

Ad5 vectors offer distinct advantages including potent immunogenicity, high transduction efficiency, non-integration characteristics, and large transgene capacity.43 Adenovirus vector-based vaccines have been developed for the treatment of persistent viral infections.44 Gabriel Dagotto et al. demonstrated that Ad26-based neoantigen delivery induces superior CD8+ T cell activation and tumour infiltration compared to peptide vaccines.45 Deletion of the E1 region which is critical for the initiation of virus replication and replacement with the foreign gene HPV E6/E7 results in replication defective virions that have low risk of insertional oncogenesis for human use.46 The adenovirus 5-vectored HPV therapeutic vaccine KDTV001 demonstrates enhanced antitumour efficacy, mediated by dual activation of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell immune responses. However, its clinical application may be limited by pre-existing or Ad5 vaccine-induced anti-vector immune responses, which can reduce viral transduction efficiency.47,48 When evaluating the impact of pre-existing carrier immunity and booster vaccination on the immunogenicity of KDTV001, the influence of pre-existing carrier immunity was weaker with a 4-week interval compared to a 2-week interval booster. Furthermore, the immunogenicity of KDTV001 was stronger, suggesting that the optimal interval for booster vaccination is 4 weeks. Optimising the vaccination schedule can further enhance the vaccine's immunogenicity. For another, optimising administration routes and injection sites is critical to maximise the clinical potential of adenovirus-based vaccines. Current mitigation strategies include heterologous prime-boost regimens, chimeric vectors, and combination platforms.49,50 For instance, Nouscom's phase Ib trial combining great ape adenoviral vectors with MVA boost demonstrated safety and immunogenicity in neoantigen vaccination.51 The pre-existing carrier immune responses revealed that antibody titres following a four-week vaccination interval were lower than those following a two-week interval, suggesting the optimal vaccination interval. We also developed a chimera oncolytic adenovirus vector that hexon hypervariable regions (HVRs) 1 and 5 of Ad5 were replaced with those from adenovirus 35.52 In future work, we will continue to develop adenovirus vaccine vectors that are more cost-effective, efficient, safe, and widely applicable.

Standard cervical cancer therapies (surgery, chemoradiation, and immunotherapy) often face the challenge of treatment resistance.53 Enhanced tumour-infiltrating CD8+ T cells correlate with improved prognosis in advanced cases.2 Remarkably, single-dose KDTV001 administration induced HPV16 E7-specific CD8+ T cell expansion and tumour regression, with complete responders exhibiting durable protection against tumour rechallenge. This sustained immunity likely involves antigen-specific memory CD8+ T cell populations, though tumour microenvironment evolution post-vaccination warrants further investigation. TCR clonal network analysis suggests that Lym_CD8T.prog cells undergo antigen-driven differentiation in lymph nodes, serving as crucial orchestrators of the primary CD8+ T cell response. On one hand, Lym_CD8T.prog cells can directly differentiate into Tum_CD8T.Prog cells. On the other hand, they can first differentiate into CD8T.Tem cells and then further differentiated into Tum_CD8T.prog cells with high-efficiency cytotoxic ability under the complex signal regulation of the tumour microenvironment, with CD4.Th1 cells playing an important auxiliary function during this process. This differentiation pattern provides a new perspective for elucidating the dynamic evolution and functional remodelling of tumour -specific CD8+ T cells, but further experimental validation is warranted to confirm this.

CD4+ T cells have been demonstrated to play a pivotal and multifaceted role in antitumour immunity, exhibiting synergistic functions with CD8+ T cells. The antitumour protection conferred by the KDTV001 vaccine was found to be contingent on CD8+ T cell deficiency, resulting in the complete loss of this protective effect. This finding serves to confirm the dominant role of CD8+ T cells in mediating potent antitumour immunity. In accordance with the findings of preceding studies in the TC-1 tumour model, CD8+ T cells have been identified as a pivotal mediator of protective immunity.45,54,55 However, it is noteworthy that a lower proportion of mice in the CD4+ T cell-deficient group attained tumour-free survival at the experimental endpoint in comparison to the KDTV001 group. This finding suggests that the absence of CD4+ T cells may potentially compromise vaccine-induced long-term protection. In vivo assays revealed discrepancies in the effective dose and killing efficiency against HPV16 E7-and HPV18 E6-targeted cells. When considered in conjunction with existing flow cytometry data, these findings suggest not only numerical disparities in CTLs but also variations in CD4+ T cell activation levels. The relationship between these variables necessitates further experimental validation. It has been demonstrated by preceding studies that CD4+ T cells further promote efficient initial activation of CD8+ T cells and the development of persistent immune memory.56, 57, 58 Within lymphoid tissues, they provide crucial assistance for CD8+ T cell initialisation.58,59 Notwithstanding these advances, the precise mechanisms by which CD4+ T cells mediate tumour cell killing in vivo remain unclear. Furthermore, the functional significance of MHC class II-dependent CD4+ T cell activation, whether directly triggered by cancer cells or indirectly induced by host APCs, requires further investigation.

The integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) into frontline metastatic management has prompted exploration of combination therapies.60 Emerging evidence from recent clinical investigations highlights the strategic integration of immune checkpoint inhibitors in managing HPV-associated malignancies. Notably, a multicenter phase II trial evaluating the synergistic combination of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody nivolumab with ISA101b, an HPV16-specific long peptide vaccine, has demonstrated promising clinical efficacy.61 Single-cell analyses revealed upregulated TIM-3 ligands (Galectin-9) on tumour-associated macrophages/monocytes and concurrent PD-1/LAG3/TIM-3 expression on CD8+ T cells (data is not shown). Although the combination of KDTV001 with anti-PD-1/TIM-3 antibodies did not achieve statistically significant tumour suppression in TC-1 tumour-bearing mice (data is not shown), it warrants further mechanistic investigation to explore its potential therapeutic applications.

Our findings highlight the strong translational potential of this Ad5-based trivalent vaccine platform. Clinical applicability could be further enhanced through strategic antigen optimisation, combination regimens with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), and vector engineering to overcome pre-existing vector immunity.

Although this study provides valuable evidence for the preclinical development of KDTV001, several limitations remain. Firstly, the present study primarily evaluated the efficacy of the vaccine in a C57BL/6 mouse model bearing established TC-1 tumours. It is important to note that this system is not capable of fully replicating the natural course of HPV infection, tissue barriers, or tumour microenvironments in humans. Consequently, the optimal vaccine deployment strategy across different disease stages or in combination therapies remains unclear. Secondly, KDTV001 is a trivalent vaccine targeting HPV 16/18/52. However, the extent to which it can provide comprehensive protection against other high-risk HPV types in humans remains to be fully validated. Finally, since the vaccine is based on a human adenovirus type 5 vector, pre-existing immunity to this vector in the population may reduce the primary immune response. Whilst preclinical studies suggest that this issue can be partially mitigated through strategic optimisation, its actual impact in humans requires confirmation through clinical trials.

Based on these preclinical findings, an Investigator-Initiated Trial (IIT) (Ethics Approval No. S211) has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology to evaluate the safety, tolerability, immunogenicity, and preliminary efficacy of KDTV001 in patients with cervical squamous intraepithelial lesions. Patient enrolment has been successfully completed.
