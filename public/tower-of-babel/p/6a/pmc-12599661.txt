A drop-based workflow for fast and robust joint profiling of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility in the same cell
Authors: Ziying Yang, Siqi Liu, Changya Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Single-cell multiomics technologies have significantly advanced our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and biological complexity. The joint profiling of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell ATAC sequencing (scATAC-seq) within the same cell offers a powerful approach for enabling direct linkage between gene expression and chromatin accessibility at single-cell resolution. This integration significantly enhances sensitivity and specificity in identifying rare cell populations and elucidating epigenetic regulatory mechanisms. In this study, we present a robust, high-throughput droplet-based microfluidic protocol that enables simultaneous profiling of RNA and chromatin accessibility from individual cells. The streamlined workflow incorporates key steps, including cell pretreatment, nuclei isolation, gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation, and the construction of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq libraries. The protocol supports parallel processing of tens of thousands of cells in a single experiment, offering exceptional scalability and reproducibility. The multimodal nature of this approach allows for integrative analysis of multiple features from the same cell, making it an invaluable tool for dissecting complex biological systems.

1. INTRODUCTION

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin sequencing (scATAC-seq) have emerged as transformative technologies that enable high-resolution dissection of cellular heterogeneity and gene regulatory mechanisms. scRNA-seq offers a comprehensive view of transcriptional states by capturing mRNA expression in individual cells, thereby facilitating the identification of distinct cell subtypes, developmental trajectories, and functional diversity within tissues.1–3 However, despite its ability to uncover tumor microenvironment heterogeneity and identify specific subtypes, scRNA-seq alone is limited in elucidating the epigenetic regulation, such as transcription factor (TF) binding and chromatin remodeling events,4–6 that drives gene expression program. In contrast, scATAC-seq directly measures chromatin accessibility at the single-cell level, identifying open chromatin regions linked to regulatory elements like enhancers, promoters, and TF binding sites, which play critical roles in controlling cell identity and fate.7,8 For instance, in leukemia, scATAC-seq has revealed chromatin accessibility heterogeneity within common myeloid progenitor cells and its correlation with alterations in TF activity.9 When combined, scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq provide a synergistic and more comprehensive view of cell states by linking transcriptional outputs with their regulatory inputs, thereby enhancing the detection of rare cell populations and offering deeper insights into lineage specification and disease mechanisms.10–12 As single-cell multiomics technologies continue to evolve, they hold tremendous potential for advancing our understanding of complex biological systems, disease mechanisms, and precision medicine. In this context, establishing a high-throughput, robust, and scalable workflow for generating high-quality single-cell multiomics data is of paramount importance.

Here, we present a comprehensive protocol for a droplet-based single-cell multiomics workflow that simultaneously profiles transcriptomes (scRNA-seq) and chromatin accessibility (scATAC-seq), enabling the dissection of cellular heterogeneity in complex tissues. This integrated approach encompasses critical steps, including pretreatment of target cells, nuclei isolation, the generation of gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM), and the construction and sequencing of scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq libraries. This standardized, scalable protocol lays the foundation for high-quality multiomics studies across diverse biological systems, offering a robust platform for future research in genomics and disease biology.

2. OVERVIEW OF THE PROTOCOL

This workflow is based on microfluidic partitioning and molecular barcoding strategies of the 10× Genomics platform, which are key to enabling the simultaneous profiling of both the transcriptome and chromatin accessibility at the single cell level (Fig. 1). The process begins by co-encapsulating nuclei with barcoded gel beads (eg, 10× Genomics Chromium beads) into oil-emulsion droplets, known as GEM. Each GEM contains a single nucleus and a single bead, ensuring the accurate and specific labeling of molecules derived from each individual cell. The beads are designed with 2 distinct barcode systems to enable the integration of transcriptomic and epigenomic data. The RNA capture barcodes for scRNA-seq have the following key components: a unique identifier that associates each transcript with its respective cell of origin; a unique molecular identifier (UMI) sequence used to tag individual RNA molecules, allowing for accurate quantification and correction of amplification bias; a poly(dT) sequence that binds to the polyadenine tail of mRNA, enabling selective capture of polyadenylated transcripts. The ATAC capture barcodes for scATAC-seq have the following key components: cell barcode that matches the RNA barcode, ensuring that transcriptomic and epigenomic data are linked to the same cell; a necessary adapter sequence for subsequent amplification and sequencing of the chromatin fragments. For scRNA-seq, mRNA molecules are released from individual nuclei and then reverse-transcribed using template-switching oligonucleotides (TSOs). This process allows each cDNA molecule to be uniquely labeled by both the cellular barcode and the UMI. In scATAC-seq, DNA fragments treated with Tn5 transposase are ligated to ATAC capture barcodes and uniquely labeled. The DNA fragments are then ligated to sequencing adapters to form ATAC libraries ready for sequencing.

Schematic overview of the microfluidic partitioning and barcoding strategy. ATAC = assay for transposase-accessible chromatin, PCR = polymerase chain reaction, UMI = unique molecular identifier.

Figure 2 illustrates the overall workflow of droplet-based scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq. The process begins with the preparation of tissue-derived cells, which undergo an initial pretreatment phase that includes cell counting, cell viability assessments, and, when necessary, enrichment by flow cytometric sorting to isolate the target cell population. Following this, nuclei are extracted from the sorted cells and prepared as single-cell nuclear suspensions. For scATAC-seq, an additional step is performed where the extracted nuclei are pre-labeled with Tn5 transposase. This enzyme selectively inserts adapter sequences into accessible chromatin regions, marking the chromatin for subsequent analysis. These labeled nuclei are then encapsulated into GEM, ensuring that each nucleus is uniquely barcoded. After the generation of the GEM, both RNA and DNA are processed through separate amplification and library preparation protocols. These steps enable the efficient capture of both transcriptomic and epigenomic data from individual cells. The final step in the workflow involves sequencing the prepared libraries on a high-throughput platform. This generates comprehensive single-cell multiomics data, allowing for the simultaneous interrogation of gene expression and chromatin accessibility in each individual cell.

3. COMPARISON WITH OTHER METHODS

Over the past decade, significant advancements in scRNA-seq have led to the development of diverse methodologies for high-throughput and highly sensitive gene expression profiling. These approaches can be broadly classified into 3 categories: plate-based technologies (eg, SMART-seq213), combinatorial indexing-based methods (eg, MARS-seq,14 Seq-Well,15 SPLiT-Seq16), and droplet microfluidics-based platforms (eg, Drop-seq,17 10× Genomics Chromium18). Plate-based methods, such as SMART-seq2, utilize an optimized TSO to generate full-length cDNA, enabling comprehensive transcriptome coverage-particularly advantageous for detecting low-abundance transcripts. However, their throughput is limited (<1000 cells per run), and substantial cell loss (>50%) during manual pipetting and plate transfers reduces their effectiveness for large-scale studies, especially when studying rare cell populations.19,20 In contrast, combinatorial indexing techniques like MARS-seq avoid the need for physical cell isolation by employing multi-step barcoding, thereby increasing throughput. Despite this advantage, these methods suffer from lower sensitivity and a tendency to preferentially capture highly expressed genes. Additionally, the complex barcoding workflows are prone to batch effects that can compromise reproducibility.21

In a similar manner to single-cell transcriptomics, scATAC-seq technologies can be classified into 3 main types: transposase-based (eg, scATAC-seq22), nuclease-based (eg, scDNase-seq23 and scMNase-seq24), and methyltransferase-based (eg, scNOMe-seq25). Nuclease-based approaches rely on DNase or MNase to digest accessible chromatin, followed by adapter ligation, but often require labor-intensive phenol-chloroform purification steps, increasing hands-on time and technical variability. Moreover, nuclease digestion may be less efficient and specific compared to transposases, resulting in lower resolution and sensitivity. Methyltransferase-based approaches, such as scNOMe-seq, utilize GpC methylation to mark open chromatin. However, these methods are hindered by DNA damage during bisulfite conversion, which can negatively affect library quality. Additionally, they require a control library to distinguish endogenous CpG methylation signals, further complicating their application. Early transposase-based methods, such as sci-ATAC-seq26 employed combinatorial indexing across plates, but faced challenges including high reagent costs, low throughput, and batch effects.

Recent advancements in single-cell multiomic technologies, including sci-CAR,27 Paired-seq,28 SNARE-seq,29 and SHARE-seq,10 have enabled simultaneous detection of transcriptomic and chromatin accessibility profiles within individual cells. However, these methods differ fundamentally in methodology, throughput, and applications. Sci-CAR-seq offers compatibility with frozen clinical samples but limited to ~1000 cells per run with sparse data resolution. Paired-seq suffers from low throughput and suboptimal data quality, restricting its utility to proof-of-concept studies. SNARE-seq achieves moderate-scale profiling (~10,000 cells per run) with enhanced sensitivity for rare cell populations but requires complex library preparation and exhibits elevated mitochondrial contamination (>5%). SHARE-seq represents a significant optimization of both Tn5 transposition efficiency and mRNA capture chemistry while maintaining cost-effectiveness, though demands labor-intensive protocol customization and struggles with tissue-specific variability.

Droplet-based microfluidic systems based on 10× Genomics platform have revolutionized single-cell analysis by encapsulating individual cells and reagents within nanoliter-scale droplets, enabling the parallel processing of tens of thousands of cells in a single experiment. This technology dramatically enhances throughput and efficiency, offering a highly automated, streamlined workflow that minimizes manual handling errors and cell loss. Furthermore, droplet-based platforms achieve exceptional gene detection sensitivity (>2000 genes per cell) and robust cell recovery rates (>65%), even with low-input samples. Similarly, droplet-based scATAC-seq workflows generate high-complexity libraries (≥20,000 fragments per cell) at a fraction of the cost compared to earlier methods. Crucially, these platforms enable multimodal integration, allowing for the simultaneous profiling of RNA expression and chromatin accessibility from the same cell, as well as immune repertoire analysis30 and proteomic analysis.31

6. TROUBLESHOOTING

6.1. Problem 1: Low nucleus yield after nucleus isolation

6.1.1. Potential solution

If the number of input cells is small, there may be no visible pellet after centrifugation. In such cases, it is crucial to proceed carefully when aspirating the supernatant to avoid inadvertently aspirating the cells. The aspirated supernatant can be temporarily retained in a 1.5 mL tube, but it is important not to retain too much supernatant because excessive supernatant may negatively impact the quality of subsequent data.

6.2. Problem 2: Low number of cells identified in scRNA/scATAC sequencing

6.2.1. Potential solution

This issue is commonly caused by inaccurate cell counting, which results in an insufficient number of cells being loaded onto the microfluidic chips. To address this, ensure that the cell suspension is gently pipetted to achieve maximum homogeneity before counting. When working with a small number of samples, it may be beneficial to perform cell counting twice to ensure accuracy.

6.3. Problem 3: Library size distribution in scRNA-seq shows enrichment of short fragments

6.3.1. Potential solution

This issue is likely due to low cell viability or degradation of mRNA during the nucleus reprocessing step, leading to the capture of a significant amount of short RNA by oligonucleotides on magnetic beads during single-cell library construction. Additionally, the use of aptamers in subsequent library construction may contribute to a reduced library complexity. To mitigate this problem, ensure proper temperature control during transport, centrifugation, and recovery processes. Minimize temperature fluctuations to maintain cell viability, and remove as much supernatant as possible during nucleus extraction.
