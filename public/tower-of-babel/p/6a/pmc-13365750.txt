Impact of an artificial intelligence automated chatbot-driven educational-counselling intervention on childbearing attitudes and motivations among childless married women: study protocol for a parallel randomised controlled trial
Authors: Havva Sheikh Azami, Fatemeh Bakouei, Mouloud Agajani Delavar, Raheleh Aghababanataj Jelodar, Mir Taher Motahari Tabari, Hossein-Ali Nikbakht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUnderstanding population dynamics is essential for achieving the Sustainable Development Goals. Global population growth has slowed, and Iran has experienced a marked fertility decline. Given the decline in fertility rates and shifts in women’s attitudes and motivations regarding childbearing, this study aims to explore whether an artificial intelligence (AI)-based chatbot can improve attitudes and increase positive motivation towards childbearing among married women of reproductive age.Methods and analysisThis parallel randomised controlled trial will be conducted on 80 childless married women who need fertility counselling according to national guidelines. Participants will be randomly allocated using permuted-block randomisation (four blocks) to either an intervention or control arm. The intervention consists of a systematic educational and counselling programme delivered via an AI-powered chatbot across 4 weekly sessions. The control arm will receive standard in-person childbearing counselling per national guidelines. Primary outcomes are attitudes and motivation towards fertility and childbearing, measured by validated questionnaires at baseline and 1-month postintervention. Introduction

A comprehensive grasp of worldwide population dynamics and their future evolution is indispensable for realising the ambitions of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and other extended development targets.1 Worldwide, the population growth rate has dropped sharply—from 2.1% in the 1960s to 0.8% in 2021. Iran mirrors this trend, with its growth rate plummeting from 5.3% in 1982 to 0.8% in 2021, largely driven by declining fertility.2 Demographic shifts, particularly population ageing, have made fertility a critical policy issue in Iran. A consistent decline in fertility rates has been observed universally across all social strata and geographic provinces, pointing to a national convergence in fertility patterns.3

The decline in fertility rates worldwide has had extensive economic, social, cultural and political consequences.4 Key variables influencing individuals’ intentions to have children include motivation and attitude. Attitude refers to a relatively stable and organised evaluation of an individual’s feelings towards a specific object, person, event or topic. Attitudes can be positive, negative or neutral and often shape individuals’ behaviour.5 An individual’s attitude towards childbearing, whether favourable or unfavourable, can directly or indirectly affect their choices and actions regarding their desire to have children, as attitudes play a crucial role in guiding life decisions.6 7

Another significant variable influencing individuals’ intentions to have children is motivation. Motivation is the driving force that compels an individual to act or behave in order to achieve a specific goal. In simple terms, motivation answers the question: ‘Why do we do something?’8 Childbearing motivation is one of the most critical factors shaping women’s reproductive preferences and behaviours.9 Research findings highlight the importance of considering cultural and social motivations in population policy-making and stress the need for appropriate counselling to shift attitudes and promote childbearing.10 11

In response to the continuing decline in fertility rates, population policies in Iran have shifted towards promoting childbearing and preventing further fertility decline. Alongside economic and social support measures, policymakers have emphasised the importance of improving fertility awareness and addressing couples’ attitudes and beliefs regarding family formation and childbearing.12

In contemporary societies, factors such as increased female employment, economic uncertainty, changing lifestyles, delayed marriage and concerns about children’s future have contributed to the postponement or avoidance of childbearing.13 This trend has become an important public health challenge in both developed and developing countries, with an increasing number of women choosing to conceive after the age of 30.14 Delayed childbearing is associated with a higher risk of maternal and neonatal complications and may ultimately result in unintended infertility, thereby reducing individuals’ opportunities to achieve their desired family size.15 16

Given the decline in fertility rates and changing attitudes towards childbearing, effective educational and counselling interventions are needed to improve fertility awareness and strengthen positive childbearing motivations (PCMs). Previous studies have shown that evidence-based fertility education can positively influence reproductive decision-making, particularly in populations with limited fertility knowledge.17 Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI), especially chatbot-based interventions, offer new opportunities to deliver accurate, personalised and accessible reproductive health information.13 Recent studies have demonstrated that chatbots provide more accurate and relevant information in medical fields compared with search engines like Google.18

Studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of AI-based chatbots in providing health education, counselling support and decision-making assistance across various medical contexts.19,23 However, limited evidence exists regarding the use of AI-based chatbots to influence childbearing attitudes and motivations among Iranian women. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of an AI-based chatbot intervention on attitudes and motivation towards childbearing among married women of reproductive age in Iran.

Methods and analysis

Study design

This article presents a protocol for a randomised controlled trial (RCT) designed to assess the efficacy of an AI-powered educational and consulting programme. The trial will utilise a parallel-group design and will focus on married women of reproductive age who do not have children and require childbearing counselling in accordance with national guidelines. This RCT will be conducted in Mahmudabad, a city in northern Iran, with participant recruitment planned to begin in August 2026.

The protocol adheres to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines and follows the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (figure 1) and (online supplemental file 1). Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Babol University of Medical Sciences (code: IR.MUBABOL.HRI.REC.1404.172) and registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on 14 November 2025 (IRCT20221109056451N7) (online supplemental file 2). In order to ensure integrity, moral strictness and data monitoring of the study, a supervisor has been appointed by the university’s ethics committee to accurately audit the research process.

Patient selection and recruitment

Participants will be recruited from urban comprehensive health centres in Mahmudabad city, located in Northern Iran. Eligibility information for participants will be extracted through the electronic health registration system (Parsa). The Parsa Electronic Health Record (EHR) system is a comprehensive digital platform designed to streamline patient data management and enhance healthcare delivery. The Parsa EHR system integrates various health records, including vaccination history, medical diagnoses, treatment plans and laboratory results, enabling healthcare providers to access and exchange vital health information efficiently.

In the initial phase, to ensure geographical diversity and prevent bias resulting from spatial concentration, a list of urban comprehensive health service centres in Mahmudabad County will be compiled. Subsequently, at least one health centre will be randomly selected from each of the four geographical zones (north, south, east and west) to ensure a balanced spatial distribution of participants. In the next step, individuals who meet the eligibility criteria within the Parsa system and are under the coverage of the selected health centres will be invited for in-person participation using a convenience sampling method.

The inclusion criteria: married women aged 18–40 years, Iranian nationality, first marriage, childless (women who have been married for at least 6 months and are not currently pregnant), low or average desire to have children based on a fertility preference question score below 8, access to the Internet and a smartphone, having at least 9 years of education (middle school education level) and the ability to interact with AI/digital platforms.

Desire for childbearing was assessed using a question asking participants to rate their desire to have children on a numerical scale from 1 (lowest desire) to 10 (highest desire). Using this scale, individuals can select a number that reflects the extent of their desire to have children. The desire for childbearing is scored using a grading scale of 0–3 for low desire, 4–7 for average desire and 8–10 for high desire.24 Participants with low or average desire for childbearing (score <8) were eligible for inclusion in the study.

Exclusion criteria also include women with maternal or fetal medical contraindications to pregnancy, known chronic physical and mental illnesses, speech and hearing disorders (preventing communication with the researcher), drug addiction according to the individual’s statements and unwillingness to participate in the study. The eligibility criteria will be evaluated using a checklist during the screening procedure to guarantee clarity and openness. Only individuals who match these requirements will be eligible to participate in the trial.

Sociodemographic-fertility data

The sociodemographic and fertility questionnaire includes variables such as age, education, occupation, income, place of birth, current residence status, perceived importance of religion in daily life, common source of fertility-related information, intentions for birth spacing, perceived need for childbearing counselling, duration of marriage and the dominant role of the decision-maker for childbearing. Before the intervention (T0), both the intervention and control groups will complete this data.

Intervention and comparator

Participants randomised to the intervention arm will receive a structured educational-counselling programme based on AI technology through an AI-powered chatbot platform, delivered across 4 weekly sessions scheduled at each participant’s convenience. The intervention protocol begins with a standardised set of researcher-developed questions derived from the National Guidelines for Childbearing Counseling for Healthcare Providers,25 which are embedded within the chatbot interface. Following this initial structured interaction, participants will have the opportunity to pose personalised questions to refine their understanding and receive tailored advice. All chatbot-mediated exchanges will be systematically reviewed and summarised for participant reflection. Throughout the intervention period, participants will maintain access to the study team’s first author via dedicated virtual messaging and telephone support to address any queries or concerns in real time.

Participants allocated to the control arm will receive conventional childbearing counselling as specified in the National Guidelines,25 26 administered through standard in-person consultations with trained healthcare providers.

Both study arms will receive identical evidence-based educational content, adapted from the Iranian Ministry of Health and Medical Education’s childbearing counselling module. The curriculum comprehensively addresses: the positive effects of having children, the benefits of pregnancy, natural childbirth and breastfeeding, the side effects of contraception methods, the positive effects of having multiple children, strategies to address common misconceptions (medical, economic or childrearing-related), national dimensions of population, complications of abortion, life skills, and maternal dignity.25

Outcome measurement

Primary endpoints

The primary outcomes are attitudes towards childbearing and motivation of childbearing, assessed using the Attitudes toward Fertility and Childbearing Scale (AFCS) and Miller’s Childbearing Questionnaire before (T0) and 1 month after the intervention (T1).

Attitudes toward Fertility and Childbearing Scale

This questionnaire was designed by Soderberg et al. The Persian version contains 23 items across four subscales: child as life foundation (8 items), child as obstacle (6 items), postponing childbearing (5 items) and prerequisites for parenthood (4 items). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1=strongly disagree to 5=strongly agree), with items 9 to 20 and 22 reverse-scored. Higher total scores indicate more positive attitudes. The validity and reliability of scale in Persian have been previously established.27

Miller’s childbearing motivation questionnaire

Childbearing motivation will be assessed using this questionnaire, which measures both positive and negative motivational dimensions. The PCM subscale comprises 28 items addressing themes such as the joys of pregnancy, birth and infancy; traditional parenthood; satisfaction from childrearing; feeling needed and connected; and instrumental values of children. The negative childbearing motivation (NCM) subscale includes 21 items covering discomforts of pregnancy and childbirth; fears and worries about parenthood; negatives of childcare; and parental stress. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). PCM scores range from 28 to 112, with higher scores indicating stronger positive motivations; NCM scores range from 21 to 84, with lower scores reflecting stronger overall fertility motivation. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were 0.94 for PCM and 0.87 for NCM.28 Content validity was previously confirmed by Razai et al, with a content validity index (CVI) of 0.91 and a content validity ratio (CVR) of 0.88; internal consistency in that study was reported as Cronbach’s alpha=0.810.24

Secondary outcome

Fertility knowledge is as the secondary outcome, assessed using the Cardiff Fertility Knowledge Scale (CFKS) before (T0) and 1 month after the intervention (T1). The CFKS was used to evaluate women’s knowledge across three domains: indicators of fertility decline, fertility facts and common misconceptions. Each item offered three response options: true, false and ‘I don’t know’. Items are scored 1 for a correct response and 0 for an incorrect response or ‘I don’t know’. A total score is computed as the sum of correct responses divided by the total number of items and multiplied by 100. Content validity indices were acceptable (CVR=0.67, CVI=0.95). The Persian version has demonstrated good reliability and validity in Iranian samples (Cronbach’s α=0.79).17

Sample size

The sample size for this study was determined based on the primary outcomes and objectives of the research. Drawing on previous studies of similar interventions aimed at improving childbearing motivation,24 and considering the comparison of means and SD differences of motivation scores before and after the intervention (effect size of 1.89), a 95% CI and 95% statistical power were applied. Using GPower V.3 and a two-tailed hypothesis, the initial required sample size was calculated to be six participants per group. However, due to the necessity of analysis will use analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) analysis to evaluate postintervention effects while controlling for baseline values and at least two confounders, an additional 30 participants were recruited for each group. Therefore, the total required sample size for the study is 36 participants per group. To account for a potential 10% attrition rate during the follow-up period, the final target sample size is 40 participants per group, resulting in a total of 80 participants for the study.

Randomisation

Eligible women will be allocated to either the intervention or control group in a 1:1 ratio using a permuted-block randomisation with block size 4, ensuring an equal one-to-one ratio. This approach prevents the investigators from predicting the next assignment. The allocation sequence will be generated via the randomisation service at http://random.org and prepared by a statistical consultant on the research team. The master randomisation file will be password-protected and accessible only to the principal investigator (concealment mechanism). Recruiters and counselling providers will have no access. These procedures are intended to minimise selection bias.

Because the counselling intervention precludes full blinding, we will implement additional measures to reduce detection bias. The data analyst will remain blinded to group allocation by working with deidentified, coded datasets. Outcome data will be collected using standardised, self-administered questionnaires to limit interviewer bias. Participant contact details and allocation codes will be stored separately, so that follow-up contacts do not reveal group assignment. Together, these steps aim to improve objectivity in outcome assessment and strengthen the trial’s internal validity.

After screening for eligibility and selecting women by convenience sampling, study aims will be explained and written informed consent will be obtained (online supplemental file 3). Participants will then be randomised to either the intervention or standard-care arm (figure 2).

Study flow diagram showing the key components of the intervention.

All participants in the AI chatbot counselling arm will receive a standardised orientation to ensure uniform delivery of the intervention, covering the structure, content and expected outcomes.

To monitor engagement and adherence, participants will be encouraged to record their level of engagement for each session, and automated or manual reminders will be sent to promote attendance and participation. To mitigate technical problems during sessions, we will provide participants with the primary researcher’s contact information for troubleshooting. Comprehensive data management procedures will be used to preserve data quality and integrity. Data will be coded according to a detailed coding manual and stored securely.

Statistical methods

After data collection and validation of data entry, descriptive statistics will be used to summarise patient data, employing measures of central tendency (mean) and measures of dispersion (SD). Where applicable, frequencies and percentages will also be reported. The normality of the data will first be assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The χ2 test will be used to examine the relationship between two categorical variables. If the assumptions for the χ2 test are not met, Fisher’s exact test will be applied. To compare means between two categorical variables, an independent t-test will be used, assuming equal variances. If the assumption of equal variances is violated, the Mann-Whitney U test will be used in its place. For comparing preintervention and postintervention means within groups, a paired t-test will be used. If the normality assumption is violated, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test will be employed. Additionally, ANCOVA will be applied to compare postintervention scores while controlling for preintervention values. Because the study includes two primary outcomes, Bonferroni-adjusted significance levels will be considered for the primary analyses to reduce the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons.

The mean differences and 95% CIs will be reported to indicate the effect size. In this randomised controlled trial, an intention-to-treat approach will be used to preserve the integrity of the random allocation process. This method ensures that all participants, even those who withdraw from the study for any reason, are included in the analysis based on their initial random assignment. To handle missing data, appropriate follow-up and monitoring will minimise data loss. If necessary, multiple imputation methods will be applied to replace missing values. Data analysis will be conducted using SPSS V.23, with a significance level of p<0.05 considered statistically significant.
