Residue Dynamics of Metaflumizone in Tomatoes and Peppers Using LC–MS/MS: Pre-Harvest Intervals and Dietary Risk Assessment
Authors: Osama I. Abdallah, Fahad S. Almulhim, Ayman F. Omar, Khaled A. Al-Jamhan, Saleh S. Alhewairini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Peppers (Capsicum annuum L.) and tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum L.) are staple vegetables of considerable economic and nutritional importance. However, their production faces challenges from pest infestations and diseases, particularly in intensive cultivation systems. This study aimed to develop and validate a method for analyzing EZ-metaflumizone, a novel sodium channel-blocking insecticide, using acetonitrile for extraction and LC–MS/MS for quantitation in peppers and tomatoes. Method optimization improved sensitivity, effectively minimizing matrix effects (ME: −2.72% for tomatoes and −9.43% for peppers). Validation demonstrated recoveries ranging from 77.2 to 94.1%, with precision (RSD) below 20%, and a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.005 mg/kg, which is well below the EU maximum residue limits (MRLs) of 1.5 mg/kg for peppers and 0.7 mg/kg for tomatoes. The dissipation kinetics of E/Z-metaflumizone (24% SC) were studied under greenhouse conditions following application at recommended and double recommended doses. The half-lives of metaflumizone residues were 2.99–3.14 days in tomatoes and 3.47–3.53 days in peppers, with dissipation following first-order kinetics. Preharvest intervals (PHIs) were determined to be 2.69–5.82 days for tomatoes and 1.86–3.4 days for peppers. Dietary exposure and hazard quotient (HQ) values indicated negligible risk to adult consumers, with HQs ranging from 8.37 to 28.05% for tomatoes and 18.15 to 41.20% for peppers. These findings provide valuable insights into residue behavior, risk assessment, and dissipation kinetics, which can inform authorities’ decision-making for high-value crops.

Introduction

Peppers (Capsicum annuum L.) and tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum L.) are vegetables of significant economic and nutritional importance due to their essential role in the human diet. − However, the intensification of cultivation practices and the nonadoption of good agricultural practices increase the risk of pest infestations and diseases, particularly when grown under greenhouse conditions. This often compels farmers to rely on extensive pesticide use to maximize crop productivity and maintain quality.

Metaflumizone­[(EZ)-2′-[2-(4-cyanophenyl)-1-(α,α,α-trifluoro-m-tolyl)­ethylidene]-4-(trifluoromethoxy)­carbanilohydrazide] (Figure ) is a novel, broad-spectrum insecticide that functions as a sodium channel blocker, belonging to the semicarbazone class. It is highly effective against Lepidoptera larvae and various pests from the orders Hemiptera, Diptera, Coleoptera, Hymenoptera, Isoptera, and Siphonaptera. , Metaflumizone comprises two isomers (90% E and 10% Z isomer). The predominant E isomer exhibits more significant activity against lepidopteran larvae than the Z isomer.

Chemical structure of E-isomer (A) and Z-isomer (B) of metaflumizone.

Accurately identifying isomeric pesticides is challenging, necessitating the separate quantification of stereoisomers. Often, only one isomer is reported due to coelution during analysis or selective disregard of one isomer. Typically, the peak areas of the individual isomers are summed for quantification, which may not be ideal given the significant differences in biological activity and toxicological profiles between the isomers. For instance, Dong et al. reported transformations between the E and Z isomers of metaflumizone in cabbage and soil, while Chatterjee and Gupta studied the persistence of metaflumizone in cabbage and soil, focusing on degradation products rather than stereoisomerization.

In recent years, several methods have been developed for pesticide residue analysis, such as the QuEChERS (Quick, Easy, Cheap, Effective, Rugged, and Safe) method, which is widely used for monitoring pesticide residues in high-water-content produce. , Different sorbents, including octadecylsilane (ODS)-modified silica (C18), primary secondary amine (PSA), and graphite carbon black (GCB), have been explored for purifying extracts to determine EZ-metaflumizone in vegetables such as Chinese broccoli. However, the separate identification of individual metaflumizone isomers has yet to be comprehensively addressed despite reports of successful isomer separation. ,

This study aimed to develop and validate a simple, efficient method using acetonitrile extraction combined with LC–MS/MS to identify and quantify EZ-metaflumizone in peppers and tomatoes. The validated method was then applied to evaluate the dissipation rates and terminal residues of EZ-metaflumizone in peppers and tomatoes after multiple greenhouse field applications of a 24% SC formulation for the first time. Additionally, the half-life (t 0.5) and preharvest interval (PHI) were calculated based on field data. The findings will provide essential data to support the safe use of metaflumizone in tomato and pepper cultivation and assist Saudi authorities in establishing maximum residue limits (MRLs) for metaflumizone in peppers and tomatoes.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

The reference standard of metaflumizone (99.5% purity) was sourced from Chem Service (West Chester, PA, USA). Acetonitrile, methanol, and glacial acetic acid (HPLC-grade), along with formic acid and ammonium formate (LC–MS grade), were procured from Fisher Scientific (Loughborough, UK). Anhydrous magnesium sulfate (MgSO4) and sodium acetate (CH3COONa) were obtained from Chem-Lab NV (Zedelgem, Belgium). The primary secondary amine (PSA) was supplied by Macherey-Nagel (Chromabond diamino, Düren, Germany). Ultrapure water was prepared using the Ultra Clear system from Evoqua Water Technologies (Guenzburg, Germany). A ceramic homogenizer (Copure) was purchased from Chrom Tech, Inc. (Apple Valley, MN, USA). The commercial formulation of 24% SC metaflumizone (Alverde, BASF, France) was obtained from the local market.

Standard Solutions

Standard stock solutions of metaflumizone (1000 mg/L) were prepared by weighing 0.0502 g of the active ingredient into a glass weighing boat and quantitatively transferring it into a 50 mL volumetric flask using acetonitrile. From this stock, an intermediate solution of 100 mg/L was prepared, followed by a working solution of 10 mg/L through serial dilution in acetonitrile. The working solution was used to prepare serial concentrations for constructing the in-solvent calibration curve and the matrix-matched calibration curve. All standard solutions were stored at −20 °C to maintain stability.

Field Experiment

Field experiments were conducted under greenhouse conditions in Buraidah City, Al-Qassim Governorate, Saudi Arabia, in September 2023. The metaflumizone commercial formulation (24% SC) was applied to bell peppers (Capsicum annuum “Jupiter”) and tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum “Lazarino F1”) at two application rates: the recommended dose of 240 g a.i./ha and a double dose of 480 g a.i./ha. Each treatment was carried out in a separate greenhouse (10 × 40 m2). The formulation was diluted in water (1000 L/ha) and applied using an electric knapsack sprayer. Dissipation kinetics were assessed by randomly collecting representative samples (∼3 kg) from each treatment at specified intervals: 0 (2 h), 1, 3, 7, 10, and 15 days postapplication.

Terminal Residues

The terminal residues of metaflumizone in tomatoes and peppers were evaluated following 2–3 applications at the recommended dose (240 g a.i./ha) and the double recommended dose (480 g a.i./ha). Residue levels were analyzed by collecting samples 1 and 3 days postapplication.

Approximately 3 kg of each sample was randomly collected from each treatment, placed in polyethylene bags, and transported to the laboratory under controlled temperature conditions (European Commission, 2002). The samples were cut into small pieces (2–3 cm) and frozen overnight at −20 °C before analysis.

Optimization of Matrix Effect (ME)

The effects of coeluted compounds from tomatoes and peppers on the instrument sensitivity of EZ-metaflumizone were optimized to minimize suppression or enhancement. The matrix effect (ME) was evaluated using raw extracts, 2× and 5× dilutions, and PSA adsorbent (25 mg/mL). Effectiveness was assessed through a t test, with significance defined as P < 0.05.

Extraction and Cleanup

Frozen samples were comminuted and homogenized using a Stephan Universal UM5 processing machine (Stephan Machinery GmbH, Germany). Ten ±0.2 g of the homogenized sample was weighed into a 50 mL centrifuge tube. For extraction, 10 mL of acetonitrile containing 1% acetic acid was added as the extraction solvent. The mixture was then vortexed using a Vortex-Genie 2 mixer (Scientific Industries, USA) for 2 min after the addition of a ceramic homogenizer. To promote phase separation, a salt mixture of 4 g of MgSO4 and 1 g of CH3COONa was added to the tube, then shaken vigorously by hand for 2 min, followed by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min using a Hettich Rotina 420R centrifuge (Andreas Hettich GmbH & Co. KG, Germany). For cleanup, 1 mL of the upper supernatant was transferred into a 2 mL tube containing 150 mg of MgSO4 and 25 mg of PSA, then vortexed for 1 min and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the cleaned extract was filtered through a 0.22 μm PTFE (polytetrafluoroethylene) syringe filter (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) into an LC vial for LC–MS/MS analysis.

Method Validation

The method performance was assessed following the SANTE guidelines, focusing on the following criteria: selectivity, linearity, limit of quantitation (LOQ), accuracy, precision, and matrix effects (ME). , The selectivity of the EZ-metaflumizone was assessed by analyzing blank tomato and pepper samples to corroborate the absence of interfering peaks at its retention time. Additionally, spiked samples were examined to guarantee the precise identification of target analytes without matrix interference. Linearity was determined using matrix-matched calibration curves created from blank sample extracts by spiking with analyte concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 0.5 mg/kg. The LOQ was established as the lowest spiking concentration that met acceptable recovery (70–120%) and precision (RSD ≤ 20%). Accuracy was evaluated as the mean recovery rate at four spiking levels: 0.005, 0.1, 1, and 5 mg/kg (n = 6 for each level). Precision was expressed as the relative standard deviation (RSD) for both intraday repeatability (RSDr) (n = 6, analyzed on a single day) and interday repeatability (RSDR) (n = 18, analyzed over three different days). The percentage of matrix effect (% ME) was assessed by comparing the slope of the matrix-matched calibration curve (MMC) with that of a pure solvent calibration curve (SCC) using eq : ME%=((SlopeofMMC/slopeofSCC)−1)×100 1

LC–MS/MS

The LC–MS/MS chromatographic analysis was conducted using a Dionex Ultimate 3000 RS UHPLC+ focused system coupled with a Thermo Fisher Scientific TSQ Altis Triple Quadrupole mass spectrometer (Austin, TX, USA). Separation was performed at 40 °C on an Accucore RP-MS C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm; Thermo Fisher, USA) with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The analysis of metaflumizone isomers employed gradient elution with two mobile phases: (A) ultrapure water with 0.1% formic acid (v/v) and (B) methanol with 0.1% formic acid. The gradient program started at 10% B, held constant for 2 min, and then increased linearly to 90% B over 6 min. This composition was maintained for 2 min before reverting to 10% B at 8.1 min. The system was equilibrated at this initial composition for 8 min to ensure consistent column performance.

The MS/MS parameters were optimized via direct infusion of standard solution (0.5 mg/L, dissolved in methanol: water (50:50, v/v)) using a Harvard infusion pump (South Natick, MA, USA) at a 5 μL/min flow rate. Electrospray ionization was operated in positive mode (ESI+). The sheath gas pressure was set to 40 Arb, and the auxiliary gas pressure to 10 Arb. The ion spray voltage was 4 kV, with an ion transfer tube temperature of 325 °C and an evaporator temperature of 350 °C. Data acquisition and processing were performed using TraceFinder software (version 4.1 SP4).

Results and Discussion

LC–MS/MS Optimization

MS/MS

The collision energy and source parameters were fine-tuned to maximize signal intensity. The analysis employed multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) with two transitions optimized for quantification and confirmation, achieved by directly infusing standard solutions into the mass spectrometer at a flow rate of 5 μL/min using a Harvard pump. The optimal ion spray voltage was set at 4 kV, resulting in stable ionization and a consistent response. The precursor ion [M + H]+ for EZ-metaflumizone was identified as 507.1 m/z. Two product ions were selected for monitoring: 178.1 m/z with a collision energy of 26 V and 287.1 m/z with a collision energy of 24 V. The RF lens voltage was set at 92 V for both product ions.

Mobile Phase and Column Temperature Optimization

The optimization of the mobile phase for the analysis of EZ-metaflumizone was conducted using different solvent combinations: methanol: water, acetonitrile: water, methanol: water with 0.1% formic acid, and methanol: water with 0.1% FA and 5 mM ammonium formate. The results (Figure ) demonstrated that the mobile phase composition significantly influenced the E/Z isomer ratio and sensitivity. Methanol: water plus 0.1% FA increased the summation of the isomer areas by 366% compared to methanol: water and 585% compared to acetonitrile: water while maintaining an E/Z ratio of 6.19. However, the addition of 5 mM ammonium formate further increased the summation of the isomers’ areas by 45% compared to methanol:water with 0.1% FA, resulting in a total improvement of 586 and 905% compared to methanol:water and acetonitrile:water, respectively. A drastic alteration in the E/Z ratio to 0.34 upon adding ammonium formate suggests a potential change in the equilibrium between the isomers. The ammonium ions may influence the equilibrium dynamics. The mobile phase containing 0.1% FA was chosen for further tests.

Effect of mobile phase composition on the sensitivity and ratio of EZ metaflumizone isomers.

Injection Constituent and Column Temperature Optimization

The effect of the injection solvent on the separation of metaflumizone isomers was tested using acetonitrile only and a 50:50 (v/v) mixture of water and acetonitrile. The results showed no significant impact on the E/Z ratio, with values of 6.34 and 6.04, respectively. This indicates that the solvent composition does not affect the separation or isomer distribution, and either can be used without compromising analytical performance.

The effect of column temperature on the separation of metaflumizone isomers was tested at 30, 40, and 50 °C. The E/Z ratio showed minimal variation, with values of 6.19, 5.89, and 6.14, respectively, indicating no significant differences in isomer distribution across the tested temperatures. However, 40 °C resulted in improved sensitivity, as evidenced by the highest total response (ΣEZ) compared to 30 and 50 °C. These results suggest that while column temperature does not significantly affect the isomer distribution, 40 °C enhances chromatographic sensitivity and is optimal within the tested temperature ranges.

Matrix Effect Optimization

The matrix effect (ME) evaluation for signal suppression of EZ-metaflumizone revealed significant differences across treatments for tomatoes and peppers (Figure ). The ME between 0 and −20% is considered negligible, while values from −20 to −50% indicate a moderate matrix effect. , The raw extract (without cleanup) exhibited an ME of −10.02% for tomatoes, indicating a weak matrix effect, while peppers showed a moderate matrix effect of −22.52%. For tomatoes, the ME was significantly reduced to negligible levels by 2× dilution (−2.34%, P = 0.0012), PSA cleanup (−1.02%, P = 0.0021), and 5× dilution (−0.64%, P = 0.0037). Similarly, in peppers, 2× dilution (−14.48%, P = 0.0025), 5× dilution (−13.08%, P = 0.0018), and PSA cleanup (−6.58%, P = 0.0009) all significantly reduced the ME to negligible levels compared to the raw extract. Peppers consistently exhibited significantly higher (P ≤ 0.0027) ME values than tomatoes across all treatments. Despite achieving negligible matrix effects in all treatments for both crops, a matrix-matched calibration curve was constructed to ensure accurate quantification of metaflumizone. PSA cleanup was preferred as it minimized the matrix effect without diluting the analyte, thereby maintaining sensitivity and avoiding a reduction in the quantitation limit.

Impact of dilution and PSA cleanup on the matrix effect (ME) of metaflumizone in tomato and pepper extract.

Method Validation

Selectivity

Chromatograms of blank samples revealed no interfering peaks at the retention time of EZ-metaflumizone (Figure A,B). In contrast, clear peaks of EZ-metaflumizone were confirmed at their respective retention times (Figure C,D), indicating the method’s capability to detect it in the presence of matrix components accurately.

Representative chromatograms of blank peppers (A) and tomatoes (B) extracts; spiked pepper (C) and tomatoes (D) with EZ-metaflumizone at the LOQ level of 0.005 mg/kg.

Linearity

The method exhibited excellent linearity across the concentration range of 0.005–0.2 mg/kg for tomato and pepper matrices. The correlation coefficients (R 2) were 0.9995 for tomatoes and 0.9994 for peppers, with residuals of ≤14.6%. These results meet the acceptance criteria of R 2 > 0.99 and residuals within ±20%.

Matrix Effect

The estimated matrix effect revealed signal suppression of EZ-metaflumizone at −2.72% in tomato extracts and −9.43% in pepper extracts. Both values fall within the 0–20% range, indicating a weak matrix effect.

Limit of Quantification (LOQ)

The LOQ for EZ-metaflumizone in both matrices was established at 0.005 mg/kg, with recoveries of 81.7% for tomatoes and 77.2% for peppers, and RSD values below 5.7%, respectively. These results comply with the acceptable ranges of 70–120% for recovery and ≤20% for RSD, confirming the reliability and validity of the selected LOQ for analyzing EZ-metaflumizone residues in these matrices. Furthermore, the LOQ is significantly lower than the maximum residue limits (MRLs) set by the EU (0.7 mg/kg for tomatoes and 1.5 mg/kg for peppers), demonstrating the method’s capability to detect residues well below regulatory standards.

Accuracy and Precision

Accuracy was evaluated using recovery experiments conducted at four spiking levels (0.005, 0.1, 1, and 5 mg/kg). Recovery rates ranged from 81.7 to 93.1% for tomatoes and 77.2 to 94.1% for peppers, with RSDs consistently below 6%. These findings meet the SANTE guideline criteria, which specify acceptable recoveries of 70–120% and RSDs of less than 20%.

Precision was evaluated based on intraday repeatability (RSDr) and interday repeatability (RSDR). The RSDr values were 8.92% for tomatoes and 11.44% for peppers, while the RSDR values, measured over 3 days, were 13.31 and 17.67%, respectively. All precision values complied with the acceptance criterion below 20%. Based on the validation results (Table ), the method is valid and in compliance with the SANTE guideline criteria for determining EZ-metaflumizone in tomatoes and peppers.

Dissipation of EZ-Metaflumizone Residues and Isomer Ratios

The dissipation of EZ-metaflumizone residues in peppers and tomatoes was assessed after applying recommended (240 g a.i./ha) and double recommended doses (480 g a.i./ha).

For peppers, the initial deposits for the recommended dose were 2.688 mg/kg on day 0, exceeding the EU-MRL of 1.5 mg/kg. Residues declined to 1.762 mg/kg on day 1 (34.4% dissipation) and below the MRL by day 3 at 1.202 mg/kg (55.3% dissipation). By day 7, residues declined to 0.639 mg/kg (76.2% dissipation), reaching 0.156 mg/kg by day 14 (94.2% dissipation). By day 21, residues were below the LOQ of 0.005 mg/kg, indicating complete dissipation. The initial residue was 4.144 mg/kg for the double dose in peppers. Residues declined to 2.393 mg/kg on day 1 (42.3% dissipation) and 1.341 mg/kg by day 3 (67.6% dissipation), falling below the MRL. By day 14, residues were 0.217 mg/kg (94.8% dissipation); by day 21, they were below the LOQ (Table ).

The E/Z isomer ratios in tomatoes also fluctuated, reflecting the dynamics of isomer transformation. For the recommended dose, the E/Z ratio was 7.98 on day 0, increased to 11.13 on day 1, decreased to 2.08 by day 3, and stabilized by day 14. A similar trend was observed for the double dose, with a ratio of 10.27 on day 0, and it reached near-equilibrium by day 14 (Figure ).

Dissipation behavior and E/Z metaflumizone in tomatoes.

The E/Z isomer ratios further highlighted crop-specific dynamics. In peppers, the ratios stabilized near 1.27 by day 14 for both doses, whereas in tomatoes, they remained slightly higher, stabilizing at 2.429 and 1.588 for the recommended and double doses, respectively. These findings suggest that the transformation of the E-isomer to the Z-isomer is slightly slower in tomatoes. The rapid, dramatic decrease in the E/Z-isomer ratio from day 3 (≤2.87) to day 14 (≤2.43), following an initial increase at 0 and 1 days, indicated a faster rate of dissipation of the E-isomer relative to the Z-isomer. In addition, the decrease in the ratio between days 1 and 3 may be due to an early conversion of the E-isomer to the Z-isomer (isomerization).

For both crops, the dissipation followed first-order kinetics, with high regression coefficients (R 2 > 0.99); however, the initial residue deposits (C 0) were higher in peppers. Despite this, residues in both crops fell below their respective MRLs by day 3. The calculated dissipation rate constants (k) were higher in tomatoes (0.232–0.221 day–1) than in peppers (0.196–0.200 day–1), resulting in shorter half-lives for tomatoes (2.99–3.14 days) compared to peppers (3.53–3.47 days).

The dissipation of pesticides in plants is affected by various factors, including the stability of pesticides, formulation, volatilization, solubility, initial deposits, plant species, application method, and environmental conditions (temperature, humidity, and air movement). , The high lipophilicity (Log p = 4.6) and low solubility of metaflumizone in water (0.0018 mg/L at 20 °C) indicate that it is easily absorbed and accumulated by tubers but hardly translocated from tubers to stems and leaves. The low vapor pressure (1.24 × 10–5 mpa at 20 °C) indicated low volatility of metaflumizone. Since tomatoes and peppers were grown under similar greenhouse conditions, we observed that tomatoes developed more rapidly than peppers and bore larger fruits earlier. ,− In addition to several other factors, crop growth dilution and photodegradation play a significant role in the dissipation of residues in this study.

Limited research exists on the dissipation of metaflumizone across various crops. Chatterjee and Gupta examined the persistence of metaflumizone (220 SC) on cabbage after two applications at the recommended dose (200 g a.i./ha) and a doubled recommended dose (400 g a.i./ha). Initial residues were reported at 0.46 and 0.51 mg/kg for the recommended dose and 0.76 and 0.85 mg/kg for the double dose following the first and second sprays, respectively. The residues persisted for over 5 days and dissipated with half-lives ranging from 1.7 to 2.1 days. Investigated metaflumizone on Aster scaber across two field sites, reporting longer half-lives of 4.5–5.9 days.

In another study, Li et al. observed a half-life of 4.5 days for metaflumizone applied to rice straw at a rate of 450 g a.i./ha. Meanwhile, Bi et al. reported a faster dissipation rate in Chinese broccoli treated with 240 g a.i./ha, with half-lives ranging from 0.7 to 2.5 days.

Terminal Residues and Risk Assessment of EZ-Metaflumizone

The terminal residues of EZ-metaflumizone in tomatoes were 0.606–1.213 and 1.033–1.719 mg/kg after the second and third application of the recommended dose of 240 g a.i/ha. At the same time, the terminal residues were 0.781–1.441 and 0.924–2.031 mg/kg after applying the second and third applications of the double recommended dose of 480 g a.i./ha, respectively. Peppers showed higher terminal residues of 1.314–2.225 mg/kg and 1.691–2.613 mg/kg, and 1.51–2.472 mg/kg and 2.213–2.983 mg/kg after the second and third application of 240 g a.i/ha and 480 g a.i/ha, respectively (Table ).

Metaflumizone Terminal Residues, Dietary Exposure, and Hazard Quotient in Tomatoes and Peppers

The residue deposits of pesticides are influenced by spraying dosage, plant morphology, and dissipation rate. Additionally, the degradation and metabolic rates of pesticides can be inhibited after successive applications due to their impact on the surface microbiota of crops. , Crop varieties and microbial diversity of plants differed between tomatoes and peppers, resulting in residual differences. The metaflumizone residues in tomatoes exceeded their EU-MRL of 0.7 mg/kg at the tested intervals at both doses. In comparison, residues in peppers exceeded their EU-MRL of 1.5 mg/kg in most cases, except 3 days after the second application of 240 g a.i./ha (1.314 mg/kg) (Table ).

Pre-Harvest Interval (PHI) and Risk Assessment

The PHI was calculated for peppers as 1.86 and 3.4 days for the recommended and double recommended dose, respectively. The PHI was slightly longer for tomatoes, at 2.69 and 5.82 days, respectively. Tomatoes consistently exhibited longer PHIs than peppers, despite faster dissipation rates due to their lower EU-MRL (0.7 mg/kg for tomatoes vs 1.5 mg/kg for peppers).

The calculated NEDI ranged from 8.37 × 10–04 to 2.81 × 10–03 mg/kg bw and from 1.81 × 10–03 to 4.12 × 10–03 mg/kg bw for tomatoes and peppers, respectively. The estimated HQ values support the safety evaluation of these PHIs. The HQ values for peppers varied from 18.15 to 41.20%, suggesting that the risk is minimal even at the higher dosage because the HQ is less than 100%. Similarly, the HQ values of tomatoes varied from 8.37 to 28.05% (Table ), indicating minimal consumer risk under the assessed circumstances.

Conclusions

This study highlights the successful development and validation of an LC–MS/MS method for precisely quantifying E/Z metaflumizone residues in tomatoes and peppers. The method’s compliance with SANTE guidelines, combined with its high sensitivity and accuracy, establishes it as a reliable procedure for analyzing metaflumizone residues. The method’s limit of quantification (0.005 mg/kg) ensures detection well below regulatory limits, offering a robust framework for assessing pesticide safety. Field trials revealed that metaflumizone residues dissipate following first-order kinetics, with distinct dissipation patterns in tomatoes and peppers. Tomatoes exhibited half-lives ranging from 2.99 to 3.14 days, while peppers showed slightly longer half-lives of 3.47 to 3.53 days. The calculated preharvest intervals (PHIs) varied between 2.69 and 5.82 days for tomatoes and 1.86 to 3.4 days for peppers, providing practical guidance for safe harvesting timelines. Risk assessments demonstrated that dietary exposure to metaflumizone and its hazard quotient (HQ) values were within acceptable limits. These findings highlighted the minimal risk for consumers when metaflumizone is used. This research comprehensively evaluates metaflumizone’s behavior in high-value crops by integrating method development with practical field data.
