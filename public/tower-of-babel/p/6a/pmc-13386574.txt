Immunoinformatic design of a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa
Authors: Maryam Babaei, Behzad Shahbazi, Parisa Sarkoohi, Abolfazl Azadi, Kimia Jafari Ranjbar, Khadijeh Ahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPseudomonas aeruginosa, a World Health Organization (WHO) priority pathogen, causes severe multidrug-resistant (MDR) infections in immunocompromised and hospitalized patients, highlighting the critical need for effective vaccines. To this end, we designed a novel multi-epitope vaccine by integrating immunodominant domains from three key virulence proteins (PcrV, OprE, and CupC2) with the cholera toxin B subunit (CTB) as a mucosal adjuvant.MethodsImmunoinformatics was used to predict epitopes and construct a vaccine with EAAAKEAAAK linkers. In silico assessments included antigenicity, allergenicity, solubility, molecular docking with TLR2/TLR4, 150 ns MD simulations, PDBsum interaction analysis, and C-ImmSim immune profiling.ResultsBoth the adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted candidates were predicted to be non-allergenic, with antigenicity scores of 0.6557 and 0.7338, respectively, indicating strong immunogenic potential. Both constructs were also predicted to exhibit high solubility, with scores exceeding the threshold (0.927 and 0.729). Each vaccine candidate showed robust interactions with TLR2 and TLR4; however, the adjuvanted construct demonstrated superior binding affinity to both receptors. Molecular docking of the adjuvanted vaccine candidate followed by 150 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulations confirmed stable and strong interactions with TLR2 and TLR4, as evidenced by RMSD values of 0.64 nm and 0.73 nm, and binding energies of ΔG = − 15.9 kcal/mol and − 12.7 kcal/mol, respectively further supporting their high immunogenic potential. Moreover, in silico immune simulation data predicted rapid induction of protective antibodies and cytokines and revealed that adjuvant-enhanced responses substantially improved the stability of the TLR2 complex.ConclusionThese computational results suggest that our multi-antigen vaccine candidate may have potential against Pseudomonas aeruginosa infections. Further validation through gene synthesis, murine immunogenicity studies (e.g., BALB/c models), and challenge experiments is required.Graphical Abstract Schematic overview of the vaccine design process for Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Background

Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a Gram-negative, aerobic, rod-shaped, slightly bent, and motile bacterium. This bacterium’s large genome compared to other bacteria allows for high genetic variability and growth in various organisms. P. aeruginosa is a leading cause of numerous hospital-acquired infections in patients with cystic fibrosis, extensive burns, and immunocompromised individuals, leading to life-threatening complications such as pneumonia, sepsis, and urinary tract infections [1]. According to a 2024 report by the CDC, P. aeruginosa is recognized as one of seven antimicrobial-resistant pathogens frequently encountered in healthcare environments. In parallel, the national antimicrobial resistance surveillance report from Jordan has placed P. aeruginosa on a list of eleven bacterial and fungal species deemed to be of significant clinical and public health concern. Furthermore, evidence from a regional study covering the Middle East and North Africa indicated that the rate of multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa isolated from clinical specimens reached 52.5% [2].

The emergence of multidrug resistance (MDR) and extensive drug resistance (XDR) in this organism has rendered treatment especially challenging. More than 67% of clinical isolates are resistant to third-generation cephalosporins, 45% to carbapenems, and 32% to aminoglycosides [3]. Resistance mechanisms of P. aeruginosa include the synthesis of extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs), multidrug efflux pumps (e.g., MexAB-OprM), and the capacity to create biofilms that are impenetrable to antibiotics and to host immune attacks [4, 5]. The WHO’s 2024 update to the Bacterial Priority Pathogens List (BPPL) includes P. aeruginosa as a high-priority pathogen, particularly focusing on carbapenem-resistant strains (CRPA). This reflects the global threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and the increasing difficulty in treating infections caused by this bacterium [6]. The limited availability of effective therapeutic agents has led to significant research into preventive measures. Over the past few decades, substantial efforts have been made to develop effective vaccines against P. aeruginosa, with several candidates being evaluated. However, none have come to reaching the approval stage [7]. Therefore, some strategies or combinations, such as immunization, can be studied as an effective preventive method against this pathogen, especially in high-risk groups.

Vaccines are perhaps the most accessible and cost-effective therapeutic options, having been employed to prevent or treat diseases for over two centuries. Because single-epitope vaccines are generally weakly immunogenic, multi-epitope construct formats have been engineered to induce antigenicity (ability to activate the immune system) and immunogenicity (protective efficacy). Epitope vaccines are designed by the combination of T-cell epitopes (e.g., peptides that activate CD8 + cytotoxic T cells and CD4 + helper T cells) and B-cell epitopes (e.g., proteins) [8, 9]. Multi-epitope vaccine design has enabled the simultaneous utilization of CTL, HTL, and B-cell epitopes in light of recent developments [10]. Adjuvants also play a very significant role in enhancing the efficacy of these vaccines; for instance, the cholera toxin B subunit (CTB) improves antigen availability by binding to the GM1 receptor on immune cells, thereby inducing IgG and IgA responses, and it could be a wonderful choice as an adjuvant in vaccine formulation [11, 12]. For example, mucosal immunization studies with vaccines against P. aeruginosa infection have shown that CTB can strengthen the immune response to a P. aeruginosa vaccine, leading to better protection against pneumonia caused by this bacterium [13, 14]. The development of sophisticated computer algorithms and databases has facilitated the easy identification of the best epitopes and the successful design of multi-valency constructs. These methods create a potential for the safe and effective vaccine development with lower side effects [15].

In designing vaccines, the knowledge of virulence factors of bacteria is pivotal in identifying their target proteins and understanding their roles in a bacterium’s ability to cause disease, which presents exciting possibilities for new vaccine design. In this study, the virulence strategies of P. aeruginosa examined mainly include bacterial cell surface-associated proteins. Proteins such as PcrV (a part of the Type III secretion system), OprE (an outer membrane porin involved in the export of antibiotics), and CupC2 (involved in biofilm formation and attachment to the host) have been demonstrated to be potential targets. These proteins play critical roles in bacterial virulence, pathology, and survival [16–18]. One of the few vaccine candidates to reach Phase II/III clinical trials against P. aeruginosa is the IC43 vaccine candidate, a recombinant protein vaccine based on the outer membrane proteins OprF and OprI. In a randomized, placebo-controlled Phase II/III trial involving mechanically ventilated intensive care unit patients, IC43 was well tolerated and immunogenic, but did not show a significant reduction in overall mortality or P. aeruginosa infection rates and failed at that stage [19]. Recent immunoinformatics studies have successfully applied multi-epitope reverse vaccinology against highly variable viruses. For instance, a mutation-based vaccine against Lassa virus (LASV) incorporated wild and mutated glycoprotein epitopes, showing strong TLR2 binding (− 19.9 kcal/mol) and stability over 100 ns simulation [20]. Similarly, a multi-epitope vaccine against Coxsackievirus A10 (CVA10) using β-defensin as an adjuvant achieved high population coverage and stable TLR2/TLR3 interactions [21]. These findings support the potential of epitope-based strategies with appropriate adjuvants, reinforcing our approach to designing a multi-epitope vaccine.

This study aims to identify the most conserved and potent immune-stimulating epitopes of the CupC2, OprE, and PcrV proteins by employing epitope-density motifs and designing a multi-epitope vaccine with the inclusion of the CTB adjuvant which is evolved to protect against P. aeruginosa infection. The vaccine aims to reduce antibiotic dependence and prevent healthcare-associated infections.

Materials and methods

In this study, a recombinant multi-epitope vaccine candidate was designed against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa. To improve the clarity of the workflow, a flowchart summarizing the main steps of the study is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the computational workflow for multi-epitope vaccine design against multidrug-resistant P. aeruginosa

Obtaining the protein sequences for PcrV, OprE, and CupC2

The PcrV, OprE, and CupC2 protein sequences from P. aeruginosa were obtained from the UniProtKB database (https://www.uniprot.org/). This database provides user-friendly access for investigating and understanding specific protein sequences and their associated data [22]. The target proteins were evaluated for antigenicity using the VaxiJen v2.0 web server (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) [23].

T-cell epitope prediction

Accurate prediction of T-cell epitopes is critical for designing robust humoral and cellular immune responses, making it a fundamental step in vaccine development. To identify T-cell epitopes, we employed the RANKPEP ID server [24] and the Immune Epitope Database and Analysis Resource (IEDB) server (http://tools.immuneepitope.org/mhcii/).

For MHC Class II epitope prediction, we selected the DRB1 allelic group (*0101, *0301, *0401, *0701, *0801, *1101, *1301, and *1501), covering ~ 98% of the global human population. For MHC Class I, we analyzed the alleles of HLA-A: A01 (9mer), A02 (11mer), A03 (11mer), A11 (11mer), A24 (9mer), A2902 (9mer), A31 (9mer), A3301 (9mer), A6801 (11mer), and A0101. Other HLA Class I: HLA-G, HLA-X, HLA-E and a range of HLA-B alleles: 08 (11mer), 07 (11mer), 07:02 (9mer), 14, 15, 18, 27, 35, 3801, 39,011, 4002, 44, 51, 53, 5401, 5502, 5701, 5801, 61, and 62 (all 9mer) [25].

Prediction of linear B-cell epitopes

For vaccine design, the prediction of linear B-cell epitopes was performed using three databases: the IEDB (hosting Bepipred and Kolaskar & Tongaonkar Antigenicity tools), BCPred, and the Bepipred server. Epitopes with optimal scores from candidate proteins were selected, with priority given to those located within structurally protected regions of the proteins.

The IEDB compiles experimental data on epitope identification, immune receptor specificity, host organisms, induced immune responses, and associated immune factors. Meanwhile, BepiPred predicts epitope regions by evaluating physicochemical properties, including hydrophobicity and mobility [26].

Selection of epitope-rich regions and design of vaccine candidates

To identify the most promising target domains for vaccine design, we selected regions of three proteins (PcrV, OprE, and CupC2) that exhibited the highest epitope abundance. Our selection was guided by three key criteria: (1) high epitope prediction scores, (2) strong sequence conservation, and (3) supporting evidence from prior studies. To maximize immunogenicity, we incorporated multiple epitopes, focusing specifically on domains rich in B-cell and T-cell epitopes.

The final vaccine construct was assembled by linking these epitopes with an EAAAKEAAAK peptide spacer, which was chosen to maintain α-helical conformation and minimize steric hindrance, and subsequently fusing them to the CTB adjuvant.

Physical and chemical characteristics of the final designed structure

The physical and chemical properties of the final structure of the designed vaccines were evaluated using the Protparam web server (http://expasy.org/tools/protparam.html). The analysis focused on several basic protein properties, including molecular weight, number of amino acids, estimated half-life, aliphatic index, number of charged amino acids, isoelectric point, amino acid composition, instability index, and the grand mean hydrophobicity [27]. A comparative analysis of these properties enhances our knowledge of proteins’ functional and structural characteristics; moreover, it provides valuable insights into molecular evolution, making it an essential approach for advancing our understanding of protein characteristics [28].

Assessment of allergenicity, toxicity, solubility, and antigenicity of the designed protein

Antigenicity prediction is a key step in the design of vaccines and antibody-based therapeutics. The VaxiJen v2.0 web server (https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html) was developed to assess the antigenic potential of engineered vaccine candidates. This platform utilizes an Automatic Cross-Covariance (ACC) method, which converts protein sequences into standardized vectors [23].

Evaluating the allergenicity of vaccine candidate peptides is an essential step in the design of safe vaccines. Peptides selected as epitopes must trigger an immune response while decisively avoiding characteristics that could induce allergic or autoimmune reactions in the body. This evaluation is particularly vital for novel vaccines, such as peptide or mRNA vaccines, because synthetic peptides or engineered proteins can resemble known allergens. We employed the AllerTOP v2.1 web server (http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/AllerTOP/) to thoroughly assess the allergenicity of the designed protein, focusing on critical physical and chemical properties [29].

Additionally, we used the SoluProt 1.0 web server (https://loschmidt.chemi.muni.cz/soluprot/) to predict protein solubility. This tool, which is one of the latest machine learning-based solubility predictors, employs gradient boosting machines and analyzes 96 sequence-based features, including amino acid composition, sequence identity to PDB entries, and aggregated physicochemical properties [30].

To assess potential toxicity, we employed the ToxinPred server (https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred/) [31]. The epitope screening criteria used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

Prediction of secondary and tertiary structures

Predicting the secondary and tertiary structures of vaccine-related proteins is a critical step in vaccine design. Bioinformatics tools accelerate this process, enhancing both the speed and accuracy of research, while molecular technologies provide deeper insights into pathogen-host interactions.

The secondary structure of the vaccine protein was predicted using the Garnier-Osguthorpe-Robson (GOR IV) method (https://npsa-prabi.ibcp.fr/NPSA/npsa_gor4.html), which incorporates parameters derived from experimentally determined tertiary structures (e.g., via X-ray crystallography) [32]. Additionally, three-dimensional protein structures offer essential insights into protein function and regulation. For this study, tertiary structure modeling was performed using the Robetta server (https://robetta.bakerlab.org/).

Evaluation of the tertiary structure of the designed proteins

The tertiary structure of proteins, which defines their three-dimensional conformation, plays a crucial role in determining their biological function by mediating interactions with other molecules, such as antibodies or cellular receptors. Given the importance of structural integrity, computational methods are indispensable for evaluating designed vaccine proteins. In this study, the tertiary structures of the vaccine constructs were assessed using three widely used web servers: MolProbity (http://molprobity.biochem.duke.edu) [33], ProSA-web (https://prosa.services.came.sbg.ac.at/prosa.php) [34], and the SAVES server (https://saves.mbi.ucla.edu) [35]. MolProbity evaluates macromolecular structures by calculating the MolProbity score, clash score, and Ramachandran plot statistics, providing a comprehensive assessment of structural validity [33]. ProSA-web was employed to analyze the global quality of the structures through Z-scores and energy plots, where deviations from the typical range observed in native proteins may indicate potential structural inaccuracies. Additionally, the SAVES server was used to generate Ramachandran plots, which assess the torsion angles of vaccine residues, both adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine candidates, to determine their distribution in favored, allowed, and outlier regions.

Prediction of conformational B cell epitopes

The structural epitopes of the candidate vaccine protein were predicted using the ElliPro web server (http://tools.iedb.org/ellipro/). This web server employs a modified version of Thornton’s Jmol MODELLER method for predicting antibody epitopes [36].

Investigation of the interaction of recombinant proteins with receptors of the immune system

The crystallographic structures of TLR2 (PDB: 5D3I) and TLR4 (PDB: 7MLM) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB, https://www.rcsb.org). Using UCSF Chimera (v1.5.3), water molecules and ligands were removed, hydrogen atoms along with charges were added via the Dock Prep tool [37]. TLR2 and TLR4 are critical immune system receptors in the defense against P. aeruginosa [38]. Key amino acids in the active site of TLR2 include Ile319, Leu324, Leu317, Pro352, Phe322, Phe349, Phe325, Val348, and Tyr326 [39]. Also, the active site of TLR4 receptor contains important amino acids, including Arg90, Glu92, Ser120, Arg434, Ser413, Ser386, Arg380, Lys341, Lys263, and Gln339 [40]. In addition to the canonical active-site residues, the N-terminal LRR region spanning residues 80–150 of TLR4 is known to play a critical role in initial ligand recognition, particularly for larger protein antigens and adjuvants such as CTB. Therefore, we also mapped interactions within this region, as engagement of the N-terminal domain can facilitate proper spatial orientation of the vaccine-receptor complex and contribute to allosteric modulation of TLR4 signaling [40]. Protein interactions between the designed proteins and TLR2 and TLR4 were analyzed by the ClusPro 2.0 web server (https://cluspro.bu.edu/home.php) [41]. In addition, the PRODIGY web server was used to predict the binding energy of these protein complexes; PRODIGY is adept at estimating the binding affinity of biological complexes and identifying biological interfaces [42]. Finally, the residues involved in vaccine-receptor interactions were mapped using the PDBsum server [43].

MM-GBSA binding free energy analysis

The binding free energies of the vaccine-receptor complexes were calculated using the MM-GBSA (Molecular Mechanics with Generalized Born and Surface Area) method via the HawkDock web server (http://cadd.zju.edu.cn/hawkdock/) [44].

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for 150 ns using GROMACS 2021 and the OPLS-AA force field to evaluate the stability of the non-adjuvanted vaccine, adjuvanted vaccine, and vaccine-receptor complexes [45]. To set up the system, each complex was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a 1 nm buffer and neutralized with Na⁺/Cl⁻ ions to compensate for any charge imbalances arising from ligand-receptor interactions and solvation. Ligand-receptor interactions can alter the net charge of the system, and the addition of water molecules shifts the charge away from zero. During simulation, the system is neutralized by adding a Cl⁻ ion if the net charge is positive, or an Na⁺ ion if it is negative. Energy minimization and equilibration were performed using the NVT ensemble (equilibration at 300 K for 100 ps) followed by the NPT ensemble (equilibration at 300 K and 0.1 bar for 100 ps) with the Parrinello-Rahman barostat. Simulation parameters included long-range electrostatics handled via the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) algorithm with a 10 Å cutoff, van der Waals interactions calculated with a 1 nm cutoff, and covalent bonds constrained using the LINCS algorithm. Following equilibration, a 150 ns production MD run was conducted. Complex stability was assessed by calculating the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), principal component analysis (PCA), hydrogen bond analysis (H-bond), and radius of gyration (Rg).

Simulation of the immune system

To estimate the probability of inducing the required immune response against the designed vaccine, non-adjuvanted and adjuvanted antigens have been studied through immunosuppression and immune response profiling. For this analysis, the C-ImmSim simulation web server (http://150.146.2.1/C-IMMSIM/index.php) was utilized to evaluate the induction of immune responses for the designed products. Simulations were conducted with default settings, with antigen injection occurring at time steps of 1, 336, and 672. In this simulation, a 10µL volume was used, involving 1000 steps, and both LPS-free and adjuvanted vaccines were included [46].

Codon optimization and cloning simulation

Codon optimization was performed using the JCat tool specifically for the E. coli K12 strain to increase efficiency and recombinant production. At this stage, the GC index (ratio of G and C nucleotides) and the codon adaptation index (CAI), an indicator of gene expression level based on codon usage alignment with E. coli K12, were calculated using the JCat tool [47]. The most optimized codons of both the non-adjuvanted and adjuvanted vaccine candidates were inserted into the pET28a(+) vector and evaluated with the SnapGene tool 3.2.1 version for double digestion by NcoI and XhoI enzymes.

Results

Retrieval of protein sequences for OmpA, OMPK17, and fimbriae proteins

Protein sequences for PcrV (Accession number: G3XD49), OprE (Accession number: G3XDA5), and CupC2 (Accession number: Q9I4 × 6) proteins were obtained from the UniProtKB database alignment. The antigenicity of OprE, PcrV, and CupC2 proteins was 0.8234, 0.4372, and 0.6662, respectively.

Prediction of B-cell epitopes

Humoral immunity‏ ‏is essential in providing complete protection against bacterial infection, and B cells are potent inducers of humoral immunity [48]. Identification of antigenic B cell epitopes is therefore important and useful in the process of creating new vaccine candidates against P. aeruginosa. B-cell epitopes for the three proteins PcrV, OprE, and CupC2 were predicted utilizing the BCPred (score range of 0.9-1.0 for high-probability epitopes), Bepipred (Score > 0.5), and IEDB servers (Kolaskar and Tongaonkar, Score > 1.000) [49] (Supplementary Table S2).

Prediction of T-cell epitopes

CD4 + T cells are significantly increased during primary infection with bacterial infection, and T cell-deficient mice are not resistant to reinfection, which implicates T cells in the protective immune response to this bacterium [50]. For the prediction of T cell epitopes, The IEDB and Rankpep databases were also utilized. A set of MHC II alleles, including DRB1*0101, *0301, *0401, *0701, *0801, *1101, *1301, and *1501, was chosen to cover most human genetic backgrounds. The most relevant epitopes, the top-scoring ones, were selected (Supplementary Table S3).

Selection of epitope-rich regions and vaccine candidates design

Seven protein regions containing conserved B‑ and T‑cell epitopes were selected for vaccine construction (Table 1). The selected regions were then linked together using the rigid alpha‑helical linker EAAAK, repeated twice as EAAAKEAAAK, to ensure optimal spatial separation. This linker forms a stable alpha‑helical structure that prevents undesirable interdomain interference between adjacent epitopes and reduces non‑specific aggregation [51]. To further enhance immunogenicity, the CTB adjuvant was incorporated at the N‑terminus of the construct. A total of 12 protein sequences were generated by different rearrangements of the seven selected regions from the three proteins of PcrV, OprE, and CupC2 and the CTB adjuvant. Each construct was evaluated for physicochemical properties, allergenicity, toxicity, antigenicity, solubility, and tertiary structure characteristics. Based on these comprehensive analyses, the most promising vaccine candidate—one that was antigenic, non‑allergenic, soluble, and had a plausible tertiary structure—was selected (Fig. 2). Comparative evaluation of constructs with and without the CTB adjuvant identified the optimal vaccine candidate against P. aeruginosa (Fig. 2).

Evaluation of antigenicity, allergenicity, toxicity, and solubility of the designed proteins

The allergenicity of the designed protein constructs was assessed using the AllerTOP v.2.1 web server, which predicted that both the adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine candidates were non-allergenic. Antigenicity was assessed using the VaxiJen server, and a score of 0.6557 was obtained for the adjuvanted vaccine candidate and 0.7338 for the non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate, confirming their antigenic potential.

Solubility analysis, performed with the SoluProt server, indicated high solubility for both candidates, with scores of 0.927 (adjuvanted vaccine candidate) and 0.729 (non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate), both exceeding the server’s threshold. Additionally, toxicity was assessed using ToxinPred, which confirmed that neither candidate exhibited toxic properties (Supplementary Table S4).

Prediction and validation of secondary and tertiary structures

The secondary structure of the designed constructs was analyzed using GOR IV software. The predicted secondary structures of the non-adjuvanted and adjuvanted proteins are detailed in Table 3 and in Fig. 3a–c for the adjuvanted vaccine candidate and in Supplementary Fig. 1a–c for the non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate. Tertiary structures for both proteins with and without adjuvant were predicted using the Robetta web server, and the most suitable models were selected after rigorous evaluation. Structural validation using MolProbity, ProSA-web, and SAVES servers confirmed acceptable quality for both constructs. Ramachandran plots showed that > 96% of residues were located in favored regions, ProSA z-scores fell within the range typical of native proteins, and clash scores were within the 97th–98th percentile (Table 4).

Conformational B-cell epitopes prediction

Unlike linear epitopes, conformational structures are the primary targets of naturally occurring neutralizing antibodies, making them essential for rational vaccine design. Therefore, predicting and incorporating conformational epitopes is critical for developing effective peptide‑based or structure‑based vaccine candidates that mimic native pathogen conformations [51]. The 3D structure analysis of the vaccine candidates with the ElliPro server identified regions that correspond to conformational epitopes. The predicted conformational B-cell epitopes for the non-adjuvanted protein ranged from 0.613 to 0.804, and this score for the adjuvanted protein ranged from 0.568 to 0.872 (Table 5).

Protein–protein molecular docking

ClusPro 2.0 software was used to analyze protein-protein interactions between the vaccine candidates (with and without adjuvant) and the TLR4 and TLR2 receptors. Several parameters were used to select the best interaction model, including weighted score, number of binding contacts, binding affinity (Kd), and binding free energy. Molecular interactions between residues in the vaccine-receptor complexes were visualized using PDBsum (Figs. 4 and 5). The strongest protein-protein complexes were selected based on the following criteria: lowest energy (most negative), lowest Kd (highest binding affinity), most negative weighted score, and largest number of amino acids involved in binding, particularly those contacting active site residues. Evaluate the stability of structures using molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted for 150 ns on the designed vaccine candidate structures and their associated protein-receptor complexes in an aqueous environment under simulated physiological conditions. The main goal of these long-term simulations was to assess the structural stability of these biological systems over time in an environment close to real cellular conditions. RMSD analysis of the adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine candidates and their complexes with the receptor revealed that the two vaccine candidates (with and without adjuvant) remained stable throughout the simulation. The average RMSD for the adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted proteins was 0.87 and 0.58 nm, respectively (Fig. 6a). In the case of the TLR2- vaccine candidate complexes, the non-adjuvanted complex with an average RMSD of 0.45 nm showed some fluctuations during the simulation, but these fluctuations were less than 0.3 nm, indicating the relative stability of the complex. On the other hand, the adjuvanted TLR2-protein complex with an average RMSD of 0.64 nm fluctuated very minimally and was very stable throughout the simulation. Furthermore, the TLR4- vaccine candidate complexes (adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted) were completely stable throughout the simulation with average RMSDs of 0.73 and 0.43 nm, respectively (Fig. 6a). The Rg parameter was used to assess the changes in vaccine candidate structure compactness during the simulation. The results showed that the adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine candidates were stable alone (with average Rg of 2.03 and 2.42 nm, respectively) and also in interaction with the TLR4 receptor (average Rg for the adjuvanted vaccine candidate: 3.36 nm and for the non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate: 3.16 nm) and TLR2 (average Rg for the adjuvanted vaccine candidate: 3.96 nm and for the non-adjuvanted: 3.69 nm) during the simulation (Fig. 6b). RMSF analysis revealed distinct flexibility patterns associated with the receptor-binding interfaces (Fig. 6c). For TLR2, most interactions occurred within residues 200–400 of the vaccine constructs, which exhibited moderate fluctuations, indicating the flexibility required for optimal receptor interaction. For TLR4, the binding interface was primarily located up to residue 150, which exhibited moderate RMSF values. In contrast, residues beyond 400 exhibited very little fluctuation, indicating a stable structural core that is not directly involved in receptor binding. These results confirm that the vaccine constructs maintain appropriate flexibility in the regions involved in the interaction while maintaining a rigid scaffold for overall stability.

To assess the stability of the vaccine-receptor complexes, the number of hydrogen bonds formed between each structure and the TLR receptors was monitored during the simulation (Fig. 6d). The vaccine-TLR4 complex with helper showed the highest average number of hydrogen bonds (17.42), followed by the vaccine-TLR2 complex with helper (15.59). In contrast, the vaccine-TLR4 complexes without helper and vaccine-TLR2 complexes without helper formed fewer hydrogen bonds, with averages of 14.04 and 13.50, respectively. These results indicate that the addition of the CTB helper significantly increases the intermolecular hydrogen bonding with both TLR2 and TLR4.

PCA was performed to evaluate the collective motions and conformational flexibility of the complexes over the 150 ns MD simulation (Fig. 7a–f). Each point represents a snapshot of the protein’s conformation projected onto the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), which capture the dominant dynamic modes of the system. Both vaccine candidate structures, with and without adjuvant, are stable and compact (Fig. 7a–b). The adjuvanted vaccine‑TLR2 complex (Fig. 7c) exhibited the most compact cluster among all complexes, reflecting the highest conformational stability and the most restrained collective motions. This indicates that the adjuvanted vaccine forms a stable and rigid complex with TLR2. The adjuvanted vaccine‑TLR4 complex (Fig. 7d) also showed a compact cluster, though slightly more dispersed than panel c, indicating good stability. The non-adjuvanted vaccine‑TLR2 complex (Fig. 7e) displayed moderate dispersion, while the non-adjuvanted vaccine‑TLR4 complex (Fig. 7f) showed the largest conformational spread, with points widely distributed across the PC space. This broad dispersion suggests weak and unstable binding between the non-adjuvanted vaccine and TLR4, leading to high conformational flexibility and possibly partial dissociation during the simulation. Collectively, these PCA results demonstrate that the CTB adjuvant significantly enhances conformational stability, particularly in complex with TLR2.

Discussion

P. aeruginosa, one of the WHO priority pathogens, is a significant threat to immunocompromised individuals, burn victims, and cystic fibrosis patients, with the high mortality driven by intrinsic resistance mechanisms and plasticity. These problems highlight the imperative need for preventive strategies like vaccines. While traditional methods exist, the pathogenic nature of P. aeruginosa calls for a new vaccine design that can establish an equilibrium between humoral and cellular ‏immunity in order to effectively neutralize the toxin secreted by the pathogen, prevent biofilm formation, and kill infected cells.

While previous vaccine efforts against P. aeruginosa focused on single antigens like OprF or flagellin, clinical translation remains elusive due to limited coverage of the pathogen’s multifaceted virulence repertoire. For instance, OprF-based vaccines (e.g., IC43) showed partial efficacy but failed in phase II/III trials, highlighting the need for multi-target strategies [19]. Similarly, previous studies focused more on one or two pathogenic factors to design a multi-epitope vaccine that could not simultaneously act against factors affecting biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and pathogenic factors [52–55], however, in this study, we bridge this gap by integrating three underexplored targets, including PcrV (essential for T3SS-mediated cytotoxicity), OprE (key porin for carbapenem resistance), and CupC2 (critical for biofilm adherence) [56, 57], into a multi epitope vaccine. Crucially, we incorporated CTB adjuvant to enhance mucosal immunity via GM1-ganglioside binding, a strategy proven effective for enteric pathogens but previously unexplored for P. aeruginosa vaccines [58, 59].

An investigation of the virulence factors of this bacterium reveals that the Type III Secretion System (T3SS) has a crucial role in the secretion of effector toxins into the host cell [60]. PcrV protein is an important T3SS needle-tip protein of P. aeruginosa, which plays a key role in allowing the translocator proteins PopB/D to form pores on the host cell membrane through which effector proteins are secreted into the host cell [55]. Micropore proteins such as OprC, OprD, and OprE constitute major components of the Outer Membrane Proteins (OMPs) of P. aeruginosa. They also serve as specific channels that allow small molecular hydrophilic substances to enter the bacterium. OprE is one of the outer membrane proteins of P. aeruginosa that functions as a porin. It plays an important role in the bacterium’s adaptation to various environments, particularly low-oxygen environments [61]. It also serves as a potential entry pathway for the import of beta-lactam antibiotics and cephalosporins like ceftazidime. This protein plays a major role in the antibiotic resistance of P. aeruginosa [54, 62].

P. aeruginosa biofilm formation is mediated by fimbrial structures assembled on the bacterial surface via chaperone-usher systems (Cup). The CupC2 gene encodes a functional chaperone protein involved in these processes. These proteins play key roles in chaperone activity, heat shock response, bacterial motility, and surface attachment [53, 63].

To design a multi-epitope vaccine candidate targeting PcrV, OprE, and CupC2, an immunoinformatic pipeline was established. This pipeline integrated the prediction of B-cell and T-cell epitopes, structural optimization, and in silico immunological assessment. For B-cell epitope prediction, the BCPred, BepiPred, and IEDB servers were employed. Given the critical role of CD4 + T cells, MHC-II epitopes were identified using IEDB and Rankpep, focusing on a broad range of HLA-DRB1 alleles to maximize population coverage. Epitope-rich regions were selected based on antigenic, protective, and non-allergenic properties. The EAAAKEAAAK linker was introduced between epitopes to facilitate proper folding and presentation, and CTB was incorporated to enhance immune stimulation via TLR2 and TLR4 pathways.

The designed vaccine exhibited favourable physicochemical properties, including a stable instability index (28.55 adjuvanted vaccine, 26.96 non-adjuvanted vaccine), favourable solubility (0.927 adjuvanted vaccine, 0.729 non-adjuvanted vaccine), and non-toxicity. The GRAVY index (-0.171 adjuvanted vaccine, -0.203 non-adjuvanted vaccine) confirmed the hydrophilic properties necessary for interaction with immune receptors. Molecular dynamics simulations (150 ns) showed structural stability with low RMSD (0.87 nm for adjuvanted vaccine, 0.58 nm for non-adjuvanted vaccine) and constant Rg values, indicating minimal structural deformation. Both candidates, adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine, showed strong interaction with TLR2 and TLR4. However, the adjuvanted vaccine showed better binding to TLR2 (ΔG = -15.9 kcal/mol) and TLR4 (ΔG = -12.7 kcal/mol), indicating enhanced receptor interaction compared to the non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate. Our PDBsum analysis revealed significant interactions between the vaccine construct and the TLR4 N-terminal LRR region (residues 80–150), particularly involving Arg90, Glu92, and Ser120 [40, 64]. This domain (LRR1–LRR5) is known to facilitate proper spatial orientation of large ligands within the TLR4 binding groove. We propose that binding to residues 80–150 positions the vaccine favorably within the receptor cleft, priming the complex for subsequent interaction with the canonical active site. This positioning effect is essential for optimal downstream signaling, even without direct contact with central active-site residues [40, 64]. Accordingly, the adjuvanted vaccine, which showed extensive N-terminal interactions, demonstrated higher binding affinity (ΔG = − 12.7 kcal/mol for TLR4) and superior immune stimulation in C-ImmSim simulations. Also, both adjuvanted and non-adjuvanted vaccine candidates established direct interactions with TLR2 active-site residues (Ile319, Pro352, Phe322, Phe349, Phe325, Tyr326). The adjuvanted construct showed enhanced contacts with these residues compared to the non-adjuvanted candidate, consistent with its higher binding affinity (ΔG = − 15.9 kcal/mol) and improved complex stability in MD simulations.

The C-ImmSim in silico simulations show that the adjuvanted vaccine induces superior IgG titers, memory B cells, and a Th1‑biased IFN‑γ response essential for bacterial clearance. The simulated expansion of B and Th cell populations confirms dual humoral and cellular immunity. Overall, these computational results highlight the CTB adjuvant’s role in enhancing vaccine performance.

While Roy et al. [52] computationally targeted OprF and OprI for P. aeruginosa vaccine design, our study introduces key innovations, including a novel multi-epitope strategy combining three antigens (PcrV, OprE, and CupC2) and the incorporation of CTB adjuvant to enhance immunogenicity. This approach addresses limitations of single-antigen vaccines like IC43 (OprF/OprI), which demonstrated only marginal efficacy in Phase III trials (mortality in ventilated ICU patients: 29.2% vs. 27.7% placebo, p = 0.67) [65]. By targeting multiple virulence factors and leveraging adjuvant synergy, our design may offer superior protection compared to conventional candidates.

Comparing our findings with recent successful immunoinformatics studies against other pathogens reinforces the validity of our strategy. For instance, a mutation-based LASV vaccine achieved stability over 100 ns simulation by incorporating wild and mutated epitopes, while a CVA10 multi-epitope vaccine using β-defensin adjuvant showed high population coverage and stable TLR2/TLR3 interactions [19, 20]. Notably, in our study, the inclusion of the CTB adjuvant significantly enhanced TLR2 binding affinity, increasing it from − 12.9 kcal/mol (in the non-adjuvanted vaccine candidate) to − 15.9 kcal/mol (in the adjuvanted vaccine candidate), demonstrating the critical role of CTB in receptor interaction. Our CTB-adjuvanted vaccine also demonstrated robust TLR4 binding (− 12.7 kcal/mol) with stable RMSD values over 150 ns. Collectively, these findings position our multi-epitope construct as a promising vaccine candidate for combatting P. aeruginosa infections, particularly by simultaneously targeting key virulence factors while leveraging CTB-mediated immune enhancement.

Limitations and future perspectives

Despite promising computational results, several inherent limitations must be acknowledged. All findings are in silico only and not experimentally verified. Predictions of antigenicity, sensitization and stability are algorithm dependent and may not fully reflect in vivo conditions. Immunity simulations do not take into account host genetic variation or preexisting immunity, and immunological concerns such as cross-reactivity or excessive inflammation cannot be definitively assessed. Therefore, future validation requires a stepwise experimental approach including in vitro expression, purification, and biophysical characterization of the recombinant protein as well as murine immunogenicity studies, measurement of antibody titer, and cytokine response. Pending successful trial approval, this vaccine candidate holds promise against antibiotic-resistant P. aeruginosa infections, particularly for high-risk populations such as cystic fibrosis patients.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully designed a novel multi‑epitope vaccine candidate against P. aeruginosa by fusing immunodominant regions of PcrV, OprE, and CupC2 with the CTB adjuvant. The importance of this work stems from the increasing threat of multidrug‑resistant P. aeruginosa, particularly for immunocompromised patients and individuals with cystic fibrosis, in whom chronic infections lead to high morbidity and mortality. Our computational analyses demonstrate that the vaccine candidate acts as a multi‑epitope immunogen capable of engaging TLR2 and TLR4, thereby priming a protective immune response while overcoming the limitations of monovalent formulations. The incorporation of CTB as an adjuvant significantly enhances vaccine efficacy and broadens its applicability. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. All findings are purely in silico and cannot fully replicate the complexity of the human immune system. Predicted binding interactions with TLR active‑site residues, immune simulation outcomes, and structural stability assessments require experimental confirmation. Therefore, future validation will require stepwise in vitro and in vivo studies, including protein expression and purification, TLR activation assays, murine immunization studies measuring antibody titers and cytokine responses, and bacterial challenge models. Pending successful experimental validation, this vaccine candidate holds particular promise for high‑risk populations, including cystic fibrosis patients, and represents a viable candidate for clinical translation against antibiotic‑resistant P. aeruginosa infections.
