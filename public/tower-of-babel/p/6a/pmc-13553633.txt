Survival benefit of surgical resection in elderly patients with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma: a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Chaoran Chen, Xuming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeExtrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) predominantly affects elderly patients, yet robust evidence evaluating surgical effectiveness in this population remains limited. This study aimed to determine the independent survival benefit of surgical resection in elderly patients with eCCA using propensity score matching to minimize selection bias.MethodPatients aged ≥ 65 years with non-metastatic eCCA diagnosed between 2010 and 2017 were identified from the SEER database. One-to-one propensity score matching (caliper 0.05 SD) balanced baseline characteristics between surgical (n = 682) and non-surgical (n = 617) groups, yielding 184 matched pairs. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were compared using Kaplan–Meier analysis and multivariable Cox regression.ResultsIn the matched cohort, surgical resection was associated with significantly improved survival for both endpoints. For OS: median survival was 29 vs 8 months (HR = 0.336, 95% CI: 0.267–0.423, P < 0.001), with 5-year OS rates of 25.3% vs 4.6%. For CSS: median survival was 36 vs 8 months (HR = 0.307, 95% CI: 0.237–0.396, P < 0.001), with 5-year CSS rates of 34.5% vs 7.4%. Surgical benefit remained consistent across all age strata (65–74y: HR = 0.32; 75–84y: HR = 0.29; ≥ 85y: HR = 0.34), with no age interaction (P = 0.89). Tumor stage modified treatment effect (P = 0.012), with greater benefit in T1–2 (HR = 0.27) than T3–4 disease (HR = 0.40), though both groups benefited significantly.ConclusionSurgical resection was associated with substantial survival benefit in elderly patients with non-metastatic, resectable eCCA, with approximately 66% reduction in mortality risk persisting across all age groups including octogenarians. Chronological age alone should not preclude surgical evaluation; decisions should incorporate comprehensive geriatric assessment and tumor biology.Keywords: Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, Elderly patients, Surgical treatment, Propensity score matching, Survival analysis, SEER

Introduction

Extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (eCCA) accounts for approximately 80–90% of all cholangiocarcinoma cases and can be anatomically subdivided into perihilar and distal subtypes based on their location within the biliary tree [1, 2]. Despite its relative rarity, the incidence of eCCA has been steadily increasing worldwide over recent decades [3–5]. The prognosis of eCCA remains dismal, with an overall 5-year survival rate of only 13% according to SEER data, declining to approximately 3% in patients with distant metastases [6]. However, due to its insidious onset and nonspecific symptoms, a substantial proportion of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages or older ages, frequently presenting with locally advanced disease [8–10]. Consequently, treatment decisions, particularly regarding surgical candidacy, remain challenging. This challenge is particularly pronounced in elderly patients, where clinicians must balance potential survival benefits against increased perioperative risks, comorbidity burden, and reduced physiological reserve associated with aging [11–13].

Although several single-center studies have reported survival advantages associated with surgical intervention, robust population-based evidence specifically evaluating surgical effectiveness in elderly patients with eCCA remains scarce [14]. Moreover, many prior registry-based studies have either combined intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma into a single analytic cohort or failed to adequately adjust for substantial baseline imbalances, including age, tumor stage, and comorbid conditions, between surgical and non-surgical groups [15–17]. These methodological limitations may substantially confound the true impact of surgical resection on survival outcomes.

Therefore, determining the independent survival benefit of surgical resection in elderly patients with eCCA using a large, nationally representative database while rigorously controlling for selection bias is critically important for informing clinical practice. To our knowledge, despite growing evidence from propensity score-matched analyses in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma [18] , similar rigorous evaluation specifically focusing on extrahepatic disease in elderly populations remains lacking. The present study addresses this critical evidence gap by utilizing contemporary SEER data and employing propensity score matching to construct a well-balanced cohort. Specifically, we sought to: (1) evaluate the independent impact of surgical resection on OS and CSS in elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with eCCA, and (2) identify patient subgroups where surgical benefit may be particularly pronounced or attenuated through comprehensive subgroup analyses, thereby providing evidence-based guidance for individualized treatment decisions in this growing and vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Data sources and study design

Patient data were obtained from the SEER database (SEER Research Data, 17 Registries, November 2024 submission, covering the period from 2000 to 2022). which is openly accessible and freely available for researchers. We used the SEER*Stat software (version 9.0.42.0) with a data user agreement. Patients with eCCA were identified using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) site code C24.0 (extrahepatic bile duct). Eligible histologic subtypes included adenocarcinoma (8140/3), cholangiocarcinoma (8160/3), papillary adenocarcinoma (8260/3), mucinous adenocarcinoma (8480/3), and signet ring cell carcinoma (8490/3). The tumor–node–metastasis (TNM) staging in this study was recoded according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition staging system. It should be noted that SEER records a collaborative stage variable that integrates available clinical, radiological, and pathological information at the time of diagnosis; purely pathological TNM staging was not uniformly available across all patients. Patients were included if they met the following criteria: (1) diagnosed with eCCA between 2010 and 2017; (2) age ≥ 65 years at the time of diagnosis; (3) diagnosis confirmed by positive histology; (4) reporting source not based on autopsy or death certificate; and (5) known surgical treatment status. Patients were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) unknown race; (2) not the first or only primary tumor; (3) survival time less than 1 month or unknown; (4) presence of distant metastasis (M1 stage); (5) missing information on T stage or N stage; or (6) missing treatment information. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata (version 18.0). Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and comparisons between groups were conducted using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. OS and CSS were evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between the surgery and no-surgery groups were assessed with the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to identify independent prognostic factors for OS and CSS, with hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported.

To reduce the influence of potential confounding factors, PSM was conducted to balance baseline characteristics between patients who underwent surgical resection and those who did not. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model incorporating clinically relevant covariates, including age group, sex, race, histological grade, T stage, N stage, chemotherapy status, and radiotherapy status. One-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was applied using a caliper width of 0.05 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. After matching, survival analyses were repeated in the matched cohort to assess the robustness of the results, and subgroup analyses were performed to explore the consistency of surgical benefit across predefined patient subgroups. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study utilized publicly available, de-identified data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) Program database. As the SEER database contains only de-identified patient information and is publicly accessible for research purposes, this study was exempt from institutional review board approval and informed consent requirements in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health and our institutional ethics committee. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the ethical standards of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Before PSM, a total of 1,299 patients were included in the analysis, comprising 682 patients (52.5%) in the surgery group and 617 patients (47.5%) in the no-surgery group. Significant differences were observed between the two groups across nearly all baseline characteristics (P < 0.001). Compared with the no-surgery group, patients who underwent surgical resection were younger, with a higher proportion aged 65–74 years (61.4% vs. 36.8%), more likely to be male (66.7% vs. 51.2%), had more clearly defined and better-differentiated tumors, presented with earlier T and N stages, and were more likely to receive chemotherapy (50.7% vs. 40.5%).

After PSM, a well-balanced cohort of 368 patients was obtained, including 184 patients in the surgery group and 184 patients in the no-surgery group. Following matching, the standardized mean differences for all covariates were less than 0.1, and no significant differences were observed between the two groups (all P > 0.05, Table 1), indicating good balance in age, sex, race, tumor stage, tumor grade, and adjuvant treatment variables. (details in Table 1) Among the 184 non-surgical patients, the majority (171 patients, 92.9%) did not undergo surgery because it was not medically recommended (including contraindications due to comorbidities). Only a small minority (8 patients, 4.3%) declined or did not proceed with recommended surgery. To further characterize the extent of selection into non-surgical management, we examined surgical receipt rates across age strata in the full unmatched cohort. The proportion of patients undergoing surgical resection declined markedly with advancing age: 64.9% among patients aged 65–74 years, 48.6% among those aged 75–84 years, and 14.9% among those aged ≥ 85 years (P < 0.001). This age-dependent gradient in surgical receipt is consistent with progressive decline in functional reserve or frailty with aging, and may also reflect age-related patterns of surgical referral — dimensions of selection bias that are not captured by the available SEER variables and cannot be fully addressed by propensity score matching.

Survival outcome

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses showed that patients who underwent surgical resection had significantly improved OS and CSS compared with those who did not receive surgery in both the unmatched and propensity score–matched cohorts (log-rank P < 0.001 for all comparisons; Fig. 2). In the unmatched cohort, surgical treatment was associated with lower risks of all-cause mortality (OS: HR = 0.326, 95% CI: 0.288–0.368) and cancer-specific mortality (CSS: HR = 0.308, 95% CI: 0.269–0.352).

After PSM, differences in survival outcomes between the surgery and no-surgery groups remained statistically significant. In the matched cohort, the surgery group demonstrated higher 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSS rates (78.0%, 48.8%, and 34.5%, respectively) compared with the no-surgery group (38.5%, 10.8%, and 7.4%, respectively). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis in the matched cohort showed that surgical resection was associated with reduced risks of death for both OS (HR = 0.336, 95% CI: 0.267–0.423) and CSS (HR = 0.307, 95% CI: 0.237–0.396), with all associations reaching statistical significance (P < 0.001). Univariate and multivariate cox regression analyses

Univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between clinicopathological variables and OS and CSS (Table 3). In univariate analyses, T stage was significantly associated with CSS, with patients classified as T2 (HR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.18–2.17; P = 0.003) and T4 (HR = 1.96, 95% CI: 1.29–2.97; P = 0.001) exhibiting a higher risk of cancer-specific mortality compared with those with T1 disease. For OS, patients with T4 tumors also demonstrated a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 1.66, 95% CI: 1.14–2.44; P = 0.009). N1 stage was associated with worse OS in univariate analysis (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.05–1.71; P = 0.018). Poorly differentiated or undifferentiated tumors were associated with increased CSS risk (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 1.03–2.66; P = 0.039).

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses of CSS and OS

Variables with clinical relevance were included in multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models. After adjustment, T stage remained independently associated with CSS, with T2 (HR = 1.52, 95% CI: 1.10–2.09; P = 0.010) and T4 disease (HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.19–2.90; P = 0.007) demonstrating significantly increased cancer-specific mortality compared with T1 disease. For OS, T4 stage showed an increased hazard of death (HR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.00–2.26), although the association did not reach conventional statistical significance (P = 0.052). Age group, sex, race, N stage, tumor grade, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy were not independently associated with OS or CSS in multivariable analyses.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the association between surgical resection and OS across predefined patient subgroups (Fig. 3). Surgical resection was consistently associated with improved OS across most subgroups stratified by age, sex, race, tumor grade, T stage, N stage, chemotherapy status, and radiotherapy status. No significant interaction was observed between surgical treatment and the majority of subgroup variables, indicating a generally consistent association across subgroups.

Discussion

Surgical resection represents the only potentially curative treatment for eCCA and remains the cornerstone of management for resectable disease. However, the insidious nature of early symptoms and aggressive tumor biology result in only a minority of patients being candidates for curative-intent surgery at diagnosis [19–21]. With progressive global aging, elderly patients constitute an increasing proportion of newly diagnosed eCCA cases [22, 23]. Despite this demographic shift, evidence evaluating surgical effectiveness specifically in elderly populations with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma has remained limited. The present study addresses this critical knowledge gap using contemporary, population-based data with rigorous propensity score matching to minimize selection bias.

Advances in perioperative care, anesthetic techniques, and surgical strategies have substantially expanded the feasibility of major hepatobiliary procedures in elderly patients over recent decades [24, 25]. Prior studies in hepatocellular carcinoma have demonstrated that advanced age alone does not preclude safe surgical intervention and that long-term outcomes can be comparable between elderly and younger patients, despite higher perioperative complication rates [26–28]. However, most existing evidence has focused on age-based comparisons rather than directly evaluating the survival benefit of surgery versus non-surgical management within elderly cohorts. Furthermore, previous analyses often combined intrahepatic and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma or lacked rigorous adjustment for selection bias. Consequently, the independent survival impact of surgical resection specifically in elderly patients with eCCA has remained inadequately defined.

The present study demonstrates a substantial and consistent association between surgical resection and improved survival in elderly patients with non-metastatic eCCA using a large, contemporary population-based cohort. After rigorous propensity score matching to control for measured confounders, surgical resection was associated with a median OS of 29 months versus 8 months in non-surgical patients (HR = 0.336, 95% CI: 0.267–0.423, P < 0.001), representing a clinically meaningful 66% reduction in all-cause mortality risk. Importantly, this survival association remained consistent across all age strata, including octogenarians and nonagenarians, with no evidence of age-related effect modification (P interaction = 0.89). These findings provide robust, population-based evidence that chronological age alone should not preclude surgical consideration in elderly eCCA patients. To our knowledge, this represents the first large-scale analysis employing propensity score matching to specifically evaluate surgical outcomes in elderly patients with extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, addressing a critical evidence gap in this growing demographic.

Our survival findings align with and extend previous literature. The observed median OS of 29 months and 5-year CSS rate of 34.5% in our surgical cohort fall within ranges reported in prior studies (20–40%) [28, 29], which is particularly remarkable given our exclusive focus on elderly patients (≥ 65 years). Importantly, these outcomes suggest that appropriately evaluated elderly patients may achieve survival outcomes comparable to general population surgical series, challenging the perception that advanced age inherently limits oncologic benefit [29, 30] .

The median OS of 8 months in non-surgical patients reflects the heterogeneity and limited efficacy of non-curative treatments for eCCA. This population likely encompasses patients receiving best supportive care, various chemotherapy regimens, or palliative radiotherapy. Real-world data indicate median OS of only 4.5 months for untreated advanced biliary tract cancer, while first-line chemotherapy extends survival to approximately 8–11 months [31, 32] . Our observed non-surgical survival aligns with this expectation and underscores the magnitude of survival difference associated with surgical resection when feasible.

An important consideration when interpreting these results is the clinical heterogeneity within the non-surgical group. Analysis of SEER-recorded reasons for non-surgical management revealed that 92.9% of non-surgical patients (171/184) did not undergo surgery because it was not medically recommended — reflecting anatomically unresectable disease or medical inoperability due to comorbidity burden — while only 2.7% (5/184) declined surgery by personal preference. The age-stratified surgical receipt rates observed in our cohort (64.9%, 48.6%, and 14.9% for patients aged 65–74, 75–84, and ≥ 85 years, respectively) provide additional evidence of this underlying selection bias: the non-surgical group becomes increasingly enriched with frailer and more functionally compromised patients at older ages, a dimension of selection that PSM cannot fully address given the absence of functional status data in SEER. Despite this predominance of medically-driven non-surgical decisions, the non-surgical group nonetheless encompasses patients who received chemotherapy (45.1% [83/184] in the matched cohort) alongside those managed with best supportive care alone, reflecting diverse clinical scenarios rather than a uniform comparator. The survival difference we observed thus represents a composite comparison of surgical resection against this mixed non-surgical population, and results may differ when surgical resection is compared specifically against modern systemic therapy. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing medically unresectable patients from those who are resectable but decline surgery when interpreting registry-based comparative effectiveness studies.

Critically, the magnitude of surgical benefit remained remarkably consistent across all age strata, including octogenarians and nonagenarians (≥ 85 years: HR = 0.34, P = 0.013). This finding contradicts the common clinical assumption that very elderly patients derive diminished benefit from major surgery. Our results align with a meta-analysis of 2,063 resected eCCA patients demonstrating comparable 5-year survival between younger (≤ 65 years) and older patients (> 65 years): 35.6% versus 34.4% [33]. Collectively, these data indicate that physiological age and functional status, rather than chronological age, should guide surgical decision-making.

Our findings are consistent with recent evidence from intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (ICC). Chen et al. (2023) reported hazard ratios of 0.258 for OS and 0.239 for CSS favoring surgery in elderly ICC patients using similar PSM methodology, slightly more favorable than our eCCA findings (HR = 0.336 and 0.307, respectively). This difference may reflect distinct biological behaviors between ICC and eCCA or differences in surgical complexity. Nevertheless, both studies converge on a consistent message: elderly patients with cholangiocarcinoma who are evaluated for surgical candidacy appear to derive meaningful survival advantages associated with resection [18]. Although perioperative morbidity and mortality can be substantial in elderly patients undergoing major hepatobiliary surgery for eCCA, our findings demonstrate that long-term survival gains significantly outweigh short-term risks in appropriately selected patients [34, 35]. Modern Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) protocols, refined anesthetic techniques, and improved critical care have collectively reduced perioperative complications in elderly surgical populations. When combined with careful patient selection based on functional status and comorbidity assessment, curative-intent surgery remains a viable option for many elderly patients with resectable eCCA.

Subgroup analyses revealed tumor stage as the only significant effect modifier (interaction P = 0.012). While surgical resection conferred survival benefit across all T stages, the magnitude of benefit was greater in early-stage disease (T1–2: HR = 0.27, 73% mortality reduction) compared with locally advanced disease (T3–4: HR = 0.40, 60% mortality reduction). Several factors may explain this differential effect. First, achieving negative resection margins (R0) becomes increasingly difficult with advancing T stage, and margin status is a critical determinant of long-term survival [36] . Second, T3–4 tumors may harbor occult micrometastatic disease not detected by staging studies, limiting curative potential. Importantly however, even patients with T3–4 disease experienced a substantial 60% reduction in mortality with surgery, indicating that locally advanced stage should not be considered an absolute contraindication when R0 resection appears technically feasible.

The absence of independent survival benefit for chemotherapy or radiotherapy in our multivariable models warrants cautious interpretation and should not be construed as evidence against adjuvant therapy. This finding almost certainly reflects unmeasured confounding by indication—patients receiving adjuvant therapy likely harbored higher-risk features (e.g., positive margins, lymph node involvement, higher tumor grade) not fully captured in SEER data. SEER lacks critical prognostic information including resection margin status (R0/R1/R2), extent of lymphadenectomy, specific chemotherapy regimens and doses, and performance status. Furthermore, treatment decisions are influenced by surgeon and institutional practice patterns, creating additional unmeasured confounding. Importantly, high-level evidence supports adjuvant therapy in appropriately selected patients. A recent randomized trial demonstrated that adjuvant chemoradiotherapy plus immunotherapy significantly improved both OS and recurrence-free survival compared with observation alone in resected eCCA patients (HR = 0.43) [37]. Our study emphasizes the primacy of surgical resection as the foundation of curative treatment, while adjuvant therapy likely provides additional benefit in high-risk subgroups such as those with advanced T stage or nodal involvement.

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, as a retrospective observational analysis using an administrative database, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded despite propensity score matching. Key variables unavailable in SEER include ECOG performance status, frailty indices, institutional surgical volume, and detailed comorbidity indices (e.g., Charlson Comorbidity Index) — all of which likely influence both treatment selection and survival outcomes. A particularly important and related limitation is the likely presence of unmeasured selection bias in the non-surgical group. Although 92.9% of non-surgical patients did not receive surgery because it was not medically recommended, this non-recommendation is typically consistent with anatomic unrespectability or medical inoperability. However, SEER does not capture key determinants of surgical candidacy such as ECOG performance status, frailty indices, cardiopulmonary reserve, or Charlson Comorbidity Index. The marked age-dependent decline in surgical receipt observed in our cohort (64.9% in patients aged 65–74, 48.6% in those aged 75–84, and 14.9% in those aged ≥ 85 years) provides indirect evidence of this unmeasured dimension: these disparities likely reflect progressive decline in functional reserve with aging, and may also reflect age-related patterns in surgical referral. Critically, propensity score matching can only balance measured confounders. Crucially, unmeasured selection into non-surgical management, especially functional inoperability, cannot be fully accounted for by PSM. These results are therefore best understood as comparing outcomes in patients deemed surgically fit versus those who were not, rather than as a purely interventional comparison of surgery versus observation in otherwise equivalent patients. To quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounding, we calculated the E-value for our primary OS result (HR = 0.336) [38] . The E-value of 5.41 indicates that an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both surgical receipt and survival by a risk ratio of at least 5.41-fold (on both exposure and outcome sides) to fully explain away the observed association, suggesting the association is relatively robust to residual confounding. Second, SEER lacks resection margin status (R0/R1/R2), which is arguably the strongest determinant of long-term survival after biliary resection, as well as anatomical subtype (perihilar versus distal eCCA), which differ substantially in surgical complexity, morbidity, and prognosis. The extent of lymph node dissection is similarly unavailable. These unmeasured variables may confound the observed associations and limit the ability to provide subtype-specific recommendations. Third, the non-surgical group is heterogeneous: it includes patients who received chemotherapy (45.1% [83/184]) as well as those who received best supportive care alone, reflecting diverse clinical scenarios and performance statuses rather than a uniform comparator. The survival difference we observed thus represents a composite comparison against this mixed group, and results may differ when surgical resection is compared specifically against modern systemic therapy. Fourth, regarding staging, SEER uses a collaborative stage variable that integrates clinical and pathological information at the time of diagnosis; purely pathological TNM staging was not uniformly available across all patients in either group (missing in 25.0% of surgical and 19.6% of non-surgical patients), reflecting the registry's reliance on combined clinical-pathological staging. Fifth, non-cancer-related mortality is a competing risk in this elderly population. We addressed this by reporting cancer-specific survival (CSS) alongside overall survival; the consistency of results across both endpoints (OS HR = 0.336; CSS HR = 0.307) supports the robustness of our findings. Finally, due to the inherent latency of the SEER database and the necessity to ensure a sufficient 5-year follow-up period, our cohort was limited to patients diagnosed through 2017. Consequently, contemporary non-surgical patients may have access to more effective systemic therapies, such as durvalumab-based immunotherapy, which could potentially attenuate the relative survival advantage of surgical resection observed in our study. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted within the context of the evolving treatment landscape. Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths. The use of a large, nationally representative database enhances generalizability to diverse healthcare settings and patient populations across the United States. Rigorous propensity score matching substantially reduced baseline imbalances between surgical and non-surgical groups, strengthening causal inference. Evaluation of both overall and cancer-specific survival provides comprehensive assessment of treatment impact. Detailed subgroup and interaction analyses identified important effect modifiers, particularly tumor stage, informing patient selection for surgery. Finally, the exclusive focus on elderly patients and extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma addresses a specific evidence gap in a growing demographic.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this propensity score-matched analysis reveals a significant and consistent survival association with s surgical resection in elderly patients (≥ 65 years) with non-metastatic extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, with approximately 66% reduction in mortality risk compared with non-surgical management. Importantly, this survival association persists across all age groups, including patients aged ≥ 85 years, with no significant age interaction. While the association is more pronounced in early-stage disease (T1–2), patients with locally advanced tumors (T3–4) also demonstrate substantially improved survival. These findings suggest that surgical resection warrants strong consideration for elderly patients with resectable eCCA when functional status and comorbidity burden are acceptable, though prospective studies are needed to confirm these observational findings.
