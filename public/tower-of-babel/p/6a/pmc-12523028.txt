Removal of organic matters, phosphates and nitrates present in pond wastewater using chemical coagulant solutions
Authors: Nouria Nabbou, Elhassan Benyagoub, Meriem Bekaddour, Boudjemaa Larabi, Abdelmadjid Hamouine
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The study aims to treat and remove of organic matters, phosphates and nitrates present in fish pond wastewater using chemical coagulants at different concentrations [FeSO4, FeCl3, and Al2(SO4)3] through coagulation-precipitation. These effluents are discharged without any treatment. Fish pond wastewaters were subjected to a series of physicochemical and bacteriological analyses before and after treatment according to standard methods. The results of the main physicochemical and bacteriological characteristics of the fish pond wastewater released from a fish farm located in Taghit-Bechar (Southwest of Algeria) show values exceed the Algerian norms for liquid discharges. These values include organic matter (chemical oxygen demand (COD) = 285.42 mgO2/L, biological oxygen demand (BOD) = 226.71 mgO2/L, oxidizable materials (OM) = 56 mgO2/L), total suspended solid (TSS) (45 mg/L), phosphate (2.60 mg/L), nitrate (80 mg/L), fecal coliforms (3.07 log MPN/100mL) and fecal streptococci (3.32 log MPN/100mL). The treatment results showed that the optimum concentration varies around 50 mg/L for ferric chloride and ferric sulfate, and 80 mg/L for aluminum sulfate. At these optimum doses, treatment yields are satisfactory for the abatement of organic pollution, namely; SS, OM, COD, and BOD5 with a rate of 80%, 64.3%, 91%, and 84% for ferric chloride; 87%, 68.6%, 93%, and 86 for ferric sulfate and 95%, 71.5%, 96% and 87 for aluminum sulfate, respectively. Nitrate and phosphate removal performance was excellent, with removal rates of 92% and 97% for ferric chloride, 94% and 99% for ferric sulfate, and 95% and 98% for aluminum sulfate, respectively. The pollutant parameters tested appear to be better eliminated with ferric chloride in a pH range between 6 and 8 for organic matter (MO, COD, BOD), and between 8 and 10 for phosphates and nitrates. The best results were recorded for ferric sulfate and aluminum sulfate. However, the three coagulants effectively removed bacterial pollution indicators, such as fecal coliforms and fecal streptococci.

Introduction

Wastewater is liquid waste or water whose quality has been adversely affected by human influence. It includes effluents discharged by residential, commercial, industrial, or agricultural complexes and can also contain a wide range of potential pollutants at different concentrations [1, 2]. Sewage pollution is a growing threat to people, marine life, and the environment and accounts for the largest proportion of coastal pollution in the world [3]. Algeria is one of the countries in the world suffering from the effects of wastewater. Due to the lack of rainfall in Algeria, attention is focused on wastewater treatment and its reuse in the agricultural sector, as well as in other areas such as industry, watering public gardens, fighting forest fires, and meeting the needs of green spaces [4]. Intensive aquaculture is considered a source of pollution. The close interactions between the farming environment and the surrounding ecosystem give this pollution its unique characteristics. It manifests in many forms, including organic, chemical, bacteriological, genetic pollution [5]. Pollutant flows can be significant both locally and regionally. It is estimated that the production of one tonne of salmon requires 1 Km2 of sea, and that Norwegian fish farms contribute 8% and 14% of the nitrogen and phosphorus, respectively, discharged into the North Sea [6]. According to the FAO, aquaculture in desert and arid regions is defined as ‘any aquaculture activities practiced in desert and arid zones characterized by low rainfall (< 250 mm/year), high sunshine, and evaporation rates, using groundwater or surface water’. Over the past ten years, this type of aquaculture has expanded gradually due to advancements in technology and alternative energy sources, which have made it feasible to use water more efficiently in these harsh conditions for both fish farming and agricultural irrigation [7]. The annual consumption of fresh fish and seafood in Algeria is approximately 4.5 kg/capita. This is still below the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommended level of 6.2 kg/capita/year and well below the global average of 19.4 kg/capita/year. A rate of 99.7% of fish products are obtained through artisanal and coastal fishing, with freshwater fishing in dams (mostly carp and barbel) accounting for the remaining 0.3% [8].

A range of conventional technologies including adsorption, membrane filtration, flotation, coagulation, flocculation, biological and electrochemical processes has been widely employed in water treatment applications. While effective for the removal of bulk contaminants, these approaches demonstrate limited efficiency in eliminating trace-level micropollutants. The continuous evolution in analytical capabilities, along with the growing and shifting use of synthetic compounds, has revealed the presence of novel and emerging pollutants, posing significant risks to water quality [9, 10]. Biological treatment, although widely adopted, exhibits reduced efficacy when dealing with low-strength wastewaters or persistent contaminants. Its performance is highly sensitive to environmental conditions such as temperature, influent composition variability, and dissolved oxygen levels. Moreover, aerobic processes entail high-energy consumption for aeration, further constraining their sustainability. These limitations underscore the pressing need for more advanced and robust treatment technologies [11, 12].

Most conventional systems also rely on the addition of chemical agents (acids, bases, chlorinating compounds), which necessitate careful dosage optimization and monitoring. Improper or excessive use may lead to the formation of substantial volumes of hazardous sludge, requiring secondary handling and disposal. In some cases, toxic by-products such as disinfection by products (DBPs) from chlorination may pose additional risks to public health and aquatic ecosystems [13]. Coagulation remains a fundamental step in both drinking water and wastewater treatment, valued for its efficiency, simplicity, and low energy demands. It can be applied on its own or combined with other treatment methods. Inorganic coagulants particularly ferric and aluminum salts are widely used due to their strong ability to bind with colloidal particles, organic and inorganic compounds, and biological contaminants. This process involves the chemical destabilization of suspended and dissolved materials, promoting their aggregation into larger flocs that can then be removed by sedimentation. In practical application, coagulants are introduced and thoroughly mixed into the water to initiate this transformation [5, 14–16].

The aim of this experimental study is to evaluate the removal of organic, inorganic, and biological contaminants from aquaculture effluent using physicochemical treatment by coagulation–precipitation, a method widely employed in water treatment worldwide. The study also aims to assess the influence of each reagent on the efficiency of the coagulation precipitation process. The coagulants selected for this treatment aluminum sulfate, ferrous sulfate, and ferric chloride, are commonly used in both wastewater and drinking water treatment. Their effectiveness largely depends on the valence of the cations involved in pollutant removal. Using Jar test experiments, we evaluated the effects of coagulant dosage and pH variation on the removal efficiency of chemical and bacterial pollutants.

Materials and methods

Study site and sampling of wastewater

The studied fish farm of Taghit, located 90 km from the center of Bechar [17], which specializesin aquaculture under arid conditions. This farm is dedicated to cultivating tilapia species (Fig. 1). It consists of three large cement basins, each with a volume ranging from 80 to 100 m3. It producesapproximately 3 tons of tilapia annually.

Fig. 1Fish ponds in Taghit-Bechar (Southwest of Algeria) – Photo by E. Benyagoub

Fish ponds in Taghit-Bechar (Southwest of Algeria) – Photo by E. Benyagoub

The wastewater sample was taken according to the protocol described by Rodier et al., [18] using sterile glass vials.

Chemical treatment process

This process involves coagulation trials carried out using Jar test experiments to select the appropriate coagulant and determine the optimal dose for effective removal of pollutants from fish pond wastewater. The reagents used in the coagulation-precipitation trials (ferric chloride FeCl3.6H2O; ferrous sulfate anhydrous FeSO4; and aluminum sulfate Al2(SO4)3.18H2O powders) are products manufactured by BIOCHEM (Canada). They were prepared as stock solutions with a concentration of 1 g/L. Coagulant doses were added in increasing amounts to beakers filled with 500 mL of the effluent, with stirring, at the following doses: 10, 30, 50, 80, and 100 mg/L. The test was performed without adjusting the pH and was carried out at room temperature. To assess the effect of pH on the reduction of organic and inorganic matter in the effluent, a pH range from 4 to 10 was tested using NaOH and HCl solutions (0.1 N) to adjust the pH of the wastewater. In this study, wastewater with coagulants was subjected to rapid stirring at 150 rpm for 3 min, followed by slow agitation at 45 rpm for 15 min. A 30-minute settling phase with zero agitation was applied to allow the sedimentation of destabilized suspended solids (TSS).

Several reaction parameters are considered, including the coagulant dose, conductivity, and pH of the solutions.

Determination of physicochemical and bacteriological parameters

The analyzed physicochemical pollution parameters -pH, electrical conductivity (EC), total suspended solids (TSS), nitrate (NO3-), phosphate (PO4-3), MO (Potassium permanganate oxidizability measuring the amount of oxygen used for the reduction of potassium permanganate (KMnO4) by the organic matter contained in the water organic matter), chemical oxygen demand (COD), and biological oxygen demand (BOD5) - were measured before and after treatment using the methods described by Rodier et al., [18], Nabbou et al., [19] and Nabbou et al., [13], Nabbou et al., 2025 [17].

The bacteriological parameters analyzed are water pollution indicators (fecal coliforms and fecal streptococci). They were analyzed using the membrane filtration technique and the most probable number (MPN) technique, respectively, as described by Benyagoub et al., [20], Nabbou et al., [21], and Nabbou et al., [13].

National regulation for liquid discharge

The physicochemical and bacteriological results of the treated wastewater were compared with Algerian regulatory standards [22, 23] to evaluate the purification efficiency of the treatment process. All experiments were conducted in three replicate, and curve plotting was performed using OriginLab software (2018).

Statistical analysis

Normality was verified by the Shapiro-Wilk test across all parameters tested. A linear model such as the MANOVA test’s was used to study the effect of coagulant factor physicochemical parameters (pH, EC, MO, COD, BOD5, TSS, PO4−3 et NO3−). When determining significant differences, Tukey test’s was used to compare the different modalities of coagulant FeCl3 at 50 mg/L, FeSO4 at 50 mg/L and Al2(SO4)3 at 80 mg/L, after verifying the homogeneity of variances using the Levene test. All statistical analyzes and graphical representations were processed by SPSS statistical software version 27 (IBM SPSS, 2020) [24].

Results and discussion

In order to investigate the effect of coagulant dosage [FeCl3, FeSO4, and Al2(SO4)3] on the coagulation-precipitation process, a series of jar tests were conducted using varying coagulant concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 mg/L, under controlled agitation conditions. Upon coagulant addition, rapid mixing is applied to ensure proper dispersion and homogenization of the solution. However, excessively vigorous mixing can hinder particle aggregation, while prolonged agitation may disrupt the interactions between the coagulant and particle surfaces, ultimately leading to the collapse of extended segments adhered to those surfaces. Efficient coagulation requires a two-step mixing regime: an initial rapid and intense mixing phase (not exceeding 120 s) to uniformly distribute the coagulant, followed by a slower and gentler stirring phase lasting between 5 and 60 min to promote floc formation and particle agglomeration [14].

Effect of coagulant dose on pollutant removal

The dosage of the reagent is a key parameter to consider. Coagulants tend to lower the pH of the water. Overdosing increases operating costs, while under dosing results in insufficient treated water quality. The main challenge lies in determining the optimal amount of reagent to inject, depending on the characteristics of the raw water [13]. The appropriate coagulant dose is determined through experimental Jar test trials.

Effect of coagulant dose on electrical conductivity variation

The results indicate that electrical conductivity values decrease with increasing dose of all coagulants used in the treatment. Suspended solids content (SS)

The variation in total suspended solids as a function of the type and dosage of the chemical coagulant is shown in Fig. 4.

Fig. 4Effect of coagulant dose on suspended solids reduction

Effect of coagulant dose on suspended solids reduction

It was clearly observed that the addition of the coagulant resulted in a noticeable reduction in the concentration of suspended solids. The results indicate that the optimal coagulant dose is the one that achieves the lowest suspended solids content. The optimum doses identified are 50 mg/L for ferric sulfate and ferric chloride, and 80 mg/L for aluminum sulfate. At these doses, the suspended solids content decreases from 45 mg/L to 9, 6, and 2 mg/L, respectively, correspond to removal rates of 80%, 86.7%, and 95.55%. According to the literature [26, 29], this reduction can be attributed to the ability of the coagulant particles to destabilize the negatively charged colloids present in the water to be treated. This destabilization occurs through charge neutralization, which reduces the Zeta potential, activation energy and electrostatic repulsion forces between colloidal particles [13, 28]. The cations Al+³, Fe+³, Fe+², Fe(OH)+2, Al(OH)+2 and Fe(OH)+ supplied by Al2(SO4)3, FeCl3, and FeSO4, effectively aggregated and captured nearly all of the colloidal suspensions in the aquaculture effluent. This resulted in significant clarification of the treated water.

Viruses and bacteria exhibit a strong affinity for organic matter. Bacterial cells tend to form colonies by secreting polysaccharides, which contribute to the development of a biofilm either attached to a surface or suspended in solution as flocs. Coagulation-precipitation is a relatively simple process that can achieve pathogen removal rates of up to 99% (equivalent to a (2 log) reduction). Pathogenic microorganisms can be eliminated through interactions with ferric and aluminum species, which interfere with cellular components [48, 49]. Pathogen removal through this method is primarily associated with the sedimentation of suspended solids (SS). According to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines, a 2 log (99%) reduction is considered effective for the removal of bacteria, viruses, and protozoa [50].

The best results were recorded with aluminum sulfate and ferric chloride because, as the indicator reduction performances were 98% at various concentrations. The presence of fecal coliforms or fecal streptococci indicates water contamination by fecal matter. Coagulation methods have shown higher efficiencies in removing biological indicators.

The results obtained in this study remain very interesting compared to those cited in the literature. Biological treatment study of the same fish pond wastewater using Bacillus cereus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa significantly reduced pollutant levels using the bioreactor during twelve days, has proven that the B. cereus achieved the highest removal efficiencies up to 92.18% for organic matter, 98.89% for nitrate, 98.68% for phosphate and 67.95% fecal coliforms and 53.46% for fecal [51]. Igwegbe et al., [52] used electrocoagulation process (EC) to treat water collected from a freshwater fish pond in the laboratory and showed that EC reduced TSS (>90%), nitrate (about 89%) and phosphate (46%) after flocculation and settlement. Fitriani et al., [53] determined the effect of bittern natural coagulant dosage and rapid stirring speed on reducing the concentrations of BOD, COD, TSS, and dye absorbance in batik industry wastewater. The results showed that the optimal coagulant dosage was found to be 5%, and the optimal stirring speed was 100 rpm, with reduction percentages for BOD, COD, TSS, and dye absorbance being 80.32%, 65.86%, 92.35%, and 70.77%, respectively. Different biocoagulants and bioflocculant are also used to treat aquaculture wastewater such as Leucaena leucocephala pods extract [54], Moringa oleifera seed and Chitosan [5], Chicken Eggshell [55, 56], filamentous algae species [57]. They have been evaluated for their potential to remove of different pollutants from aquaculture wastewater.

Effect of pH on the removal of organic and inorganic pollutants

It is important to note that studying the effect of treatment pH is crucial, as this parameter plays a key role in the coagulation process. On one hand, it determines the chemical forms of the coagulants used; on the other hand, it governs the mechanisms involved in the removal of organic and inorganic matter, which are highly influenced by the pH of the medium.

Organic matter (OM, COD and BOD)

For each type of wastewater, there is a specific pH range within which coagulation occurs most efficiently. This optimal range depends on the type and concentration of the coagulant used, as well as the physicochemical composition of the water to be treated. The following Fig. 8a, b and c illustrate the removal efficiencies of various organic pollutants namely OM, DCO and DBO as a function of the initial pH of the solutions in optimum dose of aluminum sulfate, ferric sulfate, and ferric chloride. Notably, the use of these coagulants significantly enhanced the elimination efficiency of OM, COD and BOD, achieving removal rates ranging from 80% to 99% within a pH range of 4 to 8, using the previously determined optimal coagulant dosages.

The removal efficiency of organic matter through coagulation is strongly influenced by the pH of the water, which plays a central role in determining the speciation and reactivity of coagulant derived metal species. Several studies have reported that coagulation is generally more effective under acidic conditions than near neutral pH levels [40]. As noted by Qin et al. [58], pH is among the most critical parameters affecting the performance of organic matter removal. Upon addition of metal-based coagulants, hydrolysis reactions give rise to a range of dissolved monomeric species and solid metal hydroxide precipitates. The distribution of these hydrolysis products depends largely on the pH at which metal hydroxide solubility is minimized, as well as on the total concentration of inorganic metal ions. According to literature data, the effective hydrolyzing pH ranges for aluminum- and iron-based coagulants are approximately 5.5-8 and 4–8, respectively [59].

When the pH drops below the optimal range, positively charged monomeric species such as Al(OH)2+, Fe(OH)2+ and FeOH+ dominate, enhancing the charge neutralization of negatively charged colloids. Within a slightly higher pH range (7–8), floc formation is primarily governed by two mechanisms: electrostatic adsorption and ligand exchange (or complexation) involving hydroxyl functional groups on the surfaces of solid Al(OH)2(s), Fe(OH)2(s) or Fe(OH)3(s) particles (Fig. 9) [31, 60]. However, increasing the pH beyond the optimal range leads to the formation of more negatively charged species, such as Al(OH)4−, Fe(OH)4− and Fe(OH)3⁻ (Fig. 9), which exhibit reduced coagulation efficiency [27, 61]. At extreme pH values (pH 3 or 11), the destabilization potential of colloids is significantly diminished, leading to poor aggregation and removal of organic matter. At the optimal pH, where the solubility of the coagulant is minimal, a substantial proportion of the metal ions is converted into insoluble hydroxide flocs, which are responsible for effective colloid entrapment and organic matter removal [59–62].

Conclusion

This study showed that the use of coagulation-precipitation in treating fish pond wastewater, utilizing three coagulants - ferric chloride, ferric sulfate, and aluminum sulfate- represents a highly effective approach. The results obtained highlight the following points: pH and electrical conductivity decrease with increasing concentrations of the coagulants tested. Determining the coagulant concentration is an essential parameter for the destabilization of colloid in coagulation-settling treatment trials. The optimal coagulant concentration for the best removal of the pollutants studied was 50 mg/L for ferric sulfate and ferric chloride, and 80 mg/L for aluminum sulfate. The best results were achieved with ferric and aluminum sulfate, with removal performances of 87%, 91%, 86%, 94%, and 99% and 95%, 96%, 87%, 95%, and 98% for TSS, COD, BOD5, NO3−, PO4 − 3 respectively, ensuring compliance with Algerian norms for liquid discharges. Bacteriological analysis after treatment revealed a total elimination of streptococci and fecal coliforms, with a bacterial reduction rate of 98%.

The pollutant parameters tested appear to be better eliminated with ferric chloride in a pH range between 6 and 8 for organic matter (MO, COD, BOD), and between 8 and 10 for phosphates and nitrates.

Overall, these results indicate the effectiveness of chemical coagulation in reducing organic and inorganic pollution in wastewater, and it is cost-effective, sustainable, and efficient compared to conventional wastewater treatment systems. This approach not only achieves significant pollutant removal rates but also offers additional advantages such as reduced energy demand and strong compatibility with local resource constraints, making it a practical and effective option for sustainably managing aquaculture wastewater in Algeria and similar arid regions.

Future studies should aim to test biocoagulants, optimize treatment parameters, and investigate sequential aerobic-anaerobic systems or synergistic microbial consortia to improve pollutant removal efficiency. Furthermore, integrating biological approaches with advanced physicochemical methods, such as membrane filtration or advanced oxidation processes, could improve treatment performance for more complex wastewaters.
