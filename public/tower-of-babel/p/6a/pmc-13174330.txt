Impaired Self-Other Voice Discrimination in Patients with Auditory-Verbal Hallucinations and Nonclinical Hallucination Proneness
Authors: Jakša Vukojević, Luka Jelić, Kieren McCormack, Jelena Sušac, Ivan Muselimović, Mihovil Bagarić, Petrana Brečić, Volker Dellwo, Mario Cifrek, Aleksandar Savić, Pavo Orepić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisAuditory-verbal hallucinations (AVH), hearing voices without external speakers, are a core symptom of schizophrenia. A prominent account proposes that AVH reflect failures to recognize self-generated speech. Similar effects in hallucination-prone individuals suggest that these mechanisms span a continuum from subclinical to clinical manifestations. We used a Self-Other Voice Discrimination (SOVD) task, previously identified as a potential biomarker of self-disturbance, to assess self-recognition deficits in patients with (AVH+) and without (AVH−) a history of AVH, as well as in healthy individuals.Study DesignForty-one schizophrenia patients (23 AVH+, 18 AVH−) and 40 healthy controls completed the SOVD task. In a follow-up experiment, 26 additional healthy participants performed a familiar-other voice discrimination task identical to SOVD, but without the self-voice. In patients, performance was tested in relation to symptom severity (Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale [PANSS]), and in controls to hallucination proneness scores.Study ResultsSOVD performance was selectively impaired in AVH+ patients, while AVH− patients did not substantially differ from controls. Across groups, higher PANSS and hallucination proneness scores were specifically related to reduced self-voice recognition, with no impact on other-voice recognition. This effect did not extend to familiar-other voice discrimination.ConclusionsImpaired self-voice recognition is a promising selective marker of a history of AVH in schizophrenia and extends to hallucination proneness in the general population. These findings support a dimensional view of hallucinations and point to deficits specific to self-voice processing rather than general voice perception. They also highlight SOVD as a promising cognitive biomarker of AVH, with direct implications for early identification and targeted interventions.Keywords: auditory-verbal hallucinations, schizophrenia, self-other voice discrimination, self-recognition, hallucination proneness, cognitive biomarker

Introduction

Auditory-verbal hallucinations (AVH), or hearing voices in an awake state without outside stimuli, are a very common and highly distressing symptom of schizophrenia.1–3 With current treatments being ineffective in up to a third of patients,4–6 there is a pressing need to better understand the underlying mechanisms. According to a prominent account,7–11 AVH may result from misattributing self-generated thoughts to others, reflecting a deficit in self-monitoring that normally distinguishes internal from external stimuli through feedforward sensorimotor predictions. Experimental evidence supports this account, as patients with AVH show impaired self-recognition across various source-monitoring paradigms (for review, see Waters et al.12). Neuroimaging findings parallel this behavioral dissociation: AVH patients display reduced suppression of auditory responses during self-produced speech relative to passive listening,13 which is pointing to failures in distinguishing self- from externally generated signals.

Similar patterns are also observed in healthy individuals with higher proneness to hallucinations, who show reduced sensory attenuation during vocalization,14–16 and a tendency to misattribute their own voice under perceptual ambiguity.17,18 Such findings indicate that the cognitive and neural mechanisms underlying AVH are present along a continuum,19,20 spanning from subclinical experiences to full-blown clinical disorders, with AVH accordingly reported in 10%-15% of the general population.21–25 Studying AVH in healthy individuals is particularly valuable, as it allows researchers to isolate the mechanisms specific to hallucinations while avoiding clinical confounds such as antipsychotic medication, chronic illness, and comorbidities.26–28 Furthermore, AVH are frequently found in other psychiatric conditions, such as bipolar disorder, borderline personality disorder, and posttraumatic stress disorder, but also in several neurological conditions, such as epilepsy and migraine, making them an even more prominent psychopathological research phenomenon.29–32 This notion is one of the reasons why this functional system is one of the key aspects of the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) initiative from the National Institute of Mental Health,33 but also similar projects34 incorporating transdiagnostic research like ROAMER35 and PRISM.36

One promising biomarker for assessing self-related deficits is the Self-Other Voice Discrimination (SOVD) task,37–39 which combines voice morphing, bone and air conduction stimuli to measure how individuals distinguish their own voice from others. The task is rapid, objective, and scalable, making it a practical tool for detecting self-related disturbances often missed by standard neuropsychological assessments.40 Supporting its clinical relevance, a recent study in neurosurgical patients41 demonstrated selective impairments in self-voice (versus other-voice) recognition, reflecting difficulties in processing self-related information independent of lesion location or general cognitive and auditory function. In another case study,42 a patient who developed borderline personality disorder after meningioma resection showed a self-other inversion on the SOVD task, directly mirroring her psychiatric symptoms in both behavior and EEG.

Conceptually, the SOVD paradigm probes self-recognition as a cascade of processes spanning from low-level acoustic analysis to high-level identity attribution. Successful performance requires parsing ambiguous voice acoustics, retrieving an internal representation of one’s own voice, comparing this representation to the sensory input, and making a perceptual decision. This allows distinct alterations in performance—such as reduced sensitivity, selective self-voice impairment, or response biases—to be linked to disruptions at different processing stages, providing a framework for mechanistic differentiation across clinical profiles. SOVD also offers a graded, rather than binary, assessment of self-other boundaries, aligning with contemporary models of psychosis that emphasize the dimensional nature of self-disturbances. When combined with a familiar-other voice discrimination task, the paradigm further controls for acoustic and memory-related factors, isolating dysfunctions specific to self-related processing. The main psychophysical patterns in SOVD can thus reflect: (1) generalized self-other confusion, (2) selective failure to recognize either self or other voices, or (3) systematic biases favoring self- or other-voice judgments.

Here, we applied the SOVD paradigm to schizophrenia patients with and without a history of AVH, as well as to healthy individuals varying in hallucination proneness. Our main goals were to: (1) test for a deficit in self-voice recognition specific to AVH, and (2) examine whether a similar or distinct SOVD pattern is associated with increased hallucination proneness in non-clinical participants. We expected to observe either a general SOVD deficit or an other-voice bias in AVH+, which would also occur in high hallucination proneness, but in a weaker form. To determine whether any observed effects are specific to self-processing rather than general voice recognition or attentional factors, we conducted a second study in healthy participants using a familiar-other voice discrimination task that mirrors the SOVD procedure but does not involve the participant’s own voice. This design allows us to isolate self-specific deficits and link them to distinct stages of the self-recognition cascade.

Methods

Participants

This study involved 86 adult participants: 43 patients with schizophrenia (23 AVH+, 20 AVH−) and 43 healthy controls. Two participants from the patient group and 3 from the control group were excluded from further analysis due to technical errors and incomplete data. This resulted in a total of 81 participants: 41 patients with schizophrenia (23 AVH+) and 40 healthy controls.

Inclusion criteria required a confirmed diagnosis of schizophrenia, established through a structured clinical interview conducted by a psychiatrist in accordance with ICD-10 diagnostic criteria.43 The main exclusion criterion was that the patients were in an acute phase of schizophrenia at the time of enrollment (eg, having active hallucinations). Other exclusion criteria comprised: the presence of comorbid psychiatric conditions, including other disorders within the schizophrenia spectrum, but also any current or past substance use disorder, neurological disorders, and severe somatic illnesses. The past presence of auditory verbal hallucinations (AVH) was verified through both clinical interviews and review of patients’ electronic health records. Symptom severity was evaluated using the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS),44 which was administered by an experienced psychiatrist. None of the patients exhibited hearing impairments, including hearing loss.

Healthy control participants were recruited from the general population. Eligibility criteria included the absence of self-reported psychiatric and neurological disorders, as well as the lack of hearing impairments. An experienced psychiatrist administered clinical scales before enrollment.

Procedure

This study used an SOVD task,39 which combines voice morphing, air- and bone- conduction, and psychophysics to quantify self-voice identification. In the task, participants are asked to indicate whether ambiguous self-other voice morphs more closely resemble their own or someone else’s voice. The 6 voice morph stimuli were designed in the same way as in our previous work,37,38 and consisted of 15%, 30%, 45%, 55%, 70%, and 85% of participants’ voices (Self). These were presented ten times each in a randomized order, making a total of 60 trials in one experimental block. Each session had 4 blocks, 2 with air-conduction headphones and 2 with bone-conduction headphones, counterbalanced between participants. To prevent stimulus onset predictability, all blocks had inter-trial intervals that jittered between 1.5 and 2 s. Participants’ responses were collected by computer mouse, selecting the left button for Self or the right button for Other voice. The participants were not exposed to the sound (recording) of their own voice prior to the experiment. At the end of experiment, each participant filled in Cardiff Anomalous Perceptions Scale (CAPS)46 and Peters Delusion Inventory (PDI)47 questionnaires, measuring their proneness to hallucinations and delusions, respectively.

In the follow-up experiment in Zurich, participants additionally performed a Familiar-Other Discrimination task, which was structurally identical to SOVD but used a familiar partner’s voice instead of their own. Participants were recruited in gender-matched pairs, each accompanied by a familiar partner (e.g., friend or acquaintance) whose voice was recorded and used as the familiar reference. Both tasks (Self-Other and Familiar-Other) were presented via air-conduction, with task order randomized across participants. After the experiment, participants completed the CAPS questionnaire. This design allowed us to test whether the CAPS-performance relationship observed in Zagreb would replicate in an independent cohort, and whether it was specific to self-voice as opposed to general voice perception.

Stimuli and Materials

The participants’ voices were recorded while vocalizing the phoneme /a/ for 2-3 s. The recordings were done with a microphone integrated in Huawei CM33 headphones, and each recording was processed with Audacity software to normalize for the peak intensity (−12 dBFS), trim to a duration of 500 ms, and clean the background noise. The Huawei CM33 headphones were used as air-conduction medium, while the Aftershokz Sportz Titanium headphones were used as bone-conducting medium. Both headphones were positioned on participant’s head before the start of the experiment, with matched loudness levels adjusted at lower sound intensities to avoid crosstalk. In Zurich, the stimuli were presented with Bose QC24 air-conduction headphones.

Even though in our previous work we used the TANDEM STRAIGHT algorithm,37,38 here we employed a WORLD Vocoder voice morphing system, which we specifically adapted for clinical use.39 This system features a graphical interface and fully automated morphing, enabling straightforward use for clinicians without any programming expertise. The present study also provided an opportunity to evaluate its feasibility in a clinical setting. Although using the same voice decomposition process, some acoustic parameters may differ from the TANDEM STRAIGHT-based implementation, potentially leading to perceptual differences. These considerations are further addressed in the Supplementary material. Importantly, to confirm that the findings obtained with the WORLD Vocoder system at the University of Zagreb are consistent with those from TANDEM STRAIGHT morphing, a follow-up experiment at the University of Zurich was conducted with the original TANDEM STRAIGHT procedure (see Results).

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis addressed two main questions: (1) whether SOVD performance differed across groups (AVH+, AVH−, controls) and conditions (bone and air conduction), and (2) to assess the relationship between SOVD task performance and clinical measures.

To model SOVD task performance, we applied binomial mixed-effects model with Response (Self vs Other) as dependent variable. Fixed effects predictors included Group (AVH+, AVH−, control), Condition (air vs. bone conduction), and Voice Morph (ranked levels: 15%, 30%, 45%, 55%, 70%, 85%), with a 3-way interaction and a by-participant random intercept.

The relationship between task performance and clinical measures was modeled using linear mixed-effects with task Accuracy (average percentage correct) as the dependent variable. It was modeled separately for patients and controls, with different clinical scores added to the corresponding models—PANSS for patients; CAPS and PDI for controls. Thus, for the patient model, the fixed effects were Group (AVH+, AVH−), Condition (air vs bone), Voice (binary levels: Self, Other), and PANSS (total score), with a 4-way interaction. For the controls model, the fixed effects were Condition (air vs bone), Voice (binary levels: Self, Other), CAPS and PDI total scores, with a 3-way interaction between Condition, Voice, and each scale. Both patient and control models included participant intercepts as random effects.

The model relating CAPS to the Zurich cohort had three fixed effects with a 3-way interaction: Task (Self-Other, Familiar-Other), Dominant Voice (Self/Familiar, Other), and CAPS (score).

Statistical analysis was done in R48 using lme449 package for model estimation and lmerTest50 for P-values, while the visualization was done using sjPlot51 and ggplot252 packages.

Results

Task Performance Across Groups

The mixed-effects binomial model showed a significant 2-way interaction between Group and Voice Morph, indicating a significant difference across group performances: the AVH+ group had a lower slope than both healthy controls (estimate = −0.112, z = −3.615, P < .001) and the AVH− group (estimate = −0.145, z = −3.737, P < .001), while the AVH− group did not differ from controls (estimate = 0.034, z = 0.979, P = .33) (Figure 1). There was no main effect of Group (AVH+ vs AVH−: estimate = 0.575, z = 1.296, P = .195; AVH+ vs controls: estimate = 0.467, z = 1.28, P = .202; AVH− vs controls: estimate = –0.109, z = –0.27, P = .785), and a main effect of Voice Morph (estimate = 0.159, z = 8.97, P < .001) showed the expected increase of Self responses with the increase of voice morph levels.

To relate these results to broader biases in perceptual decision-making, we conducted a signal detection analysis to compute d’ and criterion as measures of sensitivity and response bias. The hit rate is defined as the proportion of self-voice trials (signal present) correctly identified as “self.” The false alarm rate as the proportion of other-voice trials (signal absent) incorrectly identified as “self.” The miss rate as the proportion of self-voice trials identified as “other.” The correct rejection rate as the proportion of other-voice trials correctly identified as “other.” Mean d’ was lower in AVH+ (M = 0.08) compared to controls (M = 0.31) and AVH− (M = 0.26), suggesting reduced perceptual sensitivity, however, this pattern did not reach statistical significance. There were no significant differences in the criterion values across groups. These results are reported in the Supplementary material.

To conclude, performance in the AVH− and control groups was comparable, whereas the AVH+ group showed a flatter psychometric slope, indicating reduced Self-Other voice discriminability. This pattern is more consistent with a general discrimination impairment than with a directional shift in response tendency (eg, self-to-other or other-to-self), which would be expected to produce a horizontal shift in the psychometric function.

Task Performance and Clinical Measures

Linear mixed-effects models associating task performance with clinical scores revealed a relationship specific to performance in self-voice trials (Figure 2). In the patient model, there was a significant 2-way interaction between Voice and PANSS (estimate = −0.008, t = −4.91, P < .001), demonstrating a decrease only in self-voice accuracy as symptom severity increased (Figure 2A). Moreover, there was a main effect of Voice (estimate = 0.49, t = 4.04, P < .001), demonstrating overall higher accuracy for self-voice, and no main effect of PANSS (estimate = −0.00004, t = −0.02, P = .986). The main effect of Group (estimate = −0.698, t = −3.15, P = .003), showed, as above, lower performance in AVH+ participants. A 2-way interaction between Group and PANSS (estimate = 0.008, t = 2.86, P = .006) indicated a stronger decrease in performance in AVH− group with the increase of PANSS.

Mirroring the finding in patients, the model fitted for controls displayed a 2-way interaction between CAPS and Voice (estimate = −0.022, t = −5.08, P < .001), showing decrease only in self-voice accuracy with the increase of CAPS scores (Figure 2B). There was also a significant 3-way interaction between CAPS, Voice, and Condition (estimate = 0.026, t = 4.27, P < .001), showing that this effect was more prominent in air conduction. The main effect of Voice (estimate = −0.083, t = −2.50, P = .012) indicated lower accuracy for self-voice, while CAPS alone had no significant main effect (estimate = 0.007, t = 0.48, P = .637). There were no significant effects observed for PDI scores (Supplementary material).

To replicate and narrow the CAPS effect specifically to self-voice, as opposed to general voice processing, a follow-up experiment was conducted in which participants performed both a Self-Other and a Familiar-Other Discrimination task. Replicating the initial finding, we found a significant 3-way interaction between CAPS, Task, and Voice (estimate = −0.011, t = −2.27, P = .023). When splitting by task, a 2-way interaction between CAPS and Voice was significant only for the Self-Other task (estimate = −0.009, t = −2.58, P = .010; Figure 2C), again showing a decrease in performance specific to self-voice. The same 2-way interaction was not significant for the Familiar-Other task (estimate = 0.009, t = 1.63, P = .111, Figure 2D). There was no main effect of CAPS (estimate = 0.006, t = 1.44, P = .157).

Since the chlorpromazine equivalent dose (CPZ equivalent) was significantly higher in AVH− than in AVH+ groups (Table 1), we additionally explored the possible effect of medication on task performance. To that aim, we added the CPZ Equivalent (score) as a covariate in the linear mixed-effects model with Accuracy as dependent variable, together with a 3-way interaction between CPZ Equivalent, Group (AVH+, AVH−), and Voice (Self, Other). The results showed a significant effect of CPZ Equivalent (estimate = 0.0003, t = 3.092, P = .004), indicating an increase in performance with the increase of CPZ Equivalent. As the 3-way interaction was also significant (estimate = 0.0008, t = 10.738, P < .001), we ran the same model separately for each group. While in the AVH+ group the effect of CPZ Equivalent disappeared (estimate = −0.0002, t = −1.214, P = .237), it remained significant in the AVH− group (estimate = 0.0003, t = 3.4, P < .001), again indicating an increase in performance with CPZ Equivalent. This pattern likely reflects a “floor effect” in the AVH+ group, where overall performance was already low, limiting the extent to which any further improvement with medication could be observed. Full model outputs are reported in the Supplementary material. These results indicate that increase in antipsychotic medication improved task performance, but only in the AVH− group. As exploratory analyses, we ran similar models relating task performance with PANSS subscales, hospitalization, age, and gender, and the results are reported in the Supplementary material.

Overall, these results demonstrate that both symptom severity in patients and hallucination proneness in controls are related specifically to self-voice recognition, with no effects on the recognition of other familiar or unfamiliar voices.

Specificity to Hallucinations

To further assess the specificity of the observed effect to hallucinations, we extracted individual PANSS item scores corresponding to Hallucinatory Behavior (P3) and Delusions (P1) for each patient. We first compared these scores between groups (AVH+ vs. AVH−) to determine whether delusions were also elevated in the AVH+ group, which could potentially contribute to the observed behavioral differences (e.g., on Figure 1). Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that both scores were not normally distributed in either group (all P < .05), so we used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Delusions (P1) did not differ significantly between groups (W = 164, P = .124), whereas Hallucinatory Behavior (P3) was significantly higher in AVH+ participants (W = 124.5, P = .008), suggesting that the observed effects might indeed be more closely linked to hallucination-related symptomatology.

We then tested whether task performance was associated with these individual PANSS items. Neither general performance nor self-voice accuracy were significantly correlated with either item, both in the full patient sample and within each group (Tables S3 and S4). This lack of significant correlations likely reflects the limited sample size and reduced reliability of single-item measures, rather than a complete absence of association.

In controls, to further quantify the contributions of hallucination- and delusion-proneness, we compared mixed-effects models including both CAPS and PDI items (as above) to models with each item and its interactions removed. Removing CAPS significantly reduced model fit (likelihood ratio test: χ2(4) = 31.59, P < .001), whereas removing PDI did not affect model fit (χ2(4) = 4.61, P = .33). These results indicate that, in controls, the observed impairments in self-voice recognition are more associated with hallucination than delusion proneness.

Discussion

This study, assessing SOVD task performance in schizophrenia patients and healthy controls, has two main findings. First, SOVD was specifically impaired in patients with a history of AVH (AVH+ group), with no statistically significant differences between AVH− patients and healthy participants. Second, clinical symptom severity measured by PANSS and hallucination proneness measured by CAPS were explicitly associated with performance decreases in self-voice trials. The hallucination proneness effect was replicated in an independent cohort of healthy participants and remained specific to self-voice, indicating that it cannot be explained by general deficits in voice perception or attentional demands.

The selective SOVD impairment in AVH+ patients suggests that difficulties in distinguishing self from other voices are not a trait feature of schizophrenia in general but are linked to the occurrence of AVH in the acute phase of the illness. This aligns with prior evidence specifically relating AVH with self-other confusion across various paradigms and modalities.12 Traditionally, this has been interpreted within the self-monitoring account of AVH, according to which feedforward prediction errors may cause self-generated thoughts to be misattributed.7–11 This account cannot fully explain the current findings because the SOVD task does not require active speech production or motor output. Instead, our results point to a broader impairment in self-representation, potentially involving memory processes, or higher-order aspects of self-processing, such as self-identification.53,54 This perspective aligns with other theoretical accounts of AVH, including models emphasizing deficits in memory processing,55,56 and overreliance on inherent models of the world,57,58 in a complementary fashion. For instance, recent theoretical59 and empirical26,60 work has proposed that self-monitoring processes may help maintain the stability of internal models of self and world, and that disruptions in these processes give rise to self-other confusion. Our results could extend the self-monitoring model by introducing a variable which relates sensorimotor prediction errors and memory self-representations. For example, it is possible that, in AVH+ patients, prediction errors accumulate over time (e.g., repeated misattribution of inner speech), and after a certain threshold, they alter the stored self-representation (e.g., what I think my voice sounds like), resulting in a trait-like self-other confusion, observable across different experimental paradigms, including SOVD. Consistent with this view, we observed SOVD deficits specifically in patients with a history of AVH, supporting the notion of a stable, trait-like alteration rather than a transient, state-dependent effect.

The group differences in SOVD performance suggest that self-other confusion may manifest differently across the psychosis spectrum. AVH+ patients showed a generalized deficit, with flattened psychometric curves indicating a broad inability to distinguish self from other voices. In contrast, AVH− patients and healthy participants performed at a comparable overall level, yet within both groups higher PANSS or CAPS scores were specifically associated with poorer recognition of self-voice, while other-voice performance remained intact. Importantly, this association was replicated in an independent healthy cohort and remained restricted to self-voice, indicating that it is not driven by general voice-perception deficits or task-related attentional demands. Thus, whereas a global SOVD impairment appears characteristic of individuals with a clinical history of AVH, more subtle self-specific vulnerabilities might emerge along the broader psychosis spectrum and modulate performance in a dimensional fashion. In this view, SOVD performance may index a trait-like cognitive marker of vulnerability to AVH, which may become clinically manifested over time due to various additional factors such as stress, cognitive load, or neurobiological changes. Taken together, these findings support dimensional models of psychosis,20–25 in which mechanisms of self-representation underlie hallucinatory experiences across the population: subtle, self-specific liabilities in nonclinical and AVH− samples may accumulate into global self-processing impairments in AVH+.

Classic studies from the 2000s linked AVH to self-monitoring deficits using paradigms such as source-monitoring12 or talk-listen13 tasks, and proposed mechanisms like corollary discharge dysfunction to explain these effects. While conceptually informative, these paradigms often cannot separate specific processes—such as perceptual sensitivity, retrieval of self-representation, or evaluation of self-related evidence—nor do they relate these processes to distinct clinical profiles. The SOVD paradigm addresses this gap by conceptualizing self-voice recognition as a cascade from acoustic evaluation, through access to memory representations, to self-other comparison and decision-making. In our data, two distinct profiles emerge. In healthy individuals, hallucination proneness is associated specifically with reduced performance for self-voice stimuli, while recognition of other and familiar voices remains intact. This excludes general auditory or memory deficits and points to a subtle imprecision in the self-voice template. In contrast, AVH+ patients show a general SOVD deficit across self and nonself stimuli, which signal detection analysis indicates cannot be explained by perceptual sensitivity or decisional bias. The broad impairment could reflect a breakdown in one or more stages of the cascade, or partly arise from averaging across heterogeneous patients. Applied in larger samples, SOVD could reveal mechanistically distinct subgroups within AVH+ and support clinically meaningful patient stratification.

The observed link between behavior and the average PANSS score might suggest effects of overall symptom severity rather than specificity to hallucinations. However, several points support a link specific to voice hearing. First, our group definitions were strict: no patients were in the acute phase, none had comorbid psychiatric conditions, and AVH− patients had no history of hearing voices whatsoever. Second, although patients typically perform worse than controls across different tasks, here the AVH− group performed similarly to healthy participants, controlling for general clinical status. Third, while AVH+ patients had significantly higher score on the PANSS hallucination item, the groups did not differ in delusion scores, controlling for positive symptoms. Together, this indicates that, regardless of the interpretation of the PANSS effect, our main finding—the drop in task performance (Figure 1)—is indeed specific to voice hearing. Finally, while separating effects in patients is challenging, analyses in controls show a stronger relationship with hallucination than delusion-proneness, further supporting specificity to voice hearing.

We observed a relationship between CPZ equivalents and behavior. CPZ equivalents are still used as the “gold standard” for comparing dosages of different antipsychotics, but they should be viewed as an approximation of relative potency rather than a precise measure of effect. Previous work has challenged the “dogma of equal efficacy of antipsychotic drugs”.61 While they provide a practical framework for research standardization, their validity is limited by the diverse receptor profiles of currently used antipsychotics (e.g., serotonin receptors), which consequently limits our conclusions on the actual effect a specific drug might have. Furthermore, the assumption of a linear dose–response relationship often fails at higher doses where side effects increase but clinical efficacy plateaus. A smaller sample does not allow analyzing possible significant differences in medication effects between specific antipsychotics or even antipsychotic classes. Thus, the observed association between medication and behavior should be interpreted cautiously. Importantly, higher CPZ equivalents were not associated with reduced performance, arguing against a detrimental medication effect on the SOVD task.

Self-voice recognition has previously been used to probe broader deficits in self-processing. Most prior work focused on neurological62 and neurosurgical40 patients, where SOVD proved useful as a formal neuropsychological tool to quantify post-surgical changes in the sense of self.41 This approach has also been applied in psychiatry, showing that SOVD can capture altered self-experience in borderline personality disorder42 and autistic traits.63 Building on this foundation, the present study applies SOVD to schizophrenia, directly targeting self-processing mechanisms implicated in AVH. More broadly, these findings underscore the fundamental role of self-processing in human cognition: distinguishing self-generated from external signals is essential for perception, agency, and social interaction, and its disruption can manifest across neurological and psychiatric conditions. From a functional system, or RDoC, perspective,33 SOVD offers a dimensional, neurobehavioral marker of self-processing that bridges clinical and non-clinical populations. It may also help identify patients with a higher risk of transitioning to psychosis64—aligning with the goals of the Accelerating Medicines Partnership–Schizophrenia (AMP-SCZ) program in collaboration with the National Institutes of Health.65

Limitations

A main limitation of our work is the globally weaker performance, present not only in patients but also in healthy controls, likely due to the use of WORLD Vocoder in the SOVD procedure,39 which replaced the original TANDEM STRAIGHT algorithm for voice morphing. This likely reduced the sensitivity of the paradigm by attenuating the detailed acoustic signals that are critical for voice identity recognition, in particular formant frequencies.66,67 Although computationally efficient, the WORLD algorithm smooths subtle pitch and formant cues, whereas TANDEMSTRAIGHT preserves idiosyncratic acoustic details, yielding more natural morphs that likely support better performance (Supplementary material). This also complicates the interpretation of other acoustics-related effects, such as bone- versus air-conduction performance. Although the absence of conduction effects could in principle carry clinical meaning (e.g., altered sensory cancellation), in the present study these effects are likely confounded by limitations of the voice-morphing procedure. The precise differences in acoustics between the two systems and their perceptual effects will be systematically evaluated in our follow-up work. Notably, despite the overall performance drop, the main between-group differences were still observed (Figure 1). It is possible that the TANDEM STRAIGHT morphing, by avoiding putative “floor effects,” would have produced even more pronounced differences in patients. This is supported by our follow-up experiment, in which the relationship between hallucination proneness and self-voice recognition was stronger using TANDEM STRAIGHT, compared to WORLD Vocoder morphing (compare Figure 2B and C).

An additional limitation is that the study did not include measures specifically designed to assess alterations in self-experience, such as the Examination of Anomalous Self-Experience68 or the Inventory of Psychotic-like Anomalous Self-Experiences.69 Including these instruments in future studies would provide a more direct link to theoretical models of self-perception in schizophrenia,70 potentially even elucidating the observed PANSS effect.

Finally, our relatively small sample size, determined by practical constraints rather than formal a priori power calculations, calls for caution in interpreting some findings, particularly more complex interactions in mixed-effects models. Consequently, our results relating behavioral results with medication and questionnaires, such as differences in specific PANSS subscales in patients or differences between CAPS and PDI effects in controls, should be validated in larger samples.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that impaired SOVD is specifically linked to the occurrence of AVH in the acute phase of the illness and hallucination proneness, supporting the notion that there is an underlying core deficit in self-representation that spans clinical and non-clinical populations. The selective self-voice impairment highlights a trait-like vulnerability for AVH, aligning with dimensional models of psychosis. Future work should aim to disentangle trait versus state mechanisms using longitudinal designs, explore interactions with other cognitive processes implicated in AVH, and map the corresponding neural correlates of these effects.
