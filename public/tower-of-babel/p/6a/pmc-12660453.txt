Amyloid-related Imaging Abnormalities (ARIA) in the Context of Alzheimer’s Disease and Amyloid-targeting Therapies: An Introduction for Advanced Practice Providers
Authors: Curtis P. Schreiber, Amy Kovacik, James Bishop, Jon Helman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder pathologically characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) and neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau in the brain. Amyloid-targeting therapies (ATTs) are the first available disease-modifying treatments shown to slow cognitive and functional decline for patients with mild cognitive impairment owing to AD and early symptomatic AD. Currently two ATTs are commercially available, donanemab (Kisunla™) and lecanemab (Leqembi®). The main potential side effect and safety concern of ATT treatment is amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA). ARIA can be categorized into two types that can co-occur: ARIA-E (edema/sulcal effusion) and ARIA-H (hemorrhage/superficial siderosis). Although both are often asymptomatic and ARIA-E typically resolves radiographically over time, both forms can be radiologically and/or clinically serious. Treating clinicians should be equipped with a comprehensive understanding of ARIA. This review aims to provide advanced practice providers, who are pivotal to patient care in AD, with critical insights into ARIA to safely identify risk factors, understand treatment guidelines, and gain familiarity with appropriate management strategies. It emphasizes the importance of understanding APOE genotype and vascular factors in ARIA risk and recognizing the clinical and radiographic manifestations of ARIA. Practical recommendations are provided for monitoring and managing ARIA, including dose management strategies and education on symptom awareness. By fostering a comprehensive understanding of ARIA and its monitoring and management, this review aims to support the safe and effective implementation of ATTs, contributing to optimized patient care for those treated with ATTs.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that accounts for 60–80% of cases of dementia [1, 2]. AD is pathologically defined by the presence of two abnormal proteins: extracellular amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles of hyperphosphorylated tau [3].

The hallmark of AD pathology is the overproduction and/or underclearance of soluble Aβ in the brain, which accumulates into progressively larger complexes and ultimately into insoluble Aβ plaques [4–6]. This accumulation has been shown to start approximately 20 years before the appearance of clinical symptoms [2, 5]. Later, the intracellular protein tau, which stabilizes microtubules in neurons, dissociates and becomes hyperphosphorylated; this degrades axonal integrity and ultimately leads to neuronal death [1, 3].

Until recently, the only medication options for patients with AD were symptomatic treatments, which do not target the disease’s underlying pathological process [4, 7]. More recently, several amyloid-targeting therapies (ATTs) have been investigated and approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of mild cognitive impairment and mild dementia due to AD. ATTs are monoclonal antibodies (passive immunotherapies) that bind Aβ, altering plaque deposition/clearance. The insoluble (plaque) form of brain Aβ is radiographically assessed with positron emission tomography (PET) ligands and is most commonly visually read as a binary (positive or negative) scan, although computerized quantitative methods have been recently implemented [4, 8]. While ATTs do reduce the level of brain Aβ, they should not be regarded as curative for AD; instead, they are considered disease modifying-treatments capable of slowing disease progression by slowing cognitive/functional decline in patients with AD treated during the early stages of disease [1, 4, 8–11]. Two ATTs are commercially available for patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD who have Aβ pathology confirmed by biomarker testing: donanemab (Kisunla™) and lecanemab (Leqembi®) [12, 13]. Importantly, ATTs as a class have been associated with safety concerns, most notably amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA), which can be serious and life-threatening [12, 13].

The term “ARIA” was first proposed in 2010 by a group of AD and imaging experts in response to magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) abnormalities seen in some participants of ATT clinical trials. Therefore, by definition, ARIA is a radiographic finding with or without clinical symptoms. Radiographically, ARIA is defined by hemorrhage and/or effusion/edema. MRI is the optimal imaging modality to detect ARIA, while computed tomography (CT) scans are not sufficient [12–15], which is clinically relevant when considering differential diagnoses in emergency situations. MRI sequences that are sensitive to either effusion/edema or hemosiderin can identify ARIA [14]. While most radiographic ARIA events were asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic, serious events and deaths have been reported [10, 12, 13]. The FDA prescribing information for the currently-approved ATTs includes boxed safety warnings about ARIA risk [12, 13], which are provided to alert both prescribers and patients to the potentially serious or life-threatening risks associated with ARIA [16].

Advanced practice providers (APPs), including nurse practitioners and physician assistants/associates, play key roles throughout the patient journey in AD, ensuring a comprehensive approach to patient management. APPs educate patients regarding their disease, engage in discussions of the potential risks and benefits of treatments, and counsel patients and their care partners regarding APOE genetic testing results. Because of their longitudinal relationship with patients, APPs are ideally positioned to identify, manage, and monitor any emerging symptoms, whether related to the patient’s underlying disease or potential treatment-related complications, including ARIA. In particular, APPs can help educate patients and their care partners about ARIA symptoms and guide them on how to effectively communicate with their clinical care team and with emergency department personnel in the event of an emergency.

This review aims to educate APPs about ARIA events as the main treatment-related safety concern in the context of ATTs. Specifically, it provides information about ARIA pathophysiology and risk factors, as well as the key radiographic and clinical features. It will also review practical considerations for monitoring and management of ARIA. Notably, it summarizes key information from the US prescribing information for available ATTs. For further guidance, APPs will be referred to appropriate-use recommendations (AURs), which are written by expert panels to help guide the introduction of each ATT into real-world practice. APPs should be familiar with both resources.

Pathophysiology and Forms of ARIA

ARIA is most commonly, but not exclusively, associated with ATTs. Although the underlying mechanisms are incompletely understood, several hypotheses have been proposed. ARIA is thought to result from a loss of vascular integrity that may be due to Aβ clearance [4, 8, 15]. ATT binding of vascular Aβ may increase inflammation and vascular permeability [4, 8, 15], and an inflammatory response related to ATT-mediated Aβ binding in the brain [6] may lead to further blood vessel leakage [4, 8, 15]. Depending on the product leaked to the surrounding tissue, patients may experience ARIA-E, ARIA-H, or both concurrently [4, 8, 15]. Importantly, ARIA-E and ARIA-H differ not only in their leakage products, but also in their appearance on MRI scans.

ARIA-E

ARIA-E represents leakage of fluid into either the brain parenchyma (vasogenic edema) or the leptomeningeal space/sulci (sulcal effusion) [4, 8, 14, 15, 17]. It occurs more frequently with ATT treatment, which likely reflects vessel leakiness following Aβ binding [8].

ARIA-E can be detected with MRI using fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) sequences. Radiographically, ARIA-E is visualized as increased FLAIR hyperintensity (“white areas”) on MRI [18].

Most radiographic ARIA events are asymptomatic. When symptomatic ARIA occurs, it is more likely to occur with ARIA-E than ARIA-H [11]. The MRI findings associated with ARIA-E tend to resolve radiographically over time [4]. In plain language, ARIA-E can be described to patients and their care partners as “accumulation of fluid” or “swelling” in the brain [4, 12, 13, 19–21].

ARIA-H

ARIA-H represents leakage of blood, and blood degradation products such as hemosiderin, within the brain parenchyma (hemorrhage) or the subarachnoid space (superficial siderosis) [4, 8, 14, 15, 17].

ARIA-H can be detected with T2*-weighted gradient recalled echo (T2*-GRE) or susceptibility-weighted imaging (SWI) MRI sequences [6, 14, 15]. SWI is more sensitive for microhemorrhage detection than T2*-GRE, which may enhance ARIA-H detection, although use of the latter is adequate [6, 14, 15]. Despite its high sensitivity, SWI is more variable and less widely available than GRE, which was used in clinical trials [6]. ARIA-H is typically visualized as rounded foci (microhemorrhage) or curvilinear areas (superficial siderosis) with weaker signal intensity than their surroundings [8, 14, 15]. These hypointensities or “dark areas” (signal loss) that appear on MRI images are due to residual hemosiderin deposits from blood leakage product [15]. Therefore, clinicians should look for either new or larger spots, as lesions are often initially small, although large areas of bleeding can occur [14] and intracerebral hemorrhage > 1 cm has been reported [12, 13]. In contrast to ARIA-E, ARIA-H generally stabilizes radiographically but does not resolve, remaining visible on subsequent imaging rather than disappearing [4]. ARIA-H can be described to patients and their care partners as “areas of bleeding” in the brain [4, 12, 13, 19–21].

ARIA Risk Factors

ARIA events are most typically associated with ATT exposure [8], although ARIA can occur at a lower rate in patients not on ATT treatment [14]. With donanemab, 24.0% of clinical trial participants experienced ARIA-E (vs. 2.1% with placebo) and 31.4% experienced ARIA-H (vs. 13.6% with placebo). With lecanemab, 12.6% of clinical trial participants experienced ARIA-E (vs. 1.7% with placebo) and 17.3% experienced ARIA-H (vs. 9.0% with placebo) [10, 11]. Regardless of their possible relation with ATT treatment, all ARIA events need to be managed according to protocols if a patient is receiving an ATT. Risk factors, including APOE4 gene status and vascular factors, may increase a patient’s ARIA risk and should be considered before deciding whether to treat that patient with an ATT [12, 13].

APOE Genotype

There are several variants (alleles) of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) gene, including APOE ε2, ε3, and ε4, with the ε4 variant being the main genetic risk for ARIA [9, 12, 13, 22]. Each individual has two copies of the APOE gene (one from each parent), resulting in six possible genetic combinations. Around 15% of patients with AD are homozygotes for APOE ε4 (meaning they have two copies of ε4) [12, 13], ~50 are heterozygotes (one copy), and ~30% are noncarriers (no copies) [23].

The link between APOE ε4 carriership and ARIA risk may be related to APOE4 compromising the integrity of cerebrovascular structures [9, 22]. The risk of ARIA increases with the number of APOE ε4 alleles, with the highest risk in homozygotes, an intermediate risk in heterozygotes, and the lowest risk in non-carriers [10, 11]. The FDA boxed warnings in both donanemab and lecanemab prescribing information highlight the higher ARIA risk in APOE ε4 homozygotes [12, 13]. The higher incidence of ARIA among APOE ε4 homozygotes when compared with heterozygotes and non-carriers includes symptomatic and serious events [10–13].

APOE genotype testing helps clinicians to advise patients of their ARIA risk and is pivotal in individual benefit-risk considerations before deciding to treat with ATT. Clinicians should use their clinical judgement when considering APOE ε4 homozygotes, incorporating this into the shared decision-making with patients and their care partners. Patients should be informed about how their APOE genotype may affect their risk of developing ARIA, such that APOE ε4 carriers have a higher ARIA risk [9, 19–21]. APOE testing should be performed prior to treatment initiation [12, 13]. If a patient decides not to undergo genotyping, they may still be treated with an ATT in the US [12, 13], but clinicians should explain to patients that this will make it more difficult to evaluate their risk of experiencing ARIA. Clinicians should always refer to their most up-to-date local prescribing information and institutional guidance regarding ATT use in APOE ε4 homozygotes.

Vascular Risk Factors

Vascular factors including microhemorrhages, superficial siderosis, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) may increase the risk of ARIA and are assessed during baseline (pre-treatment) MRI scans [6]. These conditions may be related to Aβ pathology, which may partly explain the low but detectable incidence of ARIA in patients with AD even without ATT treatment [14].

CAA, which is frequently comorbid with AD and more likely among APOE ε4 carriers [25], is an important ARIA risk factor [26, 27]. Imaging procedures may help identify probable CAA, which is characterized by microhemorrhages, superficial siderosis, and/or multiple lobar hemorrhage lesions [28]. In individuals with these findings, clinicians should consider the possibility of CAA and its potential impact on ARIA risk prior to initiating an ATT [12, 13]. Therefore, using appropriate MRI sequences (heme-sensitive GRE or SWI) during the patient’s cognitive evaluation/workup could help identify CAA-consistent findings predictive of ARIA and thus impact eligibility for ATT [6].

Antithrombotic and Thrombolytic Therapy

The US prescribing information for both lecanemab and donanemab specifies that clinicians should carefully consider whether to use an ATT in patients at increased risk for intracerebral hemorrhage, particularly those requiring concomitant anticoagulant or thrombolytic medications (see below for considerations around antithrombotic medication use) [12, 13]. In phase 3 trials of ATTs, patients were typically excluded from participation if they had evidence of > 4 microhemorrhages, any intracerebral hemorrhage > 1 cm, or superficial siderosis (>1 areas for donanemab or any areas for lecanemab) [10, 11]. Notably, the AURs for both lecanemab and donanemab recommend against treatment in the presence of baseline superficial siderosis or > 4 microhemorrhages. Clinicians should use their clinical judgement when deciding whether to treat patients with one area of superficial siderosis, and APPs should refer to the most recent AUR for further guidance [9, 24].

Antithrombotics

Antithrombotic medications include antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants. There is no universal guidance regarding use of these medications with ATTs, as key differences exist between them.

Aspirin and other antiplatelet agents were allowed in ATT clinical trials [12, 13] and their use is supported by AURs for both lecanemab and donanemab [9, 24].

Anticoagulants were similarly allowed in ATT clinical trials [12, 13], but their use is cautioned against in the AURs, which recommend not giving lecanemab or donanemab to patients who are receiving anticoagulants (such as warfarin, vitamin K antagonists, direct oral anticoagulants [apixaban, betrixaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, rivaroxaban], or heparin) [9, 24].

Thrombolytics

The US prescribing information cautions against the use of thrombolytics with ATTs and the AURs advise specifically against the use of thrombolytic agents, commonly referred to as “clot busters,” in individuals receiving lecanemab or donanemab [9, 24]. This includes tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) and tenecteplase (TNK). Because ARIA symptoms might mimic stroke signs and symptoms and many acute stroke protocols involve CT imaging (which cannot be used to identify ARIA), it is critical that emergency department personnel know whether a patient is receiving ATT before considering the use of a thrombolytic [12]. Patients on ATTs have died after receiving thrombolytics for suspected stroke symptoms [29].

MRI Assessment

While most ARIA events are asymptomatic (see section below), APPs should remember that ARIA is defined by its distinct radiographic abnormalities [12, 13]. This contrasts with other conditions, such as migraine or transient ischemic attacks, which may occur without radiographic findings [30, 31]. ARIA cannot be reliably detected with CT scans and can only be identified with MRI [12–15]. MRI can be used to grade the radiographic severity of ARIA-E and ARIA-H, which may occur with and without clinical symptoms (Table 1) [8, 12, 13]. Variability between scans may arise from imaging sequences, sequence parameters, scanner, and field strengths; some variables cannot always be adjusted, but guidance exists to reduce variability [6]. Communication with radiologists is important for detection and reporting of ARIA, and reporting templates for radiologists reading the brain MRI scans are available [32]. APPs should consult the respective ATT prescribing information and AURs for the required schedules of MRIs for routine ARIA monitoring. Clinical Assessment

ARIA is a radiographic event that is most commonly asymptomatic and most typically occurs early during treatment, commonly within 6 months of treatment initiation, although it can occur at any time during treatment [10, 11].

When symptoms are present, they are usually nonlocalizing (not referable to a focal area of the brain) and most commonly include headache, confusion, vision changes, dizziness, nausea, and gait difficulty. Some patients may experience focal neurologic deficits that can mimic a stroke [12, 13].

When patients have symptoms due to ARIA, the severity is clinically defined as mild (no disruption of normal daily activities), moderate (normal daily activities reduced/affected), or severe (incapacitating, with inability to work or to perform normal daily activities) [12, 13]. These definitions of clinical severity are used as part of dosing decisions for patients with ARIA (see Tables 2 and 3). Because clinical symptoms may or may not correlate with the radiographic severity of ARIA, clinicians need to integrate both the clinical and radiographic severity of ARIA in their treatment decision-making. Importantly, rare serious and life-threatening events including serious intracerebral hemorrhages > 1 cm, seizures, and status epilepticus may also occur [12, 13]. In some cases, these rare but serious events have resulted in patient deaths [10, 11]. Therefore, although most ARIA events are asymptomatic, APPs should be familiar with the entire spectrum of possible symptoms and actively listen to patients and their care partners to ensure any ARIA-compatible symptoms are detected, evaluated and treated, and that a careful decision on ATT dosing is made. APPs should also inform their patients of the most common symptoms associated with ARIA, and of the need to report these immediately, or be seen in the emergency department, should the symptoms be severe [19, 20]. The ATT prescriber should collaborate with the emergency department in patient management should ARIA be diagnosed in this setting. Although electronic medical records usually differ across institutions, clinicians should familiarize themselves with their individual system, be aware of its limitations, and leverage its capabilities (e.g., medication warnings or imaging reminders). Patients and care partners should be advised to carry information about their ATT treatment (such as a wallet card) to ensure that emergency department personnel are aware of their ATT use. As discussed, this is particularly important if they experience stroke-like symptoms and are considered for thrombolytic treatment. Finally, patients should be advised to inform their providers if they are prescribed any new medications while on their ATT, especially antithrombotics [19, 20].

ARIA Monitoring and Patient Management

MRI Monitoring for ARIA

Patients receiving ATTs need to be monitored with MRI before ATT initiation and throughout their treatment, both to detect asymptomatic ARIA and to evaluate any new potential ARIA-related symptoms [12, 13, 15]. MRI field strengths of 1.5T or 3.0T were used in clinical trials and are recommended for ARIA monitoring [6, 14, 15]. A brain MRI scan is required at baseline to assess eligibility, which AURs recommend occurs within the year (and ideally < 6 months) prior to starting treatment [9, 24]. Scans are required at baseline and then before infusions 2, 3, 4, and 7 with donanemab [12], and at baseline and before infusions 5, 7, and 14 with lecanemab [13]. AURs for lecanemab and donanemab recommend additional MRI scans [9, 24].

As discussed, most ARIA events occur in the early stages of treatment. Therefore, although patient monitoring for ARIA is required throughout treatment, it should be enhanced within the first months after treatment initiation: the first 24 weeks of donanemab [12] and the first 14 weeks of lecanemab [13] treatment. Enhanced monitoring includes more frequent clinic visits as well as MRI monitoring. Frequency of MRI monitoring should be based on the protocols described in the medication’s prescribing information and consider enhanced monitoring recommended in the AURs. Clinicians need to use their clinical judgement to assess whether additional MRIs are required; for instance, if ARIA is detected on a routine scan or if a patient has new symptoms suggesting ARIA [15]. Within the prescribing information for both lecanemab and donanemab, clinicians are encouraged to consider a follow-up MRI 2–4 months after the initial identification of ARIA to evaluate radiographic resolution (ARIA-E) or stabilization (ARIA-H) before ATT treatment is resumed (if it was suspended due to ARIA) [12, 13]. Clinicians should ensure MRI assessments for ARIA are completed on the basis of the frequency recommendations outlined in the relevant prescribing information, but they may decide to arrange earlier/more frequent follow-up scans (e.g., monthly).

It is important that MRI acquisition protocols are standardized [4, 6, 8, 32] and that written protocols for serious and severe ARIA are in place before administering ATTs [9, 24].

ARIA Management

If any ARIA events occur in patients receiving an ATT, careful clinical evaluation is required before maintaining or reinitiating treatment [12, 13]. Clinicians should follow the recommendations for treatment interruption and/or discontinuation per the appropriate prescribing information and their local institutional protocols. The prescribing information for both donanemab and lecanemab present specific dose management recommendations for ARIA-E (Table 2) and ARIA-H (Table 3) depending on their clinical and radiographic severity [12, 13]. If ARIA is detected, communication of results to patients and across the healthcare team is paramount for patient safety. As outlined in the prescribing information and AURs, dosing of ATTs may be continued for asymptomatic, mild ARIA with joint decision-making by the patient and clinician, but dosing should be suspended for patients with symptomatic ARIA-H or with moderate or severe symptoms of ARIA-E, as well as for those with moderate or severe radiographic ARIA. Dosing should also be suspended in patients who develop intracerebral hemorrhage of > 1 cm during ATT treatment [12, 13].

AURs provide additional recommendations for clinically and/or radiographically severe or serious cases of ARIA. In cases with severe or serious ARIA, AURs for both lecanemab and donanemab recommend considering early initiation of high-dose glucocorticoids (e.g., intravenous methylprednisolone 1 g each day for 5 days followed by an oral steroid taper for several weeks) should be considered, and any seizures and other complications should be monitored/treated [9, 24]. Admission to a hospital or critical care unit may be required for stabilization, monitoring, and treatment [9, 24]. If a patient exhibits severe symptoms, AURs for lecanemab state that MRI with diffusion weighted imaging (DWI) sequences should be used to exclude ischemic stroke [9].

Considerations to resume treatment will depend on MRI resolution of ARIA-E and/or stabilization of ARIA-H and symptom resolution (if the patient presented with symptoms), and they should be based on clinical judgement and discussion of risks and benefits with individual patients and their care partners [9, 12, 13, 24].

Conclusions

As we enter a new era of AD treatment, clinical evidence indicates that the currently approved ATTs donanemab and lecanemab can slow cognitive decline in patients with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to AD who have confirmed Aβ accumulation. However, careful attention to the risk of ARIA cannot be understated, particularly considering the growing patient population receiving ATTs [4, 15].

ARIA was originally identified in the clinical trial setting and translation into clinical practice requires the knowledge and vigilance of APPs, as they fulfill key roles throughout the patient journey. APPs are well positioned to educate patients and their care partners about the importance of APOE testing, the possibility and impact of ARIA events, the symptoms that they may experience as a result of ARIA, and the need for routine MRI monitoring. APPs should know how to identify each patient’s ARIA risk profile, be able to recognize and interpret the clinical and radiographic aspects of ARIA, monitor key MRI findings, and manage any potential ARIA events.

While current practice guidelines provide guidance for identification and management of ARIA in patients with AD treated with ATTs, many aspects of ARIA remain unknown. Research continues to investigate the pathophysiology of ARIA, but the occurrence of ARIA (particularly ARIA-H) in the absence of ATT treatment suggests the underlying mechanism might be in part related to AD itself.

Crucially, improved knowledge about risk factors and recent advances in imaging (specifically in MRI sequences) and artificial intelligence–based ARIA detection software [33] might improve the prediction and/or identification of ARIA. Further, longitudinal imaging data might help us better understand its true incidence in the clinical population.

Finally, modified dosing strategies might help reduce the incidence of some ARIA events. For donanemab, the phase 3b TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 6 study showed that gradual titration over time reduced the incidence of ARIA-E by ~10% (which represents an approximately 40% lower relative risk) and its radiographical severity at 24 weeks compared with fixed dosing [34]. On the basis of these findings, gradual titration of donanemab is currently FDA-approved [12].

In summary, APPs play pivotal roles in the care of patients with AD, including in the safe and effective use of appropriate therapies. APPs can ensure the most appropriate care of patients receiving ATTs by understanding the important aspects of ARIA (including its pathophysiology, risk factors, associated symptoms, and monitoring/management recommendations), always using their clinical judgment, and effectively communicating with others across the care team. Although we have presented a practical overview of key information about ARIA, providers should be aware that many unknowns persist, which ongoing research aims to uncover. Keeping abreast of potential changes in the prescribing information will greatly benefit patients with AD.
