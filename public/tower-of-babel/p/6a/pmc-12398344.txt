Sustainability of a Nurse-Led End of Life (PEACH) Community Model of Care
Authors: Josephine Sau Fan Chow, Nutan Maurya, Kim Jobburn, Janeane Harlum
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Palliative Extended and Care at Home (PEACH) program, implemented in 2013, aimed to support palliative care clients in their last days of life at their own home. Understanding the factors that influence the sustainability of a program was vital to improving the longevity, adaptability, and quality of service delivery models. This study aimed to assess the sustainability of PEACH model of care through analysis of data at different time points where services were delivered by different service providers. Deidentified data were collected retrospectively for all consecutive patients receiving a PEACH package until separation from the package to explore the clinical and sociodemographic determinants of the sustainable PEACH model of care. Additionally, feedback on the services via survey was obtained from the clients’ primary carers. The majority of the PEACH package recipients who had a clear preference to die at home when the service was initiated were able to achieve their goal to die at home (77%–84%). Eighty-six percent of the carers were “satisfied” or “very satisfied” with the overall care provided by PEACH. The result shows that PEACH model of care managed to sustain and optimize the patients’ outcome despite transitioning to different service providers for partnerships and collaboration.

Palliative care aimed to prevent and relieve suffering and improve the quality of life of people with life-limiting illness1 through the early identification, correct assessment, and treatment of pain and other problems, physical, psychosocial, and spiritual. Quality palliative care services at end-of-life (EOL) were essential whether they were located in the hospital, hospice, or home to improve carer and patient wellbeing.2 Identifying and achieving patient’s preferences for place of death was recognized as a quality indicator for EOL care.3

Care should be individualized and reflect the needs and preferences of the dying person and those who were important to them. Across the globe what constitutes good care for the dying and the provision of that care, continued to be a challenge in its delivery outcomes. Shared decision-making was a critical part of making sure that patients approaching EOL are partners in their own care. It was a process that allowed patients, substitute decision-makers, families, and interdisciplinary teams to work together to make decisions in the patient’s best interests, based on the best scientific evidence available, the realities of the patient’s clinical condition and treatment options, and the patient’s choices, values and preferences.4

Reports from the Productivity Commission suggest that 70% of Australians preferred to die at home; however, only 4% to 12% did, which contrasts starkly with patients’ preferences.5 Globally, the literature suggested that preferences for 70% to 90% of the cases were constant throughout their course of illness.6 However, the preferences for locations of care or death could change over a period of time as symptoms became more acute, especially in EOL due to a number of demographic, psychosocial, and disease-related factors.3,7,8 Identifying the factors that influence the location of place of death within a tailored model of care would better inform discussions among healthcare providers, patients, and their families regarding patient preferences and the feasibility of dying in the preferred place.

Home-based Palliative care services had been expanding worldwide, and many people at the EOL preferred to be cared for and in many cases also died at home. Studies showed that these programs offered substantial savings to the health system, including a decrease in total healthcare costs and resource use9–11 and had been successful in improving patients' and caregivers’ experience at the EOL through better symptom control, reducing unwanted hospitalizations, coordination of care and improved communication among professionals, patient, and family and increased the odds of home deaths.6,7,9,11,12

PEACH PROGRAM

In 2012, the South Western Sydney Local Health District (SWSLHD) implemented an innovative and cost-effective model of care, which enabled “choice” for patients and their carer’s in preferred places of EOL care. The Palliative Extended and Care at Home (PEACH) model of care (a rapid response nursing care package delivered in conjunction with existing palliative care community services) was developed aiming to increase days at home for those who had high or complex palliative care needs. The model was evidence-based and facilitates the opportunity for patients to die at home or stay at home as long as possible (as per their preference) with clinical care and support. To support the sustainability of this model for EOL care at home, formal evaluation using well-designed and Ethical methodologies were conducted from time to time.13–16 The findings from the evaluation suggested that PEACH model of care was cost-effective.16 It not only allowed people with palliative diagnoses to meet their preference to die at home, it achieved this while maintaining symptom control.14,15

The model of care provision differed across Australia, with each state and territory having a specific approach to providing palliative care-related services. The states and territories had different approaches to planning and delivering publicly funded services, different local service delivery practices and differently structured health care systems. Considering the aging and growing population in Australia, the demand for palliative care is expected to increase by 50% between now and 2035, and double by 2050.17 The current literature reflected the need for a greater degree of standardization across models and increased rigor in research to achieve greater demand for and utilization of Home-based Palliative Care services amongst patients and healthcare professionals.18 Sustainability is important for a program to persist in the long term and can potentially be challenging, even when considered beneficial to patients, practitioners, or the public. Therefore, to develop a sustainable program, insight into the factor’s influencing sustainability was essential. Understanding factors that influence the sustainability of a program and ways to mitigate these factors was vital to improving the longevity, adaptability, and quality of service delivery models.

STUDY AIMS/OBJECTIVES

This observational study aimed to assess the sustainability of the PEACH model of care. Secondary aim was to explore the clinical and sociodemographic determinants of the sustainable nurse-led PEACH model of care. Additionally, obtain feedback on the services via a cross-sectional survey from the perspective of clients’ primary carers to identify where the service had met the needs of the client and carer and where the service needed improvement.

STUDY POPULATION

Sociodemographic and Clinical Determinants

Participants were consecutive recipients of the PEACH rapid response nurse-led model of care who were clinically assessed as being in the last week(s) of life with a terminal or deteriorating phase of illness (Palliative Care Outcomes Collaborative phase definitions) and who had an Australia-modified Karnofsky Performance Scale score ≤40 and had a preference for home as preferred place of death. In addition, the patient, in the opinion of the referrer, needed to be experiencing burdensome symptoms associated with the palliative care diagnosis that required an increased level of support, increased care needs, and/or additional caregiver support to enable the client to remain at home, and also had to be aware of the diagnosis and referral and had a caregiver residing with him/her. Reflecting real-world practice, some participants were provided packages if they had a strong wish to try for a home death although their absolute preference remained undecided and, in a few cases, because of specific circumstances where they had a preference to receive the majority of care at home but die in an inpatient setting, but with transfer when they were imminently dying (eg, due to patients’ wishes not to die in the home in the presence of young children).14,15

Carer Survey

Participants were the primary carers of clients who received PEACH package. The primary carer was identified at the time of the client’s referral to the service. During the data collection phase, all primary carers were posted an invitation package to participate in the evaluation at least 3 months after the client’s death. This is based upon previous experience with similar carer feedback surveys,13 and in keeping with research conducted with bereaved relatives in palliative care setting.

DATA SOURCE/COLLECTION

Sociodemographic and Clinical Determinants

Deidentified data including sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were collected retrospectively for all consecutive patients receiving a PEACH package until separation from the package. Deidentified data were used from administrative and clinical information systems according to standardized data requirements and clinical assessments. Participants were allocated an alpha-numeric code to ensure confidentiality and anonymity. The outcome of interest for this study was mode of separation, which was defined as the status of the patient at the end of the PEACH package episode of care (discharge, transfer, or death) and the place to which the person is transferred.19 Three possible modes of separation were identified from the PEACH package, namely (1) person died at home while still receiving a PEACH package, (2) was admitted to a hospital or inpatient palliative care unit or if (3) were discharged from the package (alive and no longer requiring PEACH package at this stage).

Assessments of Covariates

At the time of referral to the PEACH program, baseline data was collected to describe the cohort-based and to determine the association of social, demographic, and clinical predictors with mode of separation at the end of the PEACH episode of care. Sociodemographic data included; age, gender, caregiver relationship to client (spouse, child, grandchild, other relative, and others), preference for place of death on referral (classified as home, hospital/Palliative Care Unit, or undecided), diagnosis (malignant and nonmalignant), palliative care phase on referral, location of referral (hospital/palliative care unit [PCU], community), language spoken (English, no English), and as a proxy measure of geographical distance to admitted hospital services Local Government Area (metropolitan, inner and outer regional, remote or very remote as defined by Australian Classification of Local Governments determined by the Australian Bureau of Statistics). A regional area in Australia is any town, small city or area that falls beyond the major capital cities of Sydney, Melbourne and Brisbane.

Carer Survey

Primary carers of clients who used the service were identified through the PEACH database at SWSLHD, which was the computerized client management system. As part of routine care, details of the client’s primary carer were recorded by staff at the time of referral into the service and entered into the PEACH program database at SWSLHD. The SWSLHD PEACH team generated a report on a regular basis listing all clients of the service who had recently died. The client’s primary carer posted a letter inviting them to participate in a survey, together with the participant information sheet, carer feedback paper survey, and reply-paid envelope. The participants also had the option to complete the survey online (using the SurveyMonkey platform) accessible via a QR code link to the survey in the participant information sheet. Participants were advised that submitting/sending the survey indicated consent to participate. The survey data were deidentified and coded to ensure the confidentiality of the survey responses.

Survey Instrument

The postal/online survey included closed and open-ended questions. Question 1 (17 items) was based on the FAMCARE scale, a validated instrument developed in Canada to measure family satisfaction with advanced cancer care.20 The remaining questions (Questions 3 and 4) use Likert scales to measure satisfaction with aspects of the service not covered in the FAMCARE scale, as well as a section for comments about the care received and suggestions for improvement. In Questions 5, 6, and 7, carers were asked about their relationship to the client, age and gender.

STUDY INTERVENTION

The PEACH Program is an NSW government-funded program, which was a partnership between, SWSLHD and a Non-Government Organisation (NGO), geographically covering metropolitan and semirural communities. Australia provided universal healthcare (Medicare), which might be supplemented by private health insurance. The NSW Government funded interdisciplinary specialist palliative care services to provide specialist care and support to general practitioners and community nurses across the State. The program was centrally coordinated through a hub that coordinated community care services including palliative care. The Program “wraps around” and complements existing services delivered by General Practitioners, SWSLHD Community Nurses and Specialist Palliative Care Teams. The additional services were provided by the service provider (NGO) in the form of personal care service during the day (optional) and an evening visit by a Registered Nurse specializing in Palliative Care. Access to overnight support was provided through the SWSLHD Palliative Care Telephone Support Service with a Palliative Care Registered Nurse who could access the patient’s care plan on electronic medical records. The PEACH program commenced in 2012 in the selective LHDs (Illawarra Shoalhaven, Nepean Blue Mountains, South Western Sydney, Sydney, and Western Sydney) with nursing services delivered in partnership with a nongovernment organization (NGO1). Since July 2021, PEACH was transitioned to SWSLHD only and different service provider (NGO2) with minimal variation in service provisions

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Quantitative data were analyzed descriptively using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (version 22). Sociodemographic characteristics were summarized using frequency counts and percentages for categorical data and means for continuous variables. Univariate analyses were conducted to evaluate the association of sociodemographic factors with mode of separation. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated using logistic regression. A P value less than .05 was considered statistically significant. The response to the open-ended question from the survey was analyzed using a qualitative descriptive approach.

Carer Survey

NGO 1

Survey data were collated in December 2014 and again in February 2016. A combined total of 199 surveys (inclusive of other 4 LHDs) were sent to the carers, 123 (62%) of which were returned. Of 17 aspects of care provided by the PEACH program, 13 were scored with “exceptional” or “acceptable performance.” The majority of the carers were either “satisfied” or “very satisfied” with the overall care provided at home during the last week of the client’s life. The highest satisfaction was observed in meeting clients’ physical needs and providing pain relief. The outcome of the survey has been published elsewhere.13

NGO 2

Survey data were collated in October 2023. Seventy surveys were sent out (only SWSLHD), 21 carers responded to the survey (response rate: 30%). Question 1 of the survey contained 17 items addressing various aspects of care provided to the client by the PEACH program care team. Of the survey respondents, 86% were “satisfied” or “very satisfied” with the overall care provided at home during the last week of the client’s life. The highest satisfaction was observed in providing pain relief and emotional support, followed by response to the changing care needs and inclusion of family in making treatment and care decision (Figure).

Question 2 in the survey was regarding level of satisfaction with the overnight video and telephone support, logistics, and team-working aspects of the service provided. Most respondents were highly satisfied with the way the Care Team worked together (n = 19/20, 95%) and with the treating doctor (n = 12/20, 60%). A high number of respondents stated that they did not use the services, particularly overnight phone (n = 11/20, 55%), or video support (n = 15/20, 75%).

Question 3 asked respondents to comment on the service and the quality of care the client received at home during their last week of care and any suggestions for improvement. Fifteen people responded to the open-ended question. Mostly the carers were happy and satisfied with the overall care and support they received. They expressed profound gratitude for the compassionate care provided during a difficult time and support they received during the time when it is most needed.

“Very happy and satisfied with support care and emotional support to family”

Several noted the successful fulfillment of their loved one’s wish to die at home, comfortably and in the presence of the family, facilitated by the service provided by PEACH.

“Wonderful service, fill of admiration from their caring nature, professionalism, support to carer, and able to take time to show my daughter and I, how to load the dosage and amenities ourselves always available when needed. and especially at the end made enormous difference to the delivering care. we were able to provide to my husband in his last few weeks. There were extensive acknowledgements of all staff involved, especially nurses for their dedication, compassion, attentiveness, professionalism, kindness and high standards of care.

Some of the suggestions for improvement were; better information flow and communication between Care Team and treating doctor, provision of additional support services such as domestic help to enhance the overall care experience.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to assess the sustainability of the PEACH model of care through analysis of impact of the baseline demographic, social and clinical variables and separation status after completion of an episode of care receiving a PEACH package. Patients which a clear preference for home death at referral, those referred to the PEACH program from the hospital/Palliative care unit, those cared for by their child or grandchild and patients residing in the regional area were less likely to be separated from the program and admitted to the hospital. The finding was in the context of previous prior study outcomes15 that most of the PEACH package recipients who had a clear preference to die at home when the service was initiated were able to achieve their goal to die at home (77%–84%). Patient’s preference for home death was the strongest predictor of home death as the final places of care, which is consistent with findings from other studies.7

Place of residence was identified as a significant factor influencing home deaths. However irrespective of living in rural or urban area home were recognized as a preferred place of death.21 In previous study residing in metropolitan areas was identified as a predictor of admission to the hospital/PCU, which was similar to the study reporting higher likelihood of dying in a hospital for people living in urban areas.22 A systematic review examining associations between socioeconomic determinants and place of death among adult patients with life-limiting illnesses also reported strong association between dying at home and living in rural areas.23 However, these findings were in contrast with study by Cheon et al, who reported living in town/small city (regional area) were associated with decreased odds of home death.24

People who were cared for by their child or grandchild as caregiver were less likely to be admitted to hospital/PCU.14,15 Strong correlations were reported between family caregiver support and preference for home death25,26 or likelihood of home death.6,27 In a study about influences on carer preference of older people with advanced illness, areas of influence were identified to be family and care context, individual and illness. To illustrate, a preference for dying at home may be overridden by views of family supports and coping.28 Therefore, “practical realities and care needs” played a key role29 and suggested a clinical focus to support patient preferences via changes to care and support for family involvement.28 Over time due to deterioration in the patient’s illness and changes in the situation, the patient and their carers might require more intense and extensive support from the home palliative care team and home care services,30,31 which needed to be organized in a way that ensures continuity and predictability.30 In this study carer survey demonstrated that majority (86%) of the clients’ carers were “satisfied” or “very satisfied” with the overall care provided at home and felt supported by the care team during the last week of the client’s life, which is similar to the findings reported in the previous feedback study.13 The highest satisfaction was observed in providing pain relief and emotional support, followed by response to the changing care needs and inclusion of family in making treatment and care decision.

An increase in the referral from Community Health Centre (from 63% to 74%) and noncancer group (from 17% to 24%) and decrease in separation rate from the program and admission to Hospital or Palliative Care Unit (15%–8%) over the years was also noted. The result showed that despite of the changes such as alternative service providers (since 2013 as part of the requirement for procurement of service delivery) the model had continued to provide the optimal outcomes in providing choice for patients and their families at EOL care and achieving that preference. It also demonstrated how the embedding of a model into the system and sustaining a program that had good outcomes initially continued to improve as a result of the continuous quality improvement activities.

Findings from previous13–16 and current evaluation of PEACH program provided evidence for the model of care to be transferrable (smooth), scalable and sustainable. The outcomes would also inform funding models, access and policy decisions to optimize approaches to improving a person’s likelihood of dying in their preferred place without compromising care and providing the optimal use of community-based health care resources.

Strengths and Limitations

The study undertook to explore the clinical and sociodemographic determinants of sustainable nurse-led PEACH model of care, these data can be routinely collected in clinical care of the patient and included in assessments at referral which could subsequently direct further needs assessment and/or intensity of care. One of the limitations of the study was smaller sample size for the carer survey.

Future Implications

With greater demand for EOL at home and use of technology such telehealth to support the Model could continue to develop. This model had been sustained and now embedded within the SWSLHD End of Life & Palliative Care Strategy to 202832 and focus on the planning to integrate the model into primary and community health in the future to eliminate the need for NGO as service provider.

CONCLUSION

The results of this study provided an insight into the factors that influenced the sustainability of a model of best practice for palliative care at home. The information obtained will further assist the providers and the local health district to improve services locally. This model of care had continued to demonstrate outstanding achievement in enabling the “choice” for patients in their preferred place at EOL care by successfully translating both existing and emerging research evidence to ensure the delivery of safe and patient-centerd care. It had managed to sustain and optimize the patients’ outcome despite transitioning to different service providers for partnerships and collaboration; involving clinicians, patients, family, researchers, universities, industry and resulted in exceptional instances of research that has influenced policy and practice.
