Deep learning in the diagnosis, staging, and prognosis of osteonecrosis of the femoral head: a systematic review

Background

Osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) is a disabling disease caused by compromised vascular supply to the femoral head, which leads to cell death, disrupted bone remodeling, and eventually subchondral collapse [1, 2]. ONFH is a major contributor to hip dysfunction and is among the most frequent indications for hip arthroplasty in young and middle-aged adults [3]. Given the functional burden, irreversible joint damage, and the limited lifetime of prostheses, early diagnosis and intervention are crucial to alter the natural course of the disease [4].

The clinical course of ONFH is characterized by a high risk of femoral head collapse, which significantly changes treatment strategy; joint-preserving techniques (e.g. core decompression, osteotomy, bone grafting) are viable in pre-collapse stages, but once collapse or secondary osteoarthritis develops, total hip arthroplasty becomes the primary option [5, 6]. Thus, timely and accurate detection of ONFH in early stages can prevent structural collapse, preserve joint architecture, and postpone or may avoid arthroplasty [5, 7].

Imaging modalities play a key role in ONFH diagnosing and staging. Plain radiographs are often the first-line modality due to cost and accessibility, but they lack sensitivity in early disease. CT provides detailed bone morphology but still may miss early marrow changes. MRI remains the gold standard, capable of detecting bone marrow edema, necrotic zones, and early structural disruption [1, 3]. However, MRI interpretation is vulnerable to inter-reader variability and requires subspecialty expertise, especially in borderline or early cases [6]. Such challenges in consistency and reproducibility increase the need for methods that can augment or standardize interpretation.

Recently, deep learning (DL), a branch of artificial intelligence, has shown strong promise in medical imaging, including musculoskeletal applications. DL models can learn complex image features beyond human perception, and have been successfully applied for lesion detection, segmentation, classification, and prediction tasks in radiographs, CT, and MRI. In ONFH specifically, DL tools hold potential to reduce reliance on expert interpretation, improve diagnostic consistency across institutions, and support earlier intervention decisions.

Although multiple DL-based studies in ONFH have emerged, they vary considerably in design, validation rigor, reporting transparency, and adoption of explainability techniques. The field lacks a clear synthesis regarding to module’s application, performance and methodological gaps which hinder translation into clinical practice. Moreover, no prior reviews have critically appraised these DL studies in ONFH using systematic AI-specific criteria.

In this context, our review is focused exclusively on ONFH and aims to (1) systematically synthesize DL applications across imaging modalities, (2) evaluate methodological quality and reporting (with attention to explainability and clinical translatability) and (3) highlight challenges and directions for future research toward more explainable and robust AI in ONFH imaging.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines [8].

Search strategy

We performed a comprehensive literature search on 1 [9] August 2025 in four databases: PubMed, Embase, Scopus and Web of Science. The search strategy combined three conceptual domains including deep learning, osteonecrosis, and imaging modalities (radiograph, MRI, CT). using both controlled vocabulary(MeSH terms) and free-text synonyms. The complete database-specific search strategies are provided in Supplementary Document 1. The initial search yielded 301 records (PubMed: 57; Embase: 108; Scopus: 88; Web of Science: 48). After removal of 153 duplicates, 148 unique articles remained and were screened for relevance. Backward and forward citation screening were not performed; all records were identified through structured database searches.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, then assessed full texts of potentially relevant articles. Any disagreements during screening or selection were resolved by discussion or, when required, resolved by opinion of a third reviewer. Only articles meeting the following criteria were included in data extraction.

Eligibility Criteria

(i)employed deep-learning methods for any imaging-related task relevant to osteonecrosis of the femoral head (ONFH) (e.g., classification, segmentation, detection, feature extraction, or representation learning) or used hybrid DL–ML pipelines in which deep learning was applied to the imaging component (e.g., CNN-based feature extraction or U-Net segmentation);(ii)involved human subjects undergoing radiographic, CT, or MRI evaluation for ONFH;(iii)were peer-reviewed, full-text articles published in English; and.(iv)reported quantitative model performance metrics (e.g., AUC, accuracy, Dice score).

Data extraction

Data were extracted independently by two reviewers into a structured Excel spreadsheet. Extracted variables included reference number, title, author, year, journal, imaging modality, sample condition, sample size, demographics (male, female), number of hips/images/segments, mean age, model/network architecture, pretraining strategy, data augmentation, detection/segmentation method, classifier, hyperparameter tuning, best performance metrics (accuracy, sensitivity/recall, specificity, PPV, AUC, F1-score), MAIC-10 checklist items (clinical need, study design, safety and privacy, data curation, annotation, partitioning, AI model robustness, explainability, transparency, total score out of 10), and any other metrics or notes. Any discrepancies in extracted data were resolved by consensus or a third reviewer to ensure completeness and consistency. No additional data were requested from study authors; all extracted information was obtained from the published manuscripts and their supplementary materials.

Quality assessment (MAIC-10)

The methodological quality of each included study was assessed using the MAIC-10 (Medical AI Critical Appraisal Checklist, 10-item) framework. MAIC-10 evaluates ten domains relevant to the development and reporting of medical AI systems: clinical need, study design, safety and privacy, data curation, data annotation, data partitioning, AI model description, robustness, explainability, and transparency. Each domain was scored as 0 (criterion not met) or 1 (criterion met), yielding total scores ranging from 0 to 10. Two reviewers independently scored all studies, with disagreements resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer. Inter-reviewer agreement was quantified using Cohen’s kappa coefficient to ensure scoring consistency. Per-study MAIC-10 domain scores and total scores are provided in Supplementary Document 2, and summary figures showing domain-level averages and score distributions are included in the main manuscript(Figs. 2 and 3).

Data synthesis

Because of the substantial heterogeneity across studies - including differences in model architectures, imaging modalities, patient populations, validation strategies, and reported performance metrics (e.g., AUC, accuracy, sensitivity/specificity, Dice coefficient) - formal meta-analysis was considered inappropriate. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively. Studies were grouped by imaging modality (radiography, CT, MRI) and by deep-learning task (classification, segmentation, prognostic prediction). For each group, we summarized performance ranges and key methodological characteristics, and we highlighted common reporting gaps using MAIC-10 domains as a guide. This heterogeneity in study design and outcome reporting precluded quantitative pooling.

This systematic review was not prospectively registered, as PROSPERO currently does not accept registrations for non-interventional reviews such as diagnostic-imaging or AI-modeling reviews.

Results

Study selection

The database search conducted on 18 August 2025 yielded a total of 301 records (PubMed = 57, Embase = 108, Scopus = 88, Web of Science = 48). After removal of 153 duplicates, 148 unique records remained for screening.

Title and abstract screening was performed independently by two reviewers using the Rayyan tool for systematic reviews, after which 113 articles were excluded. The remaining 35 studies were retrieved for full-text assessment. Of these, 5 studies were excluded because they were non–peer-reviewed conference abstracts [10–14], 1 study was excluded because it was a case report that did not evaluate a DL model for ONFH. One study was excluded because used purely ML approaches [15] and 2 studies were excluded because they were focused on LCPD patients [16, 17]. Ultimately, 26 studies met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1 (PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [8]).

Study characteristics

A total of 26 studies were included. Apart from one historical feasibility study from 1993 (MRI-based neural network) [18], the remaining 25 studies were published between 2019 and 2025, reflecting the rapid expansion of DL applications in ONFH imaging.

Most studies employed MRI as the primary modality. Of these, 14 used MRI exclusively, while others combined MRI with CT for translation pretraining [19], or used CT as a comparator [20]. One multimodal study integrated MRI, CT, and radiographs to detect, localize, and guide intraoperative evaluation [21]. Radiographs were used in 7 studies, mainly for automated detection, classification, or staging tasks, and 2 studies relied exclusively on CT [22, 23].

Sample sizes ranged widely, from small feasibility studies with fewer than 30 patients [21, 24, 25] to a very large radiographic dataset of nearly 59,372 hip images [26]. The majority of studies included adult patients in their third to sixth decades of life, in line with ONFH epidemiology.

The objectives of the included studies varied, but several themes emerged. Many focused on early detection of ONFH, particularly in pre-collapse stages where intervention may alter disease course. A large group of studies developed classification and staging frameworks, often integrating segmentation to delineate necrotic lesions and grade disease severity, thereby supporting clinical decision-making for joint-preserving surgery versus arthroplasty. Others explored prediction models, aiming to estimate treatment outcomes such as graft survival [27], post-fixation ONFH risk [28], or likelihood of femoral head collapse [29], while some examined vascular response, lesion progression, or postoperative changes [24]. In addition, a subset of studies sought to improve segmentation and quantification, including automated delineation of necrotic regions and extraction of quantitative features to characterize disease extent [30]. A smaller but innovative group focused on integrated diagnostic frameworks [21], combining detection, classification, and automated report generation, or extending applications to intraoperative guidance by fusing MRI, CT, and radiographs.

Together, these objectives illustrate how DL has been applied not only for diagnosis but also for staging, treatment guidance, prognostication, and even surgical planning in ONFH. Summary of included studies is provided in Table 1.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies was evaluated using the Must AI Criteria-10 (MAIC-10) checklist [43], which assesses 10 key domains relevant to artificial intelligence in diagnostic imaging. Inter-reviewer agreement for MAIC-10 scoring was substantial (κ = 0.75), indicating good consistency between reviewers.

Across the 26 studies, total MAIC-10 scores ranged from 2 to 9 (out of 10), with a mean score of 6.27 and a median of 6.0, indicating an overall moderate methodological quality. Based on these scores, 1 study (3.8%) was rated as low quality (≤ 3), 15 studies (53.9%) as moderate (4–6), and 11 studies (42.3%) as high quality (≥ 7).

Most studies clearly defined a clinical need and described their AI model architecture and training procedure. However, several consistent methodological weaknesses were observed. Only a subset of studies reported external validation or multi-institutional testing, usually in the form of an independent hospital cohort or geographic/temporal test set. In these studies, performance generally remained good but was lower than in the internal test sets, with typical AUC drops of roughly 0.02–0.15 [26, 34, 36, 40]. The majority of models were evaluated only on single-center, retrospective data, underscoring the current uncertainty around generalizability.

While internal robustness was commonly addressed, external robustness including cross-scanner, cross-center, or subgroup performance was evaluated in only a small minority of studies. Explainability and transparency were also limited: only a minority implemented visualization or interpretability tools (e.g., Grad-CAM, SHAP, or LIME), and few provided open-source code or data access.

Data curation and annotation quality varied; although most datasets were labeled by experienced, inter-observer variability and consensus methods were often not reported. Sample size adequacy was another concern for several MRI-based studies, whereas radiograph studies more frequently utilized large institutional datasets.

Notably, methodological rigor improved over time: studies published after 2022 were more likely to include external datasets, employ structured validation pipelines, and report partial explainability outputs, reflecting progressive alignment with emerging AI-reporting standards.

Diagnostic classification and staging

Fifteen studies focused on diagnostic classification or staging of ONFH, encompassing automated identification of early or pre-collapse disease, severity grading, and stage-specific differentiation to assist treatment planning. Overall diagnostic performance was strong in internal evaluations, though performance varied with dataset size, imaging protocol, and model complexity.

MRI-based models (n = 6) consistently achieved strong discriminative performance, with reported AUC = 0.95–0.98 (internal) and 0.85–0.97 (external), and typical accuracies > 95%. Sensitivities and specificities ranged 0.90–0.98. Multi-sequence or multi-protocol inputs [34–36] improved stability across disease stages. The DenseNet multicenter model [34] and single-sequence DLR network [30] each achieved AUC > 0.95, performed favorably compared with expert assessments in the reported cohorts. Ensemble and attention-enhanced frameworks [32, 35, 36] further improved inter-stage differentiation, though subtle subchondral signal changes remained challenging. Some multimodal pipelines that incorporated CT or cross-domain MR–CT attention enhanced staging consistency and early progression prediction, though dataset heterogeneity limited external reproducibility.

Radiograph-based models (n = 5) demonstrated encouraging diagnostic performance with AUC = 0.90–0.97 and accuracies ranging from 0.89 to 0.99, Sensitivities 0.67–0.98 and specificities 0.88–1.00. The AVN-net system [31] achieved AUC = 0.97 across multi-view inputs, performing non-inferior to two-view expert interpretation in the study cohort, The Chee et al. [9] algorithm reached AUC = 0.93, demonstrated non-inferior performance to the study’s test cohort using MRI as reference, while YOLOv10 [37] maintained AUC = 0.93–0.94 across external datasets. The large-scale Chen et al. [26] model analyzed 59 372 radiographs, achieving internal AUC = 0.91–0.96 and external = 0.99, confirming scalability.

No purely CT-based diagnostic study was identified; however, CT pipelines have been evaluated primarily in segmentation-focused workflows (see Sect. 4.2), confirming feasibility for structural mapping rather than classification.

Collectively, MRI- and radiograph-based deep-learning models reported AUC values > 0.90 in internal test sets; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as their real-world diagnostic value remains unproven. Most models have not undergone prospective, multicenter, or workflow-integrated evaluation, and their performance across diverse clinical settings is unknown. Remaining barriers include absence of standardized multicenter datasets, under-reporting of calibration and uncertainty, and limited external validation.

Segmentation and quantitative analysis

Nine studies concentrated on segmentation or quantitative morphometry, targeting delineation of necrotic lesions, volumetric quantification, and geometric analysis of the femoral head for staging or surgical planning. Most adopted U-Net-based convolutional architectures, often extended to 3D or augmented with attention or geometric supervision.

MRI-based segmentation (n = 5) achieved Dice = 0.75–0.93, confirming reliable overlap with expert annotations. The 3-D U-Net [38] reached Dice 0.88 with accurate volume estimation, while MsgeCNN [39] improved femoral-head delineation (accuracy ≈ 91%). The nnU-Net [25] yielded Dice 0.75 (necrotic) and 0.91 (normal bone), indicating strong volumetric correlation, and the 3D-SPGR model [41] reported Dice ≈ 0.89 and 93.7% Steinberg classification accuracy.

CT-based segmentation (n = 2) achieved Dice ≈ 0.86–0.99, with the subtracting–adding strategy [22] improving recall and precision, and the early CGAN-based pipeline [23] exceeding 95% accuracy.

Multimodal integration demonstrated [21] MRI to CT registration enabling intraoperative visualization, aligning necrotic regions across modalities (femur/pelvis Dice 0.90–0.93). These frameworks leveraged CT-based anatomy to enhance spatial accuracy for detection, localization, and real-time surgical guidance.

Overall, segmentation and quantitative models produced reproducible lesion boundaries (Dice ≈ 0.85–0.93) and volumetric indices, supporting objective staging and follow-up. Limitations included small datasets, limited inter-observer validation, and inconsistent robustness testing across scanners and protocols.

Predictive and prognostic models

Five studies explored predictive or prognostic applications of DL for ONFH, focusing on post-treatment outcomes, collapse risk, or vascular response. Most combined imaging features with clinical variables to enable individualized prognostication.

MRI-based models (n = 3) predicted post-fixation outcomes, graft success, or treatment response. Chen et al. [27] combined MRI and clinical data for post-fixation necrosis (AUC ≈ 0.93); Liang et al. [42] achieved AUC ≈ 0.92 for graft-success prediction; and Kong et al. [24] linked MRI segmentation (U-Net) and temporal modeling (LSTM) for vascular-response prediction (accuracy 96.5%, AUC 0.92).

Radiograph-based prediction (n = 1) [28] integrated CNN radiograph features with clinical data in a hybrid nomogram (AUC 0.95), outperforming image-only and regression baselines.

Multimodal radiomics frameworks (n = 1) [29] fused MRI segmentation with radiograph radiomics via LightGBM to predict femoral-head collapse (AUC 0.85, Sn 0.83, Sp 0.73). Emerging time-aware frameworks further extend such models toward real-time clinical use for detection, localization, and intraoperative guidance.

Across studies, predictive and prognostic models achieved AUC ≈ 0.85–0.95 and accuracies > 90%, supporting the promise of AI-driven risk stratification and treatment planning. However, all were retrospective, single-center analyses lacking prospective validation or longitudinal follow-up.

Methodological trends & technical features

Sample size and dataset scale

Sample sizes varied widely across the included studies, from fewer than 50 patients in some MRI segmentation and progression-prediction works to more than 50,000 hip radiographs in the largest multi-center radiograph study. Most MRI-based models were trained on single-center datasets with tens to a few hundred patients, whereas only a few radiograph-based models leveraged thousands of images from multiple institutions. We did not attempt a formal correlation between sample size and performance, but models trained on the largest datasets tended to report narrower confidence intervals and more stable metrics than those derived from very small cohorts.

Annotation quality

Reporting of annotation procedures was highly heterogeneous. Many studies relied on single-reader pixel-level or ROI annotations created by an expert radiologist or orthopaedic surgeon, sometimes with secondary review, whereas only a minority used multi-reader consensus or formal adjudication workflows. Because detailed annotation protocols and annotator numbers were frequently under-reported, and only a small subset quantified inter-reader agreement, we were unable to stratify model performance by annotation scheme. Nonetheless, variability in labeling practice is likely to contribute to heterogeneity in reported metrics, particularly for early or pre-collapse stages where image interpretation is more subjective.

Architectures used, pretraining, and augmentation

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and their variants formed the methodological core.

For diagnostic and staging tasks, architectures such as ResNet, DenseNet, Inception, EfficientNet, and lightweight models like MobileNet were most frequently fine-tuned. Segmentation and quantitative analyses predominantly employed Segmentation and quantitative analyses predominantly used U-Net variants (n = 11) and nnU-Net frameworks (n = 4), while many studies relied on manual or ROI-based reference masks (n = 12) rather than fully automated segmentation.

Ensembling of 2D and 3D networks appeared in several recent works to balance spatial context and computational efficiency [27, 32, 36].

Pretraining was reported in about one-third of the studies, most commonly on ImageNet [27, 30, 32, 36, 40] A few additional studies used other pretrained weights, although explicit descriptions of pretraining protocols were often limited [20, 33].

Pretraining consistently improved convergence and mitigated overfitting in limited datasets.

Data augmentation was used in majority of studies (16/26), encompassing random flips, rotations, cropping, scaling, and intensity jittering.

Segmentation studies further used patch-based sampling and class-balance augmentation to stabilize training on small necrotic lesions.

However, only a few explicitly quantified augmentation effects or reported augmentation probabilities and parameter ranges [9, 32, 33].

Hyperparameter tuning was inconsistently detailed; some papers specified grid or Bayesian optimization and early stopping criteria [9, 29], whereas others used “default settings” without disclosing batch size, optimizer, or learning-rate schedules.

Compute environments and hardware configurations were rarely reported.

Validation schemes

Validation strategies were heterogeneous.

Most studies relied on internal hold-out testing or k-fold cross-validation, typically with 70/20/10 or 80/10/10 splits.

Only a small minority (≈ 20%) incorporated external validation on an independent institutional dataset, and none conducted prospective assessment.

Even among multi-institutional datasets, strict site-wise hold-out testing was uncommon.

While cross-validation enhanced stability, the absence of true external evaluation limits confidence in generalizability.

No studies assessed temporal drift or scanner-protocol robustness across imaging centers.

Explainability and transparency

Explainability was addressed qualitatively in most publications.

Common methods included saliency-based visualizations (e.g., Grad-CAM and Grad-CAM++), attention heatmaps, and segmentation overlays, visually confirming that the model focused on the necrotic or subchondral regions of interest.

These approaches improve transparency and allow visual plausibility checks but rarely quantified interpretability or correlated it with expert perception.

No studies performed reader-impact or decision-support analyses to evaluate whether these explanations influenced diagnostic confidence or treatment planning.

Reporting transparency improved in recent years, with clearer model descriptions and partition details, yet open-source code and public datasets remain scarce.

Formal uncertainty quantification such as Bayesian neural networks, Monte Carlo dropout, or ensemble-variance analysis was not implemented in any paper.

Synthesis of findings & gaps

Patterns and Insights

Collectively, the evidence confirms that DL achieves promising internal diagnostic performance for ONFH, particularly on MRI and radiography.

MRI-based models excel at detecting early and pre-collapse disease by leveraging subchondral signal changes, while radiograph-based models perform robustly on large datasets, supporting potential screening or triage roles.

Segmentation frameworks generate lesion masks and quantitative indices consistent with expert delineations, enabling reproducible staging.

Predictive and prognostic models integrating imaging with clinical variables accurately forecast collapse risk or post-treatment outcomes, underscoring the transition from diagnostic to decision-support paradigms.

Recent studies more frequently employed standardized architectures and validation workflows, reflecting methodological refinement, although absolute performance metrics remained comparable to earlier reports.

Key gaps

Despite encouraging performance, several methodological limitations persist.

External validation remains scarce, and no prospective evaluation has been reported.

Reporting heterogeneity, notably in inclusion criteria, reference standards, and dataset partitioning, limits reproducibility and hinders meta-analytic synthesis.

While most studies provided point estimates and many reported 95% confidence intervals for AUC or accuracy, calibration analysis and formal predictive-uncertainty quantification were largely absent.

Advanced optimization techniques (e.g., grid or Bayesian hyperparameter tuning, cross-validation, ensembling) enhanced stability but optimize performance rather than estimate model confidence.

No study applied Bayesian neural networks, Monte Carlo dropout, or ensemble-variance analysis to express per-case reliability.

Explainability was primarily qualitative, achieved through saliency or attention maps and segmentation overlays that visually highlighted necrotic regions driving predictions.

These visualizations improve anatomical plausibility but were not tested for functional impact(whether they change reader interpretation, diagnostic certainty, or management decisions).

Transparency and data-annotation quality received the lowest MAIC-10 scores, followed by explainability, indicating that deficiencies in reporting, labeling workflows, and interpretability, constitute the major barriers to clinical translation.

Heterogeneity and implications for generalizability

Substantial heterogeneity persists across imaging modality, acquisition parameters, case mix, and labeling standards.

MRI studies differ in sequence combinations and staging references (ARCO vs. Ficat), while considerable variation existed among radiograph datasets regarding image projection (AP, lateral, or frog-leg views) and patient demographics, potentially influencing model generalizability.

Training-testing partitions, augmentation settings, and reported metrics also differ widely.

Such variability explains high within-study accuracy yet uncertain cross-study comparability.

For future research, multicenter external validation, harmonized staging criteria, standardized partitions (e.g., site-wise or temporal splits), and calibration and uncertainty reporting are essential to strengthen generalizability.

Developing open benchmarking datasets and transparent pipelines will further facilitate reproducibility and accelerate clinical adoption of AI-assisted ONFH diagnosis and prognosis.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesized 26 studies investigating the use of DL models for the diagnosis, staging, segmentation, and prognosis of ONFH.

Across modalities, DL showed promising internal diagnostic performance and consistent segmentation precision, with reported AUC values commonly exceeding 0.90 and Dice coefficients above 0.85; though these results are based primarily on retrospective, single-center datasets.

Most studies relied on MRI, which captures subchondral signal changes useful for detecting early disease, whereas radiograph-based models achieved comparable specificity and scalability on larger datasets.

CT-based and multimodal frameworks remain comparatively limited but provided additional structural information and cross-modality consistency.

Predictive and prognostic models integrating imaging with clinical features achieved promising internal accuracy for collapse or treatment-outcome prediction.

Methodological quality, assessed using the Must AI Criteria-10 (MAIC-10) checklist, was moderate overall (mean = 6.27, median = 6.0; range = 2–9).

Most studies clearly defined their clinical need and network architecture but scored lower on transparency, data annotation and explainability.

Collectively, these findings suggest that while DL models for ONFH have reached a high level of internal performance, their external validity and clinical interpretability remain to be established.

The most comprehensive prior review, Wang et al. [44], summarized 16 studies up to 2023 and provided an important technical perspective on DL architectures in ONFH imaging.

That work emphasized the evolution of CNN-based and U-Net frameworks, discussed training paradigms, and reported diagnostic metrics.

However, it primarily cataloged model types rather than critically appraising methodological quality or clinical applicability.

In contrast, the present review extends the scope to 29 studies published through August 2025, incorporates structured quality appraisal with the MAIC-10 framework, and focuses on transparency, explainability, and validation rigor.

Our synthesis therefore complements and updates Wang et al.’s model-centric analysis with a methodological and translational lens.

Both reviews converge in demonstrating the superior diagnostic performance of DL on MRI and radiography, yet our findings highlight persistent limitations in reporting standards, external validation, and explainability; factors which were not captured by prior engineering-focused reviews.

The accumulated evidence indicates that DL can substantially augment clinical assessment of ONFH. MRI-based models are capable of detecting subtle subchondral marrow changes before femoral-head collapse, offering a means of early diagnosis and intervention planning. Automated staging and segmentation may standardize preoperative evaluation, reducing inter-observer variability and improving surgical decision-making. Radiograph-based networks demonstrate scalability and could serve as screening or triage tools, especially in resource-limited or high-volume environments. Predictive frameworks that incorporate clinical and imaging features can estimate collapse risk or graft success, thereby supporting individualized treatment pathways. These roles remain hypothetical until validated prospectively or across diverse clinical settings.

Explainability through saliency maps and segmentation overlays enhances clinician confidence by confirming that model attention aligns with pathoanatomically relevant regions, though formal validation of such interpretability in decision support remains a future goal.

If integrated within picture-archiving or decision-support systems under appropriate regulatory oversight, DL tools could meaningfully accelerate ONFH diagnosis and follow-up.

Quality appraisal revealed recurring methodological weaknesses consistent with broader trends in medical-imaging AI.

External validation was rare, and no study performed a prospective assessment.

Heterogeneity in cohort definitions, staging criteria, and data-partition schemes hindered reproducibility and cross-study synthesis.

Metric reporting varied widely; while most studies presented accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, confidence intervals and calibration curves were inconsistently provided.

Explainability was implemented primarily through visual means (Grad-CAM, attention maps, overlays), without evaluation of their functional influence on clinical reasoning.

Formal uncertainty quantification (Bayesian networks, Monte Carlo dropout, ensemble-variance estimation) and calibration analysis were absent, limiting interpretability under distribution shift.

Transparency in code, dataset accessibility, and compute specification also remained limited, constraining reproducibility. Supplementary Document 2 provides the complete breakdown of MAIC-10 assessments.

To advance toward clinically deployable AI, future ONFH research should prioritize multicenter external validation using harmonized staging systems (ARCO or Ficat); standardized reporting frameworks such as PRISMA-AI, CLAIM, or DECIDE-AI; inclusion of calibration metrics and formal uncertainty quantification to express case-level reliability; functional explainability studies, for example reader-impact analyses or workflow-integration pilots; development of next-generation architectures such as transformers or hybrid CNN–transformer pipelines, as well as generative diffusion models for synthetic data generation, rare-case augmentation, or domain adaptation to address data scarcity and improve model generalizability; multimodal fusion networks combining MRI, CT, and radiographs; and establishment of open benchmarking datasets and reproducible pipelines to enable meta-analysis and fair comparison across institutions. Integration of longitudinal follow-up data and clinical outcomes will further bridge algorithmic performance with patient-level benefit.

This review represents the most comprehensive and methodologically detailed synthesis of DL applications in ONFH imaging to date.

Its strengths include inclusion of studies across all imaging modalities, explicit use of the MAIC-10 framework for AI-specific quality assessment, and adherence to PRISMA 2020 reporting standards.

By extending database coverage through August 2025 and analyzing technical, validation, and explainability features, it provides a multidimensional understanding of the field.

Limitations include reliance on English-language, peer-reviewed literature; heterogeneity precluding quantitative meta-analysis; and dependence on the completeness of published data, which may introduce reporting bias.

MAIC-10 scoring also entails some subjective interpretation, though discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

Conclusion

Although deep-learning models for ONFH demonstrate promising internal performance across diagnostic classification, staging, and prognostic prediction, the current evidence base is limited by single-center, retrospective study designs, heterogeneous imaging protocols and staging systems, and incomplete reporting of calibration, uncertainty quantification, robustness testing. Using the MAIC-10 framework, we identified transparency, data-annotation quality and explainability as the domains most consistently underreported, which currently restricts generalizability and clinical applicability.

Despite these limitations, existing models show strong technical performance within development datasets, with MRI-based systems achieving encouraging sensitivity for early-stage ONFH, radiograph-based models offering potential scalability for screening workflows, and segmentation frameworks enabling consistent quantification of necrotic extent. Hybrid deep learning–machine learning pipelines further indicate early promise for predicting structural collapse and treatment outcomes.

Future work should prioritize multicenter external validation, standardized staging and reporting practices, prospective study designs, and clinically meaningful interpretability and calibration analyses. With these methodological improvements, deep-learning models have the potential to progress from technically promising tools to reproducible, generalizable, and clinically relevant applications in ONFH imaging.
