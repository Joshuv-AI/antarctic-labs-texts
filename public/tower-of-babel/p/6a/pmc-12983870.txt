Hospital services utilisation of patients with vestibular disease in France: a retrospective nationwide study using electronic hospital health records
Authors: Stéphane Sanchez, Leila Bouazzi, Christian Chabbert, Vincent Darrouzet, Xavier Dubernard, Eva Grill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWhile most acute vestibular disorders do not require a hospital stay, admission to inpatient care because of acute vertigo, dizziness and balance problems (VDB) seems to be more frequent than necessary. The aim of this study was to analyse the frequency and regional distribution of hospital admissions for acute VDB and to estimate the associated healthcare costs.SettingWe used the complete database of inpatient stays from the French healthcare system between January 2014 and December 2022.ParticipantsPatients were included if they had a typical diagnosis code and at least one of the diagnostic or therapeutic procedures for vestibular diseases. Background

Vertigo, dizziness and balance problems (VDB) are among the most frequent symptoms that lead to consultations of medical services.1 Often, VDBs are caused by peripheral or central vestibular disorders,2 where diagnosis3 and treatment4 are generally straightforward. Nevertheless, when confronted with acute VDB, healthcare providers report considerable uncertainty and fear missing clinical “red flags” that may indicate, eg, instances of acute cerebrovascular disease.5 Thus, acute VDB seems to be a common reason for referral to specialist care and for emergency hospital admissions. For example, patients with VDB accounted for 2% of emergency service visits in Sweden6 and for 4% in the United States of America.7

While most acute vestibular disorders do not require a hospital stay, admission to inpatient care because of acute VDB seems to be more frequent than necessary. Up to 20% of emergency consultations for VDB resulted in hospital admission in a tertiary care university hospital in the USA.8 The German National Health Interview Survey found that 7% of adults with current dizziness or vertigo and 10% of adults with vestibular vertigo had previously been admitted to a hospital because of vertigo or dizziness.2

Although these figures can help in estimating the magnitude of the problem in terms of public health, the true number of hospital admissions for patients with acute VDB is still unknown. Arguably, decisions about emergency service consultations and hospital admissions should be rationally based on evidence and guidelines. Regional and societal determinants seem to influence healthcare practices for vestibular disease regardless of national and international guidelines. In practice, people with lower income or without higher education are less likely to consult a clinic, which makes them less likely to benefit from evidence-based care. This has been shown for otolaryngological conditions in the United States, and specifically for vertigo.9

Analysis of these variations is crucial for health policy-makers, as it can orient the design of policy measures aiming to decrease the burden of disease resulting from vestibular disease. The French health system has universal insurance coverage with relatively low out-of-pocket payments but regulated access to tertiary care. The health information system of the country is well suited for the task of evaluating the burden of vestibular disease, as France is one of the few countries to possess a comprehensive information system that includes data from all public and private hospitalisations. Moreover, French health authorities provide access to anonymised hospital utilisation information and direct costs for healthcare researchers. Therefore, we used data from the French healthcare system to analyse the frequency and regional distribution of hospital admissions for vertigo and dizziness. Specifically, we were interested in evaluating how the frequency of hospital admission varied across regions depending on locally measured variables, eg, social deprivation, and whether this frequency changed over time. We were also interested in the associated total direct costs accrued by inpatient treatment of acute VDB.

Methods

Data sources

We conducted a nationwide retrospective study using data extracted from the French healthcare data system (Système National des Données de Santé), an aggregate of several government-funded nationwide databases.10 Among these, the comprehensive hospital stay database (Programme for Medicalized Systems of Information, PMSI) contains information about all inpatient stays that have occurred in every French public and private hospital.

Inclusion criteria

Patients from the years 2014 to 2022 were included if they had a primary diagnosis of vestibular disease, vertigo or dizziness. A primary diagnosis is defined as the diagnosis that justifies the admission to the healthcare facility and is determined at discharge from the facility. The following diagnosis codes of the International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD10) were included: dizziness (R42), benign positional paroxysmal vertigo (codes H81.11, H81.12, H81.13 and H81.10), Menière’s disease (codes H81.01, H81.02, H81.03 and H81.09), and vestibular migraine (codes G43.109, G43.839, G43.829, G43.119, G43.011, G43.019, G43.009, G43.911, G43.919, G43.901, G43.909, G43.401 and G43.409). Code R42 (unspecific vertigo) was added to the migraine codes because there is no specific code for vestibular migraine.

As a second defining characteristic and validation step, patients were only included if they underwent at least one of the typical diagnostic or therapeutic procedures for vestibular diseases. This included stays grouped under the Groupements Homogènes de Malades thesaurus categories 03M051 to 03M054 or 03M05T, or stays having at least one selected diagnostic procedure (encoded with CCAM versions 3.1.1.5 and later: procedures CERP001 to CERP005 or CEQP002), or stays having at least one therapeutic procedure of the inner ear (encoded with CCAM versions 3.4.1 and later: procedures CCPA001 to CCPA003, CCCA001, CCCA003, CCNB001, CCFA001, CCSA001) (see online supplemental table S1 for explanation).

The unit of analysis was the inpatient stay. If the same patient was admitted twice, two stays were counted.

Variables

Patient characteristics age, sex, associated diagnoses and the Elixhauser score for comorbidity11 were extracted from the PMSI database. Patients’ residence areas were defined using the geographic code of residence recorded in the PMSI, determined from the postal code.

Extracted hospital characteristics included information on whether the facility was a private or a public hospital, and if it was a teaching hospital or not. Regional information was obtained from the national French index of regional deprivation (European Deprivation Index, FDEP, version 2020, SDI12), available from OpenDataSoft.13 14 This is a composite indicator of socio-economic status at the household level, accounting for territorial disparities. Furthermore, variables indicate the density of general practitioners (GPs) and otorhinolaryngologists (ORLs) practitioners, as well as localised potential accessibility. Localised potential accessibility is a probability measure that indicates the equity of spatial access for primary care for each commune at a fine geographical level.15 Data are available from https://drees.solidarites-sante.gouv.fr/sources-outils-et-enquetes/lindicateur-daccessibilite-potentielle-localisee-apl

We used two types of economic variables. Hospital revenues are collected by hospitals per homogeneous group of patients (ie, Diagnosis-Related Group, DRG) as a variable describing what is paid for the hospital inpatient stay by the national health insurance. The Common Methodology National Cost Study is used for describing the cost of DRG. This is a database containing the average annual costs by homogeneous patient group (ie, DRG) and by type of facility (public/private).16 The Common Methodology National Cost Study collects costs for a sample of medical and surgical facilities. Based on the data collected, a national repository of costs by financing sector (private or public) is constructed. It contains the average costs of stays/sessions according to the classification of homogeneous patient groups (ie, DRG) per year of publication. The cost items are described in the methodological guide (https://www.atih.sante.fr/enc-mco/documentation.). The full cost of an inpatient stay is broken down into five categories: (1) clinical activities (including resuscitation, intensive care and continuous monitoring); (2) diagnostic and therapeutic interventions; (3) expenses directly attributable to the stay; (4) logistics and general management; (5) medical logistics. Structural costs are excluded.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables were expressed as means with SD. Categorical variables were expressed as numbers with corresponding percentages. Quantitative variables analysed using one-way ANOVA included age, Elixhauser, comorbidity score, GP density, otolaryngologist density, neurologist density, localised potential accessibility and cost. Categorical variables analysed using the χ² test included sex, quintile of social deprivation index and type of hospital. Regression coefficients were compared across the 9 years under investigation.

The crude and age- and sex-standardised rates of inpatient stays for vestibular disease, expressed per 100 000 inhabitants, were calculated for each region. When calculating these rates, the population of France in 2017 (n=66,774,482) was used as the midpoint denominator. The age- and sex-standardised rate was estimated by direct standardisation using the age and sex distribution of the 2017 French population. Age- and sex-standardised rates were calculated at the departmental level. Univariate associations between the age- and sex-standardised rate of inpatient stay for vestibular disease and predictive factors (age, gender, Elixhauser score, GP density, otolaryngologist density, neurologist density, social deprivation index and localised potential accessibility) were assessed using a Spearman correlation or a Student’s t-test. Factors were included in a multivariate linear regression model if they were deemed as potential risk factors for hospital admission. Beta coefficients and 95% CI were derived from the model.

A sensitivity analysis was performed using the incidence rate as a categorical factor. For that, the age- and sex-standardised rate of inpatient stay for vestibular disease was divided into quintiles. To focus on departments with a high potential for intervention, a department was defined as a “high incidence rate territory” if it was in the highest quintile, else it was defined as a “low incidence rate territory. Univariable associations between the two groups and predictive factors (age, gender, Elixhauser score, GP density, otolaryngologist density, social deprivation index, localised potential accessibility and calendar year) were assessed using a Student’s t-test or a χ² test. Factors were included in a multivariate logistic regression model if they were deemed as potential risk factors for hospital admission. Beta coefficients and 95% CI were derived from the model.

Significance was tested at the two-tailed α level of 0.05. Data were analysed using SAS software version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC).

Results

Out of 277 409 765 hospitalisations between January 2014 and December 2022, a total of 482 578 inpatient stays could be directly attributed to vestibular disease, vertigo or dizziness as a primary diagnosis (Flow chart online supplemental figure S1). Age- and sex-standardised hospitalisation incidence increased from 2014 to 2019 from 160 per 100 000 to 170 per 100 000 and decreased to 145–152 per 100 000 during the pandemic years 2020, 2021 and 2022. Characteristics of our population are presented in online supplemental table S2. Mean age of admitted patients increased steadily between 2014 and 2022 (52.8 years in 2014 to 56.0 years in 2022). Patients were predominantly female, with the percentage of female patients ranging from 63.0% to 64.3%. There was no substantial variation regarding sex or number of comorbidities between 2014 and 2022. Total costs per year ranged from 61.3 million Euros in 2020 to 72.8 million Euros in 2021 with an average cost per case ranging between 1237 and 1394 Euros. An 18% reduction in costs was observed between 2019 and the initial pandemic year of 2020. Similarly, a 15% decline in cases was noted during this period.

Most frequently reported diagnoses in 2022 were peripheral vertigo (27.2%), migraine (24.6%), benign paroxysmal vertigo (14.3%) and unspecific dizziness (13.4%). Vestibular symptoms as part of a disease classified elsewhere were noted in 0.3% of all cases. Primary diagnoses of patients hospitalised for vestibular disease are presented in online supplemental table S3.

Univariate analyses showed significant differences in age, sex, Elixhauser score, general medicine density, otolaryngologist density, neurologist density, social deprivation index, localised accessibility and hospital type. In multivariate analyses, presented in table 1, higher mean values were independently associated with older age (β=0.01 per year, 95% CI 0.003 to 0.01), higher general practitioner density (β=0.13 per unit, 95% CI 0.12 to 0.14), higher neurologist density (β=1.49 per unit, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.70) and higher levels of social deprivation, with the strongest association observed for the most deprived quintile compared with the least deprived (quintile 5 vs 1: β=2.04, 95% CI 1.73 to 2.36). Conversely, higher otorhinolaryngologist density (β = −2.41 per unit, 95% CI −2.49 to −2.33) and care in a university hospital (β = −1.92, 95% CI −2.09 to −1.75) were independently associated with lower mean values. Sex, Elixhauser score and localised potential accessibility were not significantly associated with the outcome after multivariate adjustment. As a sensitivity analysis, we conducted an alternative logistic regression model using a dichotomised version of the outcome (See online supplemental table S4). The direction and significance of the associations were consistent with the results of the primary linear regression model.

Discussion

The multivariate analysis highlights that statistical significance alone does not imply epidemiological relevance. Several variables reached statistical significance due to the large sample size but were associated with effect sizes close to one, indicating limited practical impact. In contrast, social deprivation and healthcare supply variables showed moderate to large effect sizes, suggesting that structural and contextual factors play a more important role in explaining high incidence than individual demographic characteristics. The association observed across social deprivation quintiles supports the hypothesis that socioeconomic context is a major determinant of disease burden, potentially through delayed access to care, differences in health-seeking behaviour or unequal distribution of healthcare resources. Likewise, the inverse association with specialist density suggests that availability of specialised care may mitigate the risk of high incidence at the population level. Our analysis is the first to comprehensively analyse the frequency and regional distribution of patients who were admitted to the hospital because of acute vertigo, VDB, using the nationwide data on 67 million beneficiaries of the French healthcare system. The incidence of inpatient hospital stays because of VDB varied considerably between regions and levels of regional deprivation and increased over time, except for the pandemic years 2020–2022.

The main strength of this study is the comprehensive view into healthcare utilisation using data from the French national hospital discharge database. Our study found a cumulative annual incidence of inpatient hospital stays for VDB of approximately 0.1% of the beneficiary population. This is in line with a study from the German federal state of Thuringia, which analysed data from all hospitals of Thuringia.19 Given that at least 500 000 persons, ie, 10% of the French population, receive specific medication for VDB each year,20 this yields a hospitalisation incidence of 1% per year among persons with VDB who consult medical care. A study from the United States reported an annual incidence of hospitalisation of 4–6% for persons with the most frequent vestibular disorders; however, they did not differentiate if vertigo was the primary diagnosis for hospitalisation.21 In contrast, the present analysis was based on an explicit VDB diagnosis at discharge, supplemented with the presence of specific procedures for the ascertainment of vestibular vertigo. In this context, it is important to note that the analysis of routine claims data is the view from the healthcare provider perspective, ie, it yields the incidence of VDB that was severe and worrying enough to elicit a consultation, referral and admission to the hospital. We can therefore infer that the present findings are very cautious and conservative estimates. As shown by cohort studies of the general population,2 the true incidence of VDB is to be expected to be considerably higher. The reduction in cases during the pandemic years is plausible and has also been noted for other pathologies.22

Unsurprisingly, the most frequent vestibular diagnoses leading to hospitalisation in our study were those that are also most frequently encountered in ambulatory practice, namely peripheral vertigo, vestibular migraine and benign paroxysmal vertigo.23 24 Likewise, a representative study from Germany found that 85% of inpatients initially admitted for acute VDB were ultimately diagnosed with peripheral or central vestibular disease.19 Neither demographic characteristics nor the number of comorbidities in the present study indicated that hospitalised cases were multimorbid or severely affected enough to justify admission to inpatient care. Also, the high frequency of unspecific dizziness and unspecified vestibular disorders as primary diagnoses points at a lack of targeted management of VDB.

In addition to the spectrum of diagnoses that could have been managed in outpatient care, our study found a considerable variation in the regional distribution of hospital admission. These factors are similar to the conclusions of previous analyses which found wide regional variations of care, although these variations were not correlated with medical needs.25 We found that higher density of GPs and lower density of specialists were associated with higher hospitalisation rates, which is in contrast to the results for cardiovascular disease and diabetes from a Swiss study26 and from a German study27 respectively, that both found lower rates of avoidable hospitalisation when GP density was higher. Both the Swiss and the German studies, however, also showed inconsistencies of avoidable hospitalisations across diagnoses, with older age being the main indicator for avoidable hospitalisations in Germany. It can be argued that, although VDB is a generally benign and manageable condition, it seems acutely threatening and may elicit immediate transfer to specialist care. If dedicated neuro-otologist care is not available, admission to emergency services might be the only option for a GP who does not have the means to initiate conclusive diagnostic measures. This indicates the urgent need for training and interdisciplinary communication between GP and specialist care.

Likewise, we found variation by regional socio-economic deprivation, with more hospitalisations than expected in regions with higher deprivation. Socio-economic disparities in otorhinolaryngology have previously been noted,9 with a lack of transport as a reason for lack of access to healthcare facilities.28 Yet, individuals with higher income tend to have a reduced risk of vestibular vertigo.28 Healthcare decisions and referral practices were shown to depend on social status and region in otorhinolaryngology in the United States.29 Likewise, in Germany, referral practices for adults with VDB have been shown to be inconsistent and to depend on regional factors.30 Similar regional inconsistencies have been found, eg, for procedures of interventional cardiology in Germany.31

This present study found that the cost for hospital inpatient stays for vertigo was 65 million Euros in 2022 with average cost per case ranging between 1237 and 1394 Euros. This is 0.05% of the total healthcare expenditures in 2022 and well below the average hospital expenditure per capita of 1714 Euro in France in 2022. Differences for other countries where more elevated cost estimates have been reported, eg, in the United States,21 may be attributable to differing approaches to healthcare spending and costs of procedures. Nevertheless, the costs presented in our study are worrying, because there is no clear rationale behind an inpatient treatment of vestibular disease, pointing at cost-saving potentials.

As in all studies based on routine data, the results must be interpreted in light of the limitations of the database. First, hospitals' code diagnoses are recorded for reimbursement purposes, not primarily as a research tool. This includes the risk for coding errors. However, with the creation of the French Health Data Hub as a public interest group in 2019, the public stakeholders involved in the national data platform explicitly include all major national research institutions with a vested interest in making data available and useable for research. This also encompasses a detailed quality control of hospital data by the national hospital agency. Second, the authors expect that the true incidence of vertigo in the population is underestimated because not all cases of vertigo will be adequately diagnosed or treated. Such a conservative approach included not only diagnoses but also typical diagnostic and therapeutic procedures, biasing the estimates of incidence towards the null. As mentioned above, this will result in a more conservative estimate of the total incidence and overall costs of vertigo but may overestimate the individual costs of vertigo, because only the more severe cases will undergo a complete diagnostic workup. Also, the authors expect this effect to be more prominent in older adults, where many cases of vertigo may be attributed to the normal course of ageing. However, we are confident that we included only a small minority of cases where vertigo was not the final primary reason for hospital admission, ie, vertigo as part of a disease classified elsewhere was noted in 0.3% of all cases.

Conclusion

Our results indicate considerable regional variations in hospital admissions for vestibular disease in France. We hypothesise that awareness of these diagnoses has to be raised and that training is needed to optimise management.
