Liquid–liquid phase separation by caged coacervating peptides
Authors: Akinari Bando, Mizuki Kitamatsu, Yuuki Kanazaki, Rika Tojo, Kazunori Watanabe, Takashi Ohtsuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLiquid–liquid phase separation is an important biomolecular process in the formation of membraneless intracellular organelles that has inspired the development of artificial droplet systems. We developed caged coacervating peptides (CCPs) based on a histidine-rich squid beak protein sequence. The peptides were caged with a photodeprotectable (7-diethylaminocoumarin-4-yl)methoxycarbonyl group. The CCPs formed coacervates in the caged state and were partially dispersed upon blue-light irradiation. Photo-uncaging occurred rapidly, inducing coacervate dispersion. A mutant CCP with reduced π–π interactions exhibited efficient photo-dependent disassembly and enabled the encapsulation and release of a fluorescently labeled adenosine 5′-triphosphate (Bodipy-ATP) upon irradiation. These CCPs offer an efficient light-controlled approach for biomolecular encapsulation within coacervates and targeted drug delivery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40774-2.Keywords: Caged coacervating peptide, Liquid–liquid phase separation, LightSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biophysics, Biotechnology, Chemistry

Introduction

Biomolecules, including proteins and nucleic acids, can form droplets through liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS)1–3. These intracellular droplets are known as membraneless organelles such as nucleoli and stress granules3–6. LLPS is instrumental in many biological processes and is increasingly associated with disease pathogenesis. For example, LLPS promotes the aggregation of the amyloid-β peptide Aβ40, which is associated with Alzheimer’s disease7. α-Synuclein aggregation, which has been implicated in Parkinson’s disease, further undergoes nucleation through LLPS8. In co-aggregation involving amyloid-β and α-synuclein, amyloid-β modulates the phase separation and aggregation of α-synuclein9. To understand the biological processes associated with membraneless organelles, it is essential to assess how biological droplets form and function. In this regard, artificial LLPS using synthetic biomolecules serves as useful research tools. Indeed, LLPS droplets (coacervates) have been successfully constructed using synthetic biomolecules, including peptides and nucleic acids10,11.

Certain peptides form coacervates through LLPS. Peptides that can form single-component coacervates include elastin-like peptides (ELPs)12–15, Humboldt squid histidine-rich beak protein-derived peptides (HBPs)16,17, and short diphenylalanine-based peptides18–20. HBPs can encapsulate biomolecules, such as proteins and nucleic acids, and deliver them into cells through an endocytic pathway17. Short diphenylalanine-based peptides form coacervates that can take up various molecules, including nucleic acids and organic dyes20. ELPs exhibit an inverse phase transition behavior with a lower critical solution temperature21. Above the transition temperature, they form coacervates primarily through hydrophobic interactions. The ability to control the formation and dissociation of peptide coacervates by temperature and pH is advantageous for biotechnological applications22–24. However, in applications using living cells, large changes in the temperature or pH can hinder cell survival and growth. Thus, the use of more noninvasive external stimuli, such as light, is preferable.

This study investigated light-dependent methods for controlling the formation of peptide coacervates. Specifically, we developed HBP-based caged coacervating peptides (CCPs) using (7-diethylaminocoumarin-4-yl)methoxycarbonyl (DEACM). DEACM-caged compounds are generally uncaged rapidly by blue light at wavelengths of 400–430 nm25–27. We designed CCPs that form coacervates but avoid coacervation after photo-uncaging, as follows. The π−π interactions of Tyr residues and hydrophobic interactions of hydrophobic residues are key in stabilizing the coacervate composed of HBP (GHGVY GHGVY GHGPY GHGPY GHGLYW)16. To prevent coacervate formation in the uncaged state, a Lys residue was inserted into the middle of the original HBP sequence, and a nearby Tyr residue was replaced with Gly (GHGVY GHGVY GHGPG K GHGPY GHGLYW). We hypothesized that Lys insertion-induced electrostatic repulsion and Tyr substitution-induced reductions in π–π interactions may inhibit coacervate formation. The inserted Lys residue was protected using the DEACM group, which may restore the coacervate-forming ability of CCP by compensating for the reduced π–π interactions and inhibiting Lys–Lys electrostatic repulsion. The CCP design is illustrated in Fig. 1. We confirmed that the CCP forms coacervates through LLPS and assessed their photoinduced dispersion ability. Finally, we loaded a fluorescently labeled adenosine triphosphate (ATP) onto CCP coacervates as a model for low-molecular-weight molecule or drug delivery.

Materials and methods

Synthesis of CCPs

The peptides were prepared using conventional 9-fluorenylmethoxycarbonyl (Fmoc) solid-phase peptide synthesis. Fmoc-NH-SAL-PEG resin (14 µmol scale; Watanabe Chemicals, Hiroshima, Japan) was used as a solid support. All synthesis steps were performed at room temperature. For Fmoc deprotection, the resin was treated with 20% piperidine (Watanabe Chemicals) in N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF; Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) for 7 min and washed five times with DMF. For the coupling step, the resin was treated with each Fmoc-derivatized amino acid and O-(benzotriazol-1-yl)-N, N,N′,N′-tetramethyluronium hexafluorophosphate/N-methylmorpholine (Watanabe Chemicals) in DMF for 40 min and washed five times with DMF. No capping step was performed. By repeating these deprotection and coupling steps, peptides with the desired sequences were elongated on the resin. The Lys residues to be protected with DEACM were first incorporated into Fmoc-Lys(Dde)-OH (BLD Pharm, Shanghai, China). Boc-Gly-OH (Watanabe Chemicals) instead of Fmoc-Gly-OH (Watanabe Chemicals) was used as the last amino acid. After the last coupling step, the resin was treated twice with 4% hydrazine (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp., Osaka, Japan) in DMF for 10 min and washed five times with DMF to deprotect the Dde group. The resin was treated with 7-diethylamino-4-hydroxymethylcoumarin (Tokyo Chemical Industry), 4-dimethylaminopyridine (Tokyo Chemical Industry), 4-nitrophenyl chloroformate (Tokyo Chemical Industry), and N, N-diisopropylethylamine (Watanabe Chemicals) in DMF for 1 day and washed five times with DMF. The resin was washed with DCM (Watanabe Chemicals) and treated with a mixture of 95% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA; Watanabe Chemicals), 2.5% water, and 2.5% triisopropylsilane (Watanabe Chemicals) for 1.5 h to deprotect and cleave crude peptides. The crude peptide was precipitated and washed with diethyl ether (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corp.) until neutralizing the pH. The peptides were analyzed and purified using reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) on C18 columns (Cadenza CD-C18 and 5CD-C18; Imtakt, Kyoto, Japan). Purified peptides were identified using AXIMA Confidence MALDI-TOFMS (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) (Figs. S1–S3) and RP-HPLC (Figs. S4–S6). The sequences of these peptides are as follows: CCP (H- GHGVY GHGVY GHGPG [K-DEACM] GHGPY GHGLYW -NH2), CCPG2 (H- GHGVY GHGVG GHGPG [K-DEACM] GHGPG GHGLYW -NH2), and non-caged CCP (H- GHGVY GHGVY GHGPG K GHGPY GHGLYW -NH2).

Coacervate formation

For coacervate formation, CCP or non-caged CCP was first dissolved in 10 mM acetic acid and mixed with a buffer containing sodium acetate (pH 5), sodium phosphate (pH 6–8), sodium borate (pH 9–10), and NaCl. The mixture (25 µL) contained 50 mM sodium acetate, sodium phosphate or sodium borate, and the concentrations of NaCl and CCP are indicated in the figures. The mixture was vortexed for 2 s to induce coacervation. The coacervate suspension was imaged using an IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The sizes of the caged coacervates were measured using dynamic light scattering (Zetasizer Pro; Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK).

Measurement of CCP proportion included in coacervates

The coacervate suspension was centrifuged (15,000 × g, 25 °C, 10 min). The precipitated coacervates were mixed with a solution containing 60% of each buffer and 40% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). The supernatant (23 µL) was mixed with 37 µL of each buffer and 40 µL of DMSO (final concentration 40%) to ensure that the solvent composition matched that of the solution dissolving the precipitate. The fluorescence intensities of the supernatant and redissolved precipitate were measured using a Spectro fluorophotometer RF-6000 (Shimadzu) to determine the CCP contents outside and inside the coacervates. The fluorescence intensities of CCP and non-caged CCP were measured at excitation wavelengths of 390 and 280 nm and emission wavelengths of 486 and 337 nm, respectively.

Photo-uncaging of CCP in coacervate

The coacervate suspension (25 µL) was photo-irradiated at around 400 nm and 100 mW/cm2 using a mercury–xenon lamp (SUNCURE 202LS, Omiya Corp., Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a long-pass filter (l > 345 nm) at room temperature. For HPLC analysis, the coacervate suspension after irradiation was mixed with 35 µL of 0.1% TFA and 40 µL of DMSO to dissolve the coacervates. The solution was analyzed using a Chromaster HPLC instrument (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with a C18 column (Symphonia C18, 4.6 × 150 mm; JASCO, Tokyo, Japan).

Use of 1,6-hexanediol

To assess whether the CCP coacervates originate from LLPS, the LLPS inhibitor 1,6-hexanediol was used. Coacervates were formed as described, after which 1,6-hexanediol was added to the coacervate suspension and mixed gently. The cell suspension was imaged using the IX71 fluorescence microscope (Olympus).

Results and discussion

Coacervate formation with CCPs and non-caged CCPs

DEACM-caged coacervating peptide (CCP) formed droplets when dissolved in a neutral buffer, whereas the non-caged CCP, which differs from CCP only in that it lacks the DEACM group, did not form coacervates (Fig. 2a). The average size of the caged coacervates was 715 ± 15 nm. Dynamic light scattering analysis (Fig. 2b) showed a small peak, indicating the presence of particles around 5000 nm (5 μm) and a large peak near 700 nm. Although CCP particles < 1 μm were observed (Fig. 2a), larger particles of around 5 μm were more prominent. This is likely because these microscopic images were captured at the bottom of the 384-well plate, where larger particles tended to precipitate more readily and may fuse as reported by other studies28,29. Imaging the coacervate suspension in the middle layer rather than the bottom layer revealed particles consistent in size with those observed in DLS experiments (Fig. S7).

Coacervation of CCPs and non-caged CCPs. (a) Bright field microscopic images of CCPs and non-caged CCPs. (b) Particle size distribution of CCP coacervate based on dynamic light scattering. Coacervates are formed in solution with 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 300 µM CCP.

Coacervate formation under various solution conditions

Coacervate suspensions prepared under various conditions were centrifuged to precipitate the coacervates, and the fluorescence intensities of the supernatant and redissolved precipitate were measured (Fig. 3a). Figure 4 presents the Trp fluorescence spectra of CCP and non-caged CCP. The Trp fluorescence intensity of the particle suspension (Fig. 4a) was significantly lower than that of the dissolved peptides. Accordingly, we dissolved the coacervates (precipitates) in 40% DMSO solution before measuring fluorescence. In CCPs, Trp fluorescence at around 337 nm was further attenuated due to fluorescence resonance energy transfer from Trp to the DEACM group; consequently, DEACM fluorescence was used to quantify CCP.

When dissolved in buffer at pH 7, CCP formed droplets, and upon centrifugation, over 97% was contained in the precipitate (leaving < 3% in the supernatant; Fig. 3b), indicating coacervation of over 97% of the CCPs. We assessed coacervate formation by varying the NaCl concentration, pH, and CCP concentration. CCP formed coacervates efficiently under all conditions except at pH 4–5 (Fig. 3b–d,h, and S8). In contrast, non-caged CCP barely formed coacervates under all conditions, except at pH 8–10 (Fig. 3e–g,i, and S8), indicating that the presence or absence of the DEACM cage determines coacervate formation. The high values of the non-caged CCP at pH 8–10 are likely due to the suppression of electrostatic repulsion, as the peptide charge approaches zero at pH 8–10. Although NaCl weakens the electrostatic interactions between the peptides, concentrations of 0–1000 mM had no significant effect on CCP coacervate formation (Fig. 3b). This indicates that electrostatic interactions are not the primary cause of coacervate formation. pH affected CCP coacervate formation, with minimal coacervates formed at pH 5.0 (Fig. 3c). Considering that the pKa of His is approximately 6, the His side chain is likely protonated at pH 5.0. Therefore, deprotonation of the His side chain is necessary for coacervate formation (Fig. 3c). CCP concentration (75–600 µM) did not affect the coacervate formation efficiency (Fig. 3d,h). Based on these results, we used 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 300 µM CCP for subsequent coacervate formation experiments.

Photo-deprotection of CCP

We assessed the photo-uncaging of CCP in the coacervates. The coacervate suspension was photo-irradiated and dissolved with 35 µL of 0.1% TFA and 40 µL of DMSO. CCP deprotection was monitored at 280 nm using HPLC (Fig. S9). Figure 5 shows the amount of remaining (non-deprotected) CCP plotted versus the light dose, with the following exponential fitting: CCP = 82.6 × exp (− 0.351 × light dose). CCP was deprotected in a photo-dependent manner, with a half-life of 20 s at 100 mW/cm2. This corresponds to a light dose of 2.0 J/cm².

Photo-uncaging of DEACM-CCP. The peptide was analyzed using HPLC, and the percentage of remaining CCP without uncaging is plotted against the photoirradiation time. The light doses 1 J/cm2 corresponded to irradiation times of 10 s. Data represent the means ± S.D. of three independent experiments.

Confirming that the caged coacervates are LLPS droplets

1,6-Hexanediol is often used to dissolve protein/peptide condensates in vitro and in cells, illustrating the reversible nature of phase separations2,30. The coacervates disappeared when mixed with 1,6-hexanediol (Fig. 6). In contrast, the negative control—2,5-hexanediol—had no significant effect. These results indicate that CCP coacervates are formed through LLPS. 1,6-Hexanediol is known to disrupt hydrophobic interactions31,32. The dissolution of the coacervates upon addition of 1,6-hexanediol suggests that hydrophobic interactions may be one of the driving forces for coacervate formation.

Bright field and DEACM fluorescence images of CCP coacervates in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) containing 500 mM NaCl with/without 10% 1,6- or 2,5-hexanediol.

Photo-dependent reduction of caged coacervates

We assessed the effect of light exposure on the coacervates formed in 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 500 mM NaCl. Immediately after mixing, the coacervate suspension was irradiated at around 400 nm with a light dose of 10 J/cm2 and centrifuged. Photoirradiation of the CCP coacervates reduced DEACM and peptide levels in the precipitate by 25% and 35%, respectively (Fig. 7a). This indicates that 25–35% of the coacervates disappeared in a photo-dependent manner and that the DEACM cage group persisted more readily than the CCP peptide moiety within the coacervates. Almost all CCPs were considered uncaged after irradiation at 10 J/cm2 (Fig. 5). Because non-caged CCPs did not form coacervates (Fig. 2a), it was surprising that not all the coacervates disappeared, and the peptide/DEACM precipitant remained after irradiation (Fig. 7a). This may reflect an increase in intramolecular interactions within the coacervates, compensating for the lost interactions when uncaged. It may also be due to the aggregation of hydrophobic DEACM groups generated from photoirradiated coacervates containing locally high concentrations of DEACM.

Light-induced reduction of coacervates. (a) CCP, (b) CCPG2. Percentages of CCP, cage (DEACM), and peptides in the precipitate and supernatant were measured after centrifugation. CCP and cage were measured by DEACM fluorescence, and the peptide was measured by Trp fluorescence. Data represent the means ± S.D. of three independent experiments.

To improve the photo-mediated dispersion of CCP coacervates, we designed a CCP mutant (CCPG2) replacing two Tyr residues with Gly to reduce π–π interactions. Coacervate formation was also tested under various solution conditions. The average size of CCPG2 coacervates (569 ± 41 nm) was slightly smaller than that of CCP coacervates (Fig. 8). CCPG2 exhibited a lower coacervate formation efficiency than CCP under all conditions tested (Fig. 9). However, the dependences of coacervate formation on NaCl concentration, pH, and peptide concentration were similar for CCPG2 and CCP. CCPG2 was also rapidly deprotected in a photo-dependent manner (Fig. S10).

We hypothesized that weakening the CCP–CCP interactions can promote photo-dependent dispersion, which was experimentally confirmed (Fig. 7b). Photoirradiation of the CCPG2 coacervates induced a 67% and 93% reduction in the DEACM cages and peptides in the precipitate, respectively. Before light irradiation, nearly 100% (97.5%) of CCP formed coacervates, whereas only 86.4% of CCPG2 formed coacervates. This difference seems to be due to the Tyr-to-Gly mutation weakening the interactions between CCPG2 molecules compared to those between CCP molecules by reducing π–π interactions between Tyr residues.

ATP encapsulation in coacervates and its photo-dependent release

We loaded the coacervates with a small cargo of labeled ATP using CCPG2, which is better suited for photo-dependent disassembly than CCP. Coacervates were formed in solution with 50 mM sodium phosphate (pH 7.0), 500 mM NaCl, and 100 µM Bodipy FL Adenosine 5′-Triphosphate (Bodipy-ATP; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The coacervate suspension was irradiated at around 400 nm with a light dose of 10 J/cm2 and centrifuged. In CCPG2 coacervates, 33% of Bodipy-ATP was taken up (Fig. 10a). When the amount of Bodipy-ATP within the coacervate (light[-]) was set to 100%, 69% was released in a photo-dependent manner. The photo-dependent dissociation of CCPG2 in the presence of Bodipy-ATP (Fig. 10b) was similar to that observed in its absence (Fig. 7b).

Encapsulation and photo-dependent release of Bodipy-ATP by CCPG2. (a) Percentages of Bodipy-ATP in precipitate and supernatant measured after centrifugation. (b) Percentages of CCPG2, cage (DEACM), and peptides in the precipitate and supernatant measured after centrifugation. Data represent the means ± S.D. of three independent experiments.

Conclusion

We developed HBP-based CCPs (CCP and CCPG2). These CCPs were designed based on HBP and caged with the DEACM group. The peptides were deprotected rapidly in a blue light-dependent manner. These peptides formed LLPS-based coacervates that partially dispersed upon light exposure. CCP exhibited low levels of photoinduced dispersion, whereas CCPG2 exhibited efficient photoinduced dispersion likely because the Tyr-to-Gly substitution weakens π–π interactions. The CCPG2 droplets can encapsulate labeled ATP and partially release it upon light exposure. OptoDroplet33 and other optogenetic droplet systems34 are well-known examples of light-responsive LLPS droplets. However, unlike our peptide-based approach, these systems are intended for intracellular use and require genetic recombination. Peptide coacervate systems can be applied to droplet-based reactions35 and intracellular drug delivery17. Our CCPs were confirmed to be non-toxic in cell culture (Fig. S11), making them suitable for use in cells and laboratory organisms. However, the particle sizes of CCP and CCPG2 coacervates were unstable and increased over time in the buffer and culture media (Fig. 11), likely due to fusion of the coacervate particles. Therefore, future studies should consider surface modifications to stabilize coacervate particles for use in cell-based applications. For example, modification of coacervating peptides with polyethylene glycol has been reported to improve particle size stability.29 Because LLPS droplet systems can be applied to droplet-based reactions35,36, our photo-responsive droplet system may also be applicable to droplet-based reactions that can be terminated by light. For photo-responsive drug delivery, future studies should focus on developing CCP variants with enhanced photo-induced dispersion, as well as targeted delivery approaches, such as fusion with targeting peptides (for example, tumor-homing peptides37.
