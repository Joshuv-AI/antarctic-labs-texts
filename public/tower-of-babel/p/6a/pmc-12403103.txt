Loss of sialic acid side-chain O-acetylation exacerbates colitis
Authors: Yang Ji, Shubham Parashar, Yukiko Miyamoto, Saurabh Srivastava, Amanda L. Lewis, Nissi Varki, Ajit Varki, Lars Eckmann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThis study uncovers the essential role of the enzyme CASD1 in maintaining intestinal health by modifying sialic acids, sugars found on the surface of the cells that line the colon. CASD1 adds O-acetyl groups to sialic acids, which prevents bacterial enzymes from degrading these sugars, thereby limiting their bacterial release and exploitation. Mice lacking CASD1 show increased vulnerability to colonic inflammation. The results provide insights into the molecular mechanisms that modulate host–microbe interactions and provide protection against intestinal injury and inflammation, offering potential therapeutic targets for inflammatory bowel disease.

Sialic acids (Sias) are nine-carbon backbone keto-aldononulosonic acids prominently located at the terminal end of glycans on the cell surface and secreted products. They are crucial components of glycoproteins and glycolipids, contributing to a variety of physiological and pathological processes. Over 50 structurally unique Sias occur in nature as a result of various covalent modifications. One of the most frequent Sia modifications is the addition of one or more O-acetyl esters to the hydroxyl groups at the C7/C8/C9 side-chain of Sias (1). O-acetylation makes Sias more hydrophobic and less susceptible to enzymatic release by sialidases, and modulates recognition by various pathogens and toxins (2), but specific physiologic functions of Sia O-acetylation in different tissues remain poorly understood.

Based on differential tissue and cell type expression patterns and linkage-specific occurrences, we and others have suggested that Sia O-acetylation is carried out by a family of O-acetyltransferases (3, 4). Cas domain-containing 1 (CASD1), the first such enzyme discovered in mammalian cells, can introduce O-acetyl modifications at the C7, C8, and/or C9 sites of nucleotide-activated Sias (5, 6). CASD1, using the donor acetyl coenzyme A, adds O-acetyl groups to the C7 position, which can then migrate to the C8 and C9 positions in a dynamic equilibrium under physiological conditions (5, 7–12). Upon migration, an additional O-acetyl group can be added to the C7 position to generate a C7(8),9-di-O-acetylated form of Sia, or even C7,8,9-tri-O-acetylated species. An enzyme, “migrase,” postulated to assist the transfer of O-acetyl group from C7 to C9 has so far not been characterized (5, 8, 9, 11). Likewise, Sia side-chain O-acetyltransferases other than CASD1 have yet to be discovered. The physiological importance of such Sia side chain modification is also suggested by the existence of specific C9-O-acetylesterases (13), but their physiologic roles have not been systematically explored.

Marked variations in the expression of C7,9-di-O-acetylated and C9-O-acetylated Sias have been reported in different animal species and in different tissues. For example, levels of C9-O-acetylated Sias are particularly high in the surface mucin of the colonic mucosa in humans and rodents (14, 15). This modification may be developmentally regulated since its levels are low prenatally but increase significantly after birth in rats and in humans (16). However, levels of C9-O-acetylation in the colon can vary significantly even in adults (10, 16–19), raising questions about its physiologic regulation and significance. Intestinal mucins form a protective mucus layer that limits direct contact between luminal microbes and the mucosal epithelium. Genetic deficiency of the protein backbones of intestinal mucins can lead to disruption of the mucous barrier and to mucosal inflammation (20–22). However, the physiological impact of subtle mucin variations such as Sia O-acetylation rather than comprehensive loss of key mucin components remains unknown.

Additional complexity in investigating the functions of specific Sia O-acetylation arises from the technical challenges associated with the analysis of specific modifications. Earlier chemical and histochemical approaches evaluated O-acetylation by indirect methods and suffered from a lack of specificity for unique Sia O-modifications (23–26). Advanced HPLC methods have overcome this limitation (12) but do not have spatial resolution in the examination of tissue samples and minimal loss and/or migration of O-acetyl group can still occur during acid release and purification. Recent progress in the development of an array of highly specific sialoglycan-recognizing probes (SGRP) for Sia O-acetylation on different carbon positions has now made it possible to address key questions about sialoglycan modifications with unprecedented resolution (26). In the present study, we employed these probes to determine the spatial expression patterns of Sia O-modifications in the intestinal tract under physiologic conditions and in inflammation. We show the importance of CASD1 in mediating Sia O-acetylation in the intestine and other organs and its role in modulating host–microbe interactions and susceptibility to intestinal inflammation.

Results

Sia C7/C8/C9-O-Acetylation Is Most Abundant in the Colon.

To explore potential functions of O-acetylated Sias, we determined their abundance in various tissues of adult C57BL/6 mice using HPLC analysis of mild acid-released DMB-derivatized Sias (5, 12). The highest levels of O-acetylated Sias were observed in the colon. By comparison, the testis, heart, brain, and spleen had 2- to 5-fold lower levels of Sia O-acetylation, while the other digestive organs (small intestine, stomach, pancreas, liver, salivary glands) had >10-fold lower levels compared to the colon (Fig. 1A). In the colon, C7-O and C9-O-acetyl modification of N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) were the most common, while C8-O-modification was less abundant (Fig. 1B). C9-O-acetyl modification of N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc) was not observed in the colon (Fig. 1B), but was found at low levels in several other organs, including the liver, adrenals, heart, and lungs, as well as in serum (SI Appendix, Table S1).

To confirm and expand the DMB-HPLC findings, we performed histochemical analysis of mouse colon sections with specific SGRP probes. The SGRP9 probe, which detects C9-O-acetyl Sias (26), bound primarily to epithelial cells in the proximal and mid crypt regions (Fig. 1C), as well as the mucus layers overlying the surface epithelium (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), but only minimally in the lamina propria. The SGRP7 probe, which binds to C7/C9-O-diacetylated Sias (26), stained epithelial cells in the mid crypt region and to a lesser extent in the proximal crypts (Fig. 1C). Matching nonbinding control probes for SGRP7 and SGRP9, which have point mutations in the critical Sia-binding domains of the respective lectins, showed no specific binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Furthermore, the SGRP3 probe, which detects the underlying α2-3 linkage of Sia to its adjacent glycan (26), highlighted multiple cells in the lamina propria but not in the epithelium (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2), underlining the epithelial selectivity of the observed C7/C9-O-acetyl modifications of Sias in the colon.

Importance of CASD1 for Sia C7/C9-O-Acetylation in the Colon and Other Organs.

Indirect evidence had predicted the existence of multiple Sia O-acetyltransferases with preferences for Sias in different linkages to the underlying glycan chains and/or classes of glycoconjugates (5, 11, 27–30), yet to date only a single enzyme, CASD1, has been identified with the requisite activity and no genomic homologs of that enzyme have been found in mice or humans (9). Deficiency in CASD1 has been reported to cause a loss of C9-O-acetylation of Sias on the surface of hematopoietic cells (6), but it has remained unclear whether CASD1 or other potential O-acetyltransferases are required for Sia C9-O-acetylation in other cells and tissues.

To determine the importance of CASD1 in the biosynthesis of acetylated Sias in different organ systems, we examined their abundance in the intestinal tract and multiple other organs of CASD1-deficient mice by DMB-HPLC and SGRP histochemistry. Loss of CASD1 led to >98% loss of C9-O-acetyl, C7-O-acetyl, and C7/C8/C9-tri-O-acetyl Sias (Neu5Ac) in the colon compared to WT mice (Fig. 1B and SI Appendix, Table S1) and loss of SGRP9 staining (Fig. 1C). Similarly, CASD1 deficiency led to a >80% reduction of C9-O-acetyl and C7/C8/C9-tri-O-acetyl Neu5Ac in most other organs tested, including small intestine, liver, pancreas, lungs, kidney, bladder, eye, and testis (SI Appendix, Table S1). By comparison, the decrease in Sia O-acetylation with CASD1 deficiency was less pronounced in stomach and salivary glands, and no reduction was seen at all in the prostate, although all three organs had much lower overall levels of O-Ac-Sias in WT mice (<2% of total Sia, compared to ~25% in the colon, SI Appendix, Table S1). The absence of CASD1 had no effect on the levels of α2-3 linked Sias in the colon (Fig. 1C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2), indicating that CASD1 deficiency did not alter the overall abundance of Sias in the colon. CASD1 deficiency also decreased, by >80%, the levels of 9-O-acetylated Neu5Gc in the organs with significant expression (SI Appendix, Table S1). These results demonstrate that CASD1 is the primary, if not only, physiologically relevant O-acetyltransferase responsible for C7/C9-O-acetylation of Sias (Neu5Ac) in the colon, the site of the most abundant O-acetylation in the body, as well as of both Neu5Ac and Neu5Gc in most other organs.

Despite the high levels of C7/C9-O-acetylated Sias in the colon and the requirement of CASD1 for C7/C9-O-acetylation of Sias, analysis of public gene expression databases indicated that CASD1 was widely expressed in different organs of mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) and humans (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Single-cell gene expression analysis confirmed CASD1 expression in colon epithelial cells, but also showed marked expression in neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, and microglial cells in the brain, as well as lower expression in T cells, B cells, and macrophages in bone marrow, spleen, and thymus, and in endothelial cells and fibroblasts of a number of organs (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).These data suggest that the relatively high tissue specificity of C7/C9-O-acetylated Sia expression cannot be solely explained by differential CASD1 expression.

Colonic Sia O-Acetylation Is Independent of Commensal Microbiota.

Biosynthesis of O-acetylated Sias has been postulated to be dynamically regulated by developmental and environmental factors (16). To explore such potential factors, we particularly focused on the impact of the microbiota on expression of the most abundant O-acetylated Sias in the colon since epithelial cells and their mucins are the primary interface and barrier between host and intestinal microbes. Using a gnotobiotic murine model, we found that the abundance of total Sias and of all O-acetylated Sias was similar in the colon of germfree compared to conventional adult mice as determined by DMB-HPLC (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the levels of C7-, C8- and C9-O-acetylated and C7(8), 9-di-O-acetylated Sias were not significantly different in the colon of germfree compared to conventional mice, albeit a slight shift with more C7-O- and less C9-O-acetylated Sias was observed (Fig. 2A). Histochemical analysis of colon sections with the SGRP9 and SGRP7 probes confirmed that germfree and conventional mice had similar levels and similar locations of C9-O- and C7-O-acetylated Sias along the crypt–surface axis and surface-adjacent mucus layer (Fig. 2B).

The lack of microbial impact on Sia O-acetylation was also apparent when we examined mice of different ages, ranging from newborn to 18 to 20 wk of age, a period during which the commensal microbiota of conventional mice undergoes a profound evolution in composition from practically germfree postnatally to a complex and stable adult microbiota by >4 mo. Germfree mice had similar levels of total O-acetylated Sias compared to conventional mice of similar age throughout the 18- to 20-wk observation period, although both groups displayed a modest overall increase in the first 6 wk (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Table S2). Together, these data demonstrate that O-acetylation of Sias in the murine colon is independent of the commensal microbiota, suggesting that its regulation is primarily by tissue-autonomous mechanisms.

Deficiency in Sia C7/C9-O-Acetylation Impacts the Composition and Gene Repertoire of the Intestinal Microbiota.

Although we found that intestinal Sia O-acetylation is not dependent on the intestinal microbiota, it may play a role in modulating host–microbial interactions, since the terminal Sias of intestinal mucins are in close contact with luminal microbes. Therefore, we asked whether loss of Sia O-acetylation impacts the composition or functional potential of the intestinal microbiota. Stool samples were collected from mice deficient in the O-acetyltransferase CASD1 and WT controls (cohoused in the same cages to minimize potential cage effects), and DNA was extracted and subjected to metagenomics analysis. The overall microbial composition was not significantly different between CASD1-deficient and WT mice (Fig. 3A), although several of the more abundant bacteria showed significant shifts, including four- to eightfold increases in Helicobacter ganmani, Anaerotruncus species, and Oscillibacter species, and a ~fourfold decrease in Turicimonas muris in the KO mice (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Table S3). Furthermore, analysis of the microbial gene repertoire revealed significant and marked increases in several metabolic pathways, particularly those for degradation of different carbohydrates (e.g., starch, lactose, and galactose) and synthesis/degradation of nucleotides (e.g., pyrimidine deoxynucleotides, 5-aminoimidazole ribonucleotides, and purine ribonucleotides) (Fig. 3C). Most notably, genes in the P441-PWY pathway for degradation of N-acetylneuraminate (the predominant Sia found in mammalian cells) were significantly increased in the microbiota of CASD1-deficient mice compared to controls, indicating that intestinal bacteria that possess this pathway increased in abundance in the absence of Sia O-acetylation in the colon (Fig. 3D).

Loss of Sia O-Acetylation in Intestinal Inflammation.

Sia expression has been reported in older studies to be affected by inflammatory changes in the intestinal tract (23, 24, 31–33) but the methods used at the time could not discriminate between different forms and locations of Sia O-acetylation. To gain deeper insights into the impact of inflammation on Sias in the colon, we employed a well-established mouse model of acute colitis by feeding adult mice with dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in the drinking water for 7 d followed by 2 d of regular drinking water. Levels of C7-, C8- and C9-O-acetylated and C7(8), 9-di-O-acetylated Sias, as determined by DMB-HPLC, were markedly (three- to fivefold) lower in acute colitis compared to the normal colon (Fig. 4A). In comparison, total Sia levels were only minimally (<twofold) and not significantly decreased in colitis (Fig. 4A), indicating that the loss of O-acetylated Sias was specific to the O-acetyl modifications and not explained by loss of total Sias.

For confirmation of the DMB-HPLC analyses, we performed histochemistry on colon sections using the SGRP9 probe. Acute colitis caused a reduction in C9-O-acetylated Sias in the crypts of inflamed regions and a complete loss of C9-O-acetylated Sias in ulcerated regions (Fig. 4B). The SGRP9 probe did not detect C9-O-acetylated Sias in the crypts of CASD1-deficient mice with acute colitis, indicating that CASD1 is solely responsible for 9-O-acetylation of Sias even under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 4B).

To validate the findings from the murine colitis model, we used the SGRP9 probe on sections of human colon from healthy individuals and patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD). Under healthy conditions, C9-O-acetylated Sias were exclusively seen in the crypt epithelium of the human colon, with slightly higher levels in surface epithelial cells in humans compared to mice (Fig. 4C). Importantly, IBD-associated acute mucosal inflammation was associated with a marked reduction in C9-O-acetylated Sias (Fig. 4C), further showing that inflammatory changes are associated with loss of Sia O-acetylation in the intestinal tract.

Furthermore, decrease in C9-O-acetylated Sias was accompanied by decreased expression of Casd1 in DSS-exposed mice compared to normal controls, while expression of the inflammation-associated cytokine IL-1β was markedly increased (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that reduced CASD1 expression may be partly responsible for diminished 9-O-Sia acetylation in acute colitis. In addition, loss of CASD1-expressing epithelial cells in ulcerated areas may also contribute to diminished 9-O-Ac Sia levels in colitis.

Sia O-Acetylation Protects Against Acute Colitis.

The observed changes in O-acetylated Sias in acute colitis suggested a potential role of O-acetylation in modulating colitis. To directly address this possibility, we evaluated the severity of colitis in CASD1-deficient and WT mice subjected to DSS feeding (34). Mice, both male and female, lacking CASD1 displayed significantly greater body weight loss compared to WT mice over the 9-d course of the experiment (Fig. 5A). In parallel, histological analysis revealed increased areas of ulceration with severe mucosal inflammation in DSS-treated CASD1-deficient compared to WT mice, while both groups of mice showed a morphologically normal colon in controls given only regular water (Fig. 5B). Morphometric analysis confirmed that CASD1-deficient mice, both males and females, had significantly greater total colonic ulceration compared to WT mice (Fig. 5C). To validate the histological findings with an independent approach, we quantitated the neutrophil product, myeloperoxidase, as a biomarker of acute inflammation severity. CASD1-deficient mice exhibited markedly increased levels of myeloperoxidase in the colon compared to WT mice (Fig. 5D), confirming the histological observation of increased mucosal inflammation in the absence of CASD1. Together, these results indicate that deficiency in CASD1 and the accompanying loss of Sia O-acetylation renders mice more susceptible to acute inflammation in the colon.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that CASD1 is the enzyme responsible for most if not all Sia C7/C9-O-acetylation of Neu5Ac and Neu5Gc in the colon and many other organs in the mouse. Earlier biochemical research had suggested the existence of multiple O-acetyltransferases with specificity for different Sia linkages, glycan classes, and cell types (5, 11, 27–30). For example, while O-acetylation of gangliosides (sialylated glycolipids) in cells of neuroectodermal origin (e.g., neurons and melanocytes) is found primarily on terminal α2-8-linked Sias (30, 35), terminal α2-6-linked Sias on N-glycans are selectively O-acetylated in the rat liver (10). Such observations led to the idea that multiple sialate O-acetyl-transferases exist, with varying substrate specificities. However, when the first bona fide enzyme, CASD1, was identified in mammalian cells, no obvious additional homologs were found in mammalian genomes (9), raising the possibilities that either independently evolved enzymes with little homology are involved in Sia O-acetylation and/or that indeed only one enzyme is responsible and able to add acetyl groups to Sias with different underlying linkages. Our findings support the latter notion, i.e., CASD1 may be the primary if not only physiologically relevant Sia O-acetyltransferase in the mouse. If so, additional as yet unidentified cofactors must be involved in selectively promoting the activity of CASD1 in different cell types with different degrees of C7/C9-O-acetylation of Sias in different classes and linkages of sialoglycans. There is precedent for such modifiers of glycosyltransferase specificity. For example, a classic example is α-lactalbumin interacting with the ancient enzyme β-1-4-galactosyltransferase, changing its substrate preference from N-acetylglucosamine to glucose, thus generating lactose (Galβ1-4Glc), the primary carbohydrate in milk and a defining feature of mammals (36).

The great abundance of C7/C9-O-acetylated Sias in the colon, greater than in any other organ, points to their potential physiological role in intestinal homeostasis and as an important factor in shaping a distinct biochemical landscape for host–microbial interactions. As terminal residues on intestinal mucins, Sias are particularly susceptible to release by microbial sialidases and subsequent microbial metabolism (37). Earlier in vitro reports had suggested that O-acetylation of Sias provides a protective mechanism that prevents microbial Sia utilization (37). Our results provide in vivo evidence for such a function, since in the absence of Sia O-acetylation, microbial gene abundance expanded of pathways implicated in the exploitation of Sias for microbial purposes, such as catabolism of the Sia, Neu5Ac, into metabolites (pyruvate, acetate) for ATP generation (38, 39). A subset of intestinal bacteria that can utilize Sias can potentially expand at the expense of other microbes that lack Sia-metabolizing pathways (see scheme in Fig. 3D), although our microbial studies did not have sufficient resolution to identify specific bacterial species with such properties. In addition to failing to limit direct microbial access to a critical nutrient, loss of terminal protective Sia residues on intestinal mucins could also compromise the mucus barrier (40) and increase its susceptibility to microbial insults (41), thereby also indirectly impacting host–microbial interactions.

Sia O-acetylation occurred in the colon in the absence of a normal microbiota, indicating that Sia modifications are not just a host response to microbial exposure. Together with prior observations that Sia O-acetylation is low prenatally but increases postnatally (16), our findings suggest that Sia O-acetylation is a tissue-autonomous, developmental process. As such, one might speculate that Sia O-acetylation has tissue autonomous functions, particularly in the intestinal epithelium, which is the primary site of Sia modifications. However, no gross epithelial phenotype was observed in the absence of Sia O-acetylation under physiologic conditions, although subtle abnormalities may have escaped our analysis and could occur, particularly in response to intestinal challenges and stresses that require epithelial adaptation. In any case, normal Sia modification in the absence of microbial exposure does not preclude a primary role in modulating host–microbial interactions. In fact, it might even suggest a physiological importance great enough for early developmental expression irrespective of particular microbial encounters, a situation not unlike that seen with postnatal baseline expression of antimicrobial peptides in the intestine (42).

Our studies have not addressed 4-O-acetylated Sias, which are less common than other O-acetylated Sia forms and have distinct functions from 9-O-acetylated Sias (43, 44). The currently known characteristics of 4-O-acetylated Sias include resistance to most sialidases and modulation of host interactions with viruses such as influenza A virus (45) and coronavirus (46). 4-O-acetylated Sias have been found in the mouse colon and various other mammalian tissues but its biosynthesis and functions are even less well understood. Besides resistance to most sialidases a further complication in studying this Sia is that it is converted into 4,8-anhydro forms upon release by acid hydrolysis (47). It remains to be seen whether CASD1 deficiency affects 4-O-acetylation.

Levels of O-acetylated Sias were markedly suppressed in acute colitis, as we found in mice and IBD patients, and also consistent with prior human studies (25), suggesting that O-acetylation might play a protective role against inflammation. This concept is directly supported by the observed increase in inflammation severity in CASD1-deficient mice upon experimental induction of acute colitis. A potential role of Sia O-acetylation in inflammation susceptibility is also suggested by the reported association of IBD with mutations of another enzyme involved in controlling Sia O-acetylation, sialate-O-acetylesterase (encoded by SIAE), which is most highly expressed in the colon and mediates Sia O-deacetylation (48). SIAE removes O-acetyl groups from Sias, making them more susceptible to cleavage by microbial sialidases and potentially weakening the mucus barrier (49). Excessive or dysregulated SIAE activity may therefore exacerbate local inflammation, as loss of O-acetyl protection might accelerate mucin breakdown. Some studies have reported SIAE mutations or dysfunction in IBD (48), while other analyses have not consistently confirmed these associations (50), indicating that the precise role of SIAE in IBD susceptibility remains uncertain. Other genetic or environmental factors beyond SIAE may be involved in modulating the potential impact of Sia O-acetylation on intestinal inflammation (20, 49, 51). For example, certain commensals, such as Bacteroidetes species, have Sia-specific 9-O-acetylesterases that can alter Sia acetylation status, so any differences in their abundance or expression patterns could potentially impact host functions in IBD (37).

The mechanisms by which Sia O-acetylation can protect against inflammatory insults in the intestinal tract remain to be established but alterations in host–microbial interactions are prime candidates, since increased availability of free Sias, which might be predicted as a consequence of the loss of acetylation-dependent protection against Sia cleavage, can cause dysbiosis in colitis (52) and dysbiosis can contribute to the development and severity of intestinal inflammation in experimental models and in IBD (53). Conversely, new strategies to stabilize Sia O-acetylation may hold promise for attenuating inflammation in IBD. However, work published while this study was under review raises the possibility that Sia acetylation may be detrimental under some conditions, since the new reports indicate that another mammalian enzyme, NXPE1, can directly acetylate Neu5Ac at the 9-hydroxyl position on terminal mucin glycans and genetic variants of the gene that impair the activity of this enzyme are associated with protection against ulcerative colitis (54, 55). This observation is complicated by the fact that while NXPE1 has some homology to CASD1, it is classified as a putative member of the GDSL/SGNH esterase superfamily and, within it, the PC-esterase family. In this regard, there are examples of “O-acetylases,” such as peptidoglycan O-acetyltransferase A in Staphylococcus aureus (56), that can act as either transferases or esterases. Nonetheless, our study and the recent papers (54, 55) emphasize the potential physiologic importance and complexities of Sia O-acetylations, common glycan modifications that had been largely ignored for a long time despite the fact that they were present in the first ever discovered Sias (57).

Materials and Methods

Mice.

Casd1 gene-targeted mice, originally from the European Conditional Mouse Mutagenesis Program followed by Cre-mediated excision of exon 2, were kindly provided by Dr. Shiv Pillai (6) and backcrossed to a C57BL/6 genetic background. C57BL/6 controls were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Germ-free C57BL/6NTac mice were obtained from Taconic Biosciences and bred in the gnotobiotic facility of the University of California, San Diego, as described (58). Germ-free status was tested and confirmed by plating homogenates of fecal pellets onto Luria–Bertani agar and Columbia blood agar, followed by incubation under aerobic and anaerobic conditions (58). All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the IACUC of the University of California, San Diego.

HPLC Analysis.

Organs were immersed into 400 to 800 µL of lysis buffer containing 10% (v/v) of PBS in water, pH 6.8, 1 mM PMSF, and a protease inhibitor cocktail and homogenized by ultrasonication on ice. Homogenates were centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min at 4° C and supernatants were collected, and protein was quantitated by BCA assay. Release and analysis of Sias were performed as previously reported (12). Briefly, Sias were released by treatment with 2 M acetic acid at 80 °C for 3 h and samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatants were collected, subjected to 10 kDa centrifugal filtration, and lyophilized. Dried samples were resuspended in water and base-treated by addition of an equal volume of 0.2 M NaOH and incubation at 37 °C for 30 min. After neutralization with 6 M HCl, samples were reacted with a 1,2-diamino-4,5-methylene dioxybenzene (DMB) at 4 °C for 48 h in the dark (12). Samples were analyzed by HPLC using a linear elution gradient of water:methanol:acetonitrile from 86:7:7 to 82:7:11 over a 75 min run and fluorescence detection of labeled Sias. Different Sia species were identified by relative retention times compared to Sia standards derived from the mucin of the bovine mandibular salivary gland (59).

Histological Techniques.

Resected mouse colon was opened longitudinally, rolled onto a wooden stick, embedded in OCT compound (Fisher Scientific) and snap frozen for cryosectioning using dry ice-cooled 2-methybutane. Frozen human colon samples were obtained from the Co-operative Human Tissue Network and were exempt from human subjects research. Frozen sections (5 µm) were prepared on a cryostat and deposited onto positively charged glass slides. Slides were dried for 1 h and treated for 30 min at room temperature with 0.03% hydrogen peroxide. After three washes with PBS, slides were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 2 min followed by 0.1% avidin in PBS for 15 min and 0.01% biotin in PBS for 15 min. Slides were washed with PBS and postfixed with 10% buffered formalin for 30 min. Biotinylated SGRP probes were prepared and used at 3 to 10 µg/mL as described (26). Slides were incubated for 1 h with the respective SGRP probes in 1% BSA in PBS with added calcium and magnesium. After washing with PBS, slides were incubated for 1 h with Cy3-labeled streptavidin or with HRP-streptavidin at a 1:500 dilution, washed again, and developed with 3-amino-9-ethylcarbazole as substrate and 0.03% hydrogen peroxide. Nuclei were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin. Slides were washed and coverslipped with an aqueous mounting medium, and evaluated on an Olympus BH2 microscope and digital images were captured equipped with a Magnafire digital camera.

Metagenomic Sequencing and analysis.

Fecal pellets were collected from individual mice and stored at −80 °C. Nucleic acids were extracted using the MagMAX Microbiome Ultra Nucleic Acid Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and automated KingFisher Flex robots (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previously (60). DNA was quantified using a PicoGreen fluorescence assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and metagenomic libraries were prepared with EpMotion automated liquid handlers using KAPA HyperPlus kits (Roche Diagnostics). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform with paired-end 150 bp cycles at the University of California, San Diego, Institute for Genomic Medicine. Sequencing depth for each sample averaged 12 million reads (range 6.6 to 17.1 million). Raw FASTQ reads were run through the KneadData QC pipeline. Reads were first trimmed then filtered against contaminate reference databases (mouse_C57BL_6NJ_0 and mouse_C57BL_6NJ_1). Reads were filtered sequentially with those reads passing the first filtering step used as input to the next filtering step. The remaining reads (representing 88 to 97% of the raw reads) were subjected to taxonomic classification of metagenomes using MetaPhlAn (version 3.0.7) (61). Taxonomic abundances were passed through a basic filter requiring each species or genus to have at least 0.01% abundance in at least two samples. Principle coordinate ANOVA among samples was done based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarities between species profiles. Significance between groups were analyzed by PERMANOVA. Functional profiling of metagenomic reads for species-specific and species-agnostic quantification of gene families, enzyme classification modules, and pathways was done by HUMAnN (v3.0.0) (62) using UniRef and MeraCyc databases. Abundance of complete pathways were derived from the profiling results and expressed relative to all complete pathways found in each sample. Mean abundances were calculated for sample groups and differences were assessed by the two-tailed t test.

Murine Colitis Model.

For colitis induction, mice were given 2.5 % (w/v) DSS in sterile, distilled water for 7 d followed by regular water for the remainder of the experiment. Frozen sections of the colon were prepared, fixed in 4% PFA and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for assessing inflammation severity. The size of ulcerations was measured microscopically and summed for each animal. Scoring was done in a blinded fashion. For biochemical evaluation of inflammation severity, tissue pieces (1 to 2 mm) were homogenized on ice in 1 mL PBS and centrifuged at 15,000×g at 4 °C for 10 min. Activity of myeloperoxidase was determined with a Myeloperoxidase Assay kit (Bio vision).

qPCR.

RNA was extracted from frozen mouse colon tissue using Aurum™ Total RNA Mini Kit (Bio-Rad Cat# 7326820). cDNAs were generated using iScript™ cDNA Synthesis Kit (Bio-Rad Cat# 1708890). All primers were obtained from Origene (GAPDH: MP205604, IL-1β: MP206724, CASD1: MP201765). Gene expression was determined by qPCR using PerfeCta SYBR Green Fast Mix (Quantabio # 95071-05 K) and an ABI StepOnePlus real-time PCR system. Gene expression was normalized relative to mouse GAPDH mRNA.

Statistical Analysis.

Data are presented as mean and SE or as individual data points and geometric means. Differences between groups were evaluated with Welch’s t test or Mann–Whitney test, as appropriate, using GraphPad prism, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
