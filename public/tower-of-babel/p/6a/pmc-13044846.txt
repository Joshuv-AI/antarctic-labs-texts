Silica- and Garcinia mangostana Fruit Peel Extract-Modified Superparamagnetic Particles for Efficient DNA Isolation
Authors: Peera Prachaapichai, Wanwisa Skolpap, Veerachai Thitapakorn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three types of superparamagnetic iron oxide particles (SPIOPs) were employed for the efficient isolation of salmon sperm DNA, including pristine SPIOP, silica-functionalized SPIOP (SPIOP@SiO2), and SPIOP modified with xanthones extracted from the fruit peel of Garcinia mangostana (SPIOP@XAN). The SPIOP@XAN variants were synthesized with different xanthone loadings (2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 25 g). Structural and physicochemical characterization of the synthesized particles was conducted using dynamic light scattering (DLS), vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), confirmed the crystalline nature and successful modification of the particles. Among the materials tested, SPIOP@XAN with 10 wt % xanthone (SPIOP@X10/W90) exhibited the highest salmon sperm DNA adsorption efficiency at 30 min, achieving over 93%. The adsorption of salmon sperm DNA onto SPIOP followed the Langmuir isotherm model, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 228.8 mg·g–1, and conformed to pseudo-first-order kinetics, indicating a monolayer adsorption mechanism. In contrast, SPIOP@X10/W90 exhibited the best fit to the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson isotherms, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 198.2 mg·g–1, and followed pseudo-second-order kinetics, suggesting a more complex and heterogeneous adsorption process. In similar vien, SPIOP@SiO2 was best described by the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson isotherms, showing a maximum adsorption capacity of 38.0 mg·g–1, and conformed to pseudo-first-order kinetics, implying that the adsorption was predominantly governed by physical interactions. These findings underscore the potential of SPIOP@X10/W90 for high-efficiency magnetic DNA extraction and its applicability in future DNA biosensing technologies.

Introduction

Magnetic nano- and microparticles are essential for efficient magnetic separation, typically measuring under 10 μm to facilitate precise and selective interactions with biological targets. To mitigate aggregation caused by hydrophobic and magnetic forces and to enable the attachment of specific ligandssuch as antibodies, aptamers, or deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA)their surfaces require functionalization with biocompatible inorganic or organic materials. The ability to modify particle surfaces in a controlled, scalable, and efficient manner, allows for the rapid, cost-effective, and gentle isolation of biological compounds. Among the critical size-dependent properties, superparamagnetism plays a pivotal role, enhancing the functionality of micromagnetic particles in various biomedical applications, including protein purification, targeted drug delivery, cell isolation, medical diagnosis, immunoassays, nucleic acid sequencing, and epitope analysis.

A diverse array of materials has been employed as matrices for embedding superparamagnetic nanoparticles (NPs) in microbead form. These include mesoporous silica, polysaccharides, plant-derived proteins and their derivatives, poly­(ethylene glycol), , and various synthetic polymers. Among the various large-scale synthesis techniques, coprecipitation is one of the most widely employed methods for producing Fe3O4 NPs. This approach utilizes an aqueous solution containing a mixture of Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions, offering high efficiency in nanoparticle synthesis. However, further refinement is needed to achieve better control over particle size and composition. Plant extracts, which are rich in phytochemicals such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and terpenoids, serve as natural stabilizers that not only facilitate the synthesis of Fe3O4 NPs but also enhance their colloidal stability and physicochemical properties. This makes them highly promising for applications in drug delivery, medical imaging, and diagnostics. Furthermore, the vast diversity of plant species provides access to a wide range of bioactive compounds, allowing for the customization of NPs with specific shapes, sizes, and functionalities tailored to particular applications. The global agri-food industry produces substantial amounts of food waste annually, with a significant portion consisting of biowaste derived from fruit processing. This waste, primarily composed of peels and seeds, accounts for approximately 25% to 60% of the fruit’s total weight. Notably, these byproducts contain a rich array of bioactive compounds that can be recovered and utilized in the development of health supplements or incorporated into food products to enhance their nutritional value. Garcinia mangostana L., commonly known as mangosteen or Mangji persimmon, belongs to the Garcinaceae family and is predominantly cultivated in Southeast Asian countries, including Thailand, Malaysia, and Indonesia. G. mangostana exhibits various pharmacological properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, antidiabetic, and neuroprotective effects. In the case of mangosteen rind, its phenolic compounds can be categorized into nonanthocyanin compoundsincluding xanthones (CH3H8O2), tannins, two flavonoids, one flavonol, and one flavanonoland anthocyanin compounds, such as pelargonidin-3-O-glucoside and cyanidin-3-O-glucoside. Extracts from G. mangostana fruit peel have been utilized as reducing and stabilizing agents , in the synthesis of various NPs. For instance, silver NPs biosynthesized using mangosteen peel extract have been studied for their effects on human breast cancer cell lines. Additionally, gold–silver core–shell NPs derived from G. mangostana peel have demonstrated potential against colon cancer cells, while Fe3O4 NPs stabilized by mangosteen peel extract have been investigated for hyperthermia applications. Despite significant advancements by numerous research groups, the demand for magnetic polymeric particles with uniform size, superparamagnetic properties, chemical stability, customizable magnetic content, biocompatibility, and easily modifiable surfaces continues to rise, particularly for applications in biochips and bioseparation. For instance, silica-coated Fe3O4 NPs synthesized via interface-mediated RAFT polymerization have been employed for high-throughput DNA extraction from pathogenic bacteria, while silica-coated iron oxide NPs have been utilized for quantitative molecular genetic analysis of the V617F mutation in the Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) gene.

This study hypothesizes that surface modification of superparamagnetic iron oxide particles (SPIOPs) with xanthones extracted from the fruit peel of G. mangostana can enhance their adsorption efficiency toward DNA molecules through synergistic interactions between the functional groups of xanthones and the phosphate backbone of DNA. The novelty of this work lies in the development of SPIOP@XAN as bioderived, magnetically separable nanomaterials for DNA isolation, which have not been previously reported. This approach integrates natural xanthone compounds with magnetic nanotechnology to achieve high-efficiency DNA adsorption and facile magnetic recovery.

Therefore, this study focuses on the preparation of G. mangostana peel–modified superparamagnetic particles and the comparative evaluation of their performance with bare SPIOP and polystyrene–divinylbenzene (PS-DVB) silica-coated SPIOP for the isolation of free DNA.

Preparation of Porous PS-DVB Silica Coated Superparamagnetic Microbeads

Preparation process comprises of four steps as follows: poly­(styrene-divinylbenzene) (PS-DVB) beads synthesis, sulfonation of macroporous PS-DVB beads, magnetization of sulfonated macroporous PS-DVB beads, and streptavidin separation of silica coated magnetic beads.

Preparation of Monodisperse Macroporous PS-DVB Beads

Monodisperse macroporous polystyrene-divinylbenzene (PS-DVB) beads were synthesized using a seeded polymerization technique. The initial PS seed particles were produced through dispersion polymerization. The dispersion medium consisted of ethanol and 2-methoxyethanol in a 3:2 ratio, with 1 g of PVP-40 serving as a steric stabilizer in a total volume of 90 mL. A solution of 150 mg AIBN in 15 mL of styrene (previously purified to remove inhibitors) was introduced into the dispersion medium. The mixture underwent sonication for 10 min before being subjected to dispersion polymerization in a 2.5 L stirred tank reactor (SIBATA, RF-25, Japan) at 70 °C with continuous agitation at 120 rpm for 20 h. The resulting suspension was centrifuged, and the precipitate was thoroughly washed three times with distilled water through repeated centrifugation and vortexing. The PS seeds were then washed three times with ethanol and subsequently vacuum-dried at 70 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of Monodisperse Macroporous poly­(styrene-co-DVB) Beads

A two-step seeded polymerization approach was utilized to synthesize monodisperse poly­(styrene-co-divinylbenzene) (PS-DVB) beads. A total of 700 mg of the synthesized PS seeds was dispersed in 0.7 mL of DBP within an emulsified aqueous medium (100 mL) containing 0.25% (w/w) SDS. This dispersion was continuously stirred at 400 rpm at room temperature for 20 h to allow the PS seeds to swell with DBP.

Separately, a monomer mixture consisting of 4.6 mL styrene and 2.3 mL DVB, in which 240 mg BPO was dissolved, was emulsified in 100 mL of an aqueous solution containing 0.25% (w/w) SDS using a mechanical stirrer for 1 min. This emulsified monomer solution was then introduced into the DBP-swollen PS seed dispersion under stirring conditions. The swelling of the monomer continued for 20 h at room temperature with constant stirring at 400 rpm.

Following the swelling process, 10 mL of a 10% (w/v) aqueous PVA solution was added to the dispersion, and the mixture was purged with nitrogen for 30 min. Seeded polymerization was carried out at 70 °C for 20 h with continuous stirring at 200 rpm to produce monodisperse PS-DVB beads. The resulting polymer beads were thoroughly washed three times with warm deionized (DI) water (50 °C), followed by three intensive washes with ethanol and THF to remove residual DBP and linear polymers. Finally, the beads were vacuum-dried at 70 °C for 24 h, yielding PS-DVB beads.

Sulfonation of Macroporous PS-DVB Beads

One gram of the synthesized PS-DVB beads was dispersed in 5 mL of acetic acid, followed by the gradual addition of 50 mL of sulfuric acid at room temperature. The mixture was stirred using a magnetic stirrer until the temperature reached 90 °C. Subsequently, the reaction vessel was transferred to an ice bath, where stirring continued for an additional 2 h. To terminate the reaction, the solution was poured into 400 mL of ice-cold water. The supernatant was removed via centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 20 min, repeated three times with iced DI water. The sulfonated PS-DVB beads were then subjected to seven consecutive washing cycles using DI water, employing centrifugation and decantation. Finally, the sulfonated beads were dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C for 24 h to eliminate residual moisture.

Magnetization of Sulfonated Macroporous PS-DVB Beads

A total of 500 mg of sulfonated PS-DVB seeds was dispersed in 10 mL of DI water and stirred at 200 rpm at room temperature for 30 min under a nitrogen purge. Meanwhile, a freshly prepared solution containing 618 mg of FeCl3·6H2O and 257 mg of FeCl2·4H2O in 10 mL of DI water was introduced into the dispersion, allowing adsorption to proceed for 2 h. Subsequently, 50 mL of 28% (v/v) NH4OH was gradually added dropwise. The resulting magnetized microparticles were separated via centrifugation and subjected to three brief washes with 25% TFA, followed by extensive rinsing with DI water and ethanol. Finally, the purified magnetized microparticles were dried under vacuum at 70 °C for 24 h.

Preparation of Silica Coated Magnetic Beads

A 100 mL solution of 1% (v/v) APTS was added to 100 mg of magnetic sulfonated PS-DVB beads and shaken for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 2 mL of 28% (v/v) NH4OH was slowly added dropwise, and the mixture was continuously shaken for an additional 20 min. Following this, 2 mL of TEOS was introduced into the dispersion and vigorously shaken for 12 h at room temperature. The resulting silica-coated magnetic beads were separated using an external magnetic field applied beneath the volumetric beaker, after which the aqueous phase was carefully removed. The beads were then washed five times with ethanol and allowed to air-dry at room temperature for 24 h. Their weight was then recorded.

Streptavidin Separation of Silica Coated Magnetic Beads

A total of 200 mg of silica-coated magnetic beads were immersed in a solution containing 4 mL of ethanol, 240 μL of APTS, and 40 μL of NH4OH for 6 h at room temperature. The resulting amine-functionalized silica-coated magnetic beads were separated from the aqueous phase using a magnet, washed five times with ethanol, and air-dried at room temperature for 24 h. Their weight was then recorded. Subsequently, the beads were incubated in a mixture containing 5 mg of biotin, 40 mg of EDC, and 20 mg of NHS in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at pH 7.2 for 1 h at room temperature, ensuring a final concentration of 10 mg of beads per mL of PBS. The biotinylated silica-coated magnetic beads were then collected using a magnet, washed five times with ethanol, and air-dried at room temperature for 24 h before their weight was recorded.

A total of 200 mg of biotinylated silica-coated magnetic beads were passivated with 5% (v/v) BSA solution in 100 mL of PBS (pH 9.0) to minimize nonspecific binding. Subsequently, 100 μg of streptavidin-FITC in PBS (pH 9.0) was added to the beads and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with continuous stirring until the streptavidin-FITC powder was fully dissolved. The beads were further incubated in the solution for an additional 30 min before being collected using a magnet. The beads were then washed five times with a PBS (pH 9.0) solution containing 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20. After washing, they were incubated in 100 μL of PBS (pH 9.0) for 24 h. After incubation, the beads were collected and dried in a vacuum oven at 70 °C for 24 h. The resulting enhanced magnetized microsphere beads were subsequently stored in a vacuum desiccator.

Synthesis of Fe3O4 Particles

A control synthesis was conducted without the extract to obtain bare Fe3O4 particles, following the procedure outlined by Nanta et al. The synthesis of Fe3O4 particles involved dissolving FeCl3·6H2O (2.53 g, 97%) and FeCl2·4H2O (0.99 g, ≥ 99%) in a 250 mL beaker at a 2:1 molar ratio with 50 mL of DI water, which was purged with nitrogen gas for 20 min. The resulting mixture was incubated in an ultrasonic bath at 70 °C and stirred at 400 rpm for 2 h. Following incubation, 50 mL of NH4OH was gradually added dropwise to the solution. The mixture was further stirred for an additional 2 h, leading to the formation of Fe3O4 particles. These particles were collected using a magnet placed beneath the beaker, allowing complete separation of the aqueous phase within 10–20 min. The collected Fe3O4 particles were then thoroughly washed with DI water until the pH stabilized between 7 and 8. Finally, the particles were dried under vacuum at 70 °C for 24 h and subsequently stored in a vacuum desiccator at 40 °C.

Synthesis of G. mangostana Fruit Peel Extract-Modified Fe3O4 Particles

The fruit peel extract of G. mangostana, rich in xanthones, was utilized as a novel stabilizing and capping agent for synthesizing Fe3O4 particles via a coprecipitation method. In this green synthesis approach, the crude extract served as a stabilizer, iron salts acted as the iron source, and NaOH functioned as a reducing agent.

To prepare the extract, fresh G. mangostana fruits were peeled, and the peels were thoroughly washed to eliminate any surface contaminants. The cleaned peels were then vacuum-dried at 45 °C for 36 h. A total of 10 g of dried peel was ground into fine particles and mixed with 100 mL of DI water, which had been purged with nitrogen gas for 20 min. The mixture was then heated to 80 °C and stirred continuously at 250 rpm for 1 h. The crude extract was separated by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 20 min and washed three times. The supernatant was discarded, and the remaining material was dried under high vacuum pressure at 50 °C for 24 h in a plastic beaker to obtain xanthone-rich extract. The dried extract was stored at 4 °C for further use.

For Fe3O4 particle synthesis, the extract was employed as a stabilizer in varying concentrations. The preparation of G. mangostana fruit peel extract-modified Fe3O4 particles was carried out following the methodology described by Yusefi et al. Five different extract solutions (wt %) were prepared by dissolving 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 25 g of dried extract powder in separate beakers containing 97.5, 95, 90, 80, and 75 g of DI water, respectively. Each solution was stirred for 15 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 2.53 g of FeCl3·6H2O and 0.99 g of FeCl2·4H2O (maintaining a 2:1 molar ratio) were added to the extract solutions sequentially, followed by continuous stirring for 30 min before introducing nitrogen gas. To adjust the pH to approximately 11, a 1 M NaOH solution was added dropwise, with vigorous stirring for an additional 30 min.

The resulting Fe3O4 particles were separated using a magnet and washed five times to ensure the pH reached neutrality. The samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 12 min, and the collected precipitates were oven-dried at 70 °C. The synthesized Fe3O4 particles, prepared with 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, and 25 wt % concentrations of peel extract, were designated as superparamagnetic iron oxide particles (SPIOP), SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5, SPIOP@X5/W95, SPIOP@X10/W90, SPIOP@X20/W80, and SPIOPX25/W75, respectively. The sample nomenclature ″SPIOP@X/W″ denotes SPIOP functionalized with xanthone (X, expressed in wt %) and dispersed in DI water (W, wt %).

Physicochemical Properties of Synthesized Fe3O4 Particles

The magnetic properties of SPIOPs, both unmodified and functionalized with silica (SPIOP@SiO2) or xanthone (SPIOP@XAN), were systematically analyzed. Magnetic characterization was carried out using a Lake Shore Cryotronics 7407 vibrating sample magnetometer to evaluate their magnetic response and behavior. Field emission scanning electron microscope analysis (FESEM, Hitachi-S-4800, Japan) was utilized to observe the surface features and to characterize the morphology and particle size of the synthesized materials. X–ray photoelectron spectrometer (XPS, Kratos Axis Ultra DLD, Kratos analytical Ltd., http://xps.web.its.man.ac.uk) was utilized to analyze the surface elemental composition of the synthesized SPIOPs. Surface charge and particle size distribution of the prepared particle suspensions were performed at 25 °C using dynamic light scattering (DLS) on a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS 90 (USA).

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed to identify surface functional groups and evaluate the microcrystalline structure of the lyophilized nanoparticle samples. Spectra were acquired using a PerkinElmer FT-IR spectrometer (Spectrum TWO universal ATR sampling, PerkinElmer, MA, USA) in transmission mode at room temperature, covering the spectral range from 4000 cm–1 to 400 cm–1 with a resolution of 0.5 cm–1.

The crystalline structure of powdered Fe3O4 particles was determined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) with a Rigaku D/max-γ X-ray diffractometer (Japan), operating with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) at 40 kV and 30 mA. Data were collected in θ–2θ geometry mode over an angular range of 15° to 100°, with a step size of 0.05° and a counting time of 10 s per step. The average crystallite size was calculated using Debye–Scherrer’s eq (eq ), based on the broadening of X-ray diffraction peaks 1 DSherrer=κλβcos⁡θ where D Sherrer represents the crystallite size (nm), κ = 0.94 is the shape factor, λ is the X-ray wavelength, β is the full width at half-maximum (fwhm), and θ is the Bragg angle.

Adsorption of DNA

The optimal conditions for DNA extraction were investigated using salmon sperm DNA as a model compound and three types of particles: SPIOPs, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@XAN. A total of 10 mg of each particle type was mixed with 2.5 mL of glycine/HCl buffer (pH 2.5) in a 10 mL plastic vial and incubated on an orbital shaker at room temperature under gentle agitation for 10 min. The salmon sperm DNA stock solution was prepared by combining 5 mM EDTA-2Na and 50 mM PBS in a 1:1 mass ratio, followed by pH adjustment to 2.5. Salmon sperm DNA was then added to reach a final concentration of 1500 ng·μL–1, as quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer at 260 nm (ND-2800-ODJ Nano DOT nucleic acid analyzer, Hercuvan Lab system, Malaysia). Next, 2.5 mL of the salmon sperm DNA stock solution was added to each particle suspension and incubated at 400 rpm and room temperature. After incubation, the particles were separated using an external magnet. Aliquots (2 μL) of the supernatant were collected at various time intervals (5, 10, 20, and 30 min) and analyzed using a NanoDrop at 260 nm to quantify the residual DNA concentration. The percentage of DNA adsorption was calculated using eq . 2 Adsorption%=(C0−C)VC0V0×100% where C 0 (ng·μL–1) and C (ng·μL–1) represent the initial and the supernatant concentration of DNA in the solution, respectively. V 0 (μL) and V (μL) represent the volumes of the originally prepared DNA solution and the supernatant, respectively.

Following DNA extraction, the particle pellets were washed with DI water. To evaluate DNA recovery, the isolated SPIOPs were redispersed in 2.5 mL of Tris/HCl buffer (1 M, pH 8) and incubated at 400 rpm and room temperature for 10 min. The particles were subsequently vortex-washed with DI water to remove any residual surface-bound DNA. For DNA desorption, 2.5 mL of Tris/HCl buffer (pH 8.0) was added to the particle suspension in a plastic vial, followed by orbital shaking at 400 rpm for 10 min. The particles were magnetically separated again and redispersed in nitrogen-degassed DI water (pH 7.5) for zeta potential measurement, while the supernatant was subjected to agarose gel electrophoresis for analysis.

Adsorption Kinetic and Adsorption Isotherm Studies

Adsorption isotherms were determined using a series of salmon sperm DNA solutions with initial concentrations ranging from 40 to 820 μg·mL–1, prepared in 20 mL EDTA-2Na/PBS and 20 mL glycine/HCl buffer (pH 2.5). For each trial, 40 mL of the prepared salmon sperm DNA solution was transferred into a 50 mL centrifuge tube containing 80 mg of the tested SPIOP samples (bare SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90). Continuous agitation at 500 rpm was applied while maintaining the temperature at 25 °C for the duration of the experiment. Aliquots of 200 μL were collected at selected time points (0, 5, 10, 20, and 30 min), and magnetic separation was applied for 1 min to ensure complete removal of SPIOPs from the supernatant. The amount of salmon sperm DNA adsorbed onto the particles was quantified by measuring the difference in salmon sperm DNA concentration before and after adsorption.

To characterize the adsorption behavior of salmon sperm DNA onto magnetic adsorbents, nonlinear isotherm modelsLangmuir, Freundlich, and Sipswere applied to fit experimental data. The Langmuir isotherm assumes monolayer adsorption on a homogeneous surface and is expressed as 3 qe=q0·b·Ce1+b·Ce where q e is the amount of DNA adsorbed at equilibrium (mg·g–1 adsorbent), b is the Langmuir equilibrium constant (L·g–1 adsorbent), Ce is the equilibrium concentration of DNA in the aqueous phase (mg·L–1), and q 0 is the maximum adsorption capacity (mg·g–1 adsorbent).

The Freundlich isotherm, suitable for heterogeneous surfaces, is given by 4 qe=KF·Ce1/n where K F is the Freundlich adsorption constant (mg·g–1 adsorbent) and n is the heterogeneity factor. It represents the distribution of adsorption energies and the degree of surface heterogeneity, with adsorption generally regarded as favorable when the heterogeneity factor n falls between 1 and 10. Adsorption is generally regarded as favorable when the value of n falls between 2 and 10, moderately favorable when it ranges from 1 to 2, and unfavorable when it is less than 1.

The Sips isotherm, which combines features of Langmuir and Freundlich models for high adsorbate concentrations, is described by 5 qe=qmS·KS·CenS1+KS·CenS where q mS is the maximum adsorption capacity (mg·g–1 adsorbent), K S is the Sips equilibrium constant (L·mg–1) nS, and n S (0 < n S < 1) denotes surface heterogeneity. When n S = 1, the model reduces to Langmuir; at low concentrations, it approximates Freundlich.

The Redlich–Peterson (R–P) isotherm model is a modified form of the Sips equation and can be expressed as 6 qe=KR·Ce1+aR·Ceβ where K R (L·g–1) and a R (L·mg−1)β are the R–P isotherm constants and β (0 < β < 1) is an exponent that characterizes the heterogeneity of the adsorbent surface. When β = 1, the equation simplifies to the Langmuir model, indicating homogeneous adsorption. Conversely, a β value approaching zero suggests that the adsorption process follows the Henry’s law region, reflecting a highly heterogeneous adsorption system.

Initial parameter estimates were obtained from linear forms of the models and optimized through nonlinear regression by minimizing the sum of squared errors (SSE) using Excel’s Solver add-in.

To analyze the adsorption kinetics, the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models were applied in nonlinear forms 7 qt,1=qe,1(1−exp−k1t) 8 qt,2=k2qe,22t1+k2qe,22t where q t is the amount of DNA adsorbed at time t (mg·g–1 adsorbent), q e,1 and q e,2 the equilibrium capacities for the pseudo-first and pseudo-second-order models, respectively, and k 1 (min–1) and k 2 (g·mg–1·min–1) are the corresponding rate constants.

As with the isotherm models, kinetic parameters were refined using Excel’s Solver tool for best fit to the experimental data.

Recovery of DNA from Agarose Gel

Agarose gel electrophoresis was conducted to confirm the presence and estimate the size of salmon sperm DNA extracted using bare SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90. A 1.2% (w/v) agarose gel was prepared in 1 × TBE buffer with a final volume of approximately 120 mL. The mixture was thoroughly melted in a microwave until fully dissolved and transparent, then poured into a casting tray fitted with a comb to form wells. After solidification at room temperature, the gel was transferred into an electrophoresis chamber filled with fresh 1 × TBE buffer, ensuring complete submersion. DNA samples were mixed with 6× loading dye to a final volume of ∼12 μL and carefully loaded into the wells, along with a 100 bp DNA ladder as a molecular size reference. Electrophoresis was carried out at 100 V for about 1.5 h, or until the dye front migrated to an appropriate distance. Following electrophoresis, the gel was poststained in freshly prepared ViSafe dye for 15–30 min with gentle rocking, briefly rinsed with DI water to reduce background fluorescence, and visualized under a blue light transilluminator (460 nm) to capture images of the DNA bands.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of SPIOPs

Zeta Potential and Magnetization Measurements

Zeta potential is widely used to assess the colloidal stability of particles. In general, particles with more negative zeta potential values exhibit greater electrostatic repulsion, which enhances their stability in suspension. Values within ±30 mV are typically associated with moderate stability and a higher likelihood of particle agglomeration due to reduced repulsive forces and a lower surface area-to-mass ratio. When the zeta potential falls within the narrow range of −10 mV to +10 mV, particles are considered unstable and prone to significant aggregation. Environmental factors within the dispersion medium can also influence particle interactions, often resulting in increased agglomeration and larger particle sizes. In this study, the zeta potentials of SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@XAN dispersed in nitrogen-degassed DI water (pH 7.5) were determined prior to DNA adsorption to assess the colloidal stability of the particles. The recorded zeta potential values were as follows: SPIOP (−33.26 ± 0.4 mV), SPIOP@SiO2 (−23.29 ± 0.6 mV), SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5 (−11.25 ± 0.2 mV), SPIOP@X5/W95 (−15.88 ± 0.9 mV), SPIOP@X10/W90 (−24.98 ± 0.1 mV), SPIOP@X20/W80 (24.40 ± 0.8 mV), and SPIOP@X25/W75 (32.36 ± 0.6 mV). These results suggest that samples with zeta potentials approaching zero, such as SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5 and SPIOP@X5/W95, exhibit lower colloidal stability and are more prone to aggregation. Among the SPIOP@XAN formulations, SPIOP@X10/W90 displayed the most negative zeta potential (−24.98 mV), suggesting a relatively high degree of electrostatic stabilization in aqueous suspension. The pronounced negative surface charge in this sample enhances interparticle repulsion, thereby minimizing agglomeration and supporting prolonged dispersion stability. Consequently, SPIOP@X10/W90 was selected for further adsorption kinetics and isotherm investigations. These observations are in agreement with previous findings by Yusefi et al., who reported a zeta potential of −20.72 ± 1.7 mV for unmodified Fe3O4 NPs. Upon functionalization with increasing concentrations of xanthone (0–10 wt %), the zeta potential ranged from −25.61 ± 1.8 mV to −34.92 ± 1.2 mV, reinforcing the role of xanthone in enhancing colloidal stability through surface charge modification.

Surface Morphology and Particle Size Analysis

FESEM analysis (Figure ) demonstrated that the SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90 samples exhibited spherical and aggregated morphologies, with mean particle diameters of 10.2 ± 4.2 nm, 9.0 ± 4.3 nm, and 10.9 ± 4.1 nm, respectively. The particle sizes observed from the FESEM micrographs were approximately 1.03-fold, 1.5-fold, and 1.2-fold smaller for SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90, respectively, compared to the TEM-based measurements reported by Yotsomnuk et al., Aderibigbe et al., and Yusefi et al.

FESEM images (a,c,e) and particle size distribution histograms (b,d,f) of SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90, respectively.

Compared to the particle sizes obtained from FESEM, the hydrodynamic diameters measured by DLS (Table S1, Supporting Information) were consistently larger for all as-prepared SPIOPs. This discrepancy arises because DLS measures the apparent hydrodynamic diameter, which includes not only the particle core but also the surrounding solvation or diffuse layer, whereas FESEM reflects only the physical core size. In aqueous suspension, interparticle interactions can further influence the DLS results. Specifically, hydrophobic attraction between Fe3O4 nanoparticles may promote slight aggregation, while the presence of hydrophilic surface extracts enhances surface hydration and colloidal stability, leading to an increased apparent particle size in DLS measurements. ,

X-ray Diffraction

The XRD patterns presented in the two images correspond to SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@XAN, highlighting variations in crystallinity and diffraction peak intensity. In all samples, the main diffraction peaks appear in the 2θ range of 20–80°, corresponding to the characteristic planes of iron oxide crystalline phases. Specifically, SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, SPIOP@X25/W75, and SPIOP@X20/W80 display prominent peaks at approximately 30.1°, 35.8°, 43.0°, 53.6°, 57.0°, and 62.5°, which can be attributed to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) planes, respectively (Figure ). These peaks are consistent with the standard pattern for magnetite (Fe3O4), as documented in the JCPDS database (Card No. 19-0629) and supported by previous reports. A broad hump between 25° and 35° 2θ, particularly visible in the SPIOP@SiO2 sample, suggests the presence of an amorphous silica (SiO2) shell, in agreement with JCPDS Card No. 00-002-0278. Meanwhile, samples with lower xanthone contentSPIOP@X10/W90, SPIOP@X5/W95, and SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5&mdash;exhibit diffraction peaks at similar positions but with reduced intensity and slight broadening, particularly near 29.8°, 35.0°, and 43.7°. These changes may indicate partial structural disorder or phase transformation influenced by the interaction with xanthone. Notably, SPIOP@X25/W75, which contains the highest proportion of xanthone, shows slightly enhanced peak intensities, especially near 35° and 43°, suggesting improved crystallite alignment or increased structural density. To estimate the crystallite size of iron oxide cores, the Scherrer eq (eq ) was applied to the XRD data. The calculated average crystallite sizes (D Scherrer) for SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, SPIOP@X25/W75, SPIOP@X20/W80, SPIOP@X10/W90, SPIOP@X5/W95, and SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5 were 11.7, 24.8, 18.9, 9.0, 6.4, 7.6, and 12.5 nm, respectively. The crystallite size of SPIOP (11.7 nm) was approximately 1.1-fold larger than that reported by Yotsomnuk et al. (10.78 nm). In contrast, SPIOP@SiO2 showed a markedly larger crystallite size (24.8 nm), about 2.8-fold greater than that reported by Turrina et al. (9.0 nm). Similarly, SPIOP@X10/W90 exhibited a slightly smaller crystallite size (6.4 nm), approximately 1.2-fold lower than the value reported by Yusefi et al. (7.81 nm).

The XRD patterns of the samples (Figure ), supported by XPS results (Figure ), indicate the formation of a highly crystalline and phase-pure Fe3O4 structure, with no detectable secondary phases. The calculated crystallite sizes (D Scherrer) for the Fe3O4 cores in SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90 were approximately 11.7, 24.8, and 6.4 nm, respectively. The discrepancies between the particle sizes estimated from XRD and those measured from FESEM micrographs for SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90 are likely attributed to measurement uncertainties arising from the manual estimation of particle dimensions in the FESEM images.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy

The functionalization of SPIOPs with SiO2 and xanthone was analyzed using FTIR spectroscopy. Figure a presents the FTIR spectrum of Fe3O4 particles synthesized via the coprecipitation method. The prominent absorption bands observed at approximately 564 cm–1, 1683 cm–1 and 3380 cm–1 correspond to the Fe–O stretching vibration, O–H bending, and O–H stretching, respectively, which are characteristic of uncoated Fe3O4 cores (SPIOPs). The Fe–O stretching vibration at 564 cm–1 is typically associated with the asymmetric stretching of Fe–O bonds within the tetrahedral sites of the inverse spinel crystal structure. , In the case of SPIOP@SiO2 (Figure b), a distinct and intense absorption band appears at around 1097 cm–1, attributed to the asymmetric stretching vibration of Si–O–Si bonds. This feature confirms the successful formation of a silica shell, consistent with findings reported by Rapp et al. For SPIOP@XAN, represented in Figure c–g, additional characteristic peaks emerge. A broad band around 3380 cm–1 corresponds to O–H stretching and hydrogen bonding, while a peak at 2873 cm–1 indicates C–H stretching vibrations. The absorption band at 1683 cm–1 is assigned to CO stretching, typical of carbonyl groups, and the peak at 1398 cm–1 reflects C–H bending. Moreover, the bands between 1300 cm–1 and 1000 cm–1 are attributed to C–O stretching vibrations, which may originate from alcohols, ethers, esters, carboxylic acids, and amide groups, possibly present in the crude extract of G. mangostana fruit peel used for xanthone incorporation. These spectral features align well with those reported by Yusefi et al., further supporting the successful surface modification.

XPS Analysis of Surface Composition

The elemental composition of the synthesized particles was characterized using XPS. The high-resolution survey spectra (Figure ) confirmed the presence of characteristic elements associated with ferrospinel structures prepared via coprecipitation. Among the samples, SPIOP@SiO2 exhibited the highest Fe content and the lowest C content (Table S2, Supporting Information). The Fe 2p spectra of SPIOP displayed two main peaks at 710.2 and 719.7 eV, corresponding to the Fe2p3/2 and Fe2p1/2 orbitals, respectively (Figure a). These peaks shifted to lower binding energies of 709.5 and 717.3 eV for SPIOP@SiO2 (Figure d). In contrast, SPIOP@X10/W90, which contained the lowest Fe and the highest C content (Table S2), exhibited Fe2p3/2 and Fe2p1/2 peaks at 710.1 and 718.3 eV (Figure h), respectivelysuggesting surface complexation between Fe and O atoms. The observed binding energies were consistent with those reported for Fe3O4. Additionally, weak satellite peaks detected at 724 and 712 eV for SPIOP (Figure a), 723.1 and 711.7 eV for SPIOP@SiO2 (Figure d), and 723.8 and 712.2 eV for SPIOP@X10/W90 (Figure h) were attributed to Fe3+ species in Fe2O3 phase, indicating slight surface oxidation of the magnetic particles under ambient conditions. Deconvolution of the O 1s spectra for SPIOP revealed three peaks centered at 529.7, 531.2, and 531.6 eV (Figure b), corresponding to Fe–O, H–O, and C–O bonds, respectively. In comparison, SPIOP@SiO2 exhibited peaks at 528.5, 529.8, and 531.4 eV (Figure e), while SPIOP@X10/W90 showed corresponding peaks at 529.0, 530.0, and 531.9 eV (Figure i). The higher-energy O 1s components (C–O and CO) were associated with partially dehydrated organic residues remaining on the particle surfaces. Similarly, deconvolution of the C 1s spectra yielded three primary peaks for SPIOP at 284.6 eV (C–C), 286.4 eV (C–OH), and 288.3 eV (CO) (Figure c). For SPIOP@SiO2 (Figure f) and SPIOP@X10/W90 (Figure j), four peaks were resolved at 282.6 eV (C–Fe), 284.6 eV (C–C), 286.2–286.4 eV (C–OH), and 288.3–288.6 eV (CO). The prominent C–C peak at 284.6 eV in all spectra was mainly due to adventitious carbon contamination. Distinctly, the XPS spectra of SPIOP@SiO2 displayed an additional photoelectron peak at 102.2 eV, corresponding to Si 2p (Figure g), confirming the successful chemisorption of SiO2 onto the Fe3O4 surface.

DNA Adsorption Study

Adsorption Efficiency

Equilibrium in salmon sperm DNA adsorption was reached at approximately 30 min, leading to maximum extraction efficiencies of 84.5% for bare SPIOPs, 11.1% for SPIOP@SiO2, 57.6% for SPIOP@X25/W75, 89.4% for SPIOP@X20/W80, 93.6% for SPIOP@X10/W90, 58.1% for SPIOP@X5/W95, and 59.6% for SPIOP@X2.5/W97.5 (Figure ). Notably, the extraction efficiencies of SPIOP, SPIOP@X20/W80 and SPIOP@X10/W90 were comparable to that of magnetic mesoporous silica NPs (M-MSNs), and were approximately 1.1-, 1.2-, and 1.25-fold higher, respectively, than the performance of multiamino-functionalized magnetic NPs (mAMNPs), which exhibited a maximum efficiency of 74.8%. In comparison with a commercial magnetic bead kit, which achieved an extraction efficiency of 56.6% and yielded a DNA concentration of 330.24 ± 9.79 μg·mg–1, only SPIOP@SiO2 performed worse. The poor salmon sperm DNA adsorption by SPIOP@SiO2 is likely attributable to electrostatic repulsion between the negatively charged silica surface and the negatively charged phosphate backbone of the DNA.

Adsorption efficiency on 10 mg SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2 and SPIOP@XAN for 595 mg·L–1 salmon sperm DNA after 30 min of extraction.

Adsorption Isotherms

To investigate the DNA adsorption behavior of SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2 and SPIOP@X10/W90, adsorption data were analyzed using three nonlinear isotherm modelsLangmuir, Freundlich, and Sips (Figure ). Model fitting quality was assessed based on the coefficient of determination (R 2) and the nonlinear chi-square statistic (χ2), as recommended. A lower χ2 value indicates a closer fit between the experimental and model-predicted data. As shown in Figure and summarized in Table , the Langmuir model provided the best fit for the adsorption of DNA onto SPIOP, with R 2 value of 0.961, and corresponding χ2 values of 9.89. These results suggest a monolayer adsorption mechanism, consistent with previous reports on salmon sperm DNA adsorption onto M-MSNs containing an Fe3O4 core and an 80 nm silica shell (S_80), as well as mAMNPs. For SPIOP@X10/W90, both the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson isotherm models provided the best fits, with an R 2 of 0.849 and 0.848, and corresponding to χ2 values of 18.73, and 19.58, respectively. Similarly, for SPIOP@SiO2, the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson models best described the adsorption behavior, yielding an R 2 of 0.624 and χ2 values of 60.52, and 59.78, respectively. The Redlich–Peterson model, a three-parameter isotherm, describes adsorption involving both monolayer formation and multisite interactions. These findings suggest that no single model can fully explain the adsorption mechanism; instead, both monolayer and multilayer adsorption processes coexist. Notably, the Freundlich model exponent n values were greater than 1, and the Redlich–Peterson exponent β values were relatively small, indicating a heterogeneous adsorption system. This observation aligns with previous findings on methylene blue adsorption onto Fe3O4/Char, where the Redlich–Peterson model provided the best fit with a β value of 0.766, suggesting that physisorption slightly dominated over chemisorption.

Adsorption Kinetics

In addition to isotherm analysis, the DNA adsorption kinetics onto SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90. were evaluated using pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order models. Accurate kinetic modeling is critical for optimizing and scaling up adsorption systems. This study introduces a validation method incorporating residual normality checks to enhance model selection across various kinetic frameworks. The rate constants derived from Figures and reveal nonlinear adsorption behavior for both kinetic models. Table summarizes the corresponding R 2, χ2, and normality test results. High R 2 values and low χ2 values indicate that both models fit the data well; however, the normality test suggests that the pseudo-first-order model better describes DNA adsorption onto SPIOP and SPIOP@SiO2. The adsorption kinetics of SPIOP align with previously reported pseudo-first-order behavior, with average rate constants (k 1) values ranging from 8.0 × 10–3 to 1.4 × 10–2 min–1, and from 1.6 × 10–3 to 1.28 min–1 for SPIOP@SiO2. In contrast, SPIOP@X10/W90 followed pseudo-second-order kinetics, consistent with prior findings on salmon sperm DNA adsorption onto magnetic Zr-MOFs (MUIO-66-COOH) functionalized with carboxyl-rich ligands. The corresponding average k 2 values for SPIOP@X10/W90 ranged from 9.51 × 10–6 to 1.44 × 10–5 g·mg–1·min–1. The as-prepared SPIOP, SPIOP@SiO2, and SPIOP@X10/W90 exhibited zeta potentials of −33.26 ± 0.4 mV, −23.29 ± 0.6 mV, and −24.98 ± 0.1 mV, respectively (Table S1, Supporting Information). Their more negative surface charges were associated with higher adsorption capacities of 110.5–221.4 mg·g–1, 13.8–38.0 mg·g–1, and 113.4–198.2 mg·g–1, respectively (Table ), in agreement with the trend reported by Serrano-Lotina et al.

Conclusion

Three types of SPIOP-based nanomaterials were successfully synthesized and comprehensively characterized using a combination of techniques, including FESEM, FTIR, DLS, XRD, XPS, and VSM analyses. The results confirmed that the samples possessed well-defined compositions, uniform morphologies, and narrow particle size distributions. Both SPIOP and SPIOP@X10/W90 exhibited high M s values exceeding 80 emu·g–1, demonstrating their strong magnetic responsiveness and potential for efficient magnetic separation and recovery from aqueous media. Following salmon sperm DNA elution, all SPIOPs exhibited more negative surface potentials, suggesting improved colloidal stability. Among the synthesized materials, SPIOP@X10/W90 showed the greatest adsorption capacity toward salmon sperm DNA, outperforming both the unmodified SPIOP and the SPIOP@SiO2 samples. Among them, SPIOP@X10/W90 exhibited the highest salmon sperm DNA adsorption efficiency, outperforming both unmodified SPIOP and SPIOP@SiO2. Under optimal conditions30 min at room temperatureSPIOP@X10/W90 achieved a DNA recovery rate exceeding 93%. Isotherm and kinetic analyses indicated that salmon sperm DNA adsorption onto SPIOP followed the Langmuir model and pseudo-first-order kinetics, suggesting monolayer adsorption. In contrast, SPIOP@X10/W90 was better described by the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson isotherms and pseudo-second-order kinetics, indicating a more heterogeneous surface interaction. Similarly, SPIOP@SiO2 was best fitted by the Freundlich and Redlich–Peterson isotherms but followed pseudo-first-order kinetics, implying that the adsorption of salmon sperm DNA was predominantly governed by physical interactions. These results highlight the potential of SPIOP@X10/W90 as an effective magnetic adsorbent for DNA extraction, owing to its superior adsorption capacity and recovery efficiency. Further optimization of the desorption solution and evaluation of reusability could enhance its practical application.
