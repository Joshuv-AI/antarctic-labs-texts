Poverty type, immigration background, and secondary school academic outcomes for children in British Columbia
Authors: Randip Gill, Mohammad Ehsanul Karim, Joseph H. Puyat, Monique Gagné Petteni, Martin Guhn, Magdalena Janus, Barry Forer, Anne Gadermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study utilized a retrospective, population-based cohort of administrative records of 167,319 children who attended school in British Columbia, Canada. The outcomes of standardized English, math, and science exam scores, as well as high school graduation were examined. The associations between poverty and educational outcomes at high school were found to be complex. Children experiencing both household and neighbourhood poverty (i.e., “combined” poverty) at age 13 had significantly lower English, math, and science exam scores at grade 10, as well as having higher odds to not graduate before age 20. The effect of combined poverty was larger than household poverty only or neighbourhood poverty only for English exam scores and for graduating. However, the association between poverty with math or science outcome scores was mixed. Experiencing neighbourhood poverty only was generally associated with lower performance in educational outcomes across children of different immigrant generation status (non-immigrant, first-generation, second-generation), immigration admission category (economic, family, refugee), or region of origin (East Asia and Pacific, Europe and Central Asia, South Asia). However, children of specific immigration backgrounds who experienced household-only poverty appeared to score better on math exams in comparison to children who did not experience poverty from those same groups. Intervention and prevention efforts to reduce childhood poverty that also include immigrant-specific considerations could potentially improve children’s educational outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11218-025-10093-x.

Introduction

The Canadian population is comprised of a growing proportion of foreign-born immigrants, as 23.0% of people in Canada in 2021 were immigrants, an increase from 21.9% in 2016 and 20.6% in 2011 (Statistics Canada, 2022a). The proportion of children in Canada under age 15 who had at least one foreign-born parent also modestly increased to 31.5% in 2021, up from 29.2% in 2016 and 26.7% in 2011 (Statistics Canada, 2022b). Different immigrant groups face their own unique challenges in Canada. For example, family class immigrants and refugees typically earn less than economic class immigrants and are 1.4 times more likely to be living in chronic low-income in comparison to economic class immigrants (Picot & Lu, 2017). More-recent immigrants also typically earn less than immigrants that have been in Canada for longer (Picot & Lu, 2017). In addition to financial challenges, immigrants face unique adaptation challenges due to the extent of differences between Canada and their home country in social cultures, educational systems, and through language difficulties (X. Li & Grineva, 2016). Examining immigrants as a monolithic group in research belies the complexities of their experience, and the diversity of supports that individuals from different regions of origin and backgrounds may require to thrive in Canada. The use of representative and long-term longitudinal data with specific groups within the immigrant subpopulation, including refugees, is also often lacking (Fazel et al., 2012). Investigating the underpinnings of student educational outcomes up to secondary school graduation, specifically in under-studied immigrant groups of differing backgrounds and income circumstances is critical towards supporting these often marginalized groups in Canada. The present study aims to address these gaps.

Poverty experience, immigration background, and its association to educational outcomes

Childhood poverty is associated with a range of harmful conditions that influence development and lifelong health (Hertzman & Boyce, 2010; Schmidt et al., 2021), and educational outcomes (Engle & Black, 2008). Children from low-income families have been found to experience detriments to cognitive functioning (Kishiyama et al., 2009; Lipina, 2016), for example in language (Noble et al., 2005) and reading (Merz et al., 2019). A study utilizing data from multiple cycles of the Statistics Canada National Longitudinal Survey of Children and Youth (NLSCY) found that higher income for children’s families followed from ages four to fifteen was consistently associated with better outcomes for children, including in math and English assessments (Phipps & Lethbridge, 2006). An individual’s educational attainment has also been associated with higher likelihood of employment, employment benefits, job satisfaction and earning potential in adulthood (Schudde & Bernell, 2019) and predicted more favourable future health and affect (Bowling et al., 2010; Lawrence, 2017).

The academic performance of immigrant children in their schooling is mixed. Children from differing backgrounds in terms of their immigration generation status (non-immigrant, first-generation immigrant, or second-generation immigrant), immigration admission category (economic, family, or refugee), or region of origin have been found to demonstrate a range of outcomes from consistently higher or consistently lower levels of academic performance relative to the non-immigrant population, as well as either gains in academic performance over time, or declines in performance over time (Gagné et al., 2021; Han, 2008; Suárez-Orozco et al., 2010; Szalacha et al., 2005). For certain immigrant groups, particularly newcomers to a country, several studies have found general patterns of academic engagement and school performance that are similar or better than their non-immigrant peers (Crosnoe & Turley, 2011; Suárez-Orozco et al., 2010; Szalacha et al., 2005). A child’s individual characteristics, such as their ethnic background, and neighbourhood characteristics such as high rates of poverty, may also be contributing to these differences in predicting educational attainment (Entwisle et al., 2005). For example, patterns of high academic achievement have been observed in Canada for students of Chinese descent (Costigan et al., 2010) and higher performance by immigrants in comparison to their non-immigrant peers in the U.S. is particularly pronounced for children of Asian and African backgrounds (Crosnoe & Turley, 2011).

Immigrant children’s socioeconomic status in the country that they settle in may also play a role in shaping their immigration experiences and adaptation outcomes. Previous studies exploring the association between poverty and educational outcomes differing by the immigration background of the child have observed contrasting results. Neighbourhood socioeconomic disadvantage (defined as a combination of mean family income, percentage of families led by one parent, and percentage of families living in rental dwellings) has also generally been associated with lower school performance as rated by teachers and persons most knowledgeable of the child (Georgiades et al., 2007). However, the children living in recent immigrant families (defined here as living in Canada for 15 years or less) had higher levels of school performance despite being more likely to live below the poverty line and in disadvantaged neighbourhoods (Georgiades et al., 2007). Newcomer immigrant youth attending schools with high-poverty concentration (based upon percentage of students who were eligible for free or reduced-cost school lunch) have also been found to be significantly more likely to have larger declines in their grade point average over time compared to those in higher average income schools (Suárez-Orozco et al., 2010). Finally, some studies have found that although first- and second-generation immigrant children are more likely to be living in poverty or low-income, indicators of SES may be weaker predictors of negative outcomes for immigrant children in comparison to non-immigrant children (Archambault et al., 2017; Beiser et al., 2002; Georgiades et al., 2007). The interaction between immigrant background and SES in predicting children’s outcomes may be due to differences for immigrant children in areas such as student motivation and importance placed on respecting teachers and the value of education (Archambault et al., 2017), as well as in family processes and children’s psychosocial adjustment (Archambault et al., 2017; Beiser et al., 2002; Georgiades et al., 2007). In an attempt to further disentangle this relationship, one study identified that poverty is not a weaker predictor of educational outcomes for all immigrant groups - mainly for a subset with specific socio-demographic advantages (e.g., economic class, high parent education) (Gagné et al., 2020).

Household poverty has been associated with fewer resources at the individual level and reduced social connections (Hjalmarsson & Mood, 2015) as well as detriments in areas such as satisfaction with life (Gill et al., 2025), which may be due to social comparisons and relative deprivation (D’Ambrosio & Frick, 2007). Neighbourhood poverty has been associated with reductions or difficulty in accessing services such as childcare and healthcare (Lavergne et al., 2023; Moran, 2021), as well as reduced safety in the community (Fitzgerald et al., 2004; Masi et al., 2007). Household and community factors may also interact, as the effect of neighbourhood poverty on educational attainment was ameliorated when parents had higher education (Troost et al., 2023).

Theoretical approach

This study is informed by the integrative model for studying the developmental competencies of minority children (Coll et al., 1996) regarding the specific challenges faced by children in minority groups facing social inequity. The present study investigates the proposed role that these minority children’s specific immigration backgrounds (e.g., refugee status; place of origin) might have on their development (Marks & Coll, 2018). This model focuses on children’s developmental competencies that result from the interplay of the child’s individual characteristics, such as their migration background, and their environment in areas such as their family socioeconomic status (SES), and their neighbourhood economic context. The capacity of children to develop competencies (e.g., cognitive, social, or linguistic abilities) may vary depending on their minority group’s ability to adapt to the contextual demands of their environment (Coll et al., 1996). For example, children of differing immigrant generation or region of origin, despite similar low-income circumstances, may vary in their ability to access resources to develop their competencies. One example of this could be due to their ability to access resources based upon the match of the languages they have learned in their country of origin and the language of the country they have settled in. Detriments to development earlier in life through these interactions may cascade over time and from one domain into another to contribute to potential challenges in multiple areas in adulthood, including in academic achievement and career outcomes (Hertzman & Boyce, 2010; Masten & Cicchetti, 2010).

The present study aims to build upon previous work by examining the relationship between different types of poverty to educational outcomes in secondary school up to graduation, including high school science performance which has not typically been examined in prior studies. Examining the association between household poverty, neighbourhood poverty, and the combination and possible cumulative effect of both household and neighbourhood poverty on children’s outcomes can inform policy as well as prevention and intervention efforts to alleviate the detriments that children living in these types of poverty may experience. Focusing on the association between inequities and future academic achievement could therefore elucidate opportunities for context-specific interventions to improve early outcomes for children with the potential for long-term benefits. The present study also aims to examine whether the association between poverty and educational outcomes in Canada differs based upon the immigration background of the child’s family.

The study objectives are: (1) Examine the relationship between early adolescent categories of poverty experience and academic achievement indicators. (2) Determine whether the association between poverty in early adolescence and academic achievement differs by the immigrant generation status of the child. (3) Determine whether the association between poverty in early adolescence and academic achievement differs by the immigration admission category of the child. We hypothesize that children who experience both household and neighbourhood poverty at age 13 (referred to as “combined poverty” in this study) experience a unique detriment to their educational outcomes in high school in comparison to those experiencing a single type of poverty or no poverty.

Methods

Data source

This study used a retrospective, population-based, linked dataset comprising: (1) Physician claims records for health insurance information (British Columbia Ministry of Health, 2017) that came from the B.C. Ministry of Health Medical Services Plan (MSP) Payment Information File; (2) Immigration records (Immigration Refugees and Citizenship Canada, 2017) that came from the Immigration, Refugees, and Citizenship Canada Permanent Resident Database; and (3) School records came from the B.C. Ministry of Education and Child Care (British Columbia Ministry of Education, 2017) to determine the child’s gender (children were coded in school records as having a “gender” of “male” or “female”), high school subject grades information, and whether the child had a designation for inclusive education (children were coded in school records as having specific inclusive (formerly referred to as “special needs”) designations). Neighbourhood income quintiles were derived from postal code data, using the Statistics Canada Postal Code Conversion File (PCCF+; Statistics Canada, 2023a). Neighbourhoods are divided equally into five categories, from 1 (lowest income quintile) to 5 (highest income quintile). Children were categorized, at each year of age, as living in an income quintile based upon their MSP registration. These requested data were linked by Population Data BC, a center in BC specializing in population-level data linkage, using a probabilistic-deterministic approach (linkage rate, 98.4%; Population Data, 2021). Individual linkage across data sources was completed using multiple identifiers (e.g., Personal Health Numbers). The study was approved by a Canadian Research Ethics Board.

The study cohort from which the analytic sample is derived includes children (48.3% female) born between 1990 and 1997 who resided in 10 of the largest by population school districts in B.C., with study data available up to 2017. Data for the full B.C. population were not available; as such, these 10 school districts were requested for linkage from data stewards because they captured the vast majority of the immigrant population in B.C. (Statistics Canada, 2017). Approximately 80% of B.C.’s recent immigrants live in these school district areas (NewtoBC, 2018). This cohort was defined using data from the B.C. Ministry of Health and B.C. Ministry of Education and Child Care, with inclusion requiring that the children were present in B.C. during the study period (Fig. 1). Yearly inclusion in provincial health insurance records (being present in the province for > 274 days/year) was utilized as a proxy for children’s presence in B.C. and inclusion in the present study (Guhn et al., 2020; Thomson et al., 2019).

Measures

Exposures of interest

The primary exposures of interest in this study are experiencing household poverty only, neighbourhood poverty only, or combined household and neighbourhood poverty at age 13. The age of 13 was chosen as the typical age at which children in B.C. transition to high school. At this age, children enter adolescence, which encompasses significant periods of biological growth, social role transitions, and is a crucial period to gain the necessary educational and social assets for future employment and health (Sawyer et al., 2018). Poverty was operationalized as “combined poverty” if children experienced both neighbourhood poverty and household poverty concurrently, utilizing definitions of neighbourhood poverty and household poverty that have been previously used in the literature (Gill et al., 2024; Guhn et al., 2020; Roos et al., 2019). The utilization of this “combined poverty” category, in addition to “household-only” and “neighbourhood-only” poverty allows for the delineation of a possible unique detriment that children may experience when exposed to concurrent household and neighbourhood poverty in comparison to children who do not experience poverty, or only experience a single poverty type. From here on in this paper, the category “neighbourhood poverty” refers to experiencing only neighbourhood poverty and “household poverty” refers to experiencing only household poverty.

Neighbourhood poverty

is a binary variable operationalized in the present study as residing in a neighbourhood area that is in the lowest income-quintile relative to other neighbourhoods (Gill et al., 2024; Roos et al., 2019) based upon census records. The neighbourhoods were assigned to children based on their family residence postal code from their MSP registration and the corresponding census data dissemination areas (Statistics Canada, 2018). Dissemination areas typically comprise of 400–700 residents, which may be geographically compact in urban locations and larger in rural regions (Statistics Canada, 2018).

Household poverty

is a binary variable defined as receiving a health insurance subsidy (MSP subsidy) due to the low-income qualification of the child’s household (Guhn et al., 2020). MSP subsidy information is available yearly for each household, with subsidies ranging from 20 to 100%, depending upon the income bracket of the household. MSP subsidy was used as a proxy for determining household poverty, as eligibility for opting-in for this subsidy is based upon the family’s adjusted net income each year. During the study period, the income ceiling for those eligible to receive a subsidy increased incrementally up to $30,000 for a family of three depending upon the year of receipt. This cut-off is comparable to the Statistics Canada after-tax, low-income cut-off for a family of three in a large metropolitan area, which was up to $29,260 during the poverty observation period in this study (Statistics Canada, 2015).

Outcomes of interest

English, math, and science exam scores

Prior to the 2016/2017 school year, all students enrolled in B.C. public and private schools were required to take an English, math, and science course (or their equivalent) at the grade 10 level and to complete a province-wide standardized written assessment for each subject (Government of BC, 2023c). Continuous outcome variables for each subject were derived from percent grades out of 100 on each of these exams that were converted into standardized scores with a mean of 0 and standard deviation of 1 to be utilized in this study’s analyses.

Secondary school graduation

A six-year completion rate from grade 8 enrolment (which typically begins at age 13) is reported by the B.C. Ministry of Education and Child Care to estimate high school graduation rates of students in B.C. (Government of BC, 2023a), and has also been utilized in previous analyses (Green et al., 2023). Most students complete high school in B.C. within the expected five-year period, though a number of students will take longer to complete for reasons such as students who take time off to study abroad or play sports, and those who require an additional year to successfully finish one or two required courses (Government of BC, 2023a). All students in B.C. were required to successfully complete an English course (or its equivalent) at the grade 12 level to meet their graduation requirements for a B.C. Dogwood Graduation Certificate (Government of BC, 2023c). Children were considered to meet graduation requirements in this study if they successfully completed the grade 12 English course requirement before age 20, and had obtained a B.C. Dogwood Certificate of Graduation in their education record. Children were categorized into a binary outcome variable of “graduated” or “not graduated”.

Immigrant generation status

Utilizing the Immigration Refugees and Citizenship Canada (IRCC) Permanent Resident Database, children were categorized as either “first-generation immigrant” (the child was born outside of Canada, having their own IRCC record), “second-generation immigrant” (the child was born in Canada, having at least one parent with an IRCC record), or “non-immigrant” (any child who did not fall under the first- or second-generation categories).

Immigration admission category

Individuals immigrating to Canada typically fall under one of three admission categories of immigration: Economic, family, or refugee admission category. Immigrants entering Canada under the economic admission category go through an application process and are selected based upon their ability to contribute to the Canadian economy through meeting labor market needs, business operation, or investment (Statistics Canada, 2023b). Immigrants entering through the family admission category are sponsored by family members who are already Canadian citizens or permanent residents (Statistics Canada, 2023b). Immigrants entering through the refugee admission category are those who are selected to enter Canada from their home countries based upon their need for protection based upon a well-founded fear of returning to their home country (Statistics Canada, 2023b).

Confounders

The confounders adjusted for in the present study’s analyses were based upon their previous identification and inclusion as relevant confounders in the literature. Previous research indicates that males tend to underperform in comparison to females in school, with males overrepresenting those failing courses and dropping out of high school (Legewie & DiPrete, 2012). Other research has shown that children with special educational needs tend to have lower high school graduation rates and more functional limitations relative to children without special needs (Russell et al., 2021). Therefore, child’s gender (male or female) and a child’s designation for inclusive education at age 12 (autism spectrum disorder; behavioural impairment; intellectual impairment; learning disability; physical impairment; no inclusive education designation) were adjusted for in this study’s analyses. Children with the “gifted” designation were not included in a designation category. Education administrative records were utilized to gather information on child’s gender and their specific designations for inclusive education.

Analyses

Descriptive statistics

Demographic characteristics of the analytic sample were compared to the overall cohort from which the sample was drawn to examine the magnitude of differences between the analytic sample and those with missing data or who were lost to follow-up (Fig. 1).

Aim 1 analysis

Effect of poverty experience on educational outcomes. Each analysis in this study examined children’s poverty exposure categorized as experiencing “no poverty”, “household poverty only”, “neighbourhood poverty only”, or “combined poverty” for each outcome. Unadjusted and adjusted linear regression models (for the continuous outcome variables of standardized grade 10 English, math, and science exam scores) and logistic regression models (for the binary outcome variable of whether children graduated high school before age 20) were completed to determine the association between poverty experience with each educational outcome. The previously outlined confounders (child’s gender, and designation for inclusive education at age 12) were adjusted for in each analysis.

Aim 2 analysis

Association of poverty and high school educational outcomes by immigrant generation status. To examine this association, we included an interaction term of poverty with immigrant generation status (non-immigrant vs. first-generation immigrant vs. second-generation immigrant) in the linear and logistic regression analyses.

Aim 3 analysis

Association of poverty and high school educational outcomes by immigration admission category. To examine this association, we included an interaction term of poverty with immigration admission category (non-immigrant vs. economic vs. family vs. refugee admission category) in the linear and logistic regression analyses.

Sensitivity analyses

To check for robustness of primary analysis results: (1) E-values (VanderWeele & Ding, 2017) were calculated for the point estimates (conditional on the measured covariates) to estimate how strongly an unmeasured confounder would have to be associated with both the combined poverty experience and each educational outcome to account for the observed associations. (2) Region of origin data were available for children who immigrated to Canada or who had parents that immigrated to Canada. To determine whether the association between poverty and educational outcomes differed by the region of origin of the child’s family, additional analyses were completed examining the association between poverty and each outcome for the three regions of origin (World Bank, 2023) that immigrants to Canada most commonly arrived from during the study period: (1) East Asia and Pacific (e.g., China, Philippines) (2) Europe and Central Asia (e.g., United Kingdom, Kazakhstan) (3) South Asia (e.g., India, Pakistan) (3) Parental education level data was available for children who had parents that immigrated to Canada (first- and second-generation immigrant children). Utilizing the subset of children with data for two parents available in IRCC records, analyses were completed to determine whether the association between poverty and the child’s educational outcomes remained after adjustment for parental education history. Each parent was assigned a numeric value for their level of education (“1” for less than a high school education, “2” for a high school education, and “3” for those with more than a high school education (Gagné et al., 2020). The average of the education level values was taken for the two parents and utilized in the adjusted analysis. (4) To determine whether the association between poverty and educational outcomes differed based upon whether the child was designated as an “English Language Learner (ELL)” (Government of BC, 2017) in education records at any point between age 10 to 12, an interaction term of poverty with ELL status (yes vs. no) was included in the linear and logistic regression analyses.

Given the low level of missing data in predictor variables (≤ 1% per variable) and the absence of systematic missingness patterns based upon Little’s Test of MCAR (p > 0.05), pairwise deletion was deemed appropriate to maximize data retention without introducing bias (Fig. 1). Data analyses were conducted in R v.3.6.1.

Results

Study population characteristics

In our sample of 167,319 children (48.3% female), 4.5% (n = 7,445, Table 1) had experienced combined poverty at age 13. The proportion of the study sample that experienced household poverty only at age 13 was 11.0% (n = 18,186), and neighbourhood poverty only was 14.5% (n = 24,059). The proportion of the study sample who were non-immigrants was 73.6% (n = 123,220), first-generation immigrants were 12.5% (n = 20,938) and second-generation immigrants were 13.8% (n = 23,161). The proportion of non-immigrants experiencing combined poverty (3.2%; n = 3,996) was smaller in comparison to first-generation immigrants experiencing combined poverty (7.9%; n = 1,662) (X2 = 1042.7, p < 0.001) or second-generation immigrants experiencing combined poverty (7.7%; n = 1,787) (X2 = 1029.6, p < 0.001). Unadjusted associations between immigrant generation status, immigration admission category, and region of origin with educational outcomes are presented in Supplemental Table 1.

Aim 1: effect of early adolescent experience of poverty on educational outcomes

In the adjusted analyses, experiencing combined poverty at age 13 was associated with significantly lower standardized English exam score at grade 10 (B = -0.30, 95% CI: [-0.33; -0.28], p < 0.001), math exam score (B = -0.05, 95% CI: [-0.07; -0.03], p < 0.001), science exam score (B = -0.13, 95% CI: [-0.15; -0.10], p < 0.001), and higher adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of not graduating (aOR = 1.98, 95% CI: [1.86; 2.10], p < 0.001) (Table 2). Experiencing household-only poverty was associated with significantly lower standardized English exam score (B = -0.19, 95% CI: [-0.20; -0.17], p < 0.001), science exam score (B = -0.08, 95% CI: [-0.09; -0.06], p < 0.001), and higher odds of not graduating (aOR = 1.61, 95% CI: [1.54; 1.68], p < 0.001), but was not significantly associated with math score (p > 0.05) (Table 2). Neighbourhood-only poverty was associated with significantly lower standardized English exam score (B = -0.22, 95% CI: [-0.23; -0.20], p < 0.001), math exam score (B = -0.12, 95% CI: [-0.14; -0.11], p < 0.001), science exam score (B = -0.17, 95% CI: [-0.19; -0.16], p < 0.001), and higher odds of not graduating (aOR = 1.80, 95% CI: [1.74; 1.87], p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Aim 2: association of poverty and educational outcomes by immigrant generation status

The interaction terms between each poverty type with both first-generation status and second-generation immigrant status was significant for nearly every outcome except for math 10 exam score (Table 3). For standardized math score, the interaction was significant only for household-only poverty for first-generation immigrants, and for both combined and household-only poverty for second-generation immigrants (Table 3). In the stratified analyses, combined poverty was generally associated with lower performance in educational outcomes, particularly for English 10 exam score and for graduating (Table 4). Neighbourhood-only poverty was universally associated with lower performance in educational outcomes for each immigrant generation group in comparison to those who did not experience any poverty at age 13. However, for first generation immigrants household-only poverty was found to be significantly associated with higher math exam score (B = 0.14, 95% CI: [0.11; 0.18], p < 0.001), higher science exam score (B = 0.06, 95% CI: [0.03; 0.10], p < 0.001), and lower odds to not graduate (aOR = 0.74, 95% CI: [0.65; 0.83], p < 0.001) (Table 4). Household-only poverty was also associated with higher math exam score for second generation immigrants (B = 0.08, 95% CI: [0.04; 0.12], p < 0.001) but with lower math exam score for non-immigrants (B = -0.06, 95% CI: [-0.08; -0.04], p < 0.001).

Aim 3: association of poverty and educational outcomes by immigration admission category

The interaction terms between each poverty type with economic, family, and refugee admission category were significant for nearly every outcome except in some combinations for standardized math 10 exam score (Table 5). In the stratified analyses, combined poverty was associated with lower academic performance for non-immigrants in each outcome examined, but only consistently associated with lower English 10 exam score for the other immigration admission category groups (Table 6). Neighbourhood-only poverty was nearly universally associated with lower educational outcomes across each subject examined and for each immigration admission category (Table 6). However, household-only poverty was found to be significantly associated with higher math exam scores for both economic category (B = 0.15, 95% CI: [0.12; 0.19], p < 0.001) and family reunion category immigrants (B = 0.10, 95% CI: [0.06; 0.15], p < 0.001). For economic, family, or refugee category immigrants, combined poverty was not significantly associated with the odds of graduating (Table 6). However, household-only poverty for economic category immigrants was associated with lower odds of not graduating (aOR = 0.84, 95% CI: [0.65; 0.83], p < 0.001), while neighbourhood-only poverty was associated with higher odds (aOR = 1.26, 95% CI: [1.12; 1.40], p < 0.001) (Table 6).

Discussion

Children who experienced any kind of poverty at age 13 generally had lower performance in English and science exam scores, and were less likely to graduate before age 20 in comparison to those who experienced no poverty. Overall, children who experienced combined poverty scored significantly lower in English and were less likely to graduate in comparison to children who experienced a single poverty type or no poverty. However, the association of household-only poverty or combined poverty to math exam scores was inconsistent, and differed depending upon the immigrant generation status, immigration admission category, or region of origin of the child. Household-only poverty was associated with significantly better math exam scores for both first- and second-generation immigrants in comparison to those who did not experience poverty, but was associated with lower scores for non-immigrants. Further analyses helped to unpack the association between household-only poverty and math scores. When examining immigrant admission category, children who were economic category immigrants and experienced household-only poverty scored higher in math and science exam scores and were more likely to graduate in comparison to economic category immigrants not experiencing poverty. Finally, it was observed that children with immigration backgrounds from the East Asia Pacific region outperformed all other groups in math exam score. When examining the interaction with poverty, children from East Asia Pacific who were experiencing household-only poverty uniquely had higher math exam scores and were more likely to graduate in comparison to those from the same region who did not experience poverty.

Consistent with previous work in this area examining the association between poverty and children’s outcomes (Archambault et al., 2017; Beiser et al., 2002; Georgiades et al., 2007), poverty was associated with lower performance in educational outcomes for non-immigrant children to a greater extent than for immigrant children. In addition, the association between poverty and math exam score appeared to be more complex and varied more by immigration background factors in comparison to performance on English exams.

Parental educational level and occupational history in both Canada and their country of origin are associated with household income and may be two factors through which the association between poverty and educational outcomes for their children could be understood and further explored. Foreign-born children may be more likely to be living in poverty due to their parents’ current employment in professions well below their training in their country of origin (Houle & Yssaad, 2010). However, despite their lower current income level, first generation immigrant families may be retaining high cultural capital upon relocating to Canada due in-part to the socioeconomic advantage that immigrant parents may have had in their home countries (Basarkod et al., 2022). This retained cultural capital may partly explain the observed interaction between household income and first generation immigrant status. While there is a positive relationship between educational level of the parents and student performance in maths, there is variability across different educational backgrounds (Statistics Canada, 2008). For example, one-quarter of the students who scored in the top 15% in the mathematics section of the Programme for International Student Assessment (PISA) (for age 15–16 students) had parents who had at most a high school education, yet one-quarter of those in the bottom 15% of students had at least one parent with a university degree (Statistics Canada, 2008). Students whose parents had occupations that specifically required strong math skills in areas such as mathematical and engineering science professionals tended to have higher math scores than other students with parents of the same level of education but in a different subject area (Statistics Canada, 2008). The wide range of student performance within each group indicates that success of many students appears to be dependent on factors other than their parents’ level of education (Statistics Canada, 2008) and accordingly factors other than their household income.

Among the 23 highest-performing countries and economies, performance differences by the child’s SES in a standardized math exam were narrower-than-average in several countries, including in Canada (OECD, 2012). A large proportion of the first-generation and second-generation immigrant population in B.C. is comprised of children from the East Asia Pacific region. This group’s uniquely high performance in math in general, and specifically when experiencing household-only poverty, may be contributing substantially to the observed effect in which household-only poverty was not associated with a detriment to math exam score. This may in part be due to the high emphasis placed on academic performance within this group (Li et al., 2008), and high math performance in specific countries from this region (OECD, 2019). For example, regardless of their current academic achievement, 95% of children in a sample of low-income Asian-American children indicated that education was the highest value to their families, and that education was seen as the only way to a good life (Li et al., 2008). This emphasis on educational outcomes for these children may be driving their higher performance in math in the present study. Math may also be an academic subject where barriers associated with immigrant background (i.e. English as a second language) may be easier to overcome than in performance in English courses. Although higher parental educational attainment is associated with higher math performance in youth, this positive effect has been found to be less of a limitation to math performance for Asian-American students in comparison to non-Hispanic White students (Assari et al., 2020). There may be other social mechanisms rather than lower familial SES that are associated with differences across ethnic groups (Assari et al., 2020).

On average, students from schools with a higher average SES tended to perform better regardless of their personal socioeconomic backgrounds (Statistics Canada, 2008). This may be associated in part with a potential positive effect for students surrounded by positive peer influences and role models (Statistics Canada, 2008) in higher income schools and neighbourhoods. The more consistent association observed between neighbourhood-only poverty and educational outcomes in the present study may be due in part to the link observed between higher levels of poverty in a school population and the surrounding area with school-level factors relevant to children’s education, reflecting extraneous factors that differ among these schools. Schools in lower income areas also tend to experience greater teacher attrition rates than are observed in schools in higher income areas (Ingersoll, 2002; Marinell & Coca, 2013), as well as higher teacher stress and burnout (Bottiani et al., 2019). This may contribute to the disruption of children’s education, fragmentation of instructional programming (Guin, 2004), and hamper educational outcomes for children in lower income schools. Schools may face additional inequities through private fundraising, as fundraising efforts for school resources and expenditures tends to be higher for higher-income area schools in Canada in comparison to schools with a lower-income population (Yoon et al., 2020). Finally, these differences in outcomes by children’s background and academic subject may also be due in-part to the expectations of teachers in classroom settings. For example, teachers’ expectations and stereotypes regarding the performance of students in language proficiency have been found to differ by whether the child was part of the ethnic minority or majority, but the same effect was not found for math achievement (Glock, 2016). These differences may influence engagement with students of differing backgrounds in subjects such as English versus math.

Strengths and limitations

The major strength of this study was the use of a linked dataset containing administrative health, immigration, and education records. The unique examination of each poverty type for children from different immigration backgrounds was made possible through this data linkage and the extensive sample size. For example, we examined the long-term outcomes for smaller subpopulations of children such as those who are refugees and were present in B.C. from age 10 to age 19, and those with the less prevalent experience of combined household and neighbourhood poverty. The unique utilization of each poverty category: combined household and neighbourhood poverty, household poverty only, or neighbourhood poverty only, allows for the delineation of the association of each exposure to future academic outcomes for children. The utilization of administrative education records for standardized exam scores, course grades, and graduation allowed us to capture the outcomes of the majority of the high school population present in the study observation period in these school districts. The use of province-wide standardized written assessments allowed for the inclusion of all children enrolled in public high school during the study period, utilizing an assessment that was identical and administered simultaneously across schools and school districts.

This study has several limitations. Self-reported or tax-filing household income data were not available. For this reason, the receipt of health insurance subsidy due to low household income was used as a proxy for household poverty. The utilization of this proxy may have underestimated household poverty, as families need to opt-in. Prior economic analyses determined that approximately one quarter of households that are eligible to receive the MSP subsidy in B.C. fail to do so (Warburton, 2005). Families that are exposed to the most severe levels of poverty could be less likely to access the health insurance subsidy, and may be less likely to interact with government services in general, possibly limiting their inclusion in the present study. It is possible that there is a misclassification bias and a bias towards the null in the relationship between poverty and educational outcomes for these children.

Observations were not clustered by neighbourhood in a multi-level model. Children may have resided within different neighbourhoods between poverty observation and educational outcomes and therefore could have belonged to multiple clusters over this period (see also Roos et al., 2019).

Another limitation is that although the use of administrative records included a large population, allowing for the examination of small subgroups that might otherwise not be possible to examine (e.g., refugee group immigrants experiencing combined poverty), it was not possible to explore some immigrant subgroups due to small sample size.

Our methodology also required children to be present in B.C. from age 10 to age 19 to be included in the analytic sample. Participants who were included in the analytic sample may differ in characteristics from those who were lost to follow-up. Specifically, a bias may exist in that children lost to follow-up may have been experiencing the most severe levels of poverty or health conditions. Children who temporarily resided outside of B.C. were also excluded. Children present at age 10 but lost to follow-up were found to be more likely to be male, experiencing household poverty at age 10, neighbourhood poverty at age 10, and more likely to be first-generation immigrants. Finally, students who either chose not to attend or were not able to attend school to write the exams that were examined are more likely to have physical or mental health conditions or lower academic engagement than those who have higher school attendance (Centeio et al., 2018; Johnsen et al., 2022). This exclusion of participants and loss to follow-up limits the generalizability of the findings and may have introduced bias due to differences between children in the analytic sample versus children who did not meet the inclusion criteria.

This study utilized data of students from the ten highest population school districts in British Columbia. The use of this study population limits the generalizability of the findings of this study to other parts of Canada or to rural regions of this province. The population of B.C. differs from the Canadian national averages regarding income and immigration background. According to Canada’s official poverty line, in 2022 there was a higher rate poverty rate (11.6%) in B.C. in comparison to the Canadian average (9.9%) (Government of Canada, 2024). In addition, B.C. has a larger proportion of its population (28.8%) that identifies as being of Asian descent than the national average (17.7%). Finally, a larger proportion of the B.C. population (28.3%) was born outside of Canada than the national average (21.9%) (NewtoBC, 2018). Reflecting this, policies impacting children’s care services and education in Canada differ between provinces (Lafontaine-Émond, 2021), which should be considered when interpreting the findings of the present study and considering implications for policy.

Implications and future directions

This study provides further evidence regarding the importance of providing economic support to families with low income. Economic policies to support these families have been found to reduce detrimental outcomes for children (Forget, 2011; Milligan & Stabile, 2011). Although the size of differences in secondary school outcomes observed across poverty conditions and immigrant backgrounds equates to a disparity of a few percentage points in exam scores or in likelihood to graduate, at the population level these relatively small effects may result in the difference of hundreds to thousands of children failing to pass a course or graduate. This earlier disadvantage in areas such as academic achievement can negatively impact other social and cognitive domains as children age which can be associated with future challenges such as in employment opportunities (Schudde & Bernell, 2019) or behavioural outcomes (Dodge et al., 2008; Hertzman & Boyce, 2010; Masten et al., 2005) into adulthood. Understanding even small effects at an individual level may be critical when considering preventative strategies or interventions at the population level to address these disparities (Matthay et al., 2021). In addition to economic policies, targeted and multi-faceted intervention or prevention efforts such as the Pathways to Education project have been found to improve high school graduation rates, enrollment into post-secondary institutions, and in-turn future educational attainment of at-risk youth in low-income communities (Government of Canada, 2022). The Pathways project includes core elements of tutoring of students by volunteers in the community, group and career mentoring, as well as financial support dependent upon the students’ successful academic work and graduation. Modifications of community-oriented interventions may however be necessary to ensure that marginalized groups from minority communities such as immigrants and refugees are able to access these resources, as difficulties in accessing resources for immigrant families due to factors such as cultural or language barriers may exacerbate challenges in navigating government systems and services such as healthcare (Salami et al., 2020) and education. Additional avenues through which to improve outcomes for ethnic minority students from diverse backgrounds could include an expansion of English language learning programs, fostering of ethnic identities, increasing the multicultural training of teachers and other school personnel, and fostering parental involvement (Costigan et al., 2010).

The findings of this study also have implications for future research in this area. The surrounding context of poverty including the type of poverty and the diversity of the demographic backgrounds of the population under study must be considered to sufficiently comprehend the relationship between poverty and children’s educational outcomes. It is possible that length of stay and years of education in a specific country of origin versus the settlement country of both the parents and/or children could be contributing to the interaction observed between poverty and immigration background in predicting future educational outcomes. In addition, extending these analyses beyond high school graduation and into post-secondary to determine how immigrant and non-immigrant children from differing socioeconomic backgrounds achieve academically into advanced education and training would provide further insight into their educational trajectories. The specific mechanisms through which poverty associates with academic performance for individuals from diverse immigrant subgroups may also be an area for further exploration through qualitative analyses. Finally, tax-free, monthly payments called the B.C. Family Benefit is available to eligible immigrant and non-immigrant families in B.C. in addition to the Canada Child Benefit (CCB) that is available across Canada (Government of BC, 2023b). These programs are aimed to help with the cost of raising children under the age of 18. The magnitude of the benefits from these programs have seen increases since the end of the observation period of this study. The expansion of these benefits could be investigated for a possible association to changes in educational outcomes of children in B.C.

Conclusion

The association between poverty and educational outcomes at secondary school is complex. Although neighbourhood-only poverty was generally associated with lower performance in outcomes for children, particularly in the subject of English, the association between household poverty and math performance was mixed. The association of household income and math performance appeared to vary widely based upon immigration background characteristics of the children. In sum, the association of poverty with educational outcomes appears to differ by the type of poverty, the educational subject examined, as well as the immigration background of the child. Intervention and prevention efforts to reduce gaps in educational outcomes that are associated with poverty may benefit from also considering the unique needs or circumstances of children from varying immigration backgrounds.
