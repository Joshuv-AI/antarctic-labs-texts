Antimicrobial Prescribing Practices for Surgical Prophylaxis in Caesarean Sections at Kawempe National Referral Hospital, Kampala, Uganda
Authors: Jonathan Nkalubo, Débora Brito Goulart, Arindam Mitra, Alexia Martin, Richard Donkor Amponsah, Meena Murugappan, Ebenezer Adewuyi Ademola, Brian Nyiro, Imelda Namatovu, Mohammed Lamorde, Henry Kajumbula, Amanda Beaudoin, Susan Kline
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global public health threat, especially in developing countries, where limited antimicrobial stewardship programs contribute to inconsistent prescribing practices for Caesarean section (CS) surgical prophylaxis. This study aimed to investigate physicians' knowledge and prescribing practices related to CS surgical antibiotic prophylaxis at Kawempe National Referral Hospital (KNRH) in Uganda.MethodsAn anonymous electronic survey was conducted among 200 prescribing healthcare providers, including obstetricians, gynecologists, residents, fellows, medical officers, and interns involved in CS procedures over 4 months in 2021. The survey assessed demographic characteristics, knowledge of AMR, stewardship practices, and adherence to CS antibiotic prophylaxis guidelines. Participation was voluntary and confidential. Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 27, with a p‐value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsThe most commonly chosen antibiotic was ceftriaxone, followed by metronidazole, either alone or in combination, both of which are not recommended according to World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines for CS prophylaxis. Most antibiotics were administered 15 to 60 min prior to surgery, which is also not in alignment with recommendations. Overall, the study revealed limited compliance with national and international guidelines on antibiotic prescription for CS prophylaxis among healthcare professionals, particularly regarding antibiotic choice, timing of administration, frequency, and dosage.ConclusionThe study reveals major gaps between recommended and actual antimicrobial prophylaxis practices for CS at KNRH. Despite clinicians’ awareness of AMR, systemic barriers such as poor microbiological testing and reliance on peer advice hinder adherence. These findings reflect on the importance of policy development and the implementation of antimicrobial stewardship programs in Uganda to improve prescribing practices among healthcare providers.Keywords: antimicrobial prophylaxis, antimicrobial resistance, Caesarean‐section, healthcare, infections, stewardship

Introduction

Caesarean section (CS) is one of the most common surgeries worldwide, accounting for about 21% of all births [1, 2]. In many low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), CS rates have risen due factors like increased maternal age, obstetric complications, and improved access to surgical care [3, 4]. Nevertheless, CS remains an important source of morbidity and mortality for both mothers and infants, with infections being a common complication [5, 6]. Antimicrobial prophylaxis is essential to reduce the risk of surgical site infections (SSI) and other postoperative morbidities [7]. Standardized antibiotic protocols are critical for lowering maternal infections [8, 9], shortening hospital stays, reducing healthcare costs, and improving patient recovery [10]. Evidence suggests adherence to these guidelines promotes optimal surgical outcomes, patient safety, and care quality [11].

Antimicrobial agents are widely used as prophylaxis for CS procedures to prevent infections caused by microbial contamination during surgery or postoperatively at the surgical site [7]. SSIs following caesarean deliveries can lead to longer hospital stays, additional surgical interventions, and increased maternal morbidity and mortality [12]. As SSIs are the second leading cause of maternal deaths in LMICs, including Uganda, this underscores the need for effective antimicrobial prophylaxis, especially in areas with limited resources and fragile health systems [13, 14].

Guidelines on antimicrobial prophylaxis for CS have been issued by professional bodies such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) [7, 15]. These recommend a single prophylactic antibiotic dose administered within 60 min of incision to reduce infection risk [16]. A first‐generation cephalosporin, such as cefazolin, is most recommended due to its activity against key CS pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Streptococcus species [17]. Alternatives such as clindamycin or vancomycin are recommended for patients with penicillin allergies or specific risk factors [18]. Although the availability of these guidelines, antimicrobial use in CS varies widely. Antimicrobial prescribing in obstetrics encompasses both initiating effective antibiotics during CS surgery and preventing postoperative infections with postsurgical prescriptions [9, 19]. The selection and timing of prophylactic antibiotics are influenced by factors such as local resistance patterns, medication availability, clinician knowledge, and institutional policies [15]. In Uganda and many LMICs, limited resources, inconsistent training, and systemic challenges hinder guideline implementation, leading to deviations from evidence‐based practices [20].

The rising threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a pressing global health issue, particularly in LMICs burdened by high rates of infectious diseases (IDs) and inappropriate antibiotic use [21]. AMR complicates infection management and limits the effectiveness of common antibiotics used for surgical prophylaxis [22]. In Western Uganda, AMR, particularly against E. coli and S. aureus, significantly reduces the efficacy of antimicrobial prophylaxis for CS [23]. Studies show that up to 40% of isolates from surgical infections in obstetric patients are resistant to first‐line antibiotics like cefazolin, the recommended prophylactic for CS [23]. This issue is compounded by weak diagnostic capacity, limited testing access, and the inappropriate or overuse of antimicrobials in clinical and community settings [24, 25]. As AMR continues to increase, local surveillance of resistance patterns is crucial to guide treatment decisions [26]. In Uganda, a country facing a growing burden of IDs, monitoring antimicrobial susceptibility rates in surgical settings, especially for CS operations, is critical for optimizing antimicrobial prescribing and reducing the burden of resistant infections [27].

Kawempe National Referral Hospital (KNRH) in Kampala, Uganda, is a leading facility for neonatal and maternal health care. As a teaching and referral facility, it influences clinical practices and antimicrobial prescribing for CS surgery. As the only national referral hospital in the region, KNRH faces challenges like shortages of essential medicines, limited diagnostic services, and high patient volumes [28]. Although research on antimicrobial prescribing in Uganda is limited, evidence suggests deviations from guidelines, such as late antibiotic administrations and inappropriate drug choices for CS prophylaxis [29]. One study found that antibiotics were often given beyond the recommended timing or were inadequate [13]. At KNRH, prescribing practices vary based on clinician experience, hospital guidelines, and drug availability [30]. For example, although cefazolin is considered the first‐line agent, clinicians may decide on broader‐spectrum antibiotics or combination therapy depending on clinical experience or available resources. The absence of standardized protocols at some hospitals contributes to inconsistent practices, complicating care standardization [31]. Understanding current prescribing patterns at KNRH is necessary to identify gaps and improve maternal and neonatal outcomes.

To conclude, antimicrobial prophylaxis for cesarean deliveries is crucial in Uganda, where infection rates remain high. While guidelines exist, prescribing practices depend on local resistance patterns, resources, and clinical factors. KNRH, with one of the highest CS caseloads in Uganda [32], is an ideal setting to assess antimicrobial prescribing practices. This study aims to provide insights into antimicrobial stewardship in LMICs, inform practices to reduce SSIs, and improve maternal health. Ongoing assessment of local resistance, guideline adherence, and the impact of education and resources on prescribing at KNRH will be critical to improving clinical practice and maternal and neonatal health outcomes in Uganda.

Methodology

Study Design

A cross‐sectional survey was administered electronically and anonymously to 200 healthcare providers at KNRH in Kampala, Uganda, from August to November 2021. Participants included obstetricians, gynecologists, residents, fellows, medical officers, and interns. The hospital's labor ward attends to an average of 80 to 100 mothers daily and approximately 2500 births per month, roughly 30,000 annually, of which 20 to 25 mothers undergo CS each day. KNRH employs around 600 staff members, including midwives and nurses of all levels, intern doctors, medical officers, obstetricians, gynecologists, Senior House Officers (SHOs)/residents, fellows, visiting specialists, consultants, and support staff. The study focused on healthcare providers, including physicians, medical officers, obstetricians, gynecologists, residents/SHOs, fellows, and interns with prescribing privileges who are directly involved in the care of patients undergoing CSs.

Eligibility Criteria

A purposive sampling technique was employed to recruit a representative sample of staff involved in antibiotic prescribing and administration, ensuring representation across roles and experience levels. Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure representation across roles and experience levels. Inclusion criteria included healthcare professionals involved in the perioperative care of patients undergoing CSs, direct participation in CS procedures or perioperative antibiotic administration, and willingness to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria included staff not directly involved in perioperative care or antibiotic administration, or those unwilling to provide informed consent.

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

The sample size of 200 was obtained using the Yamane formula (1967) [33], based on a population of 600 eligible prescribers at KNRH, with a 5% margin of error and a 95% confidence interval. Simple random sampling was used to select respondents from an email list of medical personnel in the Obstetrics unit at KNRH, obtained from the Human Resource department, including physicians, medical officers, obstetricians, gynecologists, residents/SHOs, fellows, and interns.

Survey Instrument

Surveys on AMR and stewardship strategies were conducted. They included questions on demographic information, such as sex, years of practice, designation, medical school attended, number of CSs performed, and length of practice. Additionally, the survey assessed knowledge of the importance of combating AMR, perceived barriers and facilitators to implementing stewardship programs, current resources employed for antibiotic prescribing, and antimicrobial stewardship practices. To determine heterogeneity in practice and adherence to local prescribing guidelines, the surveys also addressed standard practices for surgical antibiotic prophylaxis, including timing and route of administration, antibiotic choice and spectrum, chart documentation, and factors influencing these decisions.

Data Collection

A self‐administered survey was conducted among physicians, obstetricians, gynecologists, residents, fellows, and interns involved in prescribing antibiotics. The questionnaire aimed to explore providers' perceived barriers and facilitators to participating in antimicrobial stewardship activities, as well as adherence to national standard treatment guidelines for CS surgical antibiotic prophylaxis. It provided essential information to assess current prescribing practices, knowledge of AMR and stewardship, and potential barriers and opportunities for improving antimicrobial stewardship programs. The survey was administered electronically via Qualtrics, hosted by the University of Minnesota, using an email list of medical personnel from the Obstetrics unit at KNRH obtained from the Human Resources department. The survey initially targeted 220 responses, and data collection continued until 200 responses were received, at which point the survey was closed. Study responses were not linked to participant identifiers. At the beginning of the survey, participants were informed that participation was voluntary and that all responses would remain anonymous.

Ethical Considerations

Ethical clearance for this study and its associated tools was obtained from both the School of Medicine Research Ethics Committee (SOMREC) at Makerere University, College of Health Sciences (REC REF 2020‐177), and the University of Minnesota (STUDY00006726). Administrative clearance was also obtained from Kawempe National Referral Hospital. All stages of the study involving human participants adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD), were used to summarize participants' sociodemographic characteristics, knowledge, and prescribing practices related to CS antibiotic prophylaxis. Multiple response analyses identified the most commonly prescribed antibiotics and sources influencing prescribing behavior. Associations between categorical variables, such as years of practice and antibiotic therapy choice, were assessed using Chi‐square (χ²) tests, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (two‐tailed). Spearman's rank‐order correlation was used to evaluate relationships between confidence in antibiotic prescribing and adherence to guideline‐recommended practices. Perceived barriers and facilitators to rational antibiotic use were analyzed using mean scores from Likert‐scale responses. Missing data were minimal (< 2%) and were handled using listwise deletion; results were reported as valid percentages.

Results

Demographic Characteristics

Of the 200 respondents, two‐thirds were male, and the remainder were female. The age range of the respondents was 22−56 years, with the majority aged 20−29 years. Most participants were medical officers, senior house officers, chief residents, intern doctors, and specialists. Most respondents had 1−3 years of practice experience (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1).

Antibiotic Choice and Dosage

The most commonly used antibiotic for CS surgical prophylaxis was ceftriaxone, followed by metronidazole. More than half of the respondents reported administering ceftriaxone (54.5%), followed by metronidazole (24.8%), ampicillin/sulbactam (4.2%), and gentamicin (3.5%). The majority indicated that the most frequently prescribed dosages were 2 g of ceftriaxone alone, 2 g of ceftriaxone combined with 500 mg of metronidazole, and 1 g of ceftriaxone alone. Figure 1 illustrates the dosage patterns for CS surgical prophylaxis: 55.4% of respondents recommended 2000 mg of ceftriaxone, 12.57% reported using 2 g of ceftriaxone with 500 mg of metronidazole, and 8.0% administered 1 g of ceftriaxone. These findings indicate that most respondents used broad‐spectrum agents, such as ceftriaxone, which are not recommended for CS surgical prophylaxis according to WHO and national guidelines, which advocate for narrow‐spectrum cephalosporins such as cefazolin [15]. Table S1 presents the reported levels of confidence among healthcare workers in prescribing antibiotics during CS surgical prophylaxis, indicating that the majority of respondents expressed being either confident or very confident in their prescribing practices. Additionally, Table S2 summarizes respondents’ perceptions of AMR and their level of agreement with and support for antimicrobial stewardship programs. The majority of respondents demonstrated a high level of AMR awareness and expressed strong agreement with the need for and support of stewardship initiatives.

Timing, Frequency, and Combination of Antibiotic Administration

The majority of respondents (44.7%) indicated that they administer antibiotics 15 to 60 min before the operation. A single antimicrobial agent was used by 51% of respondents, while 48.6% reported using a combination of agents. Regarding frequency, 82 respondents (45.8%) administered antibiotics once daily (QD), while 51 (28.5%), 29 (16.2%), 15 (8.4%), and 2 (1.1%) administered a single one‐time dose, three times daily (TID), twice daily (BID), and four times daily (QID), respectively. Once‐daily administration (QD) was the most preferred regimen, followed by a single one‐time dose. The proportions of respondents using a single antibiotic and those using combination therapy were nearly equal. Most respondents indicated that they preferred administering antibiotics 15 to 60 min before surgery. Confidence in Antibiotic Prescription

Additional information on respondents’ confidence in antibiotic prescription for CS surgical prophylaxis, measured on a 4‐point scale (1 = low confidence, 4 = high confidence), is presented in Figure 3A and Table S1. The mean confidence in choosing the correct antibiotic, route, dose, duration, and frequency of administration was the highest (M = 3.40, SD = 0.78), indicating a relatively high level of confidence in this domain. This was followed by confidence in using combination therapy (M = 3.24, SD = 0.80), interpreting microbiological results (M = 3.17, SD = 0.85), and transitioning from broad‐spectrum to narrow‐spectrum antibiotic therapy, which showed the lowest confidence (M = 3.15, SD = 0.82). The standard deviations suggest moderate variability in confidence levels across all domains, ranging from 0.78 to 0.85, indicating a relatively consistent spread of responses.

Resources and Factors Influencing Antibiotic Selection

The majority of respondents are in agreement with the high‐risk factors for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) associated with CS prophylaxis, as presented in Table S3. Additionally, Table S4 presents Spearman's correlation analysis, which revealed a moderate, significant positive association between confidence in selecting antibiotics and confidence in switching to a single agent, while no significant correlations were found between confidence measures and guideline‐adherent practices such as timing or antibiotic agent selection.

Resources that can improve antibiotic prescribing among healthcare practitioners include the availability of guidelines and restrictions on the use of certain antibiotic classes, among others. Factors influencing antibiotic choice include national guidelines, workshops, seminars, Continuing Medical Education (CME), and advice from senior colleagues. Figure 3B presents the resources that improve antibiotic prescribing practices and the factors influencing antibiotic selection for CS surgical prophylaxis. National/WHO guidelines, policies, and protocols (65.7%), along with hospital guidelines, policies, and protocols (65.7%), were the most frequently cited sources influencing prescribing decisions. These were followed by knowledge acquired during medical school training (64.6%); advice from senior colleagues or previous experience (58.0%)’ workshops, conferences, and CME (48.6%); textbooks and articles (42.0%); the internet (33.1%); advice from microbiologist (29.3%); and advice from ID specialists (9.9%). Notably, local resistance patterns (0.6%) and information from antibiotic manufacturers (0.6%) were rarely reported as influencing factors. Several risk factors for AMR were cited as shown in Table S4. The majority of the participants recognized AMR as not only a national but also a global problem and demonstrated an urgent need for antimicrobial stewardship programs and their willingness to participate in them, as shown in Table S2.

Discussion

This study explored physicians’ knowledge and prescribing practices concerning surgical antibiotic prophylaxis for CS at KNRH in Uganda. The results revealed concerning gaps between recommended guidelines and actual practices. Although most participants recognized AMR and were confident in their antibiotic prescribing decisions, they frequently selected regimens that deviated from national and WHO guidelines. Broad‐spectrum antibiotics, especially ceftriaxone and metronidazole, were the most frequently reported agents, often used in combination, while narrow‐spectrum antibiotics recommended for routine CS prophylaxis were rarely prescribed. The timing of administration was typically 15 to 60 min before surgery, suggesting partial adherence to guideline recommendations. However, both frequency and dosage patterns did not align with the single‐dose regimens recommended by the WHO. Limited institutional support, including the absence of antimicrobial stewardship programs and microbiological testing, further hindered guideline adherence.

In this study, the predominant use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics likely reflected a combination of availability, perceived effectiveness, and institutional practice patterns rather than evidence‐based decision‐making. Although ceftriaxone and metronidazole are effective agents [34, 35, 36], they are not recommended as standard prophylaxis antibiotics for uncomplicated CS due to concerns about stimulating AMR [37]. The widespread use of combination therapy by nearly half of the respondents underscores this concern. Despite these deviations, respondents indicated relatively high levels of confidence in selecting antibiotics, determining dosages, and timing administration. This finding suggests that confidence in prescribing does not necessarily correlate with adherence to guidelines, indicating that other conditions, including institutional culture, supply constraints, and recommendations from peers, may influence decision‐making. The moderate variability in confidence scores suggests a generally consistent perception of self‐efficacy across respondents, regardless of actual prescribing practices.

Knowledge and awareness of guidelines were at a modest level, insufficient to support appropriate practice. Less than half of respondents reported being aware of WHO or national recommendations, and only a minority routinely consulted formal guidelines when prescribing. Reliance on peer suggestions, previous experience, and readily available information, such as hospital policies or personal notes, often replaced guideline consultation. Although awareness of AMR was high, this knowledge did not always result in rational prescribing. The lack of up‐to‐date microbiological data and local antibiograms further limited clinicians’ ability to tailor antibiotic choices to local resistance patterns. These findings illustrate the complex interplay between knowledge, system support, and habitual practice in shaping prescribing behavior.

These findings are consistent with previous studies from LMICs, where inappropriate CS prophylaxis is commonly practiced [38, 39]. Studies from Nigeria and Ethiopia have reported frequent use of broad‐spectrum antibiotics, inadequate adherence to timing and dosage guidelines, and reliance on informal sources for prescribing decisions [40, 41]. Even in high‐income countries (HICs), non‐adherence to guidelines has also been observed, although less frequently, often due to differences in institutional oversight and the availability of stewardship programs [24]. Collectively, these findings highlight a global challenge: translating awareness of guidelines into consistent, evidence‐based practice remains difficult, particularly when resources are constrained.

The impact of these results is significant. The overuse of broad‐spectrum antibiotics increases the risk of AMR, which can negatively affect patient outcomes, both within hospitals and throughout the community [42, 43]. Non‐adherence to recommended timing, dosage, and frequency may lower the effectiveness of prophylaxis and increase the risk of postoperative infections, longer hospital stays, and higher healthcare costs [44, 45]. The gap between knowledge, confidence, and practice highlights the need for interventions that address both patient‐specific and systems‐level barriers. Educational initiatives alone may be insufficient; targeted strategies involving guideline dissemination, antimicrobial stewardship programs, microbiological support, and audit‐feedback may be necessary to ensure a rational approach to antibiotic use.

Limitation of the Study

There are a few limitations that should be considered. First, the findings are based on self‐reported data, which may be subject to recall and social desirability bias. Direct observation or prescription audits could have provided a more objective assessment of actual prescribing practices. Second, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to infer causal relationships between knowledge, confidence, institutional support, and prescribing behaviors. Third, the study was conducted at a single tertiary hospital, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other healthcare settings in Uganda or similar low‐resource contexts. Additionally, the absence of medical record validation, microbiological data (such as antibiogram reports), and limited involvement of infectious disease consultations may have excluded important contextual factors influencing prescribing behavior. Finally, a few incomplete survey responses resulted in minor data gaps; however, these were not expected to significantly affect the overall conclusions.

Recommendations

Several recommendations can be derived from the findings of this study. First, targeted educational opportunities focusing on guideline knowledge, emphasizing appropriate antibiotic selection, dosing, and timing, could be implemented. Programs such as CME modules, workshops, or guideline summaries provided at the beginning of clinical rounds may facilitate uptake. Second, it is essential to implement or strengthen antimicrobial stewardship programs, including regular audits, feedback, and monitoring outcomes focused on postoperative infections and resistance patterns to reinforce adherence to evidence‐based practice. Third, improving access to microbiology testing and local antibiograms would enable clinicians to select prophylaxis based on local resistance patterns. Fourth, promoting interdisciplinary collaboration, including consultation with microbiologists and ID specialists, could narrow the gap between confidence and appropriate practice. Finally, future studies should adopt longitudinal or observational designs to assess the effects of interventions on prescribing patterns, antibiotic resistance, and patient outcomes.

Conclusions

This study identifies significant discrepancies between recommended and actual antimicrobial prophylaxis practices for CS in Uganda. Despite clinicians’ strong confidence and awareness of AMR, gaps remain in antibiotic selection, timing, and documentation. Systematic barriers, such as limited access to microbiological testing and overreliance on peer advice, hinder optimal prescribing. However, interest in stewardship initiatives provides opportunities for improvement. Strengthening institutional guidelines, expanding medical education, and fostering teamwork could drive progress toward rational antimicrobial use in surgical settings in Uganda and other LMICs.

The gaps between guidelines and clinical practice highlight the need for key recommendations. Antimicrobial stewardship programs should be established with multidisciplinary teams to ensure sustained impact. Standardized protocols for cefazolin use, single‐dose administration before incision, and adherence to a 60‐min timing window should be integrated into surgical routines. Standardized forms and electronic tools for antibiotic prescribing could reduce incomplete documentation and variability. Educational interventions should target junior, mid‐career, and senior staff. Additionally, regular auditing and feedback will be essential to track compliance and improve accountability.
