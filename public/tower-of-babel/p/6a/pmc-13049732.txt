Predictive value of the clinical frailty scale for intensive care unit mortality in elderly and very elderly patients: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Fatih Seğmen, Cihangir Doğu, Hakan Sarıcı, Çilem Bayındır Di̇cle, Temel Kayan, Onur Küçük, Semih Aydemi̇r
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimThe Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) is a clinician-rated tool used to assess frailty. Previous studies have shown that it predicts mortality and morbidity in older adults aged ≥ 65 years. However, its predictive value in the very elderly population (≥ 80 years) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the ability of CFS to predict intensive care unit (ICU) mortality among elderly patients and to determine whether this relationship differs in the very elderly subgroup.Materials and methodsThis prospective cohort study was conducted after ethical approval. All patients aged ≥ 65 years admitted to the ICU and staying > 48 h during the study period were included when frailty assessment was feasible. CFS scores at ICU admission were recorded, and patients were categorized as survivors or non-survivors. Subgroup analyses were performed for elderly patients aged 65–79 years (Group E) and very elderly patients aged ≥ 80 years (Group VE). Multiple logistic regression analysis was applied to identify independent predictors of ICU mortality, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to assess model performance. Relative risk (RR) for mortality according to age subgroups was calculated for CFS categories.ResultsA total of 173 patients were included (Group E: 96 [55.5%]). The mean CFS score was 6.2 ± 1.8 (median: Group E = 6, Group VE = 7; p = 0.066), and the mean Charlson Comorbidity Index was 8.3 ± 2.5 (p = 0.406 between groups). The mean ICU stay was 21 ± 24 days. Overall, 102 patients (59%) were discharged, while 71 (41%) died. CFS, renal replacement therapy (RRT), APACHE II, lymphocyte count, procalcitonin, and albumin levels were independent predictors of ICU mortality. CFS and RRT had the greatest contributions to the model (χ² = 16.32 and 20.36, respectively). The model demonstrated 77.5% sensitivity, 85.3% specificity, and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.90. When considered alone, CFS yielded an AUC of 0.74 with an optimal cutoff of ≥ 7 (sensitivity 77.4%, specificity 56.8%). ICU mortality risk was 2.5-fold higher for all patients with CFS ≥ 7, 2.3-fold in Group E, and 2.8-fold in Group VE (p < 0.05).ConclusionCFS independently predicts ICU mortality among elderly patients. A CFS ≥ 7 and the need for RRT identify a high-risk subgroup, emphasizing the importance of routine frailty assessment and early individualized management in this population.Keywords: Elderly, Very elderly, Geriatrics, Clinical frailty scale, Intensive care, Mortality

Introduction

The global proportion of individuals aged ≥ 65 years has increased substantially over recent decades, and this demographic shift is expected to continue [1]. As a consequence, intensive care units (ICUs) increasingly care for older and very old adults, while resource limitations and workforce constraints challenge the expansion of critical care capacity [2]. In this setting, ICU admission decisions for very elderly patients remain controversial due to high costs and potentially unfavorable outcomes [3, 4].

Although age, comorbidities, and admission severity contribute to mortality risk, chronological age alone does not adequately reflect heterogeneity in physiological reserve among older adults, and conventional severity-of-illness scores may perform suboptimally in this population [5, 6]. ICU triage and escalation decisions are therefore often individualized and may be influenced by subjective judgment [7, 8]. In this context, geriatric-specific constructs that reflect baseline vulnerability may help quantify heterogeneity among older adults and improve risk stratification [6, 8].

Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome characterized by diminished physiological and cognitive reserve and increased vulnerability to stressors, and it is distinct from disability or comorbidity [9, 10]. Frailty assessment has clinical relevance because frail patients are more likely to experience adverse outcomes including functional decline, poorer quality of life, hospitalization, and death [11, 12]. In critically ill older adults, frailty is common and has been associated with higher short- and long-term mortality and worse recovery trajectories [13–16].

Studies focusing on very elderly ICU patients generally report low survival rates and poor post-discharge outcomes [17, 18]. Besides chronological age, determinants of poor outcomes include baseline functional status, frailty, primary diagnosis, and comorbidity burden [6, 17]. However, most studies have evaluated frailty broadly in geriatric populations (≥ 65 years), and data specifically addressing the prognostic impact of frailty in very elderly ICU patients (≥ 80 years) remain limited [6, 17, 18].

Therefore, this prospective cohort study aimed to evaluate the ability of the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) to predict ICU mortality in patients aged ≥ 65 years and to explore whether this relationship differs in very elderly patients (≥ 80 years).

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective cohort study was conducted in the third-level ICUs of the Department of Anesthesiology and Reanimation at Ankara Bilkent City Hospital between March 2024 and September 2024. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Ethics Committee (date: 27/03/2024; approval no: TABED 1/126/2024). All procedures conformed to the ethical standards of the responsible institutional and national committees on human experimentation and to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (revised 2013). The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Patient population

All patients aged ≥ 65 years who were admitted to the ICU and hospitalized for > 48 h were eligible, provided that frailty status could be reliably obtained from the patient or a proxy (first-degree relative/caregiver). Written informed consent was obtained from each participant or a first-degree relative.

Exclusion criteria were ICU stay < 48 h, lack of consent, refusal to participate, admission after poisoning or postoperative surgical procedures, and death within 48 h of ICU admission. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only data from the first admission were analyzed. Clinical data were obtained from physical and electronic records, patients, care teams, and relatives or caregivers. No interventions were applied or modified during the ICU stay.

Patients were classified into two age-based subgroups: elderly (65–79 years; Group E) and very elderly (≥ 80 years; Group VE).

Data collection and variables

Patients who met the inclusion criteria and remained in the ICU for more than 48 h were included in the analysis. Data were prospectively recorded on standardized forms until ICU discharge or death.

Demographic and clinical variables included age, sex, weight, height, body mass index (BMI), admission source (emergency department, ward, palliative care, or off-site ICU), comorbidities, malignancy, and any ICU or hospital admission within the preceding 3 months. At admission, the Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), CFS, and Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were documented; the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score was calculated 24 h after admission. Body weight and height used for BMI calculation were obtained from the hospital electronic medical record. When direct measurement at ICU admission was not feasible, the most recently documented pre-admission values and/or patient/proxy-reported values were used according to routine clinical practice.

Clinical outcomes recorded were ICU length of stay, readmission, use of mechanical ventilation or inotropic support, renal replacement therapy (RRT), and transfusion of blood products. Laboratory parameters at admission included complete blood count, white-blood-cell and lymphocyte counts, urea, creatinine, total protein, albumin, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein (CRP), international normalized ratio (INR), and prothrombin time. The presence of a pressure ulcer at admission and the development of a new ulcer during ICU stay were also noted.

RRT initiation and modality selection: RRT was initiated according to Kidney Disease Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO)-based routine ICU practice, with urgent initiation in the presence of life-threatening derangements in fluid, electrolyte, or acid–base balance, and otherwise based on the overall clinical context and trends in laboratory parameters rather than single blood urea nitrogen or creatinine thresholds [19]. In practice, RRT was considered when one or more of the following indications occurred: refractory hyperkalemia, severe metabolic acidosis, diuretic-resistant fluid overload/pulmonary edema, and/or persistent oliguria/anuria with progressive azotemia accompanied by clinical deterioration, consistent with criteria commonly used in major ICU acute kidney injury (AKI) trials [20]. Intermittent hemodialysis (IHD) was delivered as scheduled intermittent sessions, whereas continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was performed as bedside continuous therapy in the ICU, with modality choice guided primarily by hemodynamic stability and clinical judgment.

Frailty assessment

Frailty was evaluated using the Canadian Study of Health and Aging CFS, a 9-point tool ranging from very fit “1” to terminally ill “9” [11]. Patients scoring 1–3 were classified as robust, 4 as vulnerable, and ≥ 5 as frail [21]. Trained research coordinators assessed CFS scores through direct patient interviews or, when necessary, by interviewing proxy decision-makers.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the predictive value of the CFS for ICU mortality among patients aged ≥ 65 years.

Secondary analyses (prespecified) included: (1) the prevalence of frailty in the cohort, (2) comparison of frailty prevalence between elderly and very elderly groups, (3) evaluation of the association between CFS and ICU mortality within each age subgroup, and (4) determination of the optimal CFS cutoff for identifying a high-risk group.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using Jamovi software (Version 2.3.28; Sydney, Australia). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (𝑥̄ ± SD) or median (IQR); categorical variables as number (n) and percentage (%). Distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Parametric data were compared using the Student’s t test and non-parametric data using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed with Pearson’s χ² or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Univariate analyses were performed to compare survivors and non-survivors at ICU discharge. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated to evaluate the discriminative ability of the CFS for mortality, and the optimal cutoff was used to define the high-risk CFS category. Relative risk (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was calculated.

Variables showing significance in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariate logistic regression model. Seventeen variables were initially considered. Multicollinearity was assessed using correlation matrices, variance inflation factors, and tolerance values. When high correlations existed, the most clinically relevant variable was retained. Model adequacy was confirmed by inspection of residuals and Cook’s distance values. The likelihood-ratio (LR) test assessed each variable’s contribution (variables with χ² < 1 were excluded).

The final model included nine predictors: APACHE II, CFS, invasive-mechanical-ventilation duration, RRT, blood-product transfusion, lymphocyte count, INR, albumin, and procalcitonin. Model fit was verified by the omnibus test (p < 0.001) and demonstrated good explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.619).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 173 patients aged ≥ 65 years who remained in the ICU for at least 48 h were included. Among them, 130 (75.1%) were admitted from the emergency department, 41 (23.7%) from the ward (2 [1.2%] from a palliative care unit), and 2 (1.2%) from another ICU. Ninety-six patients (55.5%) were aged 65–79 years (Group E) and 77 (44.5%) were aged ≥ 80 years (Group VE). The mean age was 78 ± 8 years, and the mean body mass index was 23.3 ± 3.8 kg/m². Females comprised 50.9% (n = 88) of the cohort. Thirty-seven patients (21.4%) had been admitted to an ICU within the previous 3 months, and 108 (62.4%) had a recent hospital admission. The mean GCS was 10.6 ± 3.5, APACHE II = 22.6 ± 7.5, SOFA = 6.8 ± 2.7, CFS = 6.2 ± 1.8, and CCI = 8.3 ± 2.5.

The most frequent comorbid conditions were chronic heart disease (53.8%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (37.0%), dementia (33.5%), and cerebrovascular disease (28.3%). Laboratory findings and interventions

Baseline laboratory findings at ICU admission are presented in Table 1. Overall, inflammatory markers were elevated, and hypoalbuminemia was frequently observed.

Mechanical ventilation was required in 101 patients (58.4%), with a mean duration of 11 ± 23 days. Vasoactive inotropic support was administered to 105 patients (60.7%) for a mean of 7 ± 15 days. RRT was used in 55 patients (31.8%). Among the study population, 29 patients (16.7%) received IHD and 40 patients (23.1%) received CRRT. Fourteen patients required both IHD and CRRT during their ICU course. In the IHD group, the median number of sessions was 4, whereas the median CRRT duration was 2 days. During ICU stay, 21 patients (12.1%) developed new pressure ulcers, 21 (12.1%) had gastrointestinal bleeding, and 91 (52.6%) received blood product transfusions. The mean ICU length of stay was 21 ± 24 days. Overall, 102 patients (59%) were discharged, while 71 (41%) died. Among discharged patients, 8 (7.8%) required ICU readmission within 28 days.

Factors associated with frailty

Overall, 134 patients (77.5%) were classified as frail (CFS ≥ 5). Comparisons between frail and non-frail patients are summarized in Table 1. Frailty was associated with older age, lower BMI and GCS, higher APACHE II, SOFA, and CCI scores, presence of pressure ulcers, sepsis, longer mechanical ventilation and inotrope duration, blood transfusion, elevated leukocyte count, and higher CRP and procalcitonin levels.

In multivariate analysis, age, CCI, and presence of decubitus ulcers remained independently associated with frailty (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the CFS is strongly and independently associated with mortality among elderly and very elderly ICU patients. A CFS score ≥ 7 significantly increased ICU mortality in both the 65–79-year and ≥ 80-year subgroups. As frailty severity increased, the need for organ support and the incidence of adverse clinical outcomes also rose. In multivariate regression, the CFS retained an independent association with mortality after adjustment for age, comorbidity burden, and acute-illness severity scores (APACHE II and SOFA), confirming frailty as a distinct biological determinant of critical illness trajectory.

Frailty represents a state of multisystem reserve loss and impaired stress response that cannot be explained by comorbidity or chronological age alone [9–11]. Rockwood and colleagues conceptualized frailty as an integrated reflection of functional independence, cognition, and comorbidities, validating the CFS as a practical bedside measure [11]. Fried and colleagues similarly described frailty as a “fragile physiological equilibrium,” emphasizing that it is not an inevitable consequence of aging but a pathological process [10]. The strong predictive power of the CFS in our cohort supports this distinction in critically ill patients.

Consistent with earlier multicenter studies, we found that frailty was a major determinant of both survival and clinical outcomes in the ICU [13, 14]. Le Maguet et al. demonstrated that frailty predicted short-term mortality and post-discharge functional dependence in older ICU patients [13]. Bagshaw et al. reported that frailty also influences long-term quality of life and functional recovery [14, 15]. The novelty of our study lies in the prospective, real-time CFS assessment at ICU admission with prespecified analyses in elderly (65–79 years) and very elderly (≥ 80 years) patients, demonstrating that CFS remained independently associated with ICU mortality after adjustment for acute severity (APACHE II/SOFA) and organ-support variables. We also identified a practical high-risk threshold (CFS ≥ 7) across age strata, enabling bedside-applicable early risk stratification. Notably, frailty and the need for RRT were the strongest contributors in the multivariable model, suggesting that baseline vulnerability (high CFS) and advanced organ dysfunction (RRT requirement) represent complementary dimensions of risk. Collectively, these findings support incorporating the CFS as a complementary prognostic marker at ICU admission.

Although the association between frailty and mortality persisted across age strata, the effect size was greater among patients aged ≥ 80 years. Previous literature has shown that chronological age alone does not account for mortality differences; biological reserve and stress-response capacity are stronger determinants [9, 22]. Older patients often receive ICU care in the absence of adequate palliative-care access, which may lead to excessive use of limited healthcare resources [23]. Clegg et al. highlighted that two individuals of identical chronological age may experience completely different clinical trajectories [9]. Consequently, accumulating evidence favors the notion that frailty, rather than age, is the more accurate prognostic factor [6, 22]. Our results reinforce this view, showing a steeper rise in mortality with higher frailty levels among the very elderly. These findings underline the need for individualized decisions regarding ICU admission, treatment intensity, and discharge goals, guided by frailty status rather than age alone. Such an approach could enhance both the appropriateness of care and resource efficiency.

The identification of a CFS ≥ 7 threshold is clinically meaningful. This cutoff achieved a balanced sensitivity-specificity profile in our cohort and may serve as a practical risk-stratification tool. Although optimal thresholds vary among populations, higher CFS values consistently correlate with poorer prognosis in critical illness [13, 14, 22, 24]. From a clinical perspective, a CFS ≥ 7 can be used as an early warning marker to identify patients at very high risk of adverse ICU trajectories. While frailty itself is not rapidly reversible, recognizing severe frailty at admission can support a more individualized care strategy, including closer monitoring for early organ dysfunction, proactive delirium prevention, early mobilization and physiotherapy when feasible, and nutritional assessment and optimization. In addition, systematic documentation of frailty may improve communication and shared decision-making with families regarding expected recovery potential, proportionality of invasive support, and timely involvement of multidisciplinary teams (e.g., geriatrics, rehabilitation, and palliative care) when appropriate.

The biological basis of the frailty–mortality relationship likely involves sarcopenia, immune senescence, chronic inflammation, and reduced metabolic reserve [9, 15]. These processes become pronounced during critical illness, when catabolism and organ failure accelerate. In this context, the finding that RRT remained an independent predictor of ICU mortality alongside frailty suggests that frail individuals may decompensate more severely once advanced organ failure develops. Moreover, patients requiring escalation to both IHD and CRRT in our cohort experienced very high mortality, supporting the notion that persistent or severe kidney dysfunction reflects profound systemic deterioration. Large randomized trials such as standard versus accelerated initiation of RRT in AKI (STARRT-AKI) and artificial kidney initiation in kidney injury (AKIKI) have highlighted variability in the timing and indications for initiating RRT and have not demonstrated consistent survival benefits with early initiation strategies [25, 26]; therefore, our real-world data emphasize that the need for RRT itself may represent a late and high-risk stage of critical illness, particularly among frail elderly ICU patients.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include its prospective design, real-time CFS assessment in clinical practice, and predefined age-subgroup analysis. However, its single-center nature and potential observer variability in CFS scoring limit generalizability. Additionally, long-term functional outcomes were not evaluated. Future multicenter studies with extended follow-up are warranted to validate these findings.

Conclusion

The CFS is not merely a descriptive tool but a fundamental prognostic indicator in elderly and very elderly ICU patients. A CFS ≥ 7 identified a practical high-risk group with substantially increased ICU mortality. Routine frailty assessment on ICU admission can support more proportionate treatment decisions, guide timely palliative-care referral, and enable early rehabilitation planning for patients with recovery potential.
