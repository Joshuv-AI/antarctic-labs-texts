Developing optimal sustainable menus: a guide based on university menus in Tehran Province
Authors: Kiyavash Irankhah, Karim Parastouei, Maryam Taghdir, Sepideh Abbaszadeh, Mojtaba Sepandi, Hosein Rostami, Mehdi Ghorbani Hesary
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPromoting sustainable food systems is essential to ensuring both public health and environmental protection. University dining halls, as institutional food environments, provide a strategic opportunity to implement and evaluate sustainable dietary practices.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess and optimize the sustainability of food menus in three military universities in Tehran by evaluating nutritional adequacy, environmental footprints (water and carbon), and economic cost.MethodsIn this cross-sectional analytical study, one-month food menus from three universities were analyzed. Nutrient content, cost, water footprint, and carbon emissions were calculated for each menu. Three optimization approaches—differing in menu design constraints—were developed and implemented using Linear Programming (LP) and Goal Programming (GP) to improve the sustainability of the menus while maintaining cultural acceptability.ResultsThe original menus were high in energy and fat, with considerable variation across universities. Optimization led to significant improvements, especially in the third approach, which combined menu reformulation and new food item integration. Compared to baseline, this approach reduced the water footprint by 30%, carbon emissions by 36%, and food cost by 32%, while increasing the Nutrient Rich Food (NRF) index by 25%. Micronutrient analysis confirmed that optimized menus provided adequate intake of key nutrients, including vitamin D, iron, calcium, and fiber, while significantly reducing sodium and saturated fat.ConclusionStrategic optimization of institutional food menus can substantially enhance nutritional quality, reduce environmental impact, and maintain affordability. Military universities and other public institutions can serve as platforms to promote sustainable eating habits and support national health and climate goals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24664-2.Keywords: Sustainable diet, University food menus, Carbon footprint, Water footprint, Nutrient optimization, Linear programming

Introduction

Sustainability has emerged as a guiding principle for development and progress in the 21 st century, encompassing the imperative to meet present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own [1]. At its core, sustainability seeks to balance economic growth, environmental health, and social well-being, ensuring a harmonious coexistence between humans and the planet [2, 3].

One of the most immediate and impactful areas where sustainability can be implemented is in our food systems [4, 5]. sustainable diet prioritizes the health of both people and the planet, addressing the urgent need to rethink how we produce, consume, and perceive food. At its core, a sustainable diet is characterized by its ability to meet nutritional needs while minimizing environmental impact, promoting biodiversity, and ensuring food security for future generations [4–6].

The modern food system faces numerous challenges, from the overexploitation of natural resources and the loss of biodiversity to greenhouse gas emissions and food waste. Agriculture is a major driver of these issues, contributing to deforestation, soil degradation, and water scarcity [7, 8]. Sustainable diets seek to mitigate these impacts by encouraging practices that support ecological balance and resource efficiency [7, 9].

A sustainable diet is typically plant-based, emphasizing fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds, which have lower environmental footprints compared to animal-based foods [10, 11]. Reducing meat and dairy consumption not only decreases greenhouse gas emissions but also conserves water and land resources. When animal products are included, sustainable diets advocate for choices that are produced under humane and environmentally friendly conditions [10, 12].

Adopting a sustainable diet offers numerous benefits beyond environmental impacts. It can lead to improved health outcomes, such as reduced risks of chronic diseases, better weight management, and enhanced overall well-being [13]. It also fosters a deeper connection with food, encouraging mindful eating practices and an appreciation for the sources and processes involved in food production [14, 15].

Universities play a pivotal role in advancing sustainability at both institutional and societal levels. As centers of knowledge, innovation, and social influence, universities have the unique capacity to model and promote sustainable behaviors among diverse populations, particularly students who represent the next generation of decision-makers, professionals, and global citizens [16]. University campuses function as microcosms of larger communities, offering opportunities to implement sustainable practices in dining services, food procurement, waste management, and curriculum development [17, 18]. By embedding sustainability into food systems on campus—through thoughtful menu design, sourcing practices, and educational initiatives—universities can serve as living laboratories that inspire widespread cultural and behavioral change. Their influence extends beyond students to faculty, staff, vendors, and surrounding communities, amplifying their potential as catalysts for a broader transformation toward sustainable living [19–21].

This article aims to bridge the gap between campus dining and sustainable living by guiding readers on how to craft sustainable food baskets inspired by university food menus. We will explore the key principles of a sustainable diet, including minimizing environmental impact, maintaining affordability, maximizing nutritional value, and ensuring cultural acceptability. Additionally, we will examine the typical components of a university menu, highlighting how these choices contribute to a more sustainable food system [6, 10].

Method

Food data

In this cross-sectional analytical study, the monthly food menus provided by three selected military universities in Tehran during the academic year 2024–2025 were analyzed. Information on the preparation methods and per capita consumption of each food item was obtained from the food service departments of the universities. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Baqiyatallah University of Medical Sciences (Approval ID: IR.BMSU.REC.1403.017).

Menu assessment

The analyzed monthly food menus from the three selected military universities in Tehran covered all main daily meals—breakfast, lunch, and dinner—throughout the academic month. At University 1, 41 breakfast menus, 96 lunch menus, and 76 dinner menus were evaluated. At University 2, a total of 14 menus each for breakfast, lunch, and dinner were assessed. At University 3, 24 breakfast menus, 14 lunch menus, and 24 dinner menus were analyzed. These datasets represented the full range of meals served during the study period. Each menu specified the per-capita quantity of every food item, enabling a comprehensive assessment of nutrient intake and sustainability indicators within the institutional food environment. Energy, macronutrient, and micronutrient intakes were estimated using the Iranian Food Composition Table [22] in combination with Nutritionist IV software. The sustainability of the menus was evaluated according to four key indicators: water footprint, carbon footprint, food cost, and nutritional value.

Water footprint

The water footprint refers to the amount of freshwater used to produce goods and services consumed by individuals or communities [23]. Reliable national data on the water footprint of foods in Iran were available [23, 24]. This measure is typically reported in cubic meters per ton (m³/ton) and was converted to cubic meters per gram (m³/g) for this study. The water footprint of each food item was calculated by multiplying the consumption amount by its specific water footprint.

Carbon footprint

The carbon footprint was used to estimate the amount of carbon dioxide emitted during the production of each food item. This indicator reflects the total direct and indirect CO₂ emissions throughout the product’s life cycle [25]. Carbon footprint data were obtained from the Barilla Center for Food and Nutrition (BCFN) Double Pyramid database [26].

Food cost

To assess the economic dimension of the menus, the total cost of each meal was calculated using data from the annually conducted Household Income and Expenditure Survey (HIES) by the Statistical Center of Iran [27]. This dataset includes the prices paid by households for individual food items. For each ingredient, the average price per gram was derived from HIES data and applied in the dietary models. The cost of each menu was then determined by multiplying the quantity (in grams) of each food item by its corresponding price per gram. This approach enabled the integration of economic considerations into the evaluation of more sustainable dietary patterns.

Nutritional value

The nutritional quality of the menus was evaluated using the Nutrient Rich Food 19.3 (NRF19.3) index, which measures nutrient density by balancing beneficial and limiting nutrients [28]. The development of the NRF19.3 score involves several methodological choices, including the selection of key nutrients, reference values (e.g., recommended daily allowances, RDA), and the calculation basis (per 100 g, per 100 kcal, or per portion) [29, 30].

In this study, the NRF19.3 was calculated per 100 g of food, based on 19 encouraged nutrients—namely protein, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFA), dietary fiber, potassium, vitamins A, C, D, E, B1 (thiamin), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B6, B12, folate, magnesium, calcium, iron, and zinc—and 3 nutrients to limit: saturated fat, sodium, and total sugars [29, 30]. The NRF score for each food item was obtained by subtracting the sum of negative scores from the positive ones. These scores were then adjusted according to the actual consumption amount of each item in the menus. The final NRF19.3 score for each menu was determined by summing the weighted scores of all food components.

The reference values used for nutrient evaluation were based on WHO/FAO recommended daily allowances [31–33]. Previous studies have also applied the NRF19.3 index to assess the nutritional quality of Iranian dietary patterns [34, 35].

Design and optimizing sustainable food menus

Each food item in the menus was optimized using linear programming (LP) and goal programming (GP) techniques, as detailed in the study by Eini-Zinab et al. [36]. In brief, LP models are composed of decision variables (i.e., food quantities), an objective function, and a set of constraints. The objective function defines the goal (e.g., maximizing nutritional value or minimizing environmental impact), while constraints establish the conditions that must be satisfied. In this study, the modeled menus were constrained to maintain the same total energy content and number of food items as the original menus to ensure comparability.

All optimization models were implemented using Microsoft Excel Solver (version 2020 ([37]. Decision variables in all models were the per-capita quantities (grams) of food items served in the menus. The overall modelling workflow applied to each of the three approaches was the same and comprised two steps:

Differences between the three modelling approaches:

Approach 1 — Rearrangement/repetition only: only the schedule/frequency of existing menu items was changed (re-ordering and repeating items); recipe compositions and ingredient proportions for each menu item were kept fixed and no new food items could be introduced. Decision variables primarily represented selection/frequency adjustments subject to portion bounds and the frequency constraint.

Approach 2 — Content modification of existing meals: quantities of ingredients and portion sizes within existing menu items were allowed to be adjusted (i.e., recipe-level modifications), but no new menu items were introduced. This gives more flexibility than Approach 1 for improving nutritional and environmental indicators while staying within the original food repertoire.

This gives more flexibility than Approach 1 for improving nutritional and environmental indicators while staying within the original food repertoire.

Approach 3 — Reformulation plus new items: existing recipes could be reformulated and a limited set of new, procurable food items could be added to the menu. This approach has the largest decision space and therefore the highest potential for simultaneous improvements in NRF, environmental footprints, and cost.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics (frequency, mean, and standard deviation) were used to summarize the data.

For comparisons of mean values among the three universities, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for normally distributed variables, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to determine pairwise differences when the ANOVA result was statistically significant. For variables with non-normal distribution, the Kruskal–Wallis test was applied, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study assessed the sustainability of institutional food menus provided at three military universities in Tehran using a comprehensive, multidimensional framework encompassing nutritional adequacy, environmental impact (carbon and water footprints), and economic cost. The findings yield meaningful insights into how institutional food environments can be restructured to align with international standards for healthy and sustainable diets.

Energy and macronutrient composition

Analysis of the one-month institutional menus revealed substantial variability in both nutritional composition and sustainability indicators. The average daily energy intake (2929.98 ± 491.99 kcal) exceeded recommended levels for the target demographic. Macronutrient distribution was heavily skewed toward carbohydrates (336.15 ± 59.65 g), followed by protein (122.64 ± 21.18 g), and an especially high intake of fat (501.73 ± 28.82 g), indicating a calorie-dense yet nutritionally imbalanced dietary pattern. This observation aligns with findings by de Muraro et al. [41], who reported excessive caloric provision in Brazilian school canteens, which, despite cost-efficiency, failed to ensure nutritional balance. Similarly, Van Dooren et al. [42] reported high energy and fat densities in Dutch institutional catering due to frequent use of fried and animal-based dishes.

Excess fat and energy intake have been linked to increased prevalence of overweight, obesity, and metabolic disorders among military personnel—conditions that compromise physical fitness and operational readiness [43]. Supporting this, Payab et al. [44] reported that over 50% of Iranian military personnel were overweight or obese, with a high prevalence of lipid disorders.

Food group distribution

The dietary analysis revealed low fruit consumption (97.67 ± 88.52 g/day) and moderate vegetable intake (408.83 ± 138.62 g/day), alongside high consumption of meat (~ 217 g/day) and added sugars and fats (173.62 ± 105.38 g/day). This contributed to considerable environmental impacts, with average carbon and water footprints of 5358.88 ± 2018.91 g CO₂ and 7.12 ± 2.28 m³ per day, respectively. Despite this burden, the average Nutrient Rich Food (NRF) index score remained moderate (21.45 ± 5.22), indicating suboptimal nutrient density.

These observations are in line with previous research by Willett et al. [5] and Clark et al. [45], who emphasized the adverse environmental and health impacts of meat-heavy diets and recommended transitioning to predominantly plant-based dietary models. Similarly, Sobhani et al. [46] demonstrated that the Iranian diet is heavily based on refined carbohydrates—particularly white rice and bread—a trend mirrored in institutional menus, which often prioritize rice and meat-based dishes while offering limited amounts of fruits and whole grains. Comparable results were reported by Van Dooren et al. [42] and Monteiro et al. [47] in Dutch and Brazilian institutions, respectively, both noting high reliance on ultra-processed foods and inadequate fruit and vegetable intake.

Environmental, nutritional, and economic impacts

Significant improvements were achieved following dietary optimization—particularly under the third scenario. The NRF index increased from 21.45 ± 5.22 to 26.79 ± 0.35, marking a 25% improvement in nutrient density. This was driven by increased consumption of fruits (+ 20%) and vegetables (+ 4%), and marked reductions in red meat (− 29%), dairy (− 15%), and added sugars and fats (− 45%). These enhancements mirror those reported by Maillot et al. [48], who used linear programming to optimize European school meals, resulting in increased dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals. Likewise, Sobhani et al. [36] demonstrated similar results in the Iranian context using culturally adapted modeling.

In parallel with nutritional gains, environmental and economic outcomes improved significantly. Water footprints fell by 30% (to 4.97 ± 0.17 m³/day), carbon emissions by 36% (to 3413.08 ± 197.53 g CO₂/day), and daily meal costs by 32% (to 38,518.84 ± 1916.67 tomans). These findings are consistent with the work of Poore and Nemecek [11], who showed that reducing animal-based foods can lower dietary carbon emissions by up to 73% and water use by over 50%. Comparable reductions in environmental burdens and cost were also reported by Clark et al. [49], Springmann et al. [12], and Tyszler et al. [50] following plant-based dietary transitions in institutional contexts.

In Iran, Eini‑Zinab et al. [36] found that household diet optimization could reduce costs by 20–25% while enhancing health outcomes. Our findings reinforce this perspective, demonstrating that institutional reform offers a scalable, cost-effective approach to sustainable meal provision.

Micronutrient adequacy

Despite reductions in some animal-based foods, the optimized menus maintained robust micronutrient adequacy. Sodium intake decreased by up to 81%, aligning with national goals to reduce salt intake due to its link to hypertension [51]. Both calcium and iron remained above recommended levels (≥ 100% RDA), confirming the feasibility of nutrient-dense, plant-rich diets as shown in the EAT-Lancet Commission [52]. Vitamin D intake rose substantially, increased by 116%—an important improvement given widespread deficiencies among Iranian youth [53, 54]. These results are consistent with Smetana et al. [55], who demonstrated that optimized diets can retain vitamin and mineral sufficiency while reducing environmental impact.

Moreover, post-optimization reductions in cholesterol and saturated fats are aligned with global recommendations for cardiovascular health [56]. The increase in dietary fiber intake—up to 368% of the RDA—supports improved metabolic and gastrointestinal health, echoing findings by Chai et al. [57], who associated high-fiber institutional diets with reduced chronic disease risk.

Policy implications

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that institutional food environments can be restructured to simultaneously improve health outcomes, reduce environmental footprints, and lower costs. Incorporating dietary modeling into routine menu planning enables institutions to design meals that are nutritionally balanced, ecologically responsible, and economically sustainable. Similar approaches have been successfully implemented in countries such as Finland and the United Kingdom, where evidence-based procurement strategies led to improved dietary quality and reduced carbon emissions [58–61].

Comparable results have also been reported in regional contexts. For example, a study titled “Analysis of a Campus Lunch Menu for Aspects of Sustainable Diets and Designing a Sustainable Lunch Menu” conducted in Iran found that optimizing university menus could substantially improve nutritional balance while reducing environmental burdens, mirroring the patterns observed in our work [62]. Similarly, an evaluation of menu adherence to sustainable nutrition principles in a Turkish university refectory demonstrated that aligning menus with plant-forward dietary models improved nutrient density and reduced carbon and water footprints [63]. These parallels reinforce the regional relevance of our findings and highlight the applicability of our approach in Middle Eastern institutional settings.

Institutional food policy reform should prioritize affordable, nutrient-dense, low-impact foods—particularly legumes, whole grains, and seasonal vegetables. Aligning institutional menus with global dietary frameworks, such as the EAT-Lancet reference diet, and integrating sustainability metrics into national dietary guidelines, could enhance the impact of such reforms [12, 45, 52]. Additionally, embedding nutrition and sustainability education within institutional settings can foster long-term behavioral change [64, 65]. In structured environments such as military universities, these interventions may not only improve short-term health but also instill lifelong dietary habits [66].

This study adds to a growing body of evidence indicating that institutional menus designed with a multidimensional approach can simultaneously enhance diet quality, reduce environmental pressures, and lower food costs. Expanding these strategies through intersectoral collaboration—across health, education, agriculture, and environmental sectors—can serve as a powerful mechanism for transforming food systems toward greater resilience, equity, and sustainability [67, 68].

Strengths and limitations

A key methodological strength of this study was the use of Goal Programming (GP), which allowed for the simultaneous balancing of competing priorities—nutritional adequacy, environmental sustainability, and cost-efficiency—while preserving cultural acceptability through frequency constraints on menu items. This realistic, systems-based approach offers greater implementation potential than traditional single-objective models, which often overlook practical constraints [48, 69].

However, the study has limitations. Generalizability to civilian institutions may be constrained by cultural and logistical differences. Moreover, reliance on international life cycle assessment (LCA) databases—due to a lack of Iran-specific environmental data—may limit the accuracy of environmental impact estimates [70]. Furthermore, food waste, an important determinant of both environmental and economic sustainability, was not incorporated into the analysis due to a lack of consistent institutional waste data. This omission may lead to an underestimation of the actual environmental and cost impacts of the evaluated menus. Although the NRF index is a strong proxy for overall diet quality, future research should evaluate clinical health outcomes, user satisfaction, and behavioral determinants of dietary adherence. Integrating behavioral economics strategies—such as those proposed by Just and Wansink [71]—may further support implementation and effectiveness.

Conclusion

This study underscores the potential of institutional menu optimization to simultaneously address nutritional inadequacies, reduce environmental burdens, and improve economic efficiency within structured food environments. By evaluating military university menus through an integrated lens of diet quality, environmental impact, and cost, we demonstrated that even within operational and logistical constraints, substantial improvements are achievable. The transition toward menus richer in plant-based foods and lower in red meat, dairy, and added fats not only enhanced nutrient density and fiber intake but also significantly reduced carbon and water footprints and overall costs. Importantly, these gains were achieved without compromising the adequacy of essential micronutrients—a critical concern in population-level interventions.

These findings support a growing consensus that institutional food systems represent a powerful entry point for advancing public health and environmental goals in tandem. Embedding sustainability criteria into menu planning and procurement policies—aligned with global frameworks such as the EAT-Lancet reference diet—can help institutional settings become models of dietary transformation. In military universities, where food environments shape the habits of young adults in formative years, such reforms hold particular promise for long-term impact.

Overall, this work contributes to the evidence base for actionable, context-specific strategies to reform food systems, calling for intersectoral collaboration to scale and sustain these gains across broader institutional and national contexts.
