Efficacy and safety of hetrombopag, a novel thrombopoietin receptor agonist, in children and adolescents with immune thrombocytopenia: results from a randomized, multicenter, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial

Background

Primary immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is the most common acquired bleeding disorder in children, with an annual incidence of 1.6–5.3 per 100,000 and a slight male predominance. It is characterized by immune-mediated platelet destruction and impaired platelet production, leading to thrombocytopenia and variable bleeding severity [1, 2]. Pediatric ITP is classified based on disease duration into newly diagnosed (<3 months), persistent (3–12 months), and chronic (>12 months) categories [3]. Although approximately 75% of children experience spontaneous remission within 6 months, 10–20% progress to chronic ITP, with the risk of chronicity increasing with age, reaching around 47% in patients aged ≥10 years [4, 5].

Because many children remit spontaneously, treatment is generally reserved for those with significant bleeding or high hemorrhagic risk. The primary therapeutic goal is to maintain a safe platelet count rather than to normalize platelet levels. First-line treatment options include short courses of corticosteroids or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), with observation recommended for asymptomatic patients. For patients who are refractory to or relapse after first-line therapies, second-line treatments include thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs), rituximab, immunosuppressants, and, less commonly, splenectomy [6, 7].

TPO-RAs have transformed second-line treatment for ITP by stimulating megakaryopoiesis and platelet production. Romiplostim (administered subcutaneously) was approved by the U.S. FDA in 2018 for children aged ≥1 year [8, 9]. Eltrombopag, the first oral TPO-RA, was approved in 2015 for children aged 1–17 years, demonstrating improvements in platelet counts and reduced bleeding risks; in China, its pediatric indication was expanded from adolescents (≥12 years) in 2018 to children aged ≥6 years in 2022 [10–12]. More recently, avatrombopag was approved by the FDA for children aged ≥1 year on the basis of the AVA-PED-301 trial [13]. Despite the availability of multiple TPO-RAs, evidence supporting their efficacy and safety in Asian pediatric populations remains limited, underscoring the need for additional region-specific data. Moreover, romiplostim's injection burden can affect patient adherence, and eltrombopag is associated with hepatotoxicity concerns, highlighting the ongoing need for effective, safe, and convenient oral alternatives [14, 15].

Hetrombopag is a non-peptide, orally administered TPO-RA developed based on the interaction model between eltrombopag and the thrombopoietin receptor (c-Mpl). It shares a similar mechanism of action in activating TPO-R signaling. Preclinical studies have shown that hetrombopag exhibits 2–3 times greater in vivo receptor activation than eltrombopag, with lower hepatotoxic potential [16, 17]. In a phase 3 randomized trial in adults with ITP, once-daily hetrombopag (initial doses of 2.5 mg or 5 mg) achieved significantly higher platelet responses compared to placebo at Week 8 (58.9% and 64.3% vs 5.9%, all P<0.0001), with a safety profile comparable to placebo and no new safety signals [18]. These data supported its approval in China in 2021. Long-term extension and exploratory analyses demonstrated sustained efficacy and favorable safety, even in relapsed/refractory patients and those previously exposed to TPO-RAs [19, 20].

Building on the established efficacy in adults, we conducted a phase 3 program of hetrombopag in children with ITP. Part A was an open-label, dose-finding study that evaluated pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, while Part B was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study followed by an open-label extension. This report presents the results of Part B, which assesses the efficacy and safety of once-daily hetrombopag in children aged 6–17 years with ITP who had an inadequate response or relapse following prior therapy.

Methods

Study design and oversight

This multicenter, phase 3 superiority trial (NCT04737850) evaluated hetrombopag in children and adolescents with primary ITP across 17 sites in China (Supplemental Table 1). The study consisted of Part A, an 8-week open-label dose-finding and pharmacokinetics/pharmacodynamics phase, and Part B, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled 12-week period followed by a 12-week open-label extension (Supplemental Figure 1). This report focuses on the results from Part B. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The protocol was approved by institutional review boards and ethics committees, and written informed consent/assent was obtained from all participants and their guardians.

Patients

Eligible participants were children or adolescents aged 6–17 years with primary ITP diagnosed for at least 6 months prior to the first dose. During screening, at least 75% of platelet measurements (including ≥2 consecutive assessments ≥48 hours apart) were required to be <30 × 10⁹/L, and the platelet count within 24 hours before the first dose also needed to be <30 × 10⁹/L. Patients were required to have failed or relapsed after at least one prior first-line therapy (e.g., corticosteroids or IVIg) or after splenectomy. Concomitant immunosuppressive therapy (e.g., corticosteroids, azathioprine, or ciclosporin A) was permitted if the doses remained stable for ≥14 days (for corticosteroids), ≥30 days (for azathioprine), or ≥60 days (for ciclosporin A) before dosing. Any rescue therapy (e.g., methylprednisolone, IVIg, platelet transfusion) was required to be completed at least 21 days before the first dose. Complete eligibility criteria are provided in the Supplementary Protocol.

Randomization, masking, and treatment

Randomization was centrally managed via an interactive web response system, using permuted blocks and a 2:1 allocation ratio to either hetrombopag or placebo. Stratification was based on age (6–11 years vs 12–17 years) and disease duration (6–12 months vs >12 months). Study tablets were identical in appearance, packaging, and labeling to ensure blinding. Participants, caregivers, investigators, site personnel, central laboratory staff, and sponsor teams remained blinded to treatment allocation until database lock.

In part B, during the 12-week double-blind period, hetrombopag was initiated at an initial dose of 2.5 mg once daily or matching placebo. In the subsequent 12-week open-label extension, participants in the hetrombopag group continued their treatment, while those in the placebo group crossed over to hetrombopag at an initial dose of 2.5 mg once daily. Dose adjustments, ranging from 2.5 mg to a maximum of 7.5 mg once daily, were made to maintain platelet counts between 50 and 250 × 10⁹/L, according to a prespecified algorithm (Supplemental Table S2). Discontinuation criteria included intolerable adverse events (AEs), protocol deviations, or decisions made by the investigator or patient.

Endpoints and assessments

Platelet counts were measured weekly during the double-blind period and every two weeks during the open-label period, with weekly assessments resumed following any dose adjustment. A treatment response was defined as a platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L. The primary endpoint was the proportion of patients who achieved a platelet response at Week 10. Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted based on age (6–11 vs 12–17 years), disease duration (6–12 vs >12 months), sex, body weight (<40 vs ≥40 kg), concomitant ITP medication use (yes vs no), and baseline platelet count (<10 vs 10–30 × 10⁹/L). The key secondary endpoint was the proportion of patients with a sustained platelet response, defined as a platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L maintained for ≥6 weeks without the need for rescue therapy between Weeks 5 and 12.

Other secondary endpoints included the proportion of patients with platelet responses lasting ≥4 consecutive weeks (Weeks 5–12), the proportion of responders at each weekly assessment (Weeks 3–12), and during the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, the proportions of patients who achieved a response at least once, responded at ≥75% of visits, or achieved platelet counts ≥30 × 10⁹/L with at least a ≥2-fold increase from baseline. Additional endpoints were platelet counts at each scheduled visit, time to first response, maximum continuous and total response durations, the proportion of patients requiring protocol-defined rescue therapy (platelet transfusion or IVIg), and the incidence and severity of bleeding symptoms, which were graded according to the 2021 Chinese guideline for childhood ITP [21].

Exploratory endpoints included the proportion of patients with responses at ≥75% of assessments over 24 weeks, maximum continuous and total response durations over 24 weeks, and the proportion of patients who reduced or discontinued concomitant ITP medications during the open-label extension.

Safety evaluations included the monitoring of AEs, clinical laboratory tests, vital signs, physical examinations, and electrocardiograms (ECGs). Ophthalmologic examinations, chest radiography, abdominal ultrasonography, and bone marrow assessments were conducted according to the study protocol. Adverse events of special interest (AESIs) included hepatic laboratory abnormalities, defined as concurrent elevations in AST, ALT, and total bilirubin. Other prespecified AESIs, based on previous experience with TPO-RA therapies, included hepatic function abnormalities, thromboembolic events, cataracts, QT-interval prolongation, and myelofibrosis. AEs were coded using MedDRA version 28.0 and graded according to the CTCAE v5.0.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined based on the primary and key secondary endpoints. For Part B, the primary endpoint—the proportion of patients with platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L at Week 10—was referenced from a phase 3 pediatric study of eltrombopag in chronic ITP, which reported response rates of 52.4% for eltrombopag vs 3.4% for placebo [22, 23]. Assuming response rates of 50% for hetrombopag and 5% for placebo, a 2:1 randomization ratio (90 patients total: 60 hetrombopag, 30 placebo) would provide greater than 99% power to detect a significant difference, using Fisher’s exact test (two-sided α = 0.05).

For the key secondary endpoint—the proportion of patients with a sustained platelet response (defined as a platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L maintained for ≥6 weeks without rescue therapy between Weeks 5 and 12)—the sample size was determined based on prior data, including a pediatric phase 3 eltrombopag trial (NCT01520909) that reported response rates of 40% vs. 3% overall, and 35% vs. 0% in East Asian patients [22]. Assuming response rates of 35% for hetrombopag and 5% for placebo, a total of 90 patients randomized in a 2:1 ratio (60 hetrombopag, 30 placebo) provides 86% power to detect a significant between-group difference using Fisher’s exact test (two-sided α = 0.05). To ensure a balanced age distribution and facilitate subgroup analyses, approximately 30% of patients were targeted for each age stratum (6–11 and 12–17 years; approximately 27 patients per stratum).

The primary and key secondary endpoints were analyzed using a sequential hierarchical testing strategy. The primary endpoint was tested first at a two-sided α = 0.05. The key secondary endpoint was tested at the same significance level only if the primary endpoint reached statistical significance; otherwise, it was considered exploratory. No multiplicity adjustments were made for efficacy endpoints other than the primary and key secondary endpoints, so the corresponding P values for these efficacy endpoints should be interpreted as descriptive and supportive rather than confirmatory.

Binary efficacy endpoints (primary, key secondary, and others) in the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period were analyzed using Mehrotra and Railkar’s minimum-risk (MR) method, adjusted for the randomization stratification factor (age group: 6–11 vs 12–17 years). Between-group differences (hetrombopag vs placebo) were expressed as rate differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and P-values. Responder rates were summarized by treatment group. Missing data were handled using non-responder imputation (NRI). For the primary and key secondary endpoints, patients with concurrent events, such as early discontinuation, use of prohibited platelet-raising medications, or receipt of rescue therapy (for the primary endpoint only, were imputed as non-responders. A treatment-policy strategy was applied for dose increases of concomitant maintenance therapy (including glucocorticoids, azathioprine, and cyclosporine); data collected after such dose increases were included in the analysis.

Comparisons of rescue therapy use and bleeding incidence were conducted using Fisher’s exact test on observed cases. The time from the first dose to the first platelet response (≥50 × 10⁹/L) within 12 weeks was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, with comparisons between groups made using the log-rank test. Patients without a response by 12 weeks were censored. All other efficacy endpoints were summarized descriptively based on observed data. The full analysis set included all randomized patients who received at least one dose of the study drug, while the safety set included all treated patients. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4. Please refer to the Supplementary statistical analysis plan for additional details.

Results

Patients

Between July 30, 2022, and June 17, 2025, 118 patients were screened, and 88 were randomized in a 2:1 ratio to receive either hetrombopag (n = 57) or placebo (n = 31) (Fig 1). No patients discontinued treatment early during the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period. During the subsequent 12-week open-label extension, 5 patients (5.7%) discontinued treatment early: 4 in the hetrombopag group and 1 in the placebo-hetrombopag group. Reasons for early discontinuation included lack of efficacy (n = 2), patient decision (n = 2), and an adverse event (n = 1). Overall, 83 patients (94.3%) completed the extension: 53 (93.0%) in the hetrombopag group and 30 (96.8%) in the placebo-hetrombopag group. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the groups (Table 1).

Primary and key secondary endpoints

At Week 10, 61.4% (35/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group achieved a platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L, compared to 9.7% (3/31) in the placebo group, resulting in an absolute difference of 52.7 percentage points (95% CI, 35.9–69.5; two-sided P < 0.0001; Table 2). The key secondary endpoint, defined as a sustained platelet response (≥50 × 10⁹/L for ≥6 weeks without rescue therapy between Weeks 5–12), was achieved by 43.9% (25/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group, while no patients in the placebo group met this criterion, resulting in an absolute difference of 43.7 percentage points (95% CI, 28.5–58.9; two-sided P < 0.0001; Table 2). Prespecified subgroup analyses consistently favored hetrombopag for both the primary and key secondary endpoints (Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Figure 3).

Other secondary endpoints

The proportion of patients with platelet response for ≥4 consecutive weeks (Weeks 5–12) was higher with hetrombopag than with placebo (43.9% vs 0%; rate difference, 43.5% [95% CI, 28.7%–58.3%]; nominal two-sided P < 0.0001; Table 2). As shown in Supplemental Table 3, the proportion of patients achieving a platelet count ≥50 × 10⁹/L at weekly assessments (Weeks 3–12) was consistently higher in the hetrombopag group compared to the placebo group (all nominal two-sided P values <0.01; Fig 2A).

During the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, a higher proportion of patients in the hetrombopag group achieved ≥50 × 10⁹/L at least once (91.2% vs. 64.5%; rate difference, 26.0%; 95% CI, 7.2%–44.8%; nominal P = 0.0033) and achieved ≥50 × 10⁹/L at ≥75% of scheduled assessments (26.3% vs. 0%; rate difference, 25.9%; 95% CI, 12.1%–39.7%; nominal P = 0.0027) (Table 2). Similarly, more patients in the hetrombopag group reached ≥30 × 10⁹/L with a ≥2-fold increase from baseline at least once (93.0% vs. 64.5%; rate difference, 26.9%; 95% CI, 9.5%–44.4%; nominal P = 0.0012; Table 2).

Platelet counts at each scheduled visit are shown in Fig 2B. Throughout the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, the hetrombopag group consistently exhibited higher platelet counts compared to the placebo group. Upon entering the open-label extension, platelet counts in the hetrombopag group remained stable, while the placebo-to-hetrombopag switch group experienced a marked increase, reaching a peak at Week 8, followed by stable maintenance. These data indicate that hetrombopag can maintain effective platelet increases over a 24-week period (Supplemental Figure 4).

Time-to-event analyses showed that the onset of response was faster with hetrombopag. The median time to first response (≥50 × 10⁹/L) was 20.0 days (95% CI, 16.0–23.0) in the hetrombopag group, compared to 43.0 days in the placebo group (95% CI, 24.0–NR; log-rank, nominal two-sided P = 0.0018; Fig 2C).

Among the 60 patients who achieved responses at consecutive scheduled visits (49 hetrombopag; 11 placebo), the median maximum continuous response duration was 29.0 days (range, 8.0–76.0) in the hetrombopag group, compared to 8.0 days (range, 6.0–15.0) in the placebo group. The median total response duration was 38.0 days (range, 8.0–76.0) in the hetrombopag group, compared to 14.0 days (range, 6.0–21.0) in the placebo group (Table 2).

During the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, protocol-defined rescue therapy was required in 12/57 patients (21.1%) on hetrombopag, compared to 14/31 patients (45.2%) on placebo (Table 2), with all patients receiving rescue therapy treated with IVIg.

At baseline, 59.6% of hetrombopag-treated patients had bleeding symptoms; this fell to 28.1% by Week 3 and then fluctuated between ~12% and 25%, reaching 15.8% at Week 12. Most events were grade 1–2 per the 2021 Chinese pediatric ITP guideline, with only isolated grade 3–4 episodes at individual visits. In the placebo group, 67.7% had bleeding at baseline, with incidence varying between 42% and 65% during the double-blind, placebo-controlled period and remaining high at 48.4% at Week 12; occasional grade 3 events were noted (Fig 3).

Exploratory endpoints

Across the entire 24-week treatment period (12 weeks of double-blind placebo-controlled treatment + 12 weeks of open-label extension), 26.3% (15/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group achieved platelet responses at ≥75% of scheduled assessments. Among the 52 patients who achieved responses at consecutive visits, the median maximum continuous response duration was 44.5 days (range, 8–160), and the median total response duration was 85.0 days (range, 8–160) (Supplemental Table 4).

As per protocol, patients were allowed to taper or discontinue concomitant ITP medications during the open-label extension. In the actual study population, all patients receiving concomitant ITP therapy at baseline were treated with corticosteroids. At baseline, 24.6% (14/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group were on concomitant corticosteroids; of these, 15.8% (9/57) reduced or discontinued corticosteroid therapy while receiving continuous hetrombopag. In the placebo group, 38.7% (12/31) were on concomitant corticosteroids at baseline, and after crossover to continuous hetrombopag during the extension period, 12.9% (4/31) were able to reduce or discontinue corticosteroid use.

Safety

During the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, the median treatment duration was 83.0 days (range, 51–87) for the hetrombopag group and 84.0 days (range, 75–86) for the placebo group. Over the entire 24-week study, patients in the hetrombopag group had a median cumulative exposure of 165.0 days (range, 84–199). Treatment compliance in patients on continuous hetrombopag ranged from 80% to 120%.

The overall AE profile of hetrombopag during the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled treatment period and the entire 24-week study is summarized in Table 3. During the double-blind period, the incidence of AEs was similar between the groups, with 87.7% (50/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group and 96.8% (30/31) in the placebo group experiencing at least one AE. No patient in either group discontinued treatment due to AEs. Six patients (10.5%) in the hetrombopag group experienced AEs requiring dose interruption or reduction, most commonly increased platelet count (8.8%, 5/57), whereas no patients in the placebo group had such events (Supplemental Table 5). The most frequent AEs (≥10%) in the hetrombopag group were upper respiratory tract infection (57.9%, 33/57) and epistaxis (19.3%, 11/57), compared to 45.2% (14/31) and 22.6% (7/31) in the placebo group, respectively. AEs that occurred ≥5% more frequently in the hetrombopag group compared to the placebo group included upper respiratory tract infection (57.9% vs 45.2%), increased platelet count (8.8% vs 0%), increased ALT (7.0% vs 0%), abnormal hepatic function (5.3% vs 0%), and influenza-like illness (5.3% vs 0%). Grade ≥3 AEs were less common with hetrombopag (12.3%, 7/57) compared to placebo (22.6%, 7/31). Among the grade ≥3 AEs in the hetrombopag group, those reported in at least two patients were worsening ITP (5.3%, 3/57), defined as more severe bleeding and clinical deterioration compared with baseline, and pneumonia (3.5%, 2/57) (Supplemental Table 6).

During the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, 26.3% (15/57) of patients in the hetrombopag group and 22.6% (7/31) in the placebo group experienced at least one treatment-related AE. Overall, the types and incidence of treatment-related AEs were similar between the hetrombopag and placebo groups during the double-blind period (supplemental Table 7). In the hetrombopag group, treatment-related AEs occurring in ≥2 patients included increased platelet count (n=4, 7.0%), increased ALT and increased blood lactate dehydrogenase (n=3 for each, 5.3%), and increased AST, increased blood bilirubin, hyperuricemia, and abnormal hepatic function (n=2 for each, 3.5%), with all events being grade 1–2 in severity.

During the 12-week double-blind, placebo-controlled period, a total of six patients (10.5%) in the hetrombopag group experienced at least one SAE. The SAEs occurring in ≥2 patients were worsening ITP (3/57, 5.3%) and pneumonia (2/57, 3.5%). In the placebo group, four patients (12.9%) experienced at least one SAE; worsening ITP was the only SAE reported in ≥2 patients (4/31, 12.9%) (Supplemental Table 8). One patient treated with hetrombopag (1.8%) developed intracranial hemorrhage with cerebral edema; this patient had platelet counts <20 × 10⁹/L with bleeding for more than one month before the event, and investigators assessed the SAE as unrelated to hetrombopag. Overall, all SAEs in both groups were assessed by investigators as unlikely related or not related to study treatment.

Across the 24-week treatment period, AEs occurred in 56 of 57 patients (98.2%) receiving continuous hetrombopag. The most common AEs (≥10%) were upper respiratory tract infection (70.2%, 40/57), epistaxis (22.8%, 13/57), increased ALT (14.0%, 8/57), hyperuricemia (12.3%, 7/57), pyrexia (12.3%, 7/57), and worsening ITP, increased AST, abnormal electrocardiogram, and increased platelet count (each 10.5%, 6/57). Grade ≥3 AEs were reported in 12 patients (21.1%); events occurring in ≥2 patients included worsening ITP (10.5%, 6/57) and pneumonia or upper respiratory tract infection (each 3.5%, 2/57). One patient (1.8%, 1/57) discontinued treatment because of a grade 3 AE (systemic lupus erythematosus), which investigators assessed as unlikely related to hetrombopag. Dose interruptions or reductions due to AEs occurred in 9 patients (15.8%), including 7 (12.3%) with events considered related to hetrombopag; the most common AE leading to dose modification was increased platelet count (10.5%, 6/57) (Supplemental Table 7).. SAEs occurred in 10 patients (17.5%) receiving continuous hetrombopag; those reported in ≥2 patients were worsening ITP (8.8%, 5/57) and pneumonia (3.5%, 2/57), and all were assessed by investigators as unrelated to hetrombopag (Supplemental Table 8). No prespecified AESIs or deaths were reported during the 24-week treatment period.

During the 12-week double-blind period, 8 of 57 patients (14.0%) in the hetrombopag group experienced at least one other event of special interest (hepatic laboratory abnormalities, thromboembolic events, cataract, QT-interval prolongation, or myelofibrosis), whereas no such events occurred in the placebo group. In the hetrombopag arm, these comprised increased ALT (4/57, 7.0%), increased AST (2/57, 3.5%), abnormal hepatic function (3/57, 5.3%), and cataract (1/57, 1.8%). Across the entire 24-week treatment period, 12 of 57 patients (21.1%) receiving continuous hetrombopag had at least one other event of special interest, most commonly liver enzyme abnormalities, including increased ALT (14.0%; 8/57), increased AST (10.5%; 6/57), and abnormal hepatic function (5.3%; 3/57) (Table 3). Treatment-related other events of special interest occurred in 10 of 57 patients (17.5%) and all involved liver enzyme changes (Supplemental Table 7); three ALT elevations (5.3%; 3/57) were CTCAE grade 2 and all remaining events were grade 1. Except for one case of persistent grade 1 ALT/AST elevation at the last observation, all events resolved or improved without dose modification of hetrombopag. The cataract was observed in a patient with pre-existing bilateral cataracts and prior long-term corticosteroid use; lens opacity improved after corticosteroid withdrawal, and the event was adjudicated by investigators as unrelated to hetrombopag.

Discussion

In this multicenter, randomized phase 3 trial, hetrombopag significantly increased the proportion of children and adolescents with ITP achieving a platelet response (≥50 × 10⁹/L) at Week 10 compared with placebo (61.4% vs 9.7%). This magnitude of effect is broadly in keeping with the efficacy range reported for other TPO-RAs in pediatric ITP, although cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. In PETIT2, 52.4% of children receiving eltrombopag achieved a platelet response at Week 10 versus 3.4% with placebo, and in AVA-PED-301, 81% of avatrombopag-treated patients achieved ≥2 consecutive platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L during the 12-week core period compared with none in the placebo arm [10, 13, 22, 24]. In adults with ITP, hetrombopag achieved Week-8 response rates of 58.9–64.3% versus 5.9% with placebo, further supporting a consistent treatment effect across age groups [18]. Real-world data from Chinese pediatric cohorts treated with eltrombopag have also shown high overall and complete response rates with sustained platelet gains during long-term therapy [11], underscoring the utility of oral TPO-RAs in routine practice and providing relevant context for interpreting our findings in a Chinese setting.

The key secondary endpoint—sustained response, defined as platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L maintained for ≥6 weeks without rescue therapy between Weeks 5–12—was achieved by 43.9% of patients in the hetrombopag group and by none in the placebo group. This sustained response rate falls within the range reported for other oral TPO-RAs in children: in PETIT2, 40% of eltrombopag-treated patients versus 3% of placebo-treated patients had sustained responses (35% vs 0% among East Asian patients) [22], and in AVA-PED-301, 28% of avatrombopag-treated patients achieved durable responses (≥6 of the last 8 weekly platelet counts ≥50 × 10⁹/L) versus 0% with placebo [24]. Taken together, these data suggest that hetrombopag provides durable platelet responses in pediatric ITP that are generally consistent with those observed for other oral TPO-RAs, while recognizing that all such comparisons are indirect and may be influenced by differences in study design, populations, and endpoints [10, 13, 22, 24].

The choice of a single time-point platelet response at Week 10 as the primary endpoint warrants comment. This endpoint was selected at the design stage to align with the pivotal phase 3 trial of hetrombopag in adults and to provide a clear, interpretable benchmark of short-term efficacy [18]. At that time, pediatric data with hetrombopag were lacking and no TPO-RA was approved for children with ITP in China. By contrast, more recent ITP trials, including PETIT2 and AVA-PED-301, have adopted more stringent multi–time-point durable response endpoints as primary efficacy measures [10, 13, 22]. In recognition of this evolution in trial methodology, we prospectively specified sustained response between Weeks 5–12 as the key secondary endpoint and further explored durability using visit-based measures such as responses at ≥75% of scheduled assessments. Although the single time-point primary endpoint is less rigorous than newer durable response definitions and represents a limitation of the trial, the concordant findings for sustained response and other durability measures support a clinically meaningful and maintained treatment effect.

Other platelet-related efficacy endpoints during the 12-week double-blind period also supported a treatment benefit. Compared with placebo, children receiving hetrombopag were more likely to have responses lasting ≥4 consecutive weeks between Weeks 5 and 12, to achieve at least one platelet response, to respond at ≥75% of scheduled assessments, and to reach platelet counts ≥30 × 10⁹/L with at least a ≥2-fold increase from baseline. Taken together, these results suggest that hetrombopag not only increases platelet counts but also helps maintain more stable platelet control over time, which is clinically relevant in a setting where bleeding risk, rather than complete platelet normalization, primarily guides management [2, 6, 7, 21].

Beyond platelet counts, our findings align with contemporary treatment goals in pediatric ITP, which prioritize prevention of bleeding and avoidance of prolonged corticosteroid exposure rather than complete platelet normalization [2, 6, 7, 21]. At baseline, more than 60% of participants had bleeding manifestations graded according to the 2021 Chinese pediatric ITP guideline [21]. In the hetrombopag group, the proportion with any bleeding decreased from 59.6% at baseline to 15.8% at Week 12, whereas bleeding remained more frequent in the placebo group (67.7% at baseline and 48.4% at Week 12). Hetrombopag also reduced the need for protocol-defined rescue therapy with IVIg or platelet transfusion for worsening ITP compared with placebo (21.1% vs 45.2%). Similar reductions in bleeding and rescue therapy use have been reported with romiplostim and eltrombopag in prior pediatric ITP trials [8, 10, 22], supporting that the clinical benefits observed with hetrombopag are consistent with the class effect of TPO-RAs in reducing disease-related morbidity and reliance on acute supportive treatments.

In exploratory analyses over the entire 24-week treatment period, 26.3% of patients receiving continuous hetrombopag maintained platelet responses at ≥75% of scheduled assessments, with median maximum continuous and total response durations of 44.5 and 85.0 days, respectively. These observations, together with the rapid median time to first response (20.0 days vs 43.0 days with placebo), indicate that hetrombopag can produce both relatively fast and sustained platelet increases in many children. Although the sample size limits precision, these patterns are relevant for longer-term disease management, in which maintaining platelet counts above a hemostatic threshold over time is crucial to minimize bleeding risk and cumulative exposure to rescue therapies or additional immunosuppression [2, 4–6].

Safety findings were reassuring and clinically manageable. During the double-blind period, overall AE rates were similar between groups and grade ≥3 AEs were less frequent with hetrombopag than with placebo. The most common AEs in the hetrombopag group were upper respiratory tract infection (57.9% vs 45.2% with placebo) and epistaxis (19.3% vs 22.6%). ALT elevations occurred in 7.0% of hetrombopag-treated patients and in none of the placebo-treated patients. Over 24 weeks, treatment-related hepatic laboratory abnormalities (liver enzyme elevations or hepatic function abnormalities) were reported in 17.5% (10/57) of children receiving continuous hetrombopag; all were CTCAE grade 1–2, did not require permanent treatment discontinuation, and only three patients had ALT ≥3×ULN and one had AST ≥3×ULN. In PETIT2, 7.9% (5/63) of eltrombopag-treated children developed ALT ≥3×ULN during the 13-week double-blind phase, including two Asian patients who met protocol criteria for discontinuation, and a further 6.9% (6/87; five Asian) had ALT ≥3×ULN during the subsequent 24-week open-label phase, with three discontinuations [22], suggesting that the frequency and severity of liver enzyme abnormalities with hetrombopag fall within the range observed for other pediatric TPO-RAs. One patient experienced unilateral cataract progression; bilateral cataracts were already present at screening after >1 year of intermittent glucocorticoid therapy, and the opacity improved after prednisone withdrawal, so the event was considered more likely related to corticosteroid exposure than to hetrombopag. Cataracts have been described with TPO-RAs, particularly in the context of steroid use [10, 14, 22], and periodic ophthalmologic monitoring may therefore be prudent in children receiving long-term treatment. No QT-interval prolongation, thrombotic/thromboembolic events, reticulin deposition, marrow fibrosis, clonal evolution, or deaths were observed, and the overall safety profile was consistent with that previously reported in adults treated with hetrombopag [18].

Concomitant ITP therapies are an important consideration when interpreting both efficacy and safety. In this trial, approximately one quarter of patients in the hetrombopag group and more than one third in the placebo group were receiving concomitant ITP medication at baseline, all of whom were on corticosteroids. During the extension phase, some patients in both sequences were able to reduce or discontinue corticosteroid therapy. Although these analyses were exploratory and the study was not powered to formally assess steroid-sparing effects, the possibility of tapering background steroids in responders is clinically encouraging, given current recommendations to minimize long-term corticosteroid exposure in children [2, 6, 7, 21].

Several limitations merit consideration. First, enrollment was restricted to Chinese children and adolescents, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Second, the 24-week follow-up and absence of an active comparator (e.g. eltrombopag or avatrombopag) preclude firm conclusions about long-term safety, marrow fibrosis risk, and relative benefit versus other TPO-RAs. Third, the primary endpoint used a single time-point platelet response at Week 10, which is less stringent than durable response definitions adopted in more recent ITP trials and should be borne in mind when comparing across studies, despite the concordant findings for sustained response and other durability measures [10, 13, 22]. Finally, patient-reported outcomes (e.g. fatigue, health-related quality of life, school attendance, treatment satisfaction) were not collected; given the growing emphasis on patient-centred endpoints in ITP, this limitation prevents assessment of the broader impact of hetrombopag on daily functioning and well-being [2, 6, 7].

Conclusions

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial shows that once-daily oral hetrombopag, compared with placebo, increased platelet counts, reduced bleeding events, and lowered the need for rescue therapy over 10 weeks in children and adolescents with ITP who had an inadequate response or relapse after prior therapy. Continued treatment provided sustained platelet control through 24 weeks, with most AEs being mild to moderate and liver enzyme elevations generally low grade and manageable with monitoring. Taken together, these findings support once-daily hetrombopag 2.5 mg as an appropriate starting dose and as an additional oral TPO-RA option for pediatric patients with ITP.
