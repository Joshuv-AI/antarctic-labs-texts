Lymphatic dysfunction is linked to disease pathogenesis in Duchenne muscular dystrophy animal models
Authors: Bhuvaneshwaran Subramanian, Shedreanna Johnson, Akshaya Narayanan, Wei Wang, Bonnie L. Seaberg, Jillian Ball, Ilse M. Paredes Mares, Ahana Majumder, Alexandria Aceves, Sarah E. Frazier, Alexis Rutledge, John F. Griffin, Soumiya Pal, Scott Zawieja, Michael J. Davis, Joseph M. Rutkowski, Peter P. Nghiem, Mendell Rimer, Mariappan Muthuchamy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe lymphatic system plays important roles in regulating inflammatory processes in the body. This study explored whether lymphatics were dysregulated in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) animal models. Lymph transport function was significantly decreased and pathological lymphangiogenesis and inflammation were linked in skeletal muscle of mouse and dog DMD models. Isolated lymphatic vessels from DMD mice exhibited an increased tonic but reduced phasic contractile activity. Microdystrophin treatment in DMD dogs, previously shown to improve muscle function, also mitigated inflammatory lymphangiogenesis. This work demonstrates the fine-tuned coupling of lymphatic and muscle function in DMD animals that may open therapeutic avenues in the DMD field.

Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) is a debilitating and the most common monogenic disease, affecting 1 in 3,500 to 5,000 male births worldwide, with an average life expectancy of 30 to 40 y. DMD is caused by mutations in the DMD gene, which encodes the 427 kDa full-length dystrophin protein (Dp) predominantly expressed in skeletal and cardiac muscle cells (1). Lack of dystrophin disturbs the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein complex (DGC), compromising membrane integrity in skeletal muscle, which results in muscle damage, myofiber necrosis, and leaky muscle fibers. This damage activates the innate and adaptive immune system, causing chronic inflammation as inflammatory macrophages persist near necrotic muscle, hindering muscle regeneration. Histopathology and longitudinal MRI of boys with DMD demonstrate a continuum of changes between the ages of 5 and 10 y, including: i) increased regions of damage, edema, and fat infiltration; and ii) patchy edema and inflammation in various muscle groups. Cytokines and chemokines produced by inflammatory cells further accelerate the skeletal muscle pathology in DMD animal models (2, 3). Anti-inflammatory therapies, including glucocorticoids, such as prednisone and deflazacort, and the recently approved glucocorticoid analog vamorolone, are commonly used to improve muscle strength in DMD patients (4–6). However, the beneficial effects of corticosteroid therapy vary from patient to patient, and in some cases, even lead to negative side effects. Hence, it is pertinent to investigate further the basis of inflammation in the pathogenesis of DMD in order to develop more effective, targeted therapies for DMD patients.

The lymphatic system that runs in parallel with the blood vascular system plays major roles in the maintenance of blood and tissue volume (7), absorption and transport of dietary lipids from the intestine to the liver (8), and trafficking of immune cells (9–12). To accomplish these roles, the lymphatic system consists of a network of blind-ended capillaries that converge into precollecting and collecting vessels of varying characteristics that are connected through a series of lymph nodes and other lymphoid organs. Lymphatic networks within skeletal muscle, in general, follow the branching of arteriolar networks. For example, in rat cremaster muscle, lymphatic capillaries typically run alongside the arterial branches or wrap around the arcading and transverse arterioles and sometimes venules (13–15). In the lower extremities, while lymphatic capillaries and precollectors are present alongside skeletal muscle fibers (16–18), the collecting muscular lymphatic vessels are detected in the perimysium next to arteries and veins (19). Thus, lymph transport in the skeletal muscle largely depends on muscle contractions. In the absence of skeletal muscle contraction, lymph flow is propelled by the intrinsic pumping activity of collecting lymphatic vessels and moving lymph through popliteal and inguinal nodes in the legs.

Lymphatic vessels are highly dynamic in nature, undergoing both morphological and functional changes under pathological conditions. An increase in inflammatory lymphangiogenesis has been reported in human psoriasis, rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and in lymphedema patients (20–22). However, it is not yet clear whether this expansion of lymphatic networks in response to the different pathological conditions contributes to the resolution of inflammation or is itself part of the pathology (23). We have recently reported that a mouse model of amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), the SOD1-G93A mouse, exhibits impairments in lymphatic contractile activity and delayed lymph transport function that are associated with inflammation and lymphatic marker upregulation (24). Another study (25) showing that expression of the lymphatic endothelial cell marker LYVE1 was decreased in muscles undergoing degeneration due to dystrophin deficiency (3 to 4-wk-old mdx mice) and in laminin α2 null mice at the earliest stages of disease progression (7-d-old mice). However, a thorough investigation of how skeletal muscle lymphatic networks and transport function are dysregulated in DMD animals has not been previously undertaken.

Hence, in this study, we focused on investigating the lymphatic structure and function in the D2.mdx mouse model and in the golden retriever muscular dystrophy (GRMD) dog model of DMD. Our findings show that in DMD animals, lymphatic transport function is decreased and associated with the inflammatory status of the muscle tissues.

Results

Lymphatic Muscle Cells Express Dystrophin Protein.

Since lymphatic muscle cells (LMCs) are composed of both striated and smooth muscle contractile elements (26), we first wanted to determine the expression of dystrophin isoforms in lymphatic muscle. To assess the expression of dystrophin and its associated glycoprotein complex, we analyzed our recent single-cell RNA sequencing dataset of the mouse inguinal-axillary (flank) lymphatic vessels (27). UMAP showed four clusters of LMCs (Fig. 1A) that were confirmed as LMCs by expression of the smooth muscle marker Acta2 (Fig. 1B) and the vascular and lymphatic mural muscle cell marker Itga8 (Fig. 1C), although there were significantly differentially expressed genes across the 4 subclusters (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). As seen in Fig. 1C, dystrophin (Dmd) and the dystrophin-associated glycoprotein (DAP) complex, α and β dystroglycan (Dag1), specific isoforms of sarcoglycans (Sgc-a, b, d, e, g, and z), sarcospan (Sspn), syntrophin (Snta1, Sntb1, Sntb2, Sntg1, and Sntg2), and dystrobrevin (Dtna and Dtnb) were expressed at various levels across the four LMC subclusters. LMCs from subcluster “0” had significantly higher expression of Dmd (Fig. 1E), Sntb2 (Fig. 1F), and Dtna (Fig. 1G) compared to other LMCs, while LMCs from subcluster 2 had a significant reduction in Sntb2 expression and LMCs from subcluster 3 had significantly lower expression of Dmd and multiple DAP genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B).

Whether dystrophin and DAP gene expression is unique to LMCs as compared to vascular muscle has not previously been assessed. The lymphatic vasculature is a low-pressure system as compared to the pressures recorded in many arterial networks; however, the pulmonary artery is a relatively low-pressure and low-oxygen saturation environment that resembles the pressure and oxygen saturation environment in which LMCs exist. We compared the LMC expression of Dmd and DAP genes to pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) from a previously published 10× scRNAseq dataset (GEO #GSE210248) (28) containing PASMCs harvested from 3 male control mice (GSM6424661). PASMCs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) were identified by the expression of Myh11 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D) and Itga8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E) and the absence of the pulmonary pericyte marker Higd1b (SI Appendix, Fig. S1F) (29). The LMCs and PASMCs were integrated (Fig. 1H) and assessed for differentially expressed genes (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G). We observed multiple significant differences in both Dmd expression and dichotomous expression of multiple DAP genes (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). LMCs and PASMCs were both positive for smooth muscle markers Acta2 (Fig. 1J), Myh11 (Fig. 1K), Tagln (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I), and Itga8 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1J). LMCs had significantly higher expression of Sgcd (Fig. 1L), Dmd (Fig. 1M), Dtna (Fig. 1N), Sntb2 (Fig. 1O), and Sgce (Fig. 1P). In contrast, PASMCs had significantly higher expression of Sgcb (Fig. 1Q) and Sgcg (Fig. 1R) compared to LMCs. Sntb1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1K) and Snta1 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1L) were expressed at statistically higher levels in LMCs but failed to meet the thresholds for either Log2FC (>1 or <–1) or percent expression (>40%). For some of the DAPs, the differential expression is likely in part due to significant differences in the percent of cells expressing the gene, such as was the case for Sgcd (95.45 LMCs vs 22.7% PASMCs, SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). Thus, Sgcd has the potential to be used to assist in discriminating LMCs from vascular smooth muscle. Despite widespread expression of Dmd in both LMCs (96.8%) and PASMCs (74.8%), LMCs still had Log2FC of 3.72 higher expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). These findings underscore the robust expression of dystrophin and DAP genes in LMCs.

We then performed immunofluorescence microscopy on isolated flank lymphatic vessels from control and D2.mdx mice using a dystrophin antibody that recognizes several dystrophin isoforms (Dp). A representative image of a lymphatic vessel from control mouse stained for dystrophin and α-vascular smooth muscle actin (SMA) showed a stochiometric expression of dystrophin along with α-SMA in the walls of lymphatic vessels indicating that dystrophin protein is expressed in the LMCs (Fig. 2 A, Top panel). The isotype control did not give any significant positive signal intensity (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Interestingly, the lymphatic vessels of the D2.mdx animals also showed positive expression of dystrophin protein (Fig. 2 A, Bottom panel), but disorganized fiber alignment in the walls of the D2.mdx lymphatic vessels was noted. Quantification of the fiber alignment distribution revealed that the fibers on the control DBA vessels were uniformly oriented, resulting in a normally distributed bell curve with most fibers distributed around −10 to +10°. In D2.mdx, there were several peaks indicating a haphazard orientation (Fig. 2 B, Top and Bottom panels and SI Appendix, Supplemental Figures B and C). Additionally, the quantification of the fluorescence intensity of the vessels demonstrated that while the SMA level was not significantly different in the D2.mdx vessel wall, total dystrophin protein expression was significantly decreased (Fig. 2 C and D). To further determine the presence of dystrophin isoforms in the LMCs, western blot analysis was performed in cell lysates of primary LMCs isolated from flank lymphatic vessels of control and D2.mdx mice. As seen in Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D), while Dp71 was predominantly present both in the wildtype mouse LMCs and in D2.mdx LMCs, a small amount of Dp427 was found in control LMCs but was not detected in cell lysates from D2.mdx LMCs. Additionally, Dp140 was detectable in both control and D2.mdx LMCs. Interestingly, control rat mesenteric LMCs (RmLMCs) clearly expressed Dp427, Dp116, and Dp71. Tissue lysates from control mouse tibialis anterior (TA) and brain tissues showed the expected predominant presence of Dp427 and Dp71 isoforms, respectively. These data suggest that Dp427 and Dp71 isoforms of dystrophin were present in healthy mouse LMCs, and the absence of Dp427 in LMCs of D2.mdx lymphatic vessels might have caused the disruption of the LMC fiber structure on the walls of lymphatic vessels in DMD animals.

Impaired Lymph Transport and Lymphatic Vessel Contractile Function in mdx Mice.

In vivo lymphatic function in the D2.mdx mice was determined using fluorescence microlymphangiography to quantify lymph transport in the lower pelvic limbs of control and D2.mdx mice at different ages. The representative fluorescence microlymphangiography images taken at different time points indicating the transport of FITC-Dextran (green color) through the collecting lymphatic vessels alongside the lower limb are shown in Fig. 3A for the 1-, 2-, and 8-mo-old control and D2.mdx mice, respectively. The time to attain maximum intensity fluorescence at a specified region of the lymphatic vessel was significantly longer in mdx mice of all age groups compared to the respective control groups; for 1-mo groups (in mins): control, 8.733 ± 2.03 vs. D2.mdx mice, 15.38 ± 2.73 for 2-mo groups: control, 9.37 ± 1.13 vs. D2.mdx mice, 17.77 ± 2.63; and for 8-mo groups: control 9.16 ± 1.53 vs. D2.mdx mice, 17.85 ± 2.40 (Fig. 3 A and B). These results demonstrate that lymph transport was significantly slower in D2.mdx animals during the early and later stages of DMD pathogenesis.

Since lymph transport was significantly affected in D2.mdx mice, we wanted to determine the contractile activity of lymphatic vessels and performed isolated lymphatic vessel studies using the flank collecting lymphatic vessels from 2-mo-old control and D2.mdx mice. Fig. 3C showed representative diameter tracings of pressurized (1, 2, and 4 cmH2O pressures) lymphatic vessels isolated from control and D2.mdx mice. A summary of lymphatic vessel calculated contractile parameter values for frequency, fractional pump flow, tone index, amplitude, and ejection fraction is provided in Table 1. The phasic contractile frequency for the lymphatic vessels of the D2.mdx animals was significantly reduced at the higher pressure, P = 4cmH2O but not at lower pressures P = 1 or 2 cm H20 (Fig. 3D). The fractional pump flow and contraction amplitude of the D2.mdx animals were severely impaired with a significant reduction in their values (P < 0.05) when compared with the vessels of the control animals at all tested transluminal pressures (Fig. 3 E and F). Additionally, the lymphatic tonic index (Fig. 3G) of the DMD animals was significantly increased at P = 1 and 4 cm H2O. Thus, these data demonstrate that lymphatic vessels from D2.mdx mice exhibit an increase in tonic contraction and a decrease in phasic contractile parameters.

Pathological Lymphangiogenesis in Pelvic Limb Muscles of mdx Mice.

Lymphatic vessels play a vital role in the immunopathogenesis of various diseases. Although inflammation has been extensively reported in the dystrophin-deficient muscles of DMD animals, there is a lack of evidence on pathological lymphangiogenesis and DMD pathology. To evaluate pathological lymphangiogenesis, we used RT-qPCR to measure mRNA levels of six different lymphangiogenic markers. Quadriceps (QA) of D2.mdx animals showed significant upregulation of lymphatic endothelial cell (LEC) markers [LYVE-1 and podoplanin (Pdpn)], and lymphangiogenesis growth factors (VEGF-C and VEGF-D) mRNA when compared with the QA of the control animals; Prox1, Pdpn, and VEGF-D mRNA expression were significantly increased in the extensor digitorum longus (EDL) muscle of D2.mdx mice (Fig. 4 A and B) compared to control. Additionally, there was a differential expression of LEC marker genes across other skeletal muscles, TA, gastrocnemius (GA), and soleus (SOL) muscles (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A–C). Western blots showed increased Pdpn protein expression in D2.mdx vs control muscle (Fig. 4 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Furthermore, there were significant increases in the expressions of 15 proinflammatory markers across all skeletal muscle groups examined from D2.mdx mice compared to control animals (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A–E).

To further determine lymphatic vessel density, we next evaluated the LYVE-1 positive lumen-containing lymphatic capillaries in the skeletal muscles by immunostaining. We used laminin staining to show the sarcolemma of skeletal muscles in the control and D2.mdx groups and used the ratio of LYVE-1 staining lumen-containing vessels to laminin positive staining for the quantification analyses. Fig. 4E showed the representative QA and GA muscles from control and D2.mdx mice stained with laminin and LYVE-1 antibodies. Representative immunostaining of laminin and LYVE-1 for other muscle groups was presented in SI Appendix, Fig. S5. LYVE-1 positive lymphatic capillaries were detected between the laminin-stained muscle sarcolemma in both control and D2.mdx muscles. Quantification showed that LYVE-1 positive lumen-containing lymphatic capillaries were significantly increased in QA, EDL, and GA muscles of D2.mdx mice when compared to their respective control muscle groups (Fig. 4F). As LYVE1 antibody can also stain macrophages, we further performed double LYVE1-Pdpn or LYVE1-PROX1 staining to confirm the specificity of the antibodies to lymphatic vessels along with the appropriate isotype controls. Colocalized staining of LYVE1-Pdpn and LYVE1-PROX1 lm-containing lymphatic vessels were found in all the skeletal muscles (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Interestingly, only either LYVE1+ or Pdpn+ or Prox1+ vessel-like structures were also detected in all the muscles, suggesting a heterogeneous nature of lymphatic vessels in skeletal muscle, consistent with what has been shown in other tissues before (30–32). The isotype control did not show any positive staining for the lymphatic markers. Altogether, these results suggest a correlation between pathological lymphangiogenesis and inflammation in DMD pathogenesis.

Impaired Lymphatic Function and Pathological Lymphangiogenesis in GRMD Dogs.

To further test our idea that there is a link between lymphatic dysfunction and DMD pathogenesis, we determined lymphatic function in a clinically relevant animal model, the GRMD dog, which carries a spontaneous splice site mutation in the DMD gene; the phenotype of GRMD dogs closely resembles that of DMD patients (33). We employed gadolinium-enhanced magnetic resonance lymphangiography (MRL) as described in the Methods. Fig. 5A shows the time-resolved lymphangiography with interleaved stochastic trajectories (TWIST) sequences of reconstructed images taken at 20, 500, and 1,000 s after the injection of gadolinium-based contrast in the carrier and GRMD dogs. The images showed the collecting lymphatic vessels in both the superficial and deep tibial regions up to the popliteal lymph node in carrier and GRMD dogs. For lymph transport quantification, we measured contrast density measurements from the TWIST sequence images obtained at different time points at the region of interest, the popliteal lymph node, and normalized that to the length of lymphatic vessel from the site of contrast injection. The calculated normalized intensity of the contrast agent revealed a marked reduction starting at 566 s postinjection until the end of the study in the GRMD dogs when compared with the carrier dogs (Fig. 5B). This decrease in intensity suggests a slower lymph transport in GRMD dogs, consistent with our observations in D2.mdx mice (Fig. 3 A and B).

To assess lymphangiogenesis in GRMD dogs, we examined the expression of Pdpn in pelvic limb skeletal muscles (vastus lateralis (VL), cranial tibial (CT), and medial head gastrocnemius (MHG)) and diaphragm, also known to be severely affected in advanced stages of DMD. As shown in Fig. 5C, a significant upregulation of Pdpn gene expression was detected in all skeletal muscle groups and diaphragms, but not left ventricle of the heart, of GRMD dogs when compared to the respective muscles from normal animals. Additionally, several inflammatory cytokines and chemokines were increased in the muscle groups from GRMD dogs when compared to the normal dogs (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 A-D)

Microdystrophin Treatment in GRMD Dogs Mitigated Pathological Lymphangiogenesis.

We had previously reported that AAV-microdystrophin-5 (µDys5) treatment showed significant microdystrophin expression and improved muscle function at the highest dosage (2x10^14 vg/kg BW) in GRMD dogs (33). To further establish the link between muscle function and the lymphatic system, we examined whether the pathological lymphangiogenesis and inflammation that we observed in the GRMD dogs were lessened in the AAV-µDys treated group. Gene expression analysis revealed that the proinflammatory cytokine interleukin-6 (IL-6) was significantly decreased in the cranial tibial (CT) muscle and trended downward in other skeletal muscles of µDys-treated compared to placebo-treated GRMD dogs (Fig. 5D). While the expression of podoplanin was upregulated in placebo-treated GRMD dogs, when compared to the control group, podoplanin expression was significantly decreased in the skeletal muscles of GRMD dogs receiving µDys treatment (Fig. 5E). Thus, these data demonstrating that µDys treatment lessens pathological lymphangiogenesis suggest that skeletal muscle function is closely associated with lymphatic function.

Discussion

This study provides evidence that 1) while Dp71kDa is predominantly present in both control and D2.mdx mouse LMCs, a small amount of Dp427kDa is detectable in control LMCs but not found in D2.mdx LMCs; 2) impairment in lymph transport function, pathological lymphangiogenesis in skeletal muscles and inflammation are associated with the DMD phenotype in the D2.mdx mouse model and in a clinically-relevant GRMD dog model; 3) the collecting lymphatic vessels of D2.mdx mice exhibit a decrease in phasic contractile activity along with an increase in tonic contraction; and 4) µDys treated GRMD dogs exhibit a decrease in pathological lymphangiogenesis.

Transporting interstitial fluid from the tissues is the major function of the lymphatic vessels, which ultimately helps in maintaining fluid homeostasis and the inflammatory status of the tissues. A longer time taken to transport the FITC-dextran dye in the lymphatic vessels of D2.mdx animals (Fig. 3 A–D) indicates that the lymph transport function is decreased in the 1-mo, 2-mo, and 8-mo-old D2.mdx mice. Additionally, MRI results show that the clinically relevant GRMD dog model exhibits an impairment in lymphatic transport function (Fig. 5). Our data showing a decrease in lymph transport in DMD mice and dog models are consistent with the clinical studies that demonstrated skeletal muscle edema in boys with DMD (34). The phasic contractile indices of the isolated lymphatic vessels from the D2.mdx mice are diminished, whereas tone is enhanced (Fig. 3). Additionally, several inflammatory cytokines and chemokines are significantly increased in all the muscles from the 2-mo-old D2.mdx mice and in the muscles of the GRMD dogs (SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S7), which is consistent with previous studies (33, 35–36). Thus, the impairments in lymphatic contractile activity lead to a decrease in lymph transport in the 1-mo-old D2.mdx mice, which is followed by the inflammation in 2-mo-old D2.mdx mice. Consequently, the increase in the inflammatory state of the DMD animals further affects the lymphatic contractile activity in the later stages of DMD pathogenesis, as we have observed a decrease in lymph transport in the 8-mo-old D2.mdx mice (Fig. 3). Taken together, our data demonstrate that lymphatic dysfunction precedes inflammation and might be the cause, or the reason inflammation occurs before the muscle pathology occurs in the DMD pathogenesis process.

Lymphatic vessel contraction relies on the coordination of both extrinsic and intrinsic forces, maintaining consistent frequency, amplitude, and duration to propel lymph against hydrostatic pressure gradients in a mammal’s body. Action potentials (AP) initiate the spontaneous contraction in the LMCs, and this myogenic event spreads along the lymphatic vessel (27). Lack of dystrophin has been reported to result in action potential abnormalities and altered calcium handling, leading to progressive weakening and fragile skeletal muscles in mdx mice (37). Previous research reports on morphological changes and internal organelle damage in the smooth muscle cells of DMD animals suggest that dystrophin plays an important role in maintaining the structural and functional integrity of smooth muscle cells. Dystrophin is specifically expressed in caveolae-rich domains of the sarcolemma in smooth muscle cells, preventing membrane damage during contraction (38). The confocal microscopy results revealed disrupted expression patterns of dystrophin and SMA in the walls of lymphatic vessels of D2.mdx mice, with reduced dystrophin staining compared to controls (Fig. 2 A-D), suggesting the absence of 427 kDa isoform in the D2.mdx LMCs. However, protein expression analysis by western blot (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D) demonstrated barely detectable levels of the Dp427kDa isoform in the control mice flank lymphatics LMCs but was absent in LMCs of D2.mdx mice, which is consistent with the known point mutation in exon 23 of the DMD gene in D2.mdx mice. Nevertheless, Dp427kDa is clearly present in the normal rat mesenteric LMCs (RmLMCs) (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). We also observed expression of Dp116 in RmLMCs, a dystrophin isoform typically associated with Schwann cells (39). This observation might reflect the cellular heterogeneity of rat LMCs, consistent with prior research demonstrating that the expression of contractile proteins varies among LMCs from various tissue beds. Particularly, rat LMCs have been found to exhibit higher contractile activity and express a wider variety of contractile proteins than their mouse counterparts, which typically exhibit a weaker lymphatic pumping phenotype (26, 40–41). Taken together, further detailed quantification of dystrophin isoforms in mouse lymphatics from various beds is warranted.

Studies in smooth muscle dysfunction during DMD pathogenesis indicate that lack of dystrophin resulted in smooth muscle abnormalities, both structural and functional anomalies, vascular insufficiency, aberration of the neurovascular system, abnormal vasoconstriction, and impaired vasoregulation (42–44). Other studies on mdx dystrophic gastric and intestinal smooth muscle cells have revealed impaired nitrergic relaxation and an increase in spontaneous tone (44). The findings presented in this study show lymphatic collecting vessel dysfunction in the D2.mdx mice, which is consistent with previous findings in vascular smooth muscle cells highlighting impaired vasoregulation (34, 43, 44). Further studies are warranted to assess the changes in the expression of contractile and calcium regulatory proteins associated with dystrophin deficiency in LMCs to provide insights into the role of dystrophin in the regulation of contractile activity of lymphatic vessels.

Exercise-induced hyperemia causes net capillary infiltration in skeletal muscles, thereby increasing hydrostatic and colloid osmotic pressures in the interstitial fluid that then increases lymph flow to maintain fluid homeostasis in muscle tissue (45). In chronic inflammatory conditions, increased lymphangiogenesis has been reported to improve the lymph transport; however, the expansion of lymphatic network during the inflammatory conditions is neither functional nor sufficient. Our data showing the upregulation of podoplanin and Lyve-1 positive lymphatic capillaries (Fig. 4) in the skeletal muscle groups provides evidence of pathological lymphangiogenesis associated with DMD pathophysiology. Both PROX1 and Pdpn colocalized with LYVE1 at multiple sites in most muscle sections (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B), validating the presence of LYVE1+ lymphatic vessels. The presence of LYVE1- but either Pdpn+ or PROX1+ staining of lymphatic vessels is consistent with the heterogeneity of lymphatic vessels in skeletal muscles reported in other tissues (30–32). This increase in lymphangiogenesis is not accompanied with an increase in lymph transport in the DMD animals suggesting a pathological phenotype, as previously reported in the other disease models (21, 46). IL-6 exhibited a substantial decrease in the skeletal muscles of µDys-treated GRMD dogs (Fig. 5D). IL-6 is overproduced in dystrophic muscle and IL-6 is preferentially portioned to undergo uptake by the lymphatic capillaries over blood capillaries due to its size (47). IL-6 also increases lymphatic permeability (48) and induces VEGF-C expression and lymphangiogenesis in lymphatic endothelial cells (49–50). This reduction in IL-6 expression aligns with the observed improvements in muscle function, suggesting that microdystrophin gene therapy not only enhances muscle performance but may also alleviate the chronic inflammatory state that is characteristic of DMD pathology (51). Furthermore, podoplanin expression was significantly decreased in the muscles of GRMD dogs following µDys treatment, suggesting inflammatory lymphangiogenesis was lessened. The combination of decreased IL-6 and podoplanin expression in µDys-treated GRMD dogs highlights the multifaceted benefits of the therapy. These findings suggest that beyond restoring dystrophin expression and improving muscle function, µDys treatment exerts anti-inflammatory effects and may help to regulate the abnormal lymphatic remodeling seen in DMD animals. Our future studies will address whether µDys treatment improves lymphatic function in these animal models.

In conclusion, the data presented in this study provide the first evidence that dystrophic pathophysiology negatively impacts the contractile function of lymphatic vessels, which may contribute to chronic inflammation in DMD. The present study also demonstrates a link between skeletal muscle function and the lymphatic system, thus providing insights into disease pathogenesis and opens the door to a potential therapeutic target for the treatment of DMD.

Methods

All animals were provided with care and treatment followed by the guidelines of the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. All the experimental procedures were evaluated and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Texas A&M University. This study used male mdx mice of the DBA/2 J background, referred to as D2.mdx (Jax# 013141) and the wildtype control DBA/2 J (Jax# 000671), initially obtained from Jackson Laboratory. A colony of these animals was established locally by crossing hemizygous D2.mdx male mice with homozygous D2.mdx females. This study also used golden retriever dogs with muscular dystrophy (GRMD). Lymph transport function in mice was determined by microlymphangiography and by employing isolated lymphatic vessel preparations, the collecting lymphatic vessel contractile activities were examined. Magnetic resonance lymphangiography evaluated lymph transport function in dogs. Published ScRNAseq dataset of mouse flank lymphatic vessels and mouse pulmonary arterial muscle cells were used in ScRNAseq data analyses. RNA isolated from skeletal muscle tissues were used to quantify lymphangiogenic marker genes and inflammatory cytokines and chemokines expression. Immunohistochemistry and western blot techniques were employed to determine the dystrophin isoforms in LMCs and lymphatic vessel density in skeletal muscle tissues. Appropriate statistical analyses depend upon the experimental groups, student t test, 1-way, or 2-way ANOVA with post hoc analyses were done. All values were expressed as mean ± SEM.
