Abdominal Pain After Bariatric Surgery and the Role of the Gut: A Review
Authors: Bregje de Louweren, Max Nieuwdorp, Victor E. A. Gerdes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Obesity is a major health concern, affecting over 1 in 8 people worldwide. Bariatric surgery (BS) is currently the most effective long-term treatment for morbid obesity. In addition to sustained weight loss, BS is beneficial in treating obesity related comorbidities including dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes (T2DM). The beneficial effects of BS are a result of weight loss and surgery-induced shifts in the gut microbiota and its metabolites. At the same time, BS may also lead to complications and side effects. Abdominal pain is one of the most frequently reported complaints after BS with a prevalence of 33.8–54.4% within this patient group. However, in many patients the abdominal pain remains unexplained beyond gallstones, internal herniation, and ulcers. This raises the question whether the gut microbiota itself may play a direct role in the pathophysiology of unexplained abdominal pain. Over the years several studies have shown changes in the gut microbiota and related metabolites after BS. These include increased gut microbial diversity and altered microbial composition after BS. Higher abundances of Proteobacteria and Fusobacteria are reported, while a decrease in butyrate-producing Firmicutes is reported. Along with these changes in microbiota, BS causes higher plasma bile acid levels and altered short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles. These metabolic shifts are believed to support weight control, glucose regulation, and lipid metabolism. More recently, specific microbial taxa and metabolite profiles were linked to abdominal complaints following BS. This suggests that dysbiosis and metabolites may play a role in unexplained abdominal pain after BS.

Introduction

Obesity is one of the major health concerns worldwide, with a reported prevalence of over 1 in 8 people [1]. Along with the emerging prevalence of obesity, an increased number of bariatric surgeries are being performed, which currently represent the most effective long-term treatment options for severe obesity [2–4]. In addition to promoting sustainable weight loss, bariatric surgery (BS) has proven to be beneficial in the management of metabolic diseases associated with obesity, such as dyslipidemia and type 2 diabetes (T2DM) [2–6]. Several studies have focused on the underlying mechanisms responsible for these beneficial metabolic effects after BS. It has been shown that, in addition to weight loss and increased incretin levels, changes in the gut microbiota play a significant role in the metabolic improvements observed following BS [7, 8]. Despite the positive outcomes, several side effects are also reported. Abdominal pain is the most common complaint after BS, with a prevalence of 33.8–54.4% [9–11]. Patients who have undergone BS frequently present at the emergency department with acute or non-acute but severe abdominal pain. These symptoms can result from complications such as symptomatic gallstone disease, anastomotic ulcer, or internal herniation [4, 9–11]. In addition, a study by van Olst et al. showed that 38.5% of patients who have undergone BS who initially presented at the emergency department continued to experience unexplained abdominal pain during several months of follow-up [4]. Symptoms in patients with unexplained abdominal pain after BS resemble those seen in irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). Interestingly, multiple studies have shown the involvement of gut microbiota in the pathophysiology of IBS. It has been suggested that alterations in the composition of the gut microbiota could play a role in triggering low-grade inflammation associated with IBS [12–17].

These findings raise the question whether the gut microbiota may be involved in patients who underwent BS and experience unexplained abdominal pain.

This narrative review aims to provide an overview of the role of the gut microbiota and beneficial metabolic effects observed after BS, such as improvements in glucose and lipid metabolism. In addition, it explores the potential link between the gut microbiota and abdominal pain following BS.

This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

BS: Weight Loss and Beneficial Metabolic Effects

BS has been shown to be the most effective treatment for severe obesity, leading to substantial weight loss. Laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (LRYGB) and laparoscopic sleeve gastrectomy (LSG) are the most performed procedures. LRYGB leads to more weight loss compared to LSG, but both have excellent short-term and long-term results. The mean total weight loss (TWL) after 1 year is approximately 30.0% for LRYGB and 28% for LSG, while mean long-term TWL is approximately 25.0% for LRYGB and 22.0% for LSG [18, 19]. These mean results indicate that many patients have some weight regain over time. However, long-term follow-up data demonstrate that a significant proportion of patients show sustainable weight loss results. Adams et al. observed weight loss and metabolic outcomes over a 12-year follow-up period including 1156 patients, of whom 418 underwent BS [6]. The study reported an average TWL of about 35.0% after 2 years, with durable effects of 28.0% at 6 years follow-up and 27.0% at 12 years [6]. In a 10-year follow-up study Maciejewski et al. observed a TWL of at least 20.0% in 71.8% of patients who underwent LRYGB [18]. These findings are consistent with results from Sjöström et al. and Sundbom et al., who reported similar outcomes a decade after BS (73.5% of patients) and LRYGB (67.4%), respectively [20, 21]. Thus, BS not only leads to significant short-term weight loss but also leads to sustained weight loss in the majority of patients.

Furthermore, BS has also been associated with significantly improved (postprandial) lipid metabolism. In a recent cohort study performed by Ulas Biter et al., remission of dyslipidemia was observed in 56.9% of patients following LRYGB and 42.3% after LSG [19]. Adams et al. observed remission in 60.0% of patients at 12-year follow-up in the surgical group, compared with 19% in the nonsurgical group [6]. Surgery was associated with higher high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels and lower triglycerides compared to the control group [6]. These findings are consistent with those reported by Peterli et al., who reported 5-year remission rates of 62.3% for LRYGB and 42.6% for LSG [22]. In contrast, Sundbom et al. reported lower remission rates, with complete remission observed in only 21.1% of patients at 2 years post-LRYGB and 15.1% at 10 years [21]. In addition, Mingrone et al. and Courcoulas et al. reported improvement of lipid levels after BS, with more favorable outcomes for triglycerides and HDL cholesterol levels, leading to fewer patients requiring lipid-lowering medication [23, 24]. These improvements of lipid profiles following BS are believed to result from a combination of underlying mechanisms, including weight loss itself, alterations in adipose tissue distribution and function, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and improvements in hepatic lipoprotein metabolism [25–27]. Besides its positive effects on weight and lipid metabolism, BS has profound beneficial effects on (postprandial) glucose metabolism. The improved glycemic control is thought to be mediated by weight loss, improved insulin sensitivity, reduced systemic inflammation, alterations in the gut microbiota, and bile acid profiles [28–30]. Courcoulas et al. reported that diabetes remission was achieved in 18.2% of patients following BS at 7-year follow-up with a mean glycated hemoglobin (HbA1C) reduction of − 1.6% compared to medically treated patients, leading to a lower need for diabetes medication in the BS group [24]. In a long-term follow-up study by Adams et al., 75.0% of patients that underwent LRYGB achieved remission at 2 years, 62.0% at 6 years, and 51.0% at 12-year follow-up [6]. The study reported that the surgical group had a reduced risk of about 91.0–92.0% of developing new onset T2DM at 12 years [6]. Sundbom et al. reported complete remission of T2DM in 54.7% of patients 2 years following LRYGB, although this rate declined to 29.7% at 10 years, suggesting the possibility of relapse over time [21]. In line with these findings, Mingrone et al. reported that diabetes remission was maintained at 5-year follow-up in 37.0% of patients who underwent LRYGB [23]. Those authors reported that BS led to long-term reductions in fasting glucose and insulin resistance [23]. However, Ulas Biter et al. observed significantly higher remission rates 5 years after surgery, with 56.5% of patients achieving remission after LRYGB and 55.6% after LSG [19]. These outcomes are in line with findings from Peterli et al., who reported 5-year remission rates of 67.9% and 61.5% after LRYGB and LSG, respectively [22]. Together, these findings highlight the substantial, yet variable, long-term impact of BS on T2DM, with differences likely influenced by patient characteristics, surgical technique, and follow-up duration. Complications and Side Effects of BS

While BS has proven to be the most effective long-term intervention for severe obesity and obesity-related comorbidities, it is also associated with adverse effects. Surgical complications that may occur during the first weeks after the operation include bleeding, anastomotic leakage, pneumonia or other infections, thrombosis, and stenosis [32]. Mortality at 30 days is around 0.5% after LRYGB [33]. Long-term complications include nutritional deficiencies, explained and unexplained abdominal pain, postprandial hypoglycemia, cholelithiasis, excess skin, and decreased bone mineral density leading to osteoporosis. Of these adverse effects, abdominal pain is one of the most common reasons for presentation to the emergency department [2, 34].

Abdominal Pain After BS, Causes and the Common Risk Factors

Abdominal pain is among the most frequently reported adverse effect following BS, with prevalence rates ranging from 33.8% to 54.4% [4, 9–11]. While mild, transient pain is expected during the early postoperative period, chronic or recurrent abdominal pain remains a challenge in long-term follow-up. In a prospective study by van Olst et al., 441 patients presenting to the emergency department with abdominal pain after BS were followed for 3 months to identify possible causes of abdominal pain [4]. A definitive diagnosis was found in 61.5% of cases, while in the other 38.5% of cases no explanation was found. Diagnoses that were more common included cholecystolithiasis (13.6%), anastomotic ulcer (9.8%), internal herniation (7.5%), IBS, anterior cutaneous nerve entrapment syndrome (ACNES), and constipation [4]. Internal herniation, though relatively uncommon with a reported prevalence of 2.5% according to a meta-analysis by Geubbels et al., represents a potentially life-threatening cause of acute abdominal pain [36]. Rapid weight loss after BS and the consequent reduction in intra-abdominal fat are considered key predisposing factors [4, 36]. Cholecystolithiasis is another common complication after BS, obesity itself is an established risk factor, and rapid weight loss further after BS further increases gallstone formation by altering the composition of bile acids [4, 37]. Anastomotic ulcers are also common after BS, with a suspected multifactorial etiology involving surgical technique, acid exposure, use of ulcerogenic medication, and lifestyle choices such as smoking [4, 38]. Constipation is also a well-known complaint after BS. As demonstrated by Afshar et al., it is suggested that BS slows intestinal transit time, and when combined with reduced dietary fiber intake, this may lead to constipation and even obstipation [39, 40]. Emerging evidence further indicates the key role of the gut microbiota and its metabolites in postoperative weight loss, beyond the effects of caloric restriction, and they may also contribute to the persistence of unexplained abdominal pain.

Gut Microbiota and Its Metabolites and Abdominal Pain

The gut microbiota is a complex ecosystem containing a diversity of intestinal microorganisms (comprising viruses, phages, fungi, and bacterial strains) that play a central role in host metabolism. The bacterial component of the gut microbiota plays an important role in the fermentation of otherwise indigestible dietary substrates, with the help of metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and bile acid derivatives. These metabolites are responsible for nutrient absorption and could lead to gas formation through the fermentation process. Importantly, these metabolites play a role within the intestinal environment and also play a role in systemic effects on host physiology by impacting metabolic regulation, immune function, and the energy balance [41].

Over the years, several studies have shown an interaction between the gut microbiota and abdominal pain. This could be explained by alterations in gut microbiota and its metabolites leading to increased visceral hypersensitivity and thus abdominal pain [42, 43]. The human gut microbiota comprises approximately 104 bacterial species and is known to have an equal amount of genetic information as our human genome [44]. The composition of the gut microbiota is determined in the first years of life, with external factors playing an important role. Key factors include type of birth, breast or bottle feeding, administration of antibiotics in early life, diet, and stress [45–47].

Previous studies have shown that a dysregulation of the (small) intestinal microbiota could lead to gastrointestinal disorders like IBS and celiac disease [48]. Amaral et al. reported that germ-free mice that were exposed to several inflammatory factors showed higher production of the inflammation marker interleukin-10, which is believed to cause lower hypernociceptive responses, therefore leading to a higher abdominal pain threshold [49]. In contrast, Luczynski et al. reported that upregulation of Toll-like receptors and cytokines resulted in visceral hypersensitivity in germ-free mice [50].

In addition, administration of probiotics was shown to lower abdominal pain responses, as reported by McKernan et al., who studied the administration of three probiotics in rats that experienced abdominal pain due to colorectal distension [51]. They reported that admission of Bifidobacterium infantis 35624 resulted in less pain in both the normosensitive and the visceral sensitive rats [51]. In line with these results, Ringel-Kulka et al. found that admission of Lactobacillus acidophilus NCFM reduced gut motility and showed beneficial effects on bloating and abdominal pain in patients [52]. Even though findings are inconsistent, current evidence does suggest that intestinal microbiota and its composition may play a role in the development of abdominal pain [49, 50]. Notably, much of the current research investigating changes in the gut microbiota is derived from animal studies, particularly mouse models. While these studies provide valuable insights, findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

IBS

Changes in the gut microbiota have been studied more intensively in patients with IBS. These patients are believed to experience symptoms due to enhanced visceral sensitivity and aberrant gut–brain signaling. Although the underlying cause of IBS has yet to be discovered, alterations in the gut microbiota, characterized by decreased microbial diversity and a decrease in overall strain abundance, and an increase of inflammatory markers, are believed to play a role in the development of IBS [53, 54]. In a study by Zhuang et al., patients with IBS showed microbial richness in Bacteroidetes, while the normally abundant phyla Firmicutes, Clostridiales, and Lactobacillales were decreased compared to healthy controls [55]. The study also suggested that changes in SCFAs, fermentation products of gut microbiota, could upregulate the mucosal immune system and thereby increase inflammatory responses in patients with IBS [55]. Sciavilla et al. also reported differences in gut microbiota in patients with IBS compared to healthy controls. Most notably, an increased abundance of gut microbiota with inflammatory involvement, including Enterobacteriaceae, Roseburia, Faecalibacterium, and Fusobacterium, was observed in patients with IBS [13, 56].

Currently, more studies focus on treatment strategies to treat abdominal pain due to alterations in the gut microbiota composition in patients with IBS. Besides the described studies on probiotics, studies on antibiotics (e.g., rifaximin), dietary interventions, and (donor) fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) have been performed. Pimentel et al. studied the use of rifaximin in patients with IBS; 40.8% of these patients reported adequate decrease in bloating and abdominal pain compared to 31.2% in the placebo group [57]. Worldwide, patients with IBS are often treated with adaptations of the FODMAP diet, in which certain foods that cause bloating and abdominal pain due to fermentation are avoided [58]. McIntosh et al. studied patients who followed either a 3-week low-FODMAP or high-FODMAP diet, and they reported the relief of symptoms in the group that followed the low-FODMAP diet [58]. Along with the treatments above, FMT has also been studied intensively in patients with IBS. In a systematic review comparing several studies regarding FMT in patients with IBS, the researchers reported no significant improvement after donor (allogeneic) FMT treatment [59]. However, although not statistically significant, donor-FMT seemed beneficial, as patients did report less IBS symptoms compared to controls that received autologous-FMT [59].

Small Intestinal Bacterial Overgrowth

Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) is another condition in which the composition of the gut microbiota is altered. Patients with SIBO show an abundance of colon-specific bacteria in the small intestine. Patients with SIBO show higher levels of inflammatory markers, resulting in chronic inflammation in the gut due to an increase in lipopolysaccharide permeability, eventually leading to symptoms such as abdominal pain, bloating, gas, and diarrhea [60–62]. These symptoms are similar to those observed by patients with IBS and may be explained by the co-occurrence of SIBO and IBS, as reported in several studies [60, 63, 64]. Giamarellos-Bourboulis et al. reported higher occurrence of Escherichia/Shigella spp. and Aeromonas spp. in patients with IBS with SIBO [65]. In addition, Kerckhoffs et al. reported a higher occurrence of Pseudomonas in patients with IBS compared to healthy controls [66]. Treatment options for SIBO have been evaluated in several studies, including antibiotic therapy and dietary restrictions. However, studies reporting effective treatment options remain limited, and recurrence of SIBO after treatment continues to be a challenge [60, 61]. In a study by Deng et al., patients who had surgery for colorectal cancer developed SIBO. Patients (n = 18) were treated with rifaximin for 10 days, which was effective in 33% of patients [67]. In another study, performed by Castiglione et al., the administration of ciprofloxacin (n = 14) versus metronidazole (n = 15) was assessed in patients with Crohn disease [68]. The study showed promising results, as the metronidazole and ciprofloxacin groups reported improvement of bloating in 85.0% vs 83.0% of patients, and relief of abdominal pain in 50.0% vs 43.0%, respectively [68]. The study reported recurrence of SIBO in only one patient at the 12-month follow-up [68]. Similar to IBS, a FODMAP diet could also be effective for managing SIBO, as it may reduce fermentation in the gut and thereby reduce symptoms. As described earlier, McIntosh et al. reported that a low-FODMAP diet in patients with IBS was associated with a greater gut microbiota diversity and less hydrogen production. Although this has not been specifically studied for SIBO, it is reasonable that similar treatment could be beneficial, since reducing fermentation may reduce gas production and consequently the symptoms [58].

Symptomatic Gallstone Disease

As discussed earlier, cholelithiasis is a common cause of abdominal pain, and the prevalence is higher in patients with obesity, even after BS. Studies have found an altered gut microbiota in patients with cholelithiasis. The diversity of gut microbiota decreases and there is an abundance of certain gut microbiota [69–71]. Wu et al. studied the gut microbiota in patients with cholelithiasis and reported higher levels of Proteobacteria and decreased levels of Faecalibacterium, Lachnospira, and Roseburia compared to healthy controls [72]. In a more recent study by Song et al. an increase in levels of Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria were reported, while a decrease was seen in Klebsiella, Roseburia, Collinsella, Dialister, and Enterobacer [71]. This dysbiosis may influence bile acid metabolism, as studies suggest that there is a decrease in SCFA-producing bacteria that are known to have a beneficial effect against gallstone formation [73]. Lyu et al. found that SCFA-producing bacteria Clostridium sensu stricto, Lachnospiraceae UCG-008, Butyrivibrio, and Roseburia were all decreased in patients with cholelithiasis [73].

Changes in Intestinal Microbiota and Its Metabolites Upon BS

Several studies have demonstrated that BS causes alterations in the composition and diversity of the gut microbiota. The most important changes include increase in overall microbial diversity, an abundance of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes species, and a reduction in Firmicutes species [7, 74–76].

Dang et al. performed a clinical trial to investigate changes in the gut microbiota in 80 patients, of whom 29 underwent LRYGB and 23 LSG [77]. The authors reported that patients who underwent LRYGB showed lower microbial diversity, increased levels of Proteobacteria, Verrucomicrobiota, and Desulfobacterota, and decreased levels of Firmicutes. In contrast, the LSG group only showed changes at 9 months, as Actinobacteriota was increased and levels of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota decreased [77].

In a cohort study by Fouladi et al., research data of 61 patients who underwent LRYGB were compared to other research data from comparable studies [78]. The authors reported enrichment of Firmicutes (Veillonella, Streptococcus), Verrucomicrobia (Akkermensia), and Proteobacteria (Escherichia) and a decrease in Blautia from the phylum Firmicutes [78]. Findings from the aforementioned study were compared to those of Palleja et al., who studied 13 patients who underwent LRYGB and also reported also significantly higher levels of Proteobacteria including Streptococcus species, Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Fusobacteria, after BS compared to baseline [7]. The study reported reduced amounts of butyrate-producing Faecalibacterium prausnitzii after BS [7]. In line with these previous studies, Steinert et al. investigated gut microbiota composition after BS in 16 patients and described an increase in Proteobacteria, Escherichia, Klebsiella, and Pseudomonas compared to healthy controls [79]. The study observed a decrease from the phylum Firmicutes (Blautia, Roseburia, and Faecalibacterium) and phylum Actinobacteria (Bifidobacterium) [79]. Yu et al., who studied changes in the gut microbiota in 20 patients after BS, also reported an increase in Proteobacteria after BS [80]. In contrast, this study did not find significant increased levels of the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes [80]. In another study, Sánchez-Alcoholado et al. focused on the potential differences in microbiota profiles between LRYGB and LSG. Increased levels of Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria were seen after surgery in 14 patients who underwent LRYGB, while levels of Bifidobacterium and Collinsella decreased [81]. In the 14 patients who underwent LSG, an increase in Lentisphaerae and Victivillaceae was seen and reduced levels of Clostridiaceae and Bifidobacterium [81]. Although these studies provide promising insights into postoperative shifts in the gut microbiota, current evidence remains limited. Many studies involve small sample sizes and often do not adequately account for confounders, such as diet, medication, and surgical technique/procedure.

While most changes in the gut microbiota are attributable to BS itself, it is important to recognize that these confounders may also influence the gut microbiota. Variations in postoperative diets, medications (e.g., proton pump inhibitor, probiotics, or antibiotics), and differences in surgical technique/procedure (e.g., limb length or anastomosis type) could each affect microbial composition and metabolic outcomes. These factors should be considered when interpreting microbiome findings after BS, as they may contribute to variation between studies.

These changes in the gut microbiota are thought to reflect postoperative changes in small intestinal pH, intestinal transit time, altered bile acid metabolism, and modified dietary intake. They are hypothesized to cause beneficial functional consequences in patients who have undergone BS [81]. Supporting this, Wahlström et al. studied changes in bile acids through postprandial bile acid kinetics in patients post LRYGB [82]. The authors observed increased postprandial levels of FGF19, similar to non-obese humans. These findings suggest upregulation of FXR in the small intestine after BS, which is believed to contribute to weight loss after BS [82]. Besides, studies of Ke et al. and Anhê et al. report that changes in the gut microbiota post LRYGB enhance glucose excretion and lower glucose absorption in the intestine, leading to improved (postprandial) glucose tolerance [30, 83]. Thus, the changes in gut microbiota are believed to cause enhanced production of metabolites that may contribute to weight loss and improve obesity-related comorbidities. Gut Microbiota and Abdominal Pain After BS

Beyond the beneficial metabolic effects, BS causes alterations in the gut microbiota that may contribute to postoperative abdominal pain and the related gastrointestinal symptoms. Despite increasing interest, evidence focusing specifically on this topic is still limited. A recent study by Custers et al. explored the influence of the gut microbiota in relation to abdominal pain in 65 patients; 40.0% of patients reported mild abdominal pain, while 32.3% reported moderate to severe abdominal pain [84]. Severity of abdominal complaints was linked to presence of specific microbiota. Higher abundances of taxa such as Barnesiella, Escherichia/Shigella, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and Holdemanella biformis correlated with more severe abdominal complaints. In contrast, Muribaculaceae, Desulfovibrio piger, Streptococcus, Acidaminococcus intestini, and Ruminococcus showed an inverse correlation with abdominal complaints. In addition, higher levels of the sulfated bile acid metabolite GLC-3S were detected in patients who reported abdominal complaints [84]. Custers et al. also reported on shifts in gut microbiota-derived metabolites, specifically higher plasma bile acids and elevated levels of the SCFAs acetic acid, caproic acid, butyric acid, and propionic acid; however, SCFAs isobutyric acid, methyl butyric acid, and isovaleric acid remained stable. Wijdeveld et al. prospectively studied 67 patients who underwent LRYGB and found that the composition of the gut microbiota is linked to abdominal complaints, both before and after surgery [85]. Preoperatively, higher abundances of butyrate-producing and anti-inflammatory taxa such as Lachnospira eligens, Ruminococcus faecicola, and Bacteroides fragilis were associated with fewer symptoms. In contrast, higher preoperative levels of Bifidobacterium ruminantium were associated with more symptoms. Postoperative enrichment of Bifidobacterium bifidum, Enterococcus faecalis, and Blautia was linked to more favorable outcomes regarding abdominal pain, whereas Bacteroides caccae and Paraprevotella clara correlated with more abdominal pain [85]. Taken together, these studies suggest that gut microbiota composition and its derived metabolites not only contribute to metabolic outcomes after BS but may also play a role in persistent abdominal pain. This highlights the need for more studies to establish causality. In case of a causal role, studies focusing on diagnostic and therapeutic strategies are warranted. To date, only two studies described the potential role of the gut microbiota in relation to abdominal pain after BS. These findings should therefore be interpreted with caution, while highlighting the need for further research to clarify this emerging association.

Conclusion

BS is an effective treatment for severe obesity and its obesity-related comorbidities and causes changes in the gut microbiota. Postoperative shifts in gut microbial composition and altered strain diversity, leading to altered bile acid and SCFA profiles, are thought to contribute to weight loss as well as to improved (postprandial) glucose and lipid metabolism. However, these changes in the gut microbiota and metabolites may also play a role in unexplained abdominal pain following BS. Several studies in patients who have not undergone BS suggest an influence of the gut microbiota on abdominal pain, and in recent studies, specific gut microbial taxa and metabolites were linked to abdominal complaints after BS. Given the substantial remission rates of T2DM observed following BS, these findings underscore the therapeutic relevance of the gut microbiota in diabetes care. Moving forward, a deeper understanding of microbial mechanisms may help refine or even replicate the metabolic benefits of BS through nonsurgical, microbiota-focused strategies.

This suggests that the gut microbiota plays a role in both metabolic benefits and abdominal pain. Further research is needed to achieve better understanding of the role of the gut microbiota in unexplained abdominal pain in order to explore treatment strategies aimed at reducing the burden of abdominal pain in patients who underwent BS. Future research should focus on interventional study designs, including probiotic supplementation as well as FMT. This could help to determine whether modifying the gut microbiota can treat abdominal pain after BS.
