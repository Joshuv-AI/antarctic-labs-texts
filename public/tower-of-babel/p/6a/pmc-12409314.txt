The first described case of Fomitiporella micropora infection in humans: A heart transplant recipient diagnosed by fungal PCR
Authors: Maria Daniela Jarrin Jara, Esperanza Villanueva-Siles, David Weithorn, Yoram A Puius, Phyu M Thwe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunocompromised individuals are susceptible to cutaneous mycoses, often following skin trauma. Although infections by Candida, Cryptococcus, Malassezia, Aspergillus, and Mucorales are well-documented, human infection by unusual basidiomycetes is rarely reported.We describe a 69-year-old heart transplant recipient with a cutaneous nodule that was surgically excised and identified via fungal PCR as Fomitiporella micropora, a basidiomycete not previously implicated in human disease. The patient received posaconazole, though surgery was likely curative. This case highlights the expanding spectrum of basidiomycete infections and underscores the importance of advanced molecular diagnostics in identifying rare fungal pathogens, particularly in immunocompromised hosts.Keywords: Fomitiporella micropora, Heart transplantation, Next-generation sequencing, Cutaneous mycosis

Introduction

Solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients on long-term immunosuppressive therapy are at heightened risk for fungal infections, including those caused by rare basidiomycetes. Although infections with these fungi are uncommon, they are becoming more recognized as opportunistic pathogens in immunocompromised patients. Fomitiporella micropora, a basidiomycete not previously reported as a human pathogen, was identified as the causative agent of a soft tissue infection in a heart transplant recipient at our institution. This case highlights the importance of molecular diagnostic techniques, such as nucleic acid sequencing, for identifying rare fungal infections and guiding appropriate treatment strategies.

Case presentation

A 69-year-old male from Trinidad with a history of rheumatic heart disease underwent orthotopic heart transplant in 2008. Other past medical history included well-controlled diabetes on metformin-dapagliflozin, IgA nephropathy, intermediate-risk prostate adenocarcinoma status post radiation, lumbar radiculopathy, and a positive Strongyloides serology in 2011, treated preemptively with ivermectin. The patient was on a regimen of mycophenolate and tacrolimus for immunosuppression.

In June of 2024, he presented to the outpatient clinic due to a nodule on the anterior side of his right shin. He reported the lesion had been present and slowly growing for many years and was not painful, without any accompanying erythema, discharge, or any other symptoms. On physical exam, a 3 cm rubbery, non-mobile, non-tender mass was noted below the right knee. Basic laboratory including complete blood count and basic metabolic panel were unremarkable at the time.

He was referred to surgery and underwent excision of the mass on December 18, 2024 (day 0), which was described as a 3 × 3 cm calcium-containing soft tissue mass superficial to the tibial periosteum. Pathology reported granulomatous inflammation with necrosis and associated filamentous fungal elements (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The degenerative changes and fragmentation of the fungus on both H&E and GMS stains, showing nondescript fungal elements (Fig. 1, Fig. 2), prohibited further classification of the species. The patient was referred to infectious diseases for further evaluation and, upon questioning, he reported that he used to work in construction and in placing floor tiles, for which he often needed to be on his knees. He was empirically started on posaconazole on January 7, 2025 (day 20). A noninvasive fungal workup, consisting of serum galactomannan, serum β-D-glucan, urine Histoplasma antigen, and serum cryptococcal antigen tests, was nondiagnostic.

A sample from the formalin-fixed parafilm embedded (FFPE) tissue was sent to the University of Washington for fungal PCR testing. Fungal PCR was performed with both 28S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) primers. The organism was identified as Fomitiporella micropora.

On February 16, 2025 (day 60), the patient underwent a right lower extremity MRI which showed no concern for invasive infection or osteomyelitis. On March 17, 2025 (day 89), posaconazole was stopped. The lesion has since completely healed.

Discussion

Solid organ transplant (SOT) recipients are at an increased risk for cutaneous fungal infections due to their immunocompromised state, compounded by the use of immunosuppressive therapies [1]. Fungal pathogens like Candida spp., Cryptococcus neoformans, Malassezia spp., and Aspergillus spp. can cause a range of cutaneous lesions, which may progress to systemic infections if not detected early [2,3]. Similarly, oncology patients, especially those with hematologic malignancies undergoing high-dose chemotherapy or prolonged neutropenia, are also vulnerable to opportunistic fungi such as Aspergillus, Mucor, and Fusarium, which can lead to severe cutaneous and systemic infections [4]. These infections are often treated with a combination of antifungal therapy and surgical resection.

Basidiomycetes include basidiomycete yeast, such as Cryptococcus neoformans, and filamentous fungi – all under the phylum basidiomycota. Basidiomycota encompasses diverse species, including mushrooms and smuts, which are gaining recognition as agents of human infection [5].

Filamentous basidiomycetes like Schizophyllum commune have been documented in human infections, including cases of onychomycosis and chronic lung disease [6]. S. commune with four other filamentous basidiomycetes, Coprinopsis cinerea, Emmia lacerata, Irpex lacteus, and Porostereum spadiceum, were identified as causative agents of respiratory tract infections in five Korean patients [7].

Basidiomycete infections in humans, though rare, are emerging as significant pathogens among immunocompromised patients. Infections caused by filamentous basidiomycetes, such as Phellinus undulatus, Megasporoporia setulosa, and Rigidoporus corticola, have been documented. P. undulatus was reported as the cause of soft tissue infection, identified through molecular analysis after surgical excision, with the patient recovering without antifungal therapy [8]. Similarly, M. setulosa caused subcutaneous mycosis in a farmer, marking the first human infection by this species, which was successfully treated with surgery and antifungal therapy with amphotericin B and oral itraconazole [9]. R. corticola has been associated with both sinusitis and pulmonary infections in immunocompromised patients, with diagnosis confirmed through histopathology and DNA sequencing [10]. Antifungal therapy remains challenging due to the fungus's lack of sporulation, no known standardized breakpoints, and high MICs for certain antifungals.

The typical diagnostic challenge of these filamentous basidiomycetes is the lack of sporulation for microscopic identification. The fungal elements could appear as hyaline hyphal elements, that could be mistaken for other species of hyaline molds, as such with our patient's histopathology findings. More importantly, because of a wide range of temperature that these fungi can grow and their resemblance of colonial morphologies to dimorphic fungi, care must be taken in to distinguish filamentous basidiomycetes from highly pathogenic dimorphic fungi [6]. Accurate diagnosis of these fungi is reportedly achieved through molecular-based methods, such as Sanger sequencing [11]. These cases highlight the importance of molecular identification for diagnosis.

Fomitiporella micropora is a recently described species within the genus Fomitiporella, characterized by its perennial basidiocarps and small pores [12,13]. While F. micropora has been identified and documented in molecular studies, including ITS and 28S rDNA phylogenetic analyses [13], it has not been previously reported as a human pathogen. The assay used in this case interrogates ITS1, ITS2, and 28S eukaryotic ribosomal subunits. ITS1 provided a 329 bp sequence that had 100 % identity to that reported in GenBank: KX181296.1, which established the diagnosis. Supportive sequences were obtained from 28S (583 bp, 99.48 % match to GenBank: KX181331.1) and ITS2 (391 bp, 99.17 % match to GenBank: KX181296.1).

The patient was treated with surgical resection and posaconazole. There are currently no in vitro susceptibility studies for F. micropora. One study evaluated the in vitro antifungal susceptibility of 44 clinical isolates of basidiomycetous fungi, including Schizophyllum commune, Coprinus species, Bjerkandera adusta, and other uncharacterized basidiomycetes. The antifungal agents tested included amphotericin B, fluconazole, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and flucytosine. The results showed low minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for most of the tested fungi, indicating that these agents may be effective for treating infections caused by these basidiomycetes [14]. However, the surgical resection was likely the curative intervention.

PCR and next-generation sequencing (NGS) have significantly advanced the diagnosis of fungal infections, providing more accurate results than traditional methods. It has led to the detection of difficult to grow and rare fungi, which is particularly beneficial for immunocompromised patients at higher risk for invasive fungal infections [12]. Targeted sequencing of conserved areas of the fungal genome, such as 18s rRNA, D1, D2, and internal transcribed spacers 1 and 2 (ITS1 and 2) is highly sensitive and specific in identification of invasive fungal pathogens. While targeted NGS (tNGS) has been widely performed on fresh or frozen specimens for pathogen identification, tNGS of FFPE tissue has only recently been demonstrated for improved clinical utility in cases where there is insufficient tissue/specimen or the original specimens are unavailable. The sensitivity of tNGS in FFPE is typically high when fungal elements or invading pathogens are visible on histopathology staining [15,16].

These new testing methods offer a promising approach to fungal diagnostics, especially for difficult to grow or rare pathogens.

The increasing recognition of these basidiomycetes as human pathogens highlights the need for greater awareness and improved diagnostic and treatment strategies for infections caused by these fungi.
