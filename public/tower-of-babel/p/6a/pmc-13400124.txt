Infective Endocarditis of Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement and Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair Devices
Authors: Joe Aoun, Chloe Kharsa, Parth Desai, Karl Abou Zeid, Sachin S. Goel, Neal S. Kleiman, Stephen H. Little, Nadeen N. Faza, Paul Sorajja, Gilbert HL. Tang, Larry M. Baddour, Anthony A. Bavry, Katherine Harrington, Vinay Badhwar, Vance G. Fowler, Andrea Giulio Quarti, Michael J. Reardon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infective endocarditis following transcatheter aortic valve replacement and mitral valve transcatheter edge-to-edge repair poses a significant clinical challenge, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach for timely diagnosis and appropriate management that may include surgical intervention for patients of less than prohibitive risk. Although advances in imaging modalities, including 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography, show promise in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, standardized guidelines for management are lacking, especially regarding the appropriateness of antimicrobial suppression therapy. Future research should focus on refining diagnostic criteria, optimizing therapeutic strategies, and improving patient outcomes in these populations.

Transcatheter interventions for valve repair and replacement have expanded globally and offer novel treatment options in patients who are at high risk for surgery. Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) is now accepted treatment for all surgical risk patients in the United States and in Europe, with approximately 50% of patients in the United States aged <65 years, now undergoing TAVR in certain states.1,2 In addition, the number of failed transcatheter aortic valves needing reintervention is projected to increase over the next decade.3,4

Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) of the mitral valve has expanded as a therapeutic option for patients with ≥ moderate-severe primary mitral regurgitation (MR) who are at high or prohibitive surgical risk, as well as for patients with symptomatic secondary MR despite optimal guideline-directed medical therapy for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.5 The volume of mitral TEER has now surpassed both isolated surgical mitral valve repair and replacement in the United States.6 Other transcatheter mitral and tricuspid therapies, including the Tendyne valve system (Abbott), Edwards SAPIEN 3 transcatheter heart valve (Edwards Lifesciences), Pascal (Edwards Lifesciences Inc), TriClip (Abbott Cardiovascular), and EVOQUE (Edwards Lifesciences), have also emerged as commercially available in select patients.7 Accordingly, transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement falls outside the scope of this review.

Infection of these transcatheter devices has been reported and has been characterized by unique epidemiologic, clinical, microbiologic, diagnostic, management, and preventive features.8 Given the association between device-related infections and poor clinical outcomes, prompt diagnosis and early initiation of appropriate therapy are essential to improve prognosis. Moreover, preventive measures both at the time of device placement and following the procedure are critical. Therefore, this state-of-the-art review was drafted to address these issues.

Infective endocarditis following transcatheter aortic valve replacement

Infective endocarditis (IE) following TAVR (TAVR-IE) has been classified based on the timing of infection relative to the date of valve implantation. Early infection is defined as occurring within the first year, when it is often linked to nosocomial or health care–associated sources whereas late infection occurs beyond 1-year postimplantation.9,10 Most TAVR-IE cases occur within the first year following implantation. Early TAVR-IE is more frequently associated with health care-related pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and Enterococcus spp., likely reflecting periprocedural contamination, vascular access, and health care exposure. In contrast, later presentations may more closely resemble native or surgical prosthetic valve endocarditis, with a relatively greater contribution of community-acquired organisms such as streptococcal species.11,12 Recognizing these temporal and microbiological differences may help guide clinical suspicion, diagnostic evaluation, and empiric antimicrobial therapy.

Epidemiology

The reported incidence of TAVR-IE is 0.3% to 3%.13,14 TAVR-IE has been associated with substantial early mortality, with reported 30-day mortality rates of up to 36%.9,11

Several risk factors are associated with TAVR-IE, which can be patient-specific or procedure-related. A multivariable analysis by Cahill et al reported that male sex (HR: 1.30; 95% CI: 1.17-1.44; P < 0.001), atrial fibrillation (HR: 1.39; 95% CI: 1.23-1.58; P < 0.001), and a high logistic EuroSCORE (HR: 1.004/EuroSCORE point; 95% CI: 1.000-1.001; P = 0.01) are strongly associated with IE occurrence.15 The study by Mentias et al17 demonstrated that key predictors of endocarditis following TAVR included younger age at the time of the procedure, male sex, prior history of endocarditis, end-stage renal disease, redo-TAVR, underlying liver or lung disease, and post-TAVR acute kidney injury.

Procedure-related risk factors include residual moderate or severe aortic regurgitation, valve embolization, bleeding complications, lack of balloon aortic valvuloplasty before TAVR, valve-in-valve TAVR, and elevated residual transvalvular gradients post-TAVR.13 These factors may serve as surrogates for procedural complexity and patient instability, in which prolonged intervention time, hemodynamic compromise, suboptimal device positioning, and subsequent instrumentation, increase susceptibility to postprocedural IE. Many factors related to patients and procedures are nonmodifiable, thus complicating the prevention of TAVR-IE.

Microbiology

The microbiologic profile of prosthetic valve endocarditis (PVE) following surgical AVR have evolved over time, with early infections (<2 months) typically caused by nosocomial pathogens like S aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci, intermediate infections (2-12 months) reflecting a mix of nosocomial and community sources, and late infections (>12 months) resembling native valve endocarditis (NVE), predominantly involving staphylococci and S aureus.18

In contrast, TAVR-IE has a different bacterial profile. The predominant microorganisms are Enterococcus species followed by S aureus and coagulase-negative staphylococci.16,19 This difference may be attributed to transfemoral access and the increased prevalence of genitourinary sources of infection in a population that until recently has consisted of elderly or infirm patients. In addition, a significant proportion of TAVR-IE cases is iatrogenic and health care–related and can be associated with multiresistant organisms, thus complicating the management of this syndrome.20

Presentation

Fever is the most common presentation of TAVR-IE with a prevalence of 15% to 80%, which is notably lower compared to NVE, where fever is present in 90% to 100% of cases.16,21 This phenomenon may be more characteristic of the TAVR population, which often consists of older patients with a substantial burden of comorbidities. In addition, new onset acute heart failure and systemic embolic events can occur in 40% and 13% of patients, respectively.16 However, it is noteworthy that atypical presentation and nonspecific symptoms are more frequent in patients with TAVR-IE, leading to delayed diagnosis and management.

Nearly half of the patients in the TAVR-IE registry presented with isolated involvement of the TAVR prosthesis. A third of the patients also had at least 2 other cardiac structures involved, with the mitral valve being the most frequently affected structure.22 The prosthesis design of TAVR valves may also influence the location and development of vegetations. Vegetations located on the valve leaflets are the most common types of vegetations seen in both self-expanding valves and balloon expandable valves. Self-expanding valves can also present more frequently than balloon expandable valves with vegetations attached to the stent frame (34% vs 19%), likely due to longer frames.23

Given the frequently nonspecific presentation of TAVR- and TEER-associated IE, both patient and clinician education play an important role in facilitating early recognition and timely diagnosis. Maintaining a high index of suspicion in patients presenting with unexplained fever, heart failure symptoms, embolic events, or bacteremia following transcatheter valve interventions is essential.24 In addition, patient counseling regarding warning symptoms and the importance of prompt medical evaluation may help reduce delays in diagnosis and treatment.

Diagnosis of TAVR-IE

Although TAVR-IE exhibits several unique characteristics, there are currently no specific diagnostic criteria tailored to this population. As with PVE, current clinical guidelines recommend applying the modified Duke criteria to suspected IE cases in TAVR patients.21,25,26 However, these criteria demonstrate reduced diagnostic accuracy for TAVR-IE compared to native valve IE, primarily due to a higher incidence of negative blood cultures and lower echocardiographic sensitivity.16,26 Rarely, culture-negative TAVR-IE may be associated with atypical pathogens such as mycobacteria, fungi, Coxiella spp., Bartonella spp. and others.

According to del Val et al13, the combined sensitivity of transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) for detecting TAVR-IE was only 67.8%, significantly lower than that observed in surgical PVE (73% and 89.9%, respectively).16,26 This is likely attributable to acoustic shadowing from the valve frame. Moreover, echocardiography is further limited by the presence of nonspecific findings such as leaflet thickening and elevated transvalvular gradients, which occur in 70% and 80% of TAVR-IE cases, respectively.27

These limitations underscore the need for a multimodality imaging approach to improve sensitivity in detecting endocardial involvement and extracardiac complications.27 Cardiac computed tomography (CT), cardiac magnetic resonance, and metabolic imaging have become essential tools in diagnosing workup (Figures 1 and 2, Video 1, Video 2.1, Video 2.2, Video 3, Video 4). In a multicenter study by Wahadat et al28 the addition of 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/ CT (18F-FDG-PET/CT) reclassified 33% of patients initially categorized using Duke criteria, mainly by increasing the number of definite cases.27 del Val et al13 proposed a diagnostic algorithm incorporating multimodality imaging for suspected TAVR-IE. The recently published 2023 European Society of Cardiology guidelines and 2023 Duke-International Society for Cardiovascular Infectious Diseases criteria endorse the use of 18F-FDG-PET/CT and cardiac CT as integral components of the diagnostic algorithm for both PVE and TAVR-IE.20,31 However, 18F-FDG-PET/CT remains predominantly available at tertiary referral centers and requires dedicated expertise in acquisition and interpretation by experienced nuclear cardiology and imaging specialists. Furthermore, nonspecific FDG uptake may occur following recent cardiac interventions or prosthetic implantation, potentially confounding interpretation, and reducing diagnostic specificity in the early postprocedural period.32 Adequate dietary preparation before 18F-FDG-PET/CT is critical to suppress physiological myocardial glucose uptake and improve the detection of prosthetic valve infection and inflammatory activity.33, 34, 35 Intracardiac echocardiography may also play a role in detecting vegetations in patients with suspected TAVR-IE and inconclusive TEE findings.36

TEE remains a cornerstone imaging modality in patients with suspected TAVR-IE, particularly in cases with persistent bacteremia, new conduction abnormalities, embolic phenomena, prosthetic valve dysfunction, or inconclusive transthoracic echocardiography findings. Expected findings include vegetations, leaflet thickening, prosthetic regurgitation, abscess formation, fistulas, and periannular complications.1 However, interpretation may be challenging due to acoustic shadowing from the transcatheter valve frame and the presence of nonspecific prosthetic-related abnormalities. In patients with high clinical suspicion and nondiagnostic initial imaging, repeat or serial TEE examinations may improve diagnostic sensitivity, and facilitate detection of evolving complications.

CT provides an important complementary value beyond echocardiography, particularly for identifying perivalvular extension, abscesses, pseudoaneurysms, leaflet thickening, and extracardiac complications. Dedicated ECG-gated acquisition protocols and interpretation by experienced structural and cardiac imaging specialists are essential to optimize diagnostic accuracy and reduce imaging artifacts related to prosthetic material. In equivocal cases, CT may provide incremental diagnostic information when echocardiographic findings remain inconclusive.

Importantly, delayed diagnosis remains a common and clinically significant challenge in patients with TAVR-IE, largely driven by nonspecific presentations, imaging limitations related to prosthetic material, and reduced sensitivity of traditional diagnostic criteria. Diagnostic uncertainty may delay initiation of definitive therapy, including surgical intervention when indicated, and can contribute to worsening infection, embolic complications, progressive valvular or perivalvular destruction, heart failure, and increased mortality.37, 38, 39 These challenges further complicate standardization of care pathways across institutions and underscore the importance of early recognition and multimodality evaluation.

Management

Endocarditis team approach

Effectively managing TAVR-IE poses significant challenges that demand a multidisciplinary approach. Implementing the “endocarditis team” has led to a notable decrease in both in-hospital and long-term mortality among patients who exhibit symptoms indicative of IE.40,41 As a result, a cooperative strategy involving cardiologists, cardiac surgeons, infectious disease specialists, microbiologists, neurologists, neurosurgeons, and geriatric specialists is indispensable for the decision-making processes in patients with TAVR-IE, on a case-by-case basis13 To enhance efficiency and communication, integrating the endocarditis team within the broader valve team may offer a more cohesive framework for decision-making and patient management. Medical management

No randomized trials have compared antibiotic regimens for TAVR-IE, and specific recommendations are lacking. Therefore, antibiotic therapy is advised based on existing guidelines for PVE.5,25 The administration of intravenous antibiotics in treating TAVR-IE should be based on microbial profile and antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Due to ongoing fluctuations in antimicrobial sensitivity and variations in susceptibility profiles across regions and specific sites, it is crucial to engage in concurrent management with the guidance of a consultant well-versed in these evolving patterns.5 Patients with TAVR-IE, as a subset of PVE, typically require a more extended antibiotic regimen lasting at least 6 weeks compared to those with native IE. It is crucial to note that PVE studies often lack representation of TAVR patients, making it challenging to universally apply their findings to TAVR-IE cases. Surgical management

Surgery is generally considered the standard treatment option for NVE and PVE when indicated.5,25 However, uncertainties persist regarding the optimal timing and indications for surgical intervention in the transcatheter population, particularly as these procedures commonly are performed in patients who are at high risk or ineligible for cardiac surgery. TAVR-IE is the etiology of 36% of surgical TAVR explant and SAVR operations in the United States,3 based on early experience data before the widespread recognition of bioprosthetic valve degeneration. To date, only three studies, with relatively small sample sizes, have directly compared surgery with exclusive medical therapy in unselected, high surgical risk TAVR-IE cohorts. Notably, cardiac surgery was not associated with significant improvements in in-hospital mortality, 30-day readmissions, or 1-year all-cause mortality when compared to medical therapy alone.22,44,45 However, these findings should be interpreted with caution, as no randomized trials exist, and substantial selection bias may influence outcomes, with surgically treated patients often presenting with more severe pathology such as large vegetations, abscess formation, or hemodynamic instability. Consequently, no specific recommendations have been established for surgery in TAVR-IE, and management decisions remain individualized based on patient characteristics, local experience, extent of infection, and surgical risk. In cases of local infection extension, surgery may still be considered when the operative risk is not prohibitive.

Importantly, early diagnosis and aggressive treatment of IE are crucial, as untreated or uncontrolled infections may ultimately necessitate valve explantation. Data from the multicenter EXPLANT-TAVR registry identify IE as a major indication for transcatheter valve explantation.39 These procedures are associated with substantial mortality and mortality, particularly in low-volume centers, highlighting both the severity of this complication and the importance of surgical experience. For cases with healed IE and valve prosthesis dysfunction, repeat transcatheter therapy—such as valve-in-valve implantation—may be considered in select high-risk patients.49 Although current guidelines still endorse surgical intervention as the treatment of choice for patients with prosthetic valve endocarditis and surgical indications.42 Transcatheter reinterventions may be a viable alternative in patients who are deemed inoperable, particularly if they have favorable anatomy and no signs of ongoing infection. These procedures are typically delayed by at least 1 to 3 months following completion of antibiotic therapy and confirmation of infection resolution, usually verified through a follow-up transesophageal echocardiogram.25

However, caution is warranted. In a recent multicenter registry by Mangner et al, most of TAVR-IE patients were treated medically, and surgical intervention did not significantly reduce in-hospital or 1-year mortality. Importantly, outcomes were primarily influenced by baseline patient risk profiles, infecting organism, and presence of IE-related complications. These findings underscore that transcatheter reinterventions should be reserved for patients who are truly inoperable or considered for palliation, often in conjunction with lifelong suppressive antimicrobial therapy.22

Compared with native valve IE, PVE following surgical or transcatheter valve interventions is associated with higher morbidity and mortality.50 TAVR-IE carries a particularly poor prognosis, with in-hospital mortality rates approaching 20% to 40% and 1-year mortality frequently exceeding 40%.17 Although TEER-associated IE (TEER-IE) remains less well characterized, available data similarly suggest high morbidity and mortality once infection occurs.51 Across prosthetic valve infections, delayed diagnosis, imaging limitations, and restricted surgical candidacy remain major contributors to adverse outcomes.

Infective endocarditis following transcatheter edge-to-edge repair

TEER is now indicated for patients with primary MR with high or prohibitive surgical risk, and for patients with secondary MR with left ventricular dysfunction.52, 53, 54 Currently, the MitraClip system (Abbott Vascular) and the Pascal (Edwards Lifesciences) are the only devices approved in the United States and Europe; for the MitraClip system, over 60,000 cases were identified in the United States as of June 2022.55 As with other device-related IE, combined criteria are used to define cases of IE.51,56

Epidemiology

During the 12-month follow-up of the EVEREST II trial, the incidence of TEER-IE following mitral TEER implantation was approximately 1.1% (2 of 184 patients).53 Others have reported a rate of 0% to 1.3% at 1-year, confirming its low incidence.

TEER-IE is characterized by a nearly 20-fold increase in-hospital mortality compared to patients without IE (OR: 19.52; 95% CI: 9.04-42.14; P < 0.001), resulting in a mortality up to 50%.57 This observation is consistent with the clinical profile of TEER-IE, which tends to occur in patients with high baseline surgical risk, as evidenced by EuroSCORE and EuroSCORE II (values of 41.3% and 44.6%, respectively).51 In 2023, Bertolino et al conducted a meta-analysis of reported cases of MitraClip-related IE within 1 year of device implantation. The affected cohort had a high burden of comorbidities, including ischemic heart disease (42%), chronic kidney disease (31%), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (23%), and diabetes mellitus (15%). Among the reported cases, 13 patients underwent open-heart mitral valve replacement, whereas 12 were managed conservatively with systematic antimicrobial therapy. Despite these interventions, in-hospital mortality remained high—ranging from 20% to 40%—reflecting the advanced age and complex medical profiles of this population.58

Microbiology

S aureus is the most common pathogen identified in TEER-IE.58 Other pathogens include Enterococcus faecalis (11.5%), coagulase-negative staphylococci (11.5%), Streptococcus spp. (11.5%), Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Corynebacterium spp., and Bartonella henselae, among others.58,59

Presentation

The prevailing symptom among patients is fever (65.8%), markedly less frequent than in cases of NVE, followed by heart failure symptoms which are encountered in 42.3%. Systemic embolization occurs in 15.3% of cases. Three-quarters of cases are diagnosed within 12 months following implantation.58

Management

The management of TEER-IE requires a collaborative approach by the endocarditis team. The diagnosis of TEER-IE necessitates a comprehensive evaluation including patient history and physical examination, followed by echocardiographic assessment revealing indications of IE involving the mitral valve leaflets and/or clip. In approximately 62% of cases, a vegetation is observed on the clip, whereas the remainder is located solely on the leaflets without involvement of the clip itself.58 TEE is presumed to exhibit higher sensitivity in comparison to TTE. However, the precise sensitivity rates remain indeterminate due to the limited sample size available for the investigation of these modalities. Furthermore, 18F-FDG-PET/CT has been used for diagnostic purposes in specific clinical scenarios, although current evidence remains insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about its utility.60

Currently, there are no standardized guidelines for the management of TEER-IE, and treatment strategies have been tailored for individual patient care. Consequently, patients are presently managed similarly to those with PVE. Surgical intervention is pursued for eligible candidates, involving mitral valve replacement, whereas those deemed to have high to prohibitive surgical risk are managed conservatively with medical therapy alone, consisting of an extended course of antibiotics. TEER-IE carries a poor prognosis, characterized by 30-day and in-hospital mortality rates approximately reaching 50%.58,61

Antibiotic prophylaxis and infection control strategies in TAVR and TEER

Both the 2023 European Society of Cardiology and the 2021 American Heart Association guidelines classify antibiotic prophylaxis as a class I recommendation for high-risk patients undergoing invasive dental procedures. This category includes patients with prosthetic heart valves, including TAVR and TEER. The American Heart Association statement no longer recommends clindamycin due to safety concerns (Clostridium difficile infection) and favors alternatives such as cephalexin, azithromycin, clarithromycin, or doxycycline in penicillin-allergic individuals, and cefazolin or ceftriaxone when oral administration is not feasible.50, 60, 51

Infection control measures for invasive structural interventions (e.g., TAVR and TEER) should follow WHO-recommended standards for operating rooms, strict procedural sterility, minimal room traffic, and reduced device exposure time.62 These strategies aim to mitigate risk, particularly as endocarditis remains a serious but preventable complication that may ultimately require high-risk interventions such as valve explantation.52,54

Conclusions

Both TAVR-IE and TEER-IE present complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Although the incidence of TAVR-IE and TEER-IE remains relatively low, its impact on patient outcomes is substantial. Diagnosis relies heavily on a multimodality imaging approach, including echocardiography, cardiac CT, and 18F-FDG-PET/CT, with no specific guidelines yet established for management. The mortality associated with TAVR/TEER-IE is high, underscoring the urgent need for further research and standardized protocols to improve patient care and outcomes in these high-risk populations.
