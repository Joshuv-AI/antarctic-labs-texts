From data chaos to physically interpretable deterministic mapping
Authors: Dongni Jia, Shuai Li, Xinyi Zuo, Haibo Shi, Junyi Wang, Xiaofeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Discovering governing equations directly from observational data remains a fundamental challenge in science and engineering, particularly when measurements are noisy, high-dimensional, or multi-scale. Existing approaches often cast equation discovery as a regression problem that selects candidate terms to fit observed trajectories, which can limit structural stability and identifiability under realistic data conditions. We propose a structured operator-learning framework that reformulates equation discovery as a constrained dynamical inference problem integrating spectral decomposition, physics-guided sparse projection, and cross-view consistency regularization within a unified architecture. By decomposing dynamics into scale-resolved components and enforcing invariance across perturbed observations, the framework promotes stable and interpretable equation recovery. Here, we show that the method consistently identifies compact governing equations while maintaining strong long-horizon predictive accuracy across canonical nonlinear systems and representative industrial processes, even under noisy and distribution-shifted data.

Introduction

Identifying governing equations from observational data is a fundamental challenge across science and engineering. Traditional first-principles methods, though guided by physical intuition, often require extensive assumptions and large datasets1–3. Modern systems, however, are typically high-dimensional, nonlinear, and strongly coupled, as in atmospheric dynamics influenced by temperature, humidity, and topography4–6. In the presence of noise and uncertainty, conventional modeling strategies frequently struggle to achieve robust generalization across varying operating conditions.

Recent advances in big data analytics and machine learning provide new opportunities for equation discovery7,8. Early efforts, such as Schmidt et al. combining genetic algorithms with symbolic regression9,10, were pioneering but computationally costly and prone to overfitting11,12. A major breakthrough was SINDy13,14, which employs sparsity-promoting regression to identify minimal governing terms15–17. Its balance of simplicity and accuracy has been validated across diverse systems18–21 and extended to many nonlinear dynamics18,19,22–24.

Beyond sparse regression, hybrid physics-machine learning paradigms have emerged. Gaussian processes were used to infer ODEs from sparse data25, while non-Gaussian stochastic dynamics were addressed in ref. 26. PINNs incorporate governing laws directly into neural network training27–33. Related approaches include the Symbolic Physics Learner34, moving-horizon optimization35, DSINDy36, physics-informed criteria37, and Bayesian corrections38. These methods reflect a broader trend toward embedding physical structure into data-driven discovery. Applications now span fluid dynamics, climate, materials science, and environmental systems39,40.

Despite these advances, robust identification under noisy, scarce, or distribution-shifted data remains challenging. Neural networks often interpolate well yet lack structural identifiability, motivating renewed interest in Koopman operator theory41,42. Koopman theory represents nonlinear dynamics through linear evolution in lifted observable spaces, providing a principled operator-theoretic framework. Two major directions have developed within Koopman learning. First, neural Koopman approaches construct latent embeddings in which nonlinear dynamics evolve approximately linearly. Autoencoder-based Koopman models, linearly recurrent autoencoder networks, and deep-DMD formulations emphasize predictive stability and long-horizon forecasting in latent space43–45. These methods improve representation efficiency and robustness, yet typically focus on operator approximation and prediction accuracy rather than explicit recovery of uniquely identifiable governing equations. Second, kernel-based Koopman and operator-theoretic frameworks approximate generators and eigenfunctions in reproducing kernel Hilbert spaces (RKHS) with well-established stability and spectral convergence properties46–49. Kernel-based generator approximation and eigenfunction learning provide principled spectral reconstruction and vector-field emulation, while kernel flows enable data-adaptive, stable kernel selection for dynamical systemsc50–53. These approaches prioritize high-fidelity operator approximation and spectral characterization within fixed or adaptive kernel spaces54–56. While both neural and kernel Koopman frameworks provide powerful operator-learning tools, they generally emphasize operator approximation or prediction accuracy. In contrast, equation discovery requires additional structural constraints to ensure identifiability of a unique governing law, particularly in multi-scale and noisy environments where multiple latent representations may yield similar predictive errors.

In parallel, self-supervised paradigms such as BYOL (Bootstrap Your Own Latent) learn invariant representations via multi-view consistency57,58. Recent studies have begun exploring consistency-based learning for dynamical modeling to enhance representation robustness59. However, consistency has primarily been employed as a representation-level regularizer rather than as a structural mechanism for operator identifiability.

In this work, we introduce PK-MCL (Physics-guided Koopman framework with Multi-scale Consistency Learning), extending neural Koopman modeling toward structurally identifiable operator discovery under multi-scale and perturbed observations. Building on recent advances in neural latent linearization and kernel-based operator approximation, PK-MCL incorporates additional structural principles that directly target governing-law recovery rather than prediction alone. Specifically, the framework integrates: (i) a scale-aware Koopman decomposition that separates spectral components and stabilizes cross-scale evolution; (ii) an embedded sparse physical projection that enforces explicit equation realization during training instead of post hoc regression; and (iii) a multi-view consistency mechanism that promotes invariance of latent dynamics across perturbed observations, thereby reducing ambiguity in the admissible operator class. By coupling multi-scale structure, sparsity-driven equation realization, and invariance-based regularization within a unified optimization framework, PK-MCL transforms operator learning from unconstrained latent linear embedding into a structured discovery process, yielding governing operators that are physically interpretable and stable under realistic perturbations.

In our experiments, we systematically compare several representative paradigms for dynamical modeling, including methods based on explicit mechanistic assumptions with sparse regression14, neural network models incorporating physical constraints and sparse structures27,60,61, and operator-learning approaches that achieve state prediction and implicit mechanism modeling through linearization in a lifted space44,45,62,63. In addition, kernel-based approximations of the dynamical generator and eigenfunctions46,47 are included as complementary baselines on selected representative systems. This paradigm-oriented organization enables a unified interpretation of the experimental results from complementary perspectives, including prediction accuracy, interpretability of the identified dynamics, robustness to perturbations, and scalability to high-dimensional and multiscale systems.

The proposed framework is evaluated across canonical nonlinear dynamical systems, simulated industrial reaction processes, and a real industrial grinding-classification circuit.

We first consider representative partial differential equation systems, including the Burgers’ equation, the FitzHugh-Nagumo reaction-diffusion system, and the two-dimensional Navier-Stokes equations. These benchmarks span shock-dominated convection-diffusion, excitable pattern formation, and high-dimensional multi-scale turbulence, providing a controlled setting to assess predictive accuracy and operator-learning stability under diverse nonlinear regimes.

To examine industrial relevance, we further evaluate two reaction-based process models: an isothermal batch reactor with multiple reactions and a non-isothermal continuous-stirred tank reactor with strong thermo-kinetic coupling. These systems introduce stiffness, parameter coupling, and operating-condition variability that challenge structural identification. Finally, we test the framework on a real grinding-classification system from mineral processing, where incomplete measurements and distribution shifts are intrinsic to plant operation.

Detailed descriptions of data generation procedures, parameter configurations, and experimental analyses for the benchmark systems are provided in Supplementary Results A. Burgers’ equation with shock behavior

We first consider the one-dimensional viscous Burgers’ equation, a canonical model for nonlinear advection-diffusion dynamics64, 2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial }_{t}u+u{\partial }_{x}u=\nu {\partial }_{xx}u,$$\end{document}∂tu+u∂xu=ν∂xxu,with viscosity ν = 0.01 under periodic boundary conditions. Despite its simple analytical form, the Burgers equation exhibits strong nonlinearity and sharp gradient structures, making it a suitable benchmark for examining whether accurate prediction can be achieved without sacrificing interpretability in equation discovery.

On this low-dimensional system, we compare PK-MCL with representative methods spanning sparse regression-based discovery, physics-informed neural equation learning, operator-learning approaches based on Koopman linearization, and kernel-based nonparametric modeling. While kernel-based generator and eigenfunction approaches provide competitive predictive performance on this benchmark, they tend to exhibit larger deviations in the recovered dynamical structure. In contrast, PK-MCL achieves accurate multi-step prediction while recovering a governing operator that closely matches the analytical Burgers equation, as illustrated in Fig. 2. These results indicate that, on a system where all major modeling paradigms are in principle applicable, PK-MCL is able to reconcile predictive fidelity with mechanistic interpretability. FitzHugh-Nagumo(FHN) reaction-diffusion system

As a second nonlinear benchmark, we consider the two-dimensional FitzHugh-Nagumo (FHN) reaction-diffusion system65, which describes excitable media through the interaction between fast activation and slow recovery dynamics, 3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\partial }_{t}v={D}_{v}{\nabla }^{2}v+f(v,w)+I,\qquad {\partial }_{t}w={D}_{w}{\nabla }^{2}w+\varepsilon g(v,w)$$\end{document}∂tv=Dv∇2v+f(v,w)+I,∂tw=Dw∇2w+εg(v,w)

Unlike the Burgers equation, the FHN system exhibits pronounced spatiotemporal coupling, traveling wave propagation, and sensitivity to localized perturbations, posing a qualitatively different challenge for predictive modeling. On this system, PK-MCL produces stable long-horizon predictions for both state variables and preserves the characteristic wave dynamics under perturbed initial conditions. In particular, the learned model maintains coherent wavefront propagation and recovery cycles over extended rollout horizons, whereas baseline methods tend to exhibit either accumulating phase errors or rapid degradation of spatiotemporal patterns. These observations indicate that PK-MCL effectively captures the coupled reaction-diffusion mechanisms governing excitable dynamics, beyond short-term reconstruction accuracy. Overall, the FHN benchmark highlights the robustness of PK-MCL in modeling nonlinear systems with strong spatiotemporal interactions, complementing the structural interpretability emphasized in the Burgers equation and the scalability considerations examined in the Navier-Stokes system.

Discovery of grinding and classification system

As a final case study, we apply the proposed framework to an industrial-scale grinding–classification circuit using real plant data collected over several weeks of continuous operation. The system consists of a ball mill, a hydrocyclone cluster for particle-size classification, a slurry sump, and the associated slurry–solids recirculation pipelines, forming a closed-loop process characterized by nonlinear feedback and delayed interactions. Key measured variables include the ore feed rate Ffeed(t), water addition rate Wadd(t), hydrocyclone feed pressure Pcyc(t), mill motor current Imill(t), slurry sump level Hsump(t), cyclone feed solids concentration Ccf(t), cyclone feed flow rate Qcf(t), and the overflow particle-size index P200(t).

On this industrial dataset, PK-MCL delivers stable reconstruction and forecasting performance across all major process variables. In particular, the model preserves coherent predictions of both fast transport- and load-induced fluctuations and slower recirculation-driven trends over extended horizons, despite sensor noise, missing samples, and operating condition changes. Baseline data-driven models exhibit markedly stronger error accumulation during forecasting, especially for the overflow particle-size index P200 and cyclone feed flow rate Qcf, highlighting the difficulty of capturing closed-loop delayed feedback through purely data-driven input–output mappings.

Beyond predictive performance, the identified process coefficients remain consistent with physically meaningful plant relationships, including the load-dependent coupling between throughput and slurry concentration and the balance between water addition and slurry transport in the sump–cyclone subsystem. These results indicate that PK-MCL can recover interpretable process relations while providing reliable state prediction directly from noisy and incomplete industrial measurements. Detailed descriptions of data preprocessing, model configuration, and training procedures are provided in Supplementary Results A.3.

Ablation studies

To clarify the respective roles of multi-scale spectral decomposition (MS), multi-view consistency learning (CONS), and the physics-guided loss weighted by α (PHY), we perform component-wise ablation by selectively removing each module from the full model (MS+CONS+PHY, α = α0). Specifically, we consider a single-scale variant (–MS), a variant without consistency regularization (–Cons), and a purely data-driven setting with α = 0. For the non-isothermal CSTR, we additionally conduct an explicit α-sweep to examine the trade-off induced by the physics-guided loss.

All ablation variants are trained under identical conditions, including data splits, network capacity, optimization budget, and stopping criteria. Hyperparameters unrelated to the removed component are kept fixed without re-tuning to avoid compensatory effects. Each experiment is repeated across multiple initial conditions and random seeds to assess robustness under stochastic perturbations.

Across all benchmark systems, consistent trends emerge. Removing multi-scale decomposition (–MS) primarily degrades long-horizon stability by impairing the modeling of multi-timescale interactions, with the effect most evident in systems exhibiting strong scale separation, such as Navier-Stokes (Fig. 9). In contrast, removing consistency regularization (–Cons) has limited impact on short-term accuracy but significantly increases sensitivity to noise, masking, and temporal dropout, leading to higher variance across runs and reduced robustness. Finally, we observe that jointly integrating sparse regression with representation learning contributes to smoother and more reliable convergence compared to a two-stage strategy in which equation discovery is performed after latent representations are learned, particularly in systems with rich nonlinear interactions. Discussion

Reliable equation discovery in complex dynamical systems requires more than expressive function approximation. In high-dimensional, multi-scale, and noise-contaminated regimes, the central difficulty lies in reconciling long-horizon predictive stability with explicit, physically meaningful structure. Approaches that excel at prediction often sacrifice interpretability, while sparse identification methods that recover symbolic forms frequently struggle with robustness and scalability. Bridging this divide is the primary challenge addressed in this work.

We introduced PK-MCL as a structured, end-to-end framework that unifies operator-based multi-scale modeling, physics-constrained sparsity, and invariance-driven stabilization within a single trainable architecture. Rather than treating prediction and mechanism identification as separate stages, the framework couples them during training: latent evolution is learned through a multi-scale Koopman representation capable of handling scale separation and high-dimensional dynamics, while the governing law is simultaneously constrained to admit a compact symbolic realization. Consistency across perturbed observations further stabilizes the learned dynamics, reducing sensitivity to noise and operating-condition shifts without compromising interpretability.

Across nonlinear PDE benchmarks, industrial reactor models, and real plant grinding-classification data, this unified structure consistently delivers stable long-horizon forecasts together with parsimonious and physically aligned governing equations. Importantly, the results indicate that modeling capacity, structural constraint, and robustness must be designed in concert: expanding representational power alone is insufficient, and sparsity alone does not guarantee stability. Their integration within a single differentiable objective proves essential for maintaining both predictive fidelity and mechanism-level clarity under realistic data conditions.

The present formulation nevertheless has limitations. The symbolic library defines the space of explicitly recoverable operators and may require domain-informed construction. Computational cost increases with multi-scale decomposition and long rollout horizons, particularly in very high-dimensional settings. Extending the framework to partially observed, strongly non-stationary, or fully three-dimensional multi-physics systems remains an open direction.

Future developments will explore adaptive and semi-symbolic hypothesis spaces, scalable architectures for large-scale industrial processes, and online extensions for streaming data environments. By coupling structured operator learning with explicit physical constraints and stability-promoting regularization, PK-MCL offers a practical pathway toward equation discovery methods that are simultaneously predictive, interpretable, and robust in complex scientific and industrial systems.

Methods

Overview of PK-MCL framework

We propose PK-MCL (Physics-guided Koopman framework with Multi-Scale Consistency Learning) to discover the governing equations of a complex dynamical system directly from raw observational data u[Ω] (the system state field on domain Ω). The key idea is to transform chaotic, noisy, high-dimensional measurements into deterministic and interpretable dynamics by integrating three complementary modules within a single end-to-end pipeline. First, a Multi-Scale Parallel Koopman Neural Operator (MS-KNO) decomposes the input state into multiple frequency bands and processes each band via an independent Fourier-Koopman pipeline. This captures both large-scale smooth structures and localized high-frequency details of the system, and the band-specific predictions are then fused to produce a full-spectrum spatiotemporal prediction. Second, a Physics-Guided Sparse Regression module imposes physical interpretability by constraining the predicted temporal derivatives to match a sparse combination of candidate library functions (including polynomials, trigonometric functions, and spatial derivatives of u). An ℓ1 regularization drives most coefficients to zero, yielding a parsimonious coefficient vector ξ whose few nonzero entries correspond to the active terms in the underlying governing equations. Third, a Multi-View Consistency Regularization module improves robustness and uniqueness of the learned dynamics. We generate multiple augmented versions of each trajectory (e.g. by randomly masking parts of the field or adding noise) and require the model’s latent representations of these different views to be consistent with each other. This self-supervised consistency constraint, inspired by BYOL, forces the encoder to focus on the true system dynamics instead of spurious noise or artifacts. All three components are optimized jointly via a unified training objective that balances prediction accuracy, physical law regularization, and cross-view consistency. In essence, PK-MCL is designed not merely as a latent linear predictor, but as an identifiable operator discovery framework. By jointly integrating multi-scale Koopman linearization, embedded sparse physical projection, and cross-view invariance constraints, the framework explicitly shapes the learned dynamics toward a uniquely recoverable and physically grounded governing operator. As a result, PK-MCL does not only provide accurate spatiotemporal predictions of u[Ω], but simultaneously uncovers a compact and interpretable dynamical law consistent across scales and data perturbations.

The detailed derivations and implementation specifics of each component of our method are provided in the corresponding subsections of Supplementary Method B. The proofs of differentiability and convergence are presented in Supplementary Discussion C.1.

Multi-scale parallel Koopman neural operator

To capture both global and local dependencies in spatiotemporal dynamics, we introduce a multi-scale parallel Koopman neural operator (MS-KNO). The input field is first encoded into a latent representation, which is decomposed into L frequency bands via truncated Fourier transforms with cutoffs ωℓ. Each band isolates dynamics at a specific scale. Within each band ℓ, the latent dynamics are linearized using a Koopman operator K(ℓ), learned via linear regression on time-shifted latent pairs (implemented through Hankel embedding), such that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${z}_{t+\varepsilon }^{(\ell )}\approx {K}^{(\ell )}{z}_{t}^{(\ell )}$$\end{document}zt+ε(ℓ)≈K(ℓ)zt(ℓ). Multi-step prediction is obtained via repeated application of K(ℓ). The predicted band-wise states are mapped back via inverse Fourier transform and fused into a unified latent representation. To compensate for high-frequency components lost due to spectral truncation, we introduce a lightweight correction network \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{{{\mathcal{C}}}}}}_{\psi }$$\end{document}Cψ, which predicts residual fine-scale features conditioned on the coarse prediction. The final latent state combines the fused Koopman prediction and the residual correction, and is decoded to produce the output field.

All operations in MS-KNO (Fourier transforms, Hankel embedding, linear evolution, neural network residuals, etc.) are differentiable, allowing the entire pipeline to be trained jointly with gradient-based optimization. This design extends classical neural Koopman formulations by introducing an explicit scale-aware operator decomposition. Instead of enforcing linear evolution in a single latent space, MS-KNO constrains distinct spectral components to evolve under separate Koopman operators, thereby preserving both global coherence and fine-scale dynamics. This structural decomposition provides a more controlled operator representation, which later facilitates sparse physical projection and stable equation identification.

While MS-KNO provides a structured latent linear evolution, the ultimate objective of PK-MCL is not limited to predictive accuracy but to the recovery of an explicit governing operator. To this end, we embed a physics-guided sparse regression module directly into training, rather than applying equation discovery post hoc.
