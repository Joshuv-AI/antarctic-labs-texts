Dual-function Gd–doped BiPO₄ rods enabling durable UV photocatalysis and antifungal activity
Authors: Sanjeev Kumar, Sandeep Kumar, Jyoti Gaur, Manoj A Vora, Vandana, Jasdeep Kaur, Kasim Sakran Abass, Mrinmoy Misra, Vinod Kumar, Avdhesh Kumar, Sucheta Prakash, Sandeep Kaushal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTextile effluents contain persistent dyes that resist conventional treatment, while opportunistic fungal pathogens such as Candida tropicalis are increasingly associated with difficult-to-treat infections. Here, monoclinic BiPO₄ and 10 mol% Gd–doped BiPO₄ were synthesized by chemical precipitation and linked to UV-driven methylene blue (MB) degradation and antifungal performance. XRD confirmed phase-pure monoclinic BiPO₄, with dopant-induced crystallite refinement (≈ 37 → 34.04 nm) and no detectable secondary phases. UV–Vis/Tauc’s analysis showed a red-shifted absorption edge and band-gap narrowing from 3.93 eV (BiPO₄) to 3.78 eV (Gd–doped BiPO₄). Under UV irradiation (MB 10 mg L⁻¹; catalyst 25 mg; 500 mL; 60 min dark equilibration), Gd–doped BiPO₄ accelerated MB depletion to Ct/C0 ≈ 0.06–0.07 after 70 min (≈ 93–94% removal), compared with Ct/C0 ≈ 0.22 for BiPO₄ (≈ 78% removal), following pseudo-first-order kinetics (k = 0.0381 vs. 0.0212 min⁻¹). The catalyst retained high activity over six cycles (95% → 88%) with unchanged XRD/FESEM features. In agar well diffusion, Gd–doped BiPO₄ exhibited dose-dependent antifungal inhibition against C. tropicalis with a maximum ZOI of ~ 13 mm at 200 µg well⁻¹. Introduction

Textile wet-processing generates large volumes of dye-laden wastewater: 30–150 L kg⁻¹ of cloth may be consumed, 15–50% of applied dyes can be lost due to incomplete fixation, and untreated or partially treated effluents can contain 10–250 mg L⁻¹ dyes1–5. This burden is amplified by the scale of production, as the global textile-dye industry uses an estimated 10,000–12,000 synthetic dyes and produces more than 7 × 105 tonnes of dyestuff annually6. The textile sector is widely reported to contribute approximately 17–20% of global industrial water pollution, with wet-processing steps dominating the pollution load in many fast-fashion production chains7–9. Among synthetic dyes, methylene blue (MB) is widely used as a representative cationic dye and has been reported in textile wastewater and environmental discharges, with concentrations spanning 10–200 mg L⁻¹ depending on dilution and treatment stage4,5. Residual dyes reduce light penetration and disrupt aquatic photosynthesis and oxygen balance, while their aromatic and heterocyclic structures can resist biodegradation and prolong environmental persistence in ecosystems10,11. Therefore, efficient remediation methods are needed that achieve rapid decolorization and oxidative degradation of stable dyes such as MB.

Conventional wastewater-treatment plants, typically based on primary clarification, biological activated sludge, and basic physicochemical polishing, are not engineered to reliably remove synthetic dyes from textile effluents, which combine high color, high salinity/solids, and high organic load. Reported raw textile wastewaters commonly exhibit pH 6.6–13.1, COD 635–4459 mg L⁻¹, TDS 6530–21,989 mg L⁻¹, and high suspended solids prior to treatment12,13. Even after treatment in operating plants, COD and solids can remain above regional limits, indicating that a substantial fraction of dyes and auxiliary chemicals (e.g., salts and surfactants) can pass through conventional trains and reach receiving waters14,15. Consistent with this, coagulation–flocculation and related physicochemical routes often deliver variable color removal, typically ~ 50–80% under practical conditions, with higher removal requiring tight pH control and elevated coagulant doses16–19. Biological systems can further show apparent decolorization dominated by adsorption onto biomass rather than complete molecular breakdown, shifting contaminants from water to sludge and creating secondary disposal burdens4,16,17. These limitations motivate oxidation-based technologies such as photocatalysis, which aim to cleave chromophores and drive progressive degradation rather than phase transfer.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), particularly heterogeneous photocatalysis, can outperform conventional treatment because they generate highly reactive oxidants (e.g., •OH and •O₂⁻) that attack dye chromophores and can reduce organic load. Quantitative studies on MB report rapid decolorization under controlled conditions; for example, a UV-driven composite photocatalyst achieved ~ 96% MB removal within 60 min at C₀ = 60 mg L⁻¹, while pure TiO₂ reached only ~ 66% under the same conditions, highlighting the kinetic advantage of engineered catalysts20. AOPs can also reduce bulk organics: in a UV/H₂O₂ system treating 50 mg L⁻¹ MB, removal reached ~ 86% after 120 min together with ~ 78% COD reduction21,22. However, photocatalytic performance remains strongly governed by band structure, charge-carrier recombination, and matrix effects (salts, dissolved organics, and radical scavengers), underscoring the need for robust catalysts with high activity and reusability in realistic conditions.

Among UV-responsive photocatalysts, BiPO₄ is attractive because it combines strong oxidizing capability, structural stability, and phase-dependent activity. BiPO₄ is an n-type bismuth phosphate with a band gap typically in the 3.8–4.2 eV range, which supports efficient UV absorption and good aqueous stability23,24. BiPO₄ exists in monoclinic and hexagonal polymorphs. Comparative studies commonly identify the low-temperature monoclinic phase (n-BiPO₄) as the most active. For example, n-BiPO₄ achieved 94.6% conversion and 76.8% mineralization of bisphenol A within 2 h, outperforming other phases under identical UV irradiation25. BiPO₄ has also been reported to show higher intrinsic photocatalytic activity than TiO₂ (P25) in benchmark degradation studies. Its performance can be further enhanced through size control and surface-defect engineering, which improve charge separation and interfacial reactivity. Although the wide band gap limits visible-light absorption in pristine BiPO₄, heterojunction construction and composite formation can extend the spectral response. This strategy enables high dye-degradation efficiencies under UV and, for composites, under visible irradiation26–28.

Rare-earth (Ln³⁺) doping offers a practical route to improve BiPO₄ by introducing defect/impurity levels and suppressing electron–hole recombination, often producing band-gap shifts of ~ 0.1–0.3 eV and measurable kinetic gains29,30. For BiPO₄, lanthanide substitution at the Bi site is feasible because of the same valence state and broadly comparable ionic size. Gd³⁺ is particularly suitable because incorporation can be achieved while retaining the monoclinic framework, and associated defect chemistry can promote electron trapping and improve charge separation. Prior studies on lanthanide-modified BiPO₄ report faster MB degradation than pristine BiPO₄ (e.g., 92% in 25 min for 1 mol% Eu/BiPO₄ and ~ 93% in 20 min for 5 mol% Gd/BiPO₄), supporting the potential of rare-earth incorporation to accelerate reaction kinetics31.

In addition to dye pollution, wet-processing and downstream water-handling infrastructure can support microbial colonization, where fungi are particularly problematic because they tolerate wide pH/salinity windows and persist as drug-tolerant biofilms on wet surfaces32–34. Clinically, the burden is substantial: updated global estimates indicate ~ 6.5 million invasive fungal infections annually with ~ 3.8 million associated deaths (≈ 2.5 million attributable), emphasizing the need for new antifungal platforms beyond the limited conventional drug classes35. The World Health Organization has also released a Fungal Priority Pathogens List, in which Candida tropicalis is categorized as a priority pathogen due to its clinical impact and resistance concerns. Importantly, C. tropicalis is strongly associated with invasive candidiasis and can exhibit high all-cause mortality (~ 55–60%) together with high triazole resistance (fluconazole/itraconazole/voriconazole/posaconazole reported up to ~ 40–80% in some settings), while biofilm growth further amplifies antifungal tolerance36,37. From a One-Health perspective, resistant C. tropicalis is also discussed as an emerging issue across humans, animals, and environmental reservoirs38. Consistent with this environmental dimension, Candida spp. have been detected in wastewater and shown to form biofilms and express virulence-associated traits, supporting the relevance of antifungal evaluation for materials intended for water-contact or remediation scenarios39.

Here, we provide an integrated structure–optics–kinetics–mechanism–stability evaluation of Gd–doped BiPO₄ for MB degradation under UV irradiation. Under fixed conditions (10 mg L⁻¹ MB, 25 mg catalyst, 500 mL, 60 min dark equilibration, 70 min UV), pristine BiPO₄ achieved ~ 78% degradation, whereas 10 mol% Gd–doped BiPO₄ delivered ~ 96% degradation, accompanied by a band-gap shift (3.93 → 3.78 eV) and an increase in the pseudo-first-order rate constant (0.0212 → 0.0381 min⁻¹). The operational window was quantified through pH-dependent tests, revealing an optimum at pH 7 (~ 96%) and reduced efficiencies under acidic (~ 45–59% at pH 3–4) and alkaline (~ 60% at pH 10) conditions. Scavenger experiments indicated dominant involvement of •O₂⁻ and •OH, and the catalyst retained high activity over six reuse cycles with only ~ 7% performance loss (~ 95% → ~88%). Besides photocatalysis, Gd–doped BiPO₄ displayed dose-dependent antifungal activity against Candida tropicalis, reaching a maximum zone of inhibition of ~ 13 mm at 200 µg well⁻¹ (agar well diffusion). Collectively, these results deliver a design-relevant framework linking dopant-driven changes in BiPO₄ to photocatalytic performance and durability in dye-contaminated water treatment.

Although lanthanide-doped BiPO₄ photocatalysts for dye degradation have been reported, many studies focus mainly on activity metrics without presenting a single, fully specified framework that connects structure and optical changes to kinetics, operating-window limits, mechanistic probes, and durability, and without linking photocatalysis to an additional practical bio-function. Here, we therefore provide an integrated structure–optics–kinetics–mechanism–stability evaluation of 10 mol% Gd–doped BiPO₄ for MB degradation under UV irradiation under fixed conditions (10 mg L⁻¹ MB, 25 mg catalyst, 500 mL, 60 min dark equilibration, 70 min UV). Relative to pristine BiPO₄ (~ 78% degradation), Gd–doped BiPO₄ achieves ~ 96% degradation, alongside a reduced band gap (from 3.93 to 3.78 eV) and a higher pseudo-first-order rate constant (from 0.0212 to 0.0381 min⁻¹). The operational window is quantified through pH-dependent tests, showing optimum performance at pH 7 (~ 96%) and lower efficiencies under acidic conditions (~ 45–59% at pH 3–4) and alkaline conditions (~ 60% at pH 10). Scavenger tests suggest that superoxide (•O₂⁻) and hydroxyl radicals (•OH) contribute strongly to the degradation pathway. Importantly, the catalyst retains high activity over six reuse cycles with only ~ 7% loss (~ 95% to ~ 88%), supported by post-cycle characterization. Beyond photocatalysis, Gd–doped BiPO₄ also shows dose-dependent antifungal activity against Candida tropicalis, reaching a zone of inhibition of ~ 13 mm at 200 µg well⁻¹ (agar well diffusion). Overall, this work positions Gd-doped BiPO₄ as a dual-function, durability-validated platform and clarifies how Gd incorporation modulates BiPO₄ properties to improve kinetics and robustness under defined operating conditions relevant to water-treatment contexts. The novelty of this work is the dual-function integration of UV photocatalysis and antifungal activity within a single Gd-doped BiPO₄ platform, evaluated under fully specified operating conditions. Materials and methods

Materials

Bismuth nitrate pentahydrate, Bi(NO₃)₃·5 H₂O (M = 485.07 g mol⁻¹, 99%), gadolinium nitrate hexahydrate, Gd(NO₃)₃·6 H₂O (Mv = 451.35 g mol⁻¹, 99%), oleic acid, C₁₈H₃₄O₂ (Mv = 282.47 g mol⁻¹, 99%), and ammonium dihydrogen phosphate, NH₄H₂PO₄ (Mv = 115.03 g mol⁻¹, 99%) were procured from Loba Chemie (India). Methylene blue (MB), C₁₆H₁₈ClN₃S (Mv = 319.85 g mol⁻¹, 99%), was used as the model dye without further purification. All other reagents and solvents used in synthesis, washing, and photocatalytic experiments were of analytical reagent (AR) grade and used as received. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm, Millipore) was used throughout.

Synthesis of pure and Gd-doped BiPO4 nanoparticles

Pure BiPO4 and Gd-doped BiPO4 nanoparticles (NPs) were prepared by an oleic-acid-assisted precipitation route (Fig. 1). In a typical synthesis, 1.0 g of Bi(NO3)3·5H2O (≈ 2.06 mmol, as the Bi3+ source) was dissolved in 2 mL of concentrated HCl to obtain a clear bismuth precursor solution. This acidification step suppresses premature hydrolysis of Bi3+ and stabilizes soluble bismuth species. The solution was then gradually diluted with deionized water under continuous stirring to reduce excess acidity and to enable controlled nucleation during precipitation. Subsequently, 20 mL oleic acid was introduced as a structure-directing surfactant to regulate particle growth and minimize agglomeration. For Gd-doped BiPO4, Gd(NO3)3·6H2O corresponding to 10 mol% of the Bi content (i.e., n(Gd) = 0.1 × n(Bi); for 1.0 g Bi(NO3)3·5H2O this equals ≈ 0.206 mmol, ~ 0.093 g Gd(NO3)3·6H2O) was added to the bismuth precursor. The Gd level was fixed at x = 0.10 (nominal) as a moderate substitution level chosen to provide a measurable dopant-driven electronic/defect modulation (beneficial for charge separation) while maintaining the BiPO₄ host phase and avoiding high-loading regimes where dopant-rich domains and defect overpopulation can increasingly act as recombination centers. Since the objective of this study is an integrated structure–optics–kinetics–mechanism–stability evaluation under fixed operating conditions (rather than dopant-level optimization), only one representative Gd content was investigated. A systematic concentration screening (e.g., x = 0.02–0.15) will be addressed as follow-up work. A glycerol–water mixed solvent (20 mL) was also introduced to improve phase compatibility, promote homogeneous distribution of Gd3+, and assist controlled precipitation.

Characterization techniques

The crystallographic structure and phase composition of the as-prepared samples were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical X’Pert PRO). Surface morphology and elemental composition were examined using field-emission scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (FESEM–EDS; Carl Zeiss Supra 55). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; JEOL JEM-2100, Japan) was used to assess nanoscale crystallinity and particle size. Optical absorption and band-gap characteristics were obtained from UV–Vis diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV–Vis DRS; Shimadzu UV-2600, Model COPP 01197), and band-gap values were estimated from Tauc’s plots using the Kubelka–Munk function.

Photocatalytic activity of Gd-doped BiPO4 NPs

Pollutant and instrumentation

The photocatalytic performance of the synthesized BiPO4 and Gd-doped BiPO4 NPs was evaluated using methylene blue (MB) as a model organic dye pollutant. Photocatalytic experiments were conducted in a LELESIL Innovative System photochemical reactor (Model 4D-2024) equipped with a 250 W UV lamp as the irradiation source. The reactor temperature was maintained using a water-circulated cooling jacket. Catalyst mass and reagents were weighed using a WENSER digital precision balance, and the suspensions were continuously stirred on a REMI 2MLH magnetic stirrer to ensure uniform dispersion during adsorption and irradiation.

Photocatalytic degradation of MB

An aqueous MB solution (10 mg L− 1) was prepared in deionized water. For each run, 25 mg (0.05 g L⁻¹) of photocatalyst (BiPO4 or Gd-doped BiPO4) was dispersed in 500 mL of the MB solution and stirred in the dark for 60 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. The suspension was then irradiated under UV light for 70 min with continuous stirring. At 10 min intervals, 15 mL aliquots were withdrawn and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min (REMI R-24) to remove photocatalyst particles. The residual MB concentration (Ct) in the clear supernatant was quantified by UV–Vis spectroscopy (JASCO V-750) by monitoring the absorbance at λmax = 664 nm.

Antifungal activity assay

The antifungal activity of Gd-BiPO₄ NPs was evaluated against the pathogenic yeast Candida tropicalis using the agar well diffusion method. Sabouraud Dextrose Agar (SDA) and Sabouraud Dextrose Broth (SDB) were used as solid and liquid media, respectively. A fresh yeast inoculum was prepared in SDB and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹). An aliquot of 100 µL of the standardized suspension was uniformly spread over sterile SDA plates to form a confluent microbial lawn. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched in the agar using a sterile borer. Gd-BiPO₄ suspensions at different concentrations were prepared in 10% (v/v) DMSO, and 50 µL of each suspension was carefully dispensed into the wells. 10% DMSO served as the negative control, while fluconazole (12 µg well⁻¹) was used as the positive control. The plates were incubated at 37 ± 1 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After incubation, antifungal efficacy was quantified by measuring the zone of inhibition (ZOI) around each well using a digital Vernier caliper (including the well diameter), and the mean ZOI was calculated from three independent replicates. Results were reported as mean ± SEM, enabling a quantitative comparison of concentration-dependent growth inhibition by Gd-BiPO₄.

Results and discussion

X-ray diffraction (XRD) and crystallite size analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used as the structural “fingerprint” to (i) confirm that the precipitation route yields crystalline BiPO₄, (ii) verify whether introducing Gd³⁺ preserves the BiPO₄ host phase or produces secondary crystalline phases (e.g., Gd₂O₃ or mixed Bi/Gd phosphates), and (iii) probe dopant-induced changes in crystallinity and peak broadening that are relevant to defect formation and charge-transport behavior. As shown in Fig. 2(a), pristine BiPO₄ exhibits a set of sharp Bragg reflections over 2θ = 10–80°, indicating high crystallinity and long-range order. The prominent reflections at 2θ ≈ 14.73°, 20.17°, 25.60°, 29.14–29.63°, 31.42°, 37.98°, 39.52°, 40.94°, 41.99°, 44.65° and 47.31° can be indexed to the monoclinic BiPO₄ lattice (Table S1). After Gd incorporation (Fig. 2(b)), the diffraction pattern retains the same set of characteristic reflections and matches the reference JCPDS card (00-045-1370; Fig. 2(c)), confirming that the dominant crystalline phase remains monoclinic BiPO₄. Importantly, no extra diffraction lines assignable to crystalline Gd₂O₃ or other bismuth/gadolinium phosphate phases are observed, implying that Gd³⁺ is incorporated into/associated with the BiPO₄ matrix without generating an XRD-detectable impurity phase. Compared with pristine BiPO₄, the Gd-modified sample shows slightly reduced peak intensities together with modest peak broadening (higher FWHM for several reflections in Table S2), which is consistent with crystallite refinement and dopant-induced lattice disorder/defect formation rather than a phase transformation.

XRD patterns of (a) pure BiPO₄ and (b) Gd-doped BiPO₄, with (c) reference JCPDS card No. 00-045-1370 confirming monoclinic phase formation. (d) Williamson–Hall and (e) modified Debye–Scherrer plots used for crystallite size and microstrain analysis.

A comparison of the peak positions extracted in Table S1 (BiPO₄) and Table S2 (Gd-BiPO₄) shows small but systematic shifts of several reflections toward lower 2θ after Gd addition. For instance, the intense low-angle reflection shifts from 14.73° (BiPO₄) to 14.64° (Gd-BiPO₄), and similar negative shifts of ~ 0.07–0.09° are observed for reflections near ~ 20.2°, ~ 25.6°, ~ 29.1–29.6°, ~ 31.4°, ~ 38.0°, ~ 39.5° and ~ 42.0°. According to Bragg’s law (2dsinθ = nλ), a decrease in 2θ corresponds to an increase in interplanar spacing (d), indicating a subtle lattice expansion and local structural relaxation induced by dopant incorporation and associated defect compensation. This trend is consistent with the refined lattice constants: pristine BiPO₄ exhibits a = 6.94 A˚ and c = 6.44 A˚ with V = 268.81 A˚3 (Table S3), whereas Gd-BiPO₄ shows expanded values c = 6.57 A˚ and V = 274.6 A˚3 (Table S4). The resulting ~ 2% increase in unit-cell volume supports dopant-induced lattice distortion within the monoclinic framework rather than a phase change. Importantly, such distortion is reasonable in rare-earth modified phosphate lattices because introducing Gd³⁺ can perturb Bi–O–P linkages and promote point-defect formation (e.g., oxygen-vacancy related states) to maintain local charge/coordination balance, which in turn affects both lattice metrics and peak broadening.

Crystallite sizes were estimated from the corrected line broadening using the Debye–Scherrer relation D = Kλ/(βcosθ) (Tables S1–S2). For pristine BiPO₄, the crystallite sizes derived from the principal reflections lie in the ~ 36–55 nm range with an average of ~ 37 nm, whereas Gd-BiPO₄ shows slightly smaller domains (~ 33–51 nm; average 34.04 nm). This consistent reduction in Scherrer size indicates that Gd addition suppresses crystallite coarsening and increases the fraction of grain boundaries/defect-rich interfaces, producing smaller coherent scattering domains. From a photocatalysis perspective, this refinement can be beneficial because it increases the density of surface reaction sites and can shorten the diffusion length required for photogenerated carriers to reach the surface before recombination, thereby supporting the enhanced activity observed for Gd-BiPO₄ in later sections.

The data points for both samples follow approximately linear trends, indicating that the uniform microstrain approximation is reasonable for comparative interpretation. For pristine BiPO₄, the linear fit gives an intercept corresponding to a W–H crystallite size of ~ 104 nm and a slope of 1.54 × 10− 3, yielding ε ≈ 1.54 × 10− 3. Thus, W–H analysis indicates that Gd incorporation reduces the effective coherent domain size while slightly lowering the average microstrain, consistent with dopant-assisted crystallite refinement and lattice relaxation within the preserved monoclinic framework.

To provide an additional check on size-related broadening, a modified Debye–Scherrer (MDS) treatment was performed (Fig. 2(e)) by plotting ln(β) versus ln(1/cosθ). From the linear fits, pristine BiPO₄ yields a slope of 3.91 and intercept of − 5.90, corresponding to an MDS crystallite size of ~ 50.7 nm (Table S2.1), whereas Gd-BiPO₄ gives a slope of 3.05 and intercept of − 5.72, corresponding to ~ 42.3 nm (Table S2.2). Although the absolute sizes differ across Scherrer, MDS and W–H methods due to their different assumptions (peak selection, isotropic broadening, strain distribution, and sensitivity to instrumental correction), all approaches consistently reproduce the same trend: Gd doping decreases the coherent domain size. The reduction in the MDS slope for the doped sample further suggests a change in the angular dependence of broadening, consistent with dopant-induced modification in the balance between size- and strain-related contributions at higher diffraction angles.

The crystallite size obtained for Gd-BiPO₄ in this work (≈ 34.04 nm from Debye–Scherrer analysis) falls in the expected nanometric regime for BiPO₄-based photocatalysts and is comparable to, or slightly smaller than, many reported monoclinic BiPO₄ systems. For example, microwave-assisted monoclinic BiPO₄ nanostructures commonly exhibit Scherrer crystallite sizes around40–45 nm, depending on precursor chemistry and thermal history49. Bi₂O₃-modified BiPO₄ architectures typically remain nanoscale as well (e.g., nanorod-type morphologies with diameters ~ 30 nm and lengths of 200–500 nm), highlighting that BiPO₄-based materials frequently form nanocrystalline domains even when assembled into larger anisotropic structures. Rare-earth-doped BiPO₄ assemblies (e.g., Tb³⁺/Eu³⁺ co-doped) likewise report crystallite sizes in the ~ 30–60 nm window, indicating that lanthanide modification often preserves the nanoscale domain size while tuning the defect/charge landscape50. Consistent with this behaviour, Gd³⁺ is widely reported to inhibit grain growth in Bi-based oxides; for instance, in Gd-doped Bi₂O₃ the average Scherrer size decreases from ~ 40 nm (undoped) to ~ 29 nm after Gd incorporation51, and analogous crystallite-size reductions have been noted in other rare-earth-modified oxide photocatalysts such as Sm-doped ZnO52,53. These comparisons support that the crystallite refinement observed here upon Gd addition is a realistic and literature-consistent microstructural effect rather than an artefact of peak selection.

Overall, the XRD results establish monoclinic BiPO₄ as the dominant crystalline phase in both samples (JCPDS 00-045-1370), with dopant-associated peak shifts and unit-cell expansion indicating measurable lattice perturbation, and line-broadening analyses (Scherrer/W–H/MDS) consistently indicating reduced coherent domain size after Gd addition. While XRD alone cannot unambiguously prove the exact dopant site occupancy, the combined observations (phase preservation, systematic peak shifts, lattice-parameter changes, and crystallite refinement) are fully consistent with Gd-induced lattice distortion/defect formation in the BiPO₄ matrix. From a photocatalytic standpoint, these structural features are relevant because smaller coherent domains and defect-assisted lattice perturbations can increase surface-active site density and promote charge separation by creating carrier trapping/transfer pathways, thereby improving radical generation and surface reaction kinetics, which aligns with the enhanced MB degradation performance discussed in subsequent sections.

Optical properties and band structure analysis

The optical response of pristine BiPO₄ and Gd-modified BiPO₄ NPs was first evaluated by UV–Vis absorbance spectroscopy to determine how Gd incorporation alters light-harvesting behaviour and the near-edge electronic structure that governs photocarrier generation. As shown in Fig. 3(a), both materials display strong absorption in the UV region, consistent with the wide-band-gap nature of monoclinic BiPO₄. Compared with pristine BiPO₄, the Gd-containing sample exhibits a clear increase in absorbance intensity across the entire UV range together with a more pronounced absorption tail extending toward longer wavelengths. This tailing behaviour (often described as an Urbach-type tail) is typically associated with dopant-induced structural disorder and defect-related sub-band states near the band edges, which can broaden the optical response and increase the probability of photon absorption close to the absorption edge. Although the local maximum in absorbance occurs around ~ 290 nm for BiPO₄ and ~ 291.5 nm for Gd-BiPO₄, the more meaningful change is the red-shift of the absorption edge implied by the band-gap reduction discussed below; together, the higher UV absorbance and stronger near-edge tailing indicate that Gd incorporation modifies the electronic landscape of BiPO₄ and can facilitate more efficient photocarrier generation under UV irradiation.

(c) Double-differentiation confirmation of the doped sample optical transition. (d) Band-edge positions relative to NHE derived using Mulliken electronegativity.

To independently corroborate the band-gap extracted from Tauc’s analysis, the absorbance spectrum was further examined using a second-derivative (double-differentiation) approach, which enhances subtle inflection points associated with the onset of fundamental absorption. In this method, the second derivative of the absorption response with respect to photon energy, d2(α)/d(hν)2, is plotted; the band-edge transition is typically manifested as a characteristic extremum or sharp feature near the absorption onset. As shown in Fig. 3(c) for the Gd-BiPO₄ sample, the derivative profile exhibits a distinct transition feature centred around ~ 3.78 eV, which is consistent with the Tauc’s-derived value and supports the conclusion that Gd incorporation reduces the optical gap. While derivative methods can be sensitive to spectral noise and numerical differentiation conditions, the agreement between the derivative-estimated transition energy and the Tauc’s fit strengthens the reliability of the measured Eg​ trend (3.93 eV → 3.78 eV) for the pristine and doped materials.

Similar dopant-driven narrowing has been reported in Gd-doped ZnO, where Eg decreased from 3.37 to 3.26 eV and the enhanced absorption correlated with improved MB degradation under UV irradiation54. For lanthanide-modified BiPO₄, Eu doping has been reported to decrease Eg and facilitate defect-mediated transitions that improve reactive-species generation and pollutant degradation56. In this context, the Eg = 3.78 eV obtained here for Gd-BiPO₄, together with the stronger UV absorbance and absorption tailing in Fig. 3(a), provides direct evidence that Gd incorporation tunes the BiPO₄ electronic structure in a way that is favorable for UV-driven photocatalysis and supports the kinetic enhancement observed during MB degradation.

Topographical and elemental analysis

The topographical features of pristine BiPO₄ and Gd-modified BiPO₄ were analysed by field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM) to assess how Gd addition affects particle growth and the resulting external architecture (Fig. 4a–b). Pristine BiPO₄ (Fig. 4a) consists of densely agglomerated microgranular clusters formed by the coalescence of smaller crystallites, producing a compact, irregular surface texture with limited long-range shape anisotropy. In contrast, the Gd-containing sample (Fig. 4b) shows a pronounced evolution toward anisotropic, rod-like crystallites with sharper edges and well-defined boundaries. From the micrographs (1 μm scale), the rods exhibit lengths on the order of micrometres and sub-micrometre widths, giving a high aspect-ratio morphology compared with the granular aggregates of pristine BiPO₄. This distinct change suggests that Gd addition modifies nucleation–growth kinetics during precipitation, favouring directional growth of BiPO₄ crystallites rather than isotropic aggregation. Such anisotropic architectures can be beneficial for photocatalysis because they increase the density of exposed edges/facets and can provide more continuous pathways for carrier transport along the long axis, which may contribute to the improved optical response and degradation kinetics observed for Gd-BiPO₄.

FESEM micrographs of (a) pure BiPO₄ showing densely packed microgranular morphology and (b) Gd-doped BiPO₄ exhibiting anisotropic rod-like crystallites. (c) EDS spectrum of Gd-doped BiPO₄ confirming the presence of Bi, P, O, and Gd without any impurity peaks, validating successful dopant incorporation into the BiPO₄ lattice.

The elemental composition of the Gd-modified sample was examined using energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) (Fig. 4c). The spectrum shows characteristic signals of Bi, P and O, confirming the formation of bismuth phosphate, along with distinct Gd peaks, verifying successful introduction of gadolinium into the BiPO₄ material. No additional elements are detected above the EDS detection limit, indicating that the product is free from obvious extraneous contaminants originating from reagents or processing. While EDS does not provide information on dopant site occupancy at the atomic level, the clear detection of Gd together with the absence of secondary crystalline phases in XRD supports that Gd is incorporated in the BiPO₄ product without forming a separate, XRD-detectable gadolinium-rich phase.

To verify dopant presence and its spatial distribution at the microscale, EDS elemental mapping was acquired from the same rod-rich region used for SEM imaging (Fig. 4b). The Bi, P and O maps show strong, co-localized signals that precisely follow the rod-like architecture, confirming that the elongated crystallites correspond to a bismuth phosphate phase rather than an adventitious organic residue or salt-derived by-product (Fig. 5). Importantly, the Gd map exhibits a detectable signal distributed across the same rod network, with no obvious Gd-rich “hotspots” or isolated secondary particles at the mapped scale. This spatial co-distribution supports that Gd is successfully introduced into the BiPO₄ material and is not present predominantly as a separate, micron-scale segregated phase. When considered together with the XRD results (single-phase monoclinic BiPO₄ and no additional Gd₂O₃-type reflections within the XRD detection limit), the mapping data indicate that Gd addition yields a chemically consistent Gd–doped BiPO₄ product in which the dopant is well dispersed throughout the BiPO₄ microstructure. As expected for low-level dopant introduction, the Gd intensity is lower than the host-element maps, while Bi/P/O remain dominant, consistent with BiPO₄ being the primary phase and Gd serving as a modifier rather than a major constituent.

High-resolution TEM and SAED analysis

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and selected-area electron diffraction (SAED) were used to validate the nanoscale morphology inferred from FESEM and to confirm whether Gd incorporation alters the crystal structure or introduces secondary phases. Figure 6(a–b) compares the TEM micrographs of pristine BiPO₄ and Gd–doped BiPO₄, while Fig. 6(c–d) provides the corresponding SAED patterns acquired from representative regions.

TEM analysis of BiPO₄ and Gd–doped BiPO₄: (a) pristine BiPO₄, (b) Gd-doped BiPO₄, (c, d) corresponding SAED patterns. Insets: HRTEM lattice fringes.

The pristine BiPO₄ sample (Fig. 6a) consists of agglomerated, short anisotropic crystallites together with irregular particles, forming compact clusters. The individual units appear as short rods/blocks with sub-micrometre dimensions and random orientation, consistent with the dense microgranular packing observed in FESEM. In contrast, the Gd–doped BiPO₄ sample (Fig. 6b) shows a clear morphology evolution toward 1D nanorods, where elongated particles dominate the field of view. The rods exhibit comparatively more uniform width and extended length, indicating that Gd addition modifies the growth kinetics and promotes anisotropic growth of BiPO₄ along preferred crystallographic directions.

SAED provides complementary crystallographic confirmation. The SAED pattern of pristine BiPO₄ (Fig. 6c) displays concentric diffraction rings decorated with bright spots, characteristic of a polycrystalline ensemble composed of many randomly oriented nanocrystallites within the selected area. The main rings are indexed to the monoclinic BiPO₄ phase, matching the XRD assignment and confirming that the undoped sample is crystalline and phase-pure. The Gd–doped BiPO₄ SAED pattern (Fig. 6d) similarly shows sharp rings with spotty intensity, again indicating a highly crystalline, polycrystalline/texture-like structure. Importantly, no additional rings attributable to crystalline Gd₂O₃ or mixed gadolinium phosphate phases are observed, supporting that Gd incorporation does not generate detectable secondary crystalline phases within the SAED detection limit and the host monoclinic BiPO₄ framework is retained.

The HRTEM insets in Fig. 6 show clear, continuous lattice fringes across individual nanorods, confirming high crystallinity for both pristine BiPO₄ and Gd-doped BiPO₄. The measured interplanar spacing is ~ 0.31 nm for pristine BiPO₄ and ~ 0.30 nm for Gd-doped BiPO₄. This slight shift is consistent with a subtle local lattice distortion/strain associated with Gd³⁺ incorporation and charge-compensation effects, while the persistence of sharp, periodic fringes indicates that the crystalline framework remains intact after doping. Importantly, the SAED patterns retain well-defined diffraction rings/spots that can be indexed to the BiPO₄ phase, supporting that Gd incorporation does not induce amorphization but only mildly perturbs the lattice at the nanoscale57-59. Similar use of combined SAED indexing and HRTEM d-spacing analysis to validate crystallinity and dopant-induced lattice perturbations is widely reported in catalytic/photocatalytic nanomaterials studies.

Overall, TEM and SAED confirm that Gd incorporation drives a distinct morphology transition from aggregated short crystallites to elongated nanorods without changing the BiPO₄ crystal structure. From a photocatalytic perspective, the rod-like architecture is beneficial because it can provide more continuous charge-transport pathways along the 1D axis, reduce the probability of carrier trapping at randomly packed grain interfaces, and increase the accessibility of surface reaction sites, which together support the higher MB degradation activity observed for Gd–doped BiPO₄.

Applications

Photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue dye

UV–Vis absorption studies

The photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) over pure BiPO₄ and Gd-doped BiPO₄ was tracked under UV irradiation by time-resolved UV–Vis spectroscopy. In each run, the catalyst suspension was first stirred in the dark for 60 min to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium, and aliquots were withdrawn at 10 min intervals during irradiation, centrifuged to remove photocatalyst particulates (to avoid light scattering), and analyzed by monitoring the MB absorbance at λ = 664 nm. Because MB concentration is proportional to absorbance in the working range (Beer–Lambert behaviour), the spectral decay directly reflects MB depletion; practically, Ct/C0​ is obtained from At/A0​ at 664 nm after the dark-equilibrated “time-zero” state.

Figure 7(a) shows the MB absorption profile characterized by a dominant band at ~ 664 nm with a weaker shoulder in the ~ 610–620 nm region (typical of MB electronic/vibrionic features in aqueous media). The small difference between the “initial” and “equilibrium” spectra indicates that dark adsorption contributes only marginally to dye removal, so the subsequent intensity loss under UV is primarily photocatalytic. With increasing irradiation time (10–70 min), both the main 664 nm band and the shoulder decrease steadily, confirming progressive destruction of the dye chromophore. However, a clear residual absorbance remains at 70 min, evidencing slower interfacial redox turnover and less efficient ROS-driven oxidation on undoped BiPO₄ under the same irradiation conditions.

Photocatalytic degradation of methylene blue (MB) under UV irradiation using pure BiPO₄ and Gd-doped BiPO₄: (a) time-dependent UV–Vis spectra with BiPO₄, (b) time-dependent UV–Vis spectra with Gd-doped BiPO₄, (c) concentration decay profile (Ct/C0) versus irradiation time (blank, BiPO₄, Gd-doped BiPO₄), (d) pseudo-first-order kinetic plots, (e) effect of initial solution pH on degradation efficiency, and (f) scavenger trapping test identifying the dominant reactive species. Error bars in panels (e) and (f) represent the standard deviation (SD) of replicate experiments (n = 3).

In Fig. 7(b), the same spectral signature decays much more rapidly for Gd-doped BiPO₄: the 664 nm band collapses strongly within the irradiation period and approaches the baseline by 70 min, demonstrating substantially faster chromophore depletion than pure BiPO₄. From a catalytic standpoint, this behaviour is consistent with dopant-driven electronic/defect modulation in the host lattice, where Gd incorporation can promote charge separation (electron trapping and delayed recombination) and increase the fraction of carriers that reach the surface to participate in O₂/H₂O activation. The net outcome is a higher steady-state flux of oxidizing species at the solid–liquid interface and therefore faster MB oxidation kinetics.

The concentration-decay plot in Fig. 7(c) quantitatively confirms the spectral trends. The blank curve remains essentially constant (negligible direct photolysis), whereas pure BiPO₄ shows a moderate decrease in Ct/C0​ and Gd-doped BiPO₄ shows a steep decrease throughout irradiation. Importantly, the end-point values in the plot are technically consistent with first-order kinetics reported later: at 70 min, Ct/C0​ ​ is approximately ~ 0.22 for BiPO₄ (≈ 78% removal) and ~ 0.06–0.07 for Gd-doped BiPO₄ (≈ 93–94% removal), confirming that Gd incorporation markedly accelerates MB depletion under UV light.

This expression is valid when the dye concentration is low and the surface coverage does not vary strongly with time, so the apparent rate becomes proportional to concentration (k is an “apparent” or lumped rate constant incorporating adsorption and intrinsic surface reaction). The linearity of ln(C0/Ct) versus irradiation time confirms that MB degradation follows pseudo-first-order behavior for both catalysts, indicating a stable reaction regime under the applied photon flux and catalyst loading.

The extracted rate constants clearly differentiate the materials: Gd-doped BiPO₄ (k = 0.0381 min⁻¹) shows a substantially higher slope than pure BiPO₄ (k = 0.0212 min⁻¹), while the blank (k = 0.0002 min⁻¹) indicates negligible self-photolysis. In practical terms, the kinetic half-life (t1/2 = 0.693/k) decreases from ~ 32.7 min (pure BiPO₄) to ~ 18.2 min (Gd-BiPO₄), demonstrating that Gd incorporation accelerates the oxidation sequence at the catalyst–solution interface. This higher k is consistent with more effective charge utilization (lower recombination probability) and higher steady-state ROS availability, which increases the frequency of productive dye-oxidation events rather than non-productive e⁻/h⁺ recombination.

Figure 7(e) shows a pronounced pH dependence for MB degradation over Gd-doped BiPO₄, with the efficiency rising from 45% (pH 3) to 96% (pH 7), followed by a drop to ~ 60% (pH 10). This trend is not governed by a single factor; it reflects coupled changes in (i) surface hydroxylation/charge state of the catalyst, (ii) adsorption of the cationic dye MB, and (iii) interfacial redox chemistry that controls ROS formation and lifetime.

At acidic pH, surface protonation reduces the density of negatively charged adsorption sites and can weaken MB uptake, while excess H⁺ can compete for reactive surface hydroxyls and shift interfacial charge transfer toward less efficient pathways. As pH approaches neutral, adsorption becomes more favorable and the surface availability of hydroxyl species (precursors for •OH generation by h⁺ oxidation) increases, giving the observed maximum at pH 7. At alkaline pH, despite higher OH⁻ availability, the overall degradation decreases, which is commonly attributed to reduced effective adsorption/interaction for the specific catalyst–dye system and increased scavenging of reactive radicals by background anions (for example, carbonate/bicarbonate in real alkaline media), which lowers the fraction of ROS that actually attack MB. Therefore, the neutral pH optimum indicates the condition where adsorption and ROS-mediated oxidation are simultaneously maximized for this catalyst system. Photoelectrochemical tests (transient photocurrent response and LSV) and PL spectroscopy are widely used to directly probe charge separation and interfacial charge transfer in photocatalysts, and related workflows can be found in recent reports 60,61.

The scavenger experiment in Fig. 7(f) provides mechanistic discrimination among competing oxidative pathways by selectively suppressing specific reactive species. In the absence of scavengers, Gd-BiPO₄ achieves 96% degradation. Upon adding IPA (•OH quencher), degradation decreases to 75%, confirming a meaningful contribution from hydroxyl radicals. The most drastic suppression occurs with BQ (•O₂⁻ quencher), where degradation drops to 20%, indicating that superoxide radicals make the largest contribution under these conditions. Addition of CCl₄ (electron scavenger) reduces degradation to 45%, indicating that photogenerated electrons are required for O₂ activation and downstream ROS production. With KI (h⁺ scavenger), degradation decreases only to 82%, suggesting that holes contribute but are not the primary pathway compared with ROS-mediated oxidation. Although the scavenger results show a strong •O₂⁻ contribution, this can appear inconsistent with the uncorrected CB position (more positive than the O₂/•O₂⁻ level under standard reference). This apparent mismatch can be rationalized by interfacial effects, including surface/defect-state mediated electron transfer and band bending, which create localized electron-rich sites that promote O₂ activation. In addition, the aqueous band edges can shift with pH, making O₂ reduction more feasible under the experimental conditions. Similar surface-state/electron-rich-microenvironment arguments are widely discussed in recent environmental photocatalysis literature 62,63.

Reusability and stability of Gd-BiPO₄

A key requirement for practical photocatalysis is cycle-to-cycle activity retention together with structural and morphological invariance after repeated irradiation and catalyst recovery. The reusability of Gd-BiPO₄ was therefore evaluated over six consecutive MB degradation runs under identical operating conditions. The post-reaction UV–Vis spectra collected after each cycle (Fig. 9a) show consistent suppression of the MB absorption band at ~ 664 nm, demonstrating that the catalyst continues to drive photodegradation of the chromophore across repeated use rather than showing only transient adsorption effects.

Reusability and stability of Gd-doped BiPO₄ for MB degradation under UV irradiation: (a) cycle-wise UV–Vis spectra of MB after photocatalysis over six runs, (b) degradation efficiency (%) retained over six cycles, (c) XRD pattern of fresh Gd-BiPO₄, (d) XRD pattern after six cycles, (e) FESEM image of fresh Gd-BiPO₄, and (f) FESEM image after six cycles showing preserved rod-like morphology.

Quantitatively, the catalyst exhibits strong activity retention (Fig. 9b): the degradation efficiency decreases only slightly from 95% (cycle 1) to 88% (cycle 6), following a gradual trend 95 → 93 → 91 → 90 → 89 → 88%, corresponding to an overall loss of ~ 7% after six cycles. Such a small decline is typically consistent with surface- and handling-related factors (partial coverage of active sites by strongly adsorbed intermediates, mild aggregation during recovery/drying that lowers accessible surface area, and minor catalyst mass loss during repeated washing/collection), rather than intrinsic lattice instability.

To verify whether photocatalytic cycling induces any bulk structural change, XRD patterns were recorded for the fresh catalyst and the sample recovered after six cycles (Fig. 9c–d). The diffractograms show no additional reflections, no measurable peak shift, and no noticeable peak broadening after reuse, confirming retention of the BiPO₄ crystalline framework and excluding the formation of secondary crystalline phases during reaction. Morphological stability was further supported by FESEM (Fig. 9e–f): the recycled catalyst preserves the rod-like architecture without obvious collapse, severe fragmentation, or extensive sintering, which is important for maintaining stable exposure of reactive surface sites and consistent light–matter interaction during irradiation.

Overall, the combined UV–Vis cycling response, high activity retention, unchanged XRD, and preserved rod morphology confirm that Gd-BiPO₄ is photostable and recyclable under UV-driven MB degradation.

Antifungal activity of Gd-BiPO₄ NPs

The antifungal performance of Gd-BiPO₄ NPs was assessed against the opportunistic yeast Candida tropicalis using an agar well diffusion assay, and the results are presented in Fig. 9(a–b). This method provides a rapid, comparative readout of growth inhibition based on the formation of a clear zone of inhibition (ZOI) around the test well, where the apparent zone size depends not only on intrinsic antifungal potency but also on diffusion/transport of the active agent through the agar matrix.

As shown in Fig. 10(a), the positive control (fluconazole, 12 µg well⁻¹) produced a clear ZOI of 19 mm, confirming assay validity and fungal susceptibility under the test conditions. The negative control (DMSO, 0 µg well⁻¹) showed no inhibition, indicating that the solvent system did not contribute to antifungal activity. For Gd-BiPO₄, no measurable inhibition was observed at 12.5 and 25 µg well⁻¹, whereas a detectable inhibition halo appeared at 50 µg well⁻¹ with a ZOI of ~ 2 mm. Increasing the nanoparticle dose to 100 µg well⁻¹ increased the ZOI to ~ 7 mm, and the highest tested dose (200 µg well⁻¹) produced a ZOI of ~ 13 mm, demonstrating a clear concentration-dependent response. The representative SDA plate image (Fig. 10(b)) visually corroborates the quantitative data, showing well-defined inhibition halos only at higher nanoparticle loadings.

Although the inhibition zones for Gd-BiPO₄ are smaller than those of fluconazole, direct comparison should be made cautiously because small-molecule drugs diffuse efficiently in agar, whereas NPs typically show restricted diffusion and strong sedimentation/retention near the well, which can reduce the apparent zone size even when contact-mediated activity is present. The requirement of higher loading to observe zones is also consistent with the robust fungal cell envelope (cell wall rich in β-glucans/chitin and an ergosterol-containing membrane), which can limit penetration and reduce susceptibility compared with many bacterial systems.

Mechanistically, the observed inhibition is consistent with surface-mediated interactions between Gd-BiPO₄ and fungal cells at the agar–cell interface. The rod-like, high-surface-area micro/nanostructure can promote close contact with the cell wall and membrane, potentially leading to membrane stress and loss of integrity. In addition, dopant-induced defects and surface redox-active sites may contribute to localized oxidative stress in biological media (for example, via oxygen activation at the particle surface), which can damage membrane lipids and proteins and impair cellular function. These mechanistic pathways should be treated as proposed until validated by targeted experiments (ROS probes, membrane leakage assays, microscopy of treated cells).

Overall, Gd-BiPO₄ exhibits dose-dependent antifungal activity against C. tropicalis, reaching a maximum ZOI of ~ 13 mm at 200 µg well⁻¹ in the agar diffusion assay.

Conclusion

Pristine BiPO₄ and Gd-doped BiPO₄ were synthesized via chemical precipitation and systematically correlated across structure–optics–function. XRD confirmed phase-pure monoclinic BiPO₄, while Gd incorporation induced modest lattice perturbation and crystallite refinement (≈ 37 → 34.04 nm) without secondary phases. UV–Vis/Tauc analysis showed enhanced absorption with band-gap narrowing from 3.93 to 3.78 eV. Photocatalytic evaluation toward methylene blue under UV irradiation (10 mg L⁻¹; 25 mg catalyst; 500 mL; 60 min dark equilibration) demonstrated that Gd–doped BiPO₄ markedly accelerates dye removal, reaching Ct/C0 ≈ 0.06–0.07 after 70 min (≈ 93–94% removal) compared with Ct/C0 ≈ 0.22 for pristine BiPO₄ (≈ 78% removal). Kinetic fitting followed a pseudo-first-order Langmuir–Hinshelwood regime, with k increasing from 0.0212 min⁻¹ (BiPO₄) to 0.0381 min⁻¹ (Gd–doped BiPO₄). Reusability tests confirmed strong operational stability with only ~ 7% activity loss over six cycles (95% → 88%), supported by unchanged XRD patterns and preserved rod-like morphology after reuse. Beyond photocatalysis, Gd–doped BiPO₄ also showed concentration-dependent antifungal activity against Candida tropicalis, achieving a maximum zone of inhibition of ~ 13 mm at 200 µg well⁻¹. Collectively, the dopant-tuned electronic structure and stable morphology make Gd–doped BiPO₄ a durable candidate for dye remediation and surface-associated antifungal applications.
