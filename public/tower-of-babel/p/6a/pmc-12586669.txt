The joint effects of multiple air pollutants, genetic susceptibility, and their interactions on incident cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, and mortality
Authors: Di Tian, Xu Shan, Liyuan Han, Kun Han, Hongpeng Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study assessed combined effects of air pollutants on arrhythmia, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and mortality, and explored genetic susceptibility’s role. In 435,893 participants from the UK Biobank, air pollutant concentrations (PM2.5, PM2.5–10, PM10, NO2, and NO) were obtained from established land-use regression models linked to the database.An air pollution scores integrated pollutant levels weighted by CVD/arrhythmia-associated coefficients from single-pollutant Cox models. Genetic risk scores (GRS) and pollutant-attributable fractions (PAFs) were analyzed, with GRS primarily examined in the context of gene–pollution interactions rather than as an independent risk factor. Over 12.7 median follow-up years, 56,354 incident arrhythmia cases occurred. Adjusted models showed higher quartiles of NO2, NO, and PM2.5 increased risks of arrhythmia, CVD, and mortality (all P-trend <0.001; P-trend indicated trend tests across increasing exposure categories). Men exhibited weaker associations between pollution scores and arrhythmia/CVD than women. NO2 was the primary risk factor for arrhythmia [RR:1.04 (1.01–1.08); PAF=3.45%] and CVD [RR:1.11 (1.08–1.14); PAF=8.11%], while NO and PM2.5 dominated CVD mortality [RR:1.20 (1.08–1.33); PAF=13.62%] and all-cause mortality [RR:1.10 (1.06–1.13); PAF=7.10%], respectively. Furthermore, significant interactions were observed between air pollution and GRS, indicating higher susceptibility among individuals with elevated genetic risk. Combined exposure to five pollutants heightened arrhythmia, CVD, and mortality risks, with NO2 as the key driver for arrhythmia/CVD. Genetic susceptibility modified pollution-related risks. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of disease burden worldwide, with a growing prevalence1. It is essential to implement interventions to manage CVD risk factors, such as strong prevention strategies, to effectively address the significant challenges posed by CVD and reduce its impact. Air pollution has long been recognized as a notable and modifiable risk factor for CVD, and its adverse effects have been extensively studied2,3.

Air pollution, encompassing both household and ambient varieties, remains the leading cause of death, causing 6.7 million deaths in 20194. Ambient air pollution commonly comprises PM with diameters ranging from ≤ 2.5 µm to ≤ 10 µm (PM2.5-10), as well as NO and NO2. PM is a complex amalgamation of metal oxides, acid condensates, transition metals, sulfates, nitrates, and organic compounds, is among the most rigorously studied air pollutants. It is primarily produced by combustion processes in vehicles, industrial facilities, and power plants5. Inhalation of PM2.5 into the lungs triggers an inflammatory response and oxidative damage, which can penetrate the blood-brain barrier and enter the systemic circulation. This affects a metabolically active organ, the heart, and also promotes cardiovascular disease by promoting cardiovascular disease risk factors, including obesity and diabetes6. Exposures to PM2.5 and NO2 are linked to an increased risk of all-cause mortality and cardiovascular death, and their combined exposure with other environmental factors also poses a significant health threat7.

Numerous epidemiological, experimental animal, and toxicological studies have consistently shown a strong correlation between air pollution and the occurrence of CVD8,9. Inhalable PM in polluted air is associated with both acute and chronic CVD events. Prolonged exposure to PM has been identified as a contributing factor to the development and progression of atherosclerosis10. Conversely, short-term exposure to PM can trigger plaque instability, leading to acute CVD events such as myocardial ischemia, stroke, heart failure (HF), arrhythmia, and sudden death11,12. While several studies have investigated the combined effects of multiple air pollutants on cardiovascular health and mortality13,14, evidence remains limited regarding how such joint exposures interact with genetic predisposition to influence the risks of incident cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, and mortality. Our study aims to address this gap by simultaneously evaluating multi-pollutant exposures and genetic susceptibility using a large prospective cohort.

In addition to environmental exposures, genetic susceptibility has emerged as an important determinant of CVD risk. Recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple loci associated with cardiovascular outcomes, enabling the construction of polygenic or genetic risk scores (GRS). Individuals with higher GRS have been shown to carry an elevated baseline risk of CVD, and growing evidence suggests that gene–environment interactions may further amplify pollution-related cardiovascular risks15–17.

This study comprehensively analyzed the correlation between air pollution indicators (such as PM2.5, PM10, PM2.5-10, NO2, and NO) and the risk of developing CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality. Furthermore, it explored the interplay between air pollution and genetic predisposition in influencing these health outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

UK Biobank (UKB) is a population-based cohort study investigating genetic and non-genetic determinants of disease in middle-aged and elderly individuals18. Between 2006 and 2010, ~500,000 participants (94% European descent) were recruited across 22 research centers. At baseline the mean age was 56.5 years (range: 40–69 years). The UKB database comprises genome-wide genotyping data, clinical assessments, biological samples and extensive baseline measures linked to individual electronic health records including hospitalization, mortality and cancer registrations18. For further details on UKB, including fundraising and geographical profiling, see Sudlow et al. (2015)18. This large-scale prospective study was approved by the Northwest Research Ethics Committee (ref: 11/NW/0382). The current study was conducted using UKB resources (application ID 78619). Informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardian(s). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations

The study included participants who had data available on PM2.5, PM10, PM2.5-10, NO2, and NOX exposure, and who did not have baseline CVD or arrhythmia. Figure 1 showed the associations of the air pollution score with the risk of incident cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, and all-cause mortality in the UK Biobank. A total of 435,893 participants aged 38–73 years were included in this prospective study, with a median follow-up time of 13.7 years (see Table S1 for detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria and Field IDs used). Assessment of air pollution

Land-use regression (LUR) models, as explored by the European Study of Cohorts for Air Pollution Effects project, were employed to ascertain the annual average air pollution exposures of participants selected from the UKB study19,20. These models incorporated predictors from geographic information systems, such as traffic density, and were validated against air pollution monitoring data. The model function was estimated utilizing the leave-one-out cross-validation method, a prevalent technique in the development and validation of LUR models19,20 . Cross-validation coefficients of determination (R2) for PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and NO were 77%, 88%, 87%, and 88%, respectively21. The concentration of NO was obtained by subtracting the measured NO2 level from the corresponding NOx value22. To assess the combined impact of multiple pollutants, we developed an air pollution score, defined as a weighted sum of five major air pollutants. The weights were assigned according to the multivariable-adjusted regression coefficients (β values) for arrhythmia and cardiovascular disease, such that pollutants with stronger associations contributed proportionally more to the overall score. Although most UK Biobank studies directly analyzed NOx, we focused on NO because it is a major component of traffic-related air pollution and has been independently associated with cardiovascular outcomes in prior studies23,24. Including NO in the air pollution score allows us to capture its distinct contribution and examine potential joint effects with other pollutants. The air pollution score was designed to capture the combined effects of correlated pollutants while mitigating collinearity issues in regression models. By weighting each pollutant according to its independent association with arrhythmia and CVD, the score summarizes cumulative exposure while reflecting the relative contribution of each pollutant, rather than simply aggregating all available measurements. The air pollution score was calculated using the following formula:Air pollution score = [ β (PM2.5) × PM2.5 + β (PM2.5 − 10) × PM2.5–10 + β (PM10) × PM10 +β (NO2) × NO2 + β (NO)× NO] × ( 5 / Σ β )

In our study, NO concentrations were calculated by subtracting NO2 from NOx, so that NO2 represents the major fraction of NOx and NO the remaining fraction. This additive weighted approach assumes linear and independent effects of each pollutant and allows integration of multi-pollutant exposure into a single metric for downstream analyses. While correlations among pollutants could theoretically introduce minor bias, using β coefficients from multivariable models mitigates this concern by reflecting the relative risk of each pollutant adjusted for the others.

Genotyping and genetic risk score (GRS)

Genotypes of participants were obtained from the UK Biobank dataset. A Genetic Risk Score (GRS) was calculated to quantify individual-level genetic susceptibility by summing the number of risk alleles for each single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP), weighted by their estimated effect sizes (β coefficients), providing a composite measure of genetic influence on cardiovascular outcomes25,26. The GRS is computed by attributing weights to SNP scores based on the magnitude and direction of their correlation with the disease. The GRS was formulated utilizing single-nucleotide SNPs. Each SNP’s contribution to the GRS is ascertained by the quantity of risk alleles present in an individual (ranging from 0 to 2). The assigned weights are multivariable-adjusted risk estimates (β coefficients) referenced earlier. Participants were categorized into three groups based on the distribution of the GRS (e.g., tertiles): a low-risk group, an intermediate-risk group, and a high-risk group. The air pollution score was calculated separately as a weighted sum of five major air pollutants (PM2.5, PM2.5–10, PM10, NO2, and NO), with weights corresponding to the β coefficients from multivariable-adjusted models for arrhythmia and cardiovascular disease. The GRS and air pollution score were analyzed independently and in combination to assess potential gene–environment interactions.

Assessment of outcomes

The study focused on primary outcomes such as arrhythmia, CVD, and mortality. CVD was specifically defined as a combination of CHD, congestive heart failure (CHF), and ischemic stroke. Arrhythmia was broadened to include ectopic arrhythmia, atrial fibrillation/flutter (AF/flutter), supraventricular tachycardia (SVT), and ventricular tachycardia (VT)/ventricular fibrillation (VF). Mortality encompassed both all-cause mortality and CVD-specific mortality. These outcomes were evaluated through data obtained from hospital inpatient records, self-reported information, and death registry records (Table S2).

Assessment of covariates

The study analyzed various covariates, such as age, sex, ethnicity (White/non-White), education level (0–7 years, 8–10 years, 11–15 years, or ≥16 years), body mass index (BMI), income, systolic blood pressure (SBP), physical activity, self-reported using a touchscreen questionnaire at baseline including frequency, duration, and intensity of walking, moderate, and vigorous activities, and calculated as metabolic equivalent minutes per week (MET-min/week), work status (employed/unemployed), alcohol consumption (never/past/current), and smoking status (never/past/current). Alcohol consumption and smoking status were self-reported, while MET-min/week indicated the total activity minutes per week. Additionally, the researchers utilized the Townsend deprivation index as a regional proxy indicator of socioeconomic status.

A score for assessing a healthy lifestyle was created based on seven variables: physical activity, BMI, alcohol consumption, smoking status, daily intake of fruits and vegetables, waist-to-hip ratio, and sedentary time. Predefined cutoffs for these seven variables were applied to calculate the healthy lifestyle score, and participants were categorized into low, moderate, or high score groups accordingly20. Participants were divided into three categories: individuals with a low, unhealthy score (0 or 1), those with a moderate score (2 or 3), and those with a high score (≥4).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations, were analyzed for all continuous variables, while rates and percentages were calculated for all categorical variables. Spearman’s correlation coefficients were used to examine the relationships between individual air pollutants. The Cox proportional hazards model was employed to evaluate the association between air pollution and incident CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality, presenting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 adjusted for sex and age, Model 2 additionally adjusted for income, education, occupation, ethnicity, alcohol consumption, and smoking status, and Model 3 further included adjustments for SBP, physical activity, and BMI. Interactions between genetic factors and air pollution were also explored. To examine potential gene–environment interactions, multiplicative interaction terms between the genetic risk score and air pollution score were included in Cox proportional hazards models, and likelihood ratio tests were used to evaluate statistical significance. Stratified analyses were further performed to assess whether associations differed across subgroups of sex (male, female), age (<60 years, ≥60 years), and healthy lifestyle score (0–1, 2–3, ≥4). These subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential effect modification rather than solely to address confounding. In addition to the main analysis, several sensitivity analyses were performed. Firstly, to minimize potential reverse causation, participants who developed outcomes within the first two years of follow-up were excluded. Secondly, to reduce exposure misclassification, individuals who had lived at their current address for less than 5 years were excluded. Thirdly, total cholesterol concentration was further adjusted in addition to covariates in Model 3, as cholesterol may act as an intermediate factor on the pathway between air pollution and cardiovascular outcomes, and was therefore not included in the primary models to avoid overadjustment. The population attributable fraction (PAF) was calculated using standard epidemiological formulas, representing the proportion of cases that could be prevented in the study population if a specific risk factor were eliminated27. PAFs were estimated for each pollutant and genetic factor independently, as well as for their combined effects, to quantify the proportion of cardiovascular disease, arrhythmia, and mortality risk attributable to these exposures. The analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3), and any two-sided P values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Throughout a median follow-up period of 12.7 years, a total of 56,354 participants developed arrhythmia, 5,300 developed SVT, 3,052 developed VT and ventricular fibrillation (VTVF), 29,277 developed AF/flutter, 86,924 developed CVD, 68,808 developed CHD, 20,664 developed congestive HF, 12,042 developed ischaemic stroke, 57,418 died of all-cause mortality, and 5,302 died of CVD mortality (Table S3). Individuals with higher air pollution scores, in comparison to those with lower scores, exhibited a higher likelihood of being male, having lower levels of education, being obese (with higher BMI and waist circumference), non-drinkers, current smokers, employed, having lower income, lower MET, lower SBP, and lower healthy lifestyle scores (Table 1). Specific information on the concentration data for the five air pollutants can be found in Table S4 . The Spearman’s correlation coefficients for various air pollutants can be found in Table S5 .

Associations of various air pollutants with the risks of CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality

The effects of personal exposure to air pollutants on the risk of CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality were evaluated. In model 3, after adjusting for all covariates, significant associations were observed between the highest versus lowest quartiles of NO2, NO, and PM2.5 and a higher risk of incident arrhythmia, CVD, and all-cause mortality (except VTVF). For each interquartile-range increase in other personal air pollutants, the adjusted hazard ratio was 1.03 to 1.11 for incident arrhythmia, CVD, and mortality (except VTVF) (Table S6).

Table 2 illustrates the correlation between air pollution scores and the risks associated with CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality. Upon adjusting for all covariates in model 3, it was found that individuals with higher air pollution scores had significantly increased risks of developing arrhythmia and CVD, except for VTVF (all P-trend values < 0.001). Furthermore, these individuals also faced higher risks of all-cause and CVD mortality (all P-trend values < 0.001). Cox models utilizing penalized splines indicated linear relationships for AF, arrhythmia, stroke, SVT, and CVD mortality, while demonstrating S-shaped relationships for HF, CHD, CVD, and all-cause mortality as shown in Figure 1.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses revealed a more pronounced effect of overall air pollution on incident arrhythmia, CVD, and CHD in women than in men. The HRs with 95% CIs were 1.14 (1.07–1.21), 1.17 (1.12–1.23), and 1.16 (1.10–1.23) for arrhythmia, CVD, and CHD, respectively. The p-values for interaction were 0.042, 0.012, and 0.014, respectively, indicating statistically significant gender disparities. Similarly, a heightened effect of overall air pollution on all-cause mortality and CVD mortality was observed in participants aged below 60 compared to those aged above 60. The HRs with 95% CIs stood at 1.23 (1.14–1.32) and 1.60 (1.26–2.04) for all-cause mortality and CVD mortality, respectively. The p-values for interaction were <0.001 and 0.0338, respectively. (Table S7).

Interactions between air pollution and the genetic predisposition to CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality

In our study, we discovered that individuals with high air pollution scores and low GRS for CVD faced a greater risk of CVD compared to those with low air pollution scores and high GRS. This association was found to be statistically significant, with p-values of 0.009 for CVD and 0.006 for CHD. Despite these findings, no interaction effects between air pollution scores and GRS were observed in relation to CVD mortality or all-cause mortality (Table S8-9).

Population attributable fractions

NO2 exposure was identified as the primary risk factor for arrhythmia and CVD, with PAFs of 3.45% and 8.11%, respectively. The combined PAFs of the five air pollutants for arrhythmia and CVD were 13.44% and 25.23%, respectively. Moreover, NO exposure was determined to be the key risk factor for CVD mortality, showing a PAF of 13.62%, while PM2.5 exposure was associated with all-cause mortality, having a PAF of 7.10%. The combined PAFs of the five air pollutants for CVD mortality and all-cause mortality were 37.48% and 25.01%, respectively (Table 3). Sensitivity analyses

The sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent results when utilizing air pollution scores for NO2, NO, PM10, and PM2.5. Additionally, the findings remained strong even after excluding cases within the initial 2 years or participants residing at their current address for less than 5 years. Further adjustments in model 3 for BMI, SBP, and total cholesterol concentration did not alter the results ( Table S10).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, it was found that exposure to specific air pollutants such as NO2, NO, and PM2.5, along with an air pollution score (including PM2.5, PM10, PM2.5-10, NO2, and NO), were independently associated with increased risks of incident CVD, arrhythmia, and mortality. Moreover, a stronger link between the air pollution score and incident arrhythmia, CVD, and CHD was observed in women compared to men. The impact of air pollutants on all-cause mortality and CVD mortality was more significant in individuals under 60 years old compared to those over 60 years old. Particularly, NO2 was identified as the primary risk factor for arrhythmia and CVD, while NO and PM2.5 were highlighted as the main risk factors for CVD mortality and all-cause mortality, respectively.

Compelling evidence suggests that short-term exposure to PM can increase the risk of various cardiovascular conditions, including hypertension, arrhythmia, myocardial ischemia, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and even sudden death28,29. Two separate studies conducted in St. Louis and Boston, USA, examined the impact of air pollution exposure duration. It was noted that within 24 hours before an event, the risk of ventricular arrhythmia rose by 19% for every 7.8-µg/m3 increase in the average ambient concentration of PM2.530. Additionally, Jungrnan et al. found a link between ICU discharge or exposure to air pollution (mainly PM10 and NO) within 2 hours prior to the onset of ventricular arrhythmia, resulting in an increased risk of such events (odds ratio [OR] = 1.31, 95% CI: 1.00–1.72). Particularly, within a 10 to 15 km radius from the PM10-rich air source, the OR for arrhythmia risk increased to 1.76, with the frequency and severity of ventricular arrhythmia showing a positive correlation with ambient air quality. These findings are consistent with our own research. However, a study involving 132 chronic heart failure patients did not find a significant association between air pollutants and cardiac rhythm parameters or heart rate variability (HRV), suggesting that exposure to NO2 and PM2.5/PM10 was not linked to arrhythmia31. Discrepancies in these results could be due to factors such as geographical location, research methodologies, study duration, and the potential combined influence of good air quality and low statistical power.

Previous studies have established a clear correlation between heightened levels of PM2.5 and PM10 in the atmosphere and an augmented likelihood of death from CVD32,33. Our research findings substantiate this relationship. It is noteworthy that this connection remains statistically significant even at lower PM2.5 concentrations, below 10 µg/m334. Nevertheless, the link between PM exposure and mortality demonstrates variability across different concentration levels. The immediate risks of mortality linked to each incremental rise in PM concentration are less pronounced at elevated levels of PM as opposed to lower levels35. Conversely, chronic health implications, particularly concerning CVD and overall mortality, exhibit a more pronounced effect with increased PM concentrations than with lower concentrations36.

It has been proposed that oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions play a significant role in the cardiovascular toxicity of PM. These mechanisms can lead to vascular endothelial dysfunction, formation of atherosclerotic plaques, and disruptions in heart rhythm37. Montiel-Dávalos et al. found that PM can induce the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), causing oxidative stress, as well as the production of NO and the nuclear translocation of nuclear factor kappa B, a transcription factor involved in gene regulation38. These processes in cardiovascular endothelial cells may ultimately lead to cell apoptosis. The oxidative stress and inflammatory reactions mediated by ROS are thought to be key factors in the vascular endothelial cell damage caused by PM, suggesting that PM-induced cardiovascular endothelial cell apoptosis occurs through oxidative damage, ultimately contributing to cardiovascular disease development.

The effects of air pollutants may be more pronounced in women compared to men, potentially due to physiological differences in their cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems39. Disparities in the respiratory tracts of men and women, originating during embryonic development and continuing throughout life40, could also contribute to this inequality. Notably, women typically have narrower respiratory tracts than men, rendering them more susceptible to foreign substances41.

Our research determined that air pollutants have a more pronounced effect on both all-cause mortality and CVD mortality in individuals aged below 60 compared to those aged 60 and above. This discrepancy may be attributed to the age factor, which serves as a confounding variable. Younger individuals, who are more likely to participate in outdoor work activities, may be more susceptible to the detrimental effects of air pollution42. Surprisingly, our findings indicated that participants exposed to high levels of air pollutants and with a low GRS for CVD had a higher risk of CVD compared to those with low air pollutant exposure and a high GRS for CVD or CHD. This implies that a high genetic susceptibility does not increase the susceptibility to the effects of pollutants.

Recent evidence emphasizes that individual susceptibility to air pollution is influenced by sex and age. For instance, a systematic analysis in European Journal of Preventive Cardiology highlighted that females experience disproportionately greater cardiovascular risks from air pollution compared to males, even after adjusting for confounders43,44. Similarly, a large observational study45 found that non-elderly women exhibited higher stroke incidence following exposure to pollutants such as PM₂.₅, NO₂, benzo[a]pyrene (B[a]P), and SO₂, with significant interaction effects by age and sex. These findings align with our results, where women—and particularly those under 60—demonstrated more pronounced associations between air pollution and cardiovascular outcomes. Biological hypotheses for this disparity include sex-specific differences in vascular reactivity, hormonal influences, and airway morphology. Social and behavioral factors, such as differential outdoor exposure patterns, may also modulate vulnerability. Further supporting age-related variation, a recent study on endothelial function found that associations between air pollutants (PM₂.₅, PM₁₀, NO₂, SO₂, CO) and reduced flow-mediated dilatation (FMD) were significant in younger individuals but attenuated or absent in those aged ≥6546. This suggests that younger populations may have greater exposure or fewer adaptive physiological defenses. Overall, these developments underscore the importance of recognizing heterogeneity in air pollution effects and tailoring public health interventions to vulnerable subgroups, including non-elderly women and younger adults.

This study is the first to assess the collective influence of air pollutants on the occurrence of arrhythmia, CVD, and mortalit among a subset of the UK population. Furthermore, it investigates the potential impact of genetic predisposition on the risks linked to these conditions. The findings provide a basis for further investigations into the lasting effects of air pollution on heart health and the development of interventions to prevent and address air pollution.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, data collection relied heavily on self-reported information from participants, which may introduce memory bias. Additionally, average concentrations of ambient air pollutants in participants’ residential areas were used as a proxy for actual exposure levels, potentially biasing the results. The research failed to consider meteorological factors such as wind direction, velocity, humidity, and temperature, which have been associated with cardiovascular disease mortality in earlier studies47,48. The strong correlations between PM2.5, NO2, and NO were not fully explored in the analysis, warranting further investigation into their interactive effects on health outcomes. Unmeasured confounding may be present in this study; therefore, the findings should be approached with caution. Finally, our study focused on PM2.5, PM2.5–10, PM10, NO2, and NO, while other important air pollutants, such as ozone (O3), carbon monoxide (CO), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (e.g., benzo[a]pyrene), were not included. The omission of these pollutants may lead to an underestimation of the overall cardiovascular risk associated with air pollution. Future studies should consider a broader set of pollutants to comprehensively evaluate the health effects of multi-pollutant exposure.

This study discovered that prolonged exposure to elevated levels of NO2, NO, and PM2.5, along with a high air pollution score, significantly increases the risk of incident arrhythmia, CVD, and all-cause mortality. Furthermore, the research revealed that the link between air pollution and arrhythmia, CVD, and CHD was more prominent in women than in men. Moreover, the impact of air pollution on all-cause mortality and CVD mortality was more significant in younger participants (under 60 years old) compared to older participants. Finally, the study suggests that high genetic susceptibility does not exacerbate vulnerability to the adverse effects of the pollutants examined.
