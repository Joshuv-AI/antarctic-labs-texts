A Randomized Trial of a Resilience-Building Intervention in Adult Outpatients With Congenital Heart Disease
Authors: Jill M. Steiner, Arisa Rei Marshall, Lyndia Brumback, Yixuan Connie Zhang, Thomas M. Glenn, Adrienne H. Kovacs, Karen K. Stout, Chris T. Longenecker, Joyce P. Yi-Frazier, Abby R. Rosenberg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInterventions are needed to address growing concerns regarding poor psychosocial and mental health in adult congenital heart disease (ACHD). Strengthening patients' resilience is one proposed pathway, given its ability to improve quality of life in other patient populations.ObjectivesThe objective of this study was to evaluate feasibility and efficacy of a resilience-building intervention in ACHD.MethodsWe conducted a clinical trial of outpatients with moderate or complex ACHD, randomized to receive a cognitive-behavioral intervention (Promoting Resilience in Stress Management [PRISM]) vs usual care. Participants completed surveys at randomization and 3 months. We evaluated feasibility using enrollment and retention rates. We evaluated PRISM's effect on the primary outcome (change in self-reported resilience) and exploratory outcomes using linear regression adjusted for baseline measurement.ResultsWe enrolled 78 patients from March 2023 to April 2024. Patients were randomized to treatment (PRISM; n = 40, age 36 ± 11 years; 50% female; 58% self-reported White race/ethnicity) vs control (n = 38, age 40 ± 12 years; 74% female; 79% White). Over three-quarters of participants randomized to treatment completed PRISM (80%) and follow-up measures. Of controls, 35/38 (92%) completed follow-up measures. Resilience scores at 3 months were higher in the treatment group (n = 30, 30.1 ± 8.1) compared to control (n = 35, 28.7 ± 5.8); this difference was not statistically significant. Mean quality of life at follow-up was significantly higher in the treatment group (79 ± 14 vs 73 ± 14; 95% CI: 0.8-12.7; P = 0.03). Program feedback was positive.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates intervention feasibility with modifications. Although we did not demonstrate efficacy, participants were engaged and provided valuable feedback, suggesting value in ACHD. (Assessing and Promoting Resilience in Patients With Adult Congenital Heart Disease [PRISMACHD]; NCT04738474).

Living with congenital heart disease can negatively impact childhood and adolescent psychosocial development and produce long-term consequences for adult responsibilities and decision-making. As a result, many individuals with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) experience complex social and emotional challenges in addition to physical limitations.1, 2, 3 These issues can be detrimental to quality of life (QOL) and psychological health, contributing to the outstanding palliative care needs identified in this population.4,5 For example, patients with ACHD commonly request assistance coping with symptoms and managing ACHD-related disease uncertainty.6,7

Strengthening patients' psychological resilience is one proposed solution to these concerns. Resilience is the process of harnessing personal resources to sustain physical and emotional well-being in the face of stress.8,9 It can be strengthened through targeted interventions10,11 and learning from life experiences. Resilience has been associated with improved physical and psychosocial outcomes in other chronic illness populations.10,12,13 The possibility of leveraging resilience to improve outcomes in ACHD has not been comprehensively studied.14,15

The aims of this study were to evaluate the feasibility and efficacy (change in self-reported resilience) of a resilience-building intervention in ACHD. Exploratory outcomes included QOL, psychological distress, and perceived competence for health care management.

Methods

Detailed study rationale and methods have been previously published (NCT04738474).16 The primary aim of this single-center, randomized controlled clinical trial was to determine the feasibility of conducting the “Promoting Resilience in Stress Management” (PRISM) intervention with patients with ACHD. We defined feasibility by the proportion of approached patients who: 1) enrolled; and 2) completed PRISM (defined as participating in all sessions) among those randomized to receive it. Our second aim was to evaluate PRISM's efficacy to increase participant resilience, defined as a clinically meaningful difference in mean participant-reported resilience 3 months postrandomization between those randomized to PRISM vs usual care (UC). In exploratory analyses, we evaluated PRISM's effect on QOL, psychological distress, perceived competence for health care management, and comfort with advance care planning (ACP). This study was approved by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board. A dedicated safety monitoring protocol and safety monitoring team were established for the duration of the study.

Participant recruitment and randomization

Eligible patients were adults (≥18 years of age) with moderate or complex ACHD, stages C or D,17 who received medical care at our center, identified through manual screening of ACHD outpatient clinic lists. Patients needed to be able to participate in study activities independently and in English. Patients diagnosed with another life-shortening illness were excluded.

A research assistant invited patients by telephone and email and obtained informed consent. Up to 5 contact attempts were made (telephone/email) unless patients opted out of further contact. Relevant demographic and clinical information for enrolled patients (age, insurance type, ACHD type and severity, prior hospitalizations or interventions, diagnosis of heart failure) was abstracted from the electronic health record. After completion of the baseline survey, patients were randomized 1:1 to PRISM (treatment) or UC (control), using computerized random numbers with variable block sizes. The study leader (J.M.S.) and those assessing outcomes were blinded to randomization.

The “Promoting Resilience in Stress Management” (PRISM) intervention

PRISM is a manualized, one-on-one, cognitive-behavioral intervention (Central Illustration). It teaches 4 core skills: stress management, identifying and reframing negative thoughts, setting goals, and making meaning or sense from challenging situations. PRISM also offers a facilitated family meeting to discuss learnings and access to a smartphone application for skills practice. A fifth, optional, module explores ACP in the context of recipients' chronic illness. PRISM is designed to be completed over 3 months, roughly 1 session every 2 weeks. PRISM coaches are trained on PRISM administration, follow a content-manual to conduct sessions, and participate in formal fidelity assessments.16 PRISM was initially created for adolescents and young adults with cancer and found to improve health-related QOL, hope, and benefit-finding, and to lower distress.10 Successful adaptations to other populations have since been reported, including youth with other chronic or serious illness and their caregivers.18, 19, 20Central IllustrationFeasibility and Efficacy of the PRISM Intervention Among Adult Outpatients With Congenital Heart Disease

Patient and public involvement

In preparation for this trial, we performed a qualitative assessment with patients to guide adaptations to PRISM for use in ACHD.10,16,21 Participants were provided information about PRISM and asked about its acceptability, appropriateness, and suggestions for revisions. We then created ACHD-specific examples, allowed for a peer to attend the family meeting, and chose to include the ACP session, given participants' expressed concerns about future health uncertainty. We also chose to offer PRISM remotely to extend geographic reach and minimize participant travel burden.

Study process

Enrolled patients completed electronic patient-reported outcome measures at baseline and again at approximately 3 months. They were compensated with a $50 gift card following 3-month completion. Patients randomized to the treatment group participated in PRISM sessions, held remotely via videoconference with a single, consistent coach. Sessions were scheduled over approximately 3 months, at least 1 week apart. At the conclusion, PRISM participants were asked to participate in an exit interview conducted by the coach querying program and session flow, utility of skills, and how and when skills were used. Up to 5 contact attempts were made to schedule and complete sessions, unless participants opted out of further contact. Control participants maintained standard clinical follow-up without any interval study-related contact.

Data analysis

Feasibility was assessed using descriptive statistics to summarize: 1) the proportion of patients who enrolled in the study among those reached; and 2) the proportion who completed PRISM among those randomized to treatment. Additional feasibility insights were obtained from notes/memos kept by the research coordinator and PRISM coach and from transcribed exit interview data. Interview data were assessed using a qualitative content analysis. Transcriptions were independently reviewed and analyzed by 2 study team members (J.M.S., A.R.M.). Through recurrent discussion, an inductively developed and iteratively refined coding matrix was used to identify and organize feedback. One analyst (J.M.S.) summarized data for presentation.

To determine the effect of the PRISM intervention on resilience, we evaluated the difference in mean resilience at follow-up between participants randomized to PRISM and participants randomized to UC via a linear regression model, with robust standard errors, adjusted for resilience at baseline. We used similar analyses to evaluate the effect of PRISM on exploratory outcomes: QOL, psychological distress, perceived competence for health care management, and comfort with ACP. For the Euro-QOL 5D3L, we report the indexed value (iEuro-QOL), calculated as described by Shaw et al.28 We treated the comfort with ACP variables as binary (very comfortable vs other responses) and used linear regression with robust standard errors to quantify the treatment effect by the difference in proportions. The use of robust standard errors makes linear regression appropriate for a binary variable. To address missing data, we performed analyses in 2 ways: 1) excluding participants missing the outcome measure at either baseline or follow-up, and 2) with all randomized participants using multiple imputation for missing data. More specifically, we used the mice package in R (reference) to implement multiple imputation with chained equations (based on the fully conditional specification), performed 5 imputations, and combined results using Rubin's rules. Our multiple imputation procedure used predictive mean matching for continuous variables and logistic regression imputation for binary variables and included age, gender, race/ethnicity (binary variable: White or other, given the small sample size in other categories), and all outcome measures (except comfort discussing ACP with family and with doctors) at both baseline and follow-up. For the analysis of comfort discussing ACP with family members, comfort discussing ACP with doctors was excluded from the multiple imputation procedure and vice-versa. There was an imbalance in gender and race/ethnicity between the randomization groups; thus, we also explored the effect of treatment separately for females, males, and participants who self-reported White for race/ethnicity. (This was not done for other race/ethnicity categories due to small sample size.) Since the results in the subgroups were similar to the results among all participants, only results from all participants are reported.

Complete data for 60 participants (30 per group) were calculated to provide 80% power at a 0.05 2-sided significance level to detect a difference in mean change in resilience over 3 months of at least 3 points, assuming a SD of change in resilience of 4.2 points based on a prior study (over 5 months) of people with cancer.16 To account for potential drop-out, we aimed to randomize 86 participants (43 per group).

Results

Participation and feasibility

Between March 2023 and April 2024, we screened 1,479 consecutive patients, of whom 517 were eligible for inclusion (34.9%) (Figure 1). We invited 408 consecutive patients to participate and were able to reach 170 (41.7%); the remainder did not return calls/emails. Of these, 122 provided informed consent (71.8%), and 78 (46%) completed the baseline survey. We stopped enrollment once we obtained follow-up surveys from 60 participants. This decision was also influenced by changes in research team staffing. All team members were blinded to the data when we stopped enrollment. Participants who completed the baseline survey were randomized: 40 to treatment (mean age 36 ± 11 years; 50% female; 58% self-reported White race/ethnicity), 38 to control (mean age 40 ± 12 years; 74% female; 79% self-reported White race/ethnicity). Of the 78 randomized participants, 12 were lost to follow-up: 3/38 from the control group and 8/40 from treatment. All 3 from control and 2 from treatment were male; 2 from control and 6 from treatment were non-Hispanic White; baseline mean CD-RISC scores were 32.3 and 26.1, respectively. No one asked to actively withdraw from the study, and no adverse events were reported. Thirty-two of the 40 participants randomized to treatment completed PRISM (80.0%). Thirty-one of the 32 completed the follow-up survey (1 was partially completed). Thirty-five of the 38 participants randomized to control (92.1%) completed the follow-up survey (4 partially completed). In memos, the PRISM coach noted that participants largely opted out of booster sessions and use of the digital app was low.

Efficacy

Resilience scores at follow-up were higher on average in the treatment group (30.1 ± 8.1, n = 30) compared to control (28.7 ± 5.8, n = 35); although this difference was not statistically significant (Figure 2, Table 2). The difference in means, adjusted for baseline resilience, was 1.2 (95% CI: −1.6, 4.0; P = 0.40). Mean QOL (Euro-VAS) at follow-up was significantly higher in the treatment group (79 ± 14, n = 31) compared to control (73 ± 14, n = 35); difference in means, adjusted for baseline, was 6.8 (95% CI: 0.8-12.7; P = 0.03). Means for other outcomes also tended to be better in the treatment group compared to control, but differences were not statistically significant. PRISM feedback

We interviewed 26 of the 31 participants who completed PRISM (mean length 9:39 minutes, range 3:39-17:31 minutes); the remaining 5 declined. Positive feedback was provided about the frequency and duration of PRISM by 17 participants (65%) and its remote design by 8 (31%) (Table 3). One participant said, “If you were having to go and fight a war, and somebody said, ‘here's a sword,’ it’s like that… I've probably used [these skills previously] in some way or another, but this helps me be able to utilize those types of skills more effectively”—29-year-old man, IIIB ACHD. In memos, the PRISM coach noted that participants actively experiencing cardiac events or who had higher symptom burden seemed more engaged and more often cited PRISM as facilitating their ability to manage ACHD-related stress. Discussion

We describe the results of the first randomized controlled trial of an individual resilience-building cognitive-behavioral intervention conducted in an ACHD population. We observed mixed feasibility: the study was conducted within a realistic timeline, >70% of patients randomized to treatment completed PRISM, and PRISM was viewed by recipients as valuable and desirable. However, we did not meet our enrollment metric of >70% of eligible patients. Additionally, in this group of outpatients with moderate or complex ACHD, stages C or D, PRISM did not result in a significant increase in self-reported resilience scores. However, we did see trends toward improvement in all metrics, including a significant improvement in a QOL measure. Poor psychosocial and mental health in ACHD is a massive problem, and effective mitigation approaches are urgently needed. Studies like ours provide valuable logistical and strategic insights, contributing foundational evidence for rigorous intervention development.

Overall, and perhaps most importantly, participants viewed PRISM positively. Once begun, the majority were engaged and completed the program. Feedback about program structure and content was largely positive. No one reported frank dislike or distress attributed to participation. For successful adoption, a target audience must find an intervention appealing and useful. For this reason, we previously conducted an assessment of interest and made minor adjustments to tailor PRISM to this population's needs21; these were well-received in the current study. Included skills are easy to learn and are broadly applicable to various situations. Although skills are expected to be more or less appealing for certain people, divided feedback about the Finding the Positives module suggests room for additional modification. Given similar feedback in concurrent studies, adjustments to this module in newer versions of PRISM include information about toxic positivity and additional rationale for this skill.

Study feasibility was limited by practical barriers. We have not met our enrollment metric because of constraints related to study team members' ability to recruit in-person from ACHD clinic. Using phone and email recruitment, we were unable to reach over half of the patients invited, despite 5 contact attempts. The ACHD-CARE (Adult Congenital Heart Disease-Coping And REsilience) pilot study of a group psychological intervention similarly determined that in-person recruitment was most effective.15 In future studies, planning additional research coordinator effort to recruit in-person may address this barrier. This could also likely overcome the additional loss of potential enrollees between consent and completion of baseline surveys. Another solution could be to include an electronic consent form at the start of the survey, so that initial engagement only requires a single encounter.

Once engaged in the study, retention for both groups was good. However, we did see differential drop-out between the treatment and control groups (8/40 vs 3/38 lost to follow-up, respectively), suggesting a need to reduce study burden. We may have seen higher retention if we were able to offer sessions on nights and weekends so as not to compete with participants' other daily responsibilities. Importantly, however, participants in the control group did not drop out simply because they were not assigned to intervention, reflecting willingness to participate in research.

It is encouraging that we saw expected improvement trends in mean scores for all outcomes, even though we could not prove efficacy to significantly improve resilience. Two potential reasons stand out. First, our power calculations were based on SD data collected from patients with cancer,10 since the CD-RISC 10 had not previously been used in ACHD. We felt it was important to use the same resilience measure as other PRISM trials to most accurately assess the intervention impact. However, we may not have been able to detect more subtle differences in resilience for our study population. It is also possible that this outpatient population was simply too well to need intervention: even though we excluded less severe disease, perhaps the psychosocial burden in this population was not very high. This is supported by the finding of generally low levels of psychological distress. Indeed, baseline scores from ACHD-CARE were, in general, worse.15 It is also possible that participants had already acquired PRISM's skills in the course of their prior resilience development, as baseline resilience was moderate. A recent pilot study of a similar, though group-delivered, intervention demonstrated a medium effect size on improving resilience. This study by Cousino et al29 was conducted among adolescents with Fontan-palliated congenital heart disease, which is among the most severe types, further supporting the idea that such interventions may be best applied in select populations. It may be worthwhile to specifically examine PRISM in patients with greater disease burden, greater psychological distress, or low baseline resilience.

Study Limitations

Some study limitations should be noted. First, this was a single-site study, serving a patient population which is less racially and ethnically diverse and more highly educated than the general ACHD population.7 Patients receiving care at this program have good insurance coverage and the ability to access ACHD subspecialty care. To increase generalizability, future studies should be multisite and include a more diverse population. Additionally, the decisions for a UC comparator group and to stop enrolling prior to contacting all eligible screened patients were practical decisions, which may limit our ability to differentiate the impact of the intervention. A larger sample would have improved our generalizability and precision. Minimally clinical important difference data are not available for these tools in ACHD. Although we can extrapolate to some extent from clinical judgment and work in other populations,30, 31, 32, 33, 34 truly meaningful effects remain somewhat subjective. Finally, we only evaluated the effect of intervention after 3 months, not longer durations of intervention effect. Effects at 6 months10 and 2 years35 have been demonstrated in other populations. A future study could investigate longer-term effects in ACHD.

Conclusions

This randomized trial of an individual resilience-building cognitive-behavioral intervention conducted in an outpatient ACHD population demonstrated mixed feasibility and did not demonstrate efficacy. However, participants were engaged in the study and provided valuable feedback to guide targeted use of the intervention. Findings suggest clear and actionable modifications for future studies to enhance population selection, recruitment, and effect detection.PerspectivesCOMPETENCY IN PATIENT CARE AND PROCEDURAL SKILLS: Patients with ACHD are interested in interventions that can help them build resilience as a way to manage ACHD-related stress.
