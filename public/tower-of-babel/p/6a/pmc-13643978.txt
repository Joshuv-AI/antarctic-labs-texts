3D CT-DCG reconstruction–informed inferior turbinate dacryocystorhinostomy in post-traumatic chronic dacryocystitis with displaced lacrimal sac
Authors: Jingyun Zhu, Yuhan Feng, Wenhui Dong, Wanying Lin, Minting Chen, Lang Bai, Siqi Xiao, Xiaona Hu, Haocheng Tang, Linjiang Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConventional endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) relies on the middle turbinate as a landmark for localizing the lacrimal sac. However, in post-traumatic lacrimal sac displacement, the middle turbinate approach often fails, and blind exploration may cause excessive surgical trauma.ObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility and effectiveness of a three-dimensional (3D) CT-dacryocystography (CT-DCG)–guided inferior turbinate approach, utilizing the distal nasolacrimal duct as an anatomical anchor for retrograde exploration of the displaced lacrimal sac in traumatic dacryocystitis.MethodsWe conducted a consecutive case series. Introduction

Severe traumatic dacryocystitis, particularly following orbital and naso-orbital fractures, is often complicated by irreversible displacement-related injuries of the lacrimal sac. These patients frequently develop persistent purulent discharge and chronic infection that cannot be effectively managed with conventional reconstructive procedures. In such scenarios—or after failed primary lacrimal drainage reconstruction—dacryocystectomy may be required to relieve symptoms, at the cost of permanent epiphora, representing a major clinical challenge in the management of traumatic dacryocystitis. However, post-traumatic displacement of the lacrimal sac and nasolacrimal duct frequently renders classical intranasal landmarks unreliable, making the standard middle turbinate approach (MTA) for endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) technically unfeasible. This often forces surgeons into blind, high-risk dissection, leading to greater intraoperative trauma or primary anastomosis failure, ultimately necessitating a percutaneous external dacryocystectomy as salvage [1–6].

Computed tomography dacryocystography (CT-DCG) has long been recognized as a valuable preoperative imaging modality for post-traumatic dacryocystitis, providing crucial information on sac localization and surgical pathway planning [7–9]. Nevertheless, conventional dacryocystography and standard two-dimensional CT images have inherent limitations: although they can demonstrate obstruction, they often fail to delineate the precise displacement morphology of the lacrimal sac or establish reproducible anatomical landmarks for surgical entry [9–10]. To address these shortcomings, three-dimensional CT-DCG has emerged as a significant advancement, enabling comprehensive visualization of the lacrimal drainage system and its spatial relationship to adjacent sinonasal structures [11, 12]. Building on these initial models, we employed an enhanced PACS-integrated reconstruction technique, combining window adjustment and signal fusion, to achieve full morphological and multi-axial visualization of the sac. This approach facilitated precise surgical targeting while minimizing unnecessary dissection, thereby reducing intraoperative trauma and optimizing outcomes.

In parallel with advances in imaging, surgical access also requires refinement for complex post-traumatic cases. Conventional endoscopic DCR typically utilizes the MTA, which is reliable in primary cases due to its dependence on preserved anatomical relationships. However, in the setting of sac displacement and scarring, the MTA loses accuracy, and attempts to localize the sac through this pathway often result in excessive bone removal or blind exploration [13, 14]. In contrast, the inferior turbinate approach provides distinct advantages. The nasolacrimal duct consistently drains into the inferior meatus, and this anatomical outlet usually remains identifiable even after trauma. By targeting the distal nasolacrimal duct as a fixed anatomical anchor, surgeons can perform retrograde dissection toward the sac along a shorter and more predictable pathway. Previous studies on retrograde endoscopic DCR have demonstrated the feasibility of reaching the lacrimal sac through a distal-to-proximal route and have provided valuable experience supporting this surgical concept [15, 16]. Building on this anatomical and technical basis, the present case further applied an inferior turbinate-based retrograde strategy to a complex post-traumatic condition, in which severe lacrimal sac displacement made conventional intranasal landmarks unreliable. Furthermore, the presence of a bony “Y-shaped” landmark at the inferior meatus offers reproducible intraoperative orientation, enhancing the surgeon’s ability to navigate within the abstract endoscopic field [14–18]. Pathogenesis of dacryocystitis and endoscopic anatomical landmarks. (a) Idiopathic chronic dacryocystitis is usually caused by obstruction of the distal nasolacrimal duct. The obstructed lacrimal sac lies posterior to the middle turbinate; therefore, conventional endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) via the middle-turbinate approach can accurately localize the sac. However, in post-traumatic dacryocystitis with lacrimal sac displacement, the spatial relationship between the middle turbinate and the sac is altered, making the standard approach prone to failure. (b) When the middle turbinate cannot serve as a reliable endoscopic landmark, the inferior turbinate may be used instead. In trauma-related cases, orbital fractures and adjacent tissue deformities usually involve the upper segment of the nasolacrimal duct, while the lower canaliculus and the nasal opening posterior to the inferior turbinate typically remain in their normal position. Thus, approaching the distal canaliculus from the inferior turbinate provides a more dependable route. (c) Following mucosal removal through the inferior-turbinate approach, a characteristic Y-shaped bony suture can be identified, formed by the vomer, the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid bone, and the septal cartilage. Drilling through this bony junction allows direct exposure of the distal nasolacrimal duct immediately posterior to it

When combined with 3D CT-DCG preoperative reconstruction, the inferior turbinate approach represents a precise and minimally invasive strategy for managing displaced lacrimal sacs in traumatic dacryocystitis. This integration reduces blind exploration, minimizes tissue trauma, and enhances the likelihood of long-term patency. Importantly, it provides a viable alternative to dacryocystectomy in otherwise intractable cases, thereby preserving lacrimal function and expanding the armamentarium for complex lacrimal surgery.

Methods

Patient profiles

Two patients diagnosed with post-traumatic chronic dacryocystitis were recruited from the Department of Ophthalmology, Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University, between January and June 2025. Both patients provided written informed consent before participation. The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, Southern Medical University. Both patients presented with long-standing symptoms of epiphora and purulent discharge and had previously been denied surgical reconstruction at other hospitals due to the complexity of their lacrimal injuries. They refused dacryocystectomy because of the risk of permanent postoperative epiphora and were referred to our center seeking alternative, sac-preserving solutions. After comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation by our oculoplastic and rhinologic teams, we elected to perform endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (DCR) via an inferior turbinate approach guided by three-dimensional computed tomography dacryocystography (3D CT-DCG), aiming to preserve the functional lacrimal sac and restore physiological drainage. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics

Case 1

A 37-year-old female industrial worker presented with left-sided persistent epiphora and mucopurulent reflux for over 10 years following nasal trauma. Clinical examination revealed a distended lacrimal sac with purulent regurgitation on pressure (ROPLAS positive).

Case 2

A 27-year-old male sustained left naso-orbital trauma after a fall during mountaineering and subsequently developed chronic tearing with recurrent mucopurulent discharge for 6 months. Lacrimal sac swelling and tenderness were evident, with purulent reflux on compression.

The clinical severity of dacryocystitis was assessed using a modified Dacryocystitis Clinical Severity Score (DCSS), adapted from Ali and Paulsen’s classification of chronic lacrimal infections [20]. The DCSS incorporated five domains—epiphora severity, lacrimal sac swelling, regurgitation on pressure, radiographic morphology, and disease duration—each graded on a 0–2 scale, yielding a total score of 0–10. Patients were categorized as Grade I (mild, 0–4), Grade II (moderate, 5–8), or Grade III (severe, ≥ 9). Both cases in this study were classified as Grade III, characterized by chronic purulent inflammation with radiologic evidence of sac displacement and bone deformity on CT-DCG [1, 19].

This classification guided the choice of surgical strategy, emphasizing the need for preoperative 3D imaging and a modified inferior turbinate approach to minimize intraoperative trauma and maximize anatomical precision. As below, three-dimensional CT dacryocystography reconstruction provides a more intuitive visualization of the displaced lacrimal sac, offering precise guidance for locating the sac during inferior turbinate–based surgery (Fig. 2).

Inferior turbinate approach guided by 3D CT dacryocystography reconstruction. Under a precisely planned inferior turbinate approach, the key to surgery remains accurate localization of the lacrimal sac. The rationale for adopting the inferior turbinate route is to anatomically trace the distal nasolacrimal duct upward to the lacrimal sac, thereby minimizing surgical trauma. However, conventional CT dacryocystography has inherent limitations—its two-dimensional slices provide only abstract sectional views that cannot precisely delineate the sac’s spatial orientation. By integrating multiplanar adjustment, image fusion, and artifact elimination, three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of CT dacryocystography enables clear visualization of the displaced lacrimal sac and its complete morphology. This technique provides valuable intraoperative guidance for identifying the sac following exposure of the distal nasolacrimal duct

Imaging protocol

All patients underwent computed tomography–dacryocystography (CT-DCG) for preoperative evaluation of lacrimal sac morphology and displacement trajectory. The imaging was performed using a multi-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens SOMATOM Definition AS+, Siemens Healthineers, Germany).

Prior to scanning, non-ionic contrast medium (iodixanol 270 mg I/mL) was gently instilled into the lacrimal drainage system through the inferior punctum using a 24-gauge lacrimal cannula under topical anesthesia. Approximately 0.5–1.0 mL of contrast agent was injected until resistance was felt or reflux was observed, ensuring adequate opacification of the sac and nasolacrimal duct.

Axial and coronal images were reconstructed in bone and soft-tissue algorithms, and subsequently transferred to the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS) for three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction. Using window width and level adjustment, along with signal fusion algorithms, both osseous and contrast-enhanced soft-tissue components were integrated to achieve full-field visualization of the lacrimal sac, nasolacrimal duct, and adjacent sinonasal structures. The resulting 3D CT-DCG model enabled precise assessment of the sac’s displacement pattern (superior, lateral, or posterior) and its relationship with surrounding bones, including the maxilla, lacrimal bone, and inferior turbinate. These reconstructed models were used intraoperatively as navigation references to plan the surgical entry route, determine the optimal osteotomy site, and identify the distal nasolacrimal duct as a reliable anatomical anchor for retrograde dissection toward the displaced sac. As illustrated in Fig. 3, axial, sagittal, and coronal CT images provided multi-planar visualization of the lacrimal drainage pathway. The three-dimensional reconstruction further delineated the spatial position of the contrast-filled lacrimal sac (highlighted by the red box) in relation to the inferior turbinate (red arrow), offering precise guidance for surgical planning and localization.

Surgical technique

Patients were placed in the supine position under combined intravenous–inhalation general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. Routine sterilization and draping were performed. A cotton pledget soaked in 1:10 epinephrine solution was inserted into the ipsilateral middle meatus for 5 min to achieve mucosal vasoconstriction and decongestion, then removed prior to incision.

Under 0° nasal endoscopy, an L-shaped mucosal incision was made on the lateral nasal wall projection of the inferior turbinate using a radiofrequency electrode. Submucosal dissection was carefully performed with a blunt dissector to elevate the mucosa and expose the underlying bone surface of the inferior turbinate bed. A high-speed diamond burr was used to thin and remove the anterior portion of the inferior turbinate bone.

After opening the Y-shaped bony junction, formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid, nasal septal cartilage, and vomer, the distal segment of the nasolacrimal duct was first exposed. Guided by the 3D CT-DCG reconstruction model displayed on the PACS workstation, dissection was then extended retrogradely along the nasolacrimal duct toward the predicted position of the displaced lacrimal sac, following the individualized spatial trajectory determined preoperatively. This method ensured directional exploration with minimal blind dissection and reduced trauma.

Once the displaced lacrimal sac was reached, localization was confirmed via lacrimal irrigation through the lower punctum—showing unobstructed flow without purulent reflux. A small volume of methylene blue was injected to stain the sac mucosa. A lacrimal probe introduced through the canaliculus was used to tent the sac wall, and a 3.0 mm keratome created a vertical incision in the sac wall, which was then extended posteriorly to form a reverse C-shaped mucosal flap. Visualization of the blue-stained mucosa confirmed entry into the sac lumen.

A soft epidural catheter was inserted through the lower canaliculus into the nasal cavity to maintain the newly created drainage tract. The C-shaped sac flap was rotated posteriorly and sutured to the corresponding nasal mucosa to establish a mucosa-to-mucosa anastomosis. The upper one-third of the inferior turbinate mucosa was trimmed to cover the exposed bony surface, while the remaining two-thirds were repositioned to cover the turbinate bed and secured with one interrupted absorbable suture.

Meticulous hemostasis was ensured, and the cavity was irrigated with sterile saline. At the end of surgery, tobramycin–dexamethasone ophthalmic ointment was instilled into both the ipsilateral nasal cavity and conjunctival sac. The nasal cavity was lightly packed with gelatin sponge fragments soaked in dexamethasone suspension, followed by a nasal expansion sponge placed in the common meatus for support. Postoperative care

Postoperative management included tranexamic acid (intravenous, 3 days) and Yunnan Baiyao (oral, 7 days) for hemostasis, oxymetazoline nasal spray (0.05%, twice daily for 1 week) for mucosal decongestion, and topical levofloxacin drops combined with oral cefixime (1 week) for infection prophylaxis.

Patients were followed up at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months postoperatively. Clinical evaluation included symptom improvement, ROPLAS test, lacrimal irrigation patency, and Dacryocystitis Clinical Severity Score (DCSS) grading to monitor functional restoration and recurrence.

Outcome evaluation

Postoperative outcomes were systematically evaluated through both objective lacrimal drainage assessments and subjective symptom scoring at 2 weeks, 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months after surgery.

Objective evaluation

The primary endpoint was anatomical and functional patency of the lacrimal drainage system. Functional patency was assessed using the Regurgitation on Pressure over the Lacrimal Sac (ROPLAS) test and lacrimal irrigation via the lower punctum.

Endoscopic evaluation was performed at each follow-up visit to inspect the nasolacrimal anastomotic site, assess mucosal healing, and identify any granulation, synechia, or restenosis. The presence of mucosal epithelial continuity across the osteotomy site and absence of fibrosis were considered indicators of successful healing.

Subjective evaluation

Patients’ symptoms were scored using a modified Munk epiphora grading scale (0–4) and the Dacryocystitis Clinical Severity Score (DCSS) [1, 19], which accounts for local inflammation, discharge, tenderness, and swelling. Symptom remission was defined as ≥ 2-grade improvement in both scales, with complete remission indicating absence of discharge and epiphora during daily activities.

Both patients achieved complete anatomical and functional success at the first attempt, with restoration of tear drainage confirmed endoscopically and by irrigation. There were no intraoperative or postoperative complications, such as cerebrospinal fluid leakage, orbital injury, or significant bleeding. During 6-month follow-up, both remained symptom-free, with stable mucosal epithelization and no recurrence of obstruction or infection.

Postoperative result

Two patients (aged 37 and 27 years) with chronic dacryocystitis were included in this report. Both were referred from outside hospitals after multiple unsuccessful attempts at surgical planning. Due to complex anatomical variations and severe inflammatory changes, external dacryocystectomy had been proposed elsewhere; however, both patients declined due to concerns about permanent epiphora and cosmetic outcomes. They sought alternative treatment at our department, where detailed 3D CT-dacryocystography (3D CT-DCG) evaluation was performed, revealing displacement of the lacrimal sac in both cases. Both patients successfully underwent endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy via the inferior turbinate approach without intraoperative or postoperative complications. The surgical field was clear in all cases, and the displaced lacrimal sacs were accurately localized according to the preoperative 3D reconstruction guidance.

At 1 month postoperatively, nasal endoscopic examination revealed a well-epithelialized and patent rhinostomy site with no granulation or synechiae formation (Fig. 5). No evidence of mucopurulent reflux or persistent swelling was observed. At 3 and 6 months, follow-up evaluations were conducted through outpatient interviews and lacrimal irrigation tests. Both patients reported complete resolution of epiphora and mucopurulent discharge. Irrigation confirmed unobstructed flow into the nasal cavity without reflux, indicating maintained anatomical patency. Throughout the 6-month follow-up, no recurrence of dacryocystitis or restenosis was noted, and no patients required revision surgery (Table 2).

Discussion

Chronic dacryocystitis (CDC) remains a common but challenging lacrimal drainage disorder, especially in cases complicated by long-standing inflammation or post-traumatic deformity. Chronic infection may lead to extensive fibrosis, osteitic changes, and anatomical distortion of the lacrimal sac and duct, thereby obscuring normal bony and mucosal landmarks and making conventional endoscopic dacryocystorhinostomy (En-DCR) technically demanding or even infeasible [1, 2]. External dacryocystorhinostomy is also a widely used and effective approach for traumatic nasolacrimal duct obstruction and dacryocystitis, with the advantages of direct sac exposure and relatively familiar surgical anatomy. However, in patients with severe post-traumatic lacrimal sac displacement, the spatial relationship between the lacrimal sac and nasal mucosa may be markedly altered. The displaced lacrimal sac flap and the corresponding nasal mucosal flap may be separated by a considerable distance, which can make external DCR technically difficult and potentially more traumatic.

In this context, three-dimensional computed tomographic dacryocystography (3D CT-DCG) provides a precise and practical method for delineating complex anatomical variations of the lacrimal drainage pathway and guiding individualized surgical planning. Previous studies by Alicandri-Ciufelli and colleagues have introduced retrograde endoscopic DCR as a practical distal-to-proximal route to the lacrimal sac and have compared its clinical outcomes with those of classical endoscopic DCR [15, 16]. These studies provide important anatomical and procedural experience for using the nasolacrimal duct as a guide to the lacrimal sac. In the present cases, preoperative 3D CT-DCG demonstrated marked lacrimal sac displacement and distortion of the conventional surgical corridor. Therefore, by integrating 3D CT-DCG reconstruction with an inferior turbinate-based retrograde route, we were able to define the displaced lacrimal anatomy preoperatively and use the distal nasolacrimal duct as a stable intraoperative anchor. This combined strategy may help reduce blind exploration, limit surgical trauma, and preserve lacrimal drainage function in selected complex traumatic cases where conventional external or middle turbinate-based approaches are limited by severe anatomical displacement.

In both patients, 3D CT-DCG revealed displacement of the lacrimal sac—a rare finding in idiopathic CDC but a recognized feature of chronic inflammatory remodeling. According to the Dacryocystitis Clinical Severity Score (DCSS) system, these cases were classified as grade 3–4, representing severe chronic disease with complete nasolacrimal duct obstruction and recurrent mucopurulent discharge [20]. Such high-grade cases are known to have suboptimal outcomes following standard middle meatal En-DCR, primarily due to sac displacement and the loss of reliable anatomical references. The inferior turbinate approach adopted here was specifically designed to address these issues by enabling access to the true pathological sac position under 3D-DCG guidance.

The inferior turbinate route provides several distinct advantages. It allows direct identification of the Y-shaped bony confluence formed by the perpendicular plate of the ethmoid, vomer, and nasal septal cartilage, from which the inferior segment of the nasolacrimal duct can be exposed first. This serves as a reliable intraoperative landmark to trace the displaced sac in a retrograde fashion, thereby minimizing unnecessary osteotomy and mucosal trauma [14–18]. The integration of 3D-DCG images into the intraoperative navigation process provides real-time anatomical correlation, enabling the surgeon to align dissection with the actual displacement vector of the sac. This approach enhances surgical precision, improves exposure of the true diseased cavity, and likely contributes to the favorable outcomes observed in both cases—complete symptom resolution and patent lacrimal drainage confirmed at 6 months postoperatively.

However, certain limitations and potential disadvantages should be acknowledged. The inferior turbinate approach requires a higher level of endoscopic dexterity due to the limited working space and proximity to vital structures such as the inferior meatus and nasal floor. The creation of a lower bony ostium may theoretically predispose to postoperative tear sump formation, although this was not observed in our cases. Furthermore, 3D CT-DCG demands additional radiation exposure and specialized imaging reconstruction software, which may limit its routine application in resource-limited settings. The absence of long-term data and small sample size are additional limitations that preclude broad generalization of the current findings.

Despite these challenges, the present report highlights the significant potential of 3D CT-DCG–guided inferior turbinate DCR as a personalized and anatomy-based surgical strategy for anatomically complex CDC. By integrating high-resolution imaging with targeted endoscopic intervention, this technique preserves the functional lacrimal sac, minimizes mucosal injury, and achieves reliable drainage reconstruction in cases where standard En-DCR may fail. Future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up are warranted to further validate this method, refine surgical indications, and establish standardized imaging–surgical integration protocols. The combination of 3D imaging technology with minimally invasive nasal approaches represents an important step toward precision lacrimal sac surgery.
