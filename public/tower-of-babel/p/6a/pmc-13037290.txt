Psychometric validation of the Bulgarian Ankylosing Spondylitis Quality of Life (ASQoL) questionnaire: linking quality of life with disease activity in axial spondyloarthritis
Authors: Daliya Tsvetanova Pencheva, Ellie Johnstone, Mariusz T. Grzeda, Vladimira Boyadzhieva, Nikolay Stoilov, Mariana Ivanova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAssessing quality of life in patients with axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is essential for capturing the full burden of the disease beyond clinical and imaging findings. Quality of life measures support clinicians in identifying unmet patient needs and informing treatment decisions. The aim of this study was to develop and validate the first Bulgarian version of the Ankylosing Spondylitis Quality of Life (ASQoL) questionnaire.MethodsThe development of the Bulgarian version of the ASQoL followed a three-stage process: translation, field testing, and psychometric evaluation. Following translation and field testing of the ASQoL, the psychometric evaluation involved administering the ASQoL to a random sample of axSpA patients on two occasions, 10–20 days apart, to assess its reliability and validity. Reliability was examined using internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha) and test–retest reliability (Spearman’s rank correlation). Convergent validity was evaluated using the Short Form Health Survey Version 2 (SF-36v2) and the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score with C-reactive protein (ASDAS-CRP). Known-groups validity was assessed based on self-perceived general health and self-perceived disease activity.ResultsThe Bulgarian version of the ASQoL demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.884 at time 1 and 0.882 at time 2. Test-retest reliability was excellent (Spearman’s ρ = 0.98, p < 0.001), indicating strong temporal stability. Convergent validity was supported by moderate correlations between ASQoL scores and relevant SF-36 domains, with the strongest associations observed for Physical Functioning, General Health, and Role Limitations. ASQoL scores showed a strong positive correlation with ASDAS-CRP (Spearman’s ρ = 0.70, p < 0.001), indicating close alignment between patient-reported quality of life and clinical disease activity. Known-groups validity was demonstrated by the ASQoL’s ability to distinguish between patient subgroups based on self-perceived general health and self-perceived disease activity, with statistically significant differences in ASQoL scores across general health categories.ConclusionsThe Bulgarian ASQoL demonstrated strong psychometric properties, confirming its reliability and validity in accurately capturing the impact of axSpA on patients’ quality of life.Trial registrationClinical trial number: not applicable.

Introduction

Axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA) is a chronic, progressive rheumatic disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton but may also involve peripheral musculoskeletal structures and is frequently associated with extra-musculoskeletal manifestations such as uveitis, inflammatory bowel disease, and psoriasis. The disease is marked by two main pathological processes – chronic inflammation and abnormal new bone formation [1, 2]. It is a potentially disabling condition with an unpredictable disease course, although modern advances in therapeutic interventions have significantly altered symptom severity, the progression of structural changes, and ultimately the disease phenotype. Otherwise, uncontrolled inflammation and disease activity can lead to progressive structural damage and associated loss of function, particularly in the advanced stage known as radiographic axial spondyloarthritis (r-axSpA) - a condition more commonly referred to until recently as ankylosing spondylitis (AS) [3]. As a result of these deleterious effects, many patients report work-related difficulties and a reduced ability to manage daily life independently, often requiring assistance with everyday activities. The condition also impacts other areas of life beyond physical symptoms, influencing mood, motivation, desires, expectations, and social functioning - and thereby affecting health-related quality of life. Since the disease typically begins in late adolescence or early adulthood, it can leave a lasting impact throughout much of a patient’s life [4–6]. Therefore, assessing quality of life in axSpA patients is of utmost importance.

The Ankylosing Spondylitis Quality of Life (ASQoL) scale is a widely used and valuable disease-specific tool for assessing the impact of axSpA on a patient’s quality of life. It is typically used alongside other assessments to evaluate how the condition affects various aspects of life, including physical, emotional, and social well-being. The ASQoL is an 18-item questionnaire that relies on the patient’s own perspective to assess how the disease impacts their daily life. Originally developed in UK English and Dutch, it has since been adapted into multiple languages for broader use [7].

Subsequently, country-specific (language) versions of the ASQoL questionnaire have been developed and validated. In total, the ASQoL has been adapted into at least 37 languages, covering Europe, the Americas, Asia, the Middle East, Australasia, and Africa. These strict translation and validation processes ensure that the questionnaire can be used effectively across diverse linguistic and cultural contexts whilst maintaining conceptual equivalence across all languages. Most recently, in 2025, a Serbian version of the ASQoL was published using the updated nomenclature r-axSpA, reflecting current classification terminology [8]. The ASQoL has also been translated and validated into Bulgarian as part of what is often referred to as the “third wave” of international adaptations; however, it was not tested for scaling properties, reliability, or internal consistency. While this supports its use in clinical practice, it does not report key psychometric properties (such as Cronbach’s α or test–retest reliability). For this reason, the aim of this study was to assess and thereby validate the psychometric properties of the linguistically adapted version of the ASQoL for use in the Bulgarian patient population.

Methodology

The development of a new Bulgarian language version of the ASQoL questionnaire involved three main stages: translation, field testing, and psychometric evaluation (Fig. 1). Two separate study populations were involved: the first participated in the translation process and cognitive debriefing interviews (CDIs) to ensure cultural and linguistic appropriateness, while the second, independent population was recruited for the psychometric evaluation, including assessments of reliability, internal consistency, and construct validity. For the psychometric evaluation stage, permission to use the ASQoL was obtained from Galen Research Ltd, the copyright holder of the ASQoL and all its language versions. The study protocol was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee at University Hospital “St. Ivan Rilski” (approval reference number 01/11.02.2025). Patients with r-axSpA were selected randomly as they attended their routine appointments at the Rheumatology Clinic of the University Hospital “St. Ivan Rilski,” between November 2024 and February 2025. Inclusion criteria required participants to be at least 18 years old and to have a confirmed diagnosis of axSpA by a rheumatologist; the modified New York criteria and the axSpA classification criteria were applied to verify eligibility for the study [9, 10]. Patients with cognitive impairment or severe comorbidities preventing questionnaire completion were excluded. Patients were on stable pharmacological treatment at inclusion, which was maintained between assessments to ensure unbiased test–retest reliability analysis.Written informed consent in Bulgarian was obtained from all participants.

Translation

The translation of the UK English version of the ASQoL into Bulgarian was originally carried out in 2011. The dual panel translation methodology recommended by Hunt et al. was employed [11]. This methodology emphasises the importance of achieving conceptual equivalence of translated items to the original. The process consisted of two main stages: A bilingual panel consisting of a group of individuals whose first language was Bulgarian and who were fluent in English. The purpose of this panel was to suggest translations for the instructions, items and response categories. This was followed by a lay panel consisting of monolingual Bulgarian-speaking participants with average to low educational levels to ensure that the final wording of the items was appropriate and clearly understood by patients across all levels of education.

Cognitive debriefing interviews

Semi-structured cognitive debriefing interviews (CDIs) were conducted in the prior second step (2011) with a sample of r-axSpA patients to test the face and content validity of the new translation of the ASQoL. The purpose of these interviews was to assess the applicability, relevance, and comprehensiveness of the instrument from the patient perspective. Each interview was conducted one-to-one and in person, the participant were asked to complete the questionnaire in the presence of an interviewer who noted hesitation or difficulties with over particular items. Using an interview schedule participants were then requested to comment on the questionnaire items, instructions and response format. Specifically, respondents were inquired whether they consider the items relevant, applicable and comprehensible, and if they believed that any important aspects of their experience of AS had been omitted. Also, included were questions from the earlier stages regarding any translation issues that required further exploration with patients. Where uncertainty remained regarding the choice of specific wording during translation, participants were presented with two or more alternative phrasings and asked to select the one they felt best captured the intended meaning.

Psychometric validation

Study design

Herein, a psychometric validation study was conducted to establish the reliability and validity of the newly adapted Bulgarian version of the ASQoL. Patients diagnosed with r-axSpA were invited to participate between November 2024 and February 2025. The questionnaire was administered on two separate occasions, with a 10–20 day interval between administrations [12]. At the first administration, participants also completed the SF-36v2 (The Short Form Health Survey Version 2) [13], a generic measure of health status. In addition, the Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score using C-reactive protein (ASDAS-CRP) [14], a disease-specific measure of activity, was recorded for each participant at Time 1. Participants were also asked self-reported questions regarding general health, disease severity, and the presence of flares: “How would you describe your general health at the moment?”, “In general, how severe would you say your AS is?”, and “Are you currently having a flare-up of your illness?”. All responses were entered into an electronic database, and participants were assigned unique ID numbers to ensure anonymity. Statistical analyses to evaluate the psychometric properties of the Bulgarian ASQoL were performed using SPSS (version 29).

Assessment tools

SF-36v2 comprises 36 items across eight domains: Physical Functioning, Role Physical (limitations due to physical health problems), Bodily Pain, General Health, Vitality, Social Functioning, Role Emotional (limitations due to emotional health problems), and Mental Health. Each item is scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better health-related quality of life [13]. ASDAS-CRP is a validated composite index that combines patient-reported outcomes (back pain, duration of morning stiffness, patient global assessment, and peripheral pain/swelling) with serum CRP to quantify disease activity in axSpA. ASDAS-CRP scores were interpreted using established cut-offs: <1.3 for inactive disease, 1.3–<2.1 for low disease activity, 2.1–≤3.5 for high disease activity, and > 3.5 for very high disease activity [14].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to explore the distributional properties of the ASQoL scores, including the mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (IQR). Floor and ceiling effects, defined as the percentage of participants scoring the minimum and maximum possible scores, respectively, were also reported. Effects were considered present if more than 15% of patients achieved the lowest (floor effect) or highest (ceiling effect) possible score. ASQoL scores were compared by demographic factors: gender, age group (above versus below the sample median), employment status, and marital status.

Internal consistency

Internal consistency of the ASQoL was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient, which measures the extent to which items within a scale are inter-related. An alpha value below 0.70 suggests inadequate internal consistency [15]. A coefficient of ≥ 0.70 is generally considered acceptable for group-level comparisons.

Test-retest reliability

Test-retest reliability, an indicator of the scale’s reproducibility over time when no clinical change has occurred, was evaluated using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient between ASQoL scores obtained at the two time points. A correlation coefficient of at least 0.85 was considered indicative of high reliability and low random measurement error [16].

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was assessed by examining the associations between ASQoL scores and scores on two comparator instruments: the SF-36v2 and the ASDAS-CRP. ASQoL scores were hypothesised to correlate negatively with SF-36 scores (as higher SF-36 values indicate better health status), and positively with ASDAS-CRP scores (as higher values indicate greater disease activity). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients were calculated for these comparisons, with correlations ≥ 0.30 considered moderate and ≥ 0.50 considered strong.

Known group validity

Known group validity was tested by comparing ASQoL scores across groups differing in patient-perceived general health (poor/fair/good/very good) and disease severity (mild/moderate/quite severe/very severe). These comparisons aimed to determine whether the ASQoL could discriminate between patients with different levels of self-reported health and disease impact. Non-parametric tests for independent samples were used: the Mann-Whitney U test for comparisons between two groups and the Kruskal-Wallis one-way analysis of variance for comparisons involving three or more groups.

Results

Translation

The ASQoL was originally developed from a series of interviews with patients with r-axSpA [17]. The dual-panel translation methodology, recommended for needs-based instruments, was employed in the Bulgarian adaptation performed in 2011. This approach involves two independent translation panels: a bilingual panel and a lay panel. The bilingual panel consisted of six individuals (three male and three female), aged 30 to 54 years. All were fluent in both English and Bulgarian, with Bulgarian as their native language. The panel discussed each item to reach consensus on the most appropriate translation. For several items—Item 1 (“My condition limits the places I can go”), Item 5 (“It’s impossible to sleep”), Item 6 (“I’m unable to join in activities with my friends or family”), Item 7 (“I am tired all the time”), and Item 14 (“The pain is always there”)—two translation options were provided for further review by the lay panel. The lay panel, conducted in Bulgaria, included six individuals with average to below-average educational levels, representative of the target population. Their ages ranged from 28 to 51 years. The panel reached consensus on all items that had multiple translation options. They agreed with the bilingual panel’s choices for most items, except for Item 8 (“I have to keep stopping what I’m doing to rest”) and Item 11 (“I am unable to do jobs around the house”), for which they proposed alternative versions they considered more natural and therefore more acceptable in the Bulgarian context.

Cognitive debriefing interviews

Therein, cognitive debriefing interviews (CDIs) were conducted with 10 patients diagnosed with r-axSpA, aged between 28 and 47 years. Over half of the participants were in full-time employment. All patients clearly understood the purpose of the interviews and the instructions for completing the questionnaire. The ASQoL was completed in an average of 3 min (SD = 0.99), with completion times ranging from 2 to 5 min. Most of the adapted ASQoL items were well-received and considered relevant by participants. However, three items (Item 4, 6, and 11) were revised based on participant feedback during the CDIs. Overall, the questionnaire was perceived as clear and easy to understand. No item was reported as being confusing or awkwardly worded. Most interviewees felt the items accurately reflected their experiences with r-axSpA and found them relatable, none were considered inappropriate or unacceptable. Additionally, the majority believed that the questionnaire adequately covered all important aspects of their condition.

Psychometric validation

Fifty-five patients with r-axSpA participated in the present validation study. Patient ages ranged from 28 to 69 years, and the majority of the sample were male. Over half of the participants rated their general health as “good” and perceived their disease severity as “moderate.” Demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Patients were receiving biologic or targeted synthetic disease modifying anti–rheumatic drugs, with the majority (60%) on anti–tumour necrosis factor alpha (anti-TNFα) treatment, followed by Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKi; 23.6%) and anti–interleukin-17 A (anti-IL-17 A) agents (16.4%).

Descriptive statistics for the questionnaires at both time points are presented in Table 2. High ceiling effects (i.e., a large number of patients scoring the maximum) were observed in several SF-36 subscales, while floor effects (i.e., many patients scoring the minimum) were noted particularly in the Physical Role Limitations (RP), Social Functioning (SF), and Emotional Role Limitations (RE) subscales, suggesting that these health status measures may not be optimally targeted for patients with r-axSpA (Fig. 2). Additionally, several participants scored the minimum on the ASQoL questionnaire possibly due to the sample being quite mild.

Internal consistency and test-retest reliability

Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the ASQoL was 0.884 at time 1 and 0.882 at time 2, indicating high internal consistency. The ASQoL demonstrated excellent test-retest reliability, with a correlation coefficient of 0.98 (p < 0.001). This high reliability suggests that the measure produces minimal random measurement error.

Convergent validity

Table 2 also presents Spearman’s rank-order correlation coefficients between ASQoL scores and SF-36 domain scores, which serve as various markers of disease activity. ASQoL scores demonstrated moderate negative correlations with the Physical Functioning (-0.74), General Health (-0.66), and Role Limitations (-0.63) domains of the SF-36. These findings suggest that impairments in these health domains are significantly associated with reduced quality of life in patients with r-axSpA. Additionally, Fig. 3 illustrates a strong positive association between ASQoL and ASDAS-CRP, with a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient of 0.70 (p < 0.001). This result supports the validity of ASQoL as a patient-reported outcome measure that reflects disease activity in individuals with r-axSpA.

Association with demographic factors

Table 3 presents ASQoL scores stratified by age (below vs. above the median), gender, marital status, and employment status. Mann–Whitney U tests revealed no significant differences in ASQoL scores between males and females. However, a statistically significant difference was observed between younger and older participants, with older individuals reporting higher ASQoL scores, indicating poorer quality of life. For demographic variables with more than two categories (i.e., marital and employment status), Kruskal–Wallis one-way analysis of variance tests were applied. These analyses revealed no significant differences in ASQoL scores across marital or employment groups.

Known group validity

Kruskal–Wallis tests revealed statistically significant differences in ASQoL scores across categories of self-perceived general health (p < 0.05). For this analysis, responses indicating “Poor” and “Fair” general health were combined into a single category to ensure adequate groups sizes. In contrast, Mann-Whitney U tests showed no statistically significant differences in ASQoL scores based on self-perceived disease severity (Fig. 4A and B).

Discussion

Overall functioning and health represent essential outcome measures included in the Assessment of SpondyloArthritis international Society/Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (ASAS-OMERACT) core domain set for axSpA and should be systematically assessed in all clinical trials and longitudinal observational studies evaluating treatments for patients diagnosed with the disease [18]. The ASQoL questionnaire is a disease-specific instrument developed to assess the impact of AS on patients’ quality of life. Compared to generic instruments such as the SF-36 or EQ-5D [19], which are applicable across diverse populations and health conditions, the ASQoL offers advantages in terms of sensitivity, clinical relevance, and utility [20–22]. This article presents the results of the evaluation of the psychometric properties of the previously translated and culturally adapted Bulgarian version of the ASQoL questionnaire.

The self-administered survey indicated that the majority of our patient population experienced minimal to moderate impact from r-axSpA. Descriptive statistics showed that approximately half of the participants reported a very low impact of the disease on quality of life and scored between 0 and 7–8 (time 1 and 2), which is still relatively low on the ASQoL scale (which ranges from 0 to 18). Over a quarter to one third of participants at both time points scored the minimum (0), indicating no impact of r-axSpA on their quality of life, apparently due to well-controlled disease. Only 1.8–5.5% scored the maximum, which indicates that very few participants were experiencing the worst possible quality of life according to this scale. This is a generally favourable outcome which could reflect effective disease management associated with mild disease activity. While most individuals are doing relatively well, some participants still report substantial impairment, with scores approaching the upper limit (16 out of 18). This indicates that despite overall good outcomes, a subset of patients may still require reconsidering of the targeted interventions.

Our results demonstrate strong internal consistency for the Bulgarian ASQoL, with Cronbach’s α = 0.884 at Time 1 and α = 0.882 at Time 2, falling in the “good” to borderline “excellent” range. These findings are consistent with the original validation studies, in which the ASQoL demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.89 in the UK cohort and α = 0.91 in the Netherlands [7]. The high internal consistency supports the psychometric soundness of the Bulgarian version of the ASQoL and indicates that the items in this adaptation are highly interrelated and the scale is consistently measuring a single underlying construct, i.e. individual items on the scale contribute meaningfully to the total score. The similarity of alpha values across time suggests that the internal consistency of the scale is stable and reliable over time. Its use in clinical trials or longitudinal studies is well-supported by these results.

The calculated test-retest correlation coefficient for the Bulgarian version of the ASQoL is exceptionally high and statistically significant, indicating very strong agreement between the two administrations of this instrument. This provides strong evidence of reliability over time and its usefulness for longitudinal assessments, with a minimal random measurement error. Similar results have been reported in the Greek validation study (r = 0.98) [23]. In this regard, we could conclude that Bulgarian adaptation of the ASQoL demonstrates excellent temporal stability, confirming that it is a highly reliable measure of quality of life in Bulgarian individuals with r-axSpA and strengthens its value for both clinical practice and research, particularly in tracking patient outcomes over time.

The results from the evaluation of convergent validity highlight important aspects of the accuracy and utility of the translated version of the ASQoL questionnaire in assessing the quality of life and disease activity in patients suffering from r-axSpA. In the original study, as well as in recent validations from comparable geographic regions, the Nottingham Health Profile (NHP) was used as a comparator, whereas we used the SF-36v2, a generic health questionnaire already validated in Bulgarian ([7–8, 23]). The ASQoL scores showed moderate correlations with the Physical Functioning, General Health, and Health Role Limitations domains of the SF-36, a well-established generic quality of life measure [13, 24]. These correlations suggest that this language-specific version captures key dimensions of quality of life that are significantly impacted in Bulgarian r-axSpA patients. Specifically, limitations in physical functioning and general health perceptions are central concerns for these patients, which the ASQoL appears to reflect. Moderate correlations support construct validity - the Bulgarian version of the ASQoL is measuring what it is intended to measure. However, the correlations are not so high as to indicate redundancy with the SF-36, which is beneficial, as the ASQoL is disease-specific and should provide unique insights. The strong association with the ASDAS-CRP, a preferred version for assessing disease activity in r-axSpA [14, 25], demonstrates that quality of life as measured by the adapted for the Bulgarian population questionnaire is strongly influenced by disease activity, suggesting that as objective disease activity increases (measured via ASDAS-CRP), patients report poorer quality of life. These findings support that the translated version is a reliable, patient-centred tool for assessing the impact of r-axSpA on daily life, and it could be used both in clinical practice and research to track disease progression and treatment response. In general, it performs well in capturing key aspects of health-related quality of life in r-axSpA patients. Its moderate correlation with SF-36 subdomains and strong correlation with ASDAS-CRP make it a valuable tool for holistic assessment of patients bridging subjective quality of life with objective disease activity.

The findings regarding association of this language-specific version of the ASQoL offer important insights into how demographic variables relate, or do not relate, to quality of life in patients with r-axSpA. The lack of significant differences in ASQoL scores between males and females suggests that gender does not significantly influence how patients perceive their quality of life in the context of r-axSpA. This finding supports the gender-neutral validity of the new Bulgarian adaptation of the ASQoL; it appears to function consistently across male and female respondents. It may also reflect that both genders experience a similar disease burden in terms of its impact on quality of life, despite possible differences in clinical presentation or progression. The presence of systematic differences in ASQoL scores (Bulgarian version) between older and younger individuals indicates that age does play a role in shaping quality of life in our patient group. Older patients may report poorer quality of life due to longer disease duration, accumulated structural damage, or comorbidities. Conversely, younger patients might experience a greater psychological or social impact due to disease onset during more active life stages (e.g., career building, family formation) though this would generally worsen QoL scores rather than improve them. The lack of significant variation in ASQoL scores across different employment and marital statuses suggests these social factors may not strongly influence disease-related quality of life in this sample. ASQoL appears to primarily reflect health-related impairments rather than being heavily influenced by external social roles or statuses. This also suggests a degree of robustness and specificity in the ASQoL - it captures disease impact more than general life circumstances. These demographic analyses contribute to the known-groups validity of the ASQoL and allow us to assume that it is consistent across gender, employment, and marital status, indicating the tool is not biased by these variables, and is broadly applicable and equitable across key demographic groups. Also, the questionnaire is sensitive to age, suggesting it can detect meaningful differences when they are truly present.

The statistically significant differences in ASQoL scores across levels of perceived general health (e.g., “Poor/Fair” vs. “Good” and “Very Good”) suggest that how patients view their overall health status is strongly associated with their quality of life. Combining “Poor” and “Fair” into one group likely increased statistical power by creating a clearer contrast with better health categories, helping to detect significant differences. The lack of statistically significant differences in ASQoL scores across levels of self-rated disease severity (e.g., mild/moderate, quite severe/very severe) suggests that subjective ratings of disease severity may not correspond directly to how patients experience quality of life. Both results highlight a key distinction: perceived general health is a broader, integrative construct that strongly relates to quality of life; self-rated disease severity is more narrow and potentially less reliable or consistent across individuals. This contrast supports the construct validity of the ASQoL as it aligns with meaningful health constructs like general health and may be less influenced by subjective or inconsistently defined self-categorization like “disease severity.” This suggests that ASQoL is more attuned to how patients view their overall health rather than their subjective categorization of r-axSpA severity and validates the ASQoL as a sensitive tool for capturing meaningful quality of life differences.

Limitations

Although convergent validity with patient-reported outcomes and ASDAS-CRP was demonstrated, analyses using established axSpA measures, such as the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) and the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI), were not performed, and should be considered in future studies [26]. Another limitation of our study is that our sample consisted only of patients with r-axSpA and did not include those with nr-axSpA. Considering that r-axSpA and non-radiographic axSpA (nr-axSpA) are subsets of one and the same disease entity and share comparable disease characteristics and disease burden [27, 28] we strongly believe that the Bulgarian ASQoL is also applicable to patients with the early non-radiographic stage of this disease. Our claim is by analogy to the fact that originally developed for patients with r-axSpA, the primary version of the ASQoL has subsequently been confirmed to be relevant also in nr-axSpA [29, 30]. Finally, the responsiveness of the Bulgarian ASQoL could not be assessed, as this would require a study involving an effective intervention. Nevertheless, given its excellent psychometric properties, the ASQoL would be expected to be capable of detecting meaningful changes in QoL over time.

Conclusion

The new Bulgarian adaptation of the ASQoL demonstrated good psychometric properties. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients were high at both time points, indicating excellent internal consistency. Test–retest reliability was also strong, as evidenced by a very high Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Moderately high correlations were observed between ASQoL scores and relevant sections of the SF-36, while a strong correlation with ASDAS-CRP provided further evidence of convergent validity. The ASQoL effectively distinguished between groups based on perceived general health, supporting its known-groups validity. However, there was insufficient evidence to show it could differentiate between groups based on self-rated disease severity, possibly due to the limited sample size. Overall, the Bulgarian version of the ASQoL is a valid and reliable tool, and its use is recommended in routine clinical practice, research studies, and clinical trials in Bulgaria.
