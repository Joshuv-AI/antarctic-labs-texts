ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between risk Perception of Diabetes mellitus complications and lifestyle modification is an important issue in diabetes prevention and control. Also, an individual's ability to practice lifestyle change by adhering to medication, exercise, and dietary requirements has reduced complications and deaths. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a group of metabolic diseases characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from defects in insulin secretion, insulin action, or both, and chronic hyperglycemia of diabetes is associated with long-term damage, dysfunction, and failure of different organs [1]. Diabetes mellitus, more simply called diabetes, is a serious, long-term (or “chronic”) condition that occurs when raised levels of blood glucose [2].

Diabetes is an important public health problem, one of four priority non-communicable diseases (NCDs) targeted for action by world leaders. Both the number of cases and the prevalence of diabetes have been steadily increasing over the past few decades [3]. Diabetes is one of the major health and development challenges of the twenty-first century, yet diabetes continues to kill and disable millions of people globally every year, over four million people die from diabetes, and tens of millions more suffer disabling and life-threatening complications, if nothing is done, by 2030 this number is expected to rise to 552 million with diabetes and an additional 398 million people at high risk [4].

The new classification by WHO,2019, and the American Diabetes Association (ADA),2021, is Type 1 diabetes, Type 2 DM (T2DM), and Hybrid forms of diabetes, among which T2DM accounts for between 90 and 95% of diabetes, with the highest proportions in low- and middle-income countries [5, 6]. T2DM, previously referred to as “noninsulin-dependent diabetes” or “adult-onset diabetes,” accounts for 90–95% of all diabetes, with the highest proportions in low- and middle-income countries, although the specific etiologies are not known, autoimmune destruction of β-cells does not occur, and most, but not all patients with T2DM have overweight or obesity, the excess weight itself causes some degree of insulin resistance [5, 6].

Managing a diabetic patient needs to be a combination of diet and drugs. Only drugs are given by the physician, but the other is lifestyle modification done by the patient himself. Lifestyle modification can bring these two factors under control [7]. The management of lifestyle factors may be associated with decreased incidence of T2DM, timely diagnosis, health care utilization, and quality of care for patients with T2DM, which may also reduce the burden of diabetes in a community or country [8].

Given that most of the diabetes burden is precipitated by modifiable risk factors, government and academic organizations are urgently required to make policies, allocate medical resources, and clinical guidelines for diabetes education to change unhealthy lifestyles, effectively control the proportion of those who are overweight and obese, and therefore the incidence of diabetes could be reduced [9].

In April 2021, the WHO launched the global diabetes Compact, a global initiative aiming for sustained improvements in diabetes prevention and care, with a particular focus on supporting low- and middle-income countries and bringing together national governments, nongovernmental organizations, academic institutions, other stakeholders, and international donors to work on a shared vision of reducing the risk of diabetes [10].

As per the WHO NCD prevention and control protocol or strategies Ethiopia tries to implement different activities like preparing guidelines on clinical and programmatic management of major NCD and revising policy and strategies in general [11, 12], furthermore, essential health services package of Ethiopia 2019 intervention for DM states, targeted screening for T2DM screening beginning from age 40 years with medium priority, screening and exercise intervention starting from age 25 years with medium priority, screening and treatment of people with T2DM with low priority and particularly for DM among the NCD [13].

Type 2 diabetes is largely influenced by lifestyle factors and can be prevented or delayed through comprehensive lifestyle interventions, particularly improvements in dietary practices and increased levels of physical activity [14]. Modification of lifestyle and eating behaviors plays a central role in the effective management of T2DM. Practices such as regular physical exercise, maintaining a healthy body weight, and adhering to appropriate dietary recommendations contribute significantly to reducing long-term diabetes-related complications.

Lifestyle intervention and patient education are fundamental components of diabetes care provided by the healthcare team. These interventions include diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES), medical nutrition therapy, promotion of physical activity, counseling for smoking cessation, and psychosocial support [15]. Poor adherence to lifestyle modification is influenced by a combination of individual and systemic barriers, most notably limited knowledge and education, poverty and associated costs, population changes, and inadequate access to healthcare [16].

The growing burden of diabetes contributes to increased morbidity, premature mortality, and substantial economic costs for both individuals and nations [17]. Addressing chronic non-communicable diseases has therefore become a global priority and is reflected in the Sustainable Development Goals. In particular, SDG 3 emphasizes the reduction of premature mortality from non-communicable diseases by one-third and the achievement of universal health coverage by 2030 [17].

Some studies in Africa show that Diabetic patients had a low premorbid perception of risk and engaged in diabetes-related risky behaviors, thus Diabetic patients had challenges adhering to lifestyle changes and using both biomedical and local remedies in the management of the condition [18].

Modification of lifestyle and health-related behaviors remains low overall [19]. For instance, in Debre Tabor, Ethiopia, 49% and 39.5% had good knowledge and a good attitude towards diabetes mellitus, respectively; in addition, positive relations between knowledge and positive attitude were seen [20]. Lifestyle modification of diabetes patients attending the University of Gondar Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Northwest Ethiopia, shows that 59% knowledge and 53% attitude toward lifestyle modification were found to be good. However, the result of practice 41% of lifestyle modification was low [21].

Perception of risk and lifestyle are important issues in diabetes prevention and control, also, an individual’s ability to practice lifestyle change by adhering to medication, through exercise and dietary requirements was reduce complications and deaths among people with T2D [22, 23], a factor which is essential for blood sugar control, unable to adhere to treatment (both dietary and pharmacologic) affects the medications leading to inadequate glycemic control [24]. Therefore, vigorously investigating and working on it may have contributed to controlling the blood glucose levels among the patients. This may help to achieve the stated target of the health sector transformation plan II, increasing the proportion of DM patients whose blood sugar level is controlled from 24 to 60% [19].

However, many of the studies on diabetes in this study area from our review have often focused on the epidemiological aspect of the condition, with little on the risk perception and lifestyle modification aspect. There is no study conducted to assess the association between risk perception and lifestyle modification practice to manage DM among diagnosed DM patients. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the association between perception of the risk and lifestyle modification practice among diagnosed DM patients.

Method and materials

Study setting and study period

The study was conducted in Mizan-Aman, an administrative center of the Bench Sheko zone in the South West Ethiopia Peoples' region of Ethiopia. The town is located 581 km from Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. There are 2 health centers, 2 health posts, 14 medium private clinics, and one teaching referral hospital in Mizan-Aman town. MizanTepi University Teaching Hospital (MTUTH) is one of the oldest hospitals and was established in 1987. This hospital serves the Bench Sheko Zone, West Ommo Zone, and Shaka Zone, as well as some parts of Gambella Regional State (Dimma Woreda). Basic health service coverage at the zonal level was 92% and 100% at the town level, and particularly in DM, there are diagnosis and treatment services, and currently, a total of 436 adults are served monthly through DM chronic follow-up care. The data was collected for two months from April 1/2023, to May 30/2023.

Study design

An institutional-based cross-sectional study design was used.

Population

Source population

All diabetic patients who were attending the MTUTH chronic follow-up clinic.

Study population

All diabetic patients who were attending the follow-up clinic during the study period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

All DM adult patients who attended the follow-up clinic were included.

Exclusion criteria

All DM adult patients who were critically ill were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined by including all 436 patients registered in the follow-up clinic, aiming to ensure both scientific soundness and methodological rigor. Response rate was taken into account, and an assessment was conducted to determine whether respondents differed meaningfully from non-respondents.

Sampling procedure and techniques

Given that the total number of patients in chronic follow-up at the clinic was relatively small (N = 436) and all were accessible within the in study period, a census approach was employed. This method was chosen to maximize statistical power and eliminate sampling error. All eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria were invited to participate, thereby improving the precision and generalizability of the results to the clinic's follow-up cohort.

Study variables

Perception of risk for DM complications.

Socio-demographic characteristics

age, sex, marital status, educational background, income, family size, and residence.

Lifestyle modification practice

diet, exercise, weight, alcohol consumption, smoking.

Operational definitions

Lifestyle modification Practice

engaging in regular aerobic exercise, getting regular checkups, stopping smoking, watching your alcohol intake, weight management, foot care, and healthy dietary habits [21, 25], and using 8 dichotomous items, with the score ranging from 0 to 8. Those who score greater than the mean score have a good lifestyle modification practice, while those who score below the mean score have a poor lifestyle modification practice towards DM, which was decided after the normality was checked.

Risk perception

The way a participant thinks and behaves toward DM complications, measured by 8 items, the scores were summed to obtain the total risk perception score for each participant, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 8. and who score greater than the mean score have a good risk perception if below the mean score are poor risk perception towards DM [26], which were decided after the normality was checked.

Regular exercise

20 min–30 min of aerobic exercise such as walking, jogging, and swimming for 5–7 days per week [11].

Dietary practice

was assessed based on the recommended controlled and planned diet practice from different food categories. Response categories ranged from yes (1) to no (0) for each of the four categories. Responses were summed, creating a range of scores from 0 to 4. Participants who reported that they are above the mean score were considered to have good dietary practices [21].

Stop smoking

cessation of smoking for at least a month during this data collection time.

Weight management

was assessed using activities that could be undertaken to manage weight through dietary practices such as reducing portion size, making food substitutions, and exercising to lose weight. Items were assessed in agreement with weight management activities during the past 30 days. Response categories ranged from yes (1) to no (0). Responses were summed, creating a range of scores from 0 to 3. Participants who reported that they were above the mean score were considered to have good weight management practices [21].

Data collection procedure and instrument

The data was collected by two BSc nurses and one supervisor, after having two days of training about the study and the data collection process by the principal investigator. The tool was developed based on an extensive review of relevant literature [11, 21, 24–27]. The questionnaires consist of socio-demographic characteristics, risk perception, and lifestyle modification practice-related questions.

Risk perception was assessed using 8 dichotomous items. Responses were coded as “Yes = 1” and “No/No response/I don’t know = 0.” The scores were summed to obtain the total risk perception score for each participant, with possible scores ranging from 0 to 8. Lifestyle modification practice was assessed using 8 dichotomous items. Responses were coded as “Yes/Practiced = 1” and “No/Not practiced = 0.” Item scores were summed to obtain a total lifestyle modification practice score ranging from 0 to 8.

A 5% pre-test was done in Chena Hospital to check the consistency. Data were collected using a pretested, structured interviewer-administered questionnaire, which was prepared in English and then translated into the Amharic language; finally, it was reconverted to English to check for consistency. Data were collected by exit interviews with chronic case follow-up clinic attendees using structured questionnaires (attached as Supplementary PDF file).

Data quality management

To maintain the data quality, the data collector was given a two-day orientation about the tools and the procedures. The completeness of the data was checked by primary researchers during data collection. During the data collection process, each questionnaire was checked daily in the morning by the supervisor and principal investigators for its completeness and accuracy.

Data analysis and management

Data were cleaned, coded, and organized by frequency table. The data were analyzed by SPSS Version 26. Whenever missing and/or unexpected values were identified, those values were checked in the filled hardcopy data collection questionnaire to correct data entry mistakes. The tools used were a risk perception and lifestyle modification practice compliance questionnaire. Descriptive Statistical analysis, such as range, means, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage, was done for different variables as necessary. The relation between Risk perception and lifestyle modification was analyzed using Pearson's χ2 statistics (p-value < 0.05, 95% CI, and Effect size was measured by Phi-coefficient to measure the strength of association. Finally, the result was presented by the text, frequency, statistical summary, and graph.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondents

A total of 416 DM patients who have follow-up in the chronic disease clinic unit were included in the study. Thus, the response rate was 95%. The inability to complete the full questions due to different reasons at the time of data collection was the main reason for the non-response. Of the total respondents, 236(56.7%) were men, and the remaining 180(43.3%) were females. The majority of the respondents were married, 308(74%), and the remaining were single, 108(26%). The mean age of the respondents was 38.7 with ± 15.7standard deviation. Most of them were Orthodox, 232(55.8%). Followed by Protestants, 115(27.6%) were religious followers. Regarding the educational status of respondents, 190(45.7%) were in elementary (1–8 grade) education, and 167(40.1%) were in high school (9–12 grade) and above. Among the respondents, 211(50.7%) were merchants and 136 (32.7%) were government employees (Table 1).

Discussion

The study showed that the risk perception of the DM patients in chronic clinic attendants in Mizan-Tepi Teaching Hospital in the Southwest Ethiopia region were 42.8% good risk perception, which is consistent with the study conducted in type 2 diabetic mellitus attending public hospitals in Addis Ababa, 43.4% [28]. Another similar study in African Americans’ Perception of Risk for Diabetes Complications indicates they had poor diabetes perceptions regarding its risks and severity; in addition, they perceived that diabetes was not serious [29].

However, in contrast to other studies conducted in adult diabetic individuals applied to the Diabetes Polyclinic of a university hospital in Istanbul, Turkey, and in China, the perceived risk level is high, which particularly confirms that individuals perceive complications such as heart attack, high blood pressure, and vision problems as risky at a higher level [27, 30]. This difference might be due to the socio-demographic differences of the population and the difference in the patient-provider communication approach.

This result implies that low risk perception may result in poor quality of life among the patients since this poor risk perception may lead to being reluctant to prevent them from different risk exposures. As a result, they may suffer from DM complications and premature death. So, working on risk awareness creation among the patients may be important so as to stick to the lifestyle modification practice.

Even though no more literature specifically on the risk perception among DM patients, some studies support this finding, currently, as different evidence indicates in the prevention of type 2 diabetes mellitus to reduce both microvascular and macrovascular complications, it is essential to be aware of risk factors for type 2 diabetes, in the same author the findings of the qualitative study regarding risk perception from type 2 DM patients indicate that most dimensions of the risk perception measures display low-risk awareness and that people believe they are less likely to experience a risk was observed [31].

The analysis showed that better risk perception of diabetes and its complications was linked to healthier lifestyle practices. They have a statistically significant and moderate association (χ2 = 80.07914, p < 0.001, Phi = 0.44), with 75% of respondents who practiced good lifestyle habits also exhibiting good risk perception, compared to only 29.8% among those with poor risk perception. These findings align with the Health Belief Model, which emphasizes perceived susceptibility and severity as key motivators for preventive behavior [32]. Similar to previous research by [33–35], the results suggest that individuals who recognize their personal risk are more likely to adopt healthier behaviors. Also, other findings support this, as Diabetic patients' perceptions of their disease and the expected results have a positive effect on patient adherence to diet [35]. However, as some scholars noted, the relationship between risk perception and behavior can be influenced by factors such as optimistic bias, perceived control, and socio-cultural beliefs [36]. From a public health perspective, the results highlight the importance of integrating risk perception assessments into diabetes prevention and management programs, alongside targeted, culturally sensitive education to enhance awareness and motivate sustained lifestyle changes.

In general, perceptions would be biased depending on which risk dimension and what complications and risks they considered. The scholars recommended that it is necessary to identify practical methods to correct for biased risk perceptions to increase lifestyle modification and treatment adherence in the future [31].

This finding shows the importance of patient-healthcare provider communication improvement. This is also supported by the scholar's suggestion that a potential strategy to improve communication may entail more patient-tailored messaging using appropriate literacy levels in healthcare provider discussions. As a result, additional education about the increased diabetes risk associated with hypertension or hypercholesterolemia may also pay dividends in diabetes prevention(37). Among the risk factors related to perception, only half of the respondents think that alcohol drinking can increase the risk of DM and its complications. This implies that the rest have the chance to get into alcohol drinking practice because they believe that it is not a risk, and in general, almost half of the participants perceived that they are not potentially at risk for DM complications. These are dangerous for the patients; as a result, they need special focus to overcome their fear of taking care of themselves from the potential risk factors exposure and self-care, so they need attention on providing health information from the health care providers. Being informed about an increased diabetes risk by a physician was strongly and independently related to a higher risk perception [38].

The result shows that about 48.8% respondents have good lifestyle modification practice. This was in line with the study conducted in Gonder Specialized Hospital, 41% of the patients have good lifestyle modification practices [21]. However, lower than the study conducted in Jima and Gonder,74.2%, and 74.4% of the participants had good practice of LSM and medication use, respectively [39, 40]. This difference may be due to sociodemographic differences, sample size differences, and the study setting. However, this study was higher than the study conducted in the northern parts of Bangladesh, with good practice in 33% of cases [41]. The possible reasons might be the time difference, the health facility setting, and the health care provider's approaches to communication, which may vary.

Among the lifestyle modification practices, 68.5% have regular blood glucose checkups, which is lower than the study conducted at Jimma University Medical Center, 90.5% for regular blood taste [39]. However, higher than the study done in Gondar Hospital 55% [21], this difference may be due to the time difference, sample size, the town setting, and socio-demographic differences.

In this study, good regular exercise (≥ 150 min per week) was 74%, which is higher than the pooled estimate in the meta-analysis study in Ethiopia, where almost half (49%) of diabetic patients were engaged in the recommended physical activity [42]. This discrepancy might be the study design. However, lower than the study conducted in South Africa, 89.9% of DM patients engage in some form of physical exercise [43], the difference may be the socio-demographic factors and the awareness level of the patients.

Similarly, planned and monitored good dietary practices were 26%, which is lower than the pooled estimate of dietary practice, which was 50% in Ethiopia [42]. A Similar finding was reported in India and South Africa, at 46%, 56.3% said they followed a controlled and planned diet [43, 44]. However, the findings of the current study are higher than the figures reported in Yemen (21%) and Pakistan (17%) [45, 46]. The discrepancy might be explained by socio-cultural differences, differences in the types of foods available with different nutritional values, as well as the level of education provided for diabetic patients. The importance of following a regular dietary plan in terms of both quantity and quality lies in the fact that adequate blood sugar control and proper weight management are linked to it, and the awareness level.

In this study, good weight management was 26%, which is low; however, the fact that lifestyle interventions for weight loss show effectiveness for prevention of type 2 diabetes, and Weight loss in patients with type 2 diabetes can decrease cardio metabolic risk and improve diabetes control. and to achieve this medical approach [47]. Therefore, this implies the patients were at risk for different DM-related complications. Most of the lifestyle modification practices were not properly practiced among the patients. Lifestyle intervention and education are important aspects of diabetes care for the health care team and include diabetes self-management education and support, medical nutrition therapy, physical activity, smoking cessation counseling, and psychosocial care.

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it was conducted among diabetes mellitus patients attending the chronic follow-up clinic, which ensured that the study population was clinically confirmed and directly relevant to the research objectives. The relatively large sample size increased the representativeness of the findings and provided sufficient statistical power to detect associations. In addition, since the study was conducted within a clinical setting, the results have practical value for guiding patient education and intervention programs in similar healthcare facilities.

Limitations

Despite these strengths, the study also has some limitations. Even though risk perception and practice could vary across Type 2 and Type 1 DM patients, this report did not address such issues. Data on perception and practices were collected through self-report, which is subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Lastly, because the study assessed participants at a single point in time, it may not fully capture changes in perception and practices that occur over the course of disease progression or with continued education.

Overall, despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the strong relationship between risk perception and lifestyle modification practices among diabetes patients, and serves as an important basis for designing patient-centered educational and behavioral interventions.

Conclusion

In this study, only 42.8% of diabetes patients demonstrated good risk perception regarding diabetes mellitus and its complications, and just 48.8% reported practicing good lifestyle modification. These findings indicate that both risk perception and lifestyle modification practices are relatively low among patients attending the chronic follow-up clinic. The observed association between risk perception and lifestyle modification suggests that have moderate association, so that, improving patients’ understanding and awareness of diabetes and its complications may positively influence their engagement in healthier lifestyle practices.

Recommendation

Therefore, strengthening patient education, multidisciplinary diabetes care, and patient–provider communication is essential to convey the importance of lifestyle modification in preventing complications of diabetes mellitus, improving quality of life, and helping individuals understand that those with the disease are at risk of developing complications. In addition, future studies should further explore factors influencing risk perception and lifestyle practices, while patients are encouraged to follow healthcare providers’ recommendations to reduce complications and improve quality of life.
