Joint Assessment Matters: Diagnostic and Therapeutic Benefits of Interdisciplinary Psoriasis Care
Authors: Tassilo Dege, Oliver Steffens, Özlem Kiesel, Iana Bychkova, Lara Leichtfuss, Theresa Schulze-Hagen, Matthias Goebeler, Marc Schmalzing, Jan Leipe, Victor Olsavszky, Patrick-Pascal Strunz, Caroline Glatzel, Astrid Schmieder
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriasis is a chronic inflammatory disease often accompanied by musculoskeletal symptoms and psoriatic arthritis (PsA). Early identification of PsA remains challenging, underscoring the need for interdisciplinary care between dermatology and rheumatology. To evaluate the diagnostic and therapeutic impact of an interdisciplinary dermatology-rheumatology board (IDRB) for patients with psoriasis, we initiated a non-randomized, prospective bicentric study.MethodsA total of 182 patients with psoriasis were enrolled at baseline (V0), of whom 111 completed the 12-month follow-up (V2). Forty-seven (25.8%) patients participated in the IDRB, and 135 (74.2%) patients received standard dermatological care. Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI), Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS-A/D), pain, systemic inflammation, psoriatic arthritis (PsA) diagnosis, and systemic therapy courses were analyzed. Group differences and changes over time were assessed using non-parametric and parametric tests, and predictors of therapy modification were explored using univariate logistic regression.ResultsOver 12 months, patients in the IDRB group showed statistically significant improvements in PASI, DLQI, and HADS-A (all p ≤ 0.05). Among participants without PsA at baseline and with complete PsA documentation at follow-up, new PsA diagnoses occurred more often in the IDRB cohort (31%) than in standard care (9.8%) (Fisher’s exact p = 0.0295; χ2 p = 0.0360; OR = 4.14). In univariate analyses, higher baseline PASI, DLQI, and HADS-A values were each associated with subsequent therapy modification. Within the IDRB group, biologic treatments shifted over time toward IL-17- and IL-23-targeted agents, indicating a move toward more streamlined and targeted systemic therapy patterns compared with standard care.ConclusionAn IDRB may contribute to more structured PsA assessment and to more informed therapeutic decisions in patients with psoriasis. Integrating objective clinical measures together with patient-reported burden appears crucial for guiding treatment modification and optimizing outcomes. Given the non-randomized, self-selected design, these findings should be interpreted as associations.Trial RegistrationDRKS-Deutsches Register Klinischer Studien listing: DRKS00037907.Keywords: Disease management, Biologics, Mental health, Psoriasis, Interdisciplinary consultation, Dermatological-rheumatological consultation, Psoriatic arthritis

Introduction

Psoriasis (PsO) is a common, chronic inflammatory autoimmune disease that seriously impacts patients’ physical and mental well-being because of pruritus, pain, and social stigma. Its prevalence in the global population varies between 2 and 4% [1], although it differs between continents and regions, being far less common in Asian populations [2]. Up to 41% of patients develop musculoskeletal symptoms, manifesting as psoriatic arthritis (PsA) [3]. In about 80% of all PsA patients, skin lesions appear years before the emergence of joint pain; hence, dermatologists are often the first to be consulted in these cases [4]. As PsA has a larger systemic impact, it increases the burden of disease compared to common PsO, leading to lower quality of life (QoL) and higher health costs [5], and is associated with more comorbidities, such as hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, metabolic syndrome, and obesity, as well as hyperlipidemia [6]. Earlier diagnosis and interdisciplinary treatment of patients with PsA can improve overall outcome regarding skin and musculoskeletal symptoms and especially QoL [7].

With PsA being a complex clinical entity concerning at least two medical specialties, interdisciplinary management from the earliest possible moment is important for adequate diagnosis and treatment of this disease. There are few interdisciplinary structures to manage both PsO and PsA by dermatologists and rheumatologists as a team [8]. In recent years, biologics have become the first-line treatment of moderate-to-severe PsO and PsA [9]. This calls for interdisciplinary cooperation, as selecting the best medication individually becomes more complex, especially when several comorbidities are present.

Aim of this Study

The aim of this study was to evaluate whether participation in the IDRB, compared with standard dermatological care, is associated with (1) higher detection of previously unrecognized psoriatic arthritis over 12 months, (2) differences in systemic treatment decisions (rates of therapy modification and therapy class transitions over time), and (3) changes in key clinical and patient-reported outcomes (PASI, DLQI, HADS-A/D, pain, and pruritus,) from baseline to 12-month follow-up. The present analysis focuses on the interdisciplinary care pathway.

Methods

Ethical Consideration

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Würzburg (reference number: 129/22) and of the Medical Faculty Mannheim, Heidelberg University (reference no. 2022-502). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Participants received no compensation for their participation.

Study Design

This multicenter, prospective non-randomized intervention study (DRKS00037907) was conducted at the Departments of Dermatology and Rheumatology of the University Hospital Würzburg and the University Medical Center Mannheim, Germany, between March 2023 and May 2025. Patients were recruited during visits to the outpatient clinics or during inpatient stays at the Department of Dermatology. Individuals who reported any form of joint pain during their baseline visit or who had an existing diagnosis of psoriatic arthritis (PsA) were offered participation in the IDRB. Participation in the IDRB was entirely voluntary, and patients could freely choose whether to attend the interdisciplinary consultation; no randomization was performed. Accordingly, all between-group comparisons are observational and should be interpreted as associations rather than causal effects of IDRB participation. Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years, a confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis (any subtype), and willingness to use a smartphone app for monitoring disease progression. The exclusion criterion was insufficient German language skills. All participants had access to the accompanying mHealth app; however, the results concerning its specific effects are reported elsewhere, and the changes described here can be interpreted independently of the app component. The IRDB was conducted by at least one board-certified dermatologist and rheumatologist. Each patient filled out questionnaires from the regular interim visit and was then examined for at least 15–30 min by both specialists. This examination always included taking an extensive medical history and performing a dermatological and rheumatological physical examination including swollen and tender joint counts. If deemed necessary, they could order special blood tests or imaging procedures, such as MRI in case of back pain or musculoskeletal (MSK) ultrasound. Rheumatological MSK ultrasound was performed by rheumatologists only if they were trained and certified for this specialized procedure. Selection of the investigated joints was based on the individual patient case after preassessment of swollen or tender joints. MSK ultrasound focused on typical PsA manifestations such as enthesitis, dactylitis, tenosynovitis, or synovitis. MRIs were assessed by MSK-trained radiologists and interpreted by the rheumatologists. Thus, the IDRB consultation enabled a joint dermatological and rheumatological assessment with immediate access to point-of-care musculoskeletal ultrasound, allowing early detection of subclinical synovitis or enthesitis, which can precede manifest psoriatic arthritis.

Patients who did not participate in the IDRB received routine dermatological care at the participating university outpatient clinics. These visits were conducted by board-certified dermatologists and included assessment of psoriasis severity and treatment response, review of current topical and systemic therapies, and routine safety monitoring according to standard practice. Musculoskeletal symptoms were assessed by clinical history during dermatology visits; when inflammatory arthritis was suspected, patients were referred to rheumatology for further diagnostic evaluation and management. In this study, PsA diagnoses in the standard care cohort were recorded only if documented by rheumatology (i.e., rheumatologist-confirmed diagnosis), and imaging (e.g., MRI) was obtained via standard clinical referral pathways.

Patient Characteristics

Patients were assessed at baseline (V0), 6 months (V1), and 12 months (V2). The IDRB consultation was integrated into the 6-month interim visit (V1). At each visit, standardized clinical parameters, patient-reported outcomes, and current diagnoses were documented. During the IDRB, dermatologists and rheumatologists jointly re-evaluated MSK symptoms, confirmed or revised PsA diagnoses, and reviewed ongoing dermatological and rheumatological treatment strategies. The central aims were to identify previously unrecognized PsA and to optimize therapy based on a combined specialty assessment.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients receiving a new diagnosis of psoriatic arthritis following the interdisciplinary dermatology-rheumatology board (IDRB) consultation. Secondary outcomes included changes in psoriasis severity (Psoriasis Area and Severity Index [PASI]), quality of life (Dermatology Life Quality Index [DLQI]), psychological well-being (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale-Anxiety and Depression subscales [HADS-A] and [HADS-D]), pain and pruritus intensity (numeric rating scales [NRS]), and systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein [CRP]) from baseline (V0) to 6- (V1) and 12-month (V2) follow-ups. Exploratory analyses further assessed predictors of therapy modification and evaluated whether demographic or disease-related parameters (age, sex, BMI, smoking, psoriasis duration, and systemic therapy class) were associated with treatment change.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using Python (version 3.13.7) and relevant scientific libraries including pandas, numpy, plotly, and matplotlib. A p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Group differences were assessed using χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test or Student’s t test for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Changes over time within groups (V0 vs. V2) were analyzed using Wilcoxon signed-rank or paired t-tests, as appropriate. To explore factors associated with therapy modification, univariate logistic regression analyses were performed, including clinical, demographic, and patient-reported variables as predictors. Odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated to quantify effect sizes.

For outcome analyses (Table 2), complete documentation was required for both baseline (V0) and follow-up (V2) values of the respective variable. In the case of binary variables such as psoriatic arthritis diagnosis, complete analysis was defined as the presence of valid entries at both visits without missing values. This resulted in complete paired PsA documentation for 42 of 47 IDRB participants (89.4%) and 69 of 135 non-IDRB patients (51.1%). Accordingly, newly diagnosed PsA was calculated among those with complete V0 and V2 information. For Sankey flow analyses (Fig. 3), only patients with complete systemic therapy documentation across all three visits (V0, V1, V2) were included, corresponding to the per-protocol population used for therapy dynamics (n = 95 of 182; 52.2%).

Results

Study Cohort

Patients were recruited at two university hospitals between March 2023 and February 2024; IDRB consultations took place between October 2023 and September 2024. At baseline (V0), 182 patients were enrolled: 47 (25.8%) participated in the IDRB and 135 (74.2%) received standard dermatological care (Fig. 1). At 12 months (V2), follow-up data were available for 111 patients.

Study flow chart. Flow of participants from screening through baseline assessment (V0), interim visit (V1), and final 12-month follow-up (V2)

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 182 patients with psoriasis were enrolled at baseline, of whom 47 (25.8%) participated in the interdisciplinary dermatology-rheumatology board (IDRB) and 135 (74.2%) received standard dermatological care. At baseline, patients in the IDRB group tended to be slightly older and more frequently female, but differences in age, sex, and BMI were not statistically significant (Table 1). Smoking behavior differed between groups, with a lower proportion of current smokers (31.9% vs. 44.4%) but a higher proportion of former smokers (25.5% vs. 11.1%) among IDRB participants. Baseline PASI, DLQI, and CRP values were comparable. However, HADS-A and HADS-D scores were higher in the IDRB cohort, indicating greater psychological burden (p = 0.035 and p = 0.004, respectively).

Primary Outcome: New Psoriatic Arthritis Diagnosis Over 12 Months

At baseline, pre-existing PsA was more frequent in the IDRB group than in standard care (36.2% [17/47] vs. 21.5% [29/135]; Fisher’s exact p = 0.057; OR = 2.08). Among patients without baseline PsA and with paired PsA documentation at baseline and 12 months, incident PsA occurred in 31.0% (9/29) of IDRB participants compared with 9.8% (5/51) in standard care (Fisher’s exact p = 0.0295; χ2 p = 0.0360; OR = 4.14), consistent with higher PsA detection among patients managed via the IDRB pathway.

Secondary Outcomes: Clinical and Patient-Reported Outcomes Changes

Over 12 months, PASI improved to a median of 1.0 at V2 in both cohorts, and DLQI improved to 3–4 points (Table 2). Anxiety symptoms (HADS-A) improved significantly in the IDRB cohort, whereas changes in HADS-A did not reach statistical significance in standard care. Depressive symptoms (HADS-D) and pain NRS showed no statistically significant change in either group (Table 2).

In an exploratory subgroup analysis of patients with baseline musculoskeletal pain (MSCP) (Fig. 2), PASI and DLQI improved in both the IDRB and standard care subgroups. A statistically significant reduction in HADS-A was observed only in the MSCP + IDRB subgroup, whereas changes in HADS-A/HADS-D did not reach significance in MSCP + standard care (Fig. 2).

Therapy Outcomes

Therapy class transitions over 12 months are shown in Fig. 3A/B. In the IDRB pathway (Fig. 3A), transitions during follow-up more frequently involved IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors, whereas standard dermatological care (Fig. 3B) showed a more heterogeneous distribution across systemic and non-systemic regimens.

To identify potential factors of treatment modification independently of IDRB participation, patients were divided into two subgroups according to whether they underwent a therapy change or remained on their initial regimen between baseline and follow-up. Across the total cohort, 71 of 182 patients (39%) experienced a therapy modification. In univariate logistic regression (Table 3), higher PASI (OR = 1.08 [1.04–1.13]; p = 0.0001), DLQI (OR = 1.10 [1.05–1.15]; p = 0.0001), HADS-A (OR = 1.08 [1.01–1.17]; p = 0.033), HADS-D (OR = 1.08 [1.00–1.17]; p = 0.047), pain NRS (OR = 1.12 [1.00–1.24]; p = 0.040), and pruritus NRS (OR = 1.17 [1.05–1.30]; p = 0.005) were each significantly associated with increased odds of therapy change. Conversely, a shorter psoriasis duration was likewise predictive (OR = 0.97 [0.95–0.99]; p = 0.019). No significant associations were observed for PsA diagnosis, CRP, BMI, age, smoking exposure (pack years), alcohol consumption, or metabolic comorbidities such as arterial hypertension and type 2 diabetes mellitus. Prior systemic therapy and NSAID use showed no statistically significant relationship with therapy adjustment. Therapy modification was associated with higher PASI, DLQI, HADS-A/D, and pain and pruritus scores, whereas comorbidities and lifestyle factors showed no significant relationship.

Discussion

Principal Findings

In this two-center, prospective cohort study, we evaluated a newly established IDRB for patients with psoriasis. Three main findings emerged. First, IDRB participation was associated with a higher rate of newly established PsA diagnoses compared with standard dermatological care (31.0% vs. 9.8% among patients with complete PsA documentation). Second, both groups showed significant improvements in psoriasis severity and quality of life over 12 months, but a statistically significant reduction in anxiety levels (HADS-A) was observed only in the IDRB cohort. Psychological comorbidity is common in psoriasis and can meaningfully influence patient-reported burden and treatment decisions, supporting routine mental health/PRO assessment within integrated care pathways [10]. This reduction reflects changes in the overall IDRB population and is independent of musculoskeletal symptom status. Third, therapy modification was primarily driven by clinical disease burden and patient-reported outcomes (PASI, DLQI, HADS-A/D, pain and pruritus NRS), whereas comorbidities, inflammatory markers, and lifestyle factors did not predict treatment change, despite their known importance in psoriatic disease. Notably, IDRB participants tended to be older, were more often female, and showed higher baseline psychological burden and a higher prevalence of established PsA. This suggests an inclusion of more complex and vulnerable patients into IDRB. While PASI and DLQI improved similarly in both groups, only the IDRB cohort showed a significant reduction in anxiety. This pattern was also observed in the dedicated MSCP-only subgroup, where HADS-A improved significantly only among IDRB participants. These findings indicate a potential benefit of interdisciplinary assessment, but causality cannot be established because of the self-selected nature of IDRB participation.

In our cohort, newly established PsA diagnoses were markedly more frequent in the IDRB group (31.0%) than in patients receiving standard dermatological care (9.8%). Combined dermatology-rheumatology clinic models have been implemented for more than a decade and have been proposed to improve diagnostic coordination and management in psoriatic disease [11]. These findings are comparable to data from other interdisciplinary settings: in a prospective single-center study of an IDRB, von Ahnen et al. reported diagnostic reclassification in 15.2% of 79 patients, including 8 first PsA diagnoses and 4 cases in which a prior PsA diagnosis was no longer confirmed [12]. Similarly, a Greek study evaluated a weekly IDRB with > 700 patients attending a dedicated psoriasis clinic and identified 185 newly referred individuals who required structured joint assessment [13].

Therapy modification in our cohort was primarily driven by PASI, DLQI [14], and HADS-A/D, as well as typical psoriasis symptoms (measured by pain and pruritus intensity), whereas obesity, arterial hypertension, diabetes, and smoking exposure were not associated with treatment change. However, they are established as comorbidities in psoriatic disease [5]. This pattern is comparable to other IDRB cohorts, where escalation to biologic or targeted therapy has likewise been linked more closely to residual disease activity and patient-reported burden[15] than to comorbidity load per se. Because participation was voluntary and non-randomized, these results cannot establish causality. Thus, IDRB uptake likely reflects referral and patient preference toward interdisciplinary evaluation among individuals with more complex disease and higher perceived unmet needs.

The additional finding that shorter psoriasis duration predicted therapy modification suggests a greater readiness to intensify treatment earlier in the disease course, consistent with reports from combined clinics and early PsA concepts that emphasize timely diagnosis and treatment escalation within a “window of opportunity” to prevent structural damage and long-term disability [16].

Overall, voluntary participation in the IDRB was associated with a higher rate of newly established PsA diagnoses and a significant reduction in anxiety scores (HADS-A) compared with patients receiving standard dermatological care. The greater reduction in anxiety (HADS-A) among IDRB participants must be interpreted considering their higher baseline anxiety, suggesting that individuals with greater psychological burden may preferentially opt for interdisciplinary assessment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the non-randomized design and voluntary participation in the IDRB introduce selection bias. Patients referred to and accepting IDRB participation were more likely to have musculoskeletal symptoms, established PsA, and higher psychological burden at baseline, which limits generalizability and precludes causal inference regarding the effect of the IDRB itself. Second, despite the prospective design, the dropout rate was substantial, with a limited sample size of 111 of 182 patients (61%) completing the 12-month study, which is, however, comparable to dropout rates reported in other prospective dermato-rheumatology cohorts such as the DEPAR early PsA cohort (around 25–30% over follow-up)[17] and the 24-week study by von Ahnen et al. (around 25% dropout) [12]. Loss to follow-up may have led to under- or overestimation of both diagnostic yield and treatment effects. Third, all patients were treated in two German university hospitals with high expertise in psoriasis and PsA management, which may limit external validity to other healthcare settings with different resource levels or access to ultrasound and biologics.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that an IDRB embedded in a modern psoriasis care pathway may be associated with improved PsA detection, more targeted and sustained systemic therapy, and greater reductions in anxiety compared with standard dermatological care alone. In this cohort, therapy modification was associated with objective disease severity and patient-reported burden (e.g., PASI, DLQI, HADS-A/D, pain, and pruritus), whereas comorbidities and isolated biomarkers were not associated with treatment change, underscoring the value of systematic PRO assessment in routine care.

These findings support the broader implementation of structured interdisciplinary models to optimize diagnostic and therapeutic outcomes for patients with psoriasis and psoriatic arthritis. Future studies, ideally incorporating randomization, may help further clarify the extent to which these associations reflect causal effects of interdisciplinary care.
