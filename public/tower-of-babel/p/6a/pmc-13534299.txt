Is Routine Interruption of Direct Oral Anticoagulants Necessary during Colorectal Endoscopic Mucosal Resection and Hot Snare Polypectomy? A Multicenter Propensity‐Matched Study
Authors: Yutaka Okagawa, Masanori Sato, Masahiro Hirakawa, Shogo Miura, Naotaka Hayasaka, Yusuke Tomita, Saki Ameda, Ginji Omori, Shutaro Oiwa, Akira Sakurada, Tomoyuki Abe, Shinya Minami, Tetsuya Sumiyoshi, Yasuhiro Nagaoka, Hiroyuki Hisai, Shinichi Katsuki, Kohichi Takada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsColorectal endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) and hot snare polypectomy (HSP) are high‐bleeding‐risk endoscopic procedures, and most clinical guidelines recommend stopping direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) temporarily or switching to heparin‐bridging therapy (HBT). However, existing evidence is largely derived from cohorts that combine warfarin and DOAC users or include substantial use of HBT, and data directly comparing the continuation versus interruption of DOACs are limited. We aimed to assess whether periprocedural DOAC interruption reduces delayed postpolypectomy bleeding (DPPB) in patients undergoing EMR/HSP.MethodsConsecutive patients receiving DOAC therapy who underwent colorectal EMR or HSP between April 2020 and March 2024 were included in this retrospective study. The patients were classified into continuation and interruption groups following periprocedural DOAC management. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted using clinically relevant variables. The primary outcome was DPPB within 28 days.ResultsOverall, 691 patients who were receiving DOACs underwent EMR/HSP, and 146 matched pairs were analyzed. After PSM, covariate balance between the groups was improved. DPPB occurred in six patients (4.1%) in the interruption group and three patients (2.1%) in the continuation group (p = 0.501; absolute risk difference: 2.05% [95% confidence interval, −1.90%–6.01%]). No thromboembolic events were observed.ConclusionsDOAC continuation may not substantially increase DPPB risk compared with 1‐day DOAC interruption in patients undergoing colorectal EMR/HSP. However, the small number of bleeding events and wide confidence intervals mean that a clinically meaningful difference cannot be excluded. Further prospective studies are needed to determine the optimal periprocedural DOAC management strategy.Keywords: colon polypectomy, delayed postpolypectomy bleeding, direct oral anticoagulants

Introduction

Endoscopic removal of colorectal adenomatous polyps is the cornerstone of preventing colorectal cancer, leading to a substantial reduction in cancer incidence and mortality [1, 2]. As the global population ages, the prevalence of comorbid cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases increases, and endoscopists are required to conduct polypectomies in patients taking antithrombotic agents. Clinical practice guidelines for managing antithrombotic agents in endoscopy were issued in Japan in 2014, considering the risks of postpolypectomy bleeding and thromboembolism [3], followed by an appendix addressing anticoagulants, including direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) [4], in 2018 by the Japanese Gastroenterological Endoscopy Society (JGES). The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) [5] and the European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ESGE) [6] have also provided recommendations. Endoscopic mucosal resection (EMR) and hot snare polypectomy (HSP) are high‐bleeding‐risk endoscopic procedures, and these guidelines recommend temporarily interrupting DOACs or switching to heparin‐bridging therapy (HBT). Specifically, the JGES guidelines recommend interruption on treatment days, while the ASGE guidelines recommend 10‐ to 36‐h interruption (low thrombotic risk) or HBT (high thrombotic risk), and the ESGE guidelines recommend a 48‐h drug‐free interval.

Regarding colorectal polypectomy in patients receiving anticoagulants, a retrospective study reported that delayed postpolypectomy bleeding (DPPB) occurred more frequently in warfarin and DOAC users than in nonusers [7]. Another retrospective study reported no significant difference in the DPPB rate between patients who continued anticoagulants and those who interrupted them [8]. However, many earlier studies combined warfarin and DOAC users and included a substantial number of patients managed with HBT, which has been associated with an increased risk of DPPB. Consequently, the evidence directly supporting the current guidelines that recommend DOAC interruption during colorectal polypectomy remains limited.

In routine clinical practice, the detection of a colorectal polyp in a patient receiving DOAC often leads to deferral of resection, interruption or substitution of the anticoagulant, and scheduling a subsequent colonoscopy dedicated to polypectomy. This strategy increases patient burden by requiring repeated bowel preparation and additional visits and may also increase healthcare costs. If DOAC can be safely continued during EMR/HSP without substantially increasing the risk of DPPB, same‐session resection could be performed, potentially reducing both patient burden and thromboembolic risk associated with anticoagulant interruption.

DPPB is one of the most clinically relevant adverse events after colorectal polypectomy. Nonetheless, most studies evaluating DOAC management during polypectomy are single‐center studies with relatively small sample sizes, and evidence specifically addressing continued DOAC use during high‐bleeding‐risk endoscopic procedures is limited. Recently, cold snare polypectomy (CSP) has been widely conducted for diminutive polyps; however, the DPPB rate has reportedly been very low [9], and studies on EMR/HSP, including CSP, may underestimate the true bleeding risk of high‐risk procedures. Therefore, in this multicenter study, we focused exclusively on EMR and HSP and compared the incidence of DPPB between interrupting and continuing DOACs to clarify whether guideline‐recommended DOAC management is appropriate in real‐world clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design and Patients

A retrospective, multicenter cohort study was conducted. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of the Sapporo Medical University (approval no. 362‐126, approval date: October 10, 2024) and subsequently by the IRBs of all participating institutions and was implemented in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. Instead, information about the study was disclosed on each institution's website, and patients were allowed to decline participation via an opt‐out process.

Consecutive patients receiving DOACs who underwent EMR or HSP between April 2020 and March 2024 were identified using institutional endoscopy databases. Eligible patients were ≥ 20 years of age, treated with DOACs (dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, or edoxaban), and had sufficient clinical and follow‐up data to evaluate postpolypectomy outcomes. Exclusion criteria included patients whose clinical background, endoscopic findings, and postpolypectomy outcomes could not be evaluated and patients who declined to provide their clinical data. Additionally, patients managed with HBT were excluded from the analysis.

Endoscopic Procedures and Periprocedural DOAC Management

Colonoscopies were conducted using high‐resolution colonoscopes (CF‐H260AZI, CF‐H290I, CF‐HQ290Z, or PCF‐H290ZI; [Olympus Medical Systems], EC‐590ZW, EC‐600ZP, EC‐760ZP, EI‐580BT [Fujifilm]) with carbon dioxide insufflation. Conscious sedation using intravenous midazolam or diazepam was administered per the patient's condition and institutional policy. Polypectomy was conducted using commercially available snares, including the SnareMaster Plus [Olympus Medical Systems], Captivator II [Boston Scientific], B‐Wave, or DRAGONARE [XEMEX]. The attending endoscopist at each participating institution selected HSP or EMR and determined the use of prophylactic clipping and other technical aspects of the procedure based on lesion characteristics and clinical judgment. An expert endoscopist was defined as a board‐certified endoscopist accredited by the JGES. Periprocedural DOAC management was determined according to routine clinical practice at each participating institution, and no standardized study protocol was used to assign patients to DOAC continuation or interruption. In the interruption group, DOACs were withheld on the day of the procedure and were generally resumed on the day after the procedure or after adequate hemostasis had been confirmed.

Outcome Measures

The patients were categorized into two groups based on periprocedural DOAC management: a continuation group, in which DOACs were not interrupted, and an interruption group, in which DOACs were withheld on the day of the procedure. The DOACs used in this study were dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban. Baseline patient characteristics, polyp findings, procedural methods, use of prophylactic clips, intraprocedural bleeding, and DPPB were compared between the two groups. DPPB was defined as bleeding requiring endoscopic hemostasis or a decrease in hemoglobin level of ≥ 2 g/dL associated with hematochezia within 28 days after polypectomy. The day of polypectomy was defined as Day 0 and was used to calculate the timing of the DPPB. Intraprocedural bleeding was defined as active bleeding requiring hemostasis during the procedure. Based on previously reported DPPB risks [10, 11], polyp size and number were set at a cutoff of 10 mm, and 1 versus ≥ 2, respectively. Polyp morphology was classified as pedunculated or flat/sessile.

The risk factors for DPPB were evaluated in the secondary analyses. Subgroup analyses were conducted to compare DPPB rates using polyp size, number, and concomitant antithrombotic therapy. Moreover, descriptive analyses were conducted for each DOAC, and the timing of DPPB onset after polypectomy was analyzed.

Statistical Analysis

EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R version 2.13.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), was used for all the statistical analyses [12]. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to reduce the selection bias and potential confounders. Rigorous adjustments were made for the following 16 factors in the PSM analysis: age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, liver cirrhosis, dialysis, inpatient versus outpatient status, combination with antiplatelet agents, endoscopist experience, type of procedure (EMR or HSP), polyp size, number of polyps, polyp location, polyp morphology, prophylactic clips, and intraprocedural bleeding. PSM was conducted using the nearest‐neighbor method in a ratio of 1:1 using a caliper width of 0.1 in the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. Quantitative variables were expressed as medians, while categorical variables were shown as total numbers and percentages. Pearson's chi‐squared and Mann–Whitney U‐tests were used, as appropriate. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify the factors independently associated with DPPB, and odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Time‐to‐event analyses for DPPB were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log‐rank test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Overall, 715 patients who received DOAC and underwent polypectomies between April 2020 and March 2024 were reviewed. Among these, 24 were excluded for the following reasons: patients whose data could not be evaluated (n = 21) and HBT (n = 3). Therefore, 691 patients were analyzed (Figure 1). Table 1 shows baseline patient characteristics. The median age of the patients was 76 years (517 male and 174 female patients). The prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes mellitus were 57.1% and 23.5%, respectively. The median CHADS2 and CHA2DS‐VASC scores were 2 (0–6) and 3 (0–9), respectively. The numbers of patients taking dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban were 40, 202, 182, and 267, respectively. Overall, 547 patients received DOAC monotherapy.

Outcomes

Table 2 shows patients' characteristics, polyp findings, and procedural findings before and after PSM in the groups. Polyp size was recorded categorically rather than as a continuous variable. Among the resected polyps, 1260 were < 10 mm and 374 were ≥ 10 mm. Before PSM, significant differences were observed in the type of procedure and polyp size. These differences were adjusted after matching, and 292 patients were extracted from the 146 patients in each group. After PSM, the balance of measured baseline, lesion, and procedural characteristics between the two groups was generally improved, as assessed by standardized mean differences. DPPB was observed in six patients (4.1%) in the interruption group and in three patients (2.1%) in the continuation group, with no significant difference (p = 0.501; absolute risk difference, 2.05% [95% CI, −1.90% to 6.01%]). Among patients receiving both DOACs and antiplatelet agents, all 26 patients in the DOAC continuation group continued concomitant antiplatelet agents, and DPPB occurred in 2 patients (7.7%). In the DOAC interruption group, all antiplatelet agents were continued in 42 patients and at least one antiplatelet agent was interrupted in 76 patients; DPPB occurred in 5 patients (11.9%) and 4 patients (5.3%), respectively (Table S1). No adverse events, including thromboembolism, were observed in either group.

Secondary Analysis

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, DOAC interruption was not significantly associated with DPPB (OR 1.88; 95% CI 0.55–6.45; p = 0.316). Increasing age (per 10‐year increase) was inversely associated with DPPB (OR 0.60; 95% CI 0.38–0.95; p = 0.028). Furthermore, combination therapy with antiplatelet agents was independently associated with DPPB (OR 3.30; 95% CI 1.44–7.58; p = 0.0049) (Table 3).

Discussion

Current guidelines recommend temporary interruption of DOACs or HBT for colorectal polypectomy; however, evidence supporting these recommendations remains limited. The reported DPPB rate in anticoagulated patients ranges from 3% to 13% [7, 13, 14, 15, 16]; however, only a few studies have directly compared the continuation and interruption of DOACs during polypectomy [8]. A previous systematic review indicated that the DPPB rate was higher in patients with a 24‐h interruption of DOAC before polypectomy than in those without taking anticoagulants [17]. A different interruption period is recommended in each guideline, and the ESGE guidelines indicate a 48‐h interruption of DOAC for patients with a low thrombotic risk in polypectomy [6], as it will ensure a minimal residual anticoagulant effect in most patients, and the 24‐h interruption of DOAC suggested by the JGES guidelines may be too short for the loss of its effect. However, a longer interruption period was associated with a higher risk of thromboembolism. Previous studies have reported thromboembolic event rates of approximately 1% after a 1‐day interruption of DOACs and approximately 2% after a 2‐ to 3‐day interruption [18]. Therefore, prolonged DOAC interruption may increase thromboembolic risk, highlighting the importance of balancing bleeding and thrombotic risks when determining the optimal periprocedural DOAC management strategy.

The impact of continued DOAC therapy on DPPB remains controversial. Some studies reported an increased risk of DPPB with DOAC continuation, particularly studies involving CSP [19]. Conversely, other studies have shown no significant increase in DPPB with continued DOAC therapy [8, 9]. These inconsistent findings may be explained by differences in endoscopic techniques, patient populations, definitions of bleeding, and inclusion of patients receiving HBT. In this multicenter study focusing exclusively on EMR and HSP, DOAC continuation was not associated with an increased risk of DPPB compared with 1‐day DOAC interruption, even after adjusting for PSM and multivariable analysis. These findings suggest that DOAC continuation may not substantially increase DPPB risk in selected patients undergoing EMR/HSP, particularly under conditions of high prophylactic clip use. However, because the number of bleeding events was small, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Increasing age was also inversely associated with DPPB, aligning with previous reports [10, 20]. The underlying mechanism remains unclear; however, younger patients may engage in more physical activity after polypectomy, potentially contributing to a higher risk of bleeding. Our findings are consistent with prior evidence, as similar trends have been reported.

Previous studies showed that the risk of bleeding associated with using apixaban is lower than that associated with rivaroxaban [21]. Conversely, another study reported that apixaban was associated with a lower DPPB rate than dabigatran and rivaroxaban [22]. In this study, a low DPPB rate was observed in the patients receiving edoxaban. However, formal comparisons between individual DOACs were not feasible because the number of bleeding events was limited, and these observations should be cautiously interpreted. Furthermore, because DOACs were analyzed as a single pharmacological category in the primary analysis, potential drug‐specific effects on DPPB risk may have been obscured. Therefore, larger studies are required to evaluate differences in DPPB risk among individual DOAC agents.

This study had some limitations. First, this study may not have been sufficiently powered to detect small differences in bleeding risk between the continuation and interruption of DOACs because DPPB was a relatively infrequent event. Therefore, the absence of significant differences should be cautiously interpreted. Furthermore, the OR for DOAC interruption was associated with a relatively wide 95% CI, reflecting limited precision owing to the small number of events. Therefore, no significant association was observed; however, the modest effects on bleeding risk could not be definitively excluded. Second, this was a retrospective study, and we were unable to evaluate the bowel preparation quality, specific endoscopic devices used for each patient, or electrosurgical unit settings. Additionally, the actual incidence of intraprocedural bleeding may have been underestimated because bleeding events were identified from medical records. Detailed renal function parameters were not uniformly available for all patients; therefore, the influence of renal function on DOAC anticoagulant effects and DPPB risk beyond dialysis status could not be fully evaluated. Although the dates of DOAC interruption and resumption were collected from medical records, actual medication intake by the patient before and after the procedure was not objectively confirmed in all cases. Therefore, medication nonadherence to the intended periprocedural DOAC management cannot be completely excluded. Although PSM improved the balance of measured covariates, residual confounding and indication bias may have remained because of the retrospective design. Periprocedural DOAC management was not based on a standardized protocol, and institutional practice patterns varied substantially, with some institutions more frequently continuing DOACs (Figure S1). Third, prophylactic clipping was conducted in most patients after colorectal polypectomy. The effectiveness of prophylactic clipping for DPPB remains controversial [23, 24, 25], however, its use in most cases may have led to the low DPPB rate observed in this study. The high rate of prophylactic clipping in the present study may partly reflect the fact that all included patients were receiving DOAC therapy, leading endoscopists to consider them at increased risk of postpolypectomy bleeding and to perform clipping more proactively. However, this practice was not based on a unified protocol, and the indications for clipping, completeness of mucosal defect closure, and number of clips used were not systematically collected. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as reflecting the safety of DOAC continuation under conditions of intensive prophylactic clipping, rather than proving the intrinsic safety of uninterrupted anticoagulation itself. Prospective studies with standardized procedural techniques and devices are required.

In conclusion, these findings suggest that DOAC continuation may not substantially increase DPPB risk in selected patients undergoing EMR/HSP with high use of prophylactic clipping. However, prospective, adequately powered studies are required to confirm these findings and determine the optimal periprocedural DOAC management strategy.
