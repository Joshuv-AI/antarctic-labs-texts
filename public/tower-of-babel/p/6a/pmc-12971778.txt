Adipose-derived stem cell exosomes promote endometrial carcinoma progression via MAGED4B/CDH1/EMT axis
Authors: Yifen Li, Gangwei Wang, Jianwei Chen, Tao Xu, Xiaomao Li, Yuebo Yang, Yu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSCs-Exos) enhance endometrial carcinoma (EC) cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. Mechanistically, ADSCs-Exos downregulate MAGED4B and CDH1, reduce E-cadherin expression, and upregulate vimentin, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Overexpression of MAGED4B reverses these effects and inhibits malignant behaviors. Furthermore, ADSCs-Exos increase organoid viability and confirm key protein changes. These findings demonstrate that ADSCs-Exos promote EC progression via the MAGED4B/CDH1/EMT axis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10735-026-10757-8.Keywords: Endometrial carcinoma, Exosomes, Adipose derived mesenchymal stem cells, Organoid, MAGED4B, CDH1, E-cadherin, SMAD4, EMT

Introduction

Endometrial cancer (EC) ranks as the sixth most common cancer in women globally (Bray et al. 2024), with incidence rising rapidly, especially in developed nations (Lortet-Tieulent et al. 2018). It comprises heterogeneous epithelial malignancies of the uterine endometrium, primarily affecting perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. Recognized risk factors include elevated estrogen levels, obesity, and diabetes.

Adipose-derived stem cells (ADSCs), a mesenchymal stem cell subset in adipose tissue, are increasingly used in cell therapy and regenerative medicine. They also influence tumor proliferation, invasion, and migration (Bassi et al. 2012, Gonzalez et al. 2009, Gonzalez et al. 2009, Grayson et al. 2015, Mizuno et al. 2012, Li et al. 2019), largely through paracrine factors like exosomes.

Exosomes are nanovesicles derived from multivesicular endosomes, carrying proteins, lipids, and RNAs alongside markers such as TSG101, CD9, CD63, and CD81. They facilitate intercellular communication, impacting physiological and pathological processes (Li et al. 2019, Zhang et al. 2018, Han et al. 2019). Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) significantly produce exosomes, which extend MSCs’ roles in bioenergetics, proliferation, and immunomodulation via paracrine/endocrine actions. ADSCs-derived exosomes (ADSCs-Exos) retain ADSCs’ biological traits: secreting cytokines, regulating immune and inflammatory responses (Zhou et al. 2021, Blazquez et al. 2014, Zhao et al. 2018), promoting neurogenesis and angiogenesis (Chen et al. 2019, Li 2022), and affecting cancer proliferation, invasion, and apoptosis (Hong et al. 2019).

Although “organoid” appeared in the 1980 s, the field advanced significantly in 2009 with intestinal organoids from LGR5 + stem cells (Sato et al. 2009). Organoids are 3D microtissues from adult or pluripotent stem cells that replicate native organ structure and function, overcoming limitations of 2D cultures like absent cell-cell/ECM interactions. They restore physiological processes including filtration, excretion, and neural connectivity. Revolutionizing cancer modeling, patient-derived organoids (PDOs) preserve tumor histopathological and molecular features, proving invaluable for drug screening and personalized medicine. Compared to murine models, PDOs provide superior throughput, cost-effectiveness, and clinical translatability, gaining policy support in Europe and the United States as leading alternatives to animal models.

Consequently, we investigated ADSCs-Exos in regulating EC cell behaviors and underlying mechanisms. Using endometrial cancer PDOs, we examined ADSCs-Exos’ tumor-promoting effects, offering a human-relevant platform bridging in vitro and in vivo studies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C. Experiments were conducted on cells cultured between passages 3 and 20. Human endometrial adenocarcinoma cells(ISK)were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. ADSCs-Exos were provided by Shenzhen HUAQINGZHIMEI Bio Co., Ltd.

Identification of ADSCs-Exos

The morphology of ADSCs-Exos was observed using a transmission electron microscope. Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was employed to determine the size and distribution of ADSCs-Exos. Additionally, Western blot analysis was conducted to detect the expression of exosomal protein markers, including CD63, CD81, and TSG101.The ADSCs-Exos used by us have been extracted and identified in previous studies (Li et al. 2025). The results demonstrated that ADSCs-Exos from the same donor exhibited a concentration of 3.1 × 10¹¹ particles/mL.The average size of ADSCs-Exos was approximately 139.7 nm with a concentration of 3.1 × 1011 particles per ml.

Cell proliferation

For the cell proliferation assay, ISK cells were divided into an experimental group and a control group. Cells were plated in 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells/well. The experimental group was cultured with 0.5 mL of RPMI-1640 containing ADSCs-Exos at a concentration of 5 × 107particles/mL. The control group received an equal volume of RPMI-1640 without ADSCs-Exos. After 24 h, cell activity was assessed using the CCK8 colorimetric assay. Specifically, 10 µL of CCK8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for two hours. Absorbance was then measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader. A growth curve was generated based on the optical density (OD) values.

Exosome uptake analysis

An appropriate amount of ADSCs-Exos was diluted with PBS to a total volume of 500 µL. A DI working solution was prepared by mixing the ADSCs-Exos solution (1:100) and incubating at 37 °C for 30 min. The working solution was then added to the culture medium, and the reaction was allowed to proceed in the dark for 12–24 h. The cells were fixed and stained with either cytoskeleton markers or DAPI. Subsequently, iFluor™ 488-labeled phalloidin staining was performed. Prior to first use, the phalloidin was thoroughly thawed, centrifuged at low speed for 1 min, aliquoted, and stored at −20 °C in the dark. Before the experiment, iFluor™ 488-labeled phalloidin was mixed with PBS containing 1% BSA (1:200-1:500) to obtain a 1x phalloidin staining working solution, which should be prepared immediately before use.

For the staining procedure, well-cultured cells on coverslips were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, followed by two additional washes with PBS for 5 min each. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 5 min and washed twice with PBS for 5 min each. A 100 µL aliquot of the freshly prepared phalloidin staining working solution was used to completely cover the cells, and the cells were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30–120 min. The addition of 1% BSA to the phalloidin staining working solution could effectively reduce the background staining. The cells were then washed 2–3 times with 1x PBS buffer for 5 min each, and the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 3–5 min. The cells were washed 2–3 times with PBS for 30–60 s each, mounted, and imaged.

In vitro wound healing

Cells were plated to achieve 90–100% confluence. A homogeneous scratch wound was created within each well. Following wound creation, ISK cells were rinsed with PBS. ADSCs-Exos(at a concentration of 2 × 109 particles/mL) were then added to each experimental group individually. The control group received an equal volume of serum-free media. Images were captured every 12 h, and each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted using radioimmunoprecipitation lysis buffer, followed by separate quantification using a BioRad kit. Microgram quantities of protein lysates were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred electrophoretically to a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. The polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk at 37 °C for 1 h. The membranes were then immunoblotted with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the respective secondary antibodies for 4 h. The bands were detected using an ECL kit.

Migration and invasion assays

Cell migration and invasion were assessed using Transwell Permeable Supports with a pore size of 8.0 μm. Transwell migration and invasion assays were conducted in 24-well chambers with inserts precoated without (for migration) or with (for invasion) Matrigel. Cells suspended in serum-free medium were seeded onto the upper chambers of the Transwell inserts. Medium containing 10% FBS was placed in the lower chambers. After 24 h of incubation, non-migrating or non-invading cells in the upper chambers were removed using a cotton swab. Migrating and invading cells on the opposite side of the filter were fixed with cold ethanol, stained with crystal violet solution, and counted in five random fields at ×20 original magnification.

Transcriptome sequencing and UALCAN database evaluation

Total RNA was extracted from ISK cells with or without ADSCs-Exosome treatment using TRIzol Reagent. The quality and concentration of RNA samples were analyzed using the Agilent 2100 RNA nano 6000 assay kit. mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads attached to oligos. The first and second strands of cDNA were then synthesized and purified using a QIAquick PCR Purification Kit. The purified double-stranded cDNA was amplified, and the cyclization product was qualified. Qualified cyclized products were attached to DNB, and the constructed DNB was loaded onto an MGI platform sequencer for sequencing using the PE150 sequencing strategy.

The information analysis process included sequencing data quality control, data comparison analysis, and in-depth transcriptome analysis. Differentially expressed genes were annotated based on Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis using KOBAS software. Whole transcriptome sequencing depth analysis and personalized analysis were conducted at Huayin Bio Ltd. (Jinan, China).Subsequently, the UALCAN database was used to search for target genes. The UALCAN website (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/) is capable of analyzing cancer transcriptome, proteomics, and patient survival data. The data in UALCAN are derived from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) project. We evaluated the expression of related genes in 35 normal individuals and 546 EC patients and analyzed the correlation between gene expression levels and survival days in EC patients.Additionally, to infer the potential functions of the differentially expressed miRNAs, the miRNA gene symbols were submitted to the web-based tool DAVID for Reactome pathway enrichment analysis.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from each groups using Trizol reagent respectively. The concentration and purity of total RNA were detected using a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was preformed to synthesize cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix PCR. The cDNA was used as the template to amplify targeted sequences with specific primers. The sequences of the MAGED4B, CDH1,SMAD 4 and GADPH primers were shown in Table 1. Power SYBR Green PCR Master Mix was used for qRT-PCR with an LightCycler® 480 system.The results were analyzed by the 2 − ΔΔCT method.

Lentivirus infection

ISK cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Following overnight culture, cells were infected with the empty lentivirus (titer 1.35 × 109TU/mL; Multiple of Infection = 20) and the lentivirus overexpressing MAGED4B (titer 2.23 × 109TU/mL; Multiple of Infection = 20). After infection for 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with a complete medium, and cells were cultured for another 60 h before collection. The overexpression of MAGED4B was validated with qRT-PCR and western blot.

Subculture of endometrial cancer organoids

The pre-established and characterized organoids was used for functional validation (Li et al. 2025). Organoids were passaged when reaching 100–200 μm in diameter, with visible darkening due to nutrient depletion and rapid medium acidification (evidenced by yellowing). The medium was carefully aspirated without disturbing Matrigel® domes, followed by addition of 1 mL TrypLE™ per well to dissociate the matrix. Mechanical disruption using a 1-mL pipette tip and room-temperature incubation (3–5 min) yielded cell clusters (2–3 cells/cluster, < 20 μm diameter). The digested organoids were transferred to 15-mL conical tubes with 2–3 volumes of DMEM/F12 to neutralize enzymatic activity, followed by centrifugation (1,200 rpm, 5 min) and supernatant removal. Pelleted cells were resuspended in organoid medium mixed with fresh Matrigel® at split ratios of 1:3 for healthy organoids or 1:1 for compromised viability.

HE staining was performed on the established organoids. The results demonstrated that both the endometrial cancer tissues and their derived organoids contained a large population of tumor cells characterized by large nuclei and intense eosinophilic staining. The organoids appeared as spherical aggregates composed of tumor cells, with a semi-hollow or hollow central structure. Consistent expression of ER, PR, and P53 was observed in both the endometrial cancer lesions and the corresponding organoids.

Co-culture of ADSCs-Exos with endometrial cancer organoids

For ADSCs-Exos co-culture experiments, organoids were divided into two groups: experimental (1.0 × 10⁹ particles/mL ADSCs-Exos) and control (untreated). Morphological documentation and Alamar Blue® OD₅₇₀/₆₀₀ nm measurements were conducted at days 0, 3, 6, and 9. Organoids were enzymatically dissociated to single cells using TrypLE™, counted, and standardized to 14 × 10⁴ cells in 70 µL medium. Cell-Matrigel® mixtures (1:3 ratio, 500 cells/µL) were plated as 10-µL droplets. Post-solidification, 100 µL culture medium was added per well. Viability assessments involved 10 µL Alamar Blue® incubation (5 h) followed by OD measurements and PBS washes (5×, 2 min/wash). Proliferation analysis integrated morphological changes and OD values, with subsequent single-cell preparations for WB and qRT-PCR using established protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and graphical representation of the experimental results were conducted using SPSS 26.0, Image J, and Graphpad Prism 9.0 software. Each experiment was repeated three times independently. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by using one-way and repeated-measures ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

Endometrial carcinoma (EC) ranks among the three most common gynecological malignancies. Patients with EC often present with obesity, diabetes, and hypertension—a cluster known as the “endometrial carcinoma triad” (National Health Commission of the People’s Republic of China 2020).

Adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSCs-Exos) are small bioactive vesicles secreted by ADSCs, enriched with diverse biological molecules. They play extensive roles in intercellular communication and signal transduction, modulating immune responses, promoting proliferation and migration, and influencing angiogenesis and neurogenesis. Owing to their clinical potential in tissue regeneration, immune regulation, and oncology, ADSCs-Exos have garnered increasing attention. In gynecology, studies have explored the effects of ADSCs-Exos on cervical, endometrial, and ovarian cancers. On one hand, they can inhibit proliferation, wound healing, and colony formation in ovarian cancer cells by upregulating pro-apoptotic signals such as BAX, CASP9, and CASP3, while downregulating BCL2 to induce apoptosis; their carried microRNAs also exhibit anti-tumor activity by targeting various molecules (Reza et al. 2016). On the other hand, exosomes derived from omental adipose tissue enhance the growth, migration, and invasion of ovarian cancer cells through mechanisms involving FOXM1, Cyclin F, KIF20A, and the MAPK signaling pathway (Qu et al. 2022), or confer paclitaxel resistance via microRNA-21 targeting APAF1 (Au et al. 2016). In cervical cancer, ADSCs-Exos also demonstrate certain therapeutic potential (Molly et al. 2022).

Research on ADSCs-Exos in endometrial cancer remains relatively limited. One study showed that they promote proliferation and inhibit apoptosis in HEC-251 cells, potentially via the TGF-β/Smad pathway (Wang Mingshuang et al. 2019). In our previous study, we employed the JEC cell line as a model and co-cultured it with adipose-derived stem cell exosomes (ADSCs-Exos). We found that ADSCs-Exos significantly enhanced the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of JEC cells (Li et al. 2025). The JEC cell line, derived from moderately differentiated human endometrial adenocarcinoma, is negative for both ER and PR. Further investigation using the ISK cell line is expected to deepen our understanding of how ADSCs-Exos modulate the proliferation, differentiation, and metastasis of endometrial cancer cells with varying degrees of differentiation and distinct ER/PR expression profiles. This will also provide new insights for the prevention and treatment of endometrial carcinoma. Therefore, in this study, we utilized the ISK cell line — a well-differentiated endometrial adenocarcinoma model expressing estrogen and progesterone receptors (Nishida 2002)—and demonstrated through CCK-8, wound healing, and invasion assays that ADSCs-Exos enhance the proliferation, migration, and invasion of ISK cells. Although the 24-hour OD value was lower in the ISK+ADSCs-Exos group than in the ISK group in the CCK-8 assay, we hypothesize that the internalization of ADSCs-Exos by ISK cells requires 24–48 h, and that ADSCs-Exos exert their effects rapidly following this process.

Analysis of data from 546 EC patients via ULCAN revealed significant differential expression of MAGED4B compared to normal controls, with some correlation to patient body weight. Reactome pathway analysis indicated enrichment in pathways related to viral infections (e.g., SARS-CoV-2, HIV), regulated cell death (e.g., pyroptosis, necrosis, autophagy), and nuclear processes, suggesting involvement in cell cycle regulation, stress response, and developmental mechanisms.

This study further demonstrates that ADSCs-Exos may alter the malignant behavior of ISK cells by modulating MAGED4B levels. As a member of the melanoma-associated antigen family, MAGED4B shows tissue-specific expression limited to the brain and ovaries under normal conditions, but is highly expressed in various malignancies—particularly glioblastomas—where it correlates with tumor progression and poor prognosis (Liu et al. 2023, Chong et al. 2012, Luo et al. 2023, Oya et al. 2015, Germano et al. 2012). However, its expression in endometrial cancer had not been previously reported.

Overexpression of MAGED4B reduced the migration and invasion capabilities of ISK cells, but these effects were reversed upon co-culture with ADSCs-Exos, potentially implicating epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Subsequent qRT-PCR and Western blot analyses revealed that ADSCs-Exos downregulated E-cadherin and SMAD4 protein expression (with statistically significant differences), while MAGED4B overexpression restored the expression of CDH1, E-cadherin, and SMAD4 that had been suppressed by ADSCs-Exos. These findings align with previous studies indicating that CDH1, E-cadherin, and SMAD4 are closely associated with endometrial cancer susceptibility and malignant behaviors (Chen et al. 2021, Geng et al. 2018, Chen Jing 2021, Zhao Yufeng et al. 2019, Liu Yunfei 2020, Lu Yinghua 2012, Park et al. 2014, Zhou et al. 2017, Li and Liu 2022).

In an endometrial cancer organoid model, the group co-cultured with ADSCs-Exos exhibited significantly higher cell counts than the control group (P < 0.05) and attenuated apoptosis. Molecular analysis showed downregulation of MAGED4B, CDH1, and SMAD4 gene expression along with reduced E-cadherin and SMAD4 protein levels, while vimentin expression was markedly increased (P < 0.05) in the ADSCs-Exos-treated group. These results indicate that ADSCs-Exos promote endometrial cancer organoid proliferation by suppressing MAGED4B expression, modulating the CDH1/SMAD4 signaling axis, and inducing EMT via E-cadherin downregulation and vimentin upregulation.

Based on these findings, we propose that ADSCs-Exos may downregulate MAGED4B, inhibit CDH1 expression through the SMAD4 pathway, reduce E-cadherin levels, and promote EMT, thereby driving endometrial cancer progression. Overexpression of MAGED4B may represent a novel therapeutic target. However, although MAGED4B overexpression restored CDH1 and SMAD4 gene expression, E-cadherin and SMAD4 protein levels remained decreased. Survival analysis indicated that EC patients with high MAGED4B expression had shorter survival times, which may be related to stage-specific roles of EMT (facilitating tumor cell dissemination from the primary site) and MET (promoting metastatic colonization) (Somarelli et al. 2016).The tumor microenvironment (TME) is also a key factor and may account for the observed discrepancy. Further investigations using organoid models are warranted to clarify this issue.

This study establishes a foundation for understanding the role of ADSCs-Exos in promoting the regeneration of endometrial adenocarcinoma cells in vitro, while also highlighting their potential tumor-promoting risks in clinical applications. Future research should focus on functional validation and in vivo experiments to clarify the precise mechanisms by which ADSCs-Exos influence the initiation and progression of EC. Moreover, given the hormone-sensitive nature of endometrial cancer, subsequent studies should incorporate hormonal conditions—particularly estrogen exposure—to better simulate the tumor microenvironment.

To conclude, ADSCs-Exos promote endometrial cancer cell malignancy via the MAGED4B/SMAD/EMT axis, an effect reversed by MAGED4B overexpression, suggesting a therapeutic target. However, the pro-tumor risks of ADSCs-Exos demand caution and further mechanistic investigation before clinical application.
