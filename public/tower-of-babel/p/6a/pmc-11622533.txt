The receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase OsBSK1‐2 regulates immunity via an HLH/bHLH complex
Authors: Xun Wang, Zhijuan Diao, Chang Cao, Yan Liu, Na Xia, Youlian Zhang, Ling Lu, Fanyu Kong, Houli Zhou, Lizhe Chen, Jing Zhang, Bangsheng Wang, Ronghua Huang, Dingzhong Tang, Shengping Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlants need to fine‐tune defense responses to maintain a robust but flexible host barrier to various pathogens. Helix‐loop‐helix/basic helix‐loop‐helix (HLH/bHLH) complexes play important roles in fine‐tuning plant development. However, the function of these genes in plant immunity and how they are regulated remain obscure. Here, we identified an atypical bHLH transcription factor, Oryza sativa (Os)HLH46, that interacts with rice receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinase (RLCK) Os BRASSINOSTEROID‐SIGNALING KINASE1‐2 (OsBSK1‐2), which plays a key role in rice blast resistance. OsBSK1‐2 stabilized OsHLH46 both in vivo and in vitro. In addition, OsHLH46 positively regulates rice blast resistance, which depends on OsBSK1‐2. OsHLH46 has no transcriptional activation activity and interacts with a typical bHLH protein, OsbHLH6, which negatively regulates rice blast resistance. OsbHLH6 binds to the promoter of OsWRKY45 and inhibits its expression, while OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by blocking its DNA binding and transcriptional inhibition of OsWRKY45. Consistent with these findings, OsWRKY45 was up‐regulated in OsHLH46‐overexpressing plants. In addition, the oshlh46 mutant overexpressing OsbHLH6 is more susceptible to Magnaporthe oryzae than is the wild type, suggesting that OsHLH46 suppresses OsbHLH6‐mediated rice blast resistance. Our results not only demonstrated that OsBSK1‐2 regulates rice blast resistance via the OsHLH46/OsbHLH6 complex, but also uncovered a new mechanism for plants to fine‐tune plant immunity by regulating the HLH/bHLH complex via RLCKs.

INTRODUCTION

Exposed to the threat of various pathogens, plants have evolved a two‐layered immune system: pattern‐triggered immunity (PTI) and effector‐triggered immunity (ETI) (Chang et al., 2022). In PTI, pathogen/microbe‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage‐associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) are recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), and immune responses are subsequently delivered to downstream signaling pathways by receptor‐like cytoplasmic kinases (RLCKs) (Macho and Zipfel, 2014; Tang et al., 2017). A number of RLCKs have been reported to function as central components linking PRRs and downstream defense responses. For instance, in Arabidopsis, BOTRYTIS‐INDUCED KINASE1 (BIK1) receives immune signals from FLAGELLIN SENSITIVE2 (FLS2) and BAK1 and activates downstream signaling (Lu et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2010). PBL27 acts as a linker between the cell surface chitin receptor CERK1 and the intracellular mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK) cascade (Yamada et al., 2016). In rice, RLCK185 and RLCK176 are two important defense‐related proteins that function downstream of OsCERK1 in the peptidoglycan and chitin signaling pathways (Yamaguchi et al., 2013; Ao et al., 2014).

BRASSINOSTEROID‐SIGNALING KINASE1 (BSK1) is a member of the RLCK XII subfamily and was previously shown to be a substrate of brassinosteroid insensitive 1 (BRI1) (Tang et al., 2008). BSK1 interacts with FLS2 and regulates flg22‐induced immune responses (Shi et al., 2013). Further studies showed that MAPKKK5 acts as a phosphorylation substrate of BSK1, and phosphorylation of the Ser‐289 residue of MAPKKK5 by BSK1 is critical for MAPKKK5‐mediated resistance to both bacterial and fungal pathogens (Yan et al., 2018). Moreover, RLK902 transmits immune signals by phosphorylating BSK1, and the phosphorylation of Ser‐230 in BSK1 by RLK902 is critical for RLK902‐mediated defense signaling (Zhao et al., 2019). Recently, MPK15 was found to be involved in BSK1‐mediated fungal resistance, and BSK1 enhances fungal resistance in part by enhancing the phosphorylation of MPK15 at Ser‐511 (Shi et al., 2022). In addition, dynamic spatial reorganization of BSK1 within the plasma membrane contributes to balancing plant growth and immunity (Su et al., 2021). In rice, there are two homologs of BSK1, OsBSK1‐1 and OsBSK1‐2, and OsBSK1‐2 was found to function in the regulation of rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2017b). However, the molecular mechanism of OsBSK1‐2 is still unknown.

The basic helix‐loop‐helix (bHLH) family of transcription factors is one of the largest transcription factor gene families in plants and is involved in the regulation of plant growth, development and abiotic stress responses (Yi et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2014; Zhang et al., 2015; Sharma et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2019b; Martín‐Barranco et al., 2020). Moreover, emerging evidence has shown that bHLH transcription factors also play important roles in plant immunity regulation. For instance, the bHLH transcription factor GmPIB1 enhances the resistance of soybean to Phytophthora sojae by repressing the expression of GmSPOD1 (Cheng et al., 2018). Nrd1 negatively regulates plant resistance to Pseudomonas syringae pv. tomato (Pst) DC3000 in tomato (Zhang et al., 2022). The jasmonate‐induced bHLH protein SlJIG forms a defense module with MYC2 and functions in resistance against the cotton bollworm and Botrytis cinerea (Cao et al., 2022). In rice, OsbHLH6 negatively controls disease resistance in Magnaporthe oryzae by regulating salicylic acid (SA) and jasmonic acid (JA) signaling (Meng et al., 2020). HLH proteins, a class of atypical transcription factors lacking proper DNA‐binding domains, are not expected to directly bind to DNA and usually function as dominant‐negative regulators of other bHLH proteins. For example, the HLH protein PAR1 regulates cell elongation and plant development in response to light and hormones by inhibiting the function of the bHLH transcription factors PRE1 and PIF4, respectively, in Arabidopsis (Hao et al., 2012). The HLH protein GhFP2 in cotton hinders fiber elongation by suppressing the transcriptional activation of GhACE1, a bHLH protein that interacts with GhFP2 and positively regulates fiber elongation (Lu et al., 2022b). PRE1 and ILI1 mediate brassinosteroid regulation of plant development by repressing the function of IBH1 in Arabidopsis and rice, respectively (Zhang et al., 2009).

To explore the molecular mechanism of OsBSK1‐2‐mediated immunity, yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening was used to identify proteins that interact with OsBSK1‐2 (Li et al., 2024). In total, 40 positive clones were obtained, one of which was an atypical bHLH transcription factor and was named OsHLH46. Here, we found that OsBSK1‐2 affects the stability of OsHLH46 both in vitro and in vivo and that OsHLH46 functions in OsBSK1‐2‐mediated rice blast resistance. In addition, OsHLH46 interacted with and inhibited the function of OsbHLH6, a negative regulator of rice blast resistance previously reported, by suppressing the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on OsWRKY45. In conclusion, we demonstrated the important role of the complex OsHLH46/OsbHLH6 in OsBSK1‐2‐mediated resistance to M. oryzae in rice.

RESULTS

OsBSK1‐2 directly interacted with OsHLH46

BSK1 plays important roles in both Arabidopsis and rice immune regulation (Shi et al., 2013, 2022; Wang et al., 2017b). However, the functional mechanism of OsBSK1‐2 has not been fully elucidated. To explore the key component involved in OsBSK1‐2‐mediated resistance, a Y2H screen was performed, and a predicted bHLH transcription factor (Figure S1), OsHLH46, was identified. First, a Y2H assay was used to verify the interaction between OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46. The full‐length coding sequences (CDSs) of OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46 were cloned and inserted into pGBKT7 (BD) and pGADT7 (AD), respectively, and co‐transformed into the yeast strain AH109. The yeast clones contained OsBSK1‐2‐BD and OsHLH46‐AD, and the positive control could grow on synthetic defined (SD)/‐Leu‐Trp‐His‐Ade but not the negative control (Figure 1A). A co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assay was subsequently used to confirm the interaction between the two proteins. As shown in Figure 1B, OsBSK1‐2 co‐immunoprecipitated with the OsHLH46‐GFP (green fluorescent protein) fusion protein but not with GFP. Furthermore, the interaction between OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46 was detected via a pull‐down assay (Figure 1C). In addition, a split‐luciferase complementation imaging (LCI) assay was performed, and the results showed that a luminescent signal could be detected when OsBSK1‐2 was co‐expressed with OsHLH46 but not when OsBSK1 was the negative control (Figure 1D). Finally, the bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay showed that OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46 interact on the plasma membrane (Figure 1E).

Knocking out OsHLH46 compromises rice blast resistance

To determine whether OsHLH46 was involved in rice blast resistance, we constructed oshlh46 mutants using the clustered regularly interspaced small palindromic repeats (CRISPR)/CRISPR‐associated protein 9 (Cas9) method with two different single guide RNAs (sgRNAs). As shown in Figure S2, the oshlh46‐1 mutant had a frameshift mutation caused by a 1‐bp insertion, while the oshlh46‐2 mutant had a frameshift mutation caused by a 2‐bp deletion. The growth and development of the oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 plants were not significantly different from those of the wild‐type plants (Figure S3). To explore the function of OsHLH46 in rice blast resistance, 3‐week‐old Zhongua 11 (ZH11), oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 plants were sprayed with the M. oryzae isolated strain Guy11. Three d after M. oryzae infection, oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 presented more disease lesions and fungal biomass than did the wild type (Figure 2A, B). Consistent with these findings, the expression of the pathogenesis‐related genes OsPR1a, OsPR6, OsPR1b, and OsPR10 decreased in oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 mutants compared to that in the wild type (Figure 2C–F). To further validate the results, 1‐month‐old ZH11, oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 plants were inoculated with the M. oryzae isolated strain Guy11 using the punch method. Seven d after M. oryzae inoculation, the lesion areas of the oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 mutants were larger than that of the wild type (Figure 2G), and the relative fungal growth in diseased oshlh46‐1 and oshlh46‐2 leaves was greater than that in the wild type (Figure 2H).

Overexpression of OsHLH46 increased rice blast resistance

To further explore the function of OsHLH46 in rice blast resistance, the OsHLH46‐HA fusion protein driven by the 35S promoter was expressed in ZH11 to produce OsHLH46 overexpression (OsHLH46‐OE) plants. The expression of the OsHLH46‐HA (hemagglutinin) fusion protein in the OsHLH46‐OE plants was detected by immunoblotting, and we found that the OsHLH46‐HA fusion protein was expressed normally in the OsHLH46‐OE plants (Figure 2I). Then the OsHLH46 expression level in the OsHLH46‐OE plants was examined using quantitative real‐time polymerase chain reaction (qRT‐PCR), and as shown in Figure 2J, the expression level of OsHLH46 in the OsHLH46‐OE plants was dozens of times higher than that in the wild type. And, as shown in Figure S5, OsHLH46‐OE plants had no significant difference in growth compared to that of the wild type. When ZH11 and the two OsHLH46‐OE plants grew to 1 month of age, they were inoculated with the M. oryzae isolated strain Guy11 by the punch method. Seven d post‐M. oryzae inoculation, the lesion length of the two OsHLH46‐OE lines was significantly shorter than that of the wild‐type ZH11 (Figure 2K). Consistent with these findings, the relative fungal growth in the diseased leaves of the OsHLH46‐OE plants was also lower than that in the wild type (Figure 2L).

OsHLH46 is an atypical bHLH transcription factor with no transcriptional activation activity

Proteins containing a bHLH domain usually act as homo or heterodimers to regulate the expression of their target genes (Hao et al., 2021). Therefore, we first examined whether OsHLH46 can form a homodimer. Y2H and LCI assays both showed that OsHLH46 can interact with itself (Figure 3A, B). The subcellular localization of OsHLH46 was subsequently determined. OsHLH46‐GFP and nuclear localization sequence – red fluorescent protein (NLS‐RFP) were co‐expressed in rice protoplasts, while the GFP was used as the control. The results showed that OsHLH46‐GFP can be observed mainly in the nucleus and on the cell membrane, while a single GFP was expressed throughout the whole cell (Figure 3C). To further validate the cellular localization of OsHLH46, we extracted the membrane proteins and nuclear proteins of the OsHLH46‐OE plants and detected the OsHLH46‐HA fusion protein in the two extracts using immunoblotting with an anti‐HA antibody, while H+‐ATPase was used as a membrane marker and histone was used as a nuclear marker. As shown in Figure 3D, OsHLH46 can be detected among both the membrane proteins and nuclear proteins, which is consistent with the subcellular localization of OsHLH46. Moreover, by amino acid sequence alignment, we found that the amino acid in the basic domain of OsHLH46 is not conserved compared to that in other bHLH proteins (Figure 3E), indicating that OsHLH46 may not bind to DNA. To determine the transcriptional activity of OsHLH46, full‐length OsHLH46 was fused with GAL4‐BD to generate an effector and co‐expressed with the reporter, a luciferase gene under an upstream activating sequence (UAS) driven by the 35S promoter, in rice protoplasts, while VP16 was used as the positive control, and GAL4‐BD was used as the negative control. As shown in Figure 3F, VP16, but not the negative control, had significant transcriptional activity, as did OsHLH46. These results suggest that OsHLH46 is an atypical bHLH transcription factor.

OsBSK1‐2 affects the function of OsHLH46 by stabilizing it

RLCKs, such as BSK1 and BIK1, generally transmit immune signals by phosphorylating substrates to regulate their functions (Wang et al., 2017a; Yan et al., 2018; Bai et al., 2023). However, our previous study showed that the autophosphorylation activity of OsBSK1‐2 could not be detected in vitro (Li et al., 2024). Therefore, we could not determine whether OsBSK1‐2 can phosphorylate OsHLH46. Studies have proven that maintaining a certain protein level is crucial for the function of immune‐related proteins (Zhai et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022a; Liang et al., 2023). Therefore, we examined whether OsBSK1‐2 affects the stability of OsHLH46. First, the OsHLH46‐His fusion protein was expressed in Escherichia coli and purified, and then, equal amounts of the OsHLH46‐His protein were incubated with total proteins from ZH11 and the osbsk1‐2 mutant. Samples were collected at the indicated times and detected by immunoblotting with an anti‐His antibody. The results showed that OsHLH46 degraded faster when incubated with the total extract from osbsk1‐2 than when it was incubated with the total extract from the wild type (Figure 4A). To further verify these results, the yellow fluorescent protein (YFP)‐3HA and OsBSK1‐2‐HA fusion proteins were expressed in rice protoplasts and enriched with HA beads. Then, equal amounts of the OsHLH46‐His fusion protein were incubated with YFP‐3HA or OsBSK1‐2‐HA. As shown in Figure 4B, OsHLH46 was more stable when incubated with OsBSK1‐2 than when incubated with YFP. Moreover, we performed a protein degradation assay with MG132, a 26S proteasome inhibitor and cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor in rice protoplasts. The results showed that when MG132 or CHX was added, OsHLH46‐GFP was more stable when co‐expressed with OsBSK1‐2‐HA than when it was co‐expressed with 3HA‐YFP (Figure S6), which is consistent with the results above. However, although MG132 was added, OsHLH46‐GFP accumulated at higher levels when co‐expressed with OsBSK1‐2‐HA than with 3HA‐YFP, indicating that the degradation of OsHLH46 partly depended on the 26S proteasome but also other degradation pathways.

To verify that OsBSK1‐2 can stabilize OsHLH46 in vivo, we crossed the osbsk1‐2 mutant with the OsHLH46‐OE plant to generate OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 plants. Then, the OsHLH46‐HA protein levels in the OsHLH46‐OE/ZH11 plants and OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 plants were detected by immunoblotting with an HA antibody. We found that the OsHLH46‐HA protein in the OsHLH46‐OE/ZH11 plants was expressed at a relatively high level compared to that in the OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 plants (Figure 4C). Meanwhile, we examined the copy numbers of the OsHLH46‐HA transgenes in the OsHLH46‐OE/ZH11 and OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 F2 plants, and the copy numbers of the OsHLH46‐HA transgenes were similar in each of the F2 plants (Figure S7), indicating that the differences in protein levels in the plants were not caused by differences in copy numbers of the OsHLH46‐HA transgenes. In addition, to determine whether OsBSK1‐2 was involved in the resistance mediated by OsHLH46, we compared the resistance of OsHLH46‐OE/ZH11 and OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 plants to M. oryzae and found that the rice blast resistance of OsHLH46‐OE/osbsk1‐2 was decreased compared to that of OsHLH46‐OE/ZH11 plants (Figure 4D, E), suggesting that the functions of OsHLH46 in rice blast resistance depend on OsBSK1‐2.

OsHLH46 interacts with OsbHLH6 directly in the nucleus

Previous studies have shown that atypical bHLH proteins lose DNA binding activity and usually function as repressors of typical bHLH transcription factors by forming heterodimers with them (Murre et al., 1989; Cheng et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2022b). There are approximately 183 bHLH proteins in rice (Wei and Chen, 2018); thus far, only OsbHLH6 has been reported to negatively regulate rice blast resistance (Meng et al., 2020) and knocking out of OsbHLH6 in ZH11 also increased rice blast resistance (Figure S8). We speculated that OsbHLH6 may be a partner of OsHLH46. To test this hypothesis, we first detected the interaction between OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46. A Y2H assay was performed, and we found that the yeast transformants harboring OsbHLH6‐AD and OsHLH46‐BD could grow on SD/‐Ade/‐His/‐Leu/‐Trp media but not the negative controls (Figure 5A), suggesting that OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46 can interact in yeast. A pull‐down assay was subsequently performed, and the results further verified the interaction between OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46 (Figure 5B). Moreover, we also used a Co‐IP assay to evaluate the interaction between OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46, and the results showed that OsbHLH6‐HA can Co‐IP with OsHLH46‐GFP but not with the GFP (Figure 5C). In addition, to determine the interaction site between OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46 in cells, we performed a BiFC assay. As shown in Figure 5D, OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46 interacted mainly in the nucleus. The results above suggested that OsbHLH6 and OsHLH46 interact directly in the nucleus.

OsbHLH6 regulates the expression of OsWRKY45 by directly binding to its promoter

A previous study showed that OsbHLH6 negatively regulates rice blast resistance, and OsWRKY45, an important immune regulator in rice (Shimono et al., 2012; Meng et al., 2020), is up‐regulated in the osbhlh6 mutant but down‐regulated in OsbHLH6‐OE plants (Meng et al., 2020), indicating that OsWRKY45 may be a target gene of OsbHLH6. To verify this hypothesis, we first investigated whether OsbHLH6 interacts with itself by Y2H and Co‐IP assays. As shown in Figure S9, OsbHLH6 could interact with itself, indicating that OsbHLH6 could form a homodimer. Then we analyzed the bHLH transcription factor binding motif on the 1,000 bp promoter of OsWRKY45 and found six E boxes in P1–P6 sequences (Figure 6A). A chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)‐qPCR assay was subsequently performed, and the results showed that OsbHLH6 can strongly bind to the sequence P3, which contains the third E box motif (E3) (Figure 6B). To further confirm the binding of OsbHLH6 to P3, we conducted an electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) using the bHLH domain of OsbHLH6, because the ACT (aspartate kinase, chorismite mutase, TyrA) domain has been reported to inhibit the transcriptional activity of bHLH transcription factors (Lee et al., 2023). As shown in Figure 6C, the bHLH domain of OsbHLH6 can bind to the probe containing E3 but not to the mutated probe. In addition, we also performed a yeast one‐hybrid assay and found that OsbHLH6 can bind the promoter of OsWRKY45 (Figure 6D). The results above suggest that OsbHLH6 regulates the expression of OsWRKY45 by directly binding to its promoter.

OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by inhibiting the transcriptional regulation of OsWRKY45

Our results above showed that OsHLH46 positively regulates rice blast resistance, while OsbHLH6 negatively regulates rice blast resistance (Meng et al., 2020), indicating that OsHLH46 may suppress the function of OsbHLH6. A domain swapping assay with OsHLH46 and OsbHLH6 was performed and the result revealed that the fusion protein of BD (binding domain) OsHLH46 + AD (activation domain) OsbHLH6 and BDOsbHLH6 + ADOsHLH46 could neither activate the expression of OsWRKY45, nor bind to the promoter of OsWRKY45 (Figure S10), which further suggested that OsHLH46 is not a typical transcription factor. Moreover, we found that the ACT domain of OsbHLH6 was both responsible for its homodimer formation and interaction with OsHLH46 (Figure S11), indicating that OsHLH46 may inhibit the formation of the OsbHLH6 homodimer by competitively binding to OsbHLH6, thus affecting the DNA‐binding activity of OsbHLH6. Therefore, we determined whether OsHLH46 can suppress the DNA binding activity of OsbHLH6 to the promoter OsWRKY45 using ChIP‐qPCR according to Hao et al. (2012) as previously reported. The results showed that when co‐expressed with OsHLH46, the binding of OsbHLH6 to the promoter of OsWRKY45 significantly decreased (Figure 7A, B). Moreover, we determined whether OsHLH46 can suppress the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6. As shown in Figure 7C, when co‐expressed with OsbHLH6 but not GFP, the activation of luciferase (LUC) driven by the promoter of OsWRKY45 was suppressed, indicating the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on OsWRKY45. However, when OsHLH46 was added, the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on OsWRKY45 was partly attenuated. Consistent with these findings, OsWRKY45 was up‐regulated in OsHLH46‐OE plants (Figure 7D). To determine whether OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6, we over‐expressed the OsbHLH6‐GFP fusion protein in both ZH11 and the oshlh46 mutant to generate OsbHLH6‐GFP/ZH11 and OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 plants. Then, ZH11, OsbHLH6‐GFP/ZH11 and OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 plants were inoculated with the M. oryzae isolated strain Guy11 by the spray method, and the results showed that OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 plants were more susceptible than were OsbHLH6‐GFP/ZH11 plants (Figure 7E–G), suggesting that OsHLH46 suppressed the function of OsbHLH6 in vivo. In addition, OsbHLH6 has been reported to be a JA signaling‐related gene (Koji et al., 2012; Meng et al., 2020). To determine whether OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46 are also associated with JA responses, we examined the expression level of OsJAR1, a marker gene in JA responses, in ZH11, OsHLH46‐OE, oshlh46 and osbsk1‐2 after MeJA treatment. As shown in Figure S12, the expression level of OsJAR1 was up‐regulated in the OsHLH46‐OE plants, while it was down‐regulated in osbsk1‐2 and oshlh46 mutants after methyl jasmonate (MeJA) treatment for 12 h, indicating that OsBSK1‐2 and OsHLH46 are also involved in JA responses.

DISCUSSION

Here, we showed a new function mode for the RLCK protein in which OsBSK1‐2 maintains rice immune homeostasis via an HLH/bHLH complex. We found that OsBSK1‐2 can interact with and stabilize OsHLH46, an atypical bHLH transcription factor (Figures 1, 3, 4). Knocking out OsHLH46 compromises rice blast resistance, and overexpressing OsHLH46 increases rice blast resistance (Figure 2), suggesting that OsHLH46 is a positive regulator of rice blast resistance. In addition, rice resistance mediated by OsHLH46 depends on the function of OsBSK1‐2 (Figure 4D). Furthermore, we found that OsHLH46 is located mainly in the nucleus and cell membrane and has no transcriptional activation activity (Figure 3). A typical bHLH protein, OsbHLH6, which negatively regulates rice blast resistance (Figure S6), was subsequently identified as the interacting protein of OsHLH46 (Figure 5), and OsbHLH6 can bind to the promoter of OsWRKY45 directly and regulate its expression (Figure 6). Moreover, our results showed that OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by inhibiting the transcriptional regulation of target genes, such as OsWRKY45, and that OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 plants were more susceptible than were OsbHLH6‐GFP/ZH11 plants (Figure 7). Therefore, our data demonstrated the important role of the OsBSK1‐2‐OsHLH46‐OsbHLH6 module in the regulation of rice blast resistance.

RLCKs function as central components in plant immunity, linking immune receptors to downstream defenses (Tang et al., 2017; Liang and Zhou, 2018). For example, Arabidopsis BIK1 and PBL1 integrate immune signaling from multiple PRRs, including FLS2, EFR, PEPRs, and CERK1 (Zhang et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2013; Tang et al., 2017). Rice RLCK185 and RLCK176, as well as RLCK107 and RLCK108, were reported to mediate rice defenses by interacting with OsCERK1 and XA21, respectively (Yamaguchi et al., 2013; Zhou et al., 2016). After receiving immune signals from PRRs, RLCKs regulate downstream defense responses via various immune‐related proteins. For instance, BIK1 regulates plant defense via RbohD‐mediated reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Kadota et al., 2014; Li et al., 2014), and WRKY transcription factors mediate JA and SA activation (Lal et al., 2018). OsRLCK185 mediates rice disease resistance by physically interacting with and phosphorylating OsCNGC9 to activate PAMP‐induced Ca2+ influx and ROS bursts, as well as PTI‐related defense gene transcription reprogramming (Wang et al., 2019a), and perceives chitin‐triggered immune signals from OsCERK1 to activate the MAPK cascade via OsMAPKKK18 and OsMAPKKKε (Yamada et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2017a).

BSK1 is an RLCK XII subfamily member and has been reported to be involved in immune regulation in both Arabidopsis and rice (Shi et al., 2013, 2022; Yan et al., 2018; Zhao et al., 2019). In Arabidopsis, BSK1 is involved in FLS2‐ and RLK902‐mediated resistance (Shi et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2019). Further studies showed that BSK1 regulates plant immunity by interacting with and phosphorylating MAPKKK5 (Yan et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2022) and affecting the phosphorylation of MPK15 (Shi et al., 2022), indicating the important roles of BSK1 in plant immunity. OsBSK1‐2, a homolog of BSK1 in rice, also plays an important role in rice blast resistance (Wang et al., 2017b); however, its functional mechanism remains obscure. In this study, we identified an atypical bHLH protein, OsHLH46, that interacts with OsBSK1‐2 (Figure 1). bHLH transcription factors have been reported to be important regulators of plant immunity. For example, the bHLH protein SlJIG forms a regulatory module with MYC2 to function in terpene biosynthesis and resistance in insects and fungi (Cao et al., 2022). GmPIB1, a bHLH transcription factor in soybean, enhances resistance to P. sojae by suppressing the expression of GmSPOD1 (Cheng et al., 2018). In rice, RAI1, a bHLH‐type transcriptional activator, functions as a PID3 interactor and is required for PID3‐mediated blast resistance (Zhou et al., 2019). Here, we found that OsHLH46 functions as a positive regulator of rice blast resistance (Figure 2), which is similar to the function of OsBSK1‐2. The increase in rice blast resistance resulting from the overexpression of OsHLH46 decreased in the osbsk1‐2 mutant (Figure 4E), suggesting that OsBSK1‐2 and OsbHLH46 function together and that the function of OsHLH46 depends on OsBSK1‐2. Most recently, we found that three MAPKKKs are involved in OsBSK1‐2‐mediated immunity (Li et al., 2024); therefore, whether other proteins are associated with OsBSK1‐2, such as the remaining proteins identified by yeast library screening, needs to be investigated in the future.

RLCKs commonly affect the function of their targets by phosphorylation. For instance, in Arabidopsis, BIK1 directly interacts with and phosphorylates RBOHD upon PAMP perception to promote ROS production (Kadota et al., 2014), and PBL27 phosphorylates MAPKKK5 to activate the MAPK cascade (Yamada et al., 2016). The RLCK protein CSK1 negatively regulates secondary cell wall (SCW) biosynthesis by phosphorylating VND6, thus reducing the transcriptional activation of a suite of SCW‐related genes (Cao et al., 2023). Due to the lack of detectable kinase activity of OsBSK1‐2 in vitro (Li et al., 2024), we could not determine whether OsBSK1‐2 can phosphorylate OsHLH46. Future work could include identifying the upstream receptor or receptor‐like kinases (RKs or RLKs) that can activate OsBSK1‐2 and detecting whether preactivated OsBSK1‐2 can phosphorylate OsHLH46. However, an increasing number of studies have shown that protein stability is crucial for immune protein function (Zhai et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2022a; Bai et al., 2023; Mou et al., 2023); therefore, we examined whether OsBSK1‐2 stabilizes OsHLH46 via multiple assays. Our results showed that OsBSK1‐2 can affect the stability of OsHLH46 both vitro and in vitro (Figure 4A–D), indicating that OsBSK1‐2 regulates rice blast resistance by interacting with and stabilizing OsHLH46. However, how OsBSK1‐2 affects the stability of OsHLH46 needs further investigation.

OsHLH46 has a bHLH domain and can form a homodimer (Figures 3A, B, S1). Nevertheless, the basic domain of OsHLH46 is not conserved compared to that of other typical bHLH transcription factors (Figure 3D). Furthermore, OsHLH46 had no detectable transcriptional activity (Figure 3E), indicating that OsHLH46 may function as an HLH protein. HLH proteins have been reported to function as repressors of typical bHLH transcription factors. For instance, the Arabidopsis HLH protein PAR1 interacts with the bHLH protein PIF4 and inhibits PIF4‐mediated gene expression, thus regulating plant development in response to light (Hao et al., 2012). Two antagonistic HLH/bHLH transcription factors, ILI1/OsIBH1 and PRE1/AtIBH1, mediate brassinosteroid‐induced cell elongation and plant development in rice and Arabidopsis, respectively (Zhang et al., 2009). A recent study showed that two HLH proteins, IBL1 and IBH1, interact with LP1 and LP2, two bHLH proteins, to disturb the transcriptional regulatory effects of LP1 and LP2 on LNG1 and LNG2, thereby mediating longitudinal cell elongation in Arabidopsis (Lu et al., 2021). Here, we found that OsbHLH6, a typical bHLH transcription factor, can interact with OsHLH46 (Figure 5). OsbHLH6 has been reported to function as a negative regulator of rice blast resistance (Meng et al., 2020), which is opposite to the role of OsHLH46, indicating that OsHLH46 may act as a repressor of OsbHLH6. To test this hypothesis, we first identified the target gene of OsbHLH6. A previous study showed that the important rice immunity regulator OsWRKY45 was up‐regulated in the osbhlh6 mutant and down‐regulated in OsbHLH6‐OE plants (Meng et al., 2020). Moreover, our ChIP‐qPCR, EMSA and yeast one‐hybrid assays showed that OsbHLH6 can bind to the promoter of OsWRKY45 (Figure 6), suggesting that OsWRKY45 is the target gene of OsbHLH6. Our further studies showed that OsbHLH6 transcriptionally inhibits OsWRKY45 and that OsHLH46 partly relieves this inhibition (Figure 7C), and OsWRKY45 was up‐regulated in OsHLH46‐OE plants (Figure 7D). Moreover, ChIP‐qPCR assays showed that OsHLH46 can block the binding of OsbHLH6 to the promoter of OsWRKY45 (Figure 7A). In addition, to prove that OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6, we compared rice blast resistance between OsbHLH6‐OE/ZH11 and OsbHLH6‐OE/oshlh46 and found that OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 was more susceptible than was OsbHLH6‐GFP/ZH11 (Figure 7E, F). Therefore, our results prove that OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 in rice blast resistance by blocking the binding activity and transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on its target genes. In addition, OsbHLH6 has been proven to control disease resistance by dynamically regulating SA and JA signaling via OsMYC2 and OsNPR1 (Meng et al., 2020). Therefore, it will be interesting to determine whether OsHLH46 is associated with OsMYC2 and OsNPR1, thereby regulating SA and JA signaling.

HLH/bHLH proteins have been reported to fine‐tune plant development (Zhang et al., 2009; Hao et al., 2012; Lu et al., 2022b), as well as biotic stress (Zhou et al., 2019). The HLH and bHLH proteins have opposite functions, and the HLH protein inhibits the function of the bHLH protein. However, how HLH/bHLH complexes are regulated remains unknown. Here, we characterized the RLCK protein OsBSK1‐2, which interacts with and stabilizes the HLH protein OsHLH46 (Figures 1, 4). Additionally, OsHLH46 forms an HLH/bHLH heterodimer with OsbHLH6 (Figure 5) and suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by inhibiting its DNA binding and transcription (Figure 7). Our results demonstrated that OsBSK1‐2 regulates rice immunity via the OsHLH46/OsbHLH6 complex, providing a new regulatory model for HLH/bHLH complexes via RLCKs.

In conclusion, we demonstrated the important roles of the OsHLH46‐OsbHLH6 complex in OsBSK1‐2‐mediated rice blast resistance. In wild‐type plants, OsBSK1‐2 interacts with and stabilizes OsHLH46, and OsHLH46 subsequently suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by blocking its DNA‐binding and transcription inhibition activity, thereby releasing the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on its target genes, such as OsWRKY45, which leads to relatively high resistance in wild‐type plants. However, in the osbsk1‐2 mutant, OsHLH46 is heavily degraded, and OsbHLH6 is released; thus, OsbHLH6 increases the transcription of some defense‐related genes, such as OsWRKY45, leading to decreased rice resistance (Figure 8).

Working model of the OsBSK1‐2‐OsHLH46/OsbHLH6 module in rice blast resistance

In wild‐type plants, OsBSK1‐2 interacts with and stabilizes OsHLH46, and OsHLH46 suppresses the function of OsbHLH6 by blocking its DNA binding and transcription inhibition activity, thereby relieving the transcriptional inhibition of OsbHLH6 on its target genes, such as OsWRKY45, which leads to relatively high resistance in the wild type. However, in the osbsk1‐2 mutant, OsHLH46 degrades and releases its inhibition of OsbHLH6; thus, OsbHLH6 reduces the transcription of some defense‐related genes, such as OsWRKY45, leading to decreased rice resistance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

The osbsk1‐2, oshlh46 and osbhlh6 mutants were constructed by the CRISPR/Cas9 method in the indica rice variety ZH11. The full‐length CDSs of OsHLH46 and OsbHLH6 were cloned and inserted into the pCXUN‐HA and pCAMBIA‐1300‐GFP vectors, respectively, and subsequently transformed into ZH11 to generate OsHLH46‐ and OsbHLH6‐overexpressing plants (OsHLH46‐OE and OsbHLH6‐OE). OsHLH46‐HA/osbsk1‐2 was generated by crossing OsHLH46‐HA‐OE plants and osbsk1‐2 mutants, and OsbHLH6‐GFP/oshlh46 was generated by crossing OsbHLH6‐GFP‐OE plants and oshlh46 mutants. All the rice plants were planted in the paddy field of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University and Science and Technology Backyard of Fujian Pucheng Rice Seed Industry in Fuzhou, Fujian Province, or in a greenhouse with 14 h of light and 10 h of darkness at 28°C.

Subcellular localization

The full‐length CDS of OsHLH46 was amplified by PCR using specific primers (Table S1) and inserted into the pCAMBIA2300‐35S‐GFP vector to generate the 35S‐OsHLH46‐GFP construct. The construct and GFP were subsequently co‐transformed with NLS‐RFP into rice protoplasts, and the fluorescence signals were observed using an LSM880 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany). The GFP and NLS‐RFP fusion proteins served as the control and nuclear marker, respectively.

Yeast one‐hybrid

A yeast one‐hybrid assay was performed as previously described (Lin et al., 2007). Briefly, the full‐length CDS of OsbHLH6 was amplified by PCR using specific primers (Table S1) and inserted into the pB42AD vector, and the fragment (−534 to −643) of the OsWRKY45 promoter (P3) was inserted into the pLacZi2μ vector. The two constructs were co‐transformed into the yeast strain EGY48. After 3 d, the positive clones were selected on SD/‐Trp‐Ura or SD/‐Trp‐Ura+X‐gal, and the results were observed and photographed.

Yeast two‐hybrid assay

A Y2H assay was performed as previously described (Zhao et al., 2015). The two indicated genes were cloned and inserted into pGADT7 (AD) and pGBKT7 (BD) strains, which were subsequently co‐transformed into the yeast strain Y2H Gold and subsequently grown on SD/‐Leu‐Trp (SD/‐L‐W) media. The positive clones were selected on SD/‐Leu ‐Trp ‐His ‐Ade (SD/‐L ‐W ‐H ‐A) or SD/‐Leu ‐Trp ‐His ‐Ade + X‐α‐gal (SD/‐L ‐W ‐H ‐A + X‐α‐gal).

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation assay

The BiFC assay was performed as previously described (Liu et al., 2017). The two indicated proteins were fused to the N‐terminus and C‐terminus of YFP and co‐expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation. Two d later, the YFP signal was detected with a confocal microscope.

Split‐luciferase complementation imaging assay

The LCI was performed as previously described (Chen et al., 2008). The two indicated proteins were fused with the N‐ or C‐terminus of luciferase and co‐expressed in N. benthamiana leaves via Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation. Two d later, the N. benthamiana leaves were sprayed with 1 mmol/L luciferin and observed with a low‐light cooled charge‐coupled device (CCD) imaging apparatus.

Co‐immunoprecipitation

The Co‐IP assay was performed as previously described (Lu et al., 2022a). Briefly, the two indicated proteins were fused with HA and a GFP tag and co‐expressed in rice protoplasts, while GFP and HA fusion proteins were co‐expressed as controls. Then, the GFP‐fused protein and GFP were enriched by incubating with the GFP agarose beads at 4°C for 2 h and washing with phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) buffer three times. The input, immunoprecipitation and Co‐IP proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate – polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS‐PAGE) and detected by immunoblotting with HA and GFP antibodies.

Pull‐down assay

The pull‐down assay was performed as previously described (Louche et al., 2017). Briefly, the two indicated proteins were cloned and inserted into the pET32a and pGEX4T‐1 vectors and subsequently transformed into the E. coli strain BL21. The histidine (His) and glutathione S‐transferase (GST) fusion proteins were induced by isopropylthio‐β‐galactoside (IPTG) and purified with His or GST tag affinity beads. The purified GST and His fusion proteins were incubated at 4°C for 2 h, enriched with GST tag affinity beads at 4°C for 1 h and washed with washing buffer (20 mmol/L, pH 7.4 Tris‐HCl, 0.1 mol/L NaCl, 0.1 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) and 0.2% NP40) three times. The input, immunoprecipitated and pulled down proteins were detected by immunoblotting with His and GST antibodies.

Extracting membrane proteins and nuclear proteins

The membrane proteins and nuclear proteins were extracted from the OsHLH46‐HA transgenic plants using a Minute Plasma Membrane Protein Isolation Kit for Plants (Invent, Beijing, China) (Chen et al., 2022). H+‐ATPase was used as a membrane marker and detected with an anti‐H+‐ATPase antibody (Ambart, Shanghai, China), and histone was used as a nuclear marker and detected with an anti‐histone antibody (Ambart, Shanghai, China). The OsHLH46‐HA fusion protein in the samples was detected by immunoblotting with an anti‐HA antibody.

Rice blast fungus inoculation

Rice blast fungus inoculation was performed as previously described (Yang et al., 2021). For spray inoculation, 3‐week‐old rice seedlings were sprayed with an M. oryzae spore suspension (5 × 105 spores/mL) and placed in a dark and humid chamber for 24 h at 26°C. The plants were subsequently placed in a humid room with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness at 26°C. Two to 3 d later, the diseased leaves were observed and photographed. For punch inoculation, the leaves of 1‐month‐old rice plants were wounded with a mouse ear punch, and 10 µL of spore suspension was added (5 × 105 spores/mL). The inoculated plants were subsequently placed in a dark and humid chamber for 24 h at 26°C and subsequently in a humid room with 12 h of light and 12 h of darkness at 26°C. Six to eight days later, the diseased leaves were observed and photographed.

H2O2 accumulation assay

An H2O2 accumulation assay was performed as previously described (Hou et al., 2020). Briefly, the detached leaf sheaths of 3‐week‐old rice plants were injected with an M. oryzae spore suspension (5 × 105 spores/mL), and samples were collected at the indicated times and stained in 1 mg/mL 3,3ʹ‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution (pH = 3.8). After vacuum infiltration for 30 min, the samples were placed in the dark at room temperature for 12 h and then bleached by boiling in ethanol:lactic acid:glycerol (1:1:1, v/v/v) for 15–30 min. H2O2 accumulation in the samples was observed and photographed using a microscope with a CCD camera.

Callose deposition assay

Callose deposition was performed as previously described (Niu et al., 2022). Briefly, 1.5–3.0‐cm‐long leaf stripes of 10‐d‐old rice seedlings were collected and immersed in chitin (800 nmol/L) solution for 30 min. The samples were subsequently fixed in ethanol:glacial acetic acid (1:1) for 5 h. After washing with ddH2O three times, the samples were discolored in 70% (v/v) ethanol and 50% ethanol for 2 h and washed with ddH2O three times. In the end, the samples were treated with 10% NaOH for 1 h, stained with 150 mmol/L K2HPO4 (containing 0.01% aniline blue) for 4 h, and observed with a confocal microscope.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from fresh leaves with TRIzol reagent. First‐strand complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. Quantitative PCR was performed with TransStart Green qPCR SuperMix (AQ101; TransGen, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Primers used for qRT‐PCR are provided in Table S1.

In vitro protein stability assay

An in vitro protein stability assay was performed as previously described (Fu et al., 2012). Briefly, the OsHLH46‐His fusion protein was expressed in the E. coli strain BL21 and purified. The total proteins of 3‐week‐old osbsk1‐2 mutant and ZH11 plants were subsequently extracted using degradation buffer (25 mmol/L pH 7.5 Tris‐HCl, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 10 mmol/L NaCl, and 5 mmol/L dithiothreitol (DTT)) and mixed with the same amount of purified OsHLH46‐His protein, while 10 mmol/L ATP was added to the reaction mixture. Samples were collected at the indicated times and subjected to immunoblotting with an anti‐His antibody.

Cell‐free degradation assay

The cell‐free degradation assay was performed as previously described (Wang et al., 2016). The YFP‐3HA and OsBSK1‐2‐HA fusion proteins were expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana and enriched with HA beads. The OsHLH46‐His protein was expressed in E. coli and purified. Then, equal amounts of the OsHLH46‐His fusion protein were incubated with YFP‐3HA or OsBSK1‐2‐HA in degradation buffer containing 25 mmol/L pH 7.5 Tris‐HCl, 10 mmol/L MgCl2, 10 mmol/L NaCl, 5 mmol/L DTT, and 10 mmol/L ATP. Samples were collected at the indicated times, and the protein abundance of OsHLH46‐His was analyzed by immunoblotting using an anti‐His antibody.

Protein degradation assay with MG132 and CHX treatment

Protein degradation assay with MG132 and CHX treatment was performed as previously described (Xuetao et al., 2023). The YFP‐3HA and OsBSK1‐2‐HA fusion proteins were co‐expressed with OsHLH46‐GFP in rice protoplasts for 16 h, respectively, and then MG132 or CHX was added. Two hours later, samples were collected, and total proteins in the samples were extracted and subjected to immunoblotting with anti‐HA and anti‐GFP antibodies. Samples without MG132 and CHX treatment were used as controls.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation – qPCR in rice protoplasts

Chromatin immunoprecipitation – qPCR was performed on the plant protoplasts as previously described (Lee et al., 2017). Ten‐d‐old oshlh46 mutant seedlings that grew in the dark were prepared as protoplasts, after which the pCAMBIA1300‐OsbHLH6‐GFP plasmid was transformed into the rice protoplasts. The next day, after the protoplasts were treated with 1% formaldehyde, the DNA and proteins were crosslinked. Then, the DNA was broken into small fragments of 200–700 bp with sonication. The samples were incubated with GFP polyclonal antibody or immunoglobulin G (IgG) at 4°C for 2 h. Then, 20 μL of Magna ChIP protein A + G magnetic beads was added to the precipitate. Thereafter, 0.2 mol/L NaCl was used for crosslinking, and DNA was dissociated from the complex. The enrichment of specific DNA fragments in the OsWRKY45 promoter was analyzed using qPCR.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation – qPCR of transgenic plants

Chromatin immunoprecipitation – qPCR in a transgenic plant assay was performed as previously described (Weng et al., 2015). Briefly, leaves of 20‐d‐old OsbHLH6‐GFP‐OE plants were ground in liquid nitrogen and fixed in formaldehyde under vacuum. Chromatin was sheared to DNA fragments using sonication, and the samples were subsequently incubated with GFP agarose beads at 4°C for 2 h. Then, 0.2 mol/L NaCl was used for crosslinking, and the DNA was dissociated from the complex and analyzed by qPCR.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay was performed as previously described (Niu et al., 2022). The bHLH domain of OsbHLH6 (1–200 aa) was cloned and inserted into the pGEX‐4T‐1 vector to generate the OsbHLH6 (bHLH)‐GST fusion protein. The OsWRKY45 promoter Cy5‐labeled and unlabeled probes were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Company. Binding reactions were performed at room temperature in binding buffer containing 10 mmol/L pH 7.5 Tris‐HCl, 50 mmol/L KCl, 1 mmol/L DTT, 2.5% glycerol, 5 mmol/L MgCl2, 0.5 mmol/L EDTA, 1 μL of poly(dI∙dC), 1.5 mg of purified OsbHLH6 (bHLH)‐GST protein, and 50 fmol of Cy5‐labeled PCR fragments for 30 min. The mixtures were then subjected to electrophoresis in a 6% native PAGE gel, and the fluorescence signal was detected with a Li‐COR Odyssey clx system.

Transcriptional activity assay in rice protoplasts

A transcriptional activity assay of OsHLH46 using the GAL4/UAS‐based system was performed as previously described (Lu et al., 2022a). The full‐length CDSs of OsHLH46 and VP16 were subsequently cloned and inserted into the 35S‐GAL4BD plasmid to generate the effector construct. The indicated effector was subsequently co‐expressed with the reporter vector in rice protoplasts. The luciferase activities were measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

A transcriptional activity assay of OsbHLH6 was performed as previously described with a few modifications (Niu et al., 2022). The 1 kb promoter of OsWRKY45 was cloned upstream of the luciferase (LUC) reporter to produce the reporter construct, while the GFP, OsbHLH6‐GFP and OsHLH46‐GFP fusion proteins driven by the 35S promoter acted as the effector constructs. Renilla luciferase (REN) driven by the 35S promoter was set as an internal control. The reporter and indicated effector were co‐transformed into rice protoplasts. After 16 h, the LUC and REN activities were measured. The relative LUC activity was normalized to the REN activity.

Detection of the copy numbers of OsHLH46‐HA transgenes

The total DNA of the ZH11 and OsHLH46‐OE plants was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide method. The copy numbers of the OsHLH46‐HA transgenes were calculated as the amount of the OsHLH46‐HA transgene relative to the amount of genomic ubiquitin by qPCR at the DNA level.
