Magnetically Controlled Capsule Endoscopy: Innovations, Standardized Practice, and Emerging Clinical Roles
Authors: Xi Jiang, Chen He, Xiaoou Qiu, Yizhi Chen, Wei Zhou, Zhaoshen Li, Zhuan Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Capsule endoscopy has transformed small bowel evaluation but remains limited for gastric examination because of passive, peristalsis-dependent movement. Magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy (MCE) addresses this limitation by enabling real-time external steering of the capsule, allowing systematic visualization of the spacious gastric cavity without sedation or intubation. Since the first human application in 2010, multiple MCE platforms have been developed and validated, including handheld, magnetic resonance based, and robotic arm systems, with the latter now representing the dominant clinical platform. Clinical trials have demonstrated that robotic arm MCE achieves diagnostic accuracy comparable with conventional esophagogastroduodenoscopy for the detection of gastric lesions, while offering superior patient tolerance. In parallel, standardized examination protocols have been established, ensuring reproducible gastric preparation, complete mucosal coverage, and structured reporting. Recent technological advances, including higher resolution imaging, detachable string technology for combined esophageal and gastric examination, improved magnetic steering, and artificial intelligence-assisted image analysis, have further expanded the diagnostic capability and efficiency of MCE. Importantly, the noninvasive and well tolerated nature of MCE supports its use in populations at increased risk from conventional endoscopy, including children, elderly patients, individuals with liver cirrhosis, and those receiving antiplatelet therapy. This review summarizes current MCE technologies, standardized clinical practice, and diagnostic performance, and discusses emerging clinical roles and future challenges. MCE is evolving from a novel diagnostic tool into a practical and well-tolerated noninvasive modality for upper gastrointestinal evaluation.

INTRODUCTION

Capsule endoscopy, first introduced into clinical practice in 2000, revolutionized the evaluation of small bowel diseases by providing a noninvasive, sedation free diagnostic approach (1). Despite its success in small bowel imaging, conventional capsule endoscopy relies entirely on gastrointestinal peristalsis, which limits its ability to achieve systematic and complete examination of the stomach. The large volume of the gastric cavity, complex anatomy, and variable orientation of the capsule often result in incomplete mucosal visualization, restricting its diagnostic value for gastric disease.

Magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy (MCE) was developed to overcome these fundamental limitations. By integrating magnetic materials into the capsule and applying externally generated magnetic fields, MCE allows active, real-time control of capsule position and orientation within the stomach (2,3). This capability enables targeted inspection of gastric landmarks and lesions while preserving the key advantages of capsule-based examination, including patient comfort and avoidance of sedation and intubation. Following the initial conceptual work by Carpi et al (2) and the first human application reported in 2010 (3), rapid technological development has led to multiple MCE platforms with increasing levels of precision and automation.

Among currently available systems, robotic arm-based MCE has emerged as the most clinically mature platform. Multicenter trials have demonstrated that its diagnostic accuracy for gastric lesions is comparable with that of conventional esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD), with high rates of complete mucosal visualization and excellent safety profiles (4,5). Importantly, standardized examination protocols for gastric preparation, capsule navigation, and image interpretation have now been established, enabling reproducible and high quality examinations across centers (6,7).

Beyond technological refinement, MCE has shown particular value in patient populations in whom conventional endoscopy carries increased risk or limited tolerance. These include children, elderly patients, individuals with liver cirrhosis, and patients receiving antiplatelet therapy (8–11) Recent innovations, such as detachable string technology enabling controlled esophageal examination (12) and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted image analysis (13), have further expanded the anatomical reach and clinical applicability of MCE.

Although accumulating evidence supports the diagnostic performance and safety of MCE, much of the currently available clinical data originate from Asian centers where the technology was first developed and widely implemented. Adoption in Western healthcare systems has been more limited, partly because of regulatory considerations, infrastructure requirements, and cost-effectiveness uncertainties. Therefore, although existing studies demonstrate promising clinical utility, further multicenter validation studies in diverse populations will be important to better define the global role of MCE in clinical practice.

In this review, we summarize the major types of MCE systems and their diagnostic performance, outline standardized examination practices, and examine emerging clinical roles across diverse patient populations. We also discuss current limitations and future directions to clarify the evolving role of MCE in upper gastrointestinal diagnostics. This narrative review is based on published literature identified through searches of major biomedical databases, focusing on studies describing MCE system development, examination protocols, technological advances, and clinical applications.

MAIN TYPES OF MCE AND ITS EFFICACY FOR GASTRIC EXAMINATION

Currently reported MCE systems can be broadly classified into 3 categories according to their magnetic control mechanisms: handheld systems, MRI-based systems, and robotic arm systems (14). Handheld systems rely on a manually operated external magnet to steer the capsule, whereas MRI-based systems use electromagnetic coil arrays to generate controlled magnetic fields for capsule navigation. By contrast, robotic arm systems use computer-assisted magnetic manipulation, allowing more precise and stable control of capsule position and orientation within the stomach. Handheld MCE systems

The first human application of handheld MCE was reported by Swain et al (3), demonstrating remote magnetic manipulation of a modified capsule endoscope within the esophagus and stomach using an external paddle shaped magnet. Subsequent feasibility studies in healthy volunteers confirmed that active magnetic guidance using an external handheld magnet was technically achievable, with partial to near complete visualization of the gastric mucosa (15). However, delayed capsule response to magnetic input and suboptimal gastric distension limited examination completeness and image quality in some cases.

Intromedic subsequently developed the MiroCam Navi system by incorporating magnetic materials into a small bowel capsule endoscope, enabling external magnetic steering during gastric examination. In a study of 26 healthy volunteers, Rahman et al reported visualization rates ranging from 88% to 100% across major gastric landmarks, with good concordance between MCE and conventional gastroscopy for minor pathological findings (16). Further studies demonstrated favorable diagnostic yield in patients with suspected acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding and in those with recurrent or refractory iron deficiency anemia, in some cases exceeding that of standard gastroscopy (17,18).

Building on this platform, the MiroCam 3D magnetically assisted capsule endoscopy system introduced 3 dimensional (3D) imaging capability. In a comparative study involving 55 patients, Oh et al (19) reported comparable performance between 3 dimensional MCE and conventional EGD for visualization of major gastric structures and detection of gastric lesions, with a sensitivity of 97%. Despite these promising results, large scale multicenter validation studies are still lacking, limiting the widespread clinical adoption of handheld MCE systems.

MRI-based MCE systems

MRI-based MCE systems were developed through collaboration between Olympus Medical Systems and Siemens Healthcare, using an array of electromagnetic coils to generate dynamic magnetic fields that allow multidirectional capsule control within the stomach (20). In an early feasibility study involving healthy volunteers and patients, Rey et al (20) demonstrated variable visualization success across different gastric regions, with lower completeness in the fundus and cardia compared with the body and antrum.

Subsequent comparative studies showed moderate agreement between MRI-based MCE and conventional gastroscopy (21). In a larger prospective study of 189 patients, Denzer et al (22) reported an overall diagnostic accuracy of 90.5% for major gastric lesions, with high specificity but relatively modest sensitivity. Although MRI-based systems demonstrated the feasibility of noninvasive gastric examination, their complexity, infrastructure requirements, and limited maneuverability have constrained further clinical development and widespread adoption.

Robotic arm MCE systems

Robotic arm MCE systems represent the most advanced and clinically established form of MCE. In 2012, Liao et al (23) reported the first human trial of a robotic arm-guided MCE system, demonstrating precise capsule navigation through computer-assisted external magnetic control, with high rates of complete visualization of gastric landmarks in healthy volunteers. Subsequent improvements in gastric preparation protocols and system design further enhanced examination quality.

In a self-controlled comparative trial, robotic arm MCE demonstrated high agreement with conventional gastroscopy for the detection of gastric diseases (24). This was followed by a large multicenter study involving 350 patients, which showed that MCE achieved sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy comparable with unsedated gastroscopy for gastric lesion detection (4). These findings suggest that robotic arm MCE is a promising noninvasive diagnostic option in selected clinical settings, although further validation is required.

Technological advancements have continued to improve robotic arm MCE performance. Second generation systems incorporating higher image resolution and adaptive frame rates significantly reduced examination time while improving mucosal visualization (25). Additional studies conducted in Western populations confirmed the feasibility and safety of robotic arm MCE, with no high-risk gastric lesions missed during follow-up endoscopy. Recent studies from Western populations further support the feasibility and acceptability of robotic arm MCE (26–28). A prospective European study demonstrated that MCE is safe and feasible for detecting gastric mucosal abnormalities, with high completion rates and good diagnostic performance (28). In addition, comparative studies have shown that MCE is associated with improved patient tolerance and acceptability compared with conventional flexible endoscopy in patients undergoing evaluation for dyspepsia (26,27). These findings provide important complementary evidence beyond Asian cohorts and support the broader applicability of MCE across different healthcare settings (26–28).

Several modified robotic arm MCE platforms have also undergone clinical evaluation. Standing type MCE systems demonstrated high rates of complete visualization of all gastric anatomical landmarks and diagnostic performance comparable with conventional gastroscopy (29,30). Fully automated MCE systems further reduced operator dependency while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy (5). More recently, cable transmission MCE systems achieved excellent sensitivity and specificity for detecting both esophageal and gastric lesions (31,32). Collectively, these data indicate that robotic arm MCE systems currently offer the most comprehensive and reproducible approach for noninvasive gastric examination.

STANDARD EXAMINATION PROCEDURE FOR MCE

With increasing clinical experience and refinement of gastric preparation techniques, a standardized examination protocol (Figure 2) for MCE has been established and validated in multicenter studies (6,7). This protocol integrates structured preexamination preparation, real-time magnetically guided gastric examination, and systematic postprocedural image review, aiming to ensure complete mucosal visualization while maintaining patient comfort and safety.

Gastric examination procedure of MCE. In a stomach expanded and filled with water, the capsule is precisely navigated by an external magnetic field, allowing the operator to maneuver it systematically across gastric anatomical regions in the following order: gastric cardia (a), the gastric fundus (b), gastric body (c), gastric angulus (d), gastric antrum (e), and pylorus (f). MCE, magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy.

Gastric preparation

Patients are instructed to fast for at least 8 hours before examination to minimize residual gastric contents. Colored beverages and medications are avoided after the evening before the procedure to prevent image interference. Approximately 40 minutes before capsule ingestion, patients receive 400 mg of simethicone diluted in 100 mL of water to reduce intragastric bubbles and improve mucosal visibility (33,34). Light ambulation or repeated changes in body position are encouraged after simethicone intake to enhance gastric cleanliness.

For adequate gastric distension, patients are instructed to drink approximately 800–1,000 mL of clear water 10 minutes before examination, with additional water administered during the procedure as needed. When combined small bowel examination is planned, bowel preparation with polyethylene glycol solution is administered according to established protocols (6,7).

Gastric examination procedure

After completion of gastric preparation, the patient is fitted with a data recording device, and the capsule is activated by trained personnel. To optimize visualization of the esophagus and the esophagogastric junction, the patient is positioned in the supine or left lateral decubitus position and swallows the capsule with a minimal volume of water, typically 20–30 mL.

Once the capsule enters the stomach, active magnetic control allows systematic navigation across predefined gastric regions. Using a standardized sweeping sequence, the operator guides the capsule to visualize the gastric cardia, fundus, posterior wall of the gastric body, curvatures of the gastric body, anterior wall of the gastric body, gastric angulus, antrum, and pylorus (6). This approach ensures circumferential mucosal coverage and allows targeted reassessment of suspicious areas from multiple viewing angles when necessary. Real-time image transmission enables immediate positional adjustment of the capsule to optimize visualization.

The gastric examination typically requires 15–30 minutes to complete. When small bowel evaluation is indicated, the capsule is switched to small bowel mode and proceeds passively through the gastrointestinal tract under physiological peristalsis.

Postprocedural procedures and reporting

After completion of the gastric examination, patients may resume normal oral intake unless small bowel examination is ongoing. Patients are advised to monitor for capsule excretion. Capsule retention is defined as failure to excrete the capsule within 14 days and should be confirmed using a handheld locator or abdominal radiography when suspected (6).

Image analysis is performed using a standardized review protocol to confirm complete visualization of key anatomical landmarks and to assess detected lesions. The MCE report includes 4 core components: patient and examination information, examination findings, diagnostic conclusions, and follow-up recommendations. Examination findings should document the completeness of gastric visualization, image quality, and mucosal characteristics at each anatomical site. Lesions are described individually with respect to location, size, color, and morphology. Final diagnostic conclusions are formulated using terminology consistent with conventional gastroscopy, followed by appropriate clinical recommendations.

TECHNOLOGICAL ADVANCEMENTS OF MCE

In recent years, alongside advances in magnetic control technology for capsule endoscopy, researchers have introduced several innovative techniques to expand its examination capabilities and improve diagnostic performance beyond the stomach, including the esophagus and small bowel.

A pivotal innovation addressing the traditional limitation of passive esophageal transit is the detachable string MCE (ds-MCE) technique. This modification allows controlled esophageal examination by physically tethering the capsule, enabling prolonged mucosal visualization and targeted inspection. Pilot and comparative studies demonstrated that ds-MCE significantly enhances circumferential visualization of the entire esophagus, including the Z-line, compared with untethered capsules. It also increases the detection rate of esophageal lesions and diagnostic accuracy for conditions such as reflux esophagitis and esophageal varices, while maintaining high patient tolerance and safety (12,35,36).

Concurrently, enhanced active magnetic steering has revolutionized gastroduodenal transit. Magnetic assistance during pyloric passage drastically reduces median gastric transit time and pyloric transit time (from 58.38 min to 4.69 minutes) (37). This results in a higher small bowel completion rate and increased rapid gastric transit rate, ensuring more reliable and complete small bowel evaluation (38). The second-generation MCE features higher resolution and adaptive frame rates, yielding significantly more esophageal/Z-line visualization frames, reducing gastric examination time and improving image quality and maneuverability (25). A small sized MCE device has also been developed to reduce swallowing difficulty, shorten gastric transit time, and prolong small bowel transit time, improving both accessibility and examination efficacy (39).

Integration of AI, specifically deep learning algorithms such as faster R-CNN, has further enhanced diagnostic efficiency and consistency in MCE image analysis. AI systems has achieved high sensitivity (96.2–98.9%) for detecting diverse gastric lesions (erosions, polyps, ulcers, SMTs, xanthomas) in near real-time (44–94 ms per image), significantly faster than manual review, while also accurately identifying gastric anatomical landmarks with 94.2% accuracy (13,40). This reduces interobserver variability and workload. Finally, system-level innovations enhance accessibility and autonomy. 5G-based remote MCE has demonstrated feasibility and safety, achieving 100% complete stomach visualization with low latency (∼20 ms), comparable maneuverability, image quality, and diagnostic yield to conventional MCE (41). The fully automated MCE system successfully performs gastric examinations without operator intervention, achieving 100% detection of gastric anatomical landmarks (5). These developments demonstrate significant potential for efficient, operator-independent gastrointestinal screening.

Beyond gastric examination, emerging MCE platforms have extended their application to combined upper and mid-gastrointestinal tract evaluation. The MCE enables integrated visualization of the stomach and small intestine within a single examination, improving diagnostic efficiency and patient convenience. In addition, studies on one-time gastro-small intestinal joint examination have demonstrated the feasibility of comprehensive evaluation across multiple gastrointestinal segments using magnetically controlled systems (38,42,43).

Collectively, these advancements including ds-MCE, enhanced magnetic steering, next-generation capsules, AI integration, and automation/telemedicine capabilities, have profoundly expanded the anatomical reach (esophagus to small bowel), improved procedural efficacy (reduced transit times, higher completion rates), and boosted the diagnostic accuracy and accessibility of MCE.

CLINICAL APPLICATION OF MCE IN SPECIAL POPULATIONS

MCE has been extensively validated for gastric examination, combined upper gastrointestinal and small bowel evaluation, and even screening asymptomatic population for gastric cancer (38,43–46). In addition, its noninvasive and well-tolerated nature confers unique advantages in special populations, including pediatric and elderly patients, individuals with liver cirrhosis, and those receiving antiplatelet therapy because those groups are more susceptible to complications from conventional endoscopy. In these cohorts, MCE demonstrates exceptional safety, diagnostic accuracy, and patient compliance, addressing challenges such as sedation risks, procedural intolerance, or bleeding susceptibility. Its growing clinical adoption underscores its transformative potential in personalized gastrointestinal diagnostics for vulnerable populations.

In pediatric patients

MCE has emerged as a safe, noninvasive, and well-tolerated diagnostic tool for gastrointestinal diseases in children. Studies have demonstrated its feasibility in patients as young as 6 years old, with high completion rates and no serious adverse events reported (8,47). MCE offers several advantages over conventional endoscopy, including improved patient compliance and avoidance of sedation. In addition, MCE has shown high diagnostic yield for detecting gastritis, ulcers, polyps, and rare conditions such as blue rubber bleb nevus syndrome (48). In a study involving 474 pediatric patients, MCE was found to improve the detection of Helicobacter pylori infection in children when combined with the Kyoto gastritis scoring system, offering a noninvasive alternative to traditional gastroscopy (49). Furthermore, in pediatric Crohn's disease, MCE significantly reduced gastric transit time and enhanced visualization of the upper gastrointestinal tract, with an 80% success rate in achieving transpyloric passage (50). MCE paired with magnetic resonance enterography enhances Crohn's disease activity assessment (51). Overall, MCE is a promising tool for pediatric gastrointestinal diagnostics, combining safety, diagnostic accuracy, and patient comfort. Further studies are needed to standardize protocols and evaluate its applications in younger children.

In elderly patients

A series of studies have evaluated the efficacy and safety of MCE in elderly patients, who often face higher risks of gastric diseases and EGD related complications. Zhang et al reported a 98% completion rate for gastric observation and a 72.4% detection rate of positive findings in elderly patients (65 years and older) (52). Although gastric preparation quality was slightly inferior, MCE was well-tolerated, with minimal adverse events (3.1% capsule retention). Similarly, Li et al (9) found MCE feasible in patients 60 years and older, including octogenarians, despite longer esophageal and gastric transit times. Notably, MCE demonstrated safety in elderly patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices, with no observed interference from pacemakers on imaging quality or long-term adverse cardiac events (53). In addition, MCE effectively identified aspirin-induced gastric and small-intestinal mucosal injury in elderly patients (54). For elderly patients at high-risk of the standard gastroscopy, including those with severe hypertension (systolic blood pressure >200 mm Hg), active cardiac angina, recent cerebral infarction, or temporomandibular joint disorders, MCE provides a safe and effective diagnostic alternative, eliminating risks associated with sedation and endotracheal intubation while maintaining diagnostic accuracy (55). Collectively, these findings underscore MCE's role as a safe, effective, and patient-friendly option for elderly populations, warranting broader clinical adoption.

In patients with liver cirrhosis

MCE has emerged as a promising noninvasive tool for diagnosing esophageal and gastric varices in cirrhotic patients. Early studies using handheld magnetic systems (MiroCam Navi) demonstrated moderate sensitivity (73.3%) for detecting esophageal varices (EV) but highlighted challenges in maneuverability (56). Subsequent advancements introduced detachable-string MCE (ds-MCE), which significantly improved diagnostic accuracy. A multicenter study reported ds-MCE achieved 92% sensitivity and 88% specificity for high-risk EV in patients with compensated liver cirrhosis, with higher patient satisfaction compared with unsedated EGD (57). The largest validation study to date recruited 607 cirrhotic patients and further confirmed ds-MCE's high performance, with 97.5% sensitivity and 97.8% specificity for esophagogastric varices, alongside excellent safety and comfort (10). The validated 18% esophageal luminal occupancy cutoff enhances objectivity in EV grading and hemorrhage risk prediction. In addition, ds-MCE enables simultaneous assessment of EV, gastric varices, portal hypertensive gastropathy, and even portal hypertensive enteropathy, offering a comprehensive view of portal hypertension manifestations. However, cost-effectiveness analyses and protocols to integrate therapeutic interventions remain unmet needs. Collectively, ds-MCE represents a promising screening alternative to EGD for patients with liver cirrhosis.

In patients with oral antiplatelet therapy

MCE has emerged as a valuable tool for evaluating antiplatelet therapy-induced gastrointestinal injuries. Chen et al first demonstrated MCE's capability to detect significantly higher gastric and small intestinal mucosal injuries in asymptomatic aspirin users compared with controls, establishing its role in silent injury screening (58). A multicenter trial used serial MCE examinations to reveal that dual antiplatelet therapy caused more gastrointestinal injuries (99.2% vs 94.3%, P = 0.02) than monotherapy, with MCE providing crucial endoscopic evidence for treatment optimization (11). A subsequent analysis used repeated MCE to show dual antiplatelet therapy led to greater gastric and small intestinal injury progression versus aspirin alone (59). Gao et al specifically applied MCE in elderly patients, finding significantly more small intestinal injury and highlighting MCE's value in high-risk population monitoring (54). These studies collectively establish MCE as an effective, noninvasive modality for assessing antiplatelet-related gastrointestinal damage across different patient populations and treatment regimens.

In patients with gastrointestinal bleeding

Recent studies evaluated the role of MCE in evaluating acute upper gastrointestinal bleeding (AUGIB). Compared with EGD, hand-held MCE demonstrates a higher diagnostic yield for focal lesions (40 vs 25, P = 0.02), including peptic ulcers, vascular lesions, and small-bowel bleeding sources (18). A prospective study (n = 99) using the robotic MCE system reported a 95.8% diagnostic yield, identifying lesions across the esophagus, stomach, duodenum, and small bowel, with 52.5% of patients safely discharged without further EGD (60). MCE's noninvasive nature and ability to examine the entire upper gastrointestinal tract and small bowel make it particularly valuable for stable AUGIB patients, reducing unnecessary invasive procedures. However, limitations include suboptimal visualization of the esophagus and duodenal bulb, necessitating supplemental EGD in some cases. Overall, MCE offers a well-tolerated, efficient diagnostic alternative, improving patient triage and reducing healthcare burdens. Further refinements in capsule maneuverability and mucosal coverage could enhance its utility in AUGIB scenarios.

CHALLENGES AND FUTURE PERSPECTIVES OF MCE

Despite more than a decade of technological development and extensive clinical validation, several challenges continue to limit the broader clinical adoption of MCE. Addressing these limitations will be essential for its transition from a complementary diagnostic tool to a widely accepted first line examination in selected clinical settings.

One major challenge is achieving consistently complete gastric mucosal visualization. Intraluminal mucus, foam, and residual debris may obscure focal lesions despite standardized pre procedural preparation. Although simethicone-based regimens and positional changes improve gastric cleanliness, residual contamination frequently persists after capsule entry. The development of effective intraprocedural cleansing strategies, such as controlled water infusion or novel anti foam technologies, warrants further investigation through prospective clinical studies.

Another important limitation is the detection of subtle or early neoplastic lesions, particularly early gastric cancer. Although current MCE systems demonstrate diagnostic accuracy comparable with conventional gastroscopy for many gastric lesions, further improvements in image resolution, illumination, and contrast enhancement are needed. Integration of advanced AI algorithms capable of real-time lesion recognition and risk stratification may enhance sensitivity and reduce operator-dependent variability. However, most AI-based systems remain in the research stage, and their performance may be affected by intragastric mucus, bubbles, and residual debris, which can lead to false-positive findings. Further prospective validation across diverse clinical settings is required before routine implementation.

Standardization of examination quality remains an ongoing challenge. Although structured protocols for gastric preparation, capsule navigation, and reporting have been proposed, MCE performance still depends in part on operator experience. Adequate training in magnetic capsule manipulation and image interpretation is necessary to ensure consistent examination quality. Broader implementation of automated navigation systems, objective quality metrics, and AI-assisted real-time feedback may improve procedural consistency and training efficiency. In addition, although capsule retention is uncommon, it remains a clinically relevant concern in selected patient populations and should be considered when evaluating the safety profile of MCE. Capsule retention, although uncommon, may occur in approximately 1%–3% of patients overall and at higher rates in selected populations.

Extension of reliable examination beyond the stomach represents another unresolved issue. Although detachable string technology allows controlled esophageal inspection and magnetic steering improves pyloric transit, systematic examination of the duodenum remains limited by passive capsule movement (10,12,37,38). Innovations enabling active duodenal navigation or controlled pyloric passage would substantially expand the diagnostic scope of MCE. However, capsule maneuverability remains a key technical limitation, particularly in anatomically complex regions such as the fundus, cardia, and duodenum. Despite advances in magnetic steering, precise control of capsule orientation can be challenging due to gastric motility, fluid dynamics, and anatomical variability.

A further fundamental limitation of capsule-based endoscopy is the inability to obtain tissue samples or perform therapeutic interventions. Although MCE provides a noninvasive means to visualize the upper gastrointestinal mucosa, a substantial proportion of lesions still require histopathological confirmation or timely endoscopic intervention. In recent years, prototype capsules capable of tissue biopsy and targeted drug delivery have been developed, highlighting the potential transition of capsule endoscopy from a purely diagnostic tool toward a multifunctional platform (61–63). For example, magnetically controlled capsule systems incorporating mechanical biopsy mechanisms or targeted drug delivery modules have demonstrated feasibility in preclinical and early translational studies (61). However, these technologies remain at an early stage and require further validation before clinical application. Until such capabilities become clinically available, MCE should be regarded primarily as a diagnostic and triage tool that complements, rather than replaces, conventional endoscopy.

Economic considerations may also influence the broader implementation of MCE. The cost of capsule devices, specialized magnetic control systems, and supporting infrastructure may limit accessibility in some healthcare settings, with estimated examination costs generally ranging from approximately USD 200 to 500 per procedure in many regions. In addition, most large clinical studies have been conducted in Asian countries where these systems were initially developed, and further cost-effectiveness analyses and validation studies in Western healthcare systems are needed to clarify their role in routine clinical practice.

Environmental considerations related to disposable capsule devices should also be acknowledged. Because MCE capsules are single-use devices containing electronic components, increasing adoption may raise concerns regarding medical electronic waste. Future development of environmentally sustainable manufacturing processes and recycling strategies may help mitigate these potential impacts.

In summary, MCE has evolved into a safe, accurate, and well-tolerated noninvasive modality for upper gastrointestinal evaluation. Continued technological innovation, rigorous clinical validation, and careful integration into clinical workflows will determine its future role in gastrointestinal diagnostics and population-based screening.
