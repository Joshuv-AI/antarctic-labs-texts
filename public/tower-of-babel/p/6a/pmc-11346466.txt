Anisotropic DBSCAN for 3D SMLM Data Clustering
Authors: Pilar Lörzing, Philipp Schake, Michael Schlierf
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) advanced biological discoveries beyond the diffraction limit. Various implementations enable 3D SMLM to reconstruct volumetric cell images. Yet, the inherent anisotropic point spread function of optical microscopes often limits the localization precision in the axial direction compared to the lateral precision. Such localization anisotropy could also expand spherical cellular structures to ellipsoidal cellular structures. Structure identification, however, is often performed using DBSCAN cluster algorithms, considering an isotropic search volume. Here, we show that an anisotropic DBSCAN search volume identifies anisotropic clusters more reliably using simulated ground truth data sets. Given experimental localization precisions, we suggest optimized search parameters based on an expanded computational grid search and show an enhanced performance of anisotropic DBSCAN amidst variations in localization precision. We demonstrate the capability of anisotropic DBSCAN on experimental data and anticipate that the algorithm allows for a more rigorous identification of clusters in cells, considering the anisotropic localization precisions of astigmatism-based 3D SMLM.

Introduction

Single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) enabled scientists to resolve cellular structures beyond the diffraction limit with nanometer resolution.1−6 Recent techniques also enable visualizing dynamics, multicolor labeling, and correlative studies with electron microscopy.7−10 SMLM is based on the sequential imaging of a sparse subset of fluorophores distributed over thousands of frames. Their temporal and spatial separation allows that the localization of each fluorophore can be determined from the center of their point-spread function, and an image is reconstructed from the sum of localizations. SMLM can be further extended to three dimensions, allowing to observe biological systems not only as a 2D projection but in their 3D organization.11−13 Engineering of the three-dimensional PSF through additional optics has been demonstrated to be a powerful tool for extracting axial information from the image, by measuring the changes of the PSF shape.5 For small z-ranges (±500 nm), astigmatism imaging is particularly useful due to its relatively simple and compact PSF on the detector. It requires only minor modifications of the microscope by introducing a cylindrical lens in the imaging path.11,14 The overall quality of the reconstructed image relies on the precision of localizing individual molecules. The precision to localize molecules depends strongly on the number of detected photons and the point spread function.15,16 While some PSF engineering approaches allow for isotropic 3D imaging,17 many commercial implementations are based on astigmatism. The asymmetric point spread function combined with astigmatic imaging results in a nonuniform localization precision across all three dimensions. The localization precision in the axial dimension varies with astigmatism, z-position, and also the photon budget, generally being lower than in the lateral plane.11,14 Regardless of the SMLM imaging modality, the data consist of a pointillistic set of approximate single-molecule coordinates. Such data are typically further analyzed to describe the spatial arrangement of the underlying target structure.

One frequent analysis focuses on clustering of localizations to identify larger macromolecular arrangements in cells. The density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) algorithm18 stands out as one of the widely used methods.19−22 DBSCAN can be applied to both 2D and 3D data sets and is already integrated in multiple SMLM analysis tools such as SMAP,23 LAMA,24 and PALMsiever.25 In brief, DBSCAN groups localizations into discrete clusters and distinguishes noise by calculating the number of points within a given neighborhood radius. The algorithm performance depends on two user-defined parameters: the search radius (eps) specifying the neighborhood in which to define a cluster, and a density threshold minimal number of points (MinPts) determining if a point is part of a cluster or not. Although setting these two parameters is often done manually and can be challenging, unbiased approaches to determine them have been reported.26 Notably, the 3D search radius eps is typically defined with a single value, which makes the search volume invariable and gives a boundary to the identified biological structure. In particular, with the lower localization precision in the axial direction, there is an increased probability of localizations exceeding the cluster search volume along the axial direction, as opposed to the lateral plane. This discrepancy in localization precision can lead to misclustering since the cluster search volume shape does not match the shape of the underlying data uncertainty.

Here, we present a method to access biomolecular clustering in 3D SMLM using an anisotropic DBSCAN approach. We introduce next to the lateral epsx,y an independent epsz parameter, expanding the search radius parameter space, and test the performance of the ellipsoid search volume using simulations, providing ground truth data (Figure 1). We find that the anisotropic model typically outperforms the isotropic search volume. Based on these criteria, we further provide suggested epsx,y and epsz search radii based on typical localization precisions in SMLM. We subsequently successfully apply the anisotropic search volume to a DNA nanoruler and clusters of transcriptional repressors in E. coli. We anticipate that the anisotropic DBSCAN implementation in Google Colaboratory facilitates cluster analysis in 3D SMLM.

Methods

Cluster Generation in Simulated Cell Volume

To simulate localizations for comparing the performance of different DBSCAN implementations and parameter optimization, a simple simulation framework was deployed in Python using Google Colaboratory (Supporting Information). The cell volume is simulated as a cylinder with a length of 2.4 μm along the x-axis and a radius of 0.4 μm, resembling E. coli cell dimensions. Simulating localizations involved the following steps: (i) Cluster centers are placed randomly within the volume using a uniform distribution. (ii) Ground truth localizations are placed around each cluster center following an isotropic normal distribution. (iii) Anisotropic normally distributed standard deviation is added to each localization, reflecting the localization precision of an instrument. Localizations placed outside of the cell volume are excluded. (iv) Addition of nonclustered points in the cell volume assuming a uniform distribution. We generated 100 to 10 000 independent cells depending on the corresponding conditions.

Implementation of Anisotropic DBSCAN

Anisotropic DBSCAN was implemented in the Google Colaboratory based on the 2D-DBSCAN algorithm with an extension to 3D considering a different search radius generating an ellipsoid search volume.

Grid Search for Parameter Optimization

To evaluate DBSCAN performance for the isotropic or anisotropic approach, a grid search varying either epsx,y,z or epsx,y or epsz and epsz was implemented. Clustering performance of each combination was evaluated using the adjusted Rand index,27 Mutual information score,28 or the Fowlkes–Mallows index29 using scikit learn package.30 Scores were calculated using all points in the system including simulated nonclustered points.

Culture Conditions and Sample Preparation

E. coli MG1655 chromosomally expressing LexA-PAmCherry fusion construct31 was grown in M9 minimal medium (1 × M9 salts [Sigma], 1 mM MgSO4, 0.1 mM CaCl2, 0.4% glucose) supplemented with 0.001% biotin and thiamine. Cultures were grown in 1.5 mL tubes with a punctured lid at 37 °C and 600 rpm until the exponential phase. For fixation, bacteria were cooled on ice for 10 min and incubated with 4% paraformaldehyde in PBS, pH 7.4 for 35 min at room temperature, then washed twice with PBS and stored at 4 °C. Poly-l-lysine coated coverslips were glued to 8-well chamber sticky slides (ibidi). For cell immobilization, the suspension was allowed to settle for 40 min. 100 nm Gold Nanoparticles (Alfa Aesar) were added as a fiducial marker and applied for 10 min at a concentration of 1:20 v/v. Excess gold beads were removed by washing the well twice with PBS.

Single-Molecule Localization Microscopy

3D SMLM experiments were carried out on a Nikon Ti-E STORM/PALM microscope equipped with a 1.49 NA 100 x Apo TIRF objective (Nikon) and Andor iXon Ultra 897 EMCCD camera, a perfect focus system (Ti-PFS, Nikon) and a cylindrical lens for 3D imaging (1 m focal length). 3D DNA-PAINT nanoruler (HiRes 80Y, Gattaquant) was recorded for 10,000 frames with 100 ms exposure time using 561 nm (20 mW after the objective) excitation. Emission was captured using a 605/70 bandpass filter (AHF). PAmCherry was continuously activated with a 405 nm laser and excited at 561 nm with 28 mW under HILO conditions with a 20 ms exposure time. The 405 nm activation laser was increased over 10,000 frames following a Fermi function as previously reported.32 Emission light was filtered by a 605/70 band-pass filter (AHF). Brightfield images of the same field of view were taken before data acquisition.

Raw Data Processing

Raw SMLM movies were processed using SMAP.23 The shape of the 3D point spread function was calibrated from 200 z-stacks (10 nm step size) of 0.2 μm TetraSpeck fluorescent beads (Invitrogen). After localization, the data were corrected for drift using the drift correction plugin and the gold nanoparticles. Localizations across consecutive frames were correlated to accurately capture single molecules. A fixed maximum distance of 20 nm and up to 10 dark frames between localizations were used for merging to account for fluorophores emitting across multiple frames and possible blinking events. Localization precision was calculated within SMAP using the square root of the Cramér–Rao lower bound. For cell segmentation from the brightfield images, a StarDist 2D model was trained using the ZeroCostDL4Mic platform.33,34 The StarDist model was trained on 30 annotated images for 400 epochs with a patch size of 512 × 512 px, a batch size of 2, 4-fold data augmentation, 10% validation data split, and an initial learning rate of 0.0003. Localizations were then grouped based on the cell area using a custom-written Python script.

Analysis of 3D DNA Nanoruler Data

In order to analyze individual nanorulers, the data were segmented based on the xy-coordinates using 2D DBSCAN (eps = 40, MinPts = 200). Individual rulers are further filtered based on their standard deviation in x, y, and z directions to accommodate the angular distribution of the nanoruler. Thresholds of <20 nm in x and y, and <90 nm in z were applied to isolate perpendicular orientation relative to the surface. Gaussian mixture models (GMMs) are applied to the z-coordinates of each ruler to classify them as unimodal, bimodal, or trimodal distributions. The Bayesian information criterion (BIC) is used for bimodal model selection, resembling the 3D nanoruler with two rings. The two modes extracted from the identified bimodal distribution were used to determine their respective center of mass, which is used to calculate the average distance between the binding sites.

Results and Discussion

Optimization of an Anisotropic Search Volume on Simulated Data

SMLM allows for high-resolution reconstructed images with localization precisions in the low nanometer range.6,15 For instance, we frequently localize fluorescent proteins with precisions of ∼25 nm in x,y and ∼110 nm in z or organic fluorophores with ∼3 nm in x,y and ∼17 nm in z using the astigmatism approach for 3D imaging,5,11,35 Clustering of data points is performed using a computational algorithm, e.g., DBSCAN which requires the search parameters epsx,y, epsz, and MinPts. MinPts together with the search radius implies a density of molecules. Yet, MinPts also reflects the expected size and nature of macromolecular complexes, which play a crucial role in the intracellular organization of bacteria.36 Estimating these parameters presents a considerable challenge due to their high dependence on the characteristics of the data under consideration. Additionally, assessing cluster identification confidence using experimental data becomes particularly intricate, as the ground truth is often unknown. This challenge is further increased by the consideration that point clouds are elongated due to different localization precisions, and therefore, an anisotropic search algorithm is required. To address the challenge of parameter optimization and the different levels of confidence for isotropic vs anisotropic search, we conducted a systematic analysis of different epsilon searches employing simulated experimental data.

We developed a simulation framework (implemented in Google Colaboratory) to set parameters such as number of clusters, amount of nonclustered localizations, expected biological cluster variance, and anticipated device noise due to anisotropic localization precisions in the x, y, and z directions (see Methods). Further, the simulation framework allows one to mimic individual cells with regard to their size and shape and thus explore cluster centers (randomly), distributed within the specified cell volume.

Here, we assumed six, randomly distributed clusters within a single cell given a cylinder of 2.4 μm length and 0.4 μm radius, mimicking the size of an E. coli cell, containing up to 30 points distributed around a single point with a standard deviation of 50 nm (Figure 2a). Subsequently, we randomized each of these points using an instrumental localization precision of 25 nm in x,y and 100 nm in z, e.g., for PAmCherry (Figure 2b). In a last step of simulated data generation, we added within the cell volume up to 600, randomly distributed, nonclustered data points (Figure 2c). We typically generated 10,000 cells for each condition. The simulation parameters were chosen to mimic the clustering patterns observed in our experimental PALM data, ensuring close alignment in cluster characteristics. Additionally, a slightly elevated level of noise was incorporated into the simulations to create a more realistic and challenging clustering environment. We subsequently applied DBSCAN implementations to each of the simulated cells using a constant MinPts = 10 and for an isotropic search varying epsx,y,z or for an anisotropic search epsx,y and epsz resulting in different cluster outcomes. The determination of the optimal epsilon parameters for each data set was realized using the adjusted Rand index (ARI).27,37 Briefly, the ARI computes the similarity between the ground truth clusters and the identified clusters by using DBSCAN, normalized to account for chance. Using the ARI we performed a grid search and found for an isotropic DBSCAN implementation an optimal epsx,y,z = 100 nm (Figure 2d) while for the anisotropic DBSCAN implementation we found an optimal epsx,y = 50 nm and epsz = 250 nm (Figure 2e) given the simulated localization precisions.

While our initial simulations show that different underlying search models lead to different optimal eps parameters, they could not quantitate which model yields a more accurate identification of the ground truth. Therefore, we computed again 10,000 cells and applied isotropic or anisotropic DBSCAN cluster searches using the optimal eps parameters from above. This data set enabled a comprehensive performance evaluation based on three different parameter scores: adjusted Rand index (ARI), mutual information score (MI), and Fowlkes–Mallows index (FMI). Across all three scores, the anisotropic model considerably outperformed the isotropic model (Figure 2f). The higher ARI implies a greater similarity between the clustering results of the anisotropic model and the ground truth. Similarly, the elevated MI signifies a stronger correspondence between the true and predicted cluster assignments. While ARI measures clustering agreement relative to random chance,27,37 MI quantifies shared information between predicted and true clusters.28 The higher FMI indicates improved precision and recall in the clustering outcomes.29 In summary, this emphasizes a more effective performance of the anisotropic model to identify clusters accurately and capture accurate spatial relationships, given the instrumental anisotropic localization precision.

Precomputing Optimal Epsilon Parameters for Various Localization Precisions

We hypothesized that the optimal epsx,y and epsz parameters depend on the underlying localization precision, therefore we decided to expand our evaluation to a larger grid search to guide initial eps estimations for anisotropic DBSCAN clustering (Scheme S1). Taking into account reports of localization precisions,10,11,35 we simulated a grid of 100 cells each, with six clusters based on localization precisions ranging from 2 to 53 nm in the x,y-plane and from 15 to 165 nm in the axial dimension (272 combinations). Note that we kept MinPts fixed to 10 in order to reduce the overall search space. Macromolecular complexes for intracellular organization in bacteria are also reported to be of size 10.36 As described earlier, we determined the optimal epsx,y and epsz parameters for each of the combinations of localization precisions using the ARI (Figure 3; Table S1). Comparing the ratio of epsz/epsx,y with the ratio of localization precision z/x,y, we found a strong correlation between both ratios (Figure 3a), showing a Spearman coefficient of 0.9. If the ratio of localization precisions was rather isotropic, the resulting optimal search volume appeared also more isotropic, i.e., epsz/epsx,y ∼ 1 (Figure 3b). In the case of a very high localization precision in x,y but a poor localization precision in z, the optimal search volume was elongated up to a ratio of 12 (Figure 3b). Typically the localization precision in z is a factor of 4 worse than in x,y, leading to an intermediate, but significant, elongated search volume. We provide not only the ratio of epsz/epsx,y, but also the individual search radii in Table S1 as initial estimates for cluster analysis. To account for the variability in biological data, further parameter fine-tuning is recommended. This can be achieved either by increasing the number of simulated cells or by using single-molecule localization data. Simulated data can be used to fine-tune clustering parameters and evaluate performance with the ARI score. Alternatively, for biological data with unknown ground truth additional metrics can be used to evaluate clustering performance, e.g., the silhouette score.38 To guide users in parameter optimization, we provide pseudocode for a grid search implementation (Scheme S1).

Performance of Anisotropic DBSCAN on a 3D DNA Nanoruler

To test the performance of anisotropic DBSCAN, we imaged a 3D DNA PAINT nanoruler that consisted of two spots separated by 80 nm in the axial direction. These two spots serve as binding sites for Cy3B-DNA oligos, which can repeatedly bind and unbind during acquisition. The corresponding reconstructed 3D single molecule images revealed clouds of localizations distributed around two central positions, separated by an axial distance of ∼80 nm (Figure 4a). We determined the center of mass distance between the two localized positions and obtained an on average 85 nm distance, in very good agreement with the DNA nanoruler design (Figure 4b). To identify optimal parameters for the anisotropic DBSCAN, we simulated individual ruler molecules as two clusters placed on top of each other and separated by z = 80 nm with each cluster consisting of 100 localizations distributed around a single point with a standard deviation of 1 nm, reflecting the underlying DNA nanoruler. Afterward, we normally distributed each point using the obtained localization precision of the ruler measurement with 2.5 nm in x,y and 17 nm in z (Figure 4c). Using the optimization criteria described earlier, we obtained epsx,y = 10 nm and epsz= 13 nm, a nearly isotropic search volume. Using these parameters, we have analyzed a set of DNA nanorulers using the anisotropic DBSCAN algorithm and found typically 2 clusters per nanoruler, as expected (Figure 4d).

Application of Anisotropic DBSCAN for PALM Imaging

In the last step, we aimed to test our anisotropic DBSCAN implementation using biological data. We acquired PALM data of the transcriptional repressor LexA labeled with PAmCherry in E. coli cells. Briefly, we fixed E. coli cells and immobilized the cells on a poly-l-lysine coated coverslip. Subsequently, we imaged ≈10,000 frames per field of view using astigmatism for 3D information and localized LexA-PAmCherry (Figure 5a). Localizations in 236 cells were subjected to anisotropic DBSCAN with epsx,y = 50 nm and epsz = 250 nm, MinPts = 10 for the given localization precision in x,y = 25 nm and z = 110 nm (Figure 5b). Using this set of search parameters, we extracted quantitative information on the clustering of LexA-PAmCherry in E. coli. We found on average 2.4 ± 1 clusters per μm2 cell area (i.e., 5 clusters per cell), with on average 36 ± 14 localizations per cluster.

Conclusions

Information about total number of clusters, cluster size, and variance on biological data will enable discovery of underlying structural organization in cells and their response to environmental changes. Within this study, we developed an anisotropic DBSCAN model considering the anisotropic localization precision of SMLM. We have shown the increased performance of anisotropic DBSCAN compared to isotropic DBSCAN using simulated data with a known ground truth. Anisotropic DBSCAN showed with three independent scores always an increased performance on identifying true clusters. Depending on the localization precision in x,y, and z, we provide a table of suggested eps parameters for a guided implementation on individual data sets. We provide as a guide for the reader a simulation implemented in the Google Colaboratory. Determination of an optimal search volume showed a strong correlation between variations in the localization precision and the resulting search volume. The anisotropic DBSCAN model performed better when the localization precision ratio exceeded a factor of 2. Conversely, a more uniform localization precision favored an isotropic search volume. Anisotropic DBSCAN can be readily adapted in existing cluster identification workflows using our Python-based implementation. We applied this approach to extract quantitative information about the clustering of the transcriptional repressor LexA, demonstrating its applicability in analyzing biological SMLM data. We anticipate that an anisotropic DBSCAN algorithm will allow a more rigorous identification of biomolecular clusters in cells, considering the experimental limitations of the elongated axial localization precision.
