Utilizing the coronary artery calcium score to predict coronary artery disease in individuals with presumed cardiac symptoms: A diagnostic study
Authors: Masood Dindari, Mohammad Javad Mehdizadeh Parizi, Reza Golchin Vafa, Reza Heydarzadeh, Houyar Zarifkar, Hourshad Zarifkar, Hooman Zarifkar, Nazanin Hosseini, Mohammad Montaseri, Javad Kojuri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoronary artery disease (CAD) remains the leading cause of global mortality, with coronary artery calcification (CAC) serving as a marker of atherosclerosis. While the CAC score is a well-established non-invasive tool for cardiovascular risk assessment, its precise role in predicting obstructive CAD in symptomatic patients remains debated. This study evaluates the diagnostic and screening utility of the CAC score in detecting significant CAD.MethodsIn this prospective analytical study, 498 symptomatic adults (>40 years) without prior CAD underwent CAC scoring via CT, followed by coronary angiography as the reference test (CAD defined as ≥50% stenosis). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis determined the optimal CAC cut-off for CAD detection.ResultsThe median CAC score was 55 (IQR: 0–233). Among participants, 29.3% and 31.9% were classified as very low and low risk, respectively, based on CAC. A CAC score ≥ 109 predicted obstructive CAD (≥50% stenosis) with 66.6% sensitivity, 80.1% specificity, 65.5% PPV, and 80.95% NPV. For severe CAD (≥80% stenosis), a CAC ≥133 showed 78.6% sensitivity, 74.2% specificity, 35.12% PPV, and 95.15% NPV.ConclusionA CAC score of ≥109 effectively identifies significant CAD, while a score < 133 strongly excludes severe stenosis. These findings support CAC scoring as a valuable rule-out tool in symptomatic patients over 40 without known CAD, aiding clinical decision-making. (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06311071).

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the leading cause of mortality worldwide [1]. Among people over 35, CAD is responsible for one-third of mortality in developed and developing countries [2]. Atherosclerosis is an inflammatory process that causes plaque formation, which can partially or completely block coronary arteries, leading to CAD [3]. Calcification of coronary arteries is a marker of atherosclerosis that has been used since the 1940s [4], [5]. The coronary artery calcium (CAC) score is a well-established, noninvasive method for quantifying atherosclerotic burden, with more than three decades of evidence supporting its prognostic and diagnostic value [6], [7], [8]. During a CAC test, the amount of calcification in coronary arteries is measured by cardiac computed tomography (CT) [9].

European guidelines on cardiovascular prevention in clinical practice recommend the CAC score for evaluating asymptomatic patients with intermediate risk [10]. The Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA) research concluded that in patients aged 32 to 46, the CAC score can independently predict the risk of cardiac events over a ten-year follow-up [11]. Furthermore, Yadon et al. showed that the CAC score is more accurate than C-reactive protein (CRP) and Framingham risk score (FRS) in predicting cardiovascular events [12]. The CAC score is a well-established tool for long-term cardiovascular risk stratification and preventive decision-making in asymptomatic individuals, where its prognostic value has been demonstrated over more than three decades of research. In contrast, the role of CAC scoring in symptomatic patients remains an area of ongoing investigation.

Some studies have shown that a zero CAC score means no further evaluation is required, even for symptomatic patients [13], [14]; in contrast, Osborne et al. indicated that a zero CAC score cannot rule out CAD in patients with stable angina [5]. Another prospective observational study conducted in 2011 concluded that the CAC score alone cannot be used for the risk stratification of patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) [15]. Although a CAC score of zero is generally associated with a very low likelihood of obstructive CAD in low-risk symptomatic individuals, evidence shows that a small number of symptomatic patients may still harbor significant stenosis despite the absence of calcification [16]. Therefore, defining the diagnostic utility of CAC in patients presenting with presumed cardiac symptoms—particularly those who already have abnormal primary non-invasive tests—remains clinically relevant.

Due to the proposed usefulness of this noninvasive test and variation in reported diagnostic performance, this study investigated the diagnostic and predictive value of the CAC score in predicting the likelihood of obstructive CAD in patients with presumed cardiac symptoms.

Methods

Study design

This diagnostic study was conducted between January 2022 and May 2023. The study population consisted of patients referred to Professor Kojuri Cardiology Clinic (Shiraz, Iran, E-mail: kojurij@yahoo.com, web page: http://kojuriclinic.com). After checking eligibility and applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, 498 patients were enrolled.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Inclusion criteria were age over 40 years, having cardiovascular symptoms (including chest pain, dyspnea on exertion, etc.), and positive primary non-invasive tests, such as myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI) or exercise tolerance test (ETT). Exclusion criteria were having a diagnosed cardiovascular disease (including CAD, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), peripheral artery disease (PAD), etc.), pregnancy, medical instability, hypersensitivity to contrast materials, and having atrial fibrillation (AFib).

Variables

Patients' demographic data, symptoms, comorbid diseases (such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, etc.), and history of smoking or opium addiction were obtained from the database of our clinic.

Diabetes mellitus (DM) was defined according to 2022 American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [17]. Hypertension (HTN) was defined as office systolic blood pressure (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg by repeated measurements [18]. Dyslipidemia was determined as abnormalities in serum triglyceride (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), or high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL—C). Dyslipidemia was marked by TG values greater than 150 mg/dL, HDL-C values less than 40 mg/dL, or LDL-C levels greater than 100 mg/dL [19]. Current smokers were those who had smoked at least 100 cigarettes and had been smoking in the past 30 days. Ex-smokers were those who had quit smoking for more than 30 days before the study [20]. Chest pain was categorized into cardiac, possible cardiac, and non-cardiac according to the 2021 American Heart Association (AHA) guideline for the evaluation and diagnosis of chest pain [21].

To address guideline-recommended risk stratification in symptomatic patients, we estimated the Pre-test probability (PTP) of obstructive CAD post hoc using the 2019 ESC CCS guideline, which defines PTP based on age, sex, and symptom type (typical, atypical, non-anginal chest pain, or dyspnoea as the primary symptom). Because detailed chest pain subtyping (typical vs atypical vs non-anginal) was not uniformly recorded in the database, patients with chest pain were conservatively classified as having atypical symptoms for the PTP calculation. Patients without chest pain but with dyspnoea (rest dyspnoea or dyspnoea on exertion) were classified as dyspnoea, and patients presenting with other symptoms were classified as non-anginal. PTP was categorized as <5%, 5–15%, or > 15% in accordance with ESC diagnostic thresholds [22].

All the patients underwent coronary artery calcium (CACS) testing, and total calcium scores were recorded. A calcium score of zero underscored a very low risk for CAD. A score of 1 to 99 was defined as low risk, 101 to 299 as intermediate risk, and 400 or more as high risk for CAD [23]. Then, invasive (conventional) coronary angiography by radial access, as the reference test for diagnosing CAD, was performed for all participants [24].

Invasive coronary angiography was performed only in patients with abnormal primary non-invasive testing (MPI or ETT) suggestive of myocardial ischemia. No patient proceeded to angiography based solely on symptoms. This stepwise approach is consistent with contemporary ESC diagnostic guidelines for the evaluation of suspected chronic coronary syndrome. The clinical reports categorized these tests as positive or abnormal, indicating a possible ischemic response, but the detailed subtype or severity of abnormalities (e.g., degree of ST-segment depression, ischemic burden, or reversibility patterns) was not uniformly recorded in the database and could not be retrospectively extracted. Coronary angiography was performed via radial access using standard projections. The severity of luminal stenosis was determined by visual estimation by two independent interventional cardiologists who were blinded to the patients' CAC scores. Borderline lesions were reviewed in multiple angiographic views to improve accuracy. Quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) was not routinely performed, and physiological assessments such as fractional flow reserve (FFR) or instantaneous wave-free ratio (iFR) were not systematically used, as the study protocol focused on anatomical stenosis assessment. Coronary artery stenosis equal to or more than 50% was considered significant narrowing [25]. Patients with at least one diseased coronary artery with a significant narrowing were considered to have CAD. Minor branches were considered only if their main supplying branch was not diseased. A group of expert interventional cardiologists performed and reported coronary angiographies. The study was single-blind; cardiologists were unaware of the results of CAC scores when performing conventional coronary angiographies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS for Windows edition 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables are described by mean ± standard deviation, and nonparametric variables are described by the median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Mann–Whitney U test and the independent sample t-test were used to analyze non-parametric and parametric variables, respectively. Spearman's correlation was used to assess the correlation between non-parametric variables. Statistical significance was indicated when the p-value (p) was less than 0.05. The sensitivity and specificity of the diagnostic tools were assessed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, and the area under the curve (AUC) was reported. The Youden index was used to determine the cut-off of the diagnostic tools for the optimum sensitivity and specificity.

Ethical considerations

All patients were informed of the study's details, and those who refused to participate were excluded. The study protocol was approved as the residency thesis, by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences (IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1401.200).

Results

Four hundred ninety-eight patients, consisting of 255 men (51.2%) and 243 women (48.8%), participated in this study. The average age of the participants was 58.51 ± 9.65 years. The prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors was as follows: 45.6% for HTN, 24.7% for DM, and 69.1% for dyslipidemia. The study also revealed that 58 patients (11.6%) were current smokers, 56 patients (11.2%) were ex-smokers, and 384 patients (77.1%) had never smoked. Baseline characteristics of the study population.

Among the 498 participants, dyspnea at rest and exertional dyspnea were present in 109 (21.9%) and 174 (34.9%) patients, respectively. Symptom overlap was common: 175 patients (35.1%) experienced both chest pain and dyspnea, while 65 patients (13.0%) had chest pain only and 108 patients (21.7%) had dyspnea only. A total of 240 patients (48.1%) reported chest pain, categorized as cardiac (60 patients; 12%), possible cardiac (86 patients; 17.3%), and non-cardiac (94 patients; 18.9%). Additionally, 150 patients (30.1%) presented with other symptoms, including palpitations (54 patients; 10.8%), orthopnea (9 patients; 1.8%), or paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (8 patients; 1.6%). Using ESC age–sex–symptom pre-test probability estimates, 71 patients (14.3%) were categorized as very low likelihood (<5%), 250 (50.2%) as low likelihood (5–15%), and 177 (35.5%) as intermediate/high likelihood (>15%) (Table 1).

When considering ≥50% stenosis as a significant narrowing, the prevalence of one-vessel, two-vessel, and three-vessel disease, based on coronary angiographies, were 98 (19.7%), 39 (7.8%), and 43 (8.6%), respectively. The prevalence of severe CAD, when considering at least 80% stenosis as a more important narrowing, was as follows: 12 cases of three-vessel disease (2.4%), 15 cases of two-vessel disease (3%), and 48 cases of one-vessel disease (9.6%).

When considering at least 50% stenosis as being significant, the prevalence of CAD according to conventional angiographies was significantly higher in individuals with a higher risk based on the CAC score (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). Moreover, patients with higher calcium scores were found to have a greater number of involved vessels (p < 0.001, correlation coefficient = 0.25) (Fig. 2). Among the 146 patients (29.3%) with a CAC score of 0, 9 patients (6.2%) exhibited coronary stenosis greater than 50%, and 2 patients (1.4%) had stenosis greater than 80% on invasive coronary angiography. Discussion

The CAC score is a risk assessment method for the primary prevention of cardiovascular diseases. Since its initial design in 1990 [26], numerous studies have evaluated its role in detecting CAD. Although several studies compared the CAC score with CT angiography, we confirmed CAD by conventional angiography, which is the most accurate method for detecting CAD and evaluating its severity. It is important to distinguish the preventive and prognostic role of CAC in asymptomatic individuals from its diagnostic role in symptomatic patients. Our study does not aim to evaluate CAC for primary prevention or long-term risk assessment; rather, it examines whether CAC scoring provides diagnostic value in symptomatic patients who were referred for invasive angiography after abnormal non-invasive testing.

In this study, we found that a CAC score < 109 could acceptably indicate a low probability of CAD in symptomatic patients due to its relatively high NPV (80.95%) when considering CAD as having at least one coronary artery with ≥50% stenosis. In 2016, a cross-sectional study by Hanifehpour et al. defined a CAC score cut-off point of 100 in symptomatic patients as an appropriate cut-off for excluding significant stenosis (with 87% NPV) in patients at risk for CAD. An important difference is that they used coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) to define CAD, while our definition of CAD was based on conventional angiography results, providing added accuracy [27]. Ibrahim et al. found that the NPV of the 106.5 cut-off CAC score was 100% for detecting CAD. In their study, the CAC score was a fantastic screening measure in patients suspected of having CAD, especially for those with non-diagnostic ETT with low to moderate risks. Similar to our study, they confirmed the presence of obstructive CAD by coronary angiography [28]. Alshumrani, GA et al. defined a cut-off point of 250, much higher than our proposed value, as a valuable cut-off for predicting ≥50% stenosis in symptomatic patients. Their retrospective study enrolled symptomatic patients and used CCTA as a confirmation test [29].

Unlike our study, in 2023, Chen et al. focused on defining a cut-off point of CAC score with a great PPV; they defined the score of 87.0 (with 62% sensitivity, 76.2% specificity, 91.2% PPV, and 33.7% NPV) as the optimal cut-off point for detecting coronary artery stenosis of at least 50%. They also found the CAC score of 290.8 (with 38.9% sensitivity, 85.4% specificity, 80.3% PPV, and 47.6% NPV) as a proper cut-off point for detecting coronary artery stenosis of at least 70%. Similar to our study, their design was single-blind, meaning that the results of the patients' CAC scores were not revealed to the cardiologists performing and interpreting the invasive coronary angiographies [30]. An interesting study by Natanzon et al. aimed to evaluate the utility of the CAC score for detecting CAD before transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). They defined cut-offs with great NPVs for each main coronary artery: CAC score of 252 for the left main (LM) coronary artery with 99%, CAC score of 250 for the left anterior descending (LAD) artery with 97% NPV, CAC score of 297 for the left circumflex (LCX) artery with 92% NPV, and CAC score of 250 for the right coronary artery (RCA) with 91% NPV [31].

Some studies assessed the diagnostic value of a zero CAC score for ruling out CAD. Sarwar et al., based on an NPV of 93% from a systematic review of 18 studies, and Fernandez-Friera et al., due to an NPV of 98%, claimed that patients with a CAC score of zero are very unlikely to have CAD and do not need further evaluations [32], [33]. While these studies emphasized the usefulness of a zero CAC score for ruling out CAD, our study evaluated the NPV of CAC scores other than zero.

Recent large-scale investigations have further clarified the diagnostic value of CAC scoring in symptomatic patients. Most notably, Winther et al. conducted an extensive analysis of 41,177 individuals suspected of having coronary artery disease, demonstrating a strong and graded association between CAC burden and the presence of obstructive CAD, as well as future cardiovascular risk [34]. Their findings showed that increasing CAC categories markedly improved diagnostic discrimination beyond age, sex, and symptom characteristics alone, and they emphasized that CAC = 0 is associated with a very low prevalence of obstructive CAD across most patient profiles. Importantly, the results of this landmark study directly informed revisions in the 2024 ESC Guidelines for the Management of Chronic Coronary Syndromes, which now highlight the role of CAC in refining pretest likelihood estimates and in supporting clinical decision-making in symptomatic patients when combined with symptom characterization and baseline risk assessment [35].

In our cohort, post hoc ESC-based PTP estimates showed that 14.3% of patients were in the very low likelihood category (<5%) and 50.2% were in the low likelihood category (5–15%). Despite this, invasive coronary angiography was not performed based on symptoms or PTP alone; all included individuals had abnormal functional testing (MPI or ETT) suggestive of myocardial ischaemia, which was the clinical indication for angiography. These findings illustrate the potential clinical value of CAC (and other modifiers) for refining likelihood estimates in borderline PTP ranges, and they also contextualize the relatively low yield of severe stenosis in this referral population.

Our findings demonstrate strong concordance with contemporary evidence. Similar to the observations of Winther et al., we found a clear and graded relationship between increasing CAC categories and the presence of angiographically confirmed CAD. Moreover, we identified CAC thresholds (≥109 for ≥50% stenosis and ≥ 133 for ≥80% stenosis) that offer additional diagnostic discrimination within a real-world symptomatic population. Although the majority of patients with a CAC score of 0 did not exhibit obstructive CAD, a notable minority (6.2% with ≥50% stenosis and 1.4% with ≥80% stenosis) still demonstrated anatomically significant disease. These findings reinforce that, while a CAC score of zero is generally associated with low likelihood of obstructive CAD, it cannot completely exclude significant stenosis in symptomatic individuals—particularly those already selected for further evaluation following abnormal non-invasive testing.

Clinical implications

Current diagnostic pathways for suspected chronic coronary syndromes prioritize functional testing or CCTA for anatomical evaluation. Nevertheless, CAC scoring remains widely used in many clinical environments because of its low radiation exposure, broad availability, and low cost. Emerging evidence suggests that CAC may offer complementary diagnostic information—particularly as a potential rule-out tool—in symptomatic patients. Our study contributes to this context by examining the diagnostic performance of CAC scoring in individuals who had abnormal non-invasive functional tests and were subsequently referred for invasive coronary angiography.

Interpreted alongside the updated ESC 2024 guidelines, our findings highlight the potential role of CAC scoring in refining early patient stratification, reducing unnecessary invasive angiography, and supporting more personalized diagnostic pathways in the evaluation of suspected coronary artery disease. Importantly, CAC scoring is not intended to replace CCTA or to determine which patients should undergo invasive angiography, and our results should not be interpreted as recommending CAC for procedural triage.

Beyond confirming the established association between higher CAC levels and obstructive CAD, our study provides clinically actionable CAC thresholds— ≥ 109 for ≥50% stenosis and ≥ 133 for ≥80% stenosis—derived from a real-world symptomatic cohort. These thresholds may be particularly informative in practice settings where access to CCTA is limited or when abnormal functional testing prompts consideration of invasive angiography. Additionally, although most patients with CAC = 0 were free of obstructive CAD, a meaningful minority (6.2% with ≥50% stenosis and 1.4% with ≥80% stenosis) demonstrated significant disease. This finding underscores the need for cautious interpretation of CAC = 0 in symptomatic individuals, especially when functional testing suggests possible ischemia.

Overall, our results suggest that CAC scoring may serve as a valuable complementary tool in the diagnostic work-up of symptomatic patients—helping clinicians balance diagnostic certainty, procedural risk, and resource utilization within contemporary ESC-guided pathways.

Conclusion

Our study found that the CAC score cut-off point of 109 could detect significant CAD with 66.6% sensitivity, 80.1% specificity, 65.5% PPV, and 80.95% NPV. When defining severe CAD as having at least one coronary artery with ≥80% stenosis, CAC scores ≥133 could predict severe CAD with 78.6% sensitivity, 74.2% specificity, 35.12% PPV, and a notable NPV of 95.15%. Hence, a CAC score < 109 can indicate a low probability of CAD in individuals over 40 years with any cardiovascular symptoms and without pre-established CAD, while a CAC score < 133 can almost rule out severe CAD.

Study limitations

The single-center nature of this study may limit the generalizability of the collected data; multi-center studies are recommended. Furthermore, longitudinal studies looking at long-term outcomes are required to examine the practicality of using the CAC score to guide the management of individuals presenting with presumed cardiac symptoms. Detailed downstream management outcomes, including the number of patients undergoing PCI or CABG after angiography, were not systematically documented and were therefore unavailable for analysis. Detailed parameters of MPI and ETT abnormalities were not systematically documented at the time of data collection. Although all patients were confirmed to have abnormal non-invasive tests prior to angiography, the absence of quantitative ischemia characteristics limits further stratification. Although these data would provide additional clinical context, their absence does not affect the primary aim of the study, which was to assess the diagnostic performance of CAC relative to angiographically defined stenosis.
