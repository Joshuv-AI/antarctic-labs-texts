Design and Evaluation of a Checklist for Day-care Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy
Authors: Kasi Viswanath Gali, Arun Chawla, Sunil Pillai Bhaskara, Padmaraj Hegde, Abhijit Shah, Pilar Laguna, Jean de la Rosette
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessagePercutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is feasible and safe as a day-care procedure when performed in carefully selected patients. We propose a checklist for day-care PCNL that may help in identifying the most suitable patients.Keywords: Percutaneous nephrolithotomy, Day care, Checklist, Ambulatory, Urolithiasis, Kidney stone

Introduction

It has been estimated that the global prevalence of urolithiasis ranges from 1% to 13%, varying across geographic regions [1]. According to 2019 epidemiological data, urolithiasis in India accounted for more than one-fifth of global incident cases, representing the greatest number of urolithiasis-attributable disability-adjusted life years, followed by China and Russia [1]. This epidemiological rise in urolithiasis directly contributes to the growing burden on the economy and health care systems in India, which appear to be under significant pressure.

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) is traditionally performed as an inpatient procedure, with hospital length of stay ranging from 2 to 5 d [2], mainly because of the risk of postoperative bleeding and sepsis. Significant advances in PCNL techniques have paved the way for tubeless PCNL [3] and technological improvements in terms of miniaturization, suction, and laser technology have established PCNL as a safer procedure.

The concept of day care was introduced as a framework to optimize resource utilization, promote faster patient recovery, and reduce financial and infrastructural burdens on hospitals and patients. In urology, there is a growing trend of performing procedures such as cystoscopy, prostate biopsy, and ureteroscopy on a day-care basis. The advent of minimally invasive techniques such as UroLift and Rezūm has facilitated the adoption of day-care surgical management of benign prostatic hyperplasia, which was traditionally an inpatient procedure. A day-care strategy can also be considered when selecting patients for PCNL.

Several studies support PCNL as a safe and effective day-care procedure in selected patients, although there is a lack of consensus regarding the specific criteria for determining patient suitability for day-care PCNL [4,5]. The primary objective of our study was to assess the feasibility of PCNL as a day-care procedure. Secondary objectives included the definition of patient, stone, and perioperative characteristics most suitable for day-care PCNL.

Patients and methods

This prospective observational study was conducted from March 2023 to March 2024 in patients diagnosed with renal calculus disease admitted to a tertiary center. Patients were enrolled on the basis of predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ethics committee approval and written informed consent from all participants were obtained. The study was registered in the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI/2023/03/051085). Day-care PCNL was defined as discharge within 24 h after surgery. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are listed in Table 1. The main inclusion criterion required patients to reside within a 15-km radius of at least a secondary health care center. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Data on patient demographics, comorbidities, and history of previous surgery were collected. A preoperative complete blood count, basic metabolic panel, and urine culture were obtained, along with noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) of the kidney, ureters, and bladder. Stone characteristics, including stone volume (calculated as π × length × width × diameter × 0.167) [6], stone density, and skin-to-stone distance (in cm), were measured, and the STONE score [7] was calculated.

PCNL was performed with the patient in the prone position under general anesthesia. Multiple surgeons were involved in the study, each with a minimum of 5 yr of experience or at least 500 PCNL procedures. Tract dilatation ranged from 12 to 28 Fr, according to the surgeon’s choice. Stones were fragmented using a pneumatic or laser energy source and retrieved according to the surgeon’s choice. Intraoperative data collected included the size of the renal access sheath, number of access tracts, type of postoperative drainage (stent, stent and nephrostomy tube, or tubeless), total operative time (puncture to exit), energy source used, and complications (pleural or other organ injuries). Double-J stents were placed intraoperatively at the surgeon’s discretion.

Postoperatively, a single dose of intravenous paracetamol was administered for pain control. A complete blood count and renal function tests were performed 6 h after surgery. Patients were discharged if they met the postoperative exclusion criteria. The Foley catheter was removed before discharge. Discharge medication consisted of oral cefuroxime 500 mg every 12 h for 5 d, tramadol 37.5 mg, acetaminophen 325 mg every 12 h on demand, and pantoprazole 40 mg every 24 h for 5 d.

Patients received a standardized list of postoperative instructions, which includes guidance on recovery, recognition of warning signs, and specific recommendations for the primary care physician (Supplementary material). Patients were scheduled for follow-up 1 mo after PCNL and cystoscopic removal of their double-J ureteral stents. The imaging modality for confirmation of stone-free status at 1-mo follow-up was NCCT or a combination of X-ray and ultrasound.

A day-care PCNL checklist with preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative factors was formulated on the basis of the study outcomes. Primary outcome measures included the readmission rate (<24 h, <1 wk, <30 d), unplanned emergency department (ED) visits, and complications according to the Clavien-Dindo classification for PCNL [8]. Secondary outcome measures included secondary procedures and the stone-free rate (SFR).

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics are reported as the mean ± standard deviation or the median and interquartile range for continuous variables, and as the frequency and percentage for categorical variables. The association between two categorical variables was explored using the χ2 test. In cases for which the expected frequency in the contingency tables was <5 for >25% of the cells, Fisher’s exact test was used instead. The association between a continuous variable and a categorical variable was explored using an independent-sample t test if the categorical variable had two categories, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) if it had more than two categories. A post hoc pairwise analysis was performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference test to control for α inflation in one-way ANOVA. The linear correlation between two continuous variables was explored using Pearson’s correlation if the data were normally distributed, and Spearman’s correlation for non-normally distributed data. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Hemodynamic instability (p = 0.044), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) of 2 (p < 0.001), and stone volume >5000 mm3 (p = 0.008) were significantly associated with unplanned revisits. In addition, a CCI of 2 (p = 0.024) and stone volume >5000 mm3 (p = 0.021) were significantly associated with readmission. Diabetes mellitus (p = 0.039) was significantly associated with the occurrence of Clavien-Dindo grade ≥2 complications. There was no significant correlation between tract size and readmissions or complications. The overall SFR in the study was 93%.

During the study period, 131 patients who did not meet the selection criteria (including 84 who did not meet the inclusion criteria and 47 who were excluded intraoperatively or postoperatively despite initial eligibility) underwent PCNL with a hospital stay exceeding 24 h. The revisit rate was 6.8% (n = 9), and the readmission rate was 5.3% (n = 7); five of the seven patients readmitted experienced Clavien-Dindo grade ≥2 complications. The SFR for this group of patients was 90%.

On the basis of the study outcomes and the association of various factors with revisits, readmissions, and complications after day-care PCNL, we formulated a day-care PCNL checklist (Fig. 2) that includes ten preoperative and ten intraoperative and postoperative factors to help in identifying patients most suitable for day-care discharge.

Discussion

We prepared a checklist that could potentially help in identifying the most suitable patients for day-care PCNL. If this checklist is followed, day-care PCNL can be a viable and safe procedure. This approach can reduce the length of hospital stay while maintaining low readmission and complication rates.

PCNL is a form of controlled renal trauma. The first outpatient PCNL series was reported in 1986, for which the aim was to reduce hospital expenditure; favorable results were observed [9]. At present, although prospective data are scarce, the evidence that is available supports the feasibility of PCNL as a day-care procedure when applied to carefully selected patients, with outcomes comparable to those for PCNL performed with a longer hospital stay [4,5]. Although data support day-care PCNL, there is a lack of consensus regarding patient selection, which is probably the most important factor limiting worldwide adoption of day-care PCNL in routine practice [10].

The definition of day-care surgery varies across geographic regions. In the USA, it often refers to discharge within 24 h of surgery, also termed a 23-h stay or overnight stay. However, in the UK, day-care surgery means that the patient is discharged on the same day as the surgery [11]. For our study, we defined day-care surgery as patient discharge on the same day or within 24 h of surgery.

We proposed patient selection criteria that were based on the evidence available to ensure optimal outcomes for PCNL with day-care discharge. Preoperative patient factors consisted of access to health care within a 15-km radius, which is of utmost importance in ensuring prompt patient care in cases of any untoward event or complications requiring emergency care. It has been demonstrated that elderly patients (aged >75 yr) undergoing PCNL have higher odds of readmission and complications [12]; therefore, our selection criteria excluded patients aged >75 yr. Anomalous kidneys are associated with technical challenges and a higher risk of hemorrhagic complications and inadvertent injury to other organs, so these cases should be reserved for experienced surgeons and require a prolonged observation period after surgery [13]. Patients with chronic kidney disease with renal calculi necessitating intervention often have a lower hemoglobin level, parenchymal thinning or atrophy, and poor renal reserve, and are thus at higher risk of postoperative hemorrhagic complications, a need for blood transfusion, and a decline in renal function after surgery [14]. The Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) is a compound tool that helps in predicting perioperative morbidity, and studies suggest that a CCI score of ≥3 is significantly associated with major complications (Clavien-Dindo grade ≥3) after PCNL [15], so we applied this threshold as an exclusion criterion in our study population.

The stone characteristics for patient selection were based on the STONE nephrolithometry score, which is a validated measure for predicting PCNL success and perioperative complications. A STONE score ≥9 is deemed to indicate highly complex cases and is associated with lower SFRs, technical challenges, and higher perioperative morbidity [7]. Hence, we set a STONE score of ≤8 as an inclusion criterion for day-care discharge following PCNL.

Thresholds for intraoperative characteristics were set to exclude patients who may be at higher risk of perioperative morbidity. It has been demonstrated that a higher number of access tracts, pelvicalyceal perforation, and operative time exceeding 90 min are independently predictive of higher risk of perioperative hemorrhagic and infectious complications [13]. Hence, we applied the following exclusion criteria: total number of access tracts ≤2, no pelvicalyceal perforation, and operative time <90 min. Omission of a nephrostomy tube is considered one of the most important factors in reducing hospital length of stay [16]; therefore, we only considered patients without a nephrostomy tube for day-care discharge.

Postoperative characteristics for exclusion included factors that deviated from the normal postoperative course, thereby requiring a longer period of observation or active intervention. Another key factor in determining eligibility for day-care discharge includes the patient’s willingness to accept this approach, taking into account the psychological impact of the invasive procedure [17].

Our study cohort selected for day-care discharge after PCNL according to preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative criteria had an unplanned revisit rate of 6% and a readmission rate of 4.3%. Complications leading to revisits and readmissions were predominantly of Clavien-Dindo grade ≤2, and only one patient experienced a grade 3b complication. These findings confirm that day-care discharge is safe after PCNL, with acceptable readmission and complication rates in comparison to inpatient PCNL. Notably, multiple surgeons were involved in our study, and outcomes remained favorable, suggesting that day-care PCNL may be safe regardless of the operating surgeon, provided that they have adequate experience with the procedure. Our results are consistent with those from previous studies on ambulatory PCNL, with readmission rates ranging between 0% and 11.6% across studies [5,18,19]. Our readmission and complication rates appear to be significantly lower than those reported for the CROES Global PCNL study [20] of up to 20%, probably because of our selection criteria chosen to minimize perioperative morbidity.

CCI of 2 and stone volume >5000 mm3 were significantly associated with readmission in our study, so the patient selection criteria were amended to exclude patients with CCI of 2 and stone volume >5000 mm3. These findings are in agreement with the existing literature depicting greater stone volume as an independent predictor of PCNL outcomes [21].

Our final checklist proposed for day-care PCNL has potential as an objective tool for identifying patients suitable for day-care discharge after PCNL and ensuring optimal outcomes. Validation studies are needed to confirm the adequacy of the checklist before it can be used in daily practice. We plan to conduct further studies in the near future for internal validation of our findings using a control group and external validation via multicenter research.

A few studies have evaluated the cost effectiveness of day-care PCNL, with one reporting savings of nearly $1000 per procedure, which is approximately one-third of the total cost [22,23]. However, the data remain limited and further research is warranted.

Strengths of our study include its prospective nature, large sample size, and the development of an objective checklist. Study limitations include the nonrandomized design, subjective components included in the checklist, use of NCCT to assess stone-free status in only some cases, and the lack of validation. Subjective parameters in the checklist—namely postoperative pain scores and visible hematuria—were assessed by the operating surgeon. However, no formal assessment of interobserver reliability was performed, which constitutes a significant study limitation. Although NCCT is the standard of care for assessing SFR during follow-up at our institution, it was not performed in all patients because of financial constraints. This represents a limitation in accurate SFR evaluation after PCNL in our study. All patients in our cohort underwent PCNL in the prone position; with the increasing adoption of supine PCNL worldwide, further studies are needed to evaluate the applicability of our checklist for supine PCNL. In addition, incorporation of patient-related outcome measures could have provided deeper insights into patient satisfaction and the perceived benefits of day-care PCNL. This remains a limitation and an avenue for future research.

Conclusions

Our findings strongly support the feasibility and safety of PCNL as a day-care procedure, particularly for carefully selected patients. This approach not only reduces hospital length of stay but also maintains acceptable rates of readmission and Clavien-Dindo grade ≤2 complications.

Our proposed checklist can help in identifying patients best suited for day-care discharge after PCNL and in ensuring optimal outcomes. However, further validation is required to establish the efficacy of the proposed checklist.
