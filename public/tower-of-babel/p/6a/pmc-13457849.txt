Scalable ampere-level CO2 electroreduction to ethylene enabled by descriptor-guided oxygen affinity engineering
Authors: Bing Huang, Ke Wang, Chentao Wang, Mi Yan, Zhenglong Li, Pengfei Xie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electroreduction of CO2 to ethylene using renewable electricity offers a sustainable approach for greenhouse gas mitigation. However, the efficient ethylene production is challenged by sluggish C–C coupling and wide product distribution. Guided the energy changes associated with C–C coupling and C–O cleavage as descriptors for ethylene electrosynthesis, we predict and synthesize unsaturated MgO1-x anchored on Cu via an electrochemical-induced phase separation method. Electrochemical evaluation of this catalyst achieves an ethylene Faradaic efficiency of 78.2% at 300 mA cm−2 in a flow cell. Mechanism studies reveal the bifunctionality of MgO1-x. On one side, chemical interaction of MgO1-x with Cu domain stabilizes Cu+ and gives asymmetric Cu+···Cu0 pairs, facilitating the *CO–CHO coupling. On another side, the MgO1-x with high oxygen affinity strengthens the binding with dual-carbon intermediate and promotes the C–O bond dissociation, accelerating ethylene formation. Ultimately, this catalyst delivers 60.7% ethylene selectivity at 25 A in membrane electrode assembly of 100 cm2, equivalent to a C2H4 production rate of 1.1 L h−1.

Introduction

The CO2 electroreduction reaction (CO2RR) to ethylene has emerged as a promising technology for high-value polymer manufacture using renewable electricity1,2. However, the complexity of this process typically results in a broad distribution of products even on state-of-the-art Cu-based catalysts, making it challenging to achieve desired ethylene selectivity3. In the reaction pathway of CO2RR-to-ethylene, two distinct steps have been reported to govern the efficiency of ethylene production (Fig. 1a). The first critical step is the C–C coupling reaction, which acts as a prerequisite for generating dual-carbon products (C2)3,4. The second key step is the deoxygenation via the C–O cleavage in C2 intermediate, which bifurcates the subsequent reaction preferentially toward the formation of ethylene rather than ethanol5,6. The C–C coupling step that typically involving key intermediates such as *CO, *CHO, or *COH, is essential for the formation of multi-carbon products4,7–9. The early studies have considered this step proceeding via the dimerization of *CO species10,11. However, recent insights from in situ spectroscopic techniques and first-principles calculations have revealed that direct *CO–CO coupling exhibits a high energy barrier due to the significant electrostatic repulsion between two negatively charged *CO species adsorbed on pure metallic Cu surface12–14. Instead, the alternative pathway through the CO–CHO or CO–COH asymmetric interaction is energetically more favorable, which requires the involvement of Cu cites with distinguished chemical states15–17. Therefore, the catalyst design strategies, including molecular surface modification18,19, doping20,21, and alloying22,23, have demonstrated effectiveness in facilitating the *CO–CHO/*CO–COH coupling pathway by the generation of Cu+ sites and establishment of Cu+···Cu0 pairs. In this regard, the Cu+ sites exhibit a stronger *CO adsorption capability, which promoting the necessary hydrogenation steps to give *CHO, or *COH before C–C coupling24–26. Nevertheless, the maintenance of such Cu+ species under reducing reaction conditions remains challenging due to their inherent instability, making the precise control and sustained stabilization of Cu+ sites as a critical issue for further catalyst development27.

Following C–C coupling, the synthesis of either ethanol or ethylene is determined by the chemical property of the derived C2 intermediate7–9. Generally, an enhanced kinetics of deoxygenation from this intermediate favor the ethylene formation28–30. Previous studies have shown that this process can be modulated through various catalyst design strategies, including the introduction of metal dopants29, surface modification31,32, and the engineering of interfaces30,33. Fundamentally, these approaches alter the adsorption strength of the C2 intermediate on catalysts through metal-C or metal-O binding34,35. To promote the selectivity of ethylene, the improvement of the oxygen affinity of catalysts can strengthen metal-O binding, thereby facilitating C–O bond cleavage in the C2 intermediate36. In this regard, the decoration of Cu catalysts with oxyphilic metal oxides (e.g., undercoordinated MO1-x) and the optimization of the chemical interactions between C2 intermediate and MO1-x may offer an effective strategy to promote C–O bond cleavage and steer the reaction toward ethylene.

As noted, both the C–C coupling step and the C–O bond cleavage play the crucial roles in determining the overall efficiency for ethylene production. Therefore, to achieve high ethylene selectivity, both the kinetics of two steps must be optimized synergistically. However, it remains a major challenge to simultaneously manipulate these elementary processes, as they require distinct active site properties, i.e., the C–C coupling requires asymmetric Cu sites present in the catalyst, whereas the C–O bond cleavage in C2 intermediate demands the higher oxygen affinity of the catalyst.

To tackle these difficulties, we have proposed a rational catalyst design strategy and systematically screened the MO1-x modified Cu catalysts using density functional theory (DFT) calculations, explicitly considering both C–C coupling and C–O bond cleavage. Theoretical results identified the MgO1-x modified Cu catalyst as a promising candidate for electrocatalytic CO2-to-ethylene conversion. In terms of experiment, we further synthesized the unsaturated MgO1-x on the surface of Cu catalysts via a spontaneous electrochemical induced phase separation method and optimized the loading of MgO1-x. A combined analysis of experimental characterizations and theoretical computations revealed that the bifunctionality of unsaturated MgO1-x. On one hand, chemical interaction between MgO1-x clusters and Cu surface stabilized and gave rises to the asymmetric Cu+···Cu0 pairs, facilitating the *CO–CHO coupling. On the other hand, the oxyphilic MgO1-x strengthened the binding of C2+ intermediate on catalyst surface while weakened the C–O bond in this species, thereby accelerating ethylene formation. Ultimately, electrochemical tests demonstrated that the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst achieved 78.2% ethylene at 300 mA cm−2 in flow cell and 72.9% ethylene at 500 mA cm−2 in membrane electrode assembly (MEA) cell with the durability beyond 100 h.

Results

Descriptor-guided oxygen affinity engineering and synthesis of MgO1-x/Cu

Motivated by the design strategy that simultaneously optimizes C–C coupling and the deoxygenation of C2 intermediate (C–O cleavage), we carried out theoretical screening of catalysts. Based on our previous finding24, we built a series of unsaturated oxyphilic metal oxide (i.e., MO1-x) and placed them on the commonly used and thermodynamically stable surface of a Cu(111) slab33,37, where the unsaturated MO1-x species includes the candidates commonly used in CO2RR, including GaO1-x38, CaO1-x39, AlO1-x35, MgO1-x40, InO1-x41, CeO1-x28, and ZnO1-x24 (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). In this screening stage, implicit solvation models were used to speed up the calculations.

It has been reported that the presence of Cu+ sites plays a critical role for C–C coupling. Previous studies have confirmed that Cu+ species are typically generated as the result of interfacial electron transfer between surface metal oxides and Cu catalyst42. To verify the direction of electron transfer, charge density difference analysis was performed on the MO1-x/Cu model. The results reveal clear electron accumulation around the MO1-x species and electron depletion on the adjacent Cu atoms (Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating that electrons are transferred from Cu to the unsaturated MO1-x cluster. In the following, the quantitative Bader charge values are used as the descriptor of Cu+ density to correlate with the C–C coupling barrier43–45 (Fig. 1b, and Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5). The results show a volcano-type relationship. The MgO1-x/Cu with moderate Bader charges value of Cu is located at the peak of this volcano plot with the energy barrier of 0.03 eV for C–C coupling, suggesting that an optimal fraction of Cu+ is required for the C–C coupling kinetics, consistent with the Sabatier principle46.

In addition to C–C coupling, the deoxygenation of the C2 intermediate was also investigated. As for this process, we adopted the widely accepted *CH2CHO as C2 intermediate in literature47,48. After the C–C coupling on Cu surface of MO1-x/Cu, the *CH2CHO is observed to stabilize via the oxygen end binding with the oxyphilic MO1-x (Supplementary Fig. 6). The calculation indicates the transformation of *CH2CHO to ethylene primarily involves three proton-coupled electron transfers (PCET), i.e., hydrogenation on the O site, C–O cleavage, and ethylene formation. Therefore, in order to screen out the optimal MO1-x on Cu surface for the synthesis of ethylene, the adsorption energy of *CH2CHO (ΔGads(*CH2CHO)) was used as a descriptor and correlated with the maximum free energy change during this reaction pathway from *CH2CHO to ethylene, i.e., ΔGmax(*CH2CHO → C2H4). As shown in Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 7, catalysts with relatively weak ΔGads(*CH2CHO), such as ZnO1-x/Cu, GaO1-x/Cu, and InO1-x/Cu, exhibit high energy barriers during the first hydrogenation step. In contrast, catalysts with relatively strong ΔGads(*CH2CHO), including AlO1-x/Cu, CeO1-x/Cu, and CaO1-x/Cu, show high energy barriers in the subsequent C–O cleavage step (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, MgO1-x/Cu demonstrates moderate ΔGads(*CH2CHO), which leads to the lowest ΔGmax(*CH2CHO→ethylene) value of 0.35 eV, verifying that an optimal oxygen affinity of MO1-x is required for the synthesis of ethylene. We further performed COHP analysis on the M–O bond of the OCHCH2 intermediate to assess its correlation with ΔGmax(*CH2CHO→ethylene). The resulting trend also mirrors that derived from ΔGads(*CH2CHO), with MgO1-x/Cu consistently identified as the optimal catalyst (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). It should be noted that the DFT calculations are based on idealized surface models and static approximations, which may not fully capture the complex local environment of the catalyst under operating conditions. Therefore, the calculated energy barriers and mechanistic insights should be interpreted as qualitative trends rather than absolute predictions.

Based on the DFT screening results presented above, MgO1-x is found to be able to simultaneously promote both C–C coupling and C–O cleavage among different MO1-x/Cu catalysts. This bifunctionality renders MgO1-x/Cu as a potential catalyst for ethylene production. However, conventional synthesis methods such as hydrothermal or calcination usually cause excessive growth of crystallite MgO, which making it difficult to prepare the unsaturated MgO1-x with high dispersion. To address this issue, we implemented an in situ electrochemical activation method involving spontaneous fragmentation and segregation process49,50. To obtain the unsaturated MgO1-x phase, the Mg-doped CuO was initially synthesized using the sol-gel method as the precursor (Supplementary Table 1). Both Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Fig. 1d) and X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern (Supplementary Fig. 11) reveal that magnesium exists in a dopant state with almost atomic dispersion in the CuO matrix. Subsequently, the Mg-doped CuO precursor was subjected to electrochemical reduction at a current density of −10 mA cm−2 for 120 min. Post-reduction EDS mapping shows a redistribution of magnesium. As illustrated in Fig. 1e, magnesium is eventually enriched at the exterior surface of the Cu particles, contrasting sharply with its initial homogeneous distribution. High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) was employed to investigate the crystallographic phase and degree of crystallinity after the electrochemical induced phase separation. As shown in Fig. 1f, amorphous regions are observed at the edge of the particle, corresponding to the magnesium-rich zones identified by EDS mapping, and are ascribed to amorphous MgO1-x. Moreover, the analysis of lattice fringes identified metallic Cu as the domain in the particle with a Cu2O phase underneath MgO1-x layer, the lattice spaces of which are measured as 0.209 nm and 0.218 nm, corresponding to Cu(111) and Cu2O (200) planes. The OH- adsorption experiments were also conducted to probe the exposed Cu facets of the electrochemically reduced Mg-doped CuO sample. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 12, they exhibit predominantly Cu(111)-dominant facets51, consistent with the TEM characterization results.

The quasi-in situ XRD was performed on samples at different stages of electroreduction (Supplementary Fig. 13). Quasi-in situ XRD analysis shows that after 30 min of electrochemical reaction, peaks corresponding to metallic Cu and Cu2O emerge, while the CuO phase still remains. After 90 min, the CuO phase completely disappears, the proportion of the Cu phase increases significantly, and the Cu2O phase persists. These XRD results confirm the retention of the Cu2O phase in the catalyst structure. Meanwhile, the absence of any Mg-related diffraction peaks suggests that the generated Mg species is amorphous. We further collected catalysts after 30 min and 90 min of electrochemical reaction for quasi-in situ TEM characterization. After 30 min, EDS mapping revealed regions where Mg and Cu remained largely uniformly distributed (Supplementary Fig. 14). Within such regions, HRTEM analysis indicated that the dominant phase was CuO, with CuO(111) lattice spacings (0.230 nm) slightly smaller than the standard values (0.234 nm) due to Mg doping52. After 90 min, it can be observed extensive segregation of Mg species, with Mg and Cu distributions becoming less uniform (Supplementary Fig. 14). In a region where Mg showed a strong EDS signal, HRTEM analysis clearly revealed an amorphous outer layer corresponding to the Mg-rich area identified by EDS mapping. Adjacent to the MgO-containing region, phase identification showed that Cu2O was present in close proximity to MgO, while metallic Cu dominated in areas slightly farther from MgO1-x. This observation directly demonstrates that the segregation of MgO1-x is intimately associated with the electrochemical process and that the presence of MgO1-x is a key factor stabilizing Cu+.

These characterizations demonstrate that the magnesium undergoes phase separation to the surface during electrochemical reduction. However, while this separation has not been reported in previous papers, its dynamic mechanism remains unexplored. Common phase separation processes can proceed either through direct atomic diffusion in the solid state or via a dissolution-redeposition process. To explore the magnesium separation from the Cu phase, the chelating agent of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) was intentionally added into the cathodic electrolyte to check the existence of dissolved Mg2+ ions due to the coordination effect53–55. The EDTA molecules can indeed partition into the electrical double layer, as evidenced by the change in open-circuit potential (OCP) presented in Supplementary Fig. 15. This allows EDTA to compete effectively with the precipitation pathway and chelate Mg2+ ions prior to their deposition, thereby enabling the capture and subsequent detection of dissolved Mg species. After electrochemical reduction in the EDTA cathodic electrolyte, MgO1-x fails to re-deposit on the Cu surface. As evidenced in Supplementary Fig. 16, EDS mapping results reveal very few magnesium species visible on the Cu after reduction in EDTA-contained electrolyte. Meanwhile, the concentrations of Mg2+ in the electrolyte solution after reduction with or without EDTA were quantified and compared using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 17, the residual Mg2+ concentration reaches 0.43 mg L−1 in EDTA-containing electrolyte, whereas it is only 0.04 mg L−1 in the electrolyte without EDTA, confirming that the separation between Mg and Cu phases follows a dissolution-redeposition pathway, i.e., Mg atoms diffuse from the bulk to the surface of Cu domain during electroreduction, then dissolve in the electrolyte solution and ultimately re-precipitated on the Cu surface. This finding has also been observed in previous reports on the spontaneous dissolution of oxyphilic metals in electrolyte22.

Subsequently, this phase segregation process was elucidated from thermodynamics and kinetics. Under the synthesis condition of an applied negative potential of ~1 V versus the reversible hydrogen electrode (vs. RHE) and a strongly alkaline environment (pH ≥ 14), the exsolution of Mg2+ cations from the catalyst layer to the electrolyte was estimated to have two distinct boundary concentrations. A localized concentration of 2.0 mol kg−1 was predicted in a confined layer approximately 10 μm thickness adjacent to the catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 18)56, while a much lower concentration of 2 × 10−6 mol kg−1 was calculated for the uniform dispersion in the whole bulk electrolyte. Based on these results, a Pourbaix diagram was plotted to analyze the stable phase of different magnesium species. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 19, Mg2+ can be thermodynamically stabilized as MgO under the working conditions. Given the EDS-mapping detection of surface magnesium species, it can be inferred that the actual Mg2+ local concentration near the catalyst surface have reached the precipitation threshold. Due to the low miscibility with Cu, magnesium oxide is inevitably in situ generated on the catalyst surface as shown in Fig. 1e–g. Furthermore, the nudged elastic band (NEB) method was employed to gain the kinetic insights of dissolution–redeposition process. As the initial state, the atomic MgO structure was buried beneath the Cu (111) surface, which representing the doping structure. Considering the working potential of −1.0 V (vs. RHE) used in the synthesis process, a uniform electric field with an intensity of −0.05 eVÅ−1 was set along the Z-axis with dipole correction to prevent its interference on the system. With regard to the Cu–Mg phase separation process, a series of transition states were searched and predicted, which clearly demonstrating the Mg exsolution and redeposition phenomena. The associated energy changes and dynamic structure evolution were calculated and presented in Fig. 1h and Supplementary Fig. 20. During the exsolution of Mg atoms, one barrier (step 1→step 2) is identified associated with the migration of Mg atom from catalyst bulk to the surface with the energy changes of 0.35 eV. The process is energy-favorable at the working conditions with −1.0 V (vs. RHE). Subsequently, Mg coordinates with the residual O atoms in Cu lattice, leading to an energy reduction of 0.48 eV of the whole system (step 6→step 11), suggesting that the Mg deposition on the catalyst surface and subsequent atomic rearrangement occur spontaneously. The corresponding structural and chemical states were further characterized.

Chemical and geometrical characterizations of MgO1-x/Cu

To elucidate the properties of this unsaturated MgO1-x modified Cu catalyst, we also prepared the MgO1-x/Cu-H and MgO1-x/Cu-L with two different Mg contents by the same electroreduction approach, where H and L representing the abbreviations of high and low loadings of Mg. To study the effect of oxygen affinity, a well-crystallized MgO/Cu was also obtained by impregnation and H2 thermal reduction. The Mg loadings are determined by ICP-MS as following, i.e., 2.5 wt% for MgO1-x/Cu, 2.8 wt% for MgO/Cu, 3.8 wt% for MgO1-x/Cu-H, and 1.2 wt% for MgO1-x/Cu-L (Supplementary Table 2). For comparison, a pure Cu catalyst was derived by the electroreduction of CuO with same voltage as well.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) was utilized to analyze the surface oxides coverage and morphology on the catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22, both of MgO1-x/Cu-H and MgO1-x/Cu-L also exhibit the amorphous MgO1-x structure, similar to MgO1-x/Cu. In contrast, the post-annealed MgO/Cu catalyst exhibits separated and crystalline MgO (Supplementary Fig. 23). On the other side, MgO1-x/Cu-H displays an overlay of aggregated magnesium oxides on Cu surface, while MgO1-x/Cu-L has the scattered and discrete MgO1-x motifs on Cu surface. Therefore, the surface coverage of MgO1-x follows the order of MgO1-x/Cu-H > MgO1-x/Cu > MgO1-x/Cu-L.

The chemical states of magnesium oxide were examined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Figure 2a displays the Mg 1s spectra for four Cu supported Mg catalysts. The crystalline MgO/Cu catalyst exhibits a characteristic peak at 1303.5 eV, consistent with bulk MgO. In contrast, other three samples (MgO1-x/Cu-H, MgO1-x/Cu, and MgO1-x/Cu-L) synthesized via electrochemical approach show an apparent negative shift to 1302.4 eV, aligning with prior reports of electron-rich Mg species in disordered oxides57. The soft X-ray absorption spectra (XAS) were also utilized to investigate the fine structures of the surface magnesium oxide. As shown in Fig. 2b, for crystalline MgO/Cu with a symmetric Oh structure of MgO6 (Mg–O coordination number = 6), a sharp peak at 1309.9 eV is observed. As reported by Yoshida et al., this peak corresponds to a 1s-3p transition58. However, in the three samples with electrochemical induced phase separated MgO1-x, they all exhibit diminished 1s-3p transition peaks. This is attributed to the disruption of the symmetrical structure of the MgO6 unit in the unsaturated MgO1-x. To obtain more reliable quantitative information, we performed DFT-based XANES fitting of the spectra. Upon XANES fitting (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 24), it is confirmed that the low-coordinated MgO1-x structure with Mg–O coordination number at 4 for all the in situ electrochemically synthesized catalysts, lower than that of coordination-saturated MgO/Cu, which is consistent with XPS results. The reduction of coordination number from 6 to 4 demonstrated the electrochemical induced phase separation gave rise to the unsaturated MgO1-x structure. Taken the XPS and soft XAS results into account, the variation in term of Mg loading only accounts for the surface coverage change while no significant effect on the coordination of Mg atoms probably owing to much low loading below 4 wt% compared to Cu domain and short period of Mg redeposition at room temperature were not kinetic-favorable for crystallization59.

The amorphous nature of MgO1-x may introduce abundant oxygen vacancies. To investigate this, we performed oxygen vacancy analysis on the Mg-containing catalysts. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 25, the O 1s spectra of electrochemically induced samples (MgO1-x/Cu, MgO1-x/Cu-L, and MgO1-x/Cu-H) exhibit a distinct shoulder feature at approximately 530.5 eV, which is commonly assigned to oxygen vacancies or defect sites in oxide materials60. In contrast, the thermally annealed MgO/Cu sample shows a much weaker feature in this region, indicating a lower concentration of oxygen vacancies. To quantitatively analyze the oxygen vacancy content, we deconvoluted the O 1s spectra into three components: lattice oxygen (Olattice, ~529.0 eV), oxygen vacancy/defect sites (Ovacancy, ~530.0 eV), and adsorbed OH- species (Oadsorbed OH-, ~531.0 eV)60. The fitting results reveal that the relative concentrations of oxygen vacancies are 21.7% for MgO1-x/Cu-L, 21.6% for MgO1-x/Cu, and 23.5% for MgO1-x/Cu-H, much higher than 11.9% for MgO/Cu. Notably, the similarity in oxygen vacancy concentrations across the three MgO1-x-modified samples is consistent with our previous findings from Mg 1s XPS and Mg K-edge XANES analyses, which indicated that the coordination environment and chemical state of Mg are comparable regardless of loading.

As predicted by the rational design of MO1-x/Cu in Fig. 1c, the strong oxygen affinity of unsaturated MgO1-x is believed to affect adsorption of critical oxygenated C2 intermediates during the CO2RR. To investigate the coordination effect of Mg, DFT calculations were performed to examine the chemical interaction with the intermediate *CH2CHO. The results indicate that *CH2CHO tends to adsorb on MgO1-x with a binding energy of −1.89 eV (Fig. 2d). This adsorption energy is substantially lower than that on a pure MgO/Cu (−0.16 eV), demonstrating that the unsaturated MgO1-x significantly enhances the oxygen affinities of three MgO1-x/Cu catalysts.

The HRTEM analysis revealed that there existed a transition phase of Cu2O between MgO1-x and Cu, indicating a clear chemical interaction between these two species. Based on that, the MgO1-x coverage effect on this interaction was further investigated for three MgO1-x/Cu catalysts. Firstly, we employed DFT calculations to check the valence state of Cu for the samples with different loadings of MgO1-x. As shown in Fig. 2e, models with varying coverages of MgO1-x on the Cu surface were constructed. The results reveal that the population of Cu+ species increases with increasing MgO1-x coverage, indicating a linear relationship between the fraction of Cu+ and the amount of MgO1-x. To experimentally verify the oxidation states of Cu, we performed XPS and in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) to characterize the electronic states and fine structures of the Cu phases. Cu LMM Auger spectra were first carried out as it was more sensitive to the surface properties46. Due to the high sensitivity to air for Cu, all samples were stored in an Argon-filled glovebox and directly transferred to the XPS analysis chamber without air exposure. In Supplementary Fig. 26 and Supplementary Table 3, the Cu LMM Auger spectra were deconvoluted using metal Cu and Cu2O as references. The analysis reveals that the pure CuO-derived Cu catalyst is significantly reduced after electrochemical treatment, with Cu+ accounting for only 23.5% of the copper species. For the catalyst containing crystalline MgO, the proportion of Cu+ is increased to 41.1%, suggesting that MgO is able to stabilize the Cu+. However, in the three catalysts containing unsaturated MgO1-x, the proportion of Cu+ is clearly enhanced and scaled with the MgO1-x contents, which are measured as 77.5% for MgO1-x/Cu-H, 61.3% for MgO1-x/Cu, and 53.4% MgO1-x/Cu, demonstrating that compared to well-crystallized MgO, the coordinatively unsaturated MgO1-x exhibits stronger interplay with Cu base that is beneficial to stabilize Cu+.

To directly quantify the dynamic evolution of Cu+ species under working conditions, in situ XAS was conducted in a three-electrode configuration. All samples are found to be reduced to a mixture of Cu0 and Cu+ within the applied potential window, with the Cu0 fraction gradually increasing as the potential is switched to more negative values. Specifically, for pure Cu catalyst derived from CuO reduction (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 27), analysis of the first derivatives of the X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra at Cu K-edge reveals a Cu+ proportion of 30.3% at only −0.5 V (vs. RHE). Upon further decreasing the potential, the Cu+ fraction approaches a stable plateau, reaching 12.1% at −1.1 V (vs. RHE). In contrast, at −1.1 V (vs. RHE), the crystalline MgO/Cu sample exhibits a higher Cu+ proportion of 23.3% (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 28), demonstrating that the presence of MgO can stabilize Cu+ species. Notably, all three MgO1-x loaded Cu samples exhibit even higher Cu+ proportions in total Cu species. At −1.1 V (vs. RHE), the Cu+ proportions are 40.3% for MgO1-x/Cu-L (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 29), 50.1% for MgO1-x/Cu (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 30), and 68.3% for MgO1-x/Cu-H (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 31). These results demonstrate that the unsaturated MgO1-x exhibit high ability to stabilize Cu+ species, which aligns well with the conclusions drawn from the aforementioned XPS analysis. In addition, the relatively lower Cu+ fractions by ~10% characterized by in situ XAS than XPS measurements highlight Cu+ species are more enriched on the catalyst surface, consistent with the Cu2O distribution proved by HRTEM image.

Furthermore, Fourier transform analysis and fitting were performed on the in situ XAS spectra to extract structural information regarding the dynamic changes of Cu–O and Cu–Cu bonds. All samples exhibit contributions from both Cu–Cu and Cu–O coordination shells. The coordination numbers (CN) of Cu–O bonds decrease progressively with increasing applied negative potentials, while the CNs of Cu–Cu bonds concomitantly increase (Fig. 2g, h). For the Cu sample derived from CuO reduction at −1.1 V (vs. RHE), the Cu–O CN decreases to 0.3, whereas the Cu–Cu CN reaches 8.7 (Supplementary Fig. 32 and Supplementary Table 4). Upon introducing crystalline MgO (MgO/Cu sample), the Cu–O CN is measured 0.4 at −1.1 V (vs. RHE), with the Cu–Cu CN decreases to 7.8 (Supplementary Fig. 33 and Supplementary Table 4). The maintained Cu–O CN indicates that the presence of MgO immobilizes a fraction of oxidized copper species. The introduction of amorphous MgO1-x further enhances the stability of the Cu–O coordination environment. Even at low loading (MgO1-x/Cu-L sample), the Cu–O CN remains as high as 0.7 (Supplementary Fig. 34 and Supplementary Table 4), exceeding that observed for the MgO/Cu sample. The addition of MgO1-x loading results in progressively higher Cu–O CNs, with 0.9 for MgO1-x/Cu (Supplementary Fig. 35 and Supplementary Table 4) and 1.2 for MgO1-x/Cu-H (Supplementary Fig. 36 and Supplementary Table 4). The gradual increment of Cu–O CN matches with the trend of Cu+ population determined by XANES and XPS spectra for all samples.

To directly elucidate the relationship between MgO1-x and Cu+ species, we performed depth-resolved XPS analysis on the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst using Ar+ ion etching to progressively remove surface layers. Spectra were collected after each etching cycle to monitor the evolution of Cu+ and Mg species as a function of depth. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 37, two important findings can be observed. First, the Cu⁺ content is closely tied to that of Mg, showing a consistent decrease as the Mg concentration diminishes. Second, both Cu+ and Mg species are predominantly localized within the topmost ~20 nm of the catalyst surface, indicating that the Cu+-MgO1-x interfacial region extends to a depth of approximately 20 nm. These findings are in good agreement with the XAS and XPS data, together demonstrating that the presence of MgO1-x is directly responsible for the retention of Cu+ species.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the in situ electrochemically synthesis consisting of the exsolution and redeposition processes gave rise to coordinatively unsaturated MgO1-x layer adhering to the Cu surface with a strong chemical interaction between core and shell. As the results, this interaction provides a strategy to stabilize the Cu+ site and allows to rationally control the density of Cu+ sites by adjusting the coverage of MgO1-x. This asymmetrical Cu+···Cu0 pair has been reported to play a critical role for C–C coupling during CO2RR. Moreover, due to the low coordination number of Mg–O bindings, the derived MgO1-x species exhibit a high oxygen affinity, which is predicted to enable an appropriate adsorption strength of C2 intermediates on the catalyst for the preferential production of ethylene, which is then evaluated for CO2 electroreduction reaction.

CO2 electroreduction to ethylene on MgO1-x/Cu

To study the influence of MgO1-x on CO2RR performance, Fig. 3b compares the ethylene selectivity profiles of MgO/Cu, MgO1-x/Cu-L, MgO1-x/Cu, MgO1-x/Cu-H, and bare Cu as a function of current density in the range of 50–500 mA cm−2. Basically, the bare Cu catalyst exhibits a 46.8% peak FEC2H4 at 150 mA cm−2, but quickly suffers from rapid methane formation at higher currents (≥300 mA cm−2), significantly suppressing the ethylene production (Supplementary Fig. 39). For MgO/Cu, it shows a slight increment under high-current operation, achieving 57.6% FEC2H4 at 300 mA cm−2 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 40). However, the addition of MgO1-x further improves FEC2H4. The MgO1-x/Cu catalyst achieves 78.2% FEC2H4 at 300 mA cm−2, whereas the MgO1-x/Cu-H catalyst shows a slight reduction to 74.5% FEC2H4 (Fig. 3b), highlighting the volcano-dependence of FEC2H4 on MgO1-x loading with optimal value as 2.5 wt% (Supplementary Figs. 41 and 42). In addition, the FEH2 of the MgO1-x/Cu-x samples remained consistently lower than that of pure Cu under high CO2 concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 43), indicating that CO2 mass transport is not compromised within the tested MgO1-x loading range. Furthermore, we normalized the activity by the electrochemical surface area (ECSA) and found that the volcano-type relationship remains essentially unchanged, confirming that the promotional effect of MgO1-x on Cu remains clearly evident (Supplementary Figs. 44 and 45). This MgO1-x/Cu has also represented competitive electrocatalytic CO2-to-ethylene catalysts (Supplementary Table 5). Moreover, the stability of MgO1-x/Cu was also evaluated through the chronopotentiometry test at 300 mA cm−2 for over 100 h. The potential is maintained at approximately −1.1 V (vs. RHE), and the average FEC2H4 remains at 71.8% (Fig. 3c).

The leaching behavior of Mg was also investigated over prolonged operation. Electrolysis was conducted in the flow cell at a constant current density of 300 mA cm−2, and the catholyte was collected at different time intervals for ICP-MS analysis. As summarized in Supplementary Fig. 46, the dissolved Mg2+ concentrations were 0.8 μg (3.4% of total Mg) at 5 h, 1.3 μg (5.5% of total Mg) at 15 h, and 1.7 μg (7.4% of total Mg) at 45 h of continuous operation. These results indicate that Mg leaching is minimal, and the MgO1-x species remain stably anchored on the Cu surface under the reaction conditions. Furthermore, post-reaction characterization was performed on the catalyst after 10 h of continuous operation at 300 mA cm−2. TEM-EDS mapping clearly revealed the presence of MgO1-x species (Fig. 3d), while XRD analysis showed that diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu2O remained detectable (Supplementary Fig. 47a). Post-reaction XPS analysis shows that the oxygen vacancy concentration remains at 27.0% (Supplementary Fig. 47b), demonstrating the structural stability of the catalyst. Additionally, quantitative XANES analysis confirmed the persistence of Cu+ species, with a Cu+ fraction of 40.3% (Supplementary Fig. 48). Collectively, these results demonstrate that MgO1-x not only remains stable itself under reaction conditions but also effectively stabilizes Cu+ species.

The incorporation of MgO1-x demonstrates to enhance the CO2-to-ethylene conversion. However, the underlying mechanism and structure-activity relationship remained ambiguous. To elucidate these issues, we first examined several possible contributing factors, including CO2 mass transport, surface wettability, interfacial water structure, interfacial pH, and electrical conductivity.

To probe the influence of CO2 mass transport on the catalytic performance, we conducted a control experiment under low CO2 concentration conditions, monitoring the change in FEH2. Under such conditions, the surface *CO coverage inevitably decreases, leading to a rise in H2 evolution61. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 49, upon switching from pure CO2 to 30% CO2, both catalysts exhibited a decrease in ethylene selectivity and an increase in hydrogen evolution at 300 mA cm−2. However, the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst displayed a more pronounced increase in H2 Faradaic efficiency (from 4.6% to 28.8%) compared to pure Cu (from 13.6% to 18.6%). This trend became even more evident upon further reducing the CO2 concentration to 10%. However, the observation is inconsistent with a mass-transport-dominated mechanism. If MgO1-x/Cu possessed good mass transport characteristics, it should better tolerate the reduced CO2 concentration. Therefore, attributing the enhanced activity of the MgO1-x-modified Cu catalyst to improved CO2 mass transport is not correct. Instead, the observed results suggest that the presence of MgO1-x may partially hinder CO2 transport to the catalyst surface—an effect that is not pronounced under high CO2 concentration but becomes more evident under low CO2 conditions.

Surface wettability was also examined, as it directly influences the accessibility of CO2 and H2O to the catalyst surface and may therefore affect both activity and selectivity62. After electrochemical reduction, the electrodes were cleaned, dried, and subjected to water contact angle measurements. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 50, the contact angles of all samples fall within the range of 130°–140°, with only slight variation, suggesting that surface hydrophobicity is not a critical factor governing the observed catalytic performance.

Additionally, the structure of interfacial water plays a role in modulating proton supply, which could in turn impact the CO2RR product distribution. Therefore, the interfacial water structure was investigated using in situ attenuated total reflectance-surface enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) spectroscopy for Cu, MgO/Cu, and MgO1-x/Cu (Supplementary Fig. 51). Based on their hydrogen-bonding configurations, interfacial water molecules are typically categorized into three types: 4-hydrogen-bonded (4-HB) H2O (~3200 cm−1), 2-hydrogen-bonded (2-HB) H2O (~3400 cm−1), and free H2O (~3600 cm−1)63. Free H2O, which is weakly hydrogen-bonded, is considered the most readily dissociable species and thus most relevant to proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) kinetics64. To investigate whether MgO1-x modifies the interfacial water structure, we deconvoluted the O–H stretching region in these infrared spectra (Supplementary Fig. 52). The fitting results show that introduction of MgO or MgO1-x leads to only a marginal increase (1–2%) in the proportion of free H2O (Supplementary Fig. 53), which is insufficient to account for the substantial changes in product selectivity.

Furthermore, the interfacial pH was also considered, as it plays a critical role in the C–C coupling process. We performed in situ Raman spectroscopy to probe the HCO3-/CO32- ratio (Supplementary Fig. 54), which serves as a sensitive indicator of interfacial pH65. A higher CO32-/HCO3- ratio corresponds to a more alkaline local environment. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 55–57, the CO32-/HCO3- ratio increases with the applied potential, reflecting a gradual rise in interfacial alkalinity. Nevertheless, the overall ratio remains within the range of 8–9 for all samples, suggesting that the introduction of MgO and MgO1-x does not substantially alter the local pH under operating conditions.

Catalyst conductivity was also evaluated, as the weak electrical conductivity of MgO1-x could potentially influence electron transfer processes. For the conductivity measurements, we designed a custom cell to measure the resistance of the catalyst layer (Supplementary Fig. 58). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 59, the measured resistances for the electroreduced Cu, MgO/Cu, and MgO1-x/Cu were found to be similar, all approximately 1.9 Ω. This observation is likely attributable to the relatively low MgO1-x loading, which does not substantially affect the overall conductivity of the catalyst. Based on the above results, conductivity can therefore be excluded as a major contributing factor.

Based on the above analysis, the electronic modulation imparted by MgO1-x emerges as the most plausible origin of the enhanced performance. To test this hypothesis, we correlated the C2+ product selectivity with the Cu+ fractions (determined by in situ XAS). As illustrated in Fig. 3e, a volcano relationship is observed. The FE of multi-carbon products (C2+) climbs upward linearly with Cu+ proportion and reaches a maximum of 84.5% at a Cu+ fraction of 52.5%, corresponding to the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst. The advantage of Cu+ in enhancing C2+ formation aligns well with literature that have regarded Cu+ to be able to promote asymmetric C–C coupling24,28. However, the FE of C2+ changes to decrease when Cu+ fraction increases further, which is also consistent with our previous findings, as the excessive Cu+ sites are unfavorable for the C–C coupling process24. Hence, the optimized coverage of MgO1-x with the loading of 2.5 wt% on Cu obtains a proper density of Cu+ sites and facilitates the production of C2+ species.

Although the C–C coupling has been facilitated at the optimal Cu+ fraction in total Cu species, the subsequent reaction is theoretically not prevailed by the production of any C2+ product such as ethylene or ethanol. However, the ethylene/ethanol ratios vary considerably across three MgO1-x/Cu-x catalysts, MgO/Cu and Cu (Supplementary Fig. 60). This observation implies that some factors beyond the C–C coupling affects the bifurcated formation of ethylene and ethanol. As predicted by our rational design calculation, MgO1-x/Cu with high oxygen affinity could achieve the appropriate adsorption strength of C2 intermediate at the optimal coverage of MgO1-x and then facilitate the C–O bond cleavage, thereby increasing the selectivity of ethylene. To validate this hypothesis, we assessed the deoxygenation capability (i.e, C–O cleavage) of these catalysts using the ethylene oxide reduction reaction (EORR) as the probe technique, due to the ethylene oxide (CH2CH2O) shares close structural similarity with the *CH2CHO intermediate. Its conversion to ethylene involves O adsorption and C–O bond cleavage, analogous to the *CH2CHO-to-ethylene step29,66. More importantly, ethylene is the sole product of EORR that requires the essential C–O bond cleavage pathway. However, it should be acknowledged certain limitations inherent to the use of EORR as a mechanistic probe. The reaction environment in EORR differs substantially from that of CO2RR, particularly with respect to interfacial pH, intermediate coverage. Therefore, the EORR results should be regarded as a simplified descriptor of the main reaction pathway, rather than an entirely equivalent model system. As presented in Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 61, with gaseous ethylene oxide feeding, the EORR activity toward ethylene follows the order as MgO1-x/Cu-H > MgO1-x/Cu > MgO/Cu > MgO1-x/Cu-L > Cu. Bare Cu catalyst exhibits minimal EORR activity, while significant enhancement occurs upon introducing MgO1-x. This directly links C–O bond cleavage efficacy to the presence of Mg oxide species. Further analysis reveals a strong linear correlation between EORR activity and the ratio of ethylene to ethanol, i.e., the catalysts with high EORR activity deliver higher ethylene selectivity during CO2RR (Fig. 3g). It should be mentioned that although MgO1-x/Cu-H possesses the highest EORR activity, its C–C coupling rate is slower than that of MgO1-x/Cu, thereby resulting in a lower FEC2H4.

Hence, the above correlations between electrochemical performance, in particular, the FEC2H4, and the geometric and chemical properties of MgO1-x/Cu, i.e., coverage of MgO1-x and high oxygen affinity, interpret that the high electrochemical performance of MgO1-x/Cu could be attributed to two key factors. Initially, the proper density of Cu+ obtained on the optimized MgO1-x loading enables efficient asymmetric C–C coupling. In the subsequent step, the unsaturated MgO1-x, which exhibits an appropriate binding strength toward the *CH2CHO intermediate, is proposed to facilitate C–O bond cleavage, thereby favoring ethylene formation.

Upon introduction of MgO, the *CO peak undergoes a redshift accompanied by an higher Stark shift, indicating a stronger interaction between Cu and *CO68. It is worth noting that the in situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements were performed with CO2 dissolved in the electrolyte, a configuration that differs from the gas-phase CO2 feeding conditions typically employed in CO2RR. As a result, the observed intermediates are relatively limited under these conditions. To capture a broader range of intermediates, we employed in situ Raman spectroscopy in a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) configuration, which ensures a sufficient CO2 supply. The in situ Raman spectra are presented in Supplementary Figs. 63–66. In the low-wavenumber region, asymmetric peaks corresponding to the *CO band (~350 cm−1) was observed. These peaks typically consist of two components regarding the restricted rotation of adsorbed Cu–CO (P1) and the Cu–CO stretching vibration (P2), which accounts for its asymmetric line shape69. Following background subtraction and normalization, the spectra show that, relative to bare Cu, both MgO1-x/Cu and MgO/Cu exhibit a marked reduction in the spectral intensity in the P1 region (280 cm−1) under high cathodic potentials (Supplementary Fig. 63). The weak P1 intensity has been established as a reliable indicator for *CO surface coverage, with higher values corresponding to higher *CO coverage70. This indicates that *CO coverage on MgO1-x/Cu and MgO/Cu is higher than Cu, which is consistent with the ATR-SEIRAS findings. This trend in *CO adsorption strength is further corroborated by the CO-TPD results (Supplementary Fig. 67), confirming the reliability of the observed results.

Furthermore, the characteristic bands of Cu2O (~525 cm−1) were also observed in the in situ Raman spectra (Supplementary Figs. 64–66). These bands persisted up to −1.0 V for MgO1-x/Cu, whereas they completely disappeared at the same potential for both MgO/Cu and pure Cu. This observation provides direct spectroscopic evidence for the enhanced resistance of MgO1-x/Cu against electrochemical reduction, which is in good agreement with the in situ XAS results (Fig. 2f). Additionally, a feature assignable to *CHO (~1130 cm−1) was observed exclusively on MgO/Cu and MgO1-x/Cu71, indicating that the reaction proceeds via a *CHO-involved pathway. Considering the high energy barrier for direct CO-CO coupling, the reaction is more likely to follow an asymmetric CO–CHO coupling route72.

To elucidate the enhancement of MgO1-x on the electrochemical synthesis of ethylene from CO2RR at a molecular level, we calculated the reaction pathway based on the mechanism revealed by the in situ ATR-SEIRAS and Raman analysis. As noted above, an unsaturated MgO1-x cluster was loaded on the surface of a Cu(111) slab to simulate MgO1-x/Cu sample. Besides this, a well-crystallized MgO supported on Cu(111) was also established as the control sample to study the influence of defects in magnesium oxide. In this section, explicit solvation models with 25 water molecules were added to provide a more accurate description of the aqueous environment. As shown in Fig. 4d, the reaction pathway of CO2RR was calculated and consisted of CO2 reduction to C1 intermediates, asymmetric C–C coupling and subsequent hydrodeoxygenation to produce ethylene. The initial two processes are found to occur on the Cu surface. Afterwards, the C2 intermediates migrate to the adjacent Mg site and are subjected to the succeeding reactions. To validate the advantage of Cu+, we compare the energy changes related to the CO2 reduction to *CO and the following hydrogenation to *CHO via two PCET steps on Cu surface with or without Cu+ (Supplementary Fig. 68). The results show that the lower energies of 0.07 eV verify that the presence of Cu+ is more favorable for the accumulation of *CHO compared to pure Cu0 surface (0.38 eV). The accumulation of *CHO would enhance the asymmetric C–C coupling, which is beneficial for the formation of C2+ species14.

After the formation of C1 intermediate, the C–C coupling and hydrodeoxygenation represented two key determination steps during CO2RR based on the associated energy changes computed in Fig. 4d with the corresponding adsorbate models illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 69. The comparison demonstrated that MgO1-x/Cu had much lower barriers of 0.35 eV and 0.44 eV, than MgO/Cu with the values of 1.06 eV and 0.70 eV, respectively, consistent with the high performance. Considering the higher *CO coverage on MgO1-x/Cu suggested by in situ spectra, we further evaluated the potential influence of *CO coverage on the energy barriers for the two key steps. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 70, the C–C coupling barrier decreases to 0.08 eV at 15% ML and 0.15 eV at 30% ML. The barrier for *CH2CHO hydrodeoxygenation initially decreases to 0.40 eV at 15% ML, then rises to 0.45 eV at 30% ML. In addition, the energy barrier for the ethanol pathway was also calculated. The results (Supplementary Fig. 71) show that the maximum barrier from *CH2CHO to *CH3CH2OH is 1.47 eV, indicating that the ethylene pathway is kinetically more favorable. This result is in good agreement with the experimentally observed higher selectivity toward ethylene over ethanol.

Next, we implemented the projected density of states (PDOS) analysis for Mg–O bindings in MgO1-x/Cu and MgO/Cu, which had been proven to be critical for the stabilization of Cu+. It is noted in Fig. 4e that the orbital interaction in MgO/Cu is primarily limited to weak overlap between Mg 3s and O 2py orbitals. In contrast, for MgO1-x/Cu, a significant hybridization peak between Mg 3s and O 2py orbitals appears in the energy range of −4 to −2 eV in Fig. 4f, suggesting enhanced electron delocalization. Additionally, the high overlapping characteristic of Mg–O in MgO1-x/Cu is still observable even under high potential (Supplementary Fig. 72). This electron distribution and orbital configuration not only generates more empty energy levels above the Fermi level than MgO/Cu that could gain electrons from neighboring Cu site and secure Cu+, but also strengthens the adsorption of *COCHO with a more negative energy of 1.19 eV than the value on MgO/Cu (0.38 eV), suggesting that the modification of unsaturated MgO1-x promote the C–C coupling and also enhance the formation of *COCHO via the stabilization of Cu+, which could be evidenced the higher integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) value as −0.61 (Supplementary Fig. 73) than that calculated for MgO/Cu (−0.47).

Following C–C coupling, the *CHOCO intermediate undergoes a series of PCET steps, which generate the *OCHCH2. The subsequent hydrodeoxygenation of this intermediate (i.e., C–O cleavage) has been regarded to determine the selectivity of ethylene or ethanol. The screened structure in our calculation exhibits that the *OCHCH2 is immobilized via the O end binding with the Mg site. In this regard, we firstly compared the energy differences for hydrogenation of *OCHCH2 on the oxygen and its adjacent carbon site (β-C) between MgO1-x/Cu and MgO/Cu. The calculations in Supplementary Figs. 74–75 reveal that the energy barriers for hydrogenation at the O site are much lower (0.44 eV, 0.70 eV) than that at the β-C site (0.74 eV, 0.94 eV) in both cases of MgO/Cu and MgO1-x/Cu catalysts, confirming that the initial hydrogenation preferentially occurs at the O site. However, the energy barriers for the hydrodeoxygenation of *CH2CHOH to *CH2CH on MgO1-x/Cu are much lower (−0.41 eV) compared to the value (−0.27 eV) of on MgO/Cu, which accounts for the higher FEC2H4 obtained on the former catalyst. The PDOS analysis of the hydrogenation and deoxygenation reveals that the interaction between O 2p and H 1s orbitals in *CH2CHOH on MgO leads to only a single coupling peak near −8 eV (Fig. 4g), indicating weak interaction. In contrast, on the surface of MgO1-x, the corresponding interaction gives rise to a continuous hybridization band at −7 eV (Fig. 4h). Similarly, the corresponding COHP analysis near the Fermi level further confirms that the unoccupied orbitals of the O atom on the MgO1-x surface can accept more electrons than well-crystallized MgO (Supplementary Fig. 76), thereby facilitating the PCET process (i.e., hydrodeoxygenation). As a result, the C–O cleavage rate of *CH2CHOH to *CH2CH is enhanced on MgO1-x, which is evidenced by the weak COHP of −0.40 than the value of −0.57 on MgO (Supplementary Fig. 77). Hence, the high oxygen affinity of MgO1-x/Cu facilitates the hydrodeoxygenation of *OCHCH2, contributing to the enhanced ethylene selectivity.

The membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was equipped with a Sustainion X37-50 anion-exchange membrane (AEM). An IrO2-coated Ti felt was employed as the anode to prevent potential contamination of the cathode by dissolved metal ions. The anode compartment was continuously circulated with 1 M KOH electrolyte. On the cathode side, a stream of humidified CO2 was supplied, with its flow rate and pressure precisely controlled by a mass flow controller and a back-pressure valve, respectively. The overall cell configurations are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 78.

For the MEA operation, the performance is easily limited by flooding at high-current densities. To resolve this, we optimized the catalyst layer structure by adding a porous and hydrophobic overlay of PTFE-modified carbon powder (Fig. 5b, c). The effectiveness of this process improvement is demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 79. Before the modification, a high FEH2 of 20.6% at 0.3 A cm−2 is observed, indicative of severe flooding even at a low current density. After the modification, the same electrolysis set-up maintains a low FEH2 of 10.3% at 0.5 A cm−2. The PTFE-modified carbon powder overlayer can effectively improve the hydrophobicity of the cathode and reduce direct catalyst exposure to electrolyte, thereby suppressing the flooding phenomenon.

After addressing the flooding issue, we assembled MEA electrolyzers with active areas ranging from 1 cm2 to 100 cm2 using the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst (Fig. 5d). Firstly, the performance of the 1 cm2 small-scale MEA electrolyzer was recorded (Supplementary Fig. 80). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 81a, in the 1 cm2 MEA electrolyzer, MgO1-x/Cu exhibits good performance with cell voltages below 3.3 V at 0.5 A cm−2. The MgO1-x/Cu can sustain >60% FEC2H4 across 0.4–0.6 A cm−2, achieving peak ethylene selectivity of 72.9% at 0.5 A cm−2 (Fig. 5e). Subsequently, the MEA electrolyzer was scaled up to 25 cm2. In the 25 cm2 MEA electrolyzer, a single-serpentine flow field leads to a significant decline in CO2 concentration along the flow path, resulting in insufficient CO2 supply. To address this limitation, a multi-serpentine flow field design was adopted to ensure more uniform CO2 distribution and mitigate local depletion (Supplementary Fig. 82)74. In this configuration, the cell voltage is measured at 3.1 V at 5.0 A total current (Supplementary Fig. 81b). As presented in Fig. 5f, the electrolyzer still exhibits a peak FEC2H4 of 63.8% at 5.0 A total current. Further scaling was achieved by stacking bipolar plates to form a 25 × 4 cm2 system. The stack features a series-connected current path and parallel gas/liquid distribution. As presented in Fig. 5g and Supplementary Fig. 81c, at a total current of 25.0 A (3.5 V), the stack achieves 60.7% peak FEC2H4, equivalent to the ethylene production rate of 1.1 L h−1. The above tests demonstrate that the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst enables the MEA electrolyzer to achieve high FEC2H4, which is competitive with the reported values (Supplementary Table 6).

The electrical energy consumption of the aforementioned electrolyzers was calculated (Supplementary Fig. 83). These results show that electrolyzers incorporating MgO1-x/Cu achieve a minimum energy consumption of 53.3, 56.4, and 65.6 kWh kgethylene−1 for the scales of 1 cm2, 25 cm2, and 100 cm2, respectively. Furthermore, based on these MEA performance metrics, a preliminary techno-economic analysis (TEA) was carried out11,46. Under optimistic operational scenarios, with an electricity cost assumption of US$0.01 kWh−1, the projected production cost falls within the range of US$1200 t−1 to US$1600 t−1 (Fig. 5h)23. which is close to the market price of ethylene (US$1200 t−1)75.

Moreover, the stability of the MEA electrolyzers incorporating MgO1-x/Cu catalyst was further evaluated. To mitigate the salt precipitation issues, the concentration of the circulated anolyte was reduced to 0.2 M KOH during durability tests76. In the 1 cm2 MEA electrolyzer, 200 mA cm−2 chronopotentiometry current were applied. As illustrated in Fig. 5i, the FEC2H4 of the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst fluctuates around 57.5% over a 100 h period without discernible decay. A further validation of stability was also examined in a 25 cm2 electrolyzer operating at a total current of 3 A. Over 100 h, the FEC2H4 remains ~50.0%, again with no notable degradation (Supplementary Fig. 84). More importantly, the stability measurement in a 100 cm2 stack cell was ultimately implemented at a total current of 10 A. Over 100 h, the FEC2H4 remains around 51% with no deactivation as well (Fig. 5j). The high durability and stable ethylene production demonstrated by MgO1-x/Cu across multiple operational scales, particularly at the 100 cm2 cell, represents a promise for industrial-level implementation in membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzers.

Additionally, considering that practical operation of the electrolyzer may involve CO2 streams containing impurities, such as those from flue gas, we designed control experiments using CO2 gas containing 1000 ppm SO2 and 500 ppm NO. Under this conditions, rapid failure of the MEA was observed, indicating that flue gas must undergo both desulfurization and denitrification prior to application (Supplementary Fig. 85).

Furthermore, to simulate intermittent operation under wind- and solar-powered conditions, we performed an on-off cycling test consisting of 10,000 s of electrolysis followed by 1000 s at OCP condition. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 86, the MEA exhibited stable operation for over 50 h, maintaining an FEC2H4 above 40%. After ~10 h of on-off cycling, the cell was disassembled and the catalyst was analyzed by EDS mapping, which revealed that MgO1-x remained intact on the Cu catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 87). These results demonstrate that the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst is capable of withstanding fluctuating operational conditions.

Discussion

In this work, the DFT screening predicted a MgO1-x/Cu catalyst that can simultaneously modulate C–C coupling and C–O cleavage kinetics for the efficient CO2RR to ethylene. To validate this, an electrochemical phase separation approach was implemented to synthesize this catalyst. The in situ XAS assisted by theoretical calculations confirmed MgO1-x with the optimal loading on Cu achieved an appropriate density of Cu+ sites that facilitating asymmetric C–C coupling. In addition, more structure-reactivity studies provided evidence that the unsaturated MgO1-x accelerates C–O bond cleavage via favorable interactions with the oxygen atom of the *CH2CHO intermediate, thereby contributing to its selective conversion to ethylene. As a result, in the flow cell, the MgO1-x/Cu catalyst exhibited an ethylene selectivity of 78% at a current density of 300 mA cm−2. Moreover, to demonstrate the practical potential in industrial production of ethylene, this catalyst was also applied in the membrane electrode assembly electrolyzer with the scales from 1 cm2 up to 100 cm2. And it delivered high ethylene selectivity reaching 72.9% at 500 mA cm−2 and stable ethylene production for long-term operation beyond 100 h. This work offers a catalyst design paradigm that concurrently optimizes multiple elementary steps for electrocatalytic CO2-to-ethylene conversion.

Methods

Materials

All the materials used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. In addition, to modify the carbon paper, 100 mg of PTFE powder (100 nm) was dispersed in 10 mL of ethanol by ultrasonication and then spray-coated onto the backside of the carbon paper at a loading of approximately 2 mL cm−2. After drying, the substrate was calcined at 350 °C for 1 h under N2 atmosphere.

Synthesis of Mg-doped CuO

Initially, 240 mg of Cu(NO3)2•3H2O was dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol under stirring. After complete dissolution, 20 mg of Mg(NO3)2•6H2O was added, followed by 5 min of stirring to ensure dissolution. Subsequently, 100 mg of citric acid was introduced as the chelating agent. The solution was then stirred and heated at 150 °C to evaporate the ethanol solvent. After 20 min, the solvent had completely evaporated, resulting in the gel formation. After 60 min, the gel transformed into a dark brown dry gel. Finally, the dry gel was calcined in air at 600 °C for 1 h to obtain Mg-doped CuO. For comparison, the sample without Mg (no Mg(NO3)2•6H2O addition), lower content Mg (10 mg Mg(NO3)2•6H2O addition), and higher content Mg (30 mg Mg(NO3)2•6H2O addition) were also prepared in the same way.

Electrochemical synthesis of MgO1-x/Cu

The Mg-doped CuO precursor was used after fine grinding. To prepare the catalyst ink, 10 mg of the precursor was dispersed in a mixed solvent containing 600 μL deionized water, 400 μL isopropyl alcohol, and 20 μL of 5 wt% Nafion solution. Homogeneous ink was obtained by sonicating the mixture for 2 h in an ice-water bath. An SGL 28BC hydrophobic carbon paper was employed as the gas diffusion substrate. These precursors were uniformly spray-coating onto the carbon paper at a loading of 1.0 mg cm−2. A flow cell configuration was employed in the electrochemical reduction process, with CO2 continuously fed to the cathode at 20 mL min−1. Both anode and cathode compartments contained 1 M KOH electrolyte. Reconstruction was achieved by applying a constant current density of 10 mA cm−2 for 120 min. Similarly, MgO1-x/Cu-H, MgO1-x/Cu-L, Cu were prepared from Mg-doped CuO with higher Mg content, Mg-doped CuO with lower Mg content, and pure CuO, respectively. All prepared electrodes were stored in a vacuum box.

Synthesis of MgO/Cu

The ground Mg-doped CuO precursor was treated under a 5 vol% H2/Ar atmosphere at 300 °C for 1 h, and subsequently stored in a vacuum box.

Electrochemical tests

All electrochemical tests were performed at room temperature (~25 °C). The electrochemical performance was assessed immediately following electrochemical synthesis using the same flow cell with a three-electrode configuration. The gas and liquid chambers of the flow cell each measured 0.5 × 2 × 0.5 cm3, and the catalyst was spray-coated onto carbon paper with an electrode area of 0.5 × 2 cm2. The reference electrode was Hg/HgO (1 M KOH), while the counter electrode consisted of IrO2-coated titanium felt. The Hg/HgO (1 M KOH) electrode was calibrated by comparing its potential with that of a Pt electrode in H2-saturated solution (a quasi-SHE electrode). The potential of the Hg/HgO (1 M KOH) electrode was determined to be 0.098 V vs. SHE.

All electrochemical tests were conducted on a CHI 760D electrochemical workstation using chronopotentiometry mode for data recording. All potentials were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale via the Nernst equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E({vs}.{{\rm{RHE}}})=0.059\times {{\rm{pH}}}+E({vs}.{{\rm{Hg}}}/{{\rm{HgO}}})+0.098{{\rm{V}}}$$\end{document}E(vs.RHE)=0.059×pH+E(vs.Hg/HgO)+0.098V

Both the catholyte and anolyte consisted of 1 M KOH electrolyte, and were continuously circulated at 10 mL min−1 via a peristaltic pump. The electrolyte was prepared within one week and stored in plastic volumetric flasks. The cathode and anode chambers were separated by an X37-50 anion-exchange membrane (50 μm thick). The anion-exchange membrane should be soaked in an 1 M KOH alkaline solution for activation prior to use. High-purity CO2 gas (99.95%) was supplied to the cathode at a constant flow rate of 40 mL min−1, with gas flow and pressure controlled by a mass flow controller and a back-pressure regulator. During testing, the chronopotentiometry was performed in stepwise increments starting from 50 mA cm−2. The current density was increased in 50 mA cm−2 intervals up to 500 mA cm−2, with each step maintained for ≥20 min to ensure the accuracy of product analysis.

The iR compensation was not applied during the measurement to avoid potential artifacts associated with positive feedback or overcompensation. Instead, the uncompensated resistance (Ru) was determined by high-frequency impedance spectroscopy at a frequency of 1000–10,000 Hz prior to each measurement. All reported potentials were then corrected post-experiment using the following equation:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{{{\rm{report}}}}={E}_{{{\rm{tested}}}}-i\times R{{\rm{u}}}$$\end{document}Ereport=Etested−i×RuWhere Ereport is the reported potential value, Etested is the measured potential, i is the applied current, and Ru is the measured resistance.

Gas chromatography analysis was performed using a Fuli GC9790Plus system equipped with TCD and FID detectors, along with a Ni methanizer. Gaseous products (CO, CH4, C2H4) generated during the reaction were quantified by the FID, while hydrogen byproducts were detected by the TCD. During gas-phase testing, the gas flow rate was cross-checked using both a mass flow controller (MFC) and a soap bubble flow meter.

Liquid products (ethanol, acetic acid, formic acid) were analyzed via 1H NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Avance NEO 400 MHz) using water signal suppression mode.

The Faradaic efficiency (FE) for each product was calculated according to the following equation:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{FE}}}={{\rm{znF}}}/{{\rm{Q}}}\times 100\%$$\end{document}FE=znF/Q×100%where z is the number of electrons transferred per product molecule (e.g., z = 2 for CO, 8 for CH4), n is the molar quantity of the product (mol), F is the Faraday constant (96485 C mol−1), Q is the total charge passed during CO2 reduction (C).

The OH- adsorption experiments were conducted in a flow cell using a three-electrode configuration with 0.1 M KOH as the electrolyte. Prior to the measurements, the catalysts were reduced at a constant current density of −10 mA cm−2 for 120 min to generate metallic Cu species. The gas supply was then switched to N2, and linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) was performed at a scan rate of 10 mV s−1.

ECSA measurements were performed in a three-electrode flow cell using Ar-saturated 0.1 M KOH as the electrolyte. Prior to the measurements, the catalyst was electrochemically reduced under CO2 reduction conditions at a constant current density of 10 mA cm−2 for 120 min to establish a stable surface state, after which the electrolyte was purged with Ar for 20 min to remove dissolved CO2. Cyclic voltammetry was conducted in a non-Faradaic potential region (0.00–0.15 V vs. HgO/Hg) at scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s−1, with 20 cycles recorded at each scan rate to ensure reproducibility. The double-layer capacitance (Cdl) was determined from the linear slope of the charging current density (Δj, measured at 0.07 V vs. HgO/Hg) plotted against the scan rate, and the resulting Cdl values were used directly for activity normalization.

Materials characterization

X-ray diffraction patterns were recorded using a Rigaku Miniflex 600 fitted with a Cu Kα X-ray (λ = 0.154 nm). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra were collected with a ThermoFisher ESCALAB 250Xi, calibrated against the adventitious C1s peak at 284.8 eV. TEM images were captured with a JEOL 2100F operating at 200 kV. To avoid oxidation, post-electrolysis samples were kept in a vacuum box. It should be noted that no ionomer was used for the specified TEM test to avoid ionomer coating on these electrochemically processed catalysts.

The in situ X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were carried out in transmission mode at the Cu K-edge using a RapidXAFS system (Anhui Absorption Spectroscopy Analysis Instrument), with the X-ray source operating at 20 kV and 40 mA. A custom-designed PEEK electrochemical flow cell was used during measurements, and 0.1 M KOH electrolyte was circulated continuously using a peristaltic pump. Prior to testing, the electrolyte was pre-saturated with CO2 by bubbling for 30 min. To ensure the catalyst remains stable at the applied potential, pre-potentiostatic electrolysis was performed for 20 min prior to XAS measurements. Besides, the potential was held constant throughout the entire spectral acquisition process. The acquired data were analyzed with the IFEFFIT software package.

The in situ ATR-SEIRAS measurements employed a custom electrochemical cell. A hemispherical silicon prism was first coated with a ~60 nm Au film, followed by drop-deposition of catalysts onto the Au-modified surface. Spectra acquisition utilized a Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer (ThermoFisher) equipped with an liquefied N2-cooled MCT detector. Prior to experiments, the 0.1 M KOH electrolyte was CO2-saturated via 30-min bubbling. Spectra were recorded under potentiostatic conditions, with pre-potentiostatic electrolysis conducted for 20 min at each potential to ensure stabilization before initiating spectrum acquisition.

The in situ Raman was performed in a three-electrode in situ flow cell on a Xplora confocal microprobe Raman system (HORIBA) equipped with OLYMPUS BX41 microscope, a diode laser of 633 nm, a 50x fiber optic probe and a CCD detector. The incident laser power was set to 30 mW. A grating of 600 lines adopted. The integration time for each spectrum was 60 s with 2 accumulations coadded to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. The electrolyte used during testing was 1 M KOH. Slowly introduce CO2 (5 ml min−1) from the back of the working electrode.

The CO temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD) was performed on an AMI-300 chemisorption analyzer (JWGB, China). Typically, the electrode was exposed to 10% CO/Ar (30 mL/min) for 1 h, then purged with Ar for 1 h to remove physisorbed CO. Then, TPD was carried out from 25 to >500 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min in Ar flow (30 mL/min). The desorbed CO was monitored by thermal conductivity detector (TCD).

MEA measurements

For MEA electrolyzer testing, IrO2/Ti anodes were employed alongside cathode catalysts prepared identically to the flow cell procedure. The catalyst ink was spray-coated onto SGL 28BC carbon paper at 2.0 mg cm−2 loading, with Sustainion X37-50 Grade T anion-exchange membranes (AEM) separating the electrodes. Flow fields were designed with single-serpentine channels for 1 cm2 cells, while 25 cm2 single cells and 100 cm2 stacks utilized triple-parallel serpentine configurations to mitigate CO2 concentration gradients. CO2 was humidified via a deionized water saturator prior to entering the electrolyzer, with flow rates maintained at 20 mL min−1 (1 cm2), 100 mL min−1 (25 cm2), and 400 mL min−1 (100 cm2) to ensure stoichiometric supply. During stability testing, the back-pressure regulator was adjusted to maintain CO2 at 0.1 MPa, mitigating flooding risks. MEA performance was evaluated under the chronopotentiometry operation, with reaction products quantified by gas chromatography. For the MEA electrolyzer measurements, no iR compensation was applied.

PTFE powder (50 nm particle size, 200 mg) and carbon black (XC-72R, 100 mg) were thoroughly mixed via ball milling in ethanol. The resulting mixture was dried and then calcined at 350 °C for 2 h under a N2 atmosphere to allow the PTFE to melt and uniformly coat the carbon particles. Conventional ink formulations using Nafion or XA-9 ionomers as binders are prone to flooding during long-term operation. To overcome this, we selected a perfluorinated superhydrophobic polymer, AF1601 (Chemours) as the binder. It is important to note that AF1601 cannot be dissolved in water or alcohol. Therefore, it must be dissolved in a fluorinated solvent (FC-40). The AF1601 binder was added to the PTFE/C composite powder at a mass ratio of AF1601:PTFE/C = 0.1:1. The PTFE/C/AF1601 ink was spray-coated onto the cathode. The final loading of the PTFE/C overlayer was 1 mg cm−2, corresponding to a thickness of approximately 20 μm.

During the MEA stability test, a periodic cleaning protocol was implemented in which the cathode flow field was flushed with deionized water approximately every 10 h of operation. This flushing effectively dissolves and removes carbonate salts, restoring the pristine gas diffusion pathways. In addition, the electrolyte was also replaced approximately every 10 h to prevent excessive accumulation of ethanol, which could otherwise compromise the integrity of the AEM membrane.

DFT calculations

All density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP). The electron-ion interactions were described by the projector augmented wave (PAW) method. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional was employed to treat the exchange-correlation effects. A plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was adopted. The convergence criteria for electronic self-consistent iteration and ionic relaxation were set to 10−5 eV and 0.03 eV Å−1, respectively. Grimme’s DFT-D3 method with Becke-Jonson damping was incorporated to account for the van der Waals interactions, which are crucial for accurately describing the adsorption of molecules like CO2.

The MO1-x/Cu (M = Mg, Ga, Ga, Ba, Al, Zn, and In) model was constructed by placing a MO1-x cluster (with an oxygen vacancy) on a 3 × 3 × 1 Cu slab. For MO and MO2-type oxides, an oxygen vacancy concentration of approximately 16% was used for all systems. Representative models include Mg6O5 for MgO1-x, Ce6O10 for CeO1-x, Ga6O5 for GaO1-x, and Zn6O5 for ZnO1-x. For M2O3-type Oxides, the oxygen vacancy concentration was maintained at approximately 11%. Representative models include Al6O8 for AlO1-x, In6O8 for InO1-x.

A vacuum layer of at least 15 Å in the z-direction was applied to eliminate spurious interactions between periodic images. For the DFT calculations in the descriptor screening process, an implicit solvent model was adopted. By contrast, the explicit solvent model was employed in the free energy calculations to achieve higher computational accuracy. To construct the explicit model, we placed 25 water molecules on the catalyst surface to form a continuous hydrogen-bonding network, which realistically mimics the actual aqueous environment. The atomic coordination of model is provided as a Supplementary Data 1 file. The adsorption energy (Eads) of molecules (e.g., CO2) on the MO1-x/Cu surface was defined as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${E}_{{{\rm{ads}}}}={E}_{{{\rm{total}}}}-{E}_{{{\rm{surface}}}}-{E}_{{{\rm{molecule}}}}$$\end{document}Eads=Etotal−Esurface−Emoleculewhere Etotal, Esurface, and Emolecule are the total energies of the adsorption system, the clean MO1-x/Cu surface, and the isolated molecule in the gas phase, respectively. The dissolution process of MgO1-x atoms from the Cu bulk to the surface was investigated using the nudged elastic band (NEB) method. The initial and final states were first fully optimized. Eleven intermediate images were interpolated between these states and optimized to locate the minimum energy path and the transition state. To simulate the electrochemical environment, the implicit solvation model implemented in VASP (VASPsol) was employed. A dielectric constant of ε = 78.4 was used to represent an aqueous electrolyte. Furthermore, a constant electric field, equivalent to a potential of 0.05 V Å−1, was applied perpendicular to the surface to mimic the electrode potential relevant to the experimental conditions. The electronic properties of key intermediate states were analyzed by calculating the density of states (DOS) and the crystal orbital Hamilton population (COHP). The DOS analysis provided insights into the electronic states near the Fermi level (−10 to 10 eV). The -pCOHP analysis, performed using the LOBSTER package, was utilized to quantify the bonding interactions (negative -pCOHP) and anti-bonding interactions (positive -pCOHP) between specific atoms, offering a deeper understanding of the adsorption and bonding nature of the intermediates.
