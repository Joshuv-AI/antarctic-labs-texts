Radiological Outcomes of Uncemented Total Hip Replacement: A Prospective Study on Implant Positioning and Alignment
Authors: Narendra Singh Kushwaha, Raza Ahmad Khan, Dharmendra Kumar, Sujeet Kumar Chaudhary, Khurshid Alam Ansari, Ritesh Chakoo, Ashish Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is the gold standard for advanced hip diseases. Cementless fixation has gained popularity due to its biological fixation and durability, but precise implant positioning remains crucial for optimal outcomes. Data on immediate postoperative radiological results in Indian patients are limited.Aims and Objectives:To assess immediate postoperative radiological parameters in patients undergoing unilateral uncemented THA at a tertiary center in North India.Materials and Methods:A prospective study was conducted on patients undergoing uncemented THA. Standardized postoperative radiographs were analyzed for acetabular cup inclination, anteversion, femoral stem alignment, and limb length discrepancy.Results:Most patients showed acetabular and femoral components within acceptable radiological ranges. Minor deviations were observed in a few cases due to intraoperative challenges. Limb length discrepancy was minimal and clinically insignificant.Conclusion:Uncemented THA demonstrated satisfactory immediate radiological outcomes, reflecting good surgical precision. Regular assessment of implant positioning is vital for improving long-term success and reducing complications.

INTRODUCTION

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is one of the most frequently performed orthopedic procedures globally and remains the gold standard for the management of advanced hip joint pathologies, including primary osteoarthritis, avascular necrosis, and femoral neck fractures. The demand for THA has been steadily rising, with data from the United States indicating that approximately 0.83% of the population undergoes THA annually, translating into hundreds of thousands of procedures each year.[1] The success of THA lies not only in its ability to alleviate pain and restore function but also in the evolution of implant materials, surgical approaches, and fixation techniques over the decades.

The history of THA traces back to the 19th century when Themistocles Glück first introduced the concept of hip joint replacement using ivory implants in tuberculous joints. Subsequent efforts included the use of interpositional materials such as fascia lata, pig bladder submucosa, and glass, which were eventually abandoned due to poor durability.[2] A major turning point came with Sir John Charnley in the 1960s, who introduced the low-friction arthroplasty design using a stainless steel femoral stem, polyethylene acetabular cup, and polymethylmethacrylate bone cement. This design laid the foundation for modern cemented THA.[3]

Cemented fixation has several benefits, including immediate implant stability, ease of use in osteoporotic bone, and predictable long-term outcomes. However, complications such as aseptic loosening, periprosthetic fractures, and bone cement implantation syndrome have spurred the search for alternative fixation methods.[45] Moreover, the use of cement has been associated with increased surgical time, risk of embolism, and difficulties in revision surgeries.

These challenges led to the development of cementless (uncemented) fixation systems, which have gained widespread acceptance, especially among younger and more active patients. Cementless implants typically feature porous-coated or hydroxyapatite-coated surfaces that encourage bone ingrowth, thereby achieving biological fixation. Since their introduction in the 1980s, uncemented THA has become the preferred choice in many parts of the world. In the United States, more than 93% of primary THA procedures now utilize uncemented fixation.[67] Similarly, Indian orthopedic centers are witnessing a rising preference for cementless implants due to reduced intraoperative time, lower infection risk, and favorable long-term survivorship.[8]

Despite the growing popularity of uncemented THA, achieving optimal outcomes depends heavily on accurate implant positioning. Malalignment of the acetabular or femoral components can lead to biomechanical compromise, increased wear, instability, impingement, limb length discrepancy (LLD), and early prosthesis failure.[1011] Consequently, radiological assessment of key parameters such as acetabular cup inclination (ACI), anteversion, femoral stem alignment, and LLD plays a pivotal role in evaluating surgical success.

Most literature on radiological outcomes of THA focuses on long-term follow-up; however, limited studies assess immediate postoperative radiological outcomes, particularly in the Indian population. Early assessment can provide valuable insights into intraoperative precision, allow timely identification of malpositioned implants, and potentially predict long-term complications.[12] This is particularly important in uncemented THA, where initial press-fit stability and precise alignment are critical for successful osseointegration.

Therefore, this study was undertaken to prospectively evaluate the achievement of critical radiological parameters in patients undergoing unilateral uncemented THA at a tertiary care center in North India. By examining these parameters immediately after surgery, this research aims to provide a benchmark for surgical accuracy, highlight potential pitfalls, and contribute to improving long-term outcomes in uncemented THA.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective cohort study was conducted over 1-year period at a tertiary care teaching hospital in North India. Institutional ethical clearance was obtained prior to the commencement of the study. Written informed consent was taken from all participants after providing detailed information about the study’s objectives, procedures, and potential risks.

Patients presenting to the orthopedic outpatient department or admitted with primary unilateral hip arthritis who were planned for uncemented THA were evaluated for inclusion. Eligible participants were adults aged 18 years or older of either gender who were undergoing unilateral uncemented THA for isolated hip pathology. Patients with bilateral hip involvement, neuromuscular disorders, spinal deformities, acetabular fractures, or underlying neurological conditions impairing ambulation were excluded from the study. Individuals who were planned for cemented or bilateral THA, or who were deemed unfit for surgery or unwilling to provide consent, were also excluded from the study.

Preoperatively, a detailed clinical assessment was performed, including demographic information (age, sex, height, weight, and body mass index [BMI]), clinical history, and radiographic evaluation. Standard anteroposterior and lateral radiographs of the pelvis and hips were obtained to document the radiological status of the affected and contralateral hip. Preoperative radiological parameters recorded included ACI, acetabular anteversion, horizontal and vertical centers of rotation (COR), femoral stem alignment, and LLD. These measurements served as baseline reference values.

All surgical procedures were performed by senior orthopedic consultants with experience in arthroplasty. A standardized operative protocol was followed, and the choice between the posterior and Hardinge (anterolateral) approach was made based on patient anatomy and surgeon preference. All patients received uncemented hip prostheses. Intraoperative fluoroscopy was used to assist in component positioning when necessary. Postoperatively, immediate radiographs of the pelvis and hips were obtained to assess the accuracy of implant placement and to measure the same radiological parameters as in the preoperative evaluation.

Radiological assessments were performed by independent observers who were blinded to the clinical details of the patients. The following definitions were used for measurements: ACI was determined as the angle between the transverse pelvic line and the line connecting the medial and lateral margins of the acetabular component; acetabular anteversion was calculated based on standard radiographic definitions; COR values were obtained using reference lines from the teardrop and lesser trochanter; femoral stem alignment was assessed in terms of varus, valgus, or neutral positioning relative to the femoral axis; and LLD was measured as the vertical distance between standardized pelvic and femoral landmarks.

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States) and analyzed using statistical software IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, United States). Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and percentages, were calculated. Paired t-tests were used to compare preoperative and immediate postoperative radiographic values, as well as comparisons between affected and contralateral hips. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

A total of 35 patients undergoing unilateral uncemented THA were included in this prospective cohort study. The age of the patients ranged from 18 to 74 years, with a mean age of 40.40 ± 13.68 years [Table 1]. The majority of patients (37.1%) were in the 31–40 year age group, followed by 22.9% in the 41–50 year range. Males comprised 60% of the study population, resulting in a male-to-female ratio of 1.5:1. Regarding BMI, 54.3% of patients had normal BMI (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), 31.4% were overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and 8.6% were obese (>30 kg/m2). Only two patients (5.7%) were underweight (BMI < 18.5 kg/m2) [Table 2].

Preoperative radiological profile

Comparison between the affected and contralateral hip revealed that preoperatively, the mean ACI on the affected side was 37.46° ± 6.52°, compared to 39.41° ± 3.42° on the contralateral side (P = 0.079). The mean horizontal COR was 0.83 ± 0.40 cm on the affected side versus 0.71 ± 0.36 cm on the contralateral side, showing a statistically significant difference (P = 0.047) [Figure 1]. The vertical COR was also significantly greater on the affected side (1.69 ± 0.80 cm) compared to the contralateral side (1.42 ± 0.60 cm; P = 0.003). Mean acetabular anteversion on the affected side was 16.71° ± 5.91°, significantly lower than the contralateral side (20.20° ± 5.21°; P = 0.001). The average LLD preoperatively was 0.89 ± 0.67 cm, with values ranging from 0 to 2 cm [Table 4].

Postoperative radiological evaluation

In the immediate postoperative period, the mean ACI improved to 41.41° ± 4.69° on the affected side, showing an increase of approximately 4° compared to preoperative values (P = 0.003). The horizontal COR decreased slightly to 0.77 ± 0.37 cm (P = 0.105), whereas the vertical COR reduced to 1.48 ± 0.67 cm, a statistically significant change from baseline (P = 0.008) [Figure 3]. Acetabular anteversion increased postoperatively to 19.00° ± 5.51°, a near-significant change from baseline (P = 0.051). Mean LLD decreased markedly to 0.16 ± 0.28 cm, with the improvement being highly significant (P < 0.001) [Table 6].

Radiological alignment and component positioning

Postoperative analysis revealed that 97.1% of patients had ACI within the desired range of 30°–50°, with only one patient (2.9%) showing a value <30°. All patients had acetabular anteversion ≤25°, although 22.9% had values <15°, and none exceeded 25°. Femoral stem positioning was classified as neutral in 25 patients (71.4%), whereas 7 patients (20%) had 1° of varus alignment and 3 patients (8.6%) had 2° of varus. There were no cases of valgus alignment.

The Trendelenburg sign was present in 6 patients (17.1%) at immediate postoperative evaluation. Only 3 patients (8.6%) had postoperative LLD >0.5 cm, compared to 27 patients preoperatively [Figure 4].

DISCUSSION

THA has undergone significant advancements over the past few decades, with uncemented prostheses increasingly preferred for their potential to promote biological fixation, reduce operative time, and improve long-term outcomes in suitable patients. However, the efficacy of these implants heavily depends on precise surgical technique and accurate component positioning, particularly during the immediate perioperative phase. In this prospective cohort study, we aimed to assess the immediate postoperative radiological outcomes of uncemented THA, focusing on key parameters such as acetabular inclination, anteversion, femoral stem positioning, and LLD.

Our study demonstrated that the mean postoperative ACI was 41.41° ± 4.69°, which was significantly improved from the preoperative value of 37.46° ± 6.52° (P = 0.003). Furthermore, 97.1% of the cases achieved optimal ACI within the accepted range of 30°–50°, a finding consistent with previous studies. Di Martino et al.[13] reported an average cup inclination of 40.5° (range: 18.8°–52.9°) in a series of cementless THAs, whereas Wade and Shah[14] found that 95% of their patients had acetabular inclination between 40° and 50° at 5-year follow-up. Similarly, Mathur et al.[15] reported optimal ACI in 96% of their uncemented THA cases, underscoring the reproducibility of correct acetabular positioning when meticulous surgical technique is employed.

Acetabular anteversion is another critical parameter influencing joint stability and dislocation risk. In our study, the mean postoperative anteversion improved to 19.00° ± 5.51°, with all cases remaining within the acceptable range of 5°–25°. This is consistent with the findings of Dorr and Wan[16] who reported 97.6% of their cases with anteversion within this range. Juneja et al.[17] also noted proper acetabular anteversion (mean ~ 15°) in 86% of young adults undergoing uncemented THA. These findings collectively support the feasibility of achieving targeted anteversion in uncemented THA with careful intraoperative alignment.

Femoral stem alignment plays a vital role in ensuring load distribution, reducing stress shielding, and preventing periprosthetic fractures. In our study, 71.4% of patients had neutral stem positioning, and the remaining had minimal varus alignment (1°–2°), which is within clinically tolerable limits. Our findings are similar to those of Rao et al.,[18] who found 80% neutral stem positioning, and Di Martino et al.,[13] who observed 86.2% neutral alignment and only minor varus deviation in the rest. However, previous retrospective data from our own institution had shown a higher prevalence of valgus stem alignment,[19] possibly due to variations in surgical technique or differences in patient bone morphology.

Another crucial determinant of patient satisfaction and gait restoration is the correction of LLD. Our study showed a significant reduction in mean LLD from 0.89 cm preoperatively to 0.16 cm postoperatively (P < 0.001), with only 8.6% of patients having LLD > 0.5 cm postoperatively. These outcomes align well with findings from Bindumadhavan et al.,[20] who reported a mean LLD of 0.23 cm postoperatively in bilateral uncemented THA, and with Di Martino et al.,[13] although the latter had a higher rate of postoperative LLD >10 mm in one-third of cases, possibly due to older patient age and preexisting bone deformities.

The Trendelenburg sign was noted in 17.1% of our patients at the time of immediate postoperative evaluation. This is likely attributable to gluteal muscle dysfunction rather than prosthetic malalignment, given that femoral offset and limb length were corrected adequately in most patients. Previous reports suggest that Trendelenburg positivity in the early postoperative period may resolve with rehabilitation and is not necessarily predictive of long-term outcomes.[21]

A noteworthy strength of this study is the immediate postoperative assessment of radiological outcomes, which is less frequently reported in existing literature. Most earlier studies, including those by De Santis et al.,[22] Christodoulou et al.,[23] and Rashid et al.,[24] have reported findings at mid-to-long-term follow-up or collected data retrospectively. Our prospective design allowed for standardized radiographic assessment and immediate detection of deviations from targeted alignment, enabling early risk stratification for future complications.

Despite its strengths, our study has certain limitations. The sample size was relatively small, limiting generalizability. In addition, the absence of a comparative group (e.g. cemented THA or hybrid fixation) restricts direct comparison of outcomes. Furthermore, we did not assess functional or patient-reported outcomes, which are essential to correlate radiological accuracy with real-world clinical improvement.

Future studies should explore longitudinal correlations between immediate radiological alignment and long-term implant survival, patient satisfaction, and functional recovery. Inclusion of diverse patient subgroups – particularly elderly individuals with osteoporotic bone or complex deformities – may also enrich understanding of factors influencing outcomes in uncemented THA.

CONCLUSION

This prospective cohort study demonstrates that uncemented THA is an effective and precise surgical modality capable of achieving desirable radiological parameters immediately postoperatively. The majority of patients achieved optimal ACI and anteversion, with satisfactory femoral stem alignment and minimal LLD. The early postoperative radiographic outcomes were comparable to those reported in national and international literature, reaffirming the feasibility and accuracy of uncemented THA in a diverse North Indian population.

Importantly, this study underscores the value of immediate postoperative radiological assessment in predicting surgical precision and guiding early clinical decision-making. Given the biological fixation mechanism of cementless implants, early implant positioning plays a critical role in long-term prosthesis survival and patient function. Our findings also support the safe utilization of uncemented implants in younger patients, including those without significant comorbidities, and highlight the importance of individualized surgical planning to optimize outcomes.

Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up durations, and inclusion of functional and patient-reported outcome measures are recommended to further evaluate the correlation between immediate radiological alignment and long-term clinical success in uncemented THA.
