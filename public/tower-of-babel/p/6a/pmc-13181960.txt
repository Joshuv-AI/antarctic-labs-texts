Targeting immunosenescence in lung diseases: mechanistic insights and clinical interventions
Authors: Yin Zhang, Xianzhe Yu, Ping Li, Yaobin Ouyang, Lingling Zhu, Fengming Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunosenescence, the age-related decline in immune function, plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis and progression of lung diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, lung cancer, pulmonary fibrosis, asthma, and respiratory tract infections. This comprehensive review examines the hallmarks of immunosenescence, and illustrates the association between immunosenescence and the pathogenesis of lung diseases. In addition, we discuss current and emerging therapeutic strategies that have been evaluated in human clinical trials for targeting immunosenescence in lung diseases. Specifically, this review provides in-depth insights into the therapeutic strategies, including senolytics and senomorphics, immunotherapy, stem cell therapy, thymic rejuvenation, probiotics, and lifestyle. We also highlight the potential of personalized approaches integrating multi-omics data and artificial intelligence to guide biomarker-driven interventions, enabling truly personalized therapeutic strategies. Finally, this review underscores the imperative for rigorously designed clinical trials to develop and validate interventions that specifically target immunosenescence, with the ultimate goal of improving clinical outcomes for the aged population with lung diseases.Keywords: Immunosenescence, Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, Lung cancer, Pulmonary fibrosis, COVID-19, Senolytics

Background

The global increase in life expectancy has led to a rise in the prevalence and severity of age-related lung diseases, which in turn significantly impairs the quality of life of patients and places a growing burden on healthcare systems. Aging, as a potent disease susceptibility multiplier, enables pathology when its effects converge with necessary genetic and environmental triggers [1, 2]. Immunosenescence describes the deterioration of the immune systems with aging, leading to compromised immune function [3]. It not only involves cellular senescence of immune cells but also encompasses age-related functional alterations. It ultimately leads to reduced capabilities in fighting infections and tumors, as well as an increased susceptibility to chronic inflammation [4]. It is noteworthy that immunosenescence is driven not only by intrinsic aging but also significantly influenced by extrinsic factors, such as pathogen exposure and oxidative stress [4].

Immunosenescence affects both innate and adaptive immunity, resulting in impaired immune function against novel pathogens and tumors, dysregulated inflammatory responses, and a disrupted immune homeostasis characterized by increased susceptibility to autoimmunity [5–9]. Inflammaging has emerged as a hallmark of aging, referring to a systemic inflammatory tendency that affects the immune responsiveness and vaccine response capacity of the body [10]. The gradual accumulation of senescent cells, of both immune and non-immune origin, in aging tissues drives pro-inflammatory conditions through the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) secretome, which includes a range of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [11]. In addition, cumulative exposure to immunogenic substances with aging and the accumulation of endogenous cellular debris, caused by age-related defective heterophagy, together with SASP, activates the NLR family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome and synthesizes pro-inflammatory mediators, promoting the formation of a microenvironment that exacerbates immunosenescence [12].

Extensive research has demonstrated that immunosenescence is closely associated with age-related lung diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), lung cancer, pulmonary fibrosis (PF), asthma, and respiratory tract infections (RTIs) [7, 13–16]. However, despite the significant contribution of immunosenescence to the initiation, progression, and therapeutic outcomes of lung diseases in aged populations, its underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Immunosenescence further complicates clinical management through multifaceted mechanisms. Therefore, an in-depth understanding of immunosenescence in pulmonary pathophysiology is essential to elucidate the intricate intrinsic mechanisms underlying the heightened susceptibility of aged individuals to lung diseases, and further explore the potential treatment strategies targeting immunosenescence.

This comprehensive review aims to outline the mechanisms of immunosenescence in lung diseases and explores related therapeutic strategies.

Hallmarks of aging in systemic and pulmonary immunity

Age-related changes of systemic and pulmonary immunity

Immunosenescence affects various immune cells. Pulmonary immune homeostasis is a complex network maintained by locally resident and recruited immune cells of myeloid and lymphoid origin from the circulation [17]. Here, we explore the effects of aging on both innate and adaptive immune cells, focusing on how immunosenescence disrupts systemic and pulmonary immune homeostasis.

Age-related changes in innate immunity

As the lungs’ first line of defense, the innate immune system is paramount in protecting against pathogens, pollutants, and allergens. Its key cellular effectors include monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells (DCs), and natural killer (NK) cells. Senescence of innate immune cells leads to enhanced release of proinflammatory cytokines, which exacerbate inflammaging. Simultaneously, it demonstrates functional deficiencies in multiple defense mechanisms, including impaired pathogen recognition through pattern recognition receptors such as Toll-like receptors (TLR), reduced immune cell chemotaxis and migration, diminished antigen presentation, decreased phagocytic pathogen clearance capacity, and lower production of microbicidal components, including reactive oxygen species (ROS) [17, 18].

Monocytes/macrophages

With aging, the number of circulating monocytes increases, whereas their function is diminished [19]. Monocytes exhibit impaired recruitment capacity to inflammatory sites [19, 20]. Additionally, aged individuals display a higher proportion of pro-inflammatory non-classical monocytes (CD14+CD16+), elevated expression of activated markers, reduced expression of co-inhibitory molecules, and an enhanced capacity to produce tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [19, 21]. Elevated circulating TNF-α levels caused by inflammaging have been shown to induce the premature egress of monocytes from the bone marrow. When stimulated by bacterial products, these immature monocytes secrete high levels of TNF-α, thereby exacerbating the inflammaging process [22]. Previous research has documented dysregulated expression of key homing molecules (CCR2, CX3CR1, CD11b, and CD62L) in aged individuals, a dysfunction linked to impaired monocyte tissue recruitment. Furthermore, monocytes from aged individuals exhibit diminished phagocytic capacity in response to TLR4 stimulation [21], accompanied by significant impairments in both primary and secondary RIG-I signaling pathways that regulate type I interferon (IFN-I) gene expression. These molecular alterations result in a compromised capacity to suppress viral dissemination and impaired clearance of infected cells [23, 24].

In the lung, two main types of macrophages, including alveolar and interstitial macrophages, are identified. Alveolar macrophages are tissue-resident macrophages specific to the alveolar compartment and contribute to barrier immunity and surfactant clearance. Interstitial macrophages are located in the space between the lung epithelium and capillaries, interacting with other immune cells such as lymphocytes and DCs. They participate in lung barrier immunity together with alveolar macrophages [25]. Aging induces significant alterations in macrophages, characterized by heightened production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1, TNF-α, and IL-6) and excessive release of ROS [26, 27]. Additionally, macrophages exhibit defective phagocytic function with aging, characterized by diminished efferocytosis and impaired clearance of senescent and apoptotic cells [28]. Research has further indicated that macrophages in aged individuals demonstrate insufficient phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B activation following infection, resulting in impaired bacterial clearance [29]. Furthermore, macrophages demonstrate an age-related downregulation of major histocompatibility complex class II molecules, leading to an impaired antigen-presenting capacity, which affects the activation of the adaptive immune response [30, 31].

Neutrophils

With aging, circulating neutrophil levels remain stable despite a shift in aged hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) toward the myeloid cell lineage, while the percentages of sputum neutrophils increase [32]. Nevertheless, the function of neutrophils in aged individuals is compromised [33]. The phenotype of neutrophils shifts, characterized by an expansion of immunosuppressive CD16bright/CD62Ldim neutrophils, which is associated with impaired superoxide generation and phagocytic activity [34, 35]. Aged individuals exhibit impaired neutrophil chemotaxis and aberrant migration patterns mediated by dysregulated PI3K signaling, resulting in bystander tissue damage and misdirected inflammatory responses [17]. Aging further promotes neutrophil reverse transendothelial migration (rTEM) via mast cell-derived C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) and endothelial atypical chemokine receptor 1 (ACKR1), leading to systemic dissemination of neutrophils that exacerbate remote lung injury and contribute to age-related inflammatory dysregulation [36]. The formation of neutrophil extracellular traps is a crucial host defense mechanism against pathogens and neutralization of virulence factors, which is significantly impaired in both aged mouse models and older adults [37, 38]. Defective resolution of neutrophil-mediated immune responses in aged individuals may further contribute to sustained systemic inflammation and exacerbation of tissue damage [33].

DCs

DCs serve as pivotal intermediaries bridging innate and adaptive immunity. Their primary function is to capture antigens and present them to T cells. Following pathogen internalization through pattern recognition receptors, DCs present viral peptides to T cells through human leukocyte antigen-T cell receptor (TCR) interactions, leading to T cell activation. Aging impairs the phagocytic capacity of DCs, migratory ability, and antigen presentation, thereby affecting adaptive immune priming. Abnormal cytokine secretion is evident across various DC subsets. In particular, conventional DCs display heightened baseline production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, yet exhibit a diminished capacity to respond to external antigenic stimuli [39].

NK cells

The NK cell pool exhibits an age-associated change in its constituent subsets, manifesting as a progressive shift from immature, cytokine-secreting CD56bright cells to mature CD56dim NK cells [40, 41], accompanied by reduced cytotoxic activity and cytokine production [41]. Furthermore, NK cells exhibit a decline in migratory capacity and a remodeling of their activating and inhibitory receptor repertoire with age, collectively contributing to diminished functional responsiveness. These defects have been shown to increase susceptibility to infections in aged individuals [41, 42].

Age-related changes in adaptive immunity

The adaptive immune system undergoes a progressive functional decline with aging, characterized by a shift toward myeloid-biased hematopoiesis and a reduction in the generation and diversity of naïve lymphocytes. Concurrently, lymphopoiesis becomes suppressed while regulatory T cell (Treg) populations expand. B cells exhibit both impaired adaptability and diminished antibody affinity maturation [42]. Together, these alterations lead to attenuated responses to pathogens and vaccines as well as defective tumor immunosurveillance in aged individuals.

T cells

Age-related decline in T cell function is primarily driven by thymic involution-induced reduction in naïve T cell production. Moreover, phenotype shifts occur from naïve to memory T cells, accompanied by a type 1 T-helper (Th1)/Th2 imbalance, with a predominance of Th2 cells and cytokines [43, 44]. These shifts lead to a T cell repertoire that is biased toward previously encountered antigens [45, 46], thereby compromising immunosurveillance against novel pathogens and malignant cells [47].

The distribution and activation potential of Th17 and Treg populations change with aging. Compared with young individuals, aged individuals exhibit increased proportions of Th17 cells and decreased Treg frequencies at baseline. However, this trend reverses upon stimulation. This dysregulation likely contributes to elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, heightened susceptibility to autoimmune diseases, and diminished responses to infections in aged individuals [48, 49].

Senescent T cells typically lack expression of costimulatory molecules, such as CD27 and CD28, while expressing senescence-associated markers, including killer cell lectin-like receptor subfamily G and CD57 [50]. The reduced expression of costimulatory molecules on T cells is associated with diminished immune responses. Furthermore, the depletion of CD27 and CD28 is accompanied by an upregulation of p16 and p21, both of which play a role in governing cell cycle progression [51]. With aging, the levels of molecules associated with immune checkpoints, such as Tim-3, TIGIT, and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen-4, increase, resulting in T cell functional suppression and immune response dysregulation. Furthermore, senescent T cells overexpress proinflammatory cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ and TNF-α) and cytotoxic mediators (e.g., granzyme B and perforin), which not only cause tissue damage but also crosstalk with other immune cells, e.g., resulting in defective B cell helper function [50, 52, 53]. Concurrently, age-associated dysregulation of the miR-181a/DUSP6/ERK axis emerges as a key mechanism underlying CD4+ T cell dysfunction in aging, manifesting as attenuated TCR signaling evidenced by reduced activation marker expression (e.g., CD69), impaired proliferation, and compromised Th1 differentiation [54]. Metabolically, CD4+ T cells in aged individuals demonstrate elevated CD39 expression (ATPase) that promotes apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction [55].

Pulmonary tissue-resident lymphocytes exhibit senescence-associated functional impairments [17]. In the aged lung, the IFN-γ signaling pathway is significantly activated, accompanied by T cell activation. Aging is associated with an accumulation of CD4+ effector memory T cells (TEM) in the lung and a concomitant enhancement of IFN-γ signaling responsiveness. These findings suggest a close association between senescence-associated pulmonary disorders and T cell activation [56].

B cells

B cell aging encompasses a broad range of age-related defects that collectively impair humoral immunity. Aging disrupts B cell development in the bone marrow by reducing the number of common lymphoid progenitors, diminishing IL-7 signaling, and downregulating key transcription factors like E2A and PAX5, thereby decreasing the output of mature B cells [22]. Concomitantly, in the periphery, a marked shift in B cell subset distribution is observed, characterized by an expansion of pro-inflammatory age-associated B cells and double-negative B cells in humans, which are characterized by the secretion of autoantibodies and inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, and a reduction in follicular and switched memory B cell populations [57–59]. Metabolically, these pro-inflammatory double-negative B cells exhibit heightened glucose utilization and oxidative phosphorylation, which supports their survival and autoantibody secretion [60]. Functionally, aged B cells show a profound decline in their ability to undergo class-switch recombination (CSR) and somatic hypermutation because of decreased expression of E47 and activation-induced cytidine deaminase (AID), which are critical for generating high-affinity antibodies [22, 61]. The underlying mechanisms include reduced mRNA stability of E47 mediated by impaired p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)-TTP signaling and a chronic inflammatory environment marked by elevated TNF-α, which further suppresses AID expression and B cell function [22]. Concurrently, expanded populations of inhibitory T follicular regulatory cells with elevated programmed death (PD)−1 expression in aged mice hinder germinal center maturation and function, ultimately leading to defective antibody production [62]. Consequently, these alterations collectively contribute to diminished vaccine efficacy and heightened susceptibility to infections in the aged population.

Inflammaging microenvironment in the lung

Aged individuals exhibit elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in circulation and tissues. Inflammaging is driven by multiple stimuli that induce dysregulated activation of the innate immune system with aging. Pro-inflammatory signals originate from exogenous pathogens, endogenous cellular debris, and damage-associated molecular patterns released by dying cells. In tissues, senescent cells progressively accumulate and exhibit a distinct secretory phenotype referred to as the SASP. The SASP response is activated by p21Cip1, which induces activation of p38 MAPK and Janus-activated kinases (JAK), promoting downstream inflammatory signaling. Consequently, activation of NF-κB leads to the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α), growth factors (e.g., VEGF and TGF-β), chemokines (e.g., CXCL1, CXCL8, and CCL2), and MMPs (e.g., MMP-2 and MMP-9). Collectively, these SASP factors promote a chronic inflammatory environment [63, 64], thereby disrupting the pulmonary microenvironment and contributing to tissue damage in the lung (Fig. 1) [17, 65].

Immunosenescence and lung diseases

In this section, we explore the interplay between immunosenescence and specific lung diseases. It is important to recognize that the age-related immune alterations observed in these conditions are not always clearly attributable to aging or senescence alone. Instead, they may arise from intrinsic immunosenescence, from the disease process itself, or from the combined effects of both. Given that immunosenescence can be triggered not only by aging but also by external stimuli including factors associated with lung pathology [4], our discussion focuses on how immunosenescent features, irrespective of their precise trigger, contribute to and interact with lung disease pathogenesis.

The confluence of aging immunity and the lung microenvironment in COPD

Cellular senescence is marked by shortened telomeres and reduced levels of anti-aging molecules like SIRT1, SIRT6, and Klotho [66]. In COPD, immune cells such as lymphocytes and macrophages show these same aging features, suggesting that immunosenescence may play a role in the disease [67–69]. Age-related changes in immune cells in COPD

Macrophages

Alveolar macrophages in COPD patients exhibit decreased levels of SIRT1 and Klotho, along with elevated p21CIP1 levels, indicating the presence of macrophage senescence [69–71]. In vitro study demonstrates that treatment of macrophages with cigarette smoke extract (CSE) shows decreased levels of SIRT1 and Klotho, which are associated with the activation of the NF-kB pathway [70, 72]. Knockdown or inhibition of SIRT1 and Klotho results in increased release of CSE-induced proinflammatory mediators from macrophages (such as MMP-9, IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α) [70, 72]. These findings may suggest that senescent macrophages promote airway inflammation and tissue damage in COPD in some degree.

Age-related functional impairment in macrophages reduces the resistance to oxidative stress injury in COPD patients. Alveolar macrophages play a protective role against CSE-induced oxidative stress injury through Nrf2 activation [73]. However, Nrf2 expression is decreased in the macrophages of older smokers and COPD patients, leading to a decline in antioxidant capacity. In addition, alveolar macrophages from older mice showed a decreased expression of Nrf2 and Nrf2 target genes in response to CSE exposure than those from young mice. These suggest that aging suppressed the CSE-induced upregulation of Nrf2 target genes, as well as Nrf2, in alveolar macrophages [74].

T cells

Circulating T cells in COPD patients exhibit features of immunosenescence, including significantly shortened telomere length and decreased telomerase activity [68]. In aged COPD patients, the abundance of naïve T cells is decreased. And increased cell fractions expressing non-proliferative senescent markers (KLRG1+/CD57+/PD1+) among them are observed. These indicate a constricted repertoire of naïve T cells that are capable of mediating neoimmune responses in COPD. Additionally, naïve CD8+ T cells in aged COPD display a shift toward phenotypes of advanced stages of differentiation. They predominantly expand into terminally differentiated effector memory RA+ (TEMRA) cells rather than TEM subsets. The former one has a diminished proliferative potential. COPD group showed alterations similar to a group of healthy aged individuals at least ten years older, indicating that COPD patients undergo premature immunosenescence. Collectively, these findings help to explain some of the immunological deficiencies observed in aged COPD patients [75].

The percentage of senescent CD28null cell (CD4+ T, CD8+ T, and NKT-like cells) are markedly increased in COPD patients. Decreased SIRT1 expression is identified in these CD28null cells from COPD patients compared with CD28+ counterparts [67, 75]. Functional alterations of these senescent T cells from COPD are associated with disease severity and clinical manifestations. In vitro study demonstrates that these senescent CD8+CD28null T and NKT-like cells from COPD patients have elevated expression of perforin and granzyme and increased production of IFN-γ and TNF-α. Perforin and granzyme enhance cytotoxic activity of CD8+ T cells. IFN-γ and TNF-α are known to amplify the activity of macrophages and DCs. Consequently, these senescent T cells likely contribute to persistent systemic inflammation and progressive lung tissue damage in COPD patients [67, 76]. Glucocorticoid (GC) resistance is a major barrier in COPD treatment. The expression of glucocorticoid receptor (GCR) is reduced in these senescent CD8+CD28null T and NKT-like cells from COPD patients. This result may hint the correlation between immunosenescence and corticosteroid resistance in COPD [67, 77, 78].

Age-related changes in inflammaging in COPD

Aged mice exhibit higher CSE-induced CXCL13 expression level and subsequent inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissues (iBALTs) formation compared to young mice [79]. The development of COPD involves iBALT, a tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) whose formation and growth are mediated by CXCL13 [80]. The increased susceptibility of aged mice to CS-induced iBALT formation may explain why they are prone to develop emphysema. The upregulation of Th17 and Th2 cells in iBALT, along with increased expression of Rorγt and IL-13 in the lungs, is observed in aged CSE-exposed mice compared to young mice [79]. Rorγt is the master transcription factor for Th17, and IL-13 is a Th2-associated cytokine [81, 82]. Collectively, aging increases CSE-induced iBALT formation, and it is predominantly associated with elevated levels of Th17 and Th2 cells. Additionally, compared to younger mice, CS-exposed aged mice demonstrate significantly increased BAL and lung tissue inflammatory cells (mainly consisted of macrophages, neutrophils, and lymphocytes), higher BAL cytokine levels (including MCP-1, MIP-1α, IL-6, TNF-α, and KC), and MMP12 upregulation. These findings suggest that aging induces changes in lung inflammation and immune cell susceptibility to CS, thereby accelerating the pathophysiological hallmarks of COPD [79].

The histological hallmark of COPD is persistent airway inflammation and COPD itself can directly lead to many immunological alterations [83]. Age-related immune dysfunctions and inflammation are involved in the development and exacerbation of COPD. Concurrently, increased senescence markers have been detected in immune cells of COPD, suggesting that immune-cell senescence plays a role in this mechanism. Therefore, COPD, age-related immune remodeling, and immune-cell senescence collectively form a complex, mutually reinforcing cellular and molecular pathogenic network.

The immunosuppressive ecosystem driven by immunosenescence in lung cancer

Approximately 50% of lung cancer are diagnosed in individuals aged over 70 years [84]. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), senescent immune cells promote tumor progression and metastasis through multiple pathways, thereby affecting the effectiveness of immunotherapy [85]. Immunosenescence likely plays a crucial role in determining prognosis and treatment response by driving immunosuppressive ecosystem in patients with lung cancer [5, 16, 86, 87]. Immunosenescence-driven remodeling of the tumor microenvironment and acceleration of tumor progression

Monocytes/macrophages

Age-related phenotypic transformation of monocytes/macrophages plays a crucial role in shaping the non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) TME [16]. With aging, monocytes exhibit significant downregulation of major histocompatibility complex class II and costimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86). Classical monocytes demonstrate reduced quantity and metabolic capacity, whereas senescent monocytes show decreased ROS production and diminished cytotoxicity, impairing their tumoricidal function [88].

Macrophages can inhibit tumor progression by clearing tumor cells, secreting anti-tumor cytokines, and activating immune responses. Macrophage senescence may lead to immune dysfunction and ultimately result in malignant tumors [89]. Research has further shown that senescent alveolar macrophages accumulate in lung tumorous tissue. Knocking out p16INK4a or p21Cip1 in macrophages, which are highly expressed in senescent murine and human tissues, delayed neoplasia in KrasLA1 mice [90]. Senescent alveolar macrophages impair the recruitment and/or accumulation of cytotoxic T cells within the TME [90]. Macrophages also exhibit polarization balance shifts toward the pro-tumor M2 phenotype in lung of elderly compared to young hosts [91]. Pro-tumor M2 macrophages promote tumor growth and metastasis by upregulating scavenger receptors and pro-angiogenic factors, and enhancing the secretion of various effector molecules including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), MMPs, and TGF-β [92]. Concurrently, they inhibit T cell function through B7H3, B7H4, PD-L1, and other pathways, thereby promoting tumor growth and metastasis [93].

NK cells

Age-related functional decline in NK cells plays a pivotal role in NSCLC immune evasion. Research has demonstrated functional impairments in senescent NK cells in tumor pathogenesis [94, 95]. Phenotypic changes, including elevated senescence markers (CD57 and KLRG1) [96] and a shift from the CD56brightCD16− subset to the CD56dimCD16+ subset, have been detected in aging NK cells [16]. These changes may lead to impaired cytokine production and adaptive immunity [95]. Aged NK cells exhibit reduced cytotoxicity, characterized by decreased expression of activating receptors (NKp30, NKp44, and NKG2D). This directly impairs tumor cell recognition and killing capacity [94, 97]. In addition, compromised immunoregulatory function with reduced secretion of anti-tumor cytokines (e.g., IFN-γ and TNF-α) in aged NK cells has been observed, which affects the antigen presentation of DCs and the activation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) [94, 98]. These alterations may promote NSCLC progression through multiple mechanisms, such as diminished direct tumor cell killing efficacy, impaired immune surveillance, and establishment of an immunosuppressive TME [41, 99–101].

DCs

Age-related dysfunction of DCs constitutes a key mechanism of immune evasion through several mechanisms [102]. First, DCs exhibit diminished phagocytic capacity, defective tumor antigen processing, and reduced expression of costimulatory molecules (CD80 and CD86) with age, leading to impaired antigen presentation [102]. These functional defects cause inadequate CTL activation and abnormal Treg expansion, which contribute to immune evasion in NSCLC [103]. Second, aging leads to a cytokine imbalance developing through a shift toward immunosuppressive cytokine profiles, characterized by diminished IL-12 (a pro-inflammatory factor) secretion alongside increased IL-10 (an anti-inflammatory factor) production [104]. These alterations promote tumor progression by directly weakening CTL-mediated tumor cell killing [105], which disrupts the immune balance to form an inhibitory TME and ultimately leads to immune surveillance failure and provides escape opportunities for NSCLC cells [105].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells

Myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSCs) are a heterogeneous population of immature, highly immunosuppressive myeloid cells that are expanded in disease states such as cancer [106]. There is convincing evidence that the aging process increases the frequencies of MDSCs in humans [107]. Aged MDSCs exhibit enhanced immunosuppressive activity in the TME, forming heterogeneous and immature myeloid cell populations that proliferate during tumor progression [12, 108]. Furthermore, with aging, increased population of MDSCs exhibit higher production of immunosuppressive molecules (e.g., PD-L1, arginase-1 and tryptophan), leading to more suppression of T cell activity [109–111]. They also elevate the production of anti-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β, ROS and NO), promoting tumor immune escape via direct suppression of anti-tumor cytotoxicity mediated by CTLs and NK cells, as well as induction of Treg expansion [107]. Furthermore, aged MDSCs, together with other immunosuppressive cells (e.g., Tregs, Bregs and M2 macrophages) in the TME of older patients, may represent a critical microenvironmental factor contributing to immunotherapy resistance [107].

T cells

T cell senescence facilitates tumor immune escape in lung cancer, enabling malignant growth. During aging, thymic involution results in a decrease in naïve T cell numbers and reduced receptor diversity, severely impairing the immune system's ability to recognize tumor neoantigens [112]. Senescent T cells are characterized by the loss of CD28 and CD27, upregulation of CD57, and increased expression of KLRG1 [113, 114]. This shift mirrors the immune profile seen in patients with advanced lung cancer, which is characterized by an expansion of CD28⁻CD57⁺CD8⁺ T cells and immunosuppressive Tregs. These patients exhibit diminished proliferative potential and compromised antitumor effector functions [115]. The interaction between upregulated immune checkpoint molecules (e.g., programmed death [PD]−1) on T cells and overexpressed PD-L1/PD-L2 ligands drives T cell exhaustion [116, 117], which is characterized by effector function loss and proliferative arrest, a key factor in reduced immunotherapy responses in older patients with lung cancer [118]. Aged T cells also exhibit defects in the secretion of anti-tumor cytokines (e.g., IL-2, IFN-γ, and TNF-α) [119]. These alterations significantly weaken the immune surveillance of lung cancer cells by reducing their proliferative capacity and tumor antigen responsiveness [120].

B cells

Aged B cells exhibit distinct pathological features in NSCLC immune evasion. The declining output of B cells from bone marrow hematopoietic stem cells contributes to a contraction of the naïve B cell compartment and a less diverse B cell receptor repertoire [22, 121]. This deficit directly compromises the immune system’s ability to recognize tumor-associated antigens in NSCLC [42]. Functionally, aged B cells display impaired CSR, reducing high-affinity IgG production with predominant low-efficiency immunoglobulin M (IgM) antibodies [122]. Consequently, the secretion of autoantibodies and pro-inflammatory cytokines is strongly enhanced, leading to inflammaging [10]. In addition, aged B cells also exhibit phenotypic changes characterized by CD27 loss and increased CD21 expression [123], accompanied by decreased proliferative capacity and abnormal cytokine secretion [57]. Aging promotes the expansion of regulatory B cells (Bregs) [22, 124], which secrete IL-10 and suppress T cell activity [125], further enhancing immunosuppression in the NSCLC microenvironment. These combined effects impair humoral immunity, sustain inflammaging [126], and facilitate the establishment of an immune-suppressive microenvironment favorable for tumor escape [127].

Inflammaging in lung cancer pathogenesis

Inflammaging plays a central role in accelerating immune surveillance decline and enhancing stem cell properties, thereby promoting tumor invasion [128, 129]. In the TME of lung cancer, senescent cells exacerbate inflammaging by releasing pro-inflammatory mediators [7, 46, 130]. A condition of persistent, low-grade inflammation and an immunosuppressive niche via recruitment of immunosuppressive cells (e.g., MDSCs, macrophages, Bregs and Tregs) is constructed under SASP stimulation [131], which directly promotes tumor growth and metastasis [107]. Furthermore, inflammaging leads to anti-tumor therapy resistance by activating signaling pathways in tumor cells, resulting in poor responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors and shorter progression-free survival [132, 133].

Association between immunosenescence and pulmonary fibrosis pathogenesis

Immunosenescence is closely associated with the pathogenesis of PF. Age-related alterations in both innate immune cells (macrophages, NK cells, leukocytes) and adaptive immune cells (T cells) drive the transformation of the pulmonary microenvironment into a pro-fibrotic state. These changes disrupt the normal immune surveillance and regulatory functions of immune cells, which in turn contribute to the self-reinforcing cycle of chronic inflammation and fibrosis in lungs. However, it remains to be clarified whether immunosenescence acts as an initiating cause of PF or a secondary response induced by PF’s pathological microenvironment. More likely, the two interact reciprocally and form a mutual causal relationship (Fig. 4) [42, 134].

Age-related changes of immune cells in pulmonary fibrosis

Leukocytes

Telomere attrition represents a hallmark of replicative cellular senescence [52, 135]. One previous study demonstrated telomere shortening in circulating leukocytes in patients with idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [136]. In a large-scale multinational study, researchers compared patients with rheumatoid arthritis with and without ILD, and showed that shortened peripheral blood leukocyte telomere length is a distinguishing feature of rheumatoid arthritis–associated ILD and correlates with lung function impairment [137]. This evidence suggests that telomere shortening may serve as an intrinsic and systemic driver underlying immunosenescence in IPF.

Macrophages

Senescence-associated alterations in macrophages are associated with PF. Transcriptomic profiling of lung macrophages during fibrogenesis revealed changes in expression of genes related to senescence-consistent signatures such as apoptosis, cell cycle regulation (e.g., p53 signaling), and morphological abnormalities [138, 139]. Mitochondrial dysfunction and defective autophagy are age-associated changes in macrophages. Experimental evidence confirmed that mitochondrial damage accumulates in IPF alveolar macrophages, leading to autophagy dysfunction [140]. In a mouse model of ionizing radiation-induced PF, macrophages exhibited senescence-associated β-galactosidase activity along with significantly elevated expression of senescence markers (p16, p21, Bcl-2, and Bcl-xl), profibrotic factors (TGF-β1 and activin receptor-like kinase 1), proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α and IL-6), SASP-related chemokines, and MMPs [141]. This suggests that macrophages present distinct senescent phenotypes, and their secretion of multiple profibrotic and proinflammatory factors may act as a key driver to promote the development of PF by activating fibroblasts. However, these observations only establish a functional association between macrophages presenting senescent phenotypes and PF, and cannot clarify whether PF pathologically induces the senescence-related changes in macrophages, or senescent macrophage initiates and aggravates PF.

NK cells

Lung-recruited NK cells in IPF exhibit elevated expression of senescence markers (e.g., C12FDG and IL-6) and an impaired CD57−NKG2A+ phenotype, characterized by immunosenescence, reduced cytotoxicity, and defective maturation. These NK cells also correlate with pathological severity, particularly in the lower lobes, where senescent cells accumulate [142]. In vivo experiments indicated that the lower lobe in PF provides an environment that induces NK cell senescence. In vitro experiments further confirmed the decreased cytotoxic activity of IPF pulmonary NK cells and increased β-Gal activity [142]. A bleomycin murine model demonstrated that NK cell depletion leads to impaired clearance of senescent cells, suggesting that immunosenescence and defective NK cell activity may perpetuate the accumulation of senescent cells in IPF lungs [142].

T cells

T cell senescence is characterized by an increased number of CD28− T cells [143]. These cells possess shortened telomeres, a hallmark of senescence, and abundantly secrete IFN-γ, TNF-α, perforin, and granzymes [143, 144]. Significant proportions of peripheral CD4+CD28− T cells were observed in patients with IPF [145, 146]. CD4⁺CD28− T cells have also been detected in both BALF and lung transplant tissues of patients with IPF, with increased cell accumulation significantly correlating with poor prognosis [147]. In explanted lung tissues from patients with IPF, a marked elevation in the number of CD8⁺CD28null T cells was observed compared with normal lung tissues. Besides, the CD3⁺ T cells isolated from these IPF tissues show loss of CD28 transcript expression and increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Moreover, CD28null T cells obtained from IPF patients could induce lung remodeling in humanized NSG mice, which was not observed with CD28⁺ T cells obtained from normal donor lungs [148]. Current evidence indicates a close correlation between the senescent phenotype of T cells and the pathological characteristics as well as disease progression of IPF. However, it remains unclear whether T cell senescence contributes to the pathogenesis of IPF, or if the pathological microenvironment of IPF induces senescence-related changes in T cells.

Fibrosis-driven crosstalk: senescent cells and macrophages in the lung

The crosstalk between senescent cells and macrophages may play a key role in driving pro-fibrotic state in the lung microenvironment. Senescent cells exhibit increased mitochondrial ROS production and aberrant activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages, resulting in IL-1β and IL-18 secretion. IL-1β promotes TGF-β production, thereby stimulating the proliferation of epithelial cells and resident fibroblasts as well as their trans-differentiation into collagen-producing myofibroblasts [149, 150]. Furthermore, IL-1β induces secretion of neutrophil chemotactic factors, leading to neutrophil influx, which exacerbates alveolar epithelial cell (AEC) death and enhances platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) production. These mechanisms contribute to the progression of fibrosis [151, 152]. Moreover, studies suggest that interactions between senescent AECs and macrophages may contribute to PF [153]. Specifically, macrophage inhibitory factor-1 secreted by senescent AECs enhances M2 macrophage activation and promotes fibroblast activation in PF, resulting in increased secretion of TGF-β, PDGF, FGF, and VEGF, which stimulate fibroblast proliferation and collagen synthesis [146, 154].

Age-related changes of the immune system and inflammation in asthma

Asthma is a heterogeneous disease with multiple inflammatory phenotypes. The typical asthmatic airway inflammation is eosinophilic (e.g., Th2-high and Th2-ultrahigh), mediated by Th2 lymphocytes and M2-polarized macrophages, involving cytokines such as IL-4, IL-5, IL-13, and TGF-β. Another type of asthmatic inflammation is neutrophilic (Th2-low), driven by Th1, Th17 lymphocytes, and M1 macrophages, involving the cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-17, and IL-1β/IL-6/TNF-α [155]. Asthma often starts at a young age (childhood-onset asthma), but some patients can develop asthma later in life (late-onset asthma), even after the age of 65 years [156, 157]. The prevalence of asthma in the elderly is progressively increasing, with higher rates of morbidity, mortality, and exacerbations compared to asthma in younger patients [7, 157]. They are more likely to be underdiagnosed, misdiagnosed, or undertreated [158].

Studies have documented the existence of some age-related alterations in immune cells form patients with asthma. Circulating leukocytes from asthmatic patients have shorter telomeres and reduced SIRT1 expression, and these features are more pronounced in severe asthmatic patients. It suggests that immune cell aging in asthma is a process potentially linked to disease progression [159, 160]. Age-related alterations in T cell subsets have also been observed. Bioinformatics analysis identified an aging-related immune cell subset in patients with asthma, characterized by a higher proportion of activated CD4+ memory T cells [161]. Similarly, aged mouse models of asthma have a decrease in splenic CD4+ naïve T cells and an increase in splenic CD4+ TEM and TCM cells compared to young mice [162]. Meanwhile, lack of autophagy (a hallmark of cellular senescence) in DCs is correlated with a significantly increased frequency of pulmonary Th17 effector memory T cells in a mouse model, suggesting that immune cell aging may influence T cell subset alterations [163].

Age-related changes of the immune system affect systemic and airway inflammation in asthma. Evidence regarding to these processes and differences between older and younger patients with asthma is relatively limited. Here, we provide some insights into how age-related alterations in immune responses correlate with systemic and airway inflammation and its regulation in older asthmatic patients (Fig. 5).

Age-related changes of Th2 (eosinophilic predominant) inflammation response in asthma

Eosinophils from asthma in the elderly may have some distinct functions compared to those from younger patients with asthma. Peripheral eosinophils isolated from older asthmatic patients show reduced degranulation upon IL-5 stimulation and a tendency toward lower superoxide generation compared to those from younger asthmatic patients. However, leukotriene C4 production by eosinophils remains comparable between the two age groups [7]. Thus, peripheral blood eosinophils from asthmatic patients exhibit age-related alterations in their anti-pathogen capacity. However, whether airway eosinophils also exhibit age-related decline in effector functions remains unclear.

There is limited evidence on how age-related immune changes driven Th2 inflammation in asthma. We can only infer potential connections from a limited number of studies. Reduced SIRT1 activity in PBMCs from asthmatic patients is associated with elevated IL-4 expression. In vitro study demonstrates that SIRT1 inhibition upregulates Th2 cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) by enhancing GATA-3 (a Th2 transcription factor) acetylation and activity in T lymphocyte nuclear proteins [160]. Thus, it is reasonable to speculate that immunosenescence contributes to the enhancement of Th2 inflammation in asthmatic patients. In addition, shorter telomere length is found in circulating leukocytes from asthmatic patients and is negatively correlated with elevated plasma eotaxin-1 (CCL-11) levels. CCL11 is a key chemokine for eosinophil recruitment. It upregulates C–C chemokine receptor type 3 (CCR3) on eosinophils, promoting their recruitment and ROS production [159]. Therefore, it is hypothesized that immunosenescence in circulating cells, evidenced by shortened telomeres, may drive heightened asthma-related inflammation and oxidative stress by priming the CCL11-CCR3 axis.

Age-related changes of Th1/Th17 (neutrophilic predominant) inflammation response in asthma

Accumulative evidence shows enhanced neutrophilic inflammation and Th1/Th17 response in asthma in the elderly. Levels of airway neutrophils and neutrophil-related mediators (including IL-1β, IL-6, KC/IL-8, TNF-α, MIP-3α/CCL20, and neutrophil elastase) increase with aging in both asthmatic patients and mouse models [162, 164–167]. This parallels the elevated airway neutrophils associated with physiological aging in healthy subjects [32]. Some studies included age-stratified matched healthy controls to investigate whether this increase is caused by inflammaging or represents an asthma-specific alteration. These findings indicate that while the role of asthma itself remains debated, inflammaging is the primary driver of age-related differences in neutrophil profiles among patients with asthma [165, 168]. Elderly asthmatic patients have elevated levels of Th1 and Th17 inflammatory mediators in sputum compared to younger patients, including IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-8, IL-17A, and IL-17F [165, 169]. In a mouse model of asthma, aged mice exhibit heightened Th17 responses both in the airways (elevated IL-17A) and systemically (elevated IL-17A, IL-21, IL-22 from T cells) compared to young mice [162].

The exact role of age-related immune changes in driving Th1/Th17 inflammation response in elderly patients with asthma is not yet well defined. Age-dependent alterations in DCs could be a potential factor involved. BMDCs from aged asthmatic mice demonstrated significantly lower MHC class II but higher TNF-α, IL-1β, and KC/IL-8 expression compared to young mice [162]. Impaired autophagy is considered a hallmark of cellular senescence [170]. Deletion of the autophagy-related gene5 (Atg5) gene, which leads to impairment of the autophagy pathway, specifically in pulmonary CD11c+ cells (rather than alveolar epithelial cell) triggers spontaneous IL-17A-dependent (mainly from pulmonary γδ and αβ T cells) neutrophilic airway inflammation and hyperresponsiveness in mice. Conversely, severe asthmatic lung inflammation further impairs autophagy in lung DCs [163]. In addition, such age-dependent Th17 cytokine response in asthma may be dependent on both CD4+ T cells and DCs. Co-culture experiments of CD4+ T cells and BMDCs from older asthmatic mice showed significant elevation of IL-17A and IL-17F compared to younger mice [162]. Similarly, autophagy-deficient BMDCs can enhance systemic IL-17A production from CD4+ T cells via IL-1 and IL-23 pathways (higher amounts of IL-1α, IL-1β, and IL-23 production). Thus, it drives Th17 polarization and neutrophil recruitment [163].

Taken together, in elderly patients with asthma, disease-specific pathology may synergize with normal aging processes to shape airway inflammation. Though the impact of age-related changes in immune system on this process is not clearly established, it may, in part, play a contributory role. Immune alterations caused by asthma itself and caused by aging mutually reinforce each other, participating collectively in the pathogenesis of the disease.

The role of immunosenescence in respiratory tract infections

RTIs are among the most common infections affecting aged individuals [171]. Advanced age and age-related immune responses are major risk factors for RTIs and associated complications [171–174]. Immunosenescence is considered a key contributor to the elevated mortality observed in aged individuals with RTIs [174]. Aberrant age-related innate immunity in respiratory tract infections

Neutrophils

Aged patients infected with bacteria exhibit a significant decline in the immune functions of neutrophils. The neutrophil-mediated phagocytosis of opsonized respiratory bacteria (e.g., Streptococcus pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus) is impaired in aged individuals [175–177]. The reduced response of neutrophils during aging suggests that related receptors (e.g., FcγRIII) may exist at reduced levels or have impaired signaling function in bacteria [175]. These defects result in diminished bacterial clearance efficiency, making aged individuals more susceptible to bacterial infections.

DCs

Transcriptional analysis of cDC2s in aged mice showed impaired IFN-I responsiveness, which is attributed to decreased Ifnb1 induction post-immunization [178]. DCs in aged individuals secrete elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, but lower levels of IL-10 when stimulated [179], impairing their ability to regulate immune responses effectively. During SARS-CoV-2 infection, myeloid DCs are only partially activated, whereas the activation of plasmacytoid DCs becomes significantly impaired with age, which leads to reduced secretion of protective antiviral cytokines (e.g., IFN-I, IFN-III, and IL-29) in aged individuals, subsequently impairing the priming of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells [180].

NK cells

NK cells regulate immune responses to viral infections through natural cytotoxicity and cytokine production [181]. They target infected cells through cytotoxicity to eliminate them and contribute to cellular homeostasis [181–183]. Aged mice show a decline in the numbers and function of NK cells with more immature and less fully mature NK cells. These age-related changes may impair the ability of NK cells during the early phase of influenza infection and cause fatal infection [184]. One study found that aging reshapes NK cell subsets, characterized by a reduction in early high-functional NK cells and an increase in late low-cytotoxic NK cells. Concurrently, it upregulates inflammation-related genes and downregulates antiviral-related genes in NK cells, thereby impairing their ability to clear virus-infected cells. SARS-CoV-2 can amplify these aging-induced imbalances in NK cell subsets and functional abnormalities, exacerbating the severity of the disease. Notably, age-related NK cell defects are one of the key factors contributing to the increased susceptibility of aged individuals to SARS-CoV-2 and poor prognosis. After SARS-CoV-2 infection, the NK cell-associated inflammatory state in aged individuals is difficult to resolve, and their recovery rate is slower than that in young people, leading to persistent damage to the lungs [185].

Monocytes/macrophages

Aging impairs antigen presentation, phagocytosis, and inflammatory responses in monocytes and macrophages, thereby exacerbating immune dysfunction in older patients with RTIs. Studies have shown increased non-classical monocytes in older adults and downregulated HLA-DR expression, indicating impaired antigen presentation and exaggerated cytokine response [186, 187]. Senescent macrophages with shortened telomeres exhibit canonical cellular senescence phenotypes, which can be further exacerbated by respiratory viral infection. These senescent macrophages inherently suffer from mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by abnormal morphology, insufficient ATP production, and increased ROS, and viral infection aggravates such mitochondrial damage. The leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytoplasm of senescent macrophages activates the cGAS/STING pathway, a key mechanism underlying exaggerated inflammatory responses following viral infection. Activation of the cGAS/STING pathway further induces NLRP3 inflammasome activation, promoting the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-18, thereby exacerbating inflammatory injury caused by viral infection. Notably, SARS-CoV-2 infection (or stimulation with its spike protein S1) also induces aberrant activation of the aforementioned pathways in senescent macrophages, indicating the viral universality of this mechanism. Senescent macrophages are key cells contributing to increased host susceptibility to respiratory viral infection and disease progression; eliminating senescent macrophages can effectively improve infection outcomes. Senolytics (e.g., fisetin, dasatinib plus quercetin) can alleviate pulmonary inflammation and enhance host survival by reducing the number of senescent macrophages, improving mitochondrial integrity, and inhibiting the activation of the STING-NLRP3 pathway. Furthermore, senescent monocytes in humans exhibit an inverse correlation between telomere length and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and the activation of the STING pathway and NLRP3 inflammasome is enhanced in these cells following viral infection, suggesting the clinical relevance of this mechanism in age-related viral infections in humans [188]. Beyond viral infections, macrophages from aged animals display impaired phagocytic function against Staphylococcus aureus. Their reduced adherence to the bacteria leads to decreased bacterial internalization, subsequently lowering the intracellular bacterial recovery rate and compromising bacterial clearance capacity [189]. The decline in phagocytic capacity of macrophages in the elderly, potentially linked to altered ROS activity, plays a key permissive role in facilitating the intracellular survival of MTB [190].

Aberrant age-related adaptive immunity in respiratory tract infections

T cells

Age-associated T cell abnormalities in aged individuals reflect the effect of immunosenescence on adaptive immune dysregulation, leading to worse clinical outcomes. In influenza and SARS-CoV-2 infections, older individuals exhibit significantly impaired T cell immune responses, characterized by reduced TCR diversity, T cell subset imbalance, and dysregulated interactions with other senescent cells, which disrupt the immune microenvironment. These alterations compromise antiviral defenses. Specifically, TCR repertoires of T cells recognize epitopes on pathogens; nevertheless, TCR diversity declines markedly with age [191]. Aged individuals with influenza virus infection exhibit a reduced diversity in the TCR αβ repertoire, which weakens recognition of critical viral epitopes (e.g., HLA-A*02:01-M158-66) and further increases the risk of severe infections [192]. Infection with respiratory viruses (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) can trigger T cell subset reprogramming in aged patients, characterized by reduced naïve and memory T cell proportions alongside an expansion of effector, cytotoxic, regulatory, and exhausted T cells [185]. Aged individuals also exhibit reduced Th17 and naïve Treg, and increased memory Treg populations [193]. The imbalance of T cells impairs pathogen clearance and exacerbates immunopathology in aged individuals [185]. In aged patients with MTB, the frequency of Tregs in peripheral blood is significantly elevated, accompanied by enhanced proliferative capacity of MTB-specific Treg cells. Concurrently, the number of IFN-γ⁺ effector T cells is reduced, leading to Th1/Th2 imbalance with a Th2 bias, which suppresses the anti-MTB immune response. Moreover, Tregs from elderly patients infected with MTB directly or indirectly inhibit the production of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α by Th1 through secreting IL-10, expressing membrane-bound TGF-β, and presenting PD-1 molecules, thereby exacerbating immune suppression [194]. However, it fails to distinguish whether these T cell immune abnormalities are spontaneous consequences of immunosenescence or secondary to MTB infection in aged individuals.

B cells

Aging leads to a substantial impairment in B lymphopoiesis and a concomitant reduction in antibody production, which are key factors in the increased susceptibility to infections and poor vaccine responses observed in aged individuals [195]. Aged individuals show significantly weakened B cell responses against influenza strains related to age-related defects in CSR and somatic hypermutation, defects in the differentiation of B memory cells to plasmablasts/antibody-secreting cells (ASCs), which stem partly from Blimp1 downregulation, contraction of B cell repertoires, age-associated epigenetic modifications, or a significant expansion of the most pro-inflammatory double-negative B cells [196]. In SARS-CoV-2 infection, abnormal expansion of age-associated B cells may disrupt B cell differentiation trajectories, impairing high-efficiency antibody production [185]. This contributes to a suboptimal humoral response and may underlie the persistent antibody anomalies observed in long COVID, including elevated levels of non-neutralizing or cross-reactive antibodies and decreased memory B cell function [8, 197]. Furthermore, aging impairs CD27+IgM B cell function in bacterial infections (e.g., S. pneumoniae), which compromises specific protection against pneumococcal polysaccharide antigens and thus increases disease risk [30]. Similarly, aging impairs humoral immunity against S. aureus. In healthy conditions, IgM levels in aged mice are higher than those in young mice. However, aged mice fail to further induce IgM elevation upon S. aureus infection, which may be associated with defects in IgM B-cell proliferation in aged individuals. Aging blunts IgG and IgG subclass responses by compromising B cell differentiation, and further diminishes antibody protective capacity while inducing IgG subclass imbalance [198]. These findings reveal multiple defects in B cell differentiation and antibody maturation that critically contribute to infection control failure in aged individuals.

Ongoing clinical trials targeting immunosenescence for lung diseases in the past 3 years

Senolytics and senomorphics

Senolytic drugs are a class of pharmacological agents that selectively eliminate senescent cells while sparing normal proliferating cells. They primarily target upregulated anti-apoptotic pathways in senescent cells, inducing apoptosis specifically in these dysfunctional cells. Representative senolytic agents include dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q), B cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) family inhibitors, p53 inhibitors (such as the d-retro-inverso form of Forkhead box O4 (FOXO4-DRI)), mouse double minute 2 (MDM2) inhibitors, fisetin, cardiac glycosides, heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) inhibitors, bromodomain and extra-terminal (BET) protein inhibitors, azithromycin, and roxithromycin [210]. Senomorphics can target senescence-related signaling and metabolic pathways (such as PI3K/mTOR signaling, SASP modulation, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide [NAD+] augmentation, antioxidant mechanisms, mitochondria targeting, SIRT activation, and microRNAs [miRNA] regulation), thus regulate the phenotype and function of senescent cells and modulate SASP [211]. Notably, senolytics and senomorphics target all senescent cells, including senescent lung structural cells such as fibroblasts in pulmonary fibrosis and epithelial cells in COPD [212–214]. Consequently, they may not represent the optimal therapeutic strategy for selectively targeting immunosenescence. The senotherapeutic effects of these drugs in chronic lung diseases have been fully elucidated elsewhere [215]. Here, we focus on senolytics and senomorphics that may act primarily by targeting senescent immune cells in the context of lung disease. Clinical studies from the past three years of these drugs utilized in lung diseases are summarized.

Senolytics can firstly reduce the release of SASP components, thereby mitigating systemic inflammatory responses and the subsequent aberrant activation of immune cells. For instance, in animal models of COVID-19, treatment with senolytic regimens including D + Q, fisetin, and navitoclax (ABT-263, Bcl-2 family inhibitor) significantly decreased serum levels of key pro-inflammatory SASP factors [216]. Secondly, senolytics can induce apoptosis in senescent immune cells. Disease progression in pulmonary fibrosis is associated with apoptosis resistance in pulmonary macrophages. Elevated Bcl-2 expression has been observed in pulmonary macrophages from both IPF patients and bleomycin-injured mice. Venetoclax (ABT-199), another Bcl-2 family inhibitor, can induce apoptosis in monocyte-derived macrophages and reverse established fibrosis in a murine model of IPF [217]. Based on this theoretical foundation, a clinical trial evaluating the efficacy and safety of venetoclax in patients with IPF was started in October 2023 (NCT05976217), with the results yet to come. In murine models of asthma, venetoclax has been shown to ameliorate eosinophilic and corticosteroid-resistant neutrophilic airway inflammation by inducing apoptosis in immune cells, including eosinophils, neutrophils, Th2 and Th17 lymphocytes, and DCs. Furthermore, in vitro studies have demonstrated that venetoclax induces apoptosis in granulocytes from patients with severe asthma more effectively than corticosteroids [218]. However, to date, no clinical trials have evaluated venetoclax against asthma.

Targeted senolytics are emerging as a promising alternative approach. Macrophages and endothelial cells constitute the primary senescent cell populations in KRAS-driven lung cancer. Elimination of senescent cells with diphtheria toxin (DT) or the senolytic agent ABT-737, or selective depletion of senescent macrophages with an anti-CSF1R antibody, all significantly reduced lung tumor burden and improved survival. Moreover, clearance of senescent cells promoted a shift in the T cell phenotype toward an immunostimulatory tumor microenvironment and diminished the tumor vascular network. Senescent macrophages have also been identified in human precancerous lung lesions, suggesting that targeting senescent macrophages represents a potential therapeutic avenue for lung cancer [219]. A completed phase Ib/II clinical trial (NCT02713529) including 116 solid tumor patients (19 patients with NSCLC) evaluated the therapeutic strategy of anti-CSF1R antibody, AMG 820, combined with pembrolizumab. While the combination demonstrated an acceptable safety profile, its antitumor activity was insufficient to warrant further evaluation in the studied patient populations. This highlights the need to explore additional strategies for targeting senescent macrophages [220].

Senomorphics that address specific signaling pathways in aging immune cells can facilitate restoring or enhancing their functions. These interventions can potentially reverse the effects of immunosenescence. Recent preclinical studies have shown that salvianolic acid B (Sal B) alleviates idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis by inhibiting the binding of transcription factor stimulating protein 1 (SP1) to the promoters of SASPs (P21 and P16). This reduces senescence in macrophages and alveolar type II epithelial cells, decreases their release of SASP factors, and subsequently lowers the proportion of activated lung fibroblasts. Therefore, targeting senescent immune cells may serve as a novel therapeutic perspective for pulmonary fibrosis [221]. Identifying further molecular changes associated with senescent immune cells would be advantageous for developing therapeutic approaches for targeting immunosenescence in lung diseases. In addition, senescent immune and non‑immune cells secrete SASP factors that drive chronic inflammation, which in turn accelerates the senescence of immune cells [222]. Thus, pharmacological agents that target the signaling pathways regulating SASP expression (including mTOR inhibitor, NF-κB inhibitor, etc.) or neutralize the activity of SASP factors (including monoclonal antibody against IL-6, IL-33, TGF-β, etc.) [223] have emerged as an alternative therapeutic approach to address the inflammation associated with immunosenescence in lung diseases [224]. The mechanisms of these drugs have been detailed in other review articles [215].

Although the clinical safety and efficacy of these drugs require further investigation. Growing evidence highlights the significant role of immunosenescence in lung disease progression, supporting its potential as a therapeutic target for anti-immunosenescence strategies in pulmonary conditions.

Immunotherapy

Current immunotherapy strategies used against cancer may represent a promising approach that can be repurposed for specifically targeting senescent immune cells [16]. As discussed previously, senescent immune cells have impaired immune function and declined ability to eliminate senescent cells. In addition, the accumulation of senescent immune cells would likely take up valuable immunological space required for expansion of functionally competent immune cells [4]. Thus, strategies utilizing immunotherapy intend to help restore impaired immune function, enhance the targeted clearance of senescent immune and non‑immune cells, and rejuvenate the immune cell repertoire. These therapeutic strategies are expected to collectively improve overall immune competence. Adoptive cell therapy (ACT) is a promising immunotherapy that can target immunosenescence in chronic lung diseases, including chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapy and adoptive transfer of NK cell therapy [225].

CAR-T cell therapy

CAR-T cell therapy represents a cellular approach that enables the specific redirection of T cells to target cells expressing particular surface antigens, providing superior selectivity and persistence.

Studies have identified the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (uPAR) as a widely expressed cell surface protein during senescence and have demonstrated that uPAR-specific CAR-T cells effectively eliminate senescent cells both in vitro and in vivo. In mouse models of lung adenocarcinoma treated with senescence-inducing drug combinations, uPAR-targeted CAR-T cells extended survival [226], with durable effects observed after a single administration [227]. This therapy significantly reduced senescent tumor cells in the lung tissues of mice, confirming the senolytic properties of uPAR-directed CAR T cells [226]. Similarly, NK group 2 member D ligands (NKG2DLs) are upregulated on senescent cells. NKG2D-directed CAR-T cells have demonstrated potent and selective senolytic activity, clearing senescent cells in cultured systems, mice, and non-human primates without significant adverse effects [228, 229]. NSCLC has been confirmed to express NKG2DL. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that both NKG2D CAR-T cells and CAR-NK cells exhibit enhanced anti-tumor effects in lung cancer [230–232]. Although the role of their senolytic effect in therapy remains unclear, they remain a highly promising therapeutic candidate.

CAR-T cells targeting senescence-specific surface antigens may be a plausible therapeutic perspective. However, CAR-T cells targeting characteristic biomarkers of senescent immune cells such as CD57 and KLRG-1 have not yet been conducted in chronic lung diseases. Further research may be required to explore whether they possess the safety and efficacy needed for clinical application, as well as to identify other novel senescence markers with prognostic potential.

NK cell therapy

Adoptive transfer of NK cells in aged mice and healthy human volunteers resulted in a significant reduction in SASP levels, a marked decrease in senescent CD28−, CD57+, CD28−CD57+, and CD28−KLRG1+ T cell and exhausted PD-1+ and TIM-3+ T cell populations [233, 234]. A phase I/IIa clinical trial has demonstrated that NK cell therapy combined with cytotoxic chemotherapy (including cetuximab) is safe and exhibits potential anti-tumor effects in TKI-resistant EGFR-mutated NSCLC (NCT04872634) [205]. Additionally, multiple clinical studies investigating NK cell therapy for NSCLC are currently underway (NCT07053007 and NCT06097962).

An alternative approach uses NK cells to eliminate senescent cells by targeting senescence-specific surface antigens via antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC). Studies have revealed that SMARCA4 inhibitors can augment NK cell-dependent clearance of senescent cells [235]. In addition, CD26/Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) is selectively expressed on the surface membrane of senescent fibroblasts. Using DPP4 as a membrane target can promote NK cell-mediated clearance of senescent cells [236]. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of this approach, although no lung disease-specific studies have been reported to date. Nevertheless, the exploration of novel senescence biomarkers with prognostic potential continues to gain significant research interest.

Other immunotherapy

Cytokine-induced killer (CIK) cell therapy is an extensively studied adoptive treatment approach. CD4+ T cells represent a critical subset within the CIK cell population. Emerging evidence indicates that the Th1/Th17 CD4+ subset restores functionality in CD8+ and CD3+CD56+ T cells and alleviates exhaustion in PD-1 + Tim-3 + T cells by promoting IL-17A–dependent AKT phosphorylation and upregulating T-bet and Eomes expression [237]. Based on these findings, an open-label Phase II study (NCT04836728) is currently underway to evaluate the efficacy of autologous CIKs combined with a PD-1 inhibitor as first-line treatment in stage IV NSCLC.

Stem cell therapy

Rejuvenating HSCs

Aging HSCs differentiate into various immunologically impaired cell populations, thereby promoting immunosenescence [238]. Targeting aging HSCs represents a potential therapeutic strategy for age-related diseases, comprising targeting molecular mechanisms that can rejuvenate HSCs (like targeting the TRMT6-TRMT61a complex [239]), infusion of balanced HSCs or selective clearance of myeloid-biased HSCs [240], treatment with extracellular nano-sized vesicles isolated from young donor plasm [241], and HSC transplantation [242]. These strategies have shown the potential to reverse the aging of HSCs and rejuvenate the immune system. However, all these findings are derived from preclinical studies, and their clinical applicability in chronic lung diseases is limited by challenges and requires further validation.

Mesenchymal stem cell therapy

Intravenous injection of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into aged rhesus monkeys can partially reverse age-related thymic atrophy and rebuild the decreased immune function [243]. This indicates that MSC-based therapy is a promising approach for reversing thymic aging and thereby mitigating systemic immune senescence. MSC treatment significantly reduced age-related elevated systemic and lung tissue inflammation and Treg cell ratios in elderly macaque models, demonstrating its immunomodulatory capacity in the aging immune system [244]. Utilizing of MSCs in COPD demonstrate a good safety and efficacy in alleviating inflammation [245–247]. However, recent studies of MSC-based treatments for COVID-19 have not observed significant therapeutic efficacy (NCT04362189, NCT04288102, and NCT04625738) [201, 203, 248]. Several ongoing clinical trials are investigating the safety and efficacy of MSCs for chronic lung diseases, including COPD (NCT06491043), ILD (NCT06230822, NCT06534528, NCT06825169, NCT06823063, and NCT06919380), and COVID-19 (NCT04798716, NCT04428801, NCT05741099, NCT06492798, NCT05017298, and NCT06693362). The latest results have not yet been published. MSCs exert pleiotropic therapeutic effects. The therapeutic implications of MSC-induced thymic and immune system rejuvenation in chronic lung diseases are yet to be determined.

Rejuvenating the thymus

An aging thymus leads to the depletion of T cell populations and the collapse of the TCR repertoire. The thymus-regeneration protocol (combining recombinant human growth hormone, dehydroepiandrosterone, and metformin) can restore thymic function and induce protective changes in the immune system, such as a significant increase in naïve CD4+ and CD8+ T cells and a marked reduction in PD‐1+CD8+T cells [249]. Additionally, androgen deprivation therapy using luteinizing hormone-releasing hormone has been shown to restore thymic function. In aged male baboons, subcutaneous administration of leuprolide acetate improved thymic morphology and function, accompanied by a significant increase in CD45RAhiCD4+ T cell populations in peripheral blood and a substantially elevated CD4/CD8+ T cell ratio. Similar effects have been observed with gonadectomy [250]. Androgen deprivation therapy triggers thymic revitalization and increases thymic output, enhancing baseline anti-tumor immunity and responses to immunotherapies. Anti-tumor synergism has been identified by combining androgen deprivation therapy with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy for androgen-independent tumors. Recently, a clinical trial investigating the efficacy of leuprorelin acetate combined with sintilimab is recruiting patients with advanced lung cancer (NCT06512207). However, drug-induced interstitial lung disease following combined androgen blockade is a rare, but potentially serious adverse effect that requires close attention [251].

Preclinical studies have also identified several pro-regenerative therapies that may contribute to thymic rejuvenation and mitigate immunosenescence, like exogenous administration of cytokines including IL-7, IL-12, IL-21, IL-22, and FLT3L [252]. IL-7 therapy, in particular, has been shown to restore the circulating T cell pool in older adults to a state closer to that of younger individuals, characterized by increased naïve T cells and greater TCR diversity. In a prospective, double-anonymized, randomized, placebo-controlled trial involving 109 patients with severe COVID-19 and lymphopenia, recombinant human IL-7 (CYT107) significantly increased lymphocyte counts compared to placebo group. CYT107-treated patients reported favorable tolerability and had fewer hospital-acquired infections compared to placebo-treated patients (NCT04379076, NCT04426201, NCT04442178, NCT04407689, and NCT04927169) [204].

Mouse studies have also demonstrated that extracellular vesicles and exosomes derived from young donor serum exhibit anti-aging effects, effectively suppressing inflammaging in aged mice. These components partially contribute to the reversal of thymic aging and the restoration of immune tolerance in senescent T cells, as evidenced by partial recovery of thymic atrophy, enhanced negative selection signals, and reduced peripheral autoimmunity [253].

Probiotics and lifestyle

Lifestyle modifications, including regular moderate exercise and dietary adjustments, show potential in delaying or reversing immunosenescence [254–256]. Micronutrient supplementation (including vitamins C, D, E, zinc, and selenium) [257], caloric restriction [258], time-restricted eating [259], phytochemicals like polyphenols, probiotics (particularly through promoting the colonization of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium), and dietary polyunsaturated fatty acids (especially essential omega-3 fatty acids, such as α-linolenic acid, eicosapentaenoic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid) have also demonstrated potential in counteracting immunosenescence [260].

A combination of influenza-pneumococcal vaccine with long-term oral probiotic Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG has been shown to significantly delay the recurrence of moderate-to-severe acute exacerbations of COPD (NCT04907877) [206]. Probiotics have also been reported to alleviate acute-phase symptoms of COVID-19, shorten symptom duration, and enhance humoral immune responses against SARS-CoV-2 (NCT04854941) [207], reduce hospital stay for patients with mild to moderate pulmonary lesions, potentially decrease mortality in severe cases [209], and effectively treat diarrhea associated with COVID-19 (NCT04666116) [208]. Concurrently, research on probiotics in asthma and lung cancer is ongoing (NCT05949255, NCT06943235, and NCT06428422).

Conclusions

Immunosenescence is not merely a passive consequence of aging but may constitute a pivotal driver in the pathogenesis of lung diseases, including COPD, lung cancer, PF, asthma, and RTIs. The intricate interplay between an increasingly senescent, dysfunctional immune system, and the aged lung microenvironment creates a vicious cycle of persistent inflammaging, impaired tissue repair, and diminished antimicrobial defense. This review examines the hallmarks of immunosenescence, and illustrates the association between immunosenescence and the pathogenesis of pulmonary disorders. Furthermore, current therapeutic strategies targeting immunosenescence in lung diseases are summarized, including senolytics and senomorphics, immunotherapy, stem cell therapy, thymic rejuvenation, probiotics, and lifestyle. There are also limitations in this review as it focuses scope on immunosenescence, which precludes a detailed discussion of the complex bidirectional interplay between immunosenescence and senescence of lung parenchymal cells in disease pathogenesis.

Looking ahead, several critical challenges and directions warrant priority attention. First, future research must progress beyond observational associations to definitively establish whether targeted modulation of specific senescent immune cell populations can directly improve clinical outcomes in human lung diseases. A central challenge lies in achieving the delicate balance between suppressing harmful inflammation and preserving essential immune vigilance. Therapeutic interventions must therefore be meticulously designed to avoid increasing the risk of infections or triggering autoimmunity. Moreover, the significant heterogeneity in immunological aging among individuals necessitates a paradigm shift away from one-size-fits-all approaches. Integrating multi-omics profiling, including genomic, epigenomic, and proteomic analyses, with AI will be paramount for developing predictive biomarkers of immunosenescence severity and treatment response in lung diseases, enabling truly personalized therapeutic strategies. Furthermore, the translation of these therapies from bench to bedside will require robust clinical trial designs in relevant aged populations with lung diseases.

Ultimately, targeting immunosenescence offers a transformative paradigm for treating lung diseases, shifting the focus from symptom management to fundamentally modifying the aged immune system that underpins pathology. Success in this endeavor relies on interdisciplinary collaboration across gerontology, immunology, pulmonology, and data science to extend not only lifespan but also respiratory health and quality of life in aged populations.
