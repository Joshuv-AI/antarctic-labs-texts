A national survey on the medical physics workload of external beam radiotherapy in Japan†
Authors: Naoki Tohyama, Hiroyuki Okamoto, Kohei Shimomura, Masahiko Kurooka, Ryu Kawamorita, Seiichi Ota, Toru Kojima, Naoki Hayashi, Masahiko Okumura, Masaru Nakamura, Mitsuhiro Nakamura, Atsushi Myojoyama, Hiroshi Onishi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeveral staffing models are used to determine the required medical physics staffing, including radiotherapy technologists, of radiation oncology departments. However, since Japanese facilities tend to be smaller in scale than foreign ones, those models might not apply to Japan. Therefore, in this study, we surveyed workloads in Japan to estimate the optimal medical physics staffing in external beam radiotherapy. A total of 837 facilities were surveyed to collect information regarding radiotherapy techniques and medical physics specialists (RTMPs). The survey covered facility information, staffing, patient volume, equipment volume, workload and quality assurance (QA) status. Full-time equivalent (FTE) factors were estimated from the workload and compared with several models. Responses were received from 579 facilities (69.2%). The median annual patient volume was 369 at designated cancer care hospitals (DCCHs) and 252 across all facilities. In addition, the median FTE of RTMPs was 4.6 at DCCHs and 3.0 at all sites, and the average QA implementation rate for radiotherapy equipment was 69.4%. Furthermore, advanced treatment technologies have increased workloads, particularly in computed tomography simulations and treatment planning tasks. Compared to published models, larger facilities (over 500 annual patients) had a shortage of medical physics staff. In very small facilities (about 140 annual patients), the medical physics staffing requirement was estimated to be 0.5 FTE, implying that employing a full-time medical physicist would be inefficient. However, ensuring the quality of radiotherapy is an important issue, given the limited number of RTMPs. Our study provides insights into optimizing staffing and resource allocation in radiotherapy departments.

INTRODUCTION

Medical physics staff members, including radiotherapy technologists, play an important role in ensuring the safe and efficient clinical implementation of advanced radiotherapy techniques, such as stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT), intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) and particle therapy. The introduction of these advanced techniques involves many technical processes, including acceptance testing, commissioning, implementing and managing the quality assurance (QA) of radiation treatment and imaging equipment. Furthermore, QA covers all aspects of the radiotherapy process, from simulation to treatment planning to delivery, for which the medical physics staff is responsible. In addition to providing clinical services, many departments are involved in research and development initiatives to advance clinical practice.

Appropriate radiotherapy staffing standards should be updated based on the needs of local patient populations, the capacity to provide radiotherapy and the demands of new techniques. To ensure safe and effective radiotherapy practices, a team of specialists, including radiation oncologists, medical physicists and radiotherapy technologists, must adhere to international basic safety standards [1]. To ensure the safe and efficient delivery of radiotherapy to patients, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has developed an algorithm for calculating staffing requirements based on available or easily estimable information [2]. Additionally, the American Society for Radiation Oncology, in collaboration with relevant organizations, has developed a framework for quality radiation oncology care that includes a worksheet for determining the required number of medical physicists and dosimetrists to oversee treatment planning and QA [3]. These are widely accepted forecasts for basic staff requirements for medical physics. However, according to a recent survey, a significant workload escalation is caused by the rapid implementation of advanced treatment techniques [4]. This finding suggests that staffing guidelines should be revised regularly to account for rapid and pervasive technological changes.

Although many medical physics staffing models are employed in European and American countries [2, 5–8], these models cannot be applied to Japanese radiation oncology departments. There are several differences in patient size, treatment, imaging equipment and specializations in the field of medical physics. In European and American countries, typical large facilities have >10 medical linear accelerators (Linacs) and multiple simulators, treating almost 5000 different patients each year [7]. Even in small facilities, 1500 patients are treated with multiple Linacs each year. In Japan, the number of patients treated is lower than in those facilities, and even the largest facility only treats an average of 910 cases [9]. Additionally, only 1.27 linear accelerators per facility are used on average [9]. One possible explanation is the lower utilization rate of radiotherapy for cancer patients in Japan (~30%), which is significantly lower than the global average [10]. There is also a significant difference in staffing levels. In contrast to other countries, where facilities maintain a well-staffed medical physics department composed of many specialties, including medical physicists, medical dosimetrists, physics assistants, engineering technologists and information technology specialists, Japanese radiation oncology departments have only 0.4 full-time equivalents (FTEs) of physicists per facility [9]. Therefore, radiotherapy technologists partially serve as medical physicists in most facilities, overseeing both the medical physics processes described above and the delivery of radiation treatments.

In Japan, many radiotherapy facilities face significant staff shortages [9]. To ensure adequate radiotherapy provision, it is crucial to allocate an appropriate number of staff members based on factors such as the type of irradiation technique, number of patients and quantity of radiotherapy equipment. This is essential for maintaining device quality and effectively performing complex tasks. In Japan, the Japanese Society for Radiation Oncology (JASTRO) continues to conduct structural surveys [9, 11–23]. However, comprehensive surveys on radiotherapy workload, which serves as the basis for establishing staffing systems, have not been conducted. This study surveyed the national workload for external photon beam radiation therapy to estimate the optimal number of medical physics staff required at each facility, including medical physicists and radiotherapy technologists.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Survey sample and surveying process

A survey was conducted between June and July of 2022 to gather information on the status of radiotherapy techniques and medical physics specialists (RTMPs) in Japan. The survey aimed to assess the involvement of radiotherapy technologists and medical physicists in radiotherapy practices. Invitations to participate in the survey were mailed to 837 radiotherapy facilities, and the survey was also promoted through a mailing list affiliated with national radiotherapy-related organizations. The list of institutions was compiled using information obtained from medical institutions that had received notifications of institution standards from the regional health bureaus of the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. All medical institutions that provided radiotherapy services in Japan were considered target institutions for this survey. Respondents answered the survey using an Excel file and uploaded the file through a dedicated website created using Google services. Each institution was surveyed only once, and no patient identification information was collected. All procedures used in this study were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Cancer Center Hospital in Tokyo, Japan (Approval Number 2021–476).

Historically, there has been a lack of clear differentiation between the roles of radiotherapy technologists and medical physicists within radiation oncology departments in Japan, and this situation varies across facilities. For this study, the collective term RTMPs was used for both radiotherapy technologists and medical physicists. RTMPs responsible for irradiation and imaging within the radiation oncology department were referred to as RTMP responsible for irradiation and imaging (RTMP-RT), whereas those responsible for treatment planning and QA were referred to as radiotherapy techniques and medical physics specialists responsible for treatment planning and quality assurance (RTMP-MP). The workload was calculated without separating RTMP-RT and RTMP-MP in our survey.

Survey instrument

This study presents findings related to facility information and the workload of photon external beam therapy. The questions regarding facility information covered various aspects of the facility’s current status, including the number of staff members, patient volume, equipment, staff adequacy, and QA status. The workload-related questions are clearly defined and summarized in Table 1. These questions were divided into two categories: patient-related and equipment-related workloads. The specific job titles of the individuals performing each task were not limited. Patient-related workloads included general clinical work, 3D conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT), intracranial SRT (iSRT), SBRT, IMRT and patient-specific dose verification. For patient-related workload, responses were collected by multiplying the number of staff members involved by the number of working times. The workload for IGRT was also included as part of the irradiation task. The equipment-related workload refers to tasks related to the QA of treatment equipment and computed tomography (CT) equipment for treatment planning. Responses for equipment-related task workload were collected by multiplying the number of staff members involved by the number of working times.

Statistical analysis

This study compared the workload of various irradiation techniques using the EZR statistical software, version 1.61 [24]. For multiple comparisons, Kruskal–Wallis tests followed by the Steel–Dwass test were used, with a P-value <0.05 considered statistically significant.

FTE factor calculation

FTE represents a ratio of workload to the standard workload of a full-time employee. Workload was calculated by summing the time required for each task and corresponding number of tasks performed. Therefore, to estimate the impact of a task on FTE, we defined the FTE factor as the ratio of workload assigned to an employee for a specific task to the standard annual workload of a full-time employee. To assess the workload for individual tasks, we considered the average workload time obtained from the survey conducted in this study. The standard annual workload of a full-time employee was standardized to 1760 h per year, considering a work schedule of 220 days per year and 8-h workday. The workload setting of a full-time employee is consistent with the IAEA staffing model [2].

Comparison with previously published staffing models

Several organizations have reported staffing models for medical physics staff members, including radiotherapy technologists. Four representative staffing models [2, 5–8] and national guidelines [25] were chosen and compared to staff numbers based on the calculated FTE factors. Table 2 summarizes the staffing models. This table has been simplified and condensed to combine the information into one table. To ensure an accurate comparison, the model facilities were categorized into five facility sizes based on the median number of linear accelerators and patients at designated cancer care hospitals (DCCHs), excluding departments that provided particle therapy and gamma knife treatment. The survey included uncollected clinical task implementation rates, such as the preparation of patient-specific immobilization devices. To determine the average implementation rate of the clinical tasks, an additional survey was conducted with 49 research collaborators who contributed to the development of this questionnaire. Other comparison conditions included a single planning CT, no X-ray simulator, no brachytherapy equipment, no special external or intraoperative irradiation, such as total body irradiation (TBI) or total skin electron therapy (TSET) and no research and development, teaching or radiation safety work. The staffing numbers for RTMP-MP, RTMP-RT and their totals were compared.

RESULTS

Survey response rate and respondent characteristics

The survey received responses from 579 facilities, with a response rate of 69.2%. Among the respondents, 520 (89.8%) identified themselves as radiation technologists, while 56 (9.7%) identified themselves as medical physicists. The respondents held various positions, including 34 (5.9%) equivalent to chief radiation technologists in radiology and radiation oncology departments, 71 (12.3%) equivalent to chief radiation technologists in radiation oncology departments, 344 (59.4%) equivalent to representatives in radiation oncology departments and 130 (22.5%) in other positions.

Institution characteristics

The institutions surveyed included university hospitals (n = 91; 15.7%), national hospitals (n = 61; 10.5%), public hospitals (n = 155; 26.8%), cancer centers and similar facilities (n = 11; 1.9%), Red Cross hospitals and similar institutions (n = 135; 23.3%), as well as medical corporation hospitals and similar institutions (n = 126; 21.8%). In Japan, there were 456 DCCHs accredited by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare [26], and 300 of them (65.8%) responded to the survey. In terms of JASTRO board-certified institutions [27, 28], the distribution was as follows: A (large institutions; n = 86; 15.0%), B (medium institutions; n = 63; 11.0%), C (small institutions; n = 44; 7.7%), S (particle therapy; n = 6; 1.0%), S (pediatric; n = 2; 0.3%) and uncertified (n = 374; 65.0%). The third-party postal audit certification evaluated the beam output of at least one or more treatment units in 74.7% of institutions.

Characteristics of patient caseloads, RTMP staffing and radiotherapy equipment and technology

Table 3 summarizes the patient caseloads, RTMP staff and photon radiotherapy equipment at each facility, categorized by patient size and DCCH accreditation. Some facilities had many unanswered items and were thus excluded from the table. The median (25th–75th interquartile range [IQR]) number of patients treated annually was 369 (229–577.3) for DCCHs, 169 (106–253) for non-DCCHs and 252 (139.5–440.5) for all facilities. In particular, the median number of patients treated annually by iSRT, SBRT and IMRT was 5 (0.0–20.0), 13 (2.0–31.3) and 69.5 (0.0–151.5), respectively. It is worth noting that 65.1% of radiotherapy facilities treated patients using a single treatment unit. In terms of staffing, the median number of FTEs for RTMP was 4.6 (3.0–6.8) for DCCHs, 2.3 (2.0–3.6) for non-DCCHs and 3.0 (2.0–6.0) overall.

QA implementation status of radiotherapy machines

Figure 1 depicts the QA implementation rate for photon beam external beam radiotherapy machines based on the QA list specified in the American Association of Physicists in Medicine Task Group 142 (AAPM TG 142) [29]. If all TG142 procedures were performed at the recommended frequency, the QA implementation rate was defined as 100%; however, if only half of the procedures were performed, the QA implementation rate was defined as 50%. The average implementation rate was found to be 69.4%. In terms of the status of QA tasks during statutory working hours, the responses were categorized as follows: ‘done’ 13.9%, ‘somewhat done’ 28.0%, ‘neither yes nor no’ 14.1%, ‘somewhat undecided’ 22.4% and ‘undecided’ 21.7%. When asked if QA tasks were considered essential within the institution, the responses were as follows: ‘yes’ 28.3%, ‘somewhat yes’ 34.0%, ‘neither yes nor no’ 21.0%, ‘somewhat no’ 10.6% and ‘no’ 6.1%.

Photon external beam radiotherapy-related workload

Table 4 summarizes the working time for each task, from the treatment appointment to the CT imaging. The average working time for the respective routine clinical tasks, from preliminary explanation to plain CT imaging, was 68.0 min. Additional time was required for patient-specific immobilization devices as well as additional optional scans such as contrast-enhanced CT and 4DCT imaging. Figure 2 depicts the time spent on each task, from image import to irradiation. The working time for image import and image fusion was significantly longer for SBRT than for 3DCRT (P < 0.05), with no differences for other irradiation techniques. The contouring working time did not differ between 3DCRT and iSRT but was significantly longer for SRT, SBRT and IMRT in that order (P < 0.05). In addition, the treatment planning time was significantly longer for IMRT than for the other irradiation techniques (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the planning approval time was significantly longer for IMRT than for 3DCRT (P < 0.05). Most irradiation techniques had comparable plan check times, whereas 3DCRT had significantly shorter plan check times than SBRT (P < 0.05) and IMRT (P < 0.05). The irradiation preparation time was significantly longer for SBRT than for 3DCRT (P < 0.05). Moreover, the irradiation time of the advanced radiotherapy techniques was longer than that of 3DCRT (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the additional time required for respiratory motion management was significantly longer for SBRT than for 3DCRT (P < 0.05) and IMRT (P < 0.05).

Estimated FTE factors based on workloads

Table 6 lists the FTE factors derived from the average working times for each task. To calculate the required number of RTMPs per year, multiply the FTE factor by the frequency of each task performed annually, and then sum these values together. We have demonstrated the method to calculate the minimum number of RTMP staff required using the FTE factor as an example in Supplementary Table 1. The FTE factor for irradiation time was corrected to account for the 6 min of work required to switch patients. Patient switching times were decided unanimously among research collaborators.

Staffing model comparisons

Table 7 compares the facility size settings of the published staffing models. Based on the annual patient volume, the facility size was categorized as very large (1000 or more patients), large (750 or more and fewer than 1000 patients), medium (500 or more and fewer than 750 patients), small (250 or more and fewer than 500 patients) and very small (fewer than 250 patients). The very large facilities had three linacs, while small and very small facilities had only one. Notably, the very small facilities do not utilize high-precision irradiation techniques. Table 8 summarizes the average implementation rates for the additional clinical tasks. In the iSRT, customized shell fabrication was performed for all patients. For SBRT, vacuum cushion creation was used in 89% of the patients, and 4DCT scans were performed in 73%. Respiratory motion management was used in 66% of SBRT cases, whereas 3DCRT and IMRT were used in 11 and 13% of cases, respectively. Table 9 compares our model with a previously published staffing model. In our model, the FTE numbers of RTMP-MPs (e.g. medical physicists) were 4.4, 3.2, 2.4, 1.3 and 0.55 for very large, large, medium, small and very small facilities, respectively. In our model and in practice, the total number of medical physics staff members, including radiotherapy technologists, was comparable between the small and very small facilities. However, the very large facilities had a very low staffing level of 9.2 compared with the recommended value of 14.7.

DISCUSSION

Through a comprehensive nationwide survey, the external radiotherapy workload in Japan was thoroughly assessed. The study determined the optimal staffing for each facility by identifying the time required for each task and calculating the FTE factors. The required RTMP staff can be accurately estimated by multiplying the FTE factors by the annual number of tasks at each facility and tabulating the results. This approach facilitates the efficient allocation of equipment and personnel, contributing to the effective provision of external radiotherapy services in Japan. Furthermore, the occupational structure and job categories of medical physicists, radiotherapy technologists and dosimetrists in the radiation oncology departments studied differed across countries. Therefore, we developed a work-based staffing model that considers the differences in job titles and responsibilities from facility to facility and country to country. As a result, larger facilities (over 500 annual patients) were found to have a shortage of medical physics staff members, including radiotherapy technologists, in Japan. Furthermore, special technologists who can perform the tasks of both medical physicists and radiotherapy technologists are required to properly administer radiotherapy at small facilities.

Compared with the previous surveys conducted in Japan, the findings of this survey are thought to appropriately reflect the state of radiotherapy in Japan. Numasaki et al. surveyed 737 facilities in Japan, including university hospitals (15.6%), national hospitals (7.3%), public hospitals (28.2%), cancer centers and others (3.4%), Red Cross hospitals and others (23.6%) and medical corporation hospitals (21.8%) [9]. The surveyed facilities closely matched ours, capturing the reality of personnel, workload and QA in Japan. Furthermore, to develop a quality indicator for radiotherapy, Mizuno et al. surveyed DCCHs in Japan [28], with 284 participating facilities and a median annual number of 386 patients. They reported the median FTE numbers for various healthcare professionals at DCCHs in Japan. These included physicians, QA staff (including medical physicists), irradiation/CT simulation staff (including radiation therapy therapists) and nurses, with FTE numbers of 2.0, 1.4, 3.7 and 1.6, respectively. Our findings are consistent with theirs, providing an accurate representation of the current status of DCCHs in Japan. However, the workload of only RTMPs was surveyed in this study, which did not include the workload of radiation oncologists or nurses.

According to the survey results, advanced treatment techniques, such as IMRT, significantly increase the workload. Additionally, the incorporation of additional 4DCT scans for respiratory motion management in radiotherapy has led to an increased workload for CT simulation. Previous survey studies on SBRT for lung cancer in Japan revealed that 4DCT was used to determine the internal target volume in 59.0% of patients without respiratory motion management and in 89.8% of patients with respiratory-gated treatment [30]. These findings highlight the increasing use of 4DCT scans in CT simulations. In addition, our study provides new insights into the time requirements for additional tasks, such as patient-specific shell and vacuum cushion fabrication, contrast-enhanced CT and 4DCT. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive analysis of the CT simulation workload. Compared with other treatment techniques, IMRT contouring and treatment planning tasks took more time, with an average contouring time comparable to that of a previous study [31]. However, our study demonstrated a shorter treatment planning time compared to the previous study [31] (165.6 vs 477 min) due to improvements in computation time within the treatment planning system. A further reduction in the treatment planning time can be attributed to automated treatment planning. Additionally, treatment plan preparation tasks for advanced treatment techniques were found to be significantly more time-consuming than those for 3DCRT. This task involves transferring approved treatment plans to a treatment management system, conducting dry runs for collision checks and configuring the IGRT settings. To identify potential errors in the treatment plan, advanced treatment techniques have been found to increase the cost of plan review. The radiotherapy delivery time was longer for iSRT and SBRT than for 3DCRT and IMRT, indicating that the irradiation time of IMRT no longer differed significantly from 3DCRT and was only dependent on fractional dose. Additionally, the implementation of respiratory motion management during irradiation extended the irradiation time by approximately two times, which is consistent with the IAEA staffing model [2]. Therefore, implementing respiratory motion management, which restricts the treated volume and helps reduce the dose to normal tissues, significantly extends the overall irradiation time. The AAPM Task Group 324 [32] quantified the clinical practice of respiratory motion management. The use of deep inspiration breath hold for left-sided breast cancer was 84%. The report emphasized the importance and widespread use of respiratory motion management in the treatment of breast, thoracic and abdominal cancers. Although our study does not provide the rate of respiratory motion management based on tumor site, the workload for respiratory motion management for each irradiation technique was identified.

In this study, the amount of time spent reviewing the plans in patient-specific QA for IMRT differed from that in a previous study [33]. According to Barber et al., the average time for QA plan preparation, plan review and dose verification was 24, 42 and 96 min, respectively [33]. Consistent with these findings, our average time for QA plan preparation was 23.4 min. However, our average time for plan review was less than their reported values, which ranged from 22.5 to 31.9 min. This disparity could be attributed to the limited implementation of the plan check items specified in AAPM Task Group 275 report [34] in Japan. Additionally, the limited number of RTMPs may make it difficult to allocate sufficient manpower for the plan checks. The time required for dose verification depends on the measuring device. Therefore, our findings suggest that a multidimensional detector can help reduce the time required for dose verification tasks.

Owing to the limited time spent on QA, many radiation oncology departments have been unable to meet the guidelines’ recommendations. The QA of treatment machines and other equipment is essential for the safe delivery of radiotherapy. The AAPM Task Group 198 report [35] provides recommendations regarding the time and staff required for QA items in TG142 [29]. The TG 198 report recommended that the monthly time required for monthly QA was ~6.5 h, and the annual time required for annual QA was 40–60 h. In our study, the total QA time required for monthly and annual QA was ~63 h per year (3.59 h monthly), excluding the daily and weekly QA tasks [35]. Although this estimate aligns with the TG 198 guideline, the QA implementation rate in Japan is ~70%. To fully implement QA items according to TG 142, additional working hours and an efficient QA system are required. Consequently, proper management of radiotherapy equipment necessitates a significant investment in QA time.

The required number of medical physics staff calculated by our staffing model and published staffing models from European and American countries was found to be different. All staffing models, except for Ontario, greatly overestimated the required RTMP-MP staff numbers compared with our model (Table 9). The model estimated the additional workload of medical physicists based on the additional functionality of the treatment machine, but we did not adopt this approach. The FTE factors resulting from the additional functionality of the device will be evaluated in future studies. On the other hand, the Ontario model underestimated the RTMP-MP (medical physicist) when compared to our model because the Ontario model did not include any treatment planning tasks, whereas our model did. The IAEA staffing model for RTMP-RTs was similar to our model as both models employed an activity-based approach. However, the Ontario model employed a greater number of RTMP-RT staff. The Ontario model assumed four FTEs per 10 h per linear accelerator, whereas our model assumed two FTEs per 8 ho per day for irradiation services. Additionally, the Ontario model included treatment planning personnel as part of its staffing considerations. It is worth noting that the number of treatment planning workers varies by country and region.

In Japanese radiation oncology departments, staffing problems varied depending on the scale of the facility. A comparison between our proposed staffing model and actual staffing numbers revealed a shortage of medical physics staff in both large and medium-sized DCCHs. Our model assumes a shift in treatment planning responsibility from physicians to medical physics staff. Given the ongoing transition of treatment planning work in Japan, it is not surprising that current staffing levels are low. To facilitate this task shift in many facilities, it will be necessary to assign RTMPs to perform treatment planning tasks in the future. In contrast, the estimated number of medical physics staff required in the very small department was 0.5 FTEs, as these facilities do not provide advanced radiation treatment technologies. This suggests that employing a full-time medical physicist may be inefficient in such departments. To address this issue, it may be necessary to assign a radiotherapy technologist with QA and treatment planning skills, employ part-time medical physicists, support a specialized medical physics company and utilize telemedicine technology. Additionally, legislation allowing for high-precision irradiation techniques may be required to ensure the viability of small facilities. Radiotherapy facilities in Europe and the United States are typically large, with multiple linear accelerators and a large staff treating over 1000 patients per year. In contrast, in Japan, approximately half of all treatment facilities treat only about 150 patients per year using a single linear accelerator. Establishing these facilities in small rural cities would enhance patient access to radiotherapy. However, ensuring the quality of radiotherapy poses a significant challenge for the limited radiotherapy staff. The unique approach to radiotherapy for very small facilities in Japan is an important issue.

This study had several limitations. First, the responses received in the survey might contain estimates based on respondents’ subjectivity and emotions. However, a more accurate estimation of workload could be achieved by assessing working hours using operational logs from radiation oncology information systems such as ARIA and MOSAIQ [36]. Implementing this approach may result in a lower response rate, owing to the additional effort required. Additionally, this study did not consider the effect of years of radiotherapy experience on the workload. In general, physicists with more experience had shorter work hours, but this reduction plateaued after reaching a certain level of experience. Moreover, as radiotherapy workload is influenced by the status of hardware such as treatment and treatment-planning equipment, the workload may decrease when new equipment is installed. Considering these factors, we regarded the use of an average workload value reasonable. Second, previous studies have reported that the time required for certain tasks is dependent on the treatment site [31, 37]. Additionally, the patient’s performance status can also affect the treatment room occupancy. Unfortunately, our survey did not investigate these factors. Third, our staffing model did not include FTE factors for specialized irradiation techniques such as TBI, TSET, pediatric therapy, brachytherapy and particle therapy. Additionally, online adaptive radiotherapy is already being used in clinical practice. These techniques are very unique, and only a few departments are providing them. In the future, we plan to conduct a survey focused on larger departments that provide such treatments. Furthermore, in recent years, the introduction of software utilizing artificial intelligence technology has resulted in reduced working times. This implies that periodic surveys are required to update the staffing strategy. Fourth, staffing requirements for staff education and training, research and development and clinical trials that focused on clinical work were not considered in our model. However, models proposed by EC2014 [5] and IPEM2017 [7] take these factors into account. The limited number of full-time RTMPs employed in Japan may pose a significant barrier to establishing an in-hospital educational system. One possible solution for this problem is an e-learning-based education system. It would be desirable for radiation oncology-related organizations to collaborate and establish the system. Furthermore, research and development are crucial tasks for improving patient care. We are also considering a survey focused on these points in future work. Finally, this study did not consider staff sick leave or maternity leave. The Ontario report [9] estimates an average of 2 weeks per year per FTE for these leaves, and our model should also consider these factors.

In conclusion, this study conducted a comprehensive survey in Japan to determine the working times required for each job in photon beam therapy. In addition, FTE factors that enable the calculation of the required number of RTMPs at various facilities were established. They apply to both large and small facilities, allowing for accurate estimation of staff requirements while considering variations in job responsibilities across facilities. Furthermore, the flexibility of FTE factors enables easy adaptation to the implementation of new technologies, such as adaptive radiotherapy, in clinical practice. Overall, our findings provide valuable insights for optimizing staffing and resource allocation in radiotherapy departments.
