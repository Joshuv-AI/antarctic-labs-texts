Conscious sedation as a potential optimal anesthetic regimen for endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography in elderly frail patients: A prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chao Li, Na Zeng, Fang Wang, Yiqiang Zhang, Danxu Ma, Haijun Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:To investigate the impact of adverse cardiovascular events (ACVs) in elderly frail patients undergoing emergency endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), and to explore a safe and comfortable anesthetic regimen suitable for this specific population.Materials and Methods:A single-center, prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted. Patients aged 70–90 years scheduled for emergency ERCP were enrolled and randomly divided into two groups: the conscious sedation group (Group S) and the intravenous general anesthesia group (Group A). Basic clinical data of the patients were recorded. During ERCP, the occurrence of ACVs was documented, including hemodynamic fluctuations exceeding 20% of the baseline value, angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, acute heart failure, myocardial ischemia, pulmonary embolism, and various arrhythmias. Additionally, adverse reactions such as hypoxemia, body movement, and cough during the procedure were monitored. The effects of different anesthetic methods on ACVs in elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP were compared, and the satisfaction of patients, anesthesiologists, and endoscopists was evaluated.Results:A total of 108 patients undergoing emergency ERCP were enrolled and randomly assigned to Group S (n = 53) and Group A (n = 55). The results showed that there was a statistically significant difference in the incidence of ACVs between the two groups (17% vs. 40%, P < 0.05). Compared with Group A, Group S had a significantly lower incidence of blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations ≥20% of the baseline value (13.2% vs. 25.5%, P < 0.05), and new-onset arrhythmias (5.7% vs. 18.2%, P < 0.05).Conclusion:Compared with intravenous general anesthesia, conscious sedation can reduce fluctuations in blood pressure and heart rate, and decrease the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events in elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP.

Introduction

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is an invasive digestive endoscopic diagnostic and therapeutic procedure, which is regarded as the gold standard for the treatment of biliary or pancreatic duct diseases.[1] In recent years, the application of therapeutic ERCP in elderly frail patients has increased increasingly, and the choice of anesthesia method directly affects the safety and effectiveness of the surgery. Currently, three anesthesia regimens are mainly used in clinical practice: conscious sedation, non-intubated general anesthesia, and intubated general anesthesia.[2] Studies have shown that although conscious sedation is simple to operate, it is prone to sedation-related adverse events (such as hypoxemia and airway intervention) in elderly frail patients. Moreover, due to the age-related decline in multiple organ functions and poor drug tolerance, these patients have a higher risk of respiratory and circulatory complications.[3] Meanwhile, under sedation, patients may experience discomfort or poor cooperation, which affects the surgical operation and leads to a decrease in the success rate of ERCP intubation and surgical completion rate. However, the general anesthesia regimen is also controversial. Some studies have pointed out that it may increase the risk of perioperative infections such as pneumonia, affect circulatory stability, cause delayed awakening, and aggravate the frailty burden of patients, making the choice of anesthesia a dilemma.[2]

In clinical practice, anesthesia decision-making is also constrained by practical conditions. In some regions with limited medical resources, general anesthesia is difficult to popularize due to the shortage of anesthesiologists and insufficient equipment, and conscious sedation has become a more common choice because of its simplicity of operation.[4] In addition, elderly frail patients are often complicated with multiple underlying diseases. Especially during emergency ERCP, the condition is critical and individual differences are significant. Anesthesiologists need to comprehensively evaluate the patient’s vital signs, underlying conditions, surgical urgency, and medical conditions to formulate an individualized anesthesia plan. At present, there is still a lack of special studies on anesthesia for ERCP in elderly frail patients. Most of the existing evidence is extended from that of general elderly patients, and there is a lack of large-sample and high-quality studies targeting the “frail” state. The safety and effectiveness of various anesthesia methods have not been concluded.[5] Therefore, it is urgent to carry out more in-depth studies to provide evidence for optimizing the anesthesia strategy for this group of patients.

Materials and Methods

Patient selection

This study protocol was approved by the Hospital Ethics Committee (Ethics Approval No.: BFHHZS20230208; Chairman: Zhenchang Wang) and registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration No.: NCT06192082; URL: https://register.clinicaltrials.gov). The study population included patients scheduled for emergency ERCP with a FRAIL (Frail, Resistance, Ambulation, Illness, Loss of weight) scale score ≥3. Before the procedure, the process, risks, and study details of the two anesthesia regimens were fully explained to eligible patients and their families. The principle of voluntariness and the right to withdraw at any time were emphasized, and written informed consent was obtained.

Study protocol

Preoperative evaluation

The patients’ basic information, comorbidities, ASA physical status classification, history of difficult airway, and frailty score were recorded. A history of drug allergy was excluded. All patients were ensured to have fasted for 12 hours and abstained from drinking for 8 hours before surgery. The FRAIL scale[6] assessment was performed preoperatively, which included five evaluation indicators: Fatigue, Resistance (ability to climb stairs), Ambulation (ability to walk a certain distance), Illness, and Loss of weight. A score of 0 indicated a non-frail state, 1–2 points indicated pre-frailty, and three points or more indicated frailty.

Grouping and randomization

Patients were divided into the conscious sedation group and the intravenous general anesthesia group using a stratified randomization method. The random sequence was generated by Excel and sealed in envelopes. Anesthesiologists assigned groups according to the envelopes and recorded the data. Postoperatively, follow-up was conducted by physicians, who were unaware of the grouping (blinded assessors). The anesthesia regimen could be modified if necessary to ensure safety, and the reasons for modification were recorded.

Study procedure

After entering the operating room, patients established venous access, vital signs were monitored, and preoxygenation with 100% oxygen via a face mask was performed until the end-tidal oxygen concentration reached 88%–90%.

Conscious Sedation Group: Sufentanil 0.05–0.1 μg/kg was administered intravenously 5 min before the procedure, and midazolam 1–2 mg was administered intravenously 2 min before the procedure. The procedure was performed, while maintaining a modified observer’s assessment of alertness/sedation (MOAA/S) score of 2–3. If body movement or cough occurred, midazolam 1–2 mg was supplemented; if hypoxemia or respiratory depression occurred, verbal instructions, jaw thrust, or mask ventilation was given; for bradycardia, atropine 0.5 mg was administered; for hypotension, ephedrine 6 mg was administered as a single intravenous injection.Intravenous General Anesthesia Group: After intravenous administration of sufentanil 0.05–0.1 μg/kg, propofol 1 mg/kg was administered for induction, and an additional 10 mg was supplemented every 30 sec if necessary. If body movement occurred, propofol 10–20 mg was supplemented; if respiratory depression occurred, jaw thrust, mask ventilation, or endotracheal intubation was performed; circulatory management was the same as that in the conscious sedation group.

Vital signs were recorded every 5 min in both groups. Satisfaction was evaluated postoperatively, and telephone follow-up was conducted within 3 days to assess adverse events (such as dizziness, headache, palpitations, dyspnea, nausea, and vomiting).

Assessment of sedation depth

The MOAA/S scale (0–5 points) was used for assessment every 2 min. The conscious sedation group was maintained at 2–3 points (moderate sedation), and the intravenous general anesthesia group was maintained at 0–1 point (deep sedation).

Satisfaction evaluation

The verbal rating scale (VRS, 1–10 points) was used to assess the satisfaction of patients, anesthesiologists, and endoscopists, which was classified into three grades: poor (1–4 points), moderate (5–7 points), and good (8–10 points).

Postoperative pain assessment

The VAS, 0–10 points was used, with higher scores indicating more severe pain.

Sample size calculation

Taking the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events as the primary outcome indicator, based on the pilot study (14% in the conscious sedation group and 38% in the intravenous general anesthesia group), with α = 0.05, β = 0.2, a 1:1 ratio, and a 10% dropout rate, it was estimated that 54 cases were needed in each group, totaling 108 cases.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and comparisons between groups were performed using t-test or rank-sum test; categorical data were expressed as rates and analyzed using the χ2 test; ordinal data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Walli’s rank-sum test. SPSS 23.0 software was used for statistical analysis, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient enrollment and exclusion process

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 485 patients undergoing emergency ERCP were screened in this study [Figure 1]. Based on the inclusion criteria, 133 patients were finally enrolled, including 61, 49, and 23 patients with frailty scores of 3, 4, and 5, respectively. Twenty-five patients were excluded during the operation, among that 22 were due to the intraoperative need for N-butylscopolamine bromide (a drug that can significantly affect heart rate), and 3 were due to changes in the anesthesia regimen caused by strong intraoperative intervention by endoscopists. A total of 108 patients finally completed the study and were followed up for data analysis, including 53 cases in Group S and 55 cases in Group A.

Comparison of adverse events and adverse cardiovascular events during endoscopic treatment

Adverse events occurring during endoscopic diagnosis and treatment in both groups are shown in Table 4. Discussion

This study compared the effects of intravenous general anesthesia and conscious sedation on adverse cardiovascular events in elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP. The results showed that compared with intravenous general anesthesia, conscious sedation significantly reduced the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events, and no serious adverse events occurred within 3 days after surgery. In this part of the trial, we collected cases in accordance with the principles of the “Expert Consensus on Anesthesia Management for Common Digestive Endoscopy Procedures,”[7] aiming to include procedures in the consensus that do not restrict the anesthesia method into the trial. However, since all patients need to undergo anesthesia evaluation before digestive endoscopy treatment, it is difficult for patients with poor physical condition to meet the criteria for intravenous general anesthesia. Therefore, the finally selected subjects were all patients undergoing emergency ERCP.

The clinical application of ERCP has been increasingly expanded, covering the treatment of biliary and pancreatic diseases from simple to complex, and the incidence of related complications is low, thus gaining widespread recognition.[8] The American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) pointed out that sedation managed by anesthesiologists has a positive impact on the success of ERCP.[9] During ERCP, there are mainly two commonly used anesthesia methods[10]: intravenous general anesthesia and conscious sedation. Intravenous general anesthesia induces an anesthetic state through drug intervention to alleviate or eliminate pain and discomfort that may occur during treatment or examination. Conscious sedation uses sedative and analgesic drugs to put patients into a relaxed state while maintaining consciousness and spontaneous breathing.[11] Generally, both anesthesia methods can effectively ensure the smooth progress of ERCP. A clinical study in such patients found that the incidence of adverse events was only 9.9% in patients with conscious sedation, compared with 51.5% in patients with intravenous general anesthesia.[12] However, another clinical study showed that 10% of patients with conscious sedation required conversion to intravenous general anesthesia during ERCP.[13] A 12-month prospective study involving 876 patients further confirmed that when the sedation method is pre-selected based on patient risk factors and planned therapeutic interventions, moderate conscious sedation is not only reliably safe and feasible in ERCP patients—its technical success rate (95%) and intubation success rate (97.5%) are not significantly different from those of the general anesthesia (GA) group (96% and 97.8%, respectively)—but also has the advantages of shorter operation time, higher resource utilization rate and significantly lower cost, with an adverse event rate (6.6%) even lower than that of the GA group (9.2%). It can be seen that on the premise of appropriate screening combined with individual patient conditions and surgical plans, conscious sedation can be used as an efficient and economical sedation method for ERCP examinations.[14]

The anesthetic drugs used in this study included midazolam, propofol, and sufentanil. Propofol has become the drug of choice for deep sedation in ERCP due to its short half-life, rapid induction of anesthesia, and quick recovery.[15] Midazolam is the most commonly used sedative drug for conscious sedation in ERCP and digestive endoscopy. Sufentanil is currently the most potent opioid analgesic with good hemodynamic stability.[16] The results of this study showed that there was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between intravenous general anesthesia and conscious sedation in elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP, but the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events was relatively higher under intravenous general anesthesia. Combining the results of previous studies and this study, we believe that both intravenous general anesthesia and conscious sedation have their own advantages and disadvantages for patients undergoing emergency ERCP, and anesthesiologists need to select an appropriate anesthesia or sedation plan according to the specific conditions of patients and the requirements of endoscopists.

It is worth noting that a study on sedation performed by anesthesiologists and gastroenterologists showed 16 that compared with sedation performed by endoscopists, the incidence of adverse events was lower when sedation was performed by anesthesiologists.[17] Therefore, in painless digestive endoscopy, the rational use of sedation and anesthesia technology by professional anesthetic personnel is the key to reducing sedation and anesthesia-related adverse events. The results of this study showed that there was no significant difference in satisfaction between the two groups of patients. Cough and pain during gastroscope insertion are the main factors affecting patient satisfaction. Although patients in the conscious sedation group were awake during endoscopy, there was no significant difference in the incidence of cough between the two groups, which may be the main reason for the no significant difference in patient satisfaction between the two groups. The occurrence of adverse events during endoscopy and the need to interrupt gastroscopy due to airway management during endoscopy are the main factors affecting endoscopist satisfaction.[12] In this study, there was no significant difference in the occurrence of these events between the two groups. Moreover, for the elderly frail patients included in this study, painless ERCP could not be performed originally because they could not pass the anesthesia evaluation, but the participation of anesthesiologists enabled gastroenterologists to successfully complete ERCP without serious adverse events. These may be the main reasons for the no significant difference in endoscopist satisfaction between the two groups.

Most anesthetic drugs can reduce sympathetic tone, thereby increasing venous compliance, causing vasodilation and hypotension, while reducing venous return and cardiac output. Therefore, ensuring satisfactory organ blood flow and stable perfusion pressure during anesthesia is crucial, especially in patients with cardiovascular insufficiency. Studies have shown that adverse outcomes after non-cardiac surgery are closely related to intraoperative hypotension.[1819] Existing evidence indicates that when MAP <80 mmHg persists for at least 10 min, it can lead to organ damage, such as myocardial injury, stroke, and acute kidney injury.[20] In addition, the risk increases with the gradual decrease of blood pressure. However, most previous studies adopted a retrospective observational design, resulting in significant heterogeneity in patient characteristics. At the same time, there are obvious differences in the definition of intraoperative hypotension among different studies. A recent study conducted an in-depth analysis of intraoperative hypotension,[19] the results showed that there were 48 different definitions of intraoperative hypotension, and 41% of patients had SBP lower than 80 mmHg during surgery, 93% of patients experienced at least one episode of SBP reduction greater than 20% of the baseline value, and 54% of patients had at least one episode of SBP reduction greater than 30% of the baseline value. These data not only provide important references for clinical practice but also help to further improve perioperative safety. Although there is no unified standard for defining intraoperative hypotension so far, a recent expert consensus pointed out that if the mean arterial pressure is lower than 65 mmHg during surgery, the risk of adverse cerebrovascular or cardiac events within 30 days after surgery increases by 1.52 times.[21] The occurrence of these complications is closely related to the severity and duration of hypotension. Intraoperative tachycardia can adversely affect the balance of myocardial oxygen supply and demand, thereby increasing the risk of perioperative myocardial injury. A retrospective study including 41,140 patients showed that a heart rate >90 beats/min was associated with an increased risk of myocardial injury.[22] Similar results were also found in the Vascular Events in Non-cardiac Surgery Patients Cohort Evaluation (VISION) trial. Bijker and Gelb[23] conducted a retrospective analysis of 48,241 patients undergoing non-cardiac and non-neurosurgical surgery and found that intraoperative blood pressure reduction exceeding 20% of the baseline value was directly related to the occurrence of postoperative stroke. This study mainly detected the occurrence of the following adverse cardiovascular events: (1) Abnormal heart rate events (diagnostic criteria: heart rate fluctuation >20% of the baseline value); (2) Abnormal blood pressure fluctuation events (diagnostic criteria: blood pressure fluctuation >20% of the baseline value); and (3) Angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, cardiac arrest, acute heart failure, myocardial ischemia, pulmonary embolism, and various arrhythmias. The study used ±20% of the baseline intraoperative blood pressure and heart rate as intervention thresholds. The results showed that the incidences of abnormal HR events, abnormal blood pressure fluctuation events, and new-onset arrhythmias in patients with conscious sedation were significantly lower than those in patients with intravenous general anesthesia, suggesting that conscious sedation is more suitable for elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP. Frailty is closely linked to elderly cardiovascular diseases, and the two often coexist. From a pathophysiological perspective, they share common characteristics and have a bidirectional relationship. Anesthetics have an inhibitory effect on the cardiovascular system, inhibiting sympathetic activity to a certain extent, slowing heart rate, and reducing cardiac output; blood vessels dilate to varying degrees, further causing hypotension. In this trial, the conscious sedation group used sufentanil/midazolam, while the intravenous general anesthesia group used sufentanil/propofol. Propofol can cause significant cardiovascular depression,[24] which is the main reason for the higher incidence of perioperative adverse cardiovascular events in the intravenous general anesthesia group observed in our study.

Hypoxemia is the most common cardiopulmonary-related adverse event during ERCP,[2526] with an incidence of approximately 16.2%–39.2%. During ERCP, the prone position is adopted, and the chest and abdomen are compressed by the operating table, leading to decreased thoracic and pulmonary compliance, and difficulty in respiratory management. Anesthetic drugs can cause respiratory depression and airway obstruction in patients, all of which are the main factors for hypoxemia in patients.[27] In most cases, moderate intraoperative hypoxemia during painless digestive endoscopy can be successfully treated through airway intervention (such as jaw thrust, increased oxygen flow, or both), without the need to terminate ERCP. If hypoxemia cannot be relieved, measures such as nasopharyngeal/oropharyngeal airway or endotracheal intubation can be considered.

Cummings et al.[28] retrospectively analyzed perioperative outcome indicators of more than 61,000 ERCP patients from 42 medical centers. The anesthesia methods included endotracheal intubation general anesthesia or intravenous general anesthesia, and the incidences of intraoperative hypoxemia and hypotension were observed. The results showed that the incidence of hypotension was higher in the endotracheal intubation general anesthesia group, and the incidence of hypoxemia was higher in the intravenous general anesthesia group. Neither anesthesia method was more effective than the other in terms of the combined incidence of hypoxemia and hypotension. If patients with conscious sedation were included in this large-sample, multi-center retrospective study, more statistically significant outcome indicators might be obtained. In this study, the incidence of hypoxemia in the intravenous anesthesia group was 27.3%, which was significantly higher than that in the conscious sedation group (11.3%). The main reason is that patients in the conscious sedation group can be awakened, retain spontaneous breathing, and the risk of respiratory depression is significantly reduced. However, due to the shallow anesthetic depth, patients were not in a sleep state, so the incidences of body movement (92.5% vs. 7.5%, P < 0.05) and cough (35.8% vs. 23.6%, P < 0.05) in this group were significantly higher than those in the intravenous general anesthesia group.

This study shows that conscious sedation has significant application value in ERCP, especially in clinical scenarios where a balance between safety, tolerability, and accessibility of medical resources is required. Studies have shown that the conscious sedation regimen of remimazolam combined with alfentanil can significantly reduce the incidence of hypoxia (9.6% vs. 15.7%), reduce the need for airway intervention, and have a lower risk of hemodynamic fluctuations such as hypotension (17.6% vs. 35.2%) compared with traditional sedative drugs, providing more reliable guarantee for the stability of patients’ respiratory and circulatory systems; this regimen does not require endotracheal intubation, can avoid the risk of infections such as general anesthesia-related postoperative pneumonia, and has no common propofol injection pain, resulting in higher patient satisfaction and greater willingness to choose again.[29] In addition, conscious sedation is relatively simple to operate, has lower requirements for anesthesiologists’ professionalism and equipment configuration, and can adapt to the conditions of medical institutions at different levels, especially ensuring the smooth development of ERCP in resource-limited scenarios. For patients with stable condition and no serious underlying diseases, this regimen can not only maintain an appropriate depth of sedation through precise drug administration to ensure surgical cooperation but also reduce the systemic stress and delayed recovery caused by general anesthesia, achieving dual optimization of efficacy and safety.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and the incidence of relevant adverse events is low. Therefore, the results of this study may be subject to type II statistical errors, especially for secondary endpoints. Secondly, each group included elderly patients with varying degrees of frailty. Although there was no significant difference in the proportion of patients with different frailty degrees between groups, the small sample size prevented us from determining the relationship between frailty degree and the risk of adverse cardiovascular events. Thirdly, in accordance with the requirements of existing guidelines, this study only included elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP; thus, the results may not be applicable to elderly frail patients undergoing elective ERCP. Fourthly, this study only observed adverse cardiovascular events occurring during ERCP or within 3 days after surgery, and no long-term follow-up was conducted to verify the relationship between sedation depth and long-term outcomes. To address the above issues, further large-sample, multi-center studies are still needed to validate the results of this study.

Conclusion

In elderly frail patients undergoing emergency ERCP, conscious sedation can significantly reduce the incidence of adverse cardiovascular events compared with intravenous general anesthesia, and no serious adverse events occurred within 3 days after surgery. Therefore, in clinical practice, full attention should be paid to the overall condition of patients, especially elderly frail patients. Conscious sedation may be a preferable anesthetic option for painless emergency ERCP.
