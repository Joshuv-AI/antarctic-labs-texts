Active control of single-photon spontaneous emission out-coupling enhancement by rotation modulation of hyperbolic metamaterials
Authors: Yubin Ma, Muhammad Imran, Muhideen Yahya Musa, Shafi Ullah Khan, Rujiang Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryControlling the rate and direction of spontaneous emission is critical for quantum technologies such as single-photon sources and secure communications. We propose a tunable nanophotonic device that modulates spontaneous emission by rotating the optical axis of a hyperbolic metamaterial (Au-SiO2) at room temperature. Analytical and numerical studies demonstrate that rotation alters the local density of states, enabling dynamic emission rate control without structural changes. The device’s performance depends on the metal filling fraction, rotation angle, and wavelength, allowing spectral flexibility. As a proof of concept, we design a system that efficiently out-couples emission into a nano-waveguide. This approach provides a simple, geometry-independent method to switch emission on/off, advancing practical quantum photonic devices. The work opens avenues for applications in quantum optics, sensing, and on-chip quantum networks.Subject areas: engineering, materials science, physics

Introduction

In quantum computing and communication, modulation of spontaneous emission (SE) and subsequent coupling to the nanostructures remain important stages of achieving efficient quantum devices.1,2,3,4,5,6,7 Going straight into the electrodynamics of the system, the local density of electromagnetic states of the environment is the degree of freedom for achieving SE of the quantum emitter placed in the photonic environment.8 The photonic hotspot—a photonic environment where the density of local electromagnetic states approaches infinity—can be realized in various photonic structures such as photonic crystals, guided modes, cavity mode systems, plasmonic structures, and hyperbolic metamaterials (HMMs).9,10,11,12,13,14,15,16 The HMMs are the latest among the list of photonic hotspots, which produce important exotic phenomena due to infinitely large density of states and intrinsically high-k eigenmodes.17,18,19,20,21,22 In the recent past, a tilted HMM was employed to demonstrate the possibility of achieving a large enhancement of the Purcell factor of a quantum emitter placed in a tilted HMM’s hotspot as well as the matching of the high-k modes to the guided modes of the nano-waveguide that efficiently coupled to the waveguide without any reflection in a visible spectral range.23 However, realizing effective quantum on-chip devices requires not only an enhanced Purcell factor and efficient out-coupling to nano-waveguides, but also active modulation of the SE. Active modulation of SE is the ability to control the vacuum fluctuations of the photonic hotspot to accelerate or slow down the decay of the emitted photons.23,24 The potential applications are in the energy harvesting, illumination, optical communication, and quantum information processes.25,26,27,28,29,30 The active modulation is reported to be achieved by using nano-cavities, photonic crystals, liquid crystals in plasmonic waveguides, overlapping lasers, and many more.25,31,32,33,34,35,36,37,38,39 A number of studies have demonstrated that such nanophotonic structures offer the ability to control magnetic dipole emission, enable mechanical modulation of SE, and realize high-contrast switching of photon emission, further broadening the range of capabilities for active quantum photonic devices.40,41,42,43 Despite the efforts achieved from the processes above, the challenges posed can be the rigidity of the structures which makes it difficult to integrate with the on-chip device, the realistic condition, the complexity of the structure, and the ability to integrate the control mechanism in the structures. Unlike these approaches that require structural modification or external optical control, this work introduces mechanical rotation of the HMM’s optical axis as a new degree of freedom for active modulation of SE.

This work demonstrates a method for actively modulating both the SE rate and single-photon out-coupling efficiency through mechanical rotation of the optical axis of the HMM hyper-crystal. We analytically show that the effective permittivity of the proposed HMM is strongly dependent on the rotation angle. This dependence enables active control over the local density of states (LDOS) in the photonic hotspot, which directly influences the emission rate of the quantum emitter. Furthermore, by aligning the iso-frequency contours of the HMM with the waveguide modes through rotation, we achieve an enhanced phase matching, leading to improved out-coupling efficiency into the nano-waveguide. We show that both the SE rate and out-coupling efficiency can be independently tuned by adjusting either the rotation angle or the metallic filling fraction (Fm). To validate the concept, we designed and simulated a proof-of-concept device that integrates the HMM with a Si3N4 nano-waveguide. Our results highlight the potential of the proposed approach for tunable single-photon sources, reconfigurable nanolasers, or on-chip quantum information processors, where dynamic control over both emission and routing of photons is essential.

Results

Materials, design, and methods

When the HMM is rotated, the orientation of its optical axis changes relative to the quantum emitter, as shown in Figure 1A. This rotation affects the LDOS in two distinct ways. First, it modifies the SE rate of the quantum emitter by altering the effective refractive index of the photonic hotspot through changes in the interaction with the HMM’s anisotropic modes. Specifically, at a certain orientation known as the critical angle, the real part of the effective permittivity diverges, marking a transition between metal-like and dielectric-like behavior of the HMM. This leads to a significant modulation of the LDOS, resulting in either enhancement or suppression of the SE rate. These changes in LDOS directly influence the SE rate of the quantum emitter, as demonstrated in Figure 1B, where rotation in the optical axis of the HMM within certain angular ranges can either enhance or inhibit SE by modifying vacuum fluctuations in the photonic hotspot. Rotating the optical axis of the HMM dynamically modulates the collected emissions of the SE rate of the quantum emitter, ranging from approximately 0.5 × 106 to 2.5 × 106 photons/s. These variations arise from the changing LDOS in the photonic hotspot, as illustrated in Figure 1C. The LDOS reaches maximum values near the critical angle, where the effective permittivity diverges. Second, the rotation also influences the efficiency with which emitted photons are out-coupled into the nano-waveguide. By aligning the iso-frequency contours of the HMM with those of the waveguide modes, better phase matching is achieved, thereby improving the out-coupling efficiency.

As compared to existing approaches for active SE modulation such as electro-optic modulation,44 all-optical control,31,32,33 or strain tuning41—the proposed mechanical rotation of the HMM’s optical axis offers several distinct advantages. Unlike electro-optic methods, which often require complex integration of transparent conductive oxides or liquid crystals and suffer from limited tunability at room temperature, our approach does not involve external electrical components or material refractive index changes. Similarly, all-optical methods typically depend on pump lasers to induce transient changes in photonic structures, which can lead to heating effects and lack long-term stability. Strain-based modulation, although promising, requires precise nanoscale deformation mechanisms that are difficult to integrate into compact photonic chips. In contrast, our method enables direct, reversible, and mechanically simple modulation of both LDOS and out-coupling efficiency by simply rotating the optical axis of the HMM.

The HMM is composed of gold (Au) and silicon dioxide (SiO2) with a thickness of 33% and 66% (i.e., Fm = 0.3), respectively. At all conditions, the thickness of the unit cell must be less than the wavelength of the incident electromagnetic waves for it to be feasible for adoption as an effective medium parameter in describing the electrodynamics of the system.45,46,47 The dielectric constants of Au and SiO2 are taken from experimental data.46,48 The use of Au in this work is justified by its relatively high stability and low sensitivity of its optical properties to temperature variations at room temperature.49 While plasmonic materials like Au can experience minor changes in permittivity with temperature due to interband transitions and thermal expansion, studies have shown that these effects are minimal under ambient conditions and do not significantly affect the performance of plasmonic or metamaterial structures operating at near room temperature.46,49 Similarly, SiO2 exhibits excellent thermal stability over a wide temperature range, with negligible changes in refractive index within the operational bandwidth.45 These characteristics support the feasibility of our device for a stable operation at room temperature.

The quantum emitter is simulated with a classical electric dipole source with size-dependent parameters, efficiently mimicking a two-level system. The unit cell size (i.e., the combined thickness of one Au and one SiO2 layer) is approximately 60 nm, while the operating wavelength is in the range of 634–692 nm. This means the unit cell size is significantly smaller than the wavelength, satisfying the subwavelength criterion required for effective medium theory (EMT). The substrate is made from SiO2 which allows uninterrupted interaction with the device.45 The system is simulated at the wavelength of λ = 634 nm, 660 nm and 692 nm within the low loss optical frequency regime for the application in pulse lasers. In the HMM in Figure 1A, the LDOS is modified by spatially varying the effective refractive index of the photonic hotspot. There are many approaches to achieve this varying the refractive index of the photonic hotspots, depending on the number of parameters. For the HMM, the rotation angle and filling fraction provide the opportunity for varying the refractive index as well as the local density of the states. The Fm determines the effective permittivity of the HMM, which directly influences the LDOS and out-coupling efficiency. The rotation angle (θ) modifies the alignment of the HMM’s iso-frequency contours with those of the waveguide, enabling phase matching and efficient mode coupling. This modulation of the LDOS provides an avenue to control the SE and total decay rate of any quantum emitter placed within the hotspot.

Active control of SE

To fully understand the effect of rotation angle in modulating the effective index of the HMM, the variation of the SE and out-coupling emission enhancements with the filling fraction is plotted for different rotation angles. Figure 2 shows the variations of the SE and normalized out-coupling enhancements with the filling fractions at various wavelengths. This gives the experimentalists the chance to select optimized spectra for a specific wavelength of the system. Figure 2A depicts the variation of normalized SE and out-coupling emission enhancements with the filling fraction for the wavelength of λ = 634 nm for different rotation angles. Angular dependence is observed due to the suppression and enhancement of SE and out-coupling emission enhancements for different rotation angles. It started from the enhanced attenuation at θ = 45° (red curve), strongly increased at θ = 50° (blue curve), further increased at θ = 55° (green curve), then decreased at θ = 60° (yellow curve), decreased again at θ = 65° (maroon curve), and almost attenuated at θ = 70° (cyan curve). This anomalous behavior is caused by the modulation of the LDOS due to variation in the refractive index of the hotspot. The significance can be observed within the angular value of 40° < θ < 60°. There is a small red-shifting of the filling fraction value, and this is related to the dependence of the effective index with the filling fraction. Similar behavior is observed for λ = 660 nm in Figure 2B, where a slight enhancement is observed for θ = 45° followed by a strong enhancement at θ = 50° and 55°, then a sudden decrease at θ = 60°, and complete inhibition at θ = 70°. There is a slight variation of the peaks at λ = 692 nm (Figure 2C) where the enhancement at θ = 45° is quite strong compared with the previous wavelengths. This is due to the broad-angle nature of the peaks at different wavelengths. To analyze the out-coupling emission enhancement for each modulation parameter Figures 2A–2C exhibit that for a fixed rotation angle, increasing Fm leads to a transition from dielectric-like to hyperbolic behavior, enhancing the LDOS and out-coupling efficiency. However, beyond a critical filling fraction, losses due to increased metal content begin to dominate, leading to a decrease in out-coupling efficiency. This trend is consistent across all wavelengths, indicating that the filling fraction provides a tunable parameter for optimizing out-coupling without structural reconfiguration.

Next, for the on-chip integrated quantum devices, the modulation of the out-coupled photons to the nano-waveguide will add significance to the quantum information applications. As shown in Figures 3A, a dipole emitter is placed in the vicinity of the engineered HMM. Our proposed HMM is integrated with a properly designed waveguide to improve the extraction decay rate at different filling fractions over a broad angle range due to its unique design. As a conceptual demonstration, the Fm is taken as 33% in the proposed structure. The integral of power flux over a dipole emitter with a radius of 10 nm is used to numerically compute the total emitted power of the quantum emitter. Similarly, the integral of power flow across the waveguide cross-section is used to numerically calculate the power extracted into the waveguide. Further, the stoichiometric silicon nitride (Si3N4) is a leading integrated photonics material platform that has emerged as a potential platform for quantum photonics44,51,52,53,54 and offers low propagation and insertion losses. Si3N4-based quantum photonic integrated circuit elements have been experimentally achieved in recent years. These elements include beam splitters, phase shifters, and on-chip frequency converters, which are all coupled into a completely reconfigurable quantum photonic processor.44

Figures 3B and 3C demonstrate the evolution of iso-frequency contour plots of the HMM (open hyperbolic shape in red curves), and the nano-waveguide (circular shape in blue curves) is plotted for different rotation angles, highlighting how phase matching improves as θ approaches the critical angle. At the critical angle, the momentum of the HMM aligns closely with the waveguide modes, leading to enhanced out-coupling efficiency. Beyond this angle, the mismatch increases, reducing the coupling efficiency. In Figure 3B is the unrotated HMM for θ = 0°; the out-coupled region in the contour plot is so narrow that it cannot be phase-matched with the momentum of the nano-waveguide. Rotating the structure to the critical value of θ = 50° as in Figure 3C, the momentum of the HMM and the nano-waveguide is coupled in a wider regime due to low values of propagation momentum kx that renders the index of the HMM less than that of nano-waveguide which improves the phase matching between the HMM and waveguide modes, thereby enhancing the out-coupling efficiency without necessarily increasing the SE rate itself. For the larger values of rotating angle θ < 60°, the out-coupling to the nano-waveguide is significant enough for modulating the SE (see Figure S2 for more details). It is noteworthy that different Fm shows different behavior of iso-frequency contour. For example, the iso-frequency contour is closer when the Fm is small and more open for the Fm.

Discussion

We have designed and proposed a simple means of achieving an efficient modulation of the SE of the quantum emitter by rotation modulation of the HMMs. The underlying mechanism of rotation modulation is demonstrated by employing an angle-dependent effective medium theory approach. The effect of tuning the wavelength of the structure is demonstrated by either changing the filling fraction of the HMM composites or rotation angle. To demonstrate a proof-of-concept approach to the mechanism, out-coupling the SE with the rectangular waveguide is studied. This shows the capability of the system for application in the integration of the on-chip device.

Limitations of the study

Although the study uses experimentally derived material properties for Au and SiO2, to enhance its realism, practical limitations such as fabrication tolerances and interface roughness may degrade performance. Optical losses in the Au-SiO2 layers increase at larger rotation angles, reducing out-coupling efficiency due to absorption. Furthermore, while the method enables mechanical tunability, it may not be suitable for high-speed applications. In such cases, alternative approaches like electro-optic or all-optical control could offer faster modulation, despite greater integration complexity or higher energy demands.

Rotation angle θ and critical angle θc

The hyperbolic modes studied are the transverse magnetic (TM, or p-polarized) in the proposed metamaterial. The rotation angle θ is the value that determines the changing of the effective property with respect to the angular movement of the material. The optical axis is the reference point to the rotation angle θ. The effect of the rotation to the iso-frequency contour plots of the waveguide and rotated-HMM can be seen as in Figure S2A for 0°, S2(b) for θ = 50°, and A2(c) for θ = 90°.These effects can reflect the full out-coupling capability of the rotation modulation of HMM.

In Figure S4C for the higher metallic filling fraction 0.5, the maximum normalized outcoupling emission is achieved for waveguide w = 2 μm (green curve). However, as compared to Figure S4B, the total emission enhancement is reduced in Figure S4C due to higher metallic losses. Also, the maximum emissions angle change from θ ≈ 50°to θ ≈ 45°. The x-dipole orientations in Figures S4A–S4C represented by red (dashed), blue (dashed), green (dashed) shows comparatively low outcoupling emissions while maximum emission is achieved in the range of θ ≈ 70° to θ ≈ 90° depending on the metallic filling fraction and size of the nano-fiber width. In conclusion, for z-dipoles, maximum out-coupling occurs at specific angles aligned with the critical angle, where phase matching with the waveguide is optimal while for x-dipoles, the coupling is less sensitive to angle variations, but overall enhancement is reduced due to weaker interaction with the high-k modes of the HMM.
