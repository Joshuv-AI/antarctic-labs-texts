Investigating of tomato response to different concentrations of potassium in the nutrient solution and foliar application of calcium and silicon
Authors: Hamid Reza Roosta, Seyed Zia Hashemi, Mahdi Moradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the effects of varying potassium sulfate concentrations in the nutrient solution, as well as foliar applications of calcium nitrate and potassium silicate, on growth, physiological traits, nutrient status, and fruit quality of tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill. Var. Isabella) grown in a greenhouse hydroponic system. A factorial experiment was designed with three potassium sulfate levels (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mM) and five foliar spray treatments (control, two levels each of calcium nitrate and potassium silicate). The results showed that combined application of 1.5 mM potassium sulfate with foliar calcium nitrate (0.25 g L−1) significantly enhanced plant height, leaf area, shoot and root biomass, as well as fruit yield, diameter, and vitamin C content, compared to other treatments. Application of potassium sulfate at 1.5 mM combined with foliar spraying of calcium nitrate at 0.25 g/L significantly enhanced overall plant growth. The fresh and dry weights of shoots increased by 4% and 12%, respectively, compared to the control. Additionally, the number of fruits per plant was significantly higher, resulting in a 35% increase in total fruit number per plant with this combined treatment. Calcium nitrate foliar sprays were more effective than potassium silicate in promoting most growth and physiological traits. Potassium treatments increased photosynthetic pigment concentration and nutrient uptake, while both calcium and silicon sprays improved plant vigor and fruit quality. The observed synergistic benefits are attributed to potassium role in stomatal regulation and assimilate transport, calcium contribution to cell integrity and photosynthesis, and silicon enhancement of water-use efficiency and leaf structure. Overall, the study demonstrates that managing the ratio and delivery of potassium, calcium, and silicon, through both nutrient solutions and foliar application, can significantly improve growth and nutrition of hydroponically cultivated tomatoes. These practical findings provide valuable guidance for optimizing tomato productivity and quality in soilless greenhouse agriculture.

Introduction

The plant nutrition plays an important role in crop yield. In recent years, soilless cultivation has gained considerable attention, primarily due to challenges such as soil-borne pests and diseases, soil fatigue and degradation, the accumulation of toxic elements1, and the economic advantages of more efficient water and space utilization2. To ensure adequate mineral supply for crops, it is essential to maintain optimal growth conditions through proper environmental management and the application of well-balanced nutrient solutions3. Consequently, plant nutrition is a critical factor influencing growth and yield in soilless culture systems.

Tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill) belongs to the Solanaceae family and is native to South America4. Environmental conditions such as light, temperature, and plant nutrition with nutrients are among the most important factors for success in greenhouse cultivation. In soilless cultivation methods, all nutrients must be provided to the plant in the form of solution salts5. The balance of nutrients for optimal plant growth is necessary. At the maximum growth stage, a large amount of nutrients is absorbed, and the plant will have high nutritional needs during this period. The nutrition of agricultural products such as tomatoes with mineral substances should lead to improving the quality of the fruit required by consumers and increasing post-harvest quality, rather than just focusing on increasing production. The role of minerals in other characteristics of tomatoes, such as vitamin C, should also be considered. Among these, elements such as potassium, calcium, and silicon are important. Potassium is the second most abundant element required by plants after nitrogen and plays numerous roles in plant physiology6. Through the alteration of proteins or amino acids and the reduction of cell permeability, potassium increases plant resistance to diseases7. Potassium plays a significant role in promoting growth, reducing the adverse effects of stress, increasing yield, and improving the quality of the product, including its shelf life, marketability, and economic value8. The study on tomato plants showed that, increased K fertilization significantly improved quality of fruit where vitamin C were positively correlated to K concentration of leaf, indicating that K may have a role to improve fruit quality9.

Calcium is also an important mineral in tomato nutrition. Calcium is involved in cell division, the natural function of membranes, and acts as a secondary messenger in various plant responses to hormonal and environmental signals10. Foliar application of calcium is an effective method for addressing deficiencies and correcting associated issues11. The consumption of calcium increases shoot growth and yield in strawberries12, improves quality and shelf life in apples13 and plums14. Increasing the concentrations of calcium and potassium from 7.0 to 14.0 meq/L resulted in a significant enhancement of total soluble solids (TSS%) and vitamin C content in tomato fruits, while simultaneously causing a significant reduction in total acidity15.

Silicon, the second most abundant element on Earth’s surface, is a useful nutrient in plant growth. Silicon, by depositing in cell walls and forming a cellulose-silicon layer and bonding with calcium and pectin, increases the strength of cell walls and plant tolerance to cell damage caused by pathogens. Silicon also has high mobility in plants compared to calcium16. Therefore, it has attracted attention as a complementary element in the cultivation of various plants. Silicon plays a role in improving growth, increasing photosynthesis capacity, reducing evaporation and transpiration rates, increasing leaf strength, increasing chlorophyll content per leaf area, and product quality17. In the case of silicon deficiency, the level of chlorophyll in rice plants decreases, resulting in decreased photosynthesis18. Silicon application also significantly enhances fruit firmness, as well as increases the content of total soluble solids and vitamin C in tomato fruits, thereby improving both the texture and nutritional quality of the produce19.

Tomato is one of the valuable products in which its quantitative and qualitative characteristics play a direct role in its shelf life and marketability. Potassium, calcium, and silicon are elements that play a significant role in increasing yield, improving quality, and post-harvest characteristics of many fruits and vegetables. Therefore, with the increasing use of soilless cultivation systems and the implementation of precise management practices in greenhouses to increase the quantity and quality of products, and considering the specific importance of these elements for plant growth, fruit quality, and post-harvest quality, with their interrelated effects have not been extensively studied on plants like tomatoes. Hence, in this study, the impact of applying potassium, calcium, silicon, and their interactive effects on the growth, fruit quality, and characteristics after harvest of tomato fruit in a hydroponic cultivation system was investigated.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

This study was conducted as a factorial experiment based on a completely randomized design with 3 replications (2 plant for each replication) on tomato plants (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill. Var. Isabella), in the greenhouse of Vali-e-Asr University of Rafsanjan. The treatments included different concentrations of potassium sulfate at three levels (0.5, 1, and 1.5 mM) added to the nutrient solution, and calcium nitrate and potassium silicate at 5 levels (control (without foliar application), 0.25, and 0.5 g L−1 of calcium nitrate, and 0.5 and 1 g L−1 of potassium silicate) applied as foliar sprays. Initially, tomato seeds of Isabella variety were planted in growing trays containing a mixture of cocopeat and perlite in a 50:50 volume ratio. Irrigation was done daily until germination and emergence of seedlings. After the seedlings emerged, they were fed with a half Hoagland nutrient solution on a daily basis. Once the seedlings reached the 4-leaf stage, seedlings that were stronger in growth and approximately the same size were selected and transferred to cultivation bags. The growing medium in the main bags consisted of a mixture of cocopeat and perlite in a 25:75 volume ratio, with three plants grown in each cultivation bag. Throughout the growth and flowering period, a nutrient solution was applied uniformly to all plants (Table 1). Irrigation was done using a drip irrigation system for the cultivation bags, with a daily solution volume of 2 L divided into two doses in the morning and evening, applying 1 L per plant in each dose. The treatments were applied after one month and the formation of sufficient leaves on the plants. Potassium sulfate was used for the potassium treatment, and for the foliar application of calcium, calcium nitrate was used, while potassium silicate was used for silicon. The nutrient solution was applied at a rate of 250 ml per plant through root irrigation every day manually. The foliar spray with calcium nitrate and potassium silicate was done with two week intervals for three times. The pH of the nutrient solution was adjusted to about 6.5, and the distilled water was given to each bag once a week to allow water drainage from the plant pots. The experiment was conducted for 110 days. The plant organs (shoot and roots) were harvested, weighed, oven-dried (48 h at 72 °C) for determination of shoot fresh weight, root fresh weight, shoot dry weight and root dry weight.

Measurement of growth parameters

At the end of the experiments (110 days after planting), the plant height, leaf area, leaf number, fresh and dry weight of shoots, and root were determined. Plant height was measured and recorded using a ruler. For measuring dry weight, plant samples were placed in an oven at 72 °C for 48 h and then weighed. Leaf surface area of each plant was measured using a leaf area meter (CI 202 model, made in Australia). Reproductive characteristics included fruit number, fruit weight per plant, and fruit diameter. Fruits were harvested at the ripe firm stage (full red color stage), counted, and weighed. Fruit weight per plant was obtained by summing the weights of harvested fruits from each experimental unit during the growth period. For measuring fruit diameter at the firm-ripe stage, fruits (n = 5) from each experimental unit were harvested, and their diameters were measured using a digital caliper. The average fruit diameter per experimental unit was then calculated and included in the statistical analyses.

Photosynthetic pigments

Chlorophyll and carotenoid content were measured by grinding 125 mg of dried mature leaves with 5.2 ml of 80% acetone, followed by centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 6 min. The absorption of the leaf extract was read at wavelengths of 470, 646, and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer (UV/VIS Spectrometer, T80 Instruments PG Ltd model, made in Australia). The chlorophyll a, b, total chlorophyll, and carotenoid levels were measured in mg.g-1 fresh weight20.

Nutrient analysis

For measuring the potassium (K) and calcium (Ca) concentrations in the plant, 0.5 g of dried mature leaves were carefully weighed on a sensitive balance with an accuracy of 0.001 g and then incinerated in a clean, dry crucible. The obtained ash was added with 10 ml of 2 N hydrochloric acid and placed on a hot plate at 550 °C for 6 h. After the solution turned lemon yellow, it was filtered through filter paper, and the volume was adjusted to 100 ml with distilled water. The potassium content was measured using a flame photometer (PFP7 model, made in Germany), and calcium in the plant was measured using atomic absorption spectroscopy (GBCAVANTA-PM model, made in Australia)21.

Vitamin C

The amount of vitamin C in fruit juice was determined by titration with DCP (2,6-dichlorophenolindophenol). Finally, the amount of vitamin C was calculated in mg of ascorbic acid per 100 g of fruit weight22.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis of the data obtained from this experiment was performed using the SAS statistical software, and the comparison of treatment means was calculated using the Duncan multiple range test at a 5% significance level. Graphs were plotted using Excel software.

Sole application of potassium sulfate did not have a significant effect, with the lowest plant height belonging to the treatments with potassium sulfate application and no foliar spraying. The results of this research indicated that the interactive effects of potassium sulfate application and foliar spraying with calcium nitrate and potassium silicate on photosynthetic pigment content were significant. The highest levels of chlorophyll a and total chlorophyll were recorded in the treatment with a 1.5 mM potassium sulfate solution and 0.25 g L−1 calcium nitrate. At various levels of potassium application, the effect of calcium nitrate foliar spraying on photosynthetic pigment content was greater than potassium silicate foliar spraying. The treatment with 1.5 mM potassium sulfate in the nutrient solution and foliar spraying with 0.25 and 0.5 g L−1 calcium nitrate, increased the total chlorophyll by 82% and 38%, compared to the control treatment, respectively. Vitamin C

Discussion

Vegetative growth

The results showed that the effects of applying different concentrations of potassium sulfate and foliar spraying with calcium nitrate and potassium silicate on the growth indices of tomato plants were significant. Torre et al. reported that high potassium to calcium ratios in rose plants would elongate the shoot length, which is in line with the findings of this study23. Potassium deficiency leads to a reduction in chlorophyll content and photosynthesis, negatively affecting carbohydrate production and its translocation, as well as reducing the plant’s water potential, resulting in decreased plant growth and height24. Calcium plays a role in the elongation of root and stem growing points25. Calcium deficiency leads to a decrease in the growth of meristematic tissues, and foliar calcium spray leads to an increase in stem length and plant height26. Leaf surface area is a crucial characteristic in plant growth. Potassium causes stomatal opening and closure and increases cell turgor pressure, indirectly influencing the increase in leaf area of plants27. Osmotic pressure resulting from potassium accumulation in the cell leads to cell development and leaf growth28. On the other hand, it has been proven that the optimal application of potassium to calcium increases chloroplast volume and chlorophyll, ultimately leading to an increase in fresh and dry weight in plants29. Caines and Shennan30 reported that the dry weight of the whole plant in tomato, with different calcium concentrations, significantly increased as a higher calcium concentration stabilizes the cell membrane and prevents the breakage of cell wall bands, which is consistent with the findings of this study. Yang et al.31 and Fageria and Moreira32 reported that calcium increases leaf photosynthesis, water use efficiency, and root growth in tomato and corn, therefore, it has indirectly influenced the weight of shoots. Silicon is a beneficial nutrient for plant growth and health16. Silicon increases water use efficiency and improves leaf relative humidity content, resulting in increased photosynthesis, leading to an increase in leaf area and leaf number in plants17.

Reproductive growth

A decrease in potassium sulfate application led to a reduction in fruit number, in line with the results of Rubio et al.33 indicating a decrease in fruit number in bell pepper plants under the influence of reduced potassium nutrient solution levels. In potassium deficiency, the decrease in fruit number could be due to an increase in the number of dropped flowers or fruits resulting from reduced sucrose levels due to potassium deficiency. Potassium is the most abundant cation in the phloem sap and facilitates the transport of sugars to the sink (fruits)27. Calcium application can increase fruit weight and tomato yield34. Although in this experiment, changes in calcium and silicon levels in some cases increased fruit numbers, their effect was not significant.

Silicon nutrition increased tomato performance by 15–30%, resulting from an increase in the number and size of fruits35. In a study on calcium chloride in tomatoes, Dong et al.36 reported that calcium plays a vital role in cell elongation, cell division, single fruit weight, and fruit growth and development.

Photosynthetic pigment content

The levels of chlorophyll and carotenoids in leaves are crucial factors in determining the rate of photosynthesis and plant dry matter production37. It has been established that calcium deficiency interferes with photosynthetic processes, ultimately reducing carboxylation efficiency and photosynthesis, leading to a significant decrease in plant biomass production38. Dordas39 reported that calcium foliar application led to a 23% increase in leaf chlorophyll content compared to the control, which is consistent with the results of this experiment. Calcium deficiency results in a reduction in chlorophyll content, highlighting the importance of calcium in chlorophyll maintenance due to the accumulation of apoprotein molecule groups in antenna (cytochrome) molecules39. In this study, the effects of different concentrations of potassium, calcium, and silicon on chlorophyll and carotenoid indices were significant, with the maximum index observed in potassium sulfate and calcium nitrate treatments compared to the potassium silicate treatment. Low calcium levels in leaves accelerate chlorophyll degradation and premature aging, ultimately leading to tissue necrosis, in line with the findings of Torre et al.23 and the results of this research. Potassium plays a crucial role in plant biochemical and biophysical processes27. Potassium helps maintain the photosynthetic rate, and its deficiency leads to a decrease in chlorophyll concentration and stabilized carbon transfer, consistent with the results of Zhao et al.40 and this study. The effect of silicon on increasing leaf photosynthesis occurs through its influence on the activity of the enzyme ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase and by preventing chlorophyll degradation by silicon41.

Calcium and potassium concentration in shoots

Significant increase in the calcium concentration in the shoots of strawberry plants with calcium chloride foliar spraying compared to the control treatment (without foliar spraying) has been reported42. Additionally, Kanai et al.43 stated that due to the antagonistic effect between potassium and calcium, in potassium deficiency, calcium absorption and concentration increase. In this research, the potassium concentration in the plant was significantly influenced by the treatments. The potassium concentration in plants is highly dependent on potassium nutrition27. The potassium levels in soilless grown tomatoes decrease with an increase in calcium concentration in the nutrient solution15. This decrease in potassium concentration is attributed to the competitive effect of calcium for absorption sites in the plant. Gunes et al.44 reported that the potassium concentration in tomato fruits increases with a higher potassium concentration in the nutrient solution.

Vitamin C

Dong et al.36 reported that calcium application can increase the vitamin C content but will decrease the relative content of organic acids in tomato fruits. The results of this research showed that the use of potassium, calcium, and silicon treatments increased the vitamin C retention of tomatoes. Potassium plays a key role in carbohydrate metabolism, as a close relationship between carbohydrate metabolism and ascorbic acid formation has been reported45. Additionally, another study mentioned that an increase in potassium levels leads to an increase in titratable acidity, total soluble solids, vitamin C content, average fruit weight, and fruit pH in tomatoes46. Zhao-Hui et al.47 reported a significant increase in vitamin C content with an increase in potassium consumption in tomatoes, which aligns with the results of this experiment. Salicylic acid activates the enzyme ascorbate peroxidase, resulting in antioxidant properties and increased ascorbic acid content in fruits48.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that the combined application of 1.5 mM potassium sulfate in the nutrient solution with foliar spraying of calcium nitrate at 0.25 g/L significantly enhanced tomato growth, physiological traits, and fruit quality compared to other treatments. Foliar application of calcium nitrate generally showed greater efficacy in promoting growth and yield than potassium silicate. However, potassium silicate at 1 g/L foliar concentration combined with 1.5 mM potassium sulfate also significantly improved potassium concentration in shoots and contributed to fruit quality. The optimal treatment for maximizing vegetative growth and fruit yield was 1.5 mM potassium sulfate with 0.25 g/L calcium nitrate foliar spray, while 1.5 mM potassium sulfate with 1 g/L potassium silicate foliar spray was effective for improving nutrient content. This highlights the importance of balancing potassium levels in nutrient solutions with appropriate foliar calcium or silicon supplementation for optimal tomato performance. Growers can tailor these concentrations to achieve improved growth, yield, and fruit quality in greenhouse hydroponic production systems.
