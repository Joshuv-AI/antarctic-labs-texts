Evaluating patients’ trust in health information based on different dimensions of trust
Authors: Mingming Song, Tin Nguyen, Christian Haas, Joel Elson, Dhundy Kiran Bastola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid expansion of online platforms has changed how patients access health information, shifting the challenge from obtaining data to discerning which content is more trustworthy and personalized. While health care professionals remain the most trusted source, relying solely on them is no longer sufficient for personalized care in the digital era. This study examines how source type (interpersonal vs. web-based), transparency (presence vs. absence of evidence), and interactivity (static vs. dynamic engagement) shape patients’ trust in health information. A 2 × 2 × 2 vignette-based experiment with 517 U.S. adults tested these effects. Participants reported higher trust in interpersonal and transparent sources, whereas interactivity alone showed no main effect. However, a significant three-way interaction revealed that trust peaked when interpersonal sources were transparent and interactive and was lowest when web-based systems were non-transparent and low-interactive. Interactivity partially compensated for low transparency in digital contexts. These findings underscore the need for multidimensional trust-building strategies in digital health communication. Integrating transparency and interactivity across platforms enables patients to better identify trustworthy information, thereby enhancing personalized engagement and improving health outcomes.

Introduction

The expansion of online platforms has significantly expanded the availability of health information, providing patients with a wide range of resources during their health information seeking experiences. While this availability has provided more opportunities for patients to be better informed and educated with their health conditions1,2, it also introduces new challenges. The primary challenge has shifted from simply accessing health information to effectively choosing, locating, understanding, and utilizing relevant information that precisely addresses their individual health conditions3,4.

Previous studies have shown that patients generally trust health care providers because of their recognized professionalism and responsibilities inherent to their roles5,6. Health care providers play a critical role in empowering patients by guiding and educating them with personalized information. However, because their primary responsibility is adhering to standardized medical protocols that ensure patient safety and consistent care7,8, there can be limitations on how extensively personalized health information and care can be delivered. Additionally, in many healthcare systems, time constraints and workforce shortages limit patients’ opportunities to obtain personalized explanations directly from clinicians9–11. As a result, individuals increasingly turn to alternative sources of health information, particularly online platforms12,13. However, patients may distrust such broadly accessible health information, concerned that it may be irrelevant or inaccurate for their specific needs14–16. That is, even when the information is accurate, it may not align with or be suitable for an individual’s specific health condition15. When patients mistakenly trust inaccurate health information, it may lead to adverse outcomes such as inappropriate self-medication, delays in seeking professional medical care, or adherence to ineffective treatments that could be harmful as well17–19.

Patients’ decisions about whether to act on health information can significantly influence their health outcomes. When patients bring external health information to medical appointments, it may consume valuable time that could be better spent on more effective communication20. A critical factor in improving these interactions is understanding how patients evaluate and choose to trust different sources of health information21. Rather than simply increasing the volume of available information, it is essential to foster trust in accurate and credible sources, especially when these may not initially be perceived as trustworthy. Doing so can help ensure that patients are not only informed but also empowered to engage in their health through reliable, personalized, and timely information.

Previous studies on patients’ trust in health information have focused on evaluating trust by source type, assessing which sources patients trust most, and exploring the underlying reasons such as patients’ demographic characteristics14,22. However, this source-based approach often oversimplifies the factors behind these choices and overlooks the mechanisms that drive patients’ decision-making around trust in health information. For instance, the emergence of AI-driven platforms has dramatically shifted the landscape of available health information23. A few decades ago, it would have been unimaginable for AI tools to offer health insights with a level of accuracy that could potentially challenge traditional trusted sources like health care professionals24,25. Meanwhile, many AI-based systems function as “black box” models, where the processes behind algorithmic outputs are not easily interpretable to users. This opacity can make it more difficult for patients to evaluate the credibility of digital health information, increasing the importance of transparency in digital health environments. This dynamic environment presents a need to shift the focus from simply identifying trusted sources to understanding the underlying mechanisms and elements that influence how patients develop trust in health information.

Our research moves beyond identifying where patients obtain health information or which sources they trust. Instead, we focus on how trust is formed, examining how features of information presentation shape users’ evaluations. Using a vignette-based experimental design, we hold informational content constant across conditions to isolate the effects of these presentation features, allowing us to examine how trust judgments are shaped independently of content differences.

While prior research consistently shows that physicians are the most trusted health information source26,27, the increasing use of digital health tools calls for broader investigation. We must consider how patients evaluate both the mode of presentation and the context of delivery, rather than focusing solely on source types. Technology-driven solutions designed for health care communication aim to facilitate effective health communication, enhance shared decision-making, and improve overall patient engagement28,29. However, for these tools to be truly effective, a deeper understanding is needed of how patients evaluate and develop trust in health information.

Trust in digital systems has been widely studied in human–computer interaction and information systems research. Prior work suggests that users evaluate trustworthiness through signals related to competence, integrity, and responsiveness. For instance, previous research conceptualizes trustworthiness in terms of ability, integrity, and benevolence30, which represent whether the entity is perceived as competent, honest, and attentive to users’ needs. In technology-mediated environments, these trust signals are often conveyed through system-level cues. Research on trust in automation highlights that transparency and system feedback are critical for helping users appropriately calibrate trust in algorithmic systems31. Similarly, studies of online and e-commerce systems emphasize the role of institutional and informational cues, such as source credibility, information quality, and system reliability, in shaping user trust in digital platforms32. In the context of digital health information systems, these theoretical insights suggest that users rely on observable cues embedded in system design to assess trustworthiness. In particular, cues such as information source attribution, transparency in presenting health information, and the degree of interaction supported by the system signal the competence, integrity, and responsiveness of the system. Accordingly, the present study focuses on three key dimensions that may shape patients’ trust in health information environments: source of information, transparency, and interactivity21,33. While source type and transparency are relatively straightforward signals of credibility and information quality, interactivity works differently: it shapes users’ perception of system responsiveness and personalized engagement, two attributes closely tied to the benevolence dimension of trust30. At the system level, interactivity includes features such as reciprocal communication, context-sensitive feedback, and adaptive information delivery. When a system demonstrates the ability to respond to users’ individual queries and sustain a two-way dialogue, it signals that the system is attentive to individual users’ needs. This is consistent with the Computers Are Social Actors (CASA) paradigm, which suggests that users apply social heuristics including expectations of responsiveness when evaluating digital systems34. In digital health contexts, prior research has found that conversational and interactive systems are perceived as more credible partly because they simulate the reciprocal dynamics characteristic of trusted interpersonal communication35,36. Therefore, interactivity is theorized not only as a usability feature, but as a system-level trust signal that conveys attentiveness and responsiveness, dimensions that patients may use to infer whether a health information system genuinely serves their individual needs. By examining how these presentation-level characteristics influence trust, this study aims to provide insights into how digital health information systems can be designed to better align with patients’ trust expectations.

Methods

Hypotheses development

Sources of information

Traditional health care communication followed a provider-centric model, where patients passively received information and guidance from health care providers. Physicians, in particular, were regarded as the authoritative source for medical information, diagnosing conditions and prescribing treatments37,38. However, this approach may no longer be sufficient in today’s complex and digital health information environment, where patients face a broad and often overwhelming range of trustworthy health environment and information to support personalized health care, making it critically important than ever to enhance their ability to evaluate and navigate various health care options available to them39–41. To truly achieve efficient, personalized health care, it is essential to promote multidimensional trust strategies that help patients recognize and evaluate trustworthy health information across a range of platforms and contexts.

Categorizing information sources

To understand the complex landscape of health communication, we categorize information sources into two groups: interpersonal (e.g., doctors, family, peers) and web-based (e.g., health websites, AI-driven tools, social media). This classification builds upon previous research and helps to identify differences in how trust functions across various types of sources27. Interestingly, our previous research suggests that within each category, patients’ trust perceptions do not always vary significantly across individual sources. For instance, patients may view different online platforms similarly in terms of trustworthiness, just as they may not cognitively differentiate between various interpersonal sources.

However, a notable exception occurs when the source is identified as a doctor. In such cases, patients tend to automatically trust the information without deeper scrutiny27,42,43. This automatic trust in doctors highlights the influence of perceived authority in shaping health decisions.

To better understand the underlying mechanisms of trust, our study deliberately avoids revealing the source itself and instead focuses on the deeper attributes of information sources, such as transparency and interactivity. By examining how patients evaluate trust beyond source labels, we aim to find the key dimensions that influence trust in health communication.

Transparency

Transparent communication is crucial for building trust in health information. Even when information comes from high-credibility sources such as physicians, it may still be met with skepticism if it lacks clear attribution, explicit references, or accessible presentation of credentials44,45. Transparency refers to the clear disclosure of the background, theoretical foundation, and empirical basis45,46. It encompasses key aspects such as data sources, research methodologies, clinical evidence, and references that validate the reliability of the information40,46.

Transparent communication allows people to assess credibility by openly available citations, named sources, and clearly structured content. This includes information derived from peer-reviewed medical journals, government health agencies (e.g., CDC, WHO), hospital databases, patient registries, or large-scale epidemiological studies47,48. Being transparent in methodologies can also reinforce trust46. This includes stating research methods, study designs, and analytical approaches used to generate health advice49,50. For example, if health recommendations are based on randomized controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, systematic reviews, or meta-analyses, the information should specify these methodologies to help people understand the strength of the evidence.

In today’s health information environment, patients may focus on how information is presented and what it reveals about its origins and credibility from other sources. Prior research suggests that patients are more likely to trust information that includes clear indicators of how it was developed, such as what data were used, what methods were applied, and which experts or institutions stand behind it51,52. In this study, we operationalize transparency as the presence or absence of specific references to research methodologies. Participants were not given detailed definitions of transparency due to the limited length of vignettes but instead were exposed to information that either included or omitted transparency-related cues.

Interactivity

Interactivity can critically shape patients’ perceptions of trust towards the health information27,53. It refers to how patients engage with health information, including their ability to ask questions, receive responses, or influence how that information is presented. Three key elements of interactivity are (1) Two-way communication: This allows patients to participate in mutual information exchange by asking questions, receiving clarifications, and participating in discussions about their specific health concerns54. This interaction helps patients feel heard and understood, creating a sense of responsiveness and attentiveness that fosters credibility and trust. (2) Timely responses: Delayed replies can increase patient anxiety and uncertainty55,56. When patients receive timely feedback, it signals attentiveness and a commitment to care, helping patients feel supported and engaged57. Sources that consistently provide accurate and prompt responses are more likely to be trusted and revisited36. (3) Personalization: This refers to how well health information is adapted to patients’ individual needs, preferences, and conditions. When content reflects their specific situations, patients are more likely to find the information relevant, actionable, and trustworthy58,59. Such personalized engagement signals care and understanding, which plays a crucial role in building credibility and long-term trust60,61. In this study, interactivity was operationalized as the degree to which the source allowed reciprocal engagement and context-sensitive response within the vignette scenarios. This operationalization captures whether the system supports bidirectional communication and personalized responsiveness, both of which are indicators of the attentiveness dimension of trust in digital health contexts.

Building on our understanding of trust in information sources and the two key dimensions of transparency and interactivity, we developed a set of hypotheses to be tested through a survey using vignettes that manipulate these evaluated factors.

Main effects hypothesis

We tested three main effects hypotheses to examine the individual impacts of each variable:

Source Effect (Hypothesis 1): Patients’ trust in health information will be higher for interpersonal sources (e.g., doctors, family, peers) compared to web-based sources (e.g., health websites, AI-driven tools, social media).

Transparency Effect (Hypothesis 2): Patients’ trust in health information will be higher when presented with transparent credibility information (e.g., author credentials, data sources).

Interactivity Effect (Hypothesis 3): Patients’ trust in health information will be higher when the source is more interactive (e.g., allows for questions and responses).

Interaction effects hypothesis

We also tested three interaction effects hypotheses to examine how these variables interact with each other:

Source × Transparency Interaction (Hypothesis 4): The combination of interpersonal sources and transparent credibility will result in the highest levels of trust, while web-based systems with non-transparent credibility will result in the lowest level.

Source × Interactivity Interaction (Hypothesis 5): The combination of interpersonal sources and high interactivity will result in higher trust levels, while web-based systems with low interactivity will result in lower trust levels.

Transparency × Interactivity Interaction (Hypothesis 6): The combination of transparent credibility and high interactivity will result in higher trust levels, while non-transparent credibility and low interactivity will result in lower trust levels.

Data collection

The data for this study were collected through a nationwide online survey conducted via the Qualtrics platform in Spring 2025. A total of 623 participants were recruited from across the United States. After data screening for completeness and attention check compliance, 517 valid responses were retained for the final analysis. This approach allowed the study to access a broad cross-section of adult respondents and ensured diversity in demographics such as age, gender, race, education, and geographic location to enhance the relevance and interpretability of the findings. To ensure the quality and functionality of the survey instrument, a pilot test with 35 participants was conducted prior to full deployment. This pilot phase helped refine the wording of items, the usability of the survey flow, and the presentation of the vignette scenarios.

This research was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) under protocol number 0585–24-EX at the University of Nebraska Medical Center. All procedures were conducted in accordance with ethical standards and regulatory guidelines. Informed consent was obtained electronically before participants began the survey. Confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing responses, and all data were stored securely with no personally identifiable information collected.

Study design and measurement

The vignette-based experimental approach was selected because our research objective required holding informational content constant across conditions to isolate the independent and interactive effects of source type, transparency, and interactivity on trust. This level of control is difficult to achieve in live or immersive environments, where uncontrolled variables such as system aesthetics or interaction latency could easily confound the effects of interest62,63. Additionally, developing eight functionally distinct live systems that each vary systematically across three dimensions would introduce technological heterogeneity that itself becomes a confound. Specifically, participants were randomly assigned to one of eight conditions in a 2 (Interactivity: high vs. low) × 2 (Transparency: high vs. low) × 2 (Source Type: interpersonal vs. web-based) between-subjects factorial design (Table 1). Each participant read a single vignette describing a health communication scenario that systematically varied across these three experimental dimensions. For example, web-based sources included either static websites or interactive AI-based systems, while interpersonal sources were described as interactions with clinical staff such as administrative assistants. All vignette scenarios followed the same narrative structure and contextual setting. Before vignette exposure, participants completed a series of baseline measures, including demographic information, health literacy, and a general trust assessment. The general trust measure asked participants to rate their trust in several common health information sources (e.g., doctors, friends/family, websites, social media, and AI tools). To control potential order effects, this baseline block was counterbalanced, with half of the participants completing it before the vignette and half after.

Specifically, health literacy was measured using three screening items adapted from Chew et al. 72, which assessed participants’ confidence in reading and understanding medical forms, as well as their difficulty in learning about health conditions due to written information. These items have been widely used in health communication research and are suitable for rapid assessment in online surveys. Additionally, eHealth literacy was assessed using the 8-item eHealth Literacy Scale (eHEALS) developed by Norman and Skinner64. This instrument measures participants’ perceived knowledge, comfort, and skills related to finding, evaluating, and using health information from the internet. Items included statements such as “I know how to use the Internet to answer my questions about health” and “I feel confident in using information from the Internet to make health decisions.” Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = Strongly disagree to 5 = Strongly agree. Higher scores indicated greater perceived eHealth literacy. Following vignette exposure, participants evaluated their perceived trust in the health information presented in the vignette using a 7-point Likert-scale item (1 = Greatly Distrust, 7 = Greatly Trust). The wording of this item is provided in Appendix 1. This measure served as the primary dependent variable in the analysis.

To validate the experimental manipulations, participants also completed manipulation checks assessing their perceptions of the vignette’s transparency and interactivity. These checks were designed to confirm that the vignettes successfully conveyed the intended levels of each factor across the eight experimental conditions. The combination of a fully crossed factorial design and post-vignette manipulation checks ensured that the study could evaluate both main effects and interaction effects of transparency, interactivity, and source type on trust outcomes. Results

This section presents findings from the collected data, including data description, manipulation checks, assumption testing, and the final experimental analysis examining how trust in health information is influenced by three key factors: source type (interpersonal vs. web-based), transparency (high vs. low), and interactivity (high vs. low). A series of hypotheses were tested to evaluate both main effects and interaction effects across these dimensions. Given the non-normal distribution of the trust outcome, all analyses employed non-parametric statistical methods, including the Mann–Whitney U test as an alternative to the t-test, and the Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA as an alternative to factorial ANOVA.

Data and participants

Participants were recruited across multiple rounds of data collection and 623 completed the full survey. Of these responses, 106 were excluded due to missing values, clear inattentive response patterns (e.g., flat-lining across survey items), and/or failure on the manipulation checks associated with the vignette conditions. The final analytic sample consisted of 517 participants (Table 3). Because these screening criteria were applied during the same data cleaning process, some excluded responses may have met more than one exclusion criterion. Gender distribution was relatively balanced, with 51.6% identifying as male and 48.4% as female. Participants ranged in age from 19 to 75+, with the largest age group being 25–34 years (19.0%), followed by 45–54 (16.6%), and 55–65 (15.7%). In terms of education levels, over one-third (35.0%) held a high school diploma, 26.7% had some college but no degree, and 13.0% held a bachelor’s degree. Only 1.0% of participants reported having a doctoral degree. The sample was predominantly White (71.8%), with 17.2% identifying as African American/Black, 6.4% as Hispanic or Latino, 3.5% as Asian/Asian American, and smaller proportions identifying as Native American, Middle Eastern, or other. Household income levels were diverse: 17.0% reported earning less than $25,000 annually, while 31.9% reported income levels of $100,000 or more. Manipulation check and baseline testing

Before testing the main hypotheses, manipulation checks confirmed that participants generally perceived the intended differences across the experimental conditions. A chi-square test revealed a significant association between the assigned manipulation and participants’ perceptions of transparency, interactivity, and information source type (χ2(2) = 24.72, p < 0.001), supporting the internal validity of the vignette-based design. Additionally, assumption testing indicated that the distribution of trust ratings deviated from normality, as assessed by the Shapiro–Wilk test (W = 0.927, p < 0.001). However, Levene’s test indicated no significant violations of homogeneity of variance (p = 0.418). As a result, all hypothesis testing was conducted using non-parametric statistical methods appropriate for between-subject comparisons.

To assess participants’ baseline trust in various health information sources, we computed average baseline trust scores from five survey items, each rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Not at all, 5 = A great deal). These sources included doctors, family/friends, health websites, social media, and AI tools. Descriptive statistics revealed clear distinctions in perceived trustworthiness among the sources. Doctors received the highest baseline trust score (M = 3.78), indicating that participants generally place strong trust in health care professionals. Moderate levels of trust were observed for family and friends (M = 3.11) and health websites (M = 3.10), suggesting participants view these sources as somewhat reliable but less authoritative. In contrast, AI tools (M = 2.51) and social media (M = 2.21) were trusted significantly less, indicating skepticism toward mass media and informal online platforms as sources of health information.

Additionally, health literacy was assessed using three items evaluating participants’ ability to understand, interpret, and act upon health information. Participants reported a mean score of 3.19 (SD = 0.75) on a 5-point Likert scale, indicating moderately high self-reported health literacy. eHealth literacy, measured through eight items focused on digital health information-seeking (e.g., finding, evaluating, and applying health information online), showed a mean score of 3.28 (SD = 0.82). This reflects moderate digital literacy, though the variability suggests differing levels of comfort and skill with navigating online health resources.

Hypotheses testing

The results provided support for Hypotheses 1 and 2 but not for Hypothesis 3 (Table 4). Specifically, for Hypothesis 1, participants reported significantly higher trust in interpersonal sources than in web-based sources (Mann–Whitney U = 37,887, p < 0.001). Additionally, some pairwise comparisons suggested conditional compensatory effects. That is, one trust-enhancing feature appeared to balance the absence of another, but only in specific contexts. For instance, when transparency was absent, high interactivity in web-based systems helped elevate trust to levels comparable with certain interpersonal conditions. In other words, interactivity appeared to partially compensate for the lack of transparency, but only when the source was web-based. The trust in web-based systems with high interactivity and no transparency were similar to interpersonal sources that were also non-transparent, regardless of their interactivity level (p = 0.869 and p = 0.877). Although interactivity did not yield a main effect in the overall model, these results indicate a conditional compensatory effect that when transparency of the presenting information is lacking, interactive features in web-based platforms may help restore trust to a level comparable to familiar, but not transparent, interpersonal sources.

Discussion

Main findings

This study examined how trust in health information is shaped by key presentation features, including transparency and interactivity across interpersonal and web-based contexts. Our findings reveal a clear trust inclination favoring interpersonal sources over web-based systems, with participants expressing significantly higher trust when information was presented by a human, even when the source was not identified as a physician. Notably, transparency appeared as a consistent and robust factor of trust across conditions, supporting the idea that patients value knowing where their information comes from. While interactivity alone did not significantly influence trust, our study identified a significant three-way interaction: trust was maximized when interpersonal sources delivered transparent and interactive information, and lowest when web-based systems lacked both transparency and interactivity. This nuanced pattern suggests that trust in health information is not simply about the medium or the message, but rather the collaboration between how, by whom, and with what clarity information is presented.

The design of our study specifically examined transparency and interactivity as key factors that were considered as some aspects or dimensions of trust when individuals process health information. While other factors such as authority or credibility are also relevant in trust formation, our focus was on the manipulable aspects of information presentation that can be optimized in both digital and interpersonal settings. Precisely defining these constructs is essential for both study design and measurement validity. In this study, for instance, transparency was operationalized through content-level signals such as references to data sources and applied methodologies. However, the construct itself extends beyond these features. Prior research has emphasized that transparency also includes institutional factors such as disclosure of funding sources, potential conflicts of interest, and policy rationales, particularly during public health crises66,67. For example, during the COVID-19 pandemic, transparent disclosure of vaccine development phases, adverse event reports, and real-time monitoring data helped enhance public confidence in immunization programs68. Similarly, transparency in genomic medicine and AI-assisted diagnostics ensures that patients understand how algorithms influence their health assessments and treatment recommendations69,70. These aspects, while not explicitly included in our vignettes, remain important considerations for long-term trust building. In the context of this study, we intentionally focused on transparency as patients would commonly encounter it through surface-level informational signs rather than technical or institutional disclosures. This design choice reflects typical real-world experiences where patients form impressions without access to full or multiple layers of transparency. Future research should explore how different layers of transparency such as content-level, methodological, and institutional interact to shape trust across health settings. In particular, the growing use of AI-driven health communication tools may demand new standards for transparency that extend beyond traditional citation practices, including explainability of algorithms and clarity around data provenance69.

Theoretical implications

The results of Hypotheses 1 and 2 highlight the strong and consistent influence of both information source type and transparency in shaping trust. Participants showed significantly higher trust in interpersonal sources compared to web-based systems, and in transparent presentations over non-transparent ones, regardless of the level of interactivity. These findings indicate the importance of human-delivered and transparently sourced information in fostering trust. In particular, the role of transparency stands out: when patients can clearly identify the sources, evidence, or rationale behind the content, they are more likely to perceive it as credible, whether it comes from a person or a digital system. Interactivity alone did not have a significant impact on trust identified in this study, nor did it meaningfully increase trust when paired with just one of the other two factors. One possible explanation could relate to the vignette-based experimental design. Although the scenarios described different levels of interactive functionality, participants only read about these features rather than directly experiencing them. In vignette-based studies, such experiential components are necessarily simulated through textual descriptions, which may reduce participants’ perceived sense of interaction. Consequently, the manipulation may have been sufficient to produce conditional effects in combination with other factors, but insufficient to generate a strong standalone main effect.

The lack of support for Hypotheses 4, 5, and 6, which proposed two-way interactions between source type, transparency, and interactivity, suggests that trust does not form through simple pairwise combinations. Instead, the results support a more integrated understanding of trust formation, in which the three-way interaction (exploratory study) plays a central role. In other words, trust is not just about who delivers the message or how interactive the delivery is. It’s more likely to depend on how these elements work together and whether the information itself is communicated with clarity and credibility.

While our hypotheses predicted that high transparency and interpersonal sources would enhance trust, the results reveal a more complex interplay. The findings support that the combined presence of an interpersonal source, interactive delivery, and transparent information maximizes patient trust. In several conditions from post hoc analysis of exploratory study (Appendix 2), especially those involving either interpersonal or web-based sources under specific levels of transparency or interactivity, trust outcomes converged, which suggests that certain features can compensate for others when thoughtfully designed. For instance, when transparency was missing in a web-based system, high interactivity helped increase trust to levels comparable with certain interpersonal conditions. Participants trusted the web-based source with high interactivity and no transparency nearly as much as they trusted interpersonal sources that also lacked transparency, regardless of whether those interpersonal interactions were interactive or static (p = 0.869 and p = 0.877, respectively). This highlights the practical design implication that interactivity can play a critical, context-dependent role in shaping trust, especially when transparency is difficult to achieve. Conversely, among low-interactivity web platforms, transparency significantly improved trust, reinforcing that transparency functions as a critical requirement in digital health contexts. These patterns underscore that interactivity alone is insufficient to foster trust but can become impactful under certain deficits, especially when combined thoughtfully with other trust-promoting design features.

While interactivity did not show a significant main effect in this study, previous studies have highlighted the importance of interactivity in fostering trust across various domains35,53. Besides our previous explanation that interactivity may function more as a context-dependent moderator than a universal driver of trust, another possible reason may relate to the participants’ passive orientation toward health information. Many patients are used to receiving information passively rather than actively engaging in their care. Without prior education or encouragement around the value of patient empowerment and shared decision-making, participants may not recognize or value interactive features. This underscores a broader challenge in health care: promoting patients’ willingness and capacity to take initiative in their health care journey. This suggests that the effectiveness of interactive health communication features may depend not only on system design, but also on users’ prior engagement orientations and health literacy levels.

Practical implications for digital health system design

From a design standpoint, these results emphasize two practical insights. First, transparency should be prioritized. It consistently increased trust regardless of other factors. Second, interactivity is not universally effective but becomes meaningful when trust is otherwise at risk, such as with unfamiliar or digital sources. For web-based platforms, which often lack the inherent authority of interpersonal sources, combining interactive features with clear, credible sourcing is especially important. For example, a conversational AI chatbot may not automatically inspire trust the way a clinician does. But if it references authoritative sources like CDC guidelines and allows for follow-up questions, it can reach trust levels similar to an in-person interaction that lacks transparency, such as being handed a brochure without context. Likewise, a static website with no interactive features may still foster strong trust if it includes clear references to clinical studies or expert consensus. These scenarios demonstrate that trust can be driven by how these elements are integrated to form a coherent and credible communication experience. At the same time, realizing this potential depends on whether patients are willing and able to engage with interactive features in the first place.

Many patients are accustomed to receiving health information passively, and without broader efforts to promote health literacy and patient empowerment, the trust-enhancing potential of interactivity may remain underutilized, particularly in web-based health communication tools. Future research should explore how design, education, and health literacy interventions can help activate interactivity’s role in trust formation, especially among digitally underserved or passively engaged patient populations.

Contributions to the literature

Beyond the immediate design implications, this study contributes to the broader literature on trust in health communication in several ways. Much of the existing trust research has focused either on interpersonal trust relationships or on trust in automated systems. However, digital health information environments represent a hybrid context in which individuals evaluate both technological systems and the information they deliver. Our findings suggest that presentation-level factors, such as information source attribution, transparency in information presentation, and the degree of interaction supported by the system, play a central role in shaping trust evaluations in these environments. These results extend existing research by highlighting how trustworthiness signals operate in contemporary digital health communication systems and by suggesting that additional elements of trustworthiness may emerge in virtual health contexts beyond those traditionally emphasized in interpersonal trust or human–computer interaction research.

These findings also contribute to the growing literature on patient empowerment and human–AI collaboration in health communication. Prior research has often examined features such as transparency, credibility, or interactivity as independent drivers of trust in digital environments. Our results suggest that these elements may not operate in isolation. Instead, trust appears to emerge from how these communication cues work together in the information environment, including source attribution, transparency of evidence, and opportunities for interaction. This perspective highlights that empowering patients in digital health contexts depends not only on providing access to information but also on how these signals collectively communicate credibility, responsiveness, and clarity. In addition, the findings provide insight into how AI-supported health information systems may support trust formation. Although digital systems lack many interpersonal cues present in clinician–patient communication, certain design features, such as transparency and interactive engagement, may partially compensate for the absence of direct human interaction. When these elements are integrated effectively, digital platforms might reproduce some of the trust-building signals traditionally associated with interpersonal communication, thereby supporting patient engagement with AI-assisted health information tools.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the experimental manipulation of interactivity relied on vignette-based system scenarios, which capture perceived interactivity rather than fully isolating all system characteristics. For example, the high-interactivity condition described an AI-based interactive system, which may also evoke perceptions associated with emerging technologies. Although participants were randomly assigned to experimental conditions to reduce systematic bias, future research could further disentangle these effects by directly measuring constructs such as perceived personalization, user engagement, or attitudes toward AI-based health systems. Second, participants evaluated hypothetical scenarios through vignette descriptions rather than interacting with real health systems. An immersive experimental setup would allow researchers to observe trust formation as it unfolds in real time, potentially capturing behavioral and affective dimensions of trust that text-based vignettes cannot fully replicate. The interactivity manipulation in this study described interactive features textually, which may underrepresent the experiential impact of actual two-way engagement. Future research should consider employing interactive system prototypes or field-based designs to examine whether interactivity effects are stronger when participants directly experience system responsiveness rather than read about it.

Conclusions

This study examined how trust in health information is shaped by three key factors, information source type, transparency, and interactivity, using a vignette-based experimental design. Our findings confirmed the importance of interpersonal sources and transparent presentation in fostering patient trust, while also revealing a more complex role for interactivity. Although interactivity alone did not significantly increase trust, it contributed meaningfully when paired with transparency and information sources, suggesting a conditional or compensatory role. Additionally, this significant three-way interaction emphasizes that trust is not determined by any single factor, but by how these dimensions collaborate. These insights have important implications for the design of trustworthy health communication. In particular, transparency should be prioritized across all delivery formats, and interactivity should be thoughtfully tailored based on the communication channel. For web-based tools that lack inherent human credibility, embedding interactive features and making transparency explicit may help approximate the trust typically associated with interpersonal interactions. Ultimately, the path to effective, personalized health care depends not only on delivering accurate information but also on presenting it in ways that patients perceive as credible, responsive, and empowering.
