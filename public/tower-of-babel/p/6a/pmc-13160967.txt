Comparison of short-term outcomes of intracorporeal and extracorporeal anastomosis in robotic right colectomy using the da Vinci SP system: a multi-center cohort study
Authors: Jae Ha Park, Songsoo Yang, Yong Sik Yoon, Young Il Kim, Min Hyun Kim, Jong Lyul Lee, Chan Wook Kim, In Ja Park, Seok-Byung Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntracorporeal anastomosis (ICA) has shown advantages in laparoscopic right colectomy, but its role in robotic surgery using the da Vinci single-port (SP) system remains unclear. This study compared short-term outcomes of ICA and extracorporeal anastomosis (ECA) in SP robotic right colectomy.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 110 patients who underwent SP robotic right colectomy at two tertiary centers between November 2020 and February 2025. Perioperative outcomes and 30-day postoperative complications were compared between the two groups. Pain scores were assessed during the early postoperative period.ResultsIn a total of 110 patients, ICA was 32 and ECA was 78. Baseline characteristics were comparable between the two groups, although comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and hypertension were more frequent in the ECA group (both p = 0.032). The overall rate of postoperative complications was similar (12.5 vs. 12.8%, p = 0.963), with no significant differences in individual complications. However, the ICA group experienced earlier recovery with a shorter time to first flatus (median 2.5 vs. 3.0 days, p = 0.017) and hospital stay (median 6.0 vs. 7.0 days, p = 0.024), along with consistently lower pain scores from postoperative day 0 to day 3 (all p < 0.001).ConclusionICA appears to be a safe and effective technique for SP robotic right colectomy. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) has become a standard in colorectal cancer treatment, offering benefits such as reduced pain, quicker recovery, and improved cosmetic outcomes [1–3]. Among MIS techniques, intracorporeal anastomosis (ICA) has gained attention due to favorable outcomes over extracorporeal anastomosis (ECA), including reduced bowel traction and smaller incisions that lower surgical trauma and enhance recovery [4–8]. However, ICA demands advanced laparoscopic skills and carries a risk of intra-abdominal contamination, which has limited its widespread adoption [7, 9].

Robotic systems have facilitated the adoption of ICA by providing enhanced visualization and dexterity compared to conventional laparoscopy [5, 10–14]. More recently, single-port (SP) robotic platforms like the da Vinci SP system are designed to minimize invasiveness and optimize cosmetic outcomes through a single 25-mm multichannel port. They also allow for lower abdominal incisions, such as the Pfannenstiel, which are associated with improved cosmesis and fewer wound-related complications [14–20]. While ICA can be effectively performed using the SP system, the lack of integrated staplers and energy devices introduces technical challenges, requiring assistance for stapling and vessel sealing, which may affect anastomotic quality and procedural efficiency [7, 11, 21–23].

Nevertheless, whether these technical limitations translate into clinically meaningful differences remains unclear. To date, only a few studies have directly compared ICA and ECA in SP robotic right colectomy. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the short-term outcomes of ICA versus ECA in SP robotic right colectomy across two tertiary referral centers.

Methods

Patient selection and clinical variables

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Asan Medical Center and Ulsan University Hospital from November 2020 to February 2025. We included adult patients (18–85 years) with colon cancer who underwent elective robotic right colectomy using the da Vinci SP system. Patients were excluded if they underwent preoperative colonic stenting or required emergency surgery, had clinical T4b disease, benign pathology, an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status ≥ 4, or incomplete clinical data. Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained (IRB no. 2022-0257).

Demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic records, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), ASA score, tumor location and stage, operative details, and pathological features. Right-sided colon cancer was defined as tumors from the cecum to distal transverse colon. Acute recovery parameters, such as numeric rating scale (NRS) pain scores, were assessed on POD 0–3. Other recovery variables included time to first flatus, time to soft diet, patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) opioid use, and rescue analgesic use. Postoperative complications, such as wound complications, ileus, anastomotic leakage, bleeding, pulmonary or urinary events, and extended antibiotic use, were recorded within 30 days postoperatively.

Since anastomosis time was not routinely recorded in the database, it was retrospectively measured by reviewing available surgical videos and anesthesia records for a subset of patients (ICA group: n = 30; ECA group: n = 19).

Operative procedure

Surgeries were performed by seven experienced colorectal surgeons using the da Vinci SP system. The main port was placed at the umbilicus or Pfannenstiel incision, with an assistant port nearby. The choice between ICA and ECA depended on each patient’s condition and the surgeon’s preference. Generally, ECA was preferred for larger tumors to ensure safe margin and extraction, while ICA was preferred for patients with high BMI to minimize incision-related complications. In ICA, after central vascular ligation and mesocolic excision, bowel transection was performed according to oncologic principles and confirmed using intraoperative indocyanine green (ICG) perfusion imaging (Fig. 2). An endoscopic stapler (Signia Stapling System; Covidien, Tokyo, Japan or ECHELON; Ethicon Inc., Somerville, NJ, USA) was used to divide the terminal ileum and transverse colon. The specimen remained in the abdominal cavity until anastomosis completion. The terminal ileum and colon were aligned and joined side-to-side—either isoperistaltically or antiperistaltically—with an endoscopic stapler inserted through the assistant port. Continuous suction was applied during the procedure to minimize intra-abdominal contamination. The enterotomy was closed in two layers: a continuous full-thickness layer followed by an additional seromuscular suture using absorbable barbed sutures such as Monofix PDO (Samyang Biopharmaceuticals Corp., Daejeon, Korea), Quill (Angiotech Pharmaceuticals, Vancouver, Canada), or V-Loc (Medtronic, Mansfield, MA, USA). In ECA, after mobilizing the bowel and performing central vessel ligation, the terminal ileum and proximal colon were exteriorized via a ~ 5 cm midline incision with a wound protector (SurgiTractor; SurgiCore, Gwangju, Korea). Mesocolon division and vessel ligation were performed extracorporeally. The anastomosis was completed using either side-to-side or end-to-side stapled techniques, or an isoperistaltic end-to-end hand-sewn method, based on the surgeon’s judgment and intraoperative findings.

Statistical analysis

Clinicopathological factors and perioperative outcomes were compared between the two groups. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed, as pain scores at each postoperative time point were analyzed as clinically independent endpoints. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (Version 28.0, IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

The ICA group had a longer operative time than the ECA group (175.5 min [IQR 148.8–235.2] vs. 159.5 min [IQR 135.0–189.5], p = 0.032). Specifically, in a sub-analysis of cases with available video or anesthetic records (ICA: n = 30, ECA: n = 19), the median anastomosis time was significantly longer in the ICA group (21.5 min [IQR 19.0–29.5]) than in the ECA group (12.0 min [IQR 11.0–15.0]; p < 0.001). Most ICA procedures were performed as side-to-side anastomoses, either isoperistaltic or antiperistaltic (50.0% each), while the majority of ECA procedures were end-to-side anastomoses (80.8%). There were no significant differences in conversion rates (3.1 vs. 1.3%, p = 0.511), estimated blood loss (25.0 mL [IQR 5.0–61.2] vs. 10.0 mL [IQR 5.0–55.0], p = 0.582), or transfusion rates (0.0 vs. 6.4%, p = 0.143). Specimen extraction in the ICA group was primarily through an umbilical incision (56.2%) or Pfannenstiel incisions (25.0%), while the ECA group predominantly used the umbilical site (96.2%). There were no significant differences in pathologic tumor characteristics between the two groups. The median tumor size was 3.1 cm [IQR 2.5–4.3] in the ICA group and 3.6 cm [IQR 2.5–5.4] in the ECA group (p = 0.299). Pathologic stages and the number of yielded lymph nodes (≥ 12 in all cases) were comparable between groups. The distribution of pathologic T stages was comparable (p = 0.362). Pathologic N stages also showed no significant difference (p = 0.329). The median number of harvested lymph nodes was comparable (21.5 [IQR 17.8–32.5] vs. 22.0 [IQR 18.0–29.8], p = 0.774), confirming that adequate lymphadenectomy was performed in both approaches. Postoperative complications occurred in 12.5% of ICA cases and 12.8% of ECA cases, with no significant difference (p = 0.963). Surgical site infections (SSI) were classified into superficial and organ/space infections. In the ICA group, there was 1 case (3.1%) of superficial SSI. In the ECA group, there were 3 cases (3.8%) of organ/space SSI, which included 2 cases of anastomotic leakage and 1 case of intra-abdominal abscess. Anastomotic leakage occurred in 2 cases (2.6%) and ileus in 3 cases (3.8%) in the ECA group, while urinary complications were noted in 1 case (3.1%) in the ICA group. No statistically significant differences were found for individual complications. Discussion

This study investigated whether ICA using the da Vinci SP system offers clear advantages over ECA in robotic right colectomy. We found that ICA using the da Vinci SP system in robotic right colectomy is associated with improved postoperative recovery outcomes, including significantly reduced pain scores and faster return of bowel function, and a shorter hospital stay, without increasing postoperative complications. These findings suggest that ICA is not only feasible in the SP robotic setting but also clinically advantageous despite the known limitations of the platform.

Previous studies in laparoscopic and multiport robotic right colectomy have established the benefits of ICA in terms of faster bowel function, smaller incisions, and improved pain control [4–8, 10, 12–14, 21, 24]. Our findings support and extend these conclusions to SP robotic surgery. The consistently lower pain scores and earlier time to first flatus observed in the ICA group align with prior reports and likely reflect reduced bowel traction, minimized mesenteric manipulation, and avoidance of excessive extraction force—particularly advantageous in obese patients [18, 19].

We acknowledge that the significant reduction in pain scores in the ICA group may be partially attributed to the frequent use of Pfannenstiel incisions (25.0%) compared to the ECA group, which predominantly relied on umbilical extensions (96.2%). Previous studies have demonstrated that Pfannenstiel incisions offer superior pain control and cosmetic outcomes compared to midline incisions [18, 19]. However, it is important to emphasize that the ability to select a Pfannenstiel incision for specimen extraction is a direct structural advantage of the ICA approach. Unlike ECA, which often requires extending the umbilical wound for bowel exteriorization and anastomosis, ICA allows the surgeon to complete the procedure intracorporeally and choose the most advantageous extraction site. Therefore, although incision location represents a confounding factor, the benefit of using a Pfannenstiel incision is closely related to the technical advantages provided by the ICA approach. From a technical perspective, the Pfannenstiel incision provides an additional advantage in SP robotic surgery by offering a more favorable working distance to the anastomotic site, within the optimal working range of the SP system (approximately 10–27 cm). This may facilitate safer and more comfortable anastomosis and suturing, particularly in patients with a thick abdominal wall, and may contribute to improved operative stability and surgeon ergonomics, although it is difficult to quantify objectively in a retrospective analysis.

Although the SP robotic platform was developed to overcome the ergonomic and visualization limitations of single-port laparoscopy [2, 15, 25], it introduces unique technical challenges due to the absence of an integrated stapler or energy device [7–9, 11, 21, 22, 26]. In our study, an endoscopic stapler was used via the assistant port, and suction was applied to prevent intra-abdominal contamination. Despite the need for an assistant port due to current instrumentation limitations, the port is small and placed laterally, preserving the cosmetic and pain-reduction benefits of the single-site main incision compared to the larger extraction wounds required in ECA or multi-port approaches. Importantly, the assistant port functioned as an adjunct rather than a substitute, allowing critical phases of the operation to be performed entirely by the SP instruments. This aligns with our previous multicenter study comparing the da Vinci SP and Xi systems for colon cancer, which demonstrated that the SP system offers significant advantages in terms of smaller incision lengths, reduced postoperative pain, and shorter hospital stays [27]. Even with these constraints, ICA resulted in no anastomotic leaks and comparable complication rates as ECA, supporting its safety when performed in experienced centers.

The total operative time was longer in the ICA group (175.5 min) compared to the ECA group (159.5 min), partly due to the longer anastomosis time required for ICA (21.5 min vs. 12.0 min). This finding is consistent with previous reports on multi-arm robotic systems, where intracorporeal anastomosis generally requires additional operative time [28]. Nevertheless, operative times observed in the present study were comparable to those typically reported for multi-arm robotic ICA. This suggests that the single-port platform’s technical constraints, such as the reliance on an assistant port for stapling, do not excessively prolong the surgical procedure.

This study has limitations. The retrospective nature and absence of randomization may introduce selection bias. Surgical approach was based on surgeon discretion, potentially influenced by patient characteristics and intraoperative findings. Additionally, the relatively small number of ICA cases may limit generalizability. Although both isoperistaltic and antiperistaltic side-to-side anastomoses are associated with good short- and long-term results [29], incision selection may still influence postoperative pain and recovery outcomes. Comorbidities were significantly more frequent in the ECA group, which could have influenced postoperative outcomes independent of anastomotic technique.

In conclusion, ICA using the da Vinci SP system is a safe and effective technique for robotic right colectomy, providing measurable benefits in pain control, bowel recovery, and hospital stay without increasing postoperative complications. As experience with SP platforms grows, ICA may become a promising anastomotic option in selected patients undergoing single-port robotic colorectal surgery. Prospective studies are needed to further define its role, refine the technique, and optimize patient selection.
