Enhancing enzyme activity of heterologously expressed Chit42 in Nicotiana benthamiana for chitin degradation
Authors: Naser Mirhosseini Motlagh, Mostafa Motallebi, Zahra Moghaddassi Jahromi, Lukhanyo Mekuto
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The activity of the chitinase42 enzyme was enhanced by addition of ChBD using SOEing PCR.•Nicotiana benthamiana was used as a platform for enzyme expression.•The expression was influenced by methyl jasmonate via spray induction.•Chimeric chitinase42 showed greater activity than native chitinase42 without ChBD.

Introduction

Chitin, which is composed of N-acetylglucosamine, is the second most abundant biopolymer in nature. This biopolymer is insoluble and resistant to degradation. Chitin is found in the exoskeletons of insects, crabs, shrimp, lobsters, fungi, yeast, diatoms, nematodes, crustaceans, and other invertebrates. The percentage of chitin varies among different organisms, with the highest concentrations found in crabs and shrimp, which contribute to 90 % of chitinous waste [1]. This waste poses a significant environmental problem, particularly for the aquaculture industry, which struggles with the disposal of chitin. If not adequately managed, chitin waste can severely threaten the environment and biodiversity. In shellfish industry waste, about 20–58 % of the dry weight is chitin [2]. A sustainable solution to this issue is to repurpose chitinous waste as a renewable raw material for producing valuable products that can be utilized in the aquaculture, textile, and medical industries [3]. Chemical degradation of chitin typically involves hydrolysis using strong acids like hydrochloric acid (HCl). However, this method presents several challenges, including chemical modification of the product, high costs, low yields, and the generation of acidic waste [4]. The large quantities of chemical waste produced by these processes harm the environment. These issues make direct acid hydrolysis commercially unviable, not only for technical reasons but also due to environmental concerns. However, an effective alternative is the enzymatic hydrolysis of chitin using chitinases [5]. Chitinase produced by fungi has garnered significant attention, as fungal chitinases are promising enzymes for this application. However, most naturally occurring fungi do not produce high quantities of chitinase, and their activity can be further enhanced through protein engineering, such as adding a chitin-binding domain (ChBD).

Improving the efficiency of chitinase enzymes and producing them cost-effectively is crucial. Plant-based recombinant protein production offers several advantages over other expression systems, such as bacteria, yeast, and animal cells. The benefits of using plants include low cost [6], high expression levels in a short time [[6], [7]], proper post-translational modifications such as glycosylation [6,8], and enhanced safety by eliminating the risk of disease transmission from the protein-producing host to humans [9,10].

Agroinfiltration has been widely used for protein production, studying protein functions, and investigating plant metabolisms [11,12]. One of its main advantages over stable transformation procedures is its speed. While stable transformation integrates the new gene directly into the plant's chromosomes, agroinfiltration avoids this. This bypasses the issue of "position effects" where the location of the inserted gene can impact its expression [13]. However, agroinfiltration efficiency varies significantly between plant species. For instance, high expression is achievable in Nicotiana benthamiana but not necessarily in other plants [14].

The objective of this research was to study the effect of chimeric chitinase42, expressed in N. benthamiana, on chitin degradation. Therefore, a cassette containing chitinase42 genomic DNA fused to ChBD to its N-terminal domain under the control of an inducible promoter, was constructed. The study further aimed to conduct an agrobacterium-mediated transformation via the transient expression method in N. benthamiana. The expressed chimeric protein was used to evaluate the degradation of chitin.

Materials and methods

Plants and microorganisms

Seeds of N. benthamiana were kindly provided by T. Lohrasebi, NIGEB, Tehran, Iran. For cloning procedures, the Escherichia coli strain DH5α (Cinnagen, Iran) and for plant transformation, Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 (kindly provided by M. A. Malboubi, NIGEB, Tehran, Iran) were used.

Media, growth conditions and general procedures

Bacteria were cultured in Luria-Bertani (LB) medium, consisting of Bacto-tryptone (10 g/L), Bacto-yeast extract (5 g/L), and NaCl (10 g/L), under appropriate conditions: E. coli at 37 °C and A. tumefaciens at 28 °C, both at a shaking speed of 200 rpm. General molecular biology procedures, including preparation of plasmid and plant genomic DNA and transformation, were performed according to standard protocols [15].

The chimeric chitinase42 was synthesized using Splicing by Overlap Extension (SOEing) PCR and XhoI-PF1/R3-SacI primers (Fig. 1). Initially, equimolar amounts of two fragments (F1 and F2) were mixed for overlap extension PCR, conducted without additional primers. This process involved seven cycles: denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing at 54 °C for 1 min, and elongation at 72 °C for 1.2 min. The resulting fusion product was amplified using XhoI-pfI/R3-SacI primers (Table 1) under identical PCR conditions. The amplified fragment was verified through PCR using specific primers and PCR-based Restriction Fragment Length Polymorphism (PBR) analysis, followed by confirmation of its nucleotide sequence via DNA sequencing.

Following purification, the fragment was inserted into the XbaI/SacI sites of the pGDDEE vector [17], resulting in the construction of pGDDEENM1 (Fig. 1) for transient expression in N. benthamiana. As a negative control, genomic DNA containing the signal peptide and pre-pro region of from T. atroviride was amplified using XhoI-pfI/R3-SacI primers with pMJH1 [18] as the template. This fragment was cloned into the XbaI/SacI sites of pGDDEE, yielding the construct designated as pGDDEEJN1 (Fig. 1).

Transient expression in N. benthamiana

The constructs pGDDEENM1, containing the chimeric chitinase42, and pGDDEEJN1, harboring chitinase42, were transformed into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. The transformed Agrobacterium strains were cultured overnight at 28 °C in LB medium supplemented with rifampicin (30 µg/mL) and kanamycin (50 µg/mL) with shaking at 200 rpm. A 1/5 % aliquot of this overnight culture was used to inoculate 10 mL of LB medium supplemented with 10 mM MES (2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid) buffer (pH 5.7), 50 µg/mL kanamycin, and 150 µM acetosyringone (3,5-dimethoxy-4′‑hydroxy-acetophenone). These pre-cultures were grown overnight at 28 °C with a shaking speed of 200 rpm. Cells were harvested by centrifugation (3000 g, 5 min), resuspended, and diluted to an OD600 of 0.2 in an injection solution containing 10 mM MgSO4, 10 mM MES (pH 5.7), and 150 µM acetosyringone. By using a needleless syringe, this solution was infiltrated into the leaves of greenhouse-grown N. benthamiana plants. The three primary leaves of 6-week-old plants were targeted for infiltration, which is preferably conducted in the late afternoon to ensure T-DNA transfer overnight [19]. After infiltration, the plants were placed in a 24 °C growth room under dark conditions for 16 h. All chemicals and antibiotics were purchased from Merk (Germany) and Sigma-Aldrich (USA).

Induction of inducible promoter

To assess the promoter’s efficiency in response to inducers, agro-injected leaves were treated with methyl jasmonate (200 μM) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and chitin (600 μg/mL) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) two days post-infiltration using two methods: spraying and injection, as depicted in Fig. 2. For the spray method, the inducers were applied twice a day. For the injection method, the inducers were introduced into the leaves using a needleless syringe once. Following induction, the plants were covered with transparent plastic domes for 16 h.Fig. 2Experimental design to optimize gene expression conditions in plant leaves using methyl jasmonate and chitin in different DPIs and by two methods (spray and injection).Fig 2

Chitinase activity

Total soluble protein was extracted from the leaf tissue of injected plants. Liquid nitrogen used for freezing the leaf tissue. It was ground into a fine powder in a sterile mortar with liquid nitrogen, and transferred to a vial. Soluble proteins were extracted using a 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 7.0).

Chitinase activity was assessed in a reaction mixture (total volume 500 µL) containing 3.8 mg non-colloidal chitin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as the substrate and crude enzyme extracts from transformed leaves expressing chitinase (200 µg/mL). The reaction was carried out at 37 °C for 1 hour, followed by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was boiled with 100 µL of potassium tetraborate buffer for 3 min. Next, 3 mL of DMAB reagent [10 g of dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in 100 mL of glacial acetic acid (87.5 % v/v) and 10 M hydrochloric acid (12.5 % v/v)] was added to the reaction mixture. After incubation at 37 °C for 20 min, the amount of reducing sugar produced by the enzyme reaction was determined using the Boller and Mauch method [20] with N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) as the standard. Each assay was performed in three replicates. The concentration of total soluble protein was measured using the Bradford method [21] with bovine serum albumin (BSA) as the standard.

pH and temperature optima

Determination of the optimal pH was achieved by incubating chitin with the enzymes in buffers covering a pH range of 3 to 9, using sodium citrate for pH 3 to 7, sodium phosphate for pH 8, and sodium carbonate for pH 9. At first the effect of pH on enzyme activity is evaluated in temperature 37 °C, therefor, the optimal pH is determined. Then, in obtained optimal pH, the effect of different temperatures on enzyme activity is examined to find the optimal temperature among 10 to 70 °C with 10 °C intervals. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The collected data were analyzed using an analysis of variance (ANOVA) test in SPSS software. Duncan’s multiple range test (P < 0.01) was employed to compare the significance of different means. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

The ChBD region was positioned after the signal peptide and pre-pro region of chitinase42. Consequently, following the expression of the recombinant protein and removal of the signal peptide and pre-pro region, the ChBD is located at the N-terminal of the mature expressed protein. The constructed cassette was placed under the control of an inducible synthetic promoter containing DDEE cis-acting elements and a minimal promoter (SP-DDEE) in the pGDDEE binary vector [17]. The resulting construct (pGDDEENM1) (Fig. 1) was mobilized into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101 and subsequently used for transient transformation of N. benthamiana leaves. To confirm the successful agroinfiltration of transformed N. benthamiana leaves and the accurate construction of the SOEing PCR cassette, the expected PCR-amplified DNA bands were detected via electrophoresis (Fig. 4). Overall, the findings highlight the effectiveness of the spray method, particularly on 5 DPI, in achieving optimal enzymatic activity for the chimeric chitinase42 in N. benthamiana. Moreover, the chimeric chitinase42 demonstrates superior activity in chitin degradation than chitinase42.

Discussion

Chitin, a naturally abundant biopolymer, has captured the interest of researchers and industry due to its potential applications in bioengineering, agriculture, and the food industry [23]. Its non-toxic nature, good biocompatibility, and sheer volume estimated at 1012–1014 tons produced as waste annually make it a highly attractive resource [[24], [25], [26]]. The chemical degradation of chitin using strong acids such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) comes with significant challenges. One major concern is the chemical modification of the resulting product, which can alter its properties [4]. Additionally, the process is costly and yields relatively low amounts of the desired product. Also, this method generates large volumes of acidic waste, posing a substantial risk to ecosystems. Improper disposal of such waste can lead to environmental contamination [4]. Due to these drawbacks, direct acid hydrolysis is not a commercially practical solution for chitin degradation, necessitating the exploration of alternative, more sustainable methods. However, chitinase degradation is recently adopted as an effective enzymatic hydrolysis of chitin [5,27]. Chitinase enzymes, particularly those derived from fungi, are highly efficient in this process which makes fungal chitinases a promising and sustainable option for large-scale chitin recycling [28].

The purpose of this study was to produce a Chimeric chitinase42 enzyme, which contains a chitin-binding domain (ChBD), in a plant expression system. Since the intron-containing gene cannot be expressed in Agrobacterium, its genomic DNA containing three short introns was used, which can only be expressed in plants. In previous study, the enzyme was expressed from cDNA form of this gene in E.coli and tested for its antifungal activity [29]. Earlier attempts at expressing recombinant proteins in plants often used constitutive promoters, leading to plant growth problems [30]. Therefore, this study opted for an inducible promoter system, which has distinct advantages [31]. This system utilizes specific DNA elements (cis-acting elements) fused with a minimal promoter to activate gene expression in planta upon induction [32,33]. Our previous study demonstrated that the inducible promoter (SP-DDEE) responded well to elicitors like chitin and methyl jasmonate [22]. This study employed the SP-DDEE promoter to drive the expression of our engineered chimeric chitinase42. Traditional protein production methods using mammalian cell cultures face limitations. Their high cost and restricted scalability hinder their ability to meet the rising global demand. Plants offer a promising alternative as a production platform for recombinant proteins. The plant-based system is safer and more cost-effective than current models [34].

Among these plant-based options, a preferred method is rapid transient expression. Compared to complex bioreactors, plants provide a more natural and sustainable process for protein production [35]. Large-scale protein production can be achieved using existing agricultural infrastructure, eliminating the need for expensive and time-consuming bioreactor expansion [36]. Unlike bacteria, plants can generate complex functional eukaryotic proteins. This is due to their ability to perform proper post-translational modifications, such as glycosylation, which are crucial for protein function [8]. Plant-based production can be achieved through two main methods: stable transformation, where the desired gene is integrated into the plant genome, or transient expression, offering a faster alternative approach. Transient expression offers a significant advantage in terms of speed compared to generating stable transgenic plants [12,[13], [37]]. Creating and selecting stable lines can take up to a year, whereas transient expression bypasses this lengthy step [36]. Both biolistic and Agrobacterium-based methods can be used for stable transformation and transient expression in various plant species [38]. However, Agrobacterium-based transformation emerges as the preferred method for transient protein production due to minimal tissue damage compared to biolistic transformation, which can significantly reduce the biomass available for protein extraction. Over the past three decades, Agrobacterium-mediated gene delivery has experienced significant development [39]. Among these advancements, agroinfiltration has gained widespread recognition as the most promising technology for transient expression [[11], [12],14,40]. A key benefit of transient expression systems is the absence of the position effect, leading to higher transgene expression than stable transformation [36]. Furthermore, transient expression utilizes non-transgenic plant materials, eliminating the risk of transgene escape from genetically modified (GM) plants to non-GM crops or wild relatives. This fact significantly reduces regulatory hurdles and public concerns associated with this technology.

Since higher expression is achievable by agroinfiltration of N. benthamiana in comparison to other plants [14], transient expression of chitinase enzymes was carried out in this plant via induction of inducible promoter by methyl jasmonate or chitin in different DPIs. Our findings demonstrated the superiority of spray application over injection in inducing enzyme expression. Specifically, spraying methyl jasmonate at 5 DPI resulted in a remarkable 2.47-fold increase in chimeric chitinase42 activity compared to the injection method. The significantly elevated chitinase activity observed in sprayed plants suggests that foliar spraying allows for a wider distribution of the elicitor, leading to a more comprehensive activation of inducible promoters. These results underscore the potential of methyl jasmonate spraying as a promising strategy for promoter induction to produce enzyme expression.

This study analyzed the improvement of chitinase enzyme activity by fusing a Chitin-Binding Domain (ChBD) derived from T. atroviride chitinase18.10 to the N-terminus of chitinase42 genomic DNA using SOEing PCR. The chimeric chitinase42 exhibited greater activity than the chitinase42 enzyme when tested with crystalline chitin. Our findings align with the previous studies by Kowsari et al. [16] which reported a similar result, increasing activity when adding the ChBD to a stably expressed chitinase in the fungus T. atroviride [16]. Also, Matroodi et al. [41] observed similar finding of boosting enzyme activity by combining a ChBD from Serratia marcescens ChiB with chitinase42 expressed in E. coli [42].

The highest enzymatic activity was observed at an optimal temperature of 40 °C and pH 4. For both enzymes, activity significantly declined as the pH and temperature decreased or increased. Previous studies have shown that fungal chitinases exhibit higher activity in acidic conditions compared to basic pH, consistent with the findings of this study. Reported optimal pH values include pH 4 for chitinases from Verticillium lecanii A3 [43], pH 5.0 for Penicillium oxalicum [44] and pH 6.2 for Pyrus communis [45]. Similarly, temperature optima of 40 °C have been reported for chitinases from Bacillus cereus CH2 and Monascus purpureus CCRC31499 [46]. Similar temperature and pH optima of the chimeric chitinase42 and chitinase42 provide further evidence supporting the hypothesis that incorporating ChBD does not change the active site of chitinase42 [42,42].

Overall, the results obtained in this study demonstrated that the expression of the chimeric chitinase42 gene, under the control of the SP-DDEE synthetic inducible promoter, provides an effective practical approach to successfully express the recombinant proteins using a plant platform via transient transformation. Comparison of the expressed enzyme by method in this study with expression of chimeric enzyme in T. atroviride [16] and in E. coli ([42]a), showed a higher expression of the enzyme in this platform. The findings of this study indicate that the engineered chimeric chitinase42 with ChBD retains stability and activity in acidic conditions. This engineered chitinase gene has the potential to serve as cost-effective and an efficient chitinolytic tool. This approach offers a promising platform for plant-based production of valuable proteins with potential applications to develop solutions for various environmental issues.

Conclusion

This study provides a compelling method for enhancing the activity of chitinase enzyme through incorporating of ChBD, resulting in the synthesis of chimeric chitinase42 expression, under the control of a synthetic inducible promoter. The resulting chimeric chitinase42 demonstrated increased enzyme activity over chitinase42 for chitin degradation. The optimal temperature and pH for the highest enzyme activity showed to be 40 °C and pH 4. Moreover, this research explored the transient expression of chitinase in N. benthamiana plants, leveraging an inducible promoter activated by methyl jasmonate or chitin at various DPIs. Our findings highlight the superiority of the spray method over the injection method in enhancing chitinase activity, with the most notable result being a 2.47-fold increase in enzyme levels when methyl jasmonate was sprayed at 5 DPI. Therefore, we recommend using the spray method, particularly with methyl jasmonate at 5 DPI, to optimize chitinase enzyme expression in similar applications. Nevertheless, future studies should focus on the long-term structural effects of ChBD fusion such as thermostability, and protease sensitivity to deepen the mechanistic insights.
