Safety evaluation of the food enzyme thermomycolin from the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain AR‐201
Authors: EFSA Panel on Food Enzymes (FEZ), Holger Zorn, José Manuel Barat Baviera, Claudia Bolognesi, Francesco Catania, Gabriele Gadermaier, Ralf Greiner, Baltasar Mayo, Alicja Mortensen, Yrjö Henrik Roos, Marize L. ABSTRACT
AbstractThe food enzyme thermomycolin (EC 3.4.21.65) is produced with the genetically modified Trichoderma reesei strain AR‐201 by AB Enzymes GmbH. The genetic modifications do not give rise to safety concerns. The food enzyme was considered free from viable cells of the production organism and its DNA. The food enzyme is intended to be used in seven food manufacturing processes. Dietary exposure was estimated to be up to 0.461 mg total organic solids (TOS)/kg body weight (bw) per day in European populations. Genotoxicity tests did not indicate a safety concern. The systemic toxicity was assessed by means of a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats. The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level of 1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested, which, when compared with the estimated dietary exposure, resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 2169. A search for the homology of the amino acid sequence of the thermomycolin to known allergens was made and matches with 1 food allergen, 19 respiratory allergens and 3 contact allergens were found. The Panel considered that a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to the food enzyme cannot be excluded. Based on the data provided, the Panel concluded that this food enzyme does not give rise to safety concerns, under the intended conditions of use.

INTRODUCTION

Article 3 of the Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 1 provides definition for ‘food enzyme’ and ‘food enzyme preparation’.

‘Food enzyme’ means a product obtained from plants, animals or microorganisms or products thereof including a product obtained by a fermentation process using microorganisms: (i) containing one or more enzymes capable of catalysing a specific biochemical reaction; and (ii) added to food for a technological purpose at any stage of the manufacturing, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of foods.

‘Food enzyme preparation’ means a formulation consisting of one or more food enzymes in which substances such as food additives and/or other food ingredients are incorporated to facilitate their storage, sale, standardisation, dilution or dissolution.

Before January 2009, food enzymes other than those used as food additives were not regulated or were regulated as processing aids under the legislation of the Member States. On 20 January 2009, Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes came into force. This Regulation applies to enzymes that are added to food to perform a technological function in the manufacture, processing, preparation, treatment, packaging, transport or storage of such food, including enzymes used as processing aids. Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 2 established the European Union (EU) procedures for the safety assessment and the authorisation procedure of food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. The use of a food enzyme shall be authorised only if it is demonstrated that: it does not pose a safety concern to the health of the consumer at the level of use proposed;there is a reasonable technological need;its use does not mislead the consumer.

Background and Terms of Reference as provided by the requestor

Background as provided by the European Commission

Only food enzymes included in the Union list may be placed on the market as such and used in foods, in accordance with the specifications and conditions of use provided for in Article 7(2) of Regulation (EC) No 1332/2008 on food enzymes.

On 12 January 2023, a new application has been introduced by the applicant “AB Enzymes GmbH” for the authorisation of the food enzyme Serine endopeptidase from a genetically modified Trichoderma reesei (strain AR‐201).

Terms of Reference

The European Commission requests the European Food Safety Authority to carry out the safety assessment and the assessment of possible confidentiality requests of the following food enzyme: Serine endopeptidase from a genetically modified Trichoderma reesei (strain AR‐201), in accordance with Regulation (EC) No 1331/2008 establishing a common authorisation procedure for food additives, food enzymes and food flavourings. 3

Interpretation of the Terms of Reference

The present scientific opinion addresses the European Commission's request to carry out the safety assessment of the food enzyme serine endopeptidase from T. reesei strain AR‐201.

In the technical dossier the applicant named this food enzyme as serine endopeptidase and indicated that its catalytic activity is described by EC 3.4.21.65 according to the IUBMB classification system. Therefore, the EFSA scientific opinion reports this food enzyme in accordance with the IUBMB classification system (EC 3.4.21.65) and the IUBMB accepted name thermomycolin is used throughout the opinion.

Production of the food enzyme

The food enzyme is manufactured according to the Food Hygiene Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, 14 with food safety procedures based on Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points, and in accordance with current Good Manufacturing Practice. 15

Purity

The lead content in the three commercial batches and in the batch used for toxicological studies was below 0.05 mg/kg which complies with the specification for lead as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). In addition, arsenic, mercury and cadmium concentrations were below the limits of quantification (LoQs) of the employed methods. 26 , 27

The food enzyme complies with the microbiological criteria for total coliforms, Escherichia coli and Salmonella, as laid down in the general specifications for enzymes used in food processing (FAO/WHO, 2006). No antimicrobial activity was detected in any of the tested batches. 28

Strains of Trichoderma, in common with most filamentous fungi, have the capacity to produce a range of secondary metabolites (Frisvad et al., 2018). The presence of T‐2 and HT‐2 toxins was examined in all food enzyme batches and was below the LoQ of the applied methods. Adverse effects caused by the possible presence of other secondary metabolites are addressed by the toxicological examination of the food enzyme–TOS. 29 , 30

The Panel considered that the information provided on the purity of the food enzyme was sufficient.

Toxicological data

A battery of toxicological tests including an in vitro mammalian cell gene mutation test using the thymidine kinase gene (a mouse lymphoma assay), an in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test and a repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rats has been provided.

Batch 4 (Table 1) used in these studies has a similar protein pattern and a similar activity/TOS ratio as the batches used for commercialisation, and thus is considered suitable as a test item.

A bacterial reverse mutation test (Ames test) was not provided. According to the applicant, serine endopeptidases can release free amino acids during the Ames test. A screening test, performed with Salmonella Typhimurium strain TA100 with or without metabolic activation, showed a concentration‐dependent increase in the number of visible colonies, resulting in revertant colonies, probably due to released histidine. The applicant also expected that the precipitation of peptides may occur during the performance of the modified treat and wash method. Therefore, in this case, instead of an Ames test, an in vitro mammalian cell gene mutation test using the thymidine kinase gene (the mouse lymphoma assay (MLA)) was provided, as recommended in the EFSA guidance (EFSA CEP Panel, 2021). 33

Genotoxicity

In vitro mammalian cell gene mutation test using the thymidine kinase gene

The in vitro mammalian cell gene mutation test using the thymidine kinase gene (mouse lymphoma assay) was performed according to the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development (OECD) Test Guideline 490 (OECD, 2015) and following Good Laboratory Practice (GLP). 34 The assay was performed with the mouse lymphoma cell line L5178Y. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix)

Based on the results of the pre‐experiment, the cells were exposed to eight concentrations of food enzyme at 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, 1400, 1600 and 1800 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term 4‐h treatment without S9‐mix and at 250, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500, 1750 and 2000 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term 4‐h treatment with S9‐mix.

Cytotoxicity was 85.5% at 1800 μg TOS/mL (based on relative total growth (RTG) = 14.5%) in a short‐term treatment without S9‐mix and 57% at 2000 μg TOS/mL (RTG = 43%) in a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix.

In a short‐term treatment without S9‐mix, the mutant frequencies were statistically significantly increased over those of the negative controls at concentrations of 1600 and 1800 μg TOS/mL. As the corresponding induced mutant frequency (IMF) was clearly lower than the global evaluation factor (GEF) of 126, this effect was considered as not biologically relevant.

In a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix, the mutant frequency was statistically significantly increased over those of the negative controls at concentration of 1000 μg TOS/mL. As the increase was not concentration dependent and the corresponding IMF was clearly lower than the GEF of 126, this effect was considered as not biologically relevant.

Colony sizing was performed at three concentrations of the food enzyme. In a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix, the percentage of small colonies was found to be 19%, 24% and 13% at 1500, 1750 and 2000 μg TOS/mL, respectively. In a short‐term treatment without S9‐mix, the percentage of small colonies was found to be 26%, 26% and 40% at 1400, 1600 and 1800 μg TOS/mL, respectively.

The study was considered reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme thermomycolin did not induce gene mutations under the test conditions applied in this study.

In vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test

The in vitro mammalian cell micronucleus test was carried out according to the OECD Test Guideline 487 (OECD, 2014) and following GLP. 35 Two separate experiments were performed with duplicate cultures of human peripheral whole blood lymphocytes. The cell cultures were treated with the food enzyme with or without metabolic activation (S9‐mix).

In the pre‐experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme at concentrations from 7.8 to 5000 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment (4‐h exposure and 40‐h recovery period) without or with S9‐mix. Cytotoxicity of 85% and 65% was seen at 5000 μg TOS/mL without and with S9‐mix, respectively.

Based on these results, in the first experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for the frequency of binucleated cells with micronuclei (MNBN) at concentrations of 1000, 2500 and 2750 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment without S9‐mix and at concentrations of 2000, 3000 and 3500 μg TOS/mL in a short‐term treatment with S9‐mix.

In the second experiment, cells were exposed to the food enzyme and scored for MNBN at concentrations of 25, 100, 300 and 400 μg TOS/mL in a long‐term treatment (44‐h exposure without recovery period) without S9‐mix.

In the short‐term treatment, cytotoxicity (based on the decrease of the proliferation index) of 65% and 60% was observed at the highest concentration tested with and without S9‐mix, respectively. A cytotoxicity of 56% was reported in the long‐term treatment without S9‐mix. The frequency of MNBN was not statistically significantly different to the negative controls at all concentrations tested.

The study was considered reliable without restrictions and the results of high relevance.

The Panel concluded that the food enzyme thermomycolin did not induce an increase in the frequency of MNBNs under the test conditions applied in this study.

Repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study in rodents

The repeated dose 90‐day oral toxicity study was performed under GLP and according to the OECD Test Guideline 408 (OECD, 1998). 36

Groups of 10 male and 10 female Wistar Crl: WI(Han) rats received the food enzyme by gavage in doses of 100, 300, 600 or 1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day (referred further as low, lower medium, higher medium and high doses). Controls received the vehicle (water for injection).

One high‐dose male and two high‐dose females were found dead on days 15, 73 and 50, respectively, and one higher medium‐dose female was found dead on day 64. Based on histopathological changes observed, the Panel considered mis‐dosing as the cause of death for two animals. For the other rats found dead, the cause of death could not be established due to autolysis, nevertheless, the deaths were considered unrelated to the test item due to absence of previous clinical signs of toxicity and of histological changes in any other animal in the study.

In the clinical observations, an increased incidence of salivation (in 3/10 high‐dose males and 10/10 high‐dose females), nasal discharge (in 3/10 high‐dose males and 2/10 high‐dose females), abnormal breathing (in 2/10 lower medium‐ and 5/10 high‐dose females) were observed. The Panel considered the changes as possibly related to the local irritant effect of the test item and not to systemic toxicity.

The body weight was statistically significantly decreased at different time points in low‐dose males (−8% to −14%), in lower medium‐dose males (−9% to −13%), in higher medium‐dose males (−9% to −13%) and in high‐dose males (−10% to −12%). The body weight gain was statistically significantly decreased on days 1–8 in low‐dose males (−17%), lower medium‐dose males (−23%) and higher medium‐dose males (−19%), on days 36–43 in higher medium‐dose males (−43%) and high‐dose males (−53%), and a total for the treatment period (days 1–90) in all treated males (−22%, −19%, −19%, −19%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as, they were only recorded randomly, they were only observed in one sex, there was no dose–response relationship and the changes were within the historical control values.

In the functional observations, some statistically significant differences from controls were observed. In weeks 3 and 4, the salivation score was increased (+20% on both occasions) and the spontaneous activity score was decreased (−20% and −15%) in high‐dose females; in week 13, the score of sleeping was increased in lower medium‐, higher medium‐ and high‐dose females (+150% each), the score for supported rearing position was decreased (−22%), urination was increased (+178%) and body temperature was lower (−0.5%) in lower medium‐dose females, and the response to handling score was decreased in low‐dose females (−28%). In high‐dose males, the score for spontaneous activity was decreased in weeks 6 and 8 (−17% and −14%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as, they were only recorded sporadically, they were only observed in one sex (mean score of sleeping, response to handling, occurrence of a supported rearing position, urination, average body temperature) and there was no dose–response relationship (mean score of sleeping, response to handling, occurrence of a supported rearing position, urination, average body temperature).

Haematological investigations revealed a statistically significant increase in mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) in high‐dose males (+6%), haemoglobin (Hb; +7%) and haematocrit (Ht; +7%) in higher medium‐dose females, percentage of neutrophils (% Neu; +35%) in lower medium‐dose females, a decrease in percentage of basophils in lower medium‐ and higher medium‐dose males (% Baso; −50%, −60%) and prothrombin time (PT) in low‐, lower medium‐ and higher medium‐dose group (−9%, −6%, −6%). The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as, they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (Hb, Ht, % Neu, % Baso, PT), the changes were small (all parameters), there were no changes in other relevant parameters (other red and white blood cell parameters, activated partial thromboplastin time), there were no histopathological changes in lymphohaematopoietic tissues and the changes were within the historical control values.

Clinical chemistry investigations revealed a statistically significant decrease in serum creatinine in high‐dose males (−40%). The Panel considered the change as not toxicologically relevant as, it was only observed in one sex, there were no changes in other relevant parameters (urea), there were no histopathological changes in kidneys and the change was within the historical control values.

Statistically significant changes detected in organ weights were a decrease in absolute kidney weight (−11%) and absolute prostate gland weight (−15%) in lower medium‐dose males, a decrease in absolute liver weight in lower medium‐ and high‐dose males (both −13%), an increase in relative brain weight in lower medium‐ and higher medium‐ and high‐dose males (+13%, +13%, +14%) and relative heart weight in higher medium‐dose males (+12%) were reported. The Panel considered the changes as not toxicologically relevant as they were only observed in one sex (all parameters), there was no dose–response relationship (all parameters except for relative brain weight), there were no histopathological changes in these organs and the changes were within the historical control values (all except the relative brain weight).

No other statistically significant or biologically relevant differences from controls were reported.

The Panel identified a no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) of 1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day, the highest dose tested.

Allergenicity

The allergenicity assessment considered only the food enzyme and not additives, carriers or other excipients that may be used in the final formulation.

The potential allergenicity of the thermomycolin produced with the T. reesei strain AR‐201 was assessed by comparing its amino acid sequence with those of known allergens as described in the EFSA GMO Scientific Opinion (EFSA GMO Panel, 2010). Using higher than 35% identity in a sliding window of 80 amino acids as the criterion, matches with 1 food allergen, 19 respiratory allergens and 3 contact allergens were found in the AllergenOnline database. 37

The matching food allergen was Bac s 1 (53.7% sequence identity), a nattokinase from Bacillus subtilis.

Among the 19 matching respiratory allergens were alkaline serine proteases of Penicillium chrysogenum and P. citrinum group 13 allergens (63.8%–71.3% sequence identity), e.g. Pen ch 13. Further matches were alkaline serine proteases of Aspergillus versicolor, A. flavus, A. oryzae and A. fumigatus group 13 allergens (53.8%–58.0% sequence identity), e.g. Asp v 13. In addition, matches to subtilisins from Bacillus spp. (46.9%–48.8% sequence identity) and the subtilisin‐like serine proteases of Rhodotorula mucilaginosa, Fusarium proliferatum, Cladosporium spp., Alternaria alternata, Penicillium spp. and Aspergillus spp. (55.5%–60.0% sequence identity) were observed.

The three matching contact allergens were subtilisin‐like serine proteases (66.2% sequence identity) from different Trichophyton species, e.g. Tri r 2 from the Athlete's foot fungus Trichophyton rubrum.

No reports on oral or respiratory sensitisation or elicitation reactions of the thermomycolin under assessment have been published. 38

Bac s 1, the subtilisin‐like serin protease nattokinase from B. subtilis, has been identified as a food allergen. Few case reports of allergic reactions after consumption of natto have been reported (Inomata et al., 2012; Suzuki et al., 2023).

Considering the matches with respiratory allergens, the Panel notes that several studies have shown that individuals respiratorily sensitised to a food enzyme are usually able to ingest the corresponding enzyme without acquiring clinical symptoms of food allergy (Armentia et al., 2009; Cullinan et al., 1997; Poulsen, 2004).

Considering the matches with contact allergens, no reports of allergic reactions to these allergens after oral exposure have been published.

No allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to any thermomycolin have been reported in the literature. 39

The Panel considered that the results of the sequence homology search and the available literature indicate a risk of allergic reactions for natto allergic individuals upon dietary exposure to the thermomycolin under assessment.

■■■■■, a product from ■■■■■ that may cause allergies or intolerances (listed in the Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 40 ) is used as a raw material. During the fermentation process, this product will mostly be degraded and utilised by the production strain.

The Panel considered that residual amounts of allergenic proteins could be present in the food enzyme. Taking into account the level of dietary exposure (see Section 3.5.2), this would result in minute amounts in the final foods, from which allergic reactions are usually not expected.

In conclusion, the Panel considered that under the intended conditions of use, a risk of allergic reactions upon dietary exposure to this food enzyme, particularly for natto allergic individuals, cannot be excluded. However, the likelihood of such reactions will not exceed the risk of reactions after natto consumption.

In the production of modified milk proteins, the food enzyme is added to dairy‐derived raw materials (e.g. whey, casein) for hydrolysis 41 to increase digestibility. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the modified milk proteins.

In the production of modified meat and fish products, the food enzyme is added to meat or fish during grinding 42 to increase protein solubility and the emulsifying capacity of the resultant meat and fish extracts. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the meat and fish extracts. 43

In the production of protein hydrolysates, the food enzyme is added to a variety of protein‐rich materials from plant or animal by‐products (e.g. collagen, bones, skins, hides) in the hydrolysis step 44 to improve the yield. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final protein hydrolysates.

In the production of baked products and cereal‐based products other than baked, the food enzyme is added to flour during the preparation of the dough or batter. 45 The thermomycolin cleaves peptide bonds in the gluten network, thus reducing stiffness and the processing time. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the final food products (e.g. bread, cakes, biscuits, pasta, breakfast cereals).

In the processing of yeast and yeast products, the food enzyme can be added at different stages to yeast cultures, autolysed yeast, yeast extract or yeast cell walls. 46 The hydrolysis by thermomycolin increases yields and reduces production time. The food enzyme–TOS remain in the yeast products. Yeast extracts are used as an ingredient to enhance the umami taste in a wide range of foods, such as soups and savoury sauces.

Based on data provided on thermostability (see Section 3.3.1) the Panel considered that the food enzyme may remain in its active form in all food manufacturing processes listed in Table 2, depending on the heat treatment conditions during the respective food manufacturing process.

The conservative approach applied to estimate the dietary exposure to the food enzyme–TOS, in particular assumptions made on the occurrence and use levels of this specific food enzyme, is likely to have led to an overestimation of the exposure.

Margin of exposure

A comparison of the NOAEL (1000 mg TOS/kg bw per day) identified from the 90‐day rat study with the derived exposure estimates of 0.020–0.202 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the mean and from 0.053 to 0.461 mg TOS/kg bw per day at the 95th percentile resulted in a margin of exposure of at least 2169.

CONCLUSIONS

Based on the data provided and the derived margin of exposure, the Panel concluded that the food enzyme thermomycolin produced from the genetically modified T. reesei strain AR‐201 does not give rise to safety concerns under the intended conditions of use.

The Panel considered the food enzyme free from viable cells of the production organism and its DNA.
