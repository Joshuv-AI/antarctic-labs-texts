Modifiable health-risk patterns among patients with back pain: a latent class analysis
Authors: Mikhail Saltychev, Konsta Koivunen, Juhani Juhola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited information exists on how modifiable health risks cluster among patients with back pain. The aim of this study was to identify latent classes defined by selected modifiable-risk indicators and to describe the clinical and demographic characteristics of these classes.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational survey included consecutive patients with back pain referred to a university-hospital outpatient clinic of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine between April 2014 and February 2017. Latent class analysis was used to identify classes based on four dichotomous modifiable-risk indicators: current smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, and physical inactivity. Demographic characteristics, pain severity, and disability were used to describe the identified classes.ResultsThe analysis included 1,379 patients; 881 (64%) were women and the mean age was 47.9 (15.0) years. A two-class solution was selected because it showed better AIC and BIC than the one-class model, while the three-class model did not converge. Entropy was modest (0.466), indicating limited individual-level classification certainty. The two classes were differentiated mainly by physical inactivity: the estimated probability of physical inactivity was 0.38 (95% CI 0.28 to 0.48) in Class 1 and 0.90 (95% CI 0.82 to 0.95) in Class 2. Smoking and excessive alcohol consumption showed smaller differences between classes, while obesity was more common in Class 2. Lower educational attainment and severe pain were associated with a higher probability of belonging to Class 2.ConclusionIn this specialized rehabilitation-clinic sample, LCA identified two modifiable-risk pattern classes that differed mainly by physical inactivity. The findings are exploratory and cross-sectional; they should not be interpreted as evidence that physical inactivity causes worse back pain or disability. Further studies are needed to examine whether similar patterns are present in primary care and community settings and whether addressing physical inactivity improves outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10256-7.Keywords: Latent class analysis, Back pain, Health risk behaviors, Smoking, Alcohol drinking, Obesity, Sedentary behavior

Background

Modifiable risk factors increase the probability of developing a disease [1]. They can be changed, treated, or controlled. Unlike non-modifiable factors, such as age, genetics, or sex, modifiable factors may be influenced through lifestyle changes, medical interventions, or environmental changes. Commonly recognized examples include obesity, physical inactivity, excessive alcohol consumption, and smoking. These risk factors have been implicated in the development and exacerbation of musculoskeletal disorders (MSK). In this context, the range of relevant factors should also include MSK-specific exposures, such as poor posture, repetitive movements, occupational hazards, and inadequate ergonomic design of work environments [2]. Recent studies have suggested that modifiable risks may be overshadowed by current MSK symptoms and by their treatment and rehabilitation [3]. This is unfortunate because many of these risks can be reduced or, in some cases, removed. Musculoskeletal disorders are also linked to health conditions beyond the joints and muscles. The relationship between chronic systemic diseases and MSK appears to be bidirectional: MSK can provoke or exacerbate systemic disease, while systemic disease can increase the severity of MSK symptoms [4]. Therefore, reducing modifiable risks related to MSK may, in some situations, also influence systemic diseases with substantial public-health implications.

A recent systematic review evaluated associations between back pain and lifestyle-related risks, such as BMI, insomnia, smoking, alcohol consumption, and sedentary behavior, and concluded that these modifiable risks may contribute to the development of back pain [5]. An older review from the 1980s suggested that hard physical work, particularly frequent lifting and postural stress, and motor-vehicle driving are likely to contribute to disc degeneration, low back pain, and sciatica [6]. Obesity, smoking, and psychological distress were also associated with chronic back pain, but their causal roles remain uncertain. Obesity has inconsistently predicted outcomes during the 12 months after the onset of acute back pain [7]. Obesity, occupational or ergonomic risk factors, and smoking have been suggested as major global risks for chronic back pain when calculating years lived with disability (YLDs) [8]. The relative contribution of obesity in this context has increased more rapidly than that of other modifiable factors. The same review stated that the role of physical inactivity still needs to be clarified. In an analysis of more than one million people from the general population, back pain was significantly associated with smoking, obesity (BMI > 30 kg/m2), and alcohol abuse [9]. While the incidence of back pain has been associated with obesity, smoking, and strenuous physical work, walking or cycling to work has been associated with a reduced risk of back pain [10]. Ageing, obesity, and physical inactivity have been associated with poorer paralumbar muscle health and, further, with back pain [11]. Although BMI has generally been identified as a risk factor for chronic back pain, this relationship is not consistent across all studies [12–14]. High body weight, but not BMI, has also been suggested to be significantly associated with chronic back pain [15]. Obesity and sleep difficulties have predicted the development of back pain, whereas physical activity has shown a protective association [16].

The evidence on the role of different modifiable risks in back pain is often heterogeneous. Several explanations have been suggested for these inconsistent associations. One explanation is that traditional epidemiological study designs may not adequately account for genetic, environmental, and other confounding factors [17, 18]. A twin study suggested that associations between modifiable lifestyle risks and chronic back pain (CBP) are mostly explained by familial and genetic confounding [17]. In that study, only severe obesity, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder were considered independent risk factors for developing back pain. Different modifiable risks may also have different roles across age groups [16]. Strong evidence of confounding has been reported for age, female sex, educational level, marital status, financial strain, anxiety, fear avoidance, and catastrophizing [18].

The aim of this study was to identify latent classes defined by selected modifiable health-risk indicators among patients with back pain and to describe the demographic, pain-related, and disability characteristics of these classes. The purpose was not to identify causal risk factors for developing back pain, because all patients included in the analysis already had back pain.

Materials and methods

This was a cross-sectional observational survey of consecutive patients with musculoskeletal conditions referred to an outpatient Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine clinic at a university hospital between April 2014 and February 2017. The questionnaire was completed before the physician appointment and included questions on demographics, pain intensity, functioning, perceived general health, work ability, and physical activity.

The present analysis included patients with back pain who completed the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI). ODI completion was used to identify patients with back-pain-related disability in the survey dataset. Patients without ODI data were not included in this analysis. The ODI was used to characterize disability in the sample, not to define the latent classes.

Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by squared height and expressed in kg/m2. For the LCA, obesity was defined as BMI > = 30 kg/m2. Smoking was defined as current smoking (yes/no). Excessive drinking was defined as alcohol consumption > = 210 g/week. Physical inactivity was defined as < 15 MET-h/week. These four binary variables were used as the indicators defining the latent classes.

Education, occupational status, and pain severity were used to describe the classes. Educational status was dichotomized as high school vs. no high school. Occupational status was dichotomized as managers/professionals vs. technicians/manual workers. Average pain severity was measured on an 11-point numeric rating scale from 0 (“no pain”) to 10 (“most severe pain”) and dichotomized as < 7/10 vs. >=7/10 points. These cut-points were chosen to create clinically interpretable binary variables for an exploratory LCA and class-description analysis. We acknowledge that dichotomization reduces information and may influence model results.

The ODI contains 10 items covering disability caused by low back pain. Each item is scored from 0 (“no limitation”) to 5 (“extreme limitation or inability to function”). The ODI total score is expressed as a percentage from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating greater disability. The ODI was not used as a latent class indicator.

The self-administered 12-item WHODAS 2.0 was used as a generic measure of functioning. It covers common limitations of functioning in the general population during the preceding 30 days. Each item is scored from 0 (“no limitation”) to 4 (“extreme limitation or inability to function”), and the total score ranges from 0 to 48. The WHODAS was not used as a latent class indicator.

Statistical analysis

The descriptive statistics were reported as means and standard deviations (SD) or as frequencies and percentages, as appropriate. The main statistical method was latent class analysis (LCA). The LCA model used four dichotomous modifiable-risk indicators: smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, and physical inactivity. Models with one, two, and three classes were estimated. Model selection was based on convergence, clinical interpretability, Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), entropy, and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood-ratio test when available. Lower AIC and BIC values were considered to indicate better relative model fit. The three-class model did not converge. Latent class models were estimated using Bernoulli-logit models in Stata/IC 19. Random class-membership starting values were generated using 100 random draws with a fixed seed. For each random draw, 50 EM iterations were used to identify the best starting values before final maximum-likelihood estimation. Final estimation allowed up to 1,000 iterations, with coefficient-vector tolerance set at 1 × 10⁻⁶, log-likelihood tolerance at 1 × 10⁻⁷, and scaled-gradient tolerance at 1 × 10⁻⁵. Convergence was assessed from Stata’s convergence status and iteration logs. Therefore, the one- and two-class solutions were compared, and the two-class solution was retained. Because entropy was modest, class membership was interpreted cautiously at the group level rather than as a precise classification of individual patients.

After class estimation, the estimated probability of each modifiable-risk indicator was reported for each class with 95% confidence intervals. Class labels were assigned post hoc based on the observed item-response probabilities. Demographic and clinical characteristics were then compared descriptively across classes. All analyses were conducted using Stata/IC Statistical Software, Release 19 (StataCorp LP, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

The analysis included 1,379 patients. Overall, 881 (64%) were women and the mean age was 47.9 (15.0) years (Table 1). The majority (76%) were cohabiting. High-school education was present in 398 (30%) patients and 192 (14%) belonged to the higher occupational grade of managers and professionals. Smoking was reported by 358 (27%) patients, obesity by 483 (35%), excessive drinking by 224 (16%), and physical inactivity by 459 (33%). The mean BMI was 28.0 (SD 5.5) kg/m2. The mean ODI score was 34.0 (SD 16.4), indicating a moderate level of back-pain-related disability, and the mean WHODAS 2.0 score was 14.0 (SD 9.4).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that two modifiable-risk pattern classes could be identified in this specialized rehabilitation-clinic sample. The classes did not represent broad or strongly separated lifestyle profiles. Instead, they were differentiated mainly by physical inactivity, with a lower probability of physical inactivity in Class 1 and a higher probability in Class 2. Obesity was also more common in Class 2, whereas smoking and excessive alcohol consumption differed less clearly between classes.

Although modifiable-risk profiles in patients with back pain have not, to our knowledge, previously been studied using LCA, this method has been used to identify clusters of modifiable risks in other health conditions. In studies of behavioral and metabolic risk factors for chronic noncommunicable diseases, distinct latent classes have been identified on the basis of alcohol use and metabolic risks [19]. Age, male sex and lower educational level were associated with higher probability of being classified in a class with higher risk of getting sick Age, male sex, and lower educational level were associated with a higher probability of belonging to a higher-risk class. Among college students, smoking, physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, binge drinking, and obesity have been studied in relation to cancer risk [20]. Distinct classes were identified especially on the basis of unhealthy diet and physical inactivity, with clustering differing by race. A study assessing risks for serious chronic diseases, such as cancer, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes, identified several classes characterized by differences in alcohol consumption, substance use, and physical inactivity [21]. Another study reported several LCA classes among Indigenous Australians, based on dietary habits, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, drug use, and obesity [22]. In a study of modifiable lifestyle-related behaviors and colorectal cancer risk, distinct classes were observed based on obesity, physical inactivity, meat consumption, smoking, alcohol consumption, and fruit and vegetable consumption [23]. Men had a higher probability of being classified in high-risk classes. Several distinct classes have also been identified among middle-aged adults with two or more potential dementia risk factors [24]. These classes differed by hyperlipidemia, low cognitive activity, and obesity. A study from China examined modifiable risks related to multimorbidity [25]. Several classes were observed based on sleep duration, physical activity, alcohol intake, smoking status, and body mass index.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. First, the design was cross-sectional. Therefore, the analysis cannot determine whether physical inactivity contributed to greater pain and disability, whether pain and disability reduced physical activity, or whether both were influenced by unmeasured factors. The identified classes should therefore be understood as patterns of coexisting measured risks rather than causal pathways. Second, entropy was modest, and the three-class model did not converge. This limits confidence in assigning individual patients to classes and supports a group-level, exploratory interpretation. Third, all risk indicators were dichotomized to create clinically interpretable LCA indicators. This approach simplifies interpretation but may reduce information and influence class formation. Fourth, several potentially important modifiable or partially modifiable factors were not available, including sleep disturbance, psychological distress, fear avoidance, occupational loading, ergonomic exposures, and comorbidity burden. The classes therefore do not represent comprehensive biopsychosocial risk profiles. Fifth, the study was conducted in a single specialized university-hospital rehabilitation clinic. Patients referred to such a clinic may differ from patients with back pain in primary care, occupational health, surgical spine clinics, or community settings. Generalizability to these settings is uncertain.

The results may still be clinically useful as a descriptive signal. In this sample, physical inactivity was the clearest characteristic distinguishing the two classes. This does not prove that increasing physical activity would improve pain or disability, but it suggests that physical activity status is worth measuring and reporting in similar clinical datasets. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to examine whether these risk-pattern classes are stable over time, whether they predict outcomes, and whether interventions addressing physical inactivity or other modifiable risks can change clinical trajectories.

Recent work in lumbar spinal stenosis also illustrates the complex relationship between disability measures and functional impairment. Regmi et al. reported that ODI and the narrowest dural sac diameter independently predicted claudication distance in lumbar canal stenosis [26]. This finding is relevant as background evidence that patient-reported disability and condition-specific clinical features may both contribute to functional limitations. However, imaging data were not available in the present study, and our sample was not restricted to patients with lumbar spinal stenosis.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional sample of patients with back pain referred to a specialized rehabilitation clinic, LCA identified two modifiable-risk pattern classes based on smoking, excessive alcohol consumption, obesity, and physical inactivity. The classes were differentiated mainly by the probability of physical inactivity, and to a lesser extent by obesity. Because the design was cross-sectional, the findings do not establish causality or show that targeting physical inactivity would improve outcomes. Similar analyses in other clinical settings and longitudinal studies are needed to determine the stability, generalizability, and prognostic relevance of these patterns.
