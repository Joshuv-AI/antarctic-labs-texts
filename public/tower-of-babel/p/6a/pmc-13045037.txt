Dual-center validation of a multi-sequence MRI radiomics nomogram for noninvasive assessment of renal fibrosis in chronic kidney disease
Authors: Tingting Zha, Liang Pan, Jie Chen, Lei Peng, Yanan Du, Zhenxing Jiang, Zhiping Zhang, Wei Xing
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate, noninvasive assessment of renal fibrosis (RF) in chronic kidney disease (CKD) remains challenging. This study aimed to develop and externally validate a dual-center multi-sequence MRI radiomics nomogram integrating imaging and clinical parameters for evaluating RF severity.MethodsThis retrospective dual-center study included 164 patients with CKD who underwent multi-sequence MRI and renal biopsy, divided into a training set (n = 128) and an external test set (n = 36). Radiomics features were extracted from intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) and blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) MRI. Feature selection involved inter-observer correlation coefficient, Mann–Whitney U test, Pearson correlation coefficients, and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. Three radiomics models (Rad_IVIM, Rad_BOLD, Rad_IVIM+BOLD) and one clinic model were developed to distinguish mild from moderate-to-severe RF. A nomogram was constructed by integrating the Rad_IVIM+BOLD score with significant clinical variables. Model performance was evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), DeLong test, decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration curves.ResultsThe Rad_IVIM+BOLD model achieved AUCs of 0.898 (95% confidence intervals [CI]: 0.845–0.951) in the training set and 0.749 (95% CI: 0.584–0.914) in the test sets, showing a favorable performance trend compared with the single-sequence radiomics models. The nomogram demonstrated good discrimination with AUCs of 0.922 (95% CI: 0.876–0.969) and 0.861 (95% CI: 0.728–0.993) in the training and test sets, respectively. The DeLong test showed its superiority over the clinic and radiomics models. DCA and calibration curves further confirmed its high predictive value and robustness.ConclusionA nomogram combining multi-sequence MRI radiomics and clinical features provides a noninvasive tool for assessing RF severity in CKD, supporting quantitative imaging-guided clinical decision-making and disease follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02234-1.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global health problem, characterized by high prevalence, increasing incidence, and substantial socioeconomic burden [1]. Regardless of etiology, progressive kidney injury is often accompanied by maladaptive repair processes, leading to dysregulated fibrogenesis and ultimately renal fibrosis (RF) [2]. RF plays a pivotal role in CKD progression, and its severity has been strongly associated with adverse renal outcomes. Patients with moderate-to-severe RF are at a significantly increased risk of progression to end-stage renal disease. Therefore, accurate assessment of RF severity is essential for disease monitoring and treatment planning.

Currently, renal biopsy is considered the gold standard for evaluating RF. However, its invasiveness, sampling bias, and limited ability to capture the heterogeneous distribution of fibrosis restrict its routine clinical use. On the other hand, commonly used clinical indicators such as serum creatinine (SCr) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) reflect functional decline only at relatively late stages of kidney damage. These limitations underscore the urgent need for noninvasive, accurate, and reproducible methods to assess RF.

With advances in imaging technology, functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a promising noninvasive tool for characterizing renal tissue properties [3–4]. In particular, intravoxel incoherent motion (IVIM) MRI enables simultaneous assessment of water diffusion and microperfusion [5], whereas blood oxygenation level-dependent (BOLD) MRI provides information on renal tissue oxygenation [6]. Given that microcirculatory impairment and tissue hypoxia are key mechanisms driving the onset and progression of RF [7–8], these imaging techniques hold considerable potential for fibrosis evaluation. However, conventional manual region of interest (ROI) analysis often overlooks subtle or spatially heterogeneous tissue alterations associated with RF.

Radiomics, through the extraction of high-throughput quantitative features from medical images, enables a more comprehensive characterization of tissue heterogeneity [9]. Radiomics-based approaches have been increasingly applied across a broad spectrum of diseases, enabling quantitative characterization of tissue heterogeneity for purposes such as disease staging, noninvasive diagnosis, phenotypic differentiation, risk stratification, prognosis assessment, and treatment response evaluation [10–11]. Previous studies have demonstrated the value of radiomics based on ultrasound [12–16], computed tomography [17–20], and single-sequence MRI [21–22] in diagnosing diabetic nephropathy, IgA nephropathy, and RF. Nevertheless, the diagnostic performance of multi-sequence MRI radiomics—particularly those incorporating IVIM and BOLD sequences reflecting renal microcirculation and oxygenation—for evaluating RF severity remains unclear.

In this study, we aimed to develop a multi-sequence radiomics model derived from IVIM and BOLD MRI, and to construct a nomogram by integrating radiomics features with clinical parameters, in order to enhance the diagnostic performance and clinical applicability of RF assessment.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective dual-center study was approved by the respective institutional review boards and ethics committee, and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature.

Medical records of patients diagnosed with CKD from two centers between August 2020 and February 2025 were reviewed. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age between 18 and 75 years; (2) diagnosis of CKD confirmed according to the KDIGO 2024 guidelines [23]; (3) percutaneous renal biopsy performed; (4) renal MRI including IVIM and BOLD sequences completed within one week prior to the biopsy; and (5) serum and urine samples obtained on the day of MRI scan. Exclusion criteria were: (1) history of renal transplantation; (2) renal lesions > 1 cm in diameter on MRI; (3) contraindications to MRI (e.g., metallic implants or severe claustrophobia); or (4) poor MRI image quality. Clinical data collection and pathology analysis

Collected clinical variables included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), 24-hour proteinuria, serum albumin, total protein, and fasting blood glucose.

Renal biopsies were obtained from the lower pole of the right kidney and stained with Masson’s trichrome. Histopathological evaluation was performed by a pathologist (L.P., with 11 years of experience in kidney pathology), who was blinded to the clinical and imaging data. Three non-overlapping fields (×100 magnification) per patient were randomly selected and fibrosis areas (blue-stained) were quantified using ImageJ software (version Fiji, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). The average proportion of fibrotic area was calculated. Fibrosis severity was graded according to the T score of the Oxford MEST-C classification [24]: T0 (≤ 25%, mild), T1 (26–50%, moderate), and T2 (> 50%, severe). For binary classification, T0 was defined as mild fibrosis, and T1-T2 as moderate-to-severe fibrosis.

Image acquisition

MRI scans were performed on a 3.0-T scanner (Discovery MR 750 W, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) with a 32-channel phase array body coil. The imaging protocol included IVIM and BOLD sequences. (1) IVIM: Coronal diffusion-weighted imaging with TR = 2000 ms, TE = 112 ms, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 360 × 360 mm², matrix size = 64 × 32, bandwidth = 125 Hz/pixel, b-values = 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 300, 500, 800, 1000, and 1300 s/mm², and scan time = 202 s. (2) BOLD: Coronal multi-echo gradient-echo sequences with TR = 11.5 ms, TEs = 1.9/4.6/7.4/10.1/12.9/15.6/18.4/21.1 ms, slice thickness = 5 mm, FOV = 360 × 360 mm², matrix size = 256 × 192, bandwidth = 83.3 Hz/pixel, and scan time = 55 s.

IVIM data were post-processed using MITK software (Medical Imaging Interaction Toolkit, German Cancer Research Center, Heidelberg, Germany) to generate parametric maps of the true diffusion coefficient (D), pseudo-diffusion coefficient (D*), and perfusion fraction (f). BOLD data were processed on the Advantage Workstation (GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) to generate T2* maps automatically.

Image segmentation

Image segmentation was performed using ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0; http://www.itksnap.org). ROIs encompassing the whole right renal parenchyma were manually delineated slice-by-slice to construct a three-dimensional volume of interest (VOI). For IVIM, ROIs were initially drawn on b = 0 s/mm² diffusion-weighted images and subsequently mapped onto the corresponding parametric maps (D, D*, and f). Two radiologists (T.T.Z. with 8 years and Y.N.D. with 5 years of experience in renal MRI), blinded to the clinical and pathological data, independently performed the segmentations.

Radiomic feature extraction

PyRadiomics, a Python open-source package (version 3.7.12; https://pyradiomics.readthedocs.io), was used to extract radiomic features from each VOI. Prior to extraction, all images were resampled to a uniform voxel size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm. Gray-level discretization was performed using a fixed bin width of 5, with intensity normalization enabled (normalize = true; normalizeScale = 100) and a voxel array shift of 300. Radiomics features were extracted in a three-dimensional (3D) manner from the entire VOI. All feature classes implemented in PyRadiomics were extracted, including first-order, shape, gray-level co-occurrence matrix, gray-level dependence matrix, gray-level run-length matrix, gray-level size zone matrix, and neighboring gray-tone difference matrix features. A total of 1197 radiomic features were extracted for each parametric map (D, D*, f, and T2*).

The selection of radiomics features included the following steps: (1) features with an inter-observer correlation coefficient (ICC) < 0.75 were excluded to ensure reproducibility. Remaining features were standardized using Z-score normalization; (2) univariate analysis using the Mann–Whitney U test was performed to identify features that significantly differed between the mild and moderate-to-severe RF groups (p < 0.05); (3) Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to address multicollinearity. For highly correlated feature (|r| ≥ 0.9), the feature with superior diagnostic performance was retained; (4) the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation selected features with non-zero coefficients for model construction.

Model construction and evaluation

Two machine learning algorithms, logistic regression (LR) and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), were used to construct the radiomics models. Three radiomics models were conducted: single-sequence IVIM, BOLD (Rad_IVIM, Rad_BOLD) and multi-sequence IVIM_BOLD (Rad_IVIM+BOLD).

For clinic models, variables significantly different between mild and moderate-to-severe RF in the training set were employed for model construction. Clinic models were conducted using the same machine learning algorithms applied in the radiomics models.

Performance comparisons were made between the two algorithms in each radiomics and clinic models. The optimal algorithm was employed for nomogram model construction by integrating selected Rad_IVIM+BOLD and clinic models.

Model performance was evaluated in the test set by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to assess its discriminative ability, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV); negative predictive value (NPV), precision, recall and F1 score were calculated. Comparisons of AUCs among different models were performed using the DeLong test. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was conducted to evaluate the clinical utility of the models. In addition, calibration curves and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test were used to assess model calibration and reliability. Figure 2 showed the study workflow.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The normality of quantitative measurements was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Continuous variables were compared using the independent sample t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test based on their distribution characteristics. Categorical variables were examined using Chi-square tests. The remaining analysis processes mentioned previously were executed utilizing Python 3.7.12 and R software (version 4.1.3; https://www.r-project.org/). A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 164 patients with pathologically confirmed CKD from two centers were ultimately included and divided into a training set (n = 128) and an external test set (n = 36). In the training set, there were 60 patients with mild RF and 68 with moderate-to-severe RF. The test set contained 17 patients with mild RF and 19 patients with moderate-to-severe RF. The baseline clinical characteristics across the sets were detailed in Table 1. Except for age, demographics and clinical variables were well-balanced between the training and test sets. In the training set, eGFR, BUN and SCr differed significantly between patients with mild and moderate-to-severe RF (all p < 0.01), and were therefore selected for constructing clinical models. Representative images of IVIM-derived D, D*, f maps, BOLD-derived T2* maps, and Masson’s trichrome staining (×200) for CKD patients with mild and moderate-to-severe RF were shown in Supplementary Figure S1.

Comparison of single- and multi-sequence radiomics models

After features selection, the Rad_IVIM, Rad_BOLD and Rad_IVIM+BOLD models were constructed using both LR and LightGBM algorithms. In the test set, the Rad_IVIM+BOLD model demonstrated favorable discriminative performance across both algorithms. Specifically, the LR-based Rad_IVIM+BOLD model achieved an AUC of 0.749, while the corresponding LightGBM model showed comparable performance (AUC = 0.714). Compared with the single-sequence radiomics models, the multi-sequence Rad_IVIM+BOLD model exhibited improved overall performance trends, reflecting the complementary information provided by IVIM- and BOLD-derived features. In the external test set, the confusion matrix suggested a slightly improved sensitivity for identifying moderate-to-severe cases at the default threshold, while maintaining reasonable specificity (Supplementary Figure S3). Considering its stable performance across the training and external test sets, together with its transparency and suitability for nomogram construction, the LR-based Rad_IVIM+BOLD model was selected for subsequent integration with clinical variables. The AUCs (95% CI) of the nomogram were 0.922 (95% CI: 0.876–0.969) in the training set and 0.861 (95% CI: 0.728–0.993) in the test set, outperforming both the Rad_IVIM+BOLD model [training: 0.898 (95% CI: 0.845–0.951); test: 0.749 (95% CI: 0.584–0.914)] and the clinic model [training: 0.720 (95% CI: 0.631–0.808); test: 0.703 (95% CI: 0.527–0.878)].

The DeLong test indicated that there were significant differences in AUC values between the Rad_IVIM+BOLD model and the clinic model, as well as between the nomogram model and the clinic model (all p < 0.01) in the training set. In the test set, although pairwise AUC comparisons did not reach statistical significance, the nomogram consistently showed improved discrimination, suggesting a meaningful performance trend (Fig. 5b, f). DCA revealed that both the Rad_IVIM+BOLD and nomogram models provided greater net benefit for clinical decision-making in the test set, with the nomogram offering the highest net benefit (Fig. 5c, g). Additionally, the calibration curves demonstrated that the nomogram model had a good fit, which was better than the Rad_IVIM+BOLD model and the clinic model (Fig. 5d, h).

Discussion

In this dual-center study, we developed a multimodal fusion model for the noninvasive and accurate prediction of RF severity in patients with CKD. Although several ultrasound-based radiomics models have recently been proposed for RF assessment [12–13, 25–27], their clinical utility may be limited by operator dependency and relatively low soft-tissue contrast. In contrast, fMRI-based radiomics provides more standardized image acquisition and comprehensive tissue characterization. Notably, relatively few studies have explored radiomics models that integrate multiple fMRI sequences, such as IVIM and BOLD, for evaluating RF. In this study, we extracted radiomics features from multi-sequence MRI and combined them with clinical variables to construct a radiomics-clinical nomogram. This combined model demonstrated improved discriminative performance compared to single-sequence or clinic models, supporting the potential added value of a multiparametric approach for grading RF severity.

Based on the biexponential fitting model, IVIM separately quantifies tissue diffusion and microperfusion, thereby more accurately revealing the interaction between water molecule motion and tissue structure [28]. Hu et al. [29] evaluated the severity of renal interstitial fibrosis using multiple diffusion MRI techniques and found that IVIM-derived perfusion fraction (f) achieved a diagnostic performance exceeding 0.8. However, conventional parameter-based analyses rely on averaged ROI measurements and may overlook valuable information from anatomical regions, such as the renal columns, and fail to capture the true spatial distribution of fibrosis due to the kidney’s complex architecture. Radiomics analysis of IVIM parametric maps allows high-dimensional characterization of signal heterogeneity related to microcirculatory impairment and fibrotic remodeling. Previous studies have demonstrated the clinical value of IVIM-based radiomics in assessing tumor lymphovascular invasion, predicting therapeutic response, and other related applications [30–33]. In this study, the Rad_IVIM model achieved an AUC of 0.848 (95% CI: 0.780–0.915) in the training set but showed reduced performance in the external test set 0.690 (95% CI: 0.506–0.875), indicating the limitations of single-parametric functional imaging approaches when applied in independent cohorts.

BOLD MRI utilizes deoxygenated hemoglobin as an endogenous contrast agent to assess tissue oxygenation and has been widely applied to investigate renal hypoxia. Ebrahimi et al. [34] developed an experimental model of renal artery stenosis and extracted renal oxygenation features using BOLD-MRI radiomics. Their results showed that the BOLD-radiomics model predicted post-percutaneous transluminal renal angioplasty GFR (r = 0.58, p = 0.007) and renal blood flow (r = 0.68, p = 0.001), demonstrating significant correlations with physiological measurements. These findings validated the sensitivity of BOLD-radiomics to renal cortical oxygenation patterns and highlighted its potential for spatially characterizing renal hypoxia noninvasively. In our study, the Rad_BOLD model showed slightly higher discriminative performance compared to Rad_IVIM, with the AUC of 0.709 (95% CI: 0.534–0.884) in the external test set. These findings suggest that oxygenation-related signal heterogeneity captured by BOLD radiomics may provide complementary information to diffusion- and perfusion-based metrics. Nevertheless, the performance of single-sequence models remained suboptimal, indicating that RF assessment based on a single physiological dimension may be insufficient.

To further explore the complementary roles of different functional MRI sequences, we constructed a multi-sequence radiomics model integrating IVIM- and BOLD-derived features. In the Rad_IVIM+BOLD model, 19 radiomics features were selected, with 6 features from IVIM-D map, 5 features from IVIM-D* map, 3 features from IVIM-f map, and 5 features from BOLD-T2* map. A higher Rad_IVIM+BOLD score may therefore represent a composite imaging phenotype characterized by reduced microvascular perfusion, restricted water diffusion, and spatially heterogeneous tissue oxygenation, which together correspond to a higher burden of RF. This interpretation is consistent with recent evidence from multiparametric MRI studies. Hua et al. [35] employed a multiparametric MRI framework incorporating diffusion-based parameters (ADC, IVIM-derived metrics) and relaxation-based mapping (T1 and R2*), and demonstrated that the combined evaluation of T1 mapping and diffusion imaging was more effective in assessing CKD severity and renal interstitial fibrosis than individual MRI parameters alone. This integrative characterization helps explain the improved discriminative ability of the multi-sequence radiomics model compared with single-sequence approaches.

To construct the clinic model, we incorporated three serum biomarkers (eGFR, BUN and SCr), which showed significant differences between mild and moderate-to-severe RF groups. Among these, eGFR is the most widely used laboratory marker for renal function in clinical practice. Although it does not directly reflect RF, numerous studies have demonstrated a close association between the extent of RF and renal function decline [36–37]. Therefore, eGFR contributed substantially to the nomogram’s assessment of RF. Notably, eGFR exhibited a strong inverse association with RF, whereas the Rad_IVIM+BOLD score was positively associated. These findings suggested that both impaired filtration function and microcirculatory damage contribute jointly to RF progression.

Subsequently, we integrated the multi-sequence radiomics signature with clinical variables to construct a radiomics–clinical nomogram. The integrated nomogram showed an increased AUC compared with the clinical model alone in the external test set, indicating an incremental diagnostic benefit beyond conventional laboratory markers. Although the improvement in AUC did not reach statistical significance in pairwise DeLong’s tests—a limitation likely attributable to the sample size of the external validation set—the consistent performance gain and favorable net benefit observed in DCA support the potential clinical relevance of the combined model. Overall, the nomogram provided an intuitive and comprehensive tool for individualized RF risk stratification, longitudinal disease monitoring, and the identification of patients who may benefit from closer surveillance or biopsy consideration.

This study had several limitations. First, although it was a double-center study, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. However, all included cases were histopathologically confirmed by renal biopsy, ensuring accurate labeling of fibrosis severity. Further multicenter studies with larger cohorts are warranted to further assess the robustness and clinical applicability of the proposed model. Second, renal segmentation did not differentiate between the renal cortex and medulla. This decision was primarily due to the challenges of delineating cortical and medullary boundaries on functional MRI, especially in patients with advanced fibrosis where cortical thinning is prominent. Third, this study primarily focused on radiomics features derived from functional MRI, without incorporating structural imaging features. While functional parameters are more sensitive to microcirculatory alterations, combining them with structural changes might further enhance the diagnostic performance and provide a more comprehensive assessment of RF. In addition, BOLD-derived T2* values may be affected by systemic factors such as hemoglobin concentration, anemia status, and medication use. Future studies incorporating hematologic data are warranted to further refine model robustness.

Conclusions

We developed and externally validated a nomogram model that combines microcirculation-based multi-sequence MRI radiomics with clinical variables for noninvasive assessment of RF severity in CKD. By capturing complementary information related to renal microcirculation and oxygenation, this approach may hold great potential for individualized assessment of RF and longitudinal monitoring.
