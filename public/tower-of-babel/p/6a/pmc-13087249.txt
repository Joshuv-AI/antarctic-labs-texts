Physics-guided estimation of freight vehicle loading status using digital tachograph data
Authors: Jihoon Tak, Jungyeol Hong, Dongjoo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurately distinguishing between empty and loaded freight vehicle operations is essential for improving logistics efficiency, reducing environmental impacts, and supporting evidence-based freight and infrastructure policies. In Korea, where empty running accounts for over 40% of truck trips, reliable load-status information can enable demand-driven routing, enhance freight origin–destination (OD) estimation, strengthen overloading enforcement, and optimize road maintenance strategies. This study proposes a scalable framework for load-status estimation using Digital Tachograph (DTG) data, a legally mandated system that provides high-frequency records of speed, acceleration, and engine RPM without requiring additional sensors. Physics-informed features—derived from vehicle dynamics, drivetrain behavior, and resistance forces—were constructed and used in a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) classifier to incorporate both predictive accuracy and uncertainty quantification. Empirical results show that the proposed method achieves an average accuracy of 85.3% when using 9-s averages, exceeding 90% at highway speeds, and approaching full accuracy when temporal aggregation is applied. These findings demonstrate not only the technical feasibility of DTG-based estimation but also its capacity for nationwide, real-time monitoring of freight operations. Beyond model performance, the results highlight the broader policy relevance of this approach. By leveraging existing DTG infrastructure, the method offers a cost-effective and field-deployable solution for enhancing freight system visibility, reducing empty running, improving sustainability, supporting overloading detection, and informing infrastructure management. Introduction

The loading status of freight vehicles, whether operating empty or carrying goods, represents a critical yet often underutilized source of information for freight policy and infrastructure management. High empty-running rates, which account for more than 40% of truck operations in Korea1, generate inefficiencies in logistics systems23,, increase fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions4–6, and accelerate pavement deterioration caused by cumulative heavy loads7,8. Accurately distinguishing between empty and loaded trips is therefore not only a technical challenge but also a prerequisite for designing evidence-based freight policies that improve efficiency, sustainability, and safety. At the system level, load-status information can inform demand-responsive vehicle routing, enhance freight origin–destination (OD) estimation, guide overloading enforcement, and optimize road maintenance priorities—applications with direct implications for both industry competitiveness and public welfare.

Despite its importance, existing approaches to monitoring freight vehicle loading status face substantial limitations. Conventional methods such as national freight traffic surveys and weigh-in-motion (WIM) stations provide useful information but are costly, limited in spatial coverage, and not scalable for real-time monitoring. Moreover, WIM systems are prone to measurement errors due to factors such as speed variation, temperature effects, and pavement deflection9,10. Alternative approaches that estimate vehicle mass from in-vehicle sensors, such as Controller Area Network (CAN) bus or torque signals, require additional hardware and controlled testing environments11,12. These characteristics limit their practicality for nationwide deployment and integration into policy decision-making frameworks.

This study addresses these challenges by leveraging Digital Tachograph (DTG) data—a legally mandated system for commercial freight vehicles in Korea. Since 2011, all newly manufactured freight trucks above a gross weight threshold have been required to install DTGs, creating a comprehensive nationwide database of vehicle operations. Similar to OBD systems in the United States and on-board units (OBUs) in Europe, DTGs record second-by-second data on speed, acceleration, braking, engine RPM, and GPS trajectories13–15. These data offer unique advantages in terms of granularity, frequency, and geographic coverage, while avoiding the need for additional sensing infrastructure. Importantly, DTGs provide a policy-relevant pathway for monitoring freight operations in real time, enabling applications in logistics planning, infrastructure management, and enforcement.

The objective of this study is to propose and empirically validate a scalable methodology for estimating the loading status of freight vehicles using DTG data. A Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) is employed to capture uncertainty in classification, thereby supporting risk-sensitive decision-making in operational and policy contexts. A distinctive contribution of the study lies in embedding mechanical principles—Newtonian acceleration, drivetrain performance, and driving resistance—into the analytical process, enabling interpretable and policy-relevant load-status estimation. Unlike previous Vehicle Mass Estimation (VME) research, which has largely emphasized precision under constrained conditions, this study demonstrates how legally mandated DTG data can be used to deliver scalable, real-time monitoring that directly informs freight and infrastructure policy. Importantly, the goal is not to develop a universally applicable model for all freight vehicle types, but to assess the feasibility of inferring loading status using only the minimal information available from DTG data under realistic operational constraints.

Rather than assuming access to sensor-rich on-board systems such as weight sensors, tire-pressure monitoring, or detailed CAN-bus torque signals, this study deliberately focuses on what can be inferred from the minimal set of DTG variables that are already mandated nationwide. Accordingly, the contribution of this study should be interpreted as demonstrating a transferable framework—rather than a single fixed model—that can be extended to other vehicle classes through retraining with appropriately labeled DTG data. In doing so, the proposed framework targets the practically relevant question of how far loading status can be monitored using only existing DTG infrastructure, without retrofitting additional hardware.

Section "Data collection and attribute identification" describes the DTG dataset and attribute construction. Section "Results and discussion" presents the BNN-based model, results, and policy implications. Section "Conclusion" concludes with a summary of findings and directions for future research.

Literature review

Estimating the loading status of freight vehicles has been a critical area of research because accurate knowledge of loading zones and states is vital for optimizing vehicle operations and ensuring infrastructure sustainability. Prior studies have approached this problem using diverse methodologies, primarily (i) computer vision–based methods and (ii) on-board sensor and telematics–based methods.

Computer vision–based loading status estimation

Computer vision–based studies typically use machine learning and computer vision techniques to identify freight vehicle loading statuses. Sun et al.16 employed deep learning and shape-detection algorithms, and Chen et al.17 used monocular cameras to determine loading status. These approaches demonstrate the potential of image processing for estimating loading status, despite their limitations in environmental sensitivity. Amorim et al.18 integrated advanced LiDAR sensors into automated systems to estimate loading zones for ore transport vehicles. Liu et al.19 developed a system to classify loading statuses at construction sites and thus provide operational statistics. However, these approaches have notable limitations. Their accuracy often depends on the quality of shape detection algorithms and is sensitive to environmental factors, such as lighting, weather conditions, and load type. These constraints limit their applicability in dynamic and diverse real-world scenarios. Beyond these specific applications, quantitative evaluations of camera-based parking guidance systems have reported substantial performance declines under adverse weather conditions. For example, Tschentscher et al.20, as summarized by Kaur et al.21, show that classification accuracy decreases from about 93% in rain to around 70% in snow for a video-based parking occupancy detector, highlighting the vulnerability of purely image-based methods to severe environmental conditions.

On-board sensor and telematics–based approaches

Sensor-based and data-driven research has also contributed significantly to the estimation of loading status. Zheng et al.22 developed a system for detecting and monitoring freight vehicle loading conditions, whereas Regehr et al.23 explored load distribution characteristics and calculated average payloads. Hernandez24 proposed a weight-based classification method for payload estimation across different freight vehicle categories. Real-time algorithms for determining the gross vehicle weight using load sensors were introduced by Liu and Liu25. Additionally, Yao et al.26 applied deep learning to predict and optimize uneven load distribution in mining trucks, considering its impacts on performance and operational lifespan. Acuna and Sosa27 used structure-from-motion (SfM) photogrammetry and commercial 3D image-processing software to measure timber loads automatically. Despite these advancements, sensor-based and data-driven methods face challenges, including reliance on specific sensor configurations, high costs, and limited generalizability across diverse operational environments.

Applications of loading status information

Developing reliable systems for estimating, detecting, and monitoring loading status provides numerous benefits, including safer driving practices, enhanced operational efficiency, and reduced costs. These systems also improve the accuracy of loading distribution assessments and optimize freight operations to achieve better economic and environmental outcomes. Comprehensive and accurate loading information can facilitate more sustainable freight transport systems and thereby contribute to the broader goals of logistics efficiency and industry innovation. For example, previous studies extended loading status analyses to applications involving fuel consumption, environmental impacts, and traffic safety. Soofastaei et al.28 estimated fuel consumption by analyzing the interplay between vehicle weight, operating speed, and resistance at excavation sites. Hoffman29 examined long-distance fuel consumption using multiple influencing factors, including the payload. Siami-Irdemoosa and Dindarloo30 as well as Golbasi and Kina31 studied fuel consumption under excavation and stochastic loading conditions, respectively. Other research has linked vehicle loads to emissions, as demonstrated by Dente and Tavasszy32 and Wang et al.33, who analyzed carbon emissions and operational modes under various loading scenarios. Regarding traffic safety, Conrad34 explored the economic feasibility of log transport under vehicle weight limits, whereas Yasanthi and Mehran35 analyzed the effects of loading status on vehicle speed under varying weather and traffic conditions. Ling et al.36 assessed the impact of large freight vehicle loads on bridge structures and considered the integration of automated truck platooning for highway freight transport. Li et al.37 proposed a real-time monitoring system to reduce traffic accidents by tracking loading conditions.

While these studies highlight the broad implications of loading status information for fuel consumption, environmental impacts, and safety, relatively few works have investigated how minimal telematics data can be leveraged to infer vehicle states such as mass or loading status in a scalable manner. This motivates a closer look at telematics-based vehicle state estimation.

Vehicle state estimation using telematics and machine learning

Beyond image- and infrastructure-based approaches, an emerging line of research uses on-board sensing and telematics data to infer vehicle states such as mass, axle loads, and longitudinal dynamics in real time. Many studies formulate vehicle mass estimation as an observer design or parameter identification problem based on longitudinal dynamics, often using CAN-bus signals, inertial measurement units (IMU), or torque sensors as inputs. For example, Jensen38 estimated the mass of ground vehicles from combined IMU and CAN-bus data by exploiting a longitudinal dynamics model and recursive filtering, and showed that accurate mass tracking is feasible under normal driving conditions. Similar longitudinal-dynamics-based frameworks have been proposed in the vehicle engineering literature, typically focusing on continuous mass estimation for individual vehicles under controlled sensor configurations.

Recent work has also explored data-driven and hybrid approaches that combine physical modeling with machine learning. Gallego39proposed a real-time mass and axle load estimator for multi-axle trucks using tire-pressure monitoring system data and monocular vision to infer tire deformation, embedding a single-degree-of-freedom tire model in a numerical estimation framework. Other studies have used telematics data to develop statistical models of fuel consumption for heavy-duty trucks, where variables such as average speed, gross vehicle weight, and road gradient act as key explanatory factors (e.g.40,). These examples demonstrate that telematics-based modeling can recover latent vehicle states and energy use at a fine temporal resolution, provided sufficiently rich on-board signals are available.

Taken together, these studies show that telematics and on-board sensing can be leveraged to estimate vehicle mass, axle loads, and related performance variables. However, most existing mass- or state-estimation studies rely on rich sensor configurations such as chassis-mounted IMUs, wheel-force sensors, detailed CAN-bus torque signals, tire-pressure monitoring systems, or camera-based measurements. Such setups are not universally mandated for commercial fleets and are difficult to scale to nationwide applications. By contrast, digital tachographs (DTGs) used in this study record only speed, engine revolutions, and a limited set of auxiliary variables at relatively low sampling rates, but are legally mandated for all commercial freight vehicles in Korea. These institutional and technical characteristics motivate the DTG-based framework for loading status estimation developed in this study.

Physics-guided machine learning frameworks

Physics-guided machine learning has recently attracted increasing attention in transportation and related dynamical systems. The central idea is to integrate first-principles knowledge, such as conservation laws or motion equations, into data-driven models in order to improve sample efficiency, interpretability, and generalization41. In traffic flow modeling, a growing body of work augments deep networks with macroscopic or microscopic traffic flow models. For example, Mo et al.42 developed physics-informed deep car-following models in which classical car-following equations are embedded into neural network architectures and training objectives, and demonstrated that such hybrid models can better capture longitudinal driving behavior than purely data-driven baselines when data are sparse. Ji et al.43 proposed a physics-guided neural network for traffic flow forecasting that incorporates differential-equation-based traffic dynamics into a spatio-temporal deep learning framework.

Beyond individual contributions, recent reviews highlight that hybrid physics–data approaches are particularly promising for traffic state estimation under sparse or noisy sensor coverage. Zhang et al.44 reviewed hybrid physics-based machine learning methods for macroscopic traffic state estimation and concluded that incorporating physical constraints into learning objectives helps to reduce physically implausible predictions and improves transferability across networks and demand conditions. These studies illustrate several roles that physics guidance can play in transportation models, including constraining the hypothesis space, defining physically meaningful loss terms, and improving the robustness of learned representations.

The present work is conceptually related to this physics-guided paradigm but differs in both application focus and implementation. Existing physics-guided models in transportation primarily target traffic flow, traffic state, or car-following behavior, and typically embed traffic flow equations or car-following models directly into the network structure or loss function. In contrast, our study applies physics guidance to the problem of freight truck loading status classification using minimal telematics data from digital tachographs. Rather than enforcing full vehicle dynamics equations within the neural network, we use physical principles—Newton’s second law, drivetrain kinematics, and resistance forces—to design informative features from raw speed and engine speed signals, while the classifier itself remains a flexible Bayesian neural network. In this way, our study extends physics-guided machine learning ideas to a new application domain that focuses on loading status estimation using DTG-type telematics data.

Uncertainty quantification and Bayesian neural networks in transportation modeling

Transportation systems are inherently uncertain due to fluctuating demand, heterogeneous driver behavior, and incomplete sensor coverage. Consequently, recent studies have emphasized not only point prediction accuracy but also explicit quantification of predictive uncertainty. In traffic flow forecasting, Qian et al.45provided a comprehensive overview of uncertainty quantification methods and distinguished between aleatoric (data-related) and epistemic (model-related) uncertainty. They highlighted that accounting for both types of uncertainty is essential for reliable deployment of data-driven models in intelligent transportation systems. Deep ensemble methods, variational Bayesian approaches, and Monte Carlo dropout have all been explored as practical tools to propagate uncertainty through complex neural architectures (e.g.46,).

Bayesian neural networks (BNNs) constitute a natural framework for quantifying model uncertainty by placing probability distributions over network weights and deriving posterior predictive distributions. In the transportation domain, BNNs and hybrid architectures with Bayesian components have been applied to short-term traffic flow prediction, travel time estimation, and parking occupancy forecasting. Wang et al.47 proposed a hybrid LSTM–BNN framework that jointly models traffic flow and its uncertainty, showing that probabilistic forecasts can support more robust traffic management decisions. Rahman et al.48 developed an attention-based hybrid network with Bayesian inference for traffic flow prediction, demonstrating that uncertainty-aware models can better handle anomalies and sensor errors. Nezhadettehad et al.49 used a Bayesian neural classifier for parking availability prediction and showed how predictive probability distributions can be thresholded to suppress low-confidence recommendations in operational systems.

More broadly, recent work has argued that uncertainty-aware models are particularly valuable in safety–critical and high-stakes contexts, where decision makers may wish to prioritize high-confidence predictions or explicitly flag ambiguous cases45. In this study, we adopt a Bayesian neural network to classify the loading status of freight trucks based on physics-guided features. This choice is motivated by two considerations. First, the driving environment and road conditions introduce variability that cannot be fully captured by deterministic models, especially when only a limited number of vehicles and operating conditions are observed. Second, the resulting posterior class probabilities and credible intervals enable practitioners to distinguish between high-confidence empty/loaded predictions and uncertain observations, which is crucial for applications such as enforcement planning, infrastructure management, and risk-aware policy design. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior work has combined physics-guided feature engineering with Bayesian neural networks for truck loading status estimation using DTG-type telematics data.

Research gaps and motivation

Prior research on estimating the loading status of freight vehicles has predominantly utilized image-based analysis, sensor-based measurements, or physics-based modeling through simulation environments. While these approaches have demonstrated certain strengths, they also present notable limitations. First, image-based methods are highly sensitive to external conditions such as ambient lighting and weather, which may significantly degrade their accuracy in real-world applications. Second, sensor-dependent techniques often require expensive equipment or access to proprietary in-vehicle data such as Controller Area Network (CAN) signals or torque sensors, thereby limiting their scalability and generalizability. For example, Ghosh et al.11 estimated vehicle mass using CAN data and drivetrain torque observers,however, this method is applicable only under constrained conditions where sensor accessibility is ensured. Third, many previous studies have relied on laboratory-controlled experiments rather than real-world operational data, which reduces their relevance to actual freight transport environments.

In contrast, Digital Tachograph (DTG) data—recorded at regular short intervals and including key vehicle parameters such as speed and engine RPM, from which acceleration can be derived—offer a promising alternative. As a legally mandated device for commercial vehicles in many countries, DTG enables the collection of large-scale driving data with high temporal and spatial resolution. Yun and Park50 demonstrated the feasibility of using DTG data to assess real-time traffic conditions, highlighting its potential for data-driven analyses under realistic roadway scenarios. Nevertheless, the application of DTG data has largely been confined to domains such as accident risk assessment, drowsy driving detection, and behavioral analysis, with minimal research directed toward estimating vehicle loading status.

From a policy and operational perspective, this research gap is particularly critical. Accurate and scalable estimation of freight vehicle loading status is not only a technical challenge but also a prerequisite for evidence-based policy design in freight transport. Load condition information can directly inform policies on freight efficiency, environmental sustainability, and road safety—for instance, improving logistics efficiency, enforcing overloading regulations, and optimizing priorities for road infrastructure maintenance as well as transport policy development. At the same time, for transport operators, real-time loading status provides a practical basis for enhancing fleet management and reducing operational costs.

To address these limitations and opportunities, and building on recent advances in telematics-based vehicle state estimation, physics-guided machine learning, and uncertainty-aware neural modeling reviewed above, the present study proposes a novel framework for loading status estimation using DTG data collected under actual driving conditions. The approach integrates principles from vehicle dynamics and drivetrain mechanics into a physics-guided feature engineering process to construct interpretable input variables. Furthermore, a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) is employed to account for prediction uncertainty, thereby enhancing the model’s robustness. By combining physical interpretability with data-driven inference, this study aims to provide a scalable, reliable solution for real-world monitoring of freight vehicle loading status.

Analytical methodology

Research premises: fundamental mechanics principles

This study applies fundamental mechanics principles based on Newton’s second law, the vehicle drivetrain, and the driving resistance to estimate the loading status of freight vehicles. Newton’s second law states that the acceleration of a vehicle is proportional to its applied force (F) and inversely proportional to its mass (m). The drivetrain transmits engine power to the wheels, and its effective tractive effort depends on engine torque, gear ratio, and powertrain efficiency. In addition, vehicle motion is affected by driving resistance, which consists of aerodynamic drag, gradient resistance, rolling resistance, and inertial resistance. While the exact formulations are well established in the vehicle dynamics literature, their practical implication is straightforward: heavier vehicles face greater resistance and therefore tend to display lower longitudinal acceleration than lighter vehicles under comparable tractive efforts and driving conditions.

By embedding these fundamental mechanics into the analytical framework, this study develops physics-informed features from DTG data that enhance interpretability and policy relevance. Rather than treating the load estimation task as a purely black-box prediction problem, the approach leverages transparent, physically grounded indicators that can support operational decision-making and evidence-based transport policy. Importantly, in this study the Newtonian relationship between force, mass, and acceleration is used as a qualitative guide for feature design, not as an assumption that tractive force remains constant over time or across all operating states.

Empirical cumulative distribution (ECD) analysis insights

This study employed DTG data to estimate the loading status of freight vehicles by utilizing the fundamental principles of vehicle dynamics and drivetrains. A comprehensive understanding of the driving forces acting on freight vehicles is essential for this analysis. The empirical cumulative distribution (ECD) of the acceleration-phase values, shown in Fig. 1, provides critical insights into the differences between the empty and loaded states. For this figure, the ECD curves were computed separately for all DTG records labeled as empty and all records labeled as loaded in the filtered dataset (57,543 records in total), as defined in Section "DTG data preprocessing".

Cumulative Distribution Function of Acceleration According to Loading Status.

In Fig. 1, the x-axis represents the acceleration values (m/s2) recorded during the acceleration phase, while the y-axis indicates the cumulative percentage (%) of observations with acceleration values less than or equal to the corresponding x-axis values. As the cumulative percentage approaches 100%, a larger proportion of observations falls below a given acceleration value, thereby highlighting the distribution of acceleration across the dataset.

The ECD curve for the loaded state is steeper than that for the empty state, implying that the observations for the loaded state are more concentrated at lower acceleration values. These results indicate that vehicles in the loaded state experience a reduced range of acceleration compared with those in the empty state.

Workflow for classifier construction

This study aims to estimate the loading status of freight vehicles using DTG data collected under real-world operating conditions. The available variables are limited to those recorded by DTG—such as speed, RPM, GPS, and acceleration—and thus do not include driver inputs or proprietary in-vehicle signals. Within these constraints, the study incorporates physics-based interpretations to strengthen interpretability. While tractive force depends not only on speed and RPM but also on driver behavior, vehicle-specific characteristics, and external conditions, many of these factors cannot be directly observed in large-scale DTG datasets11,50–52.

Despite these limitations, the proposed approach embeds physics-inspired heuristics into the modeling process rather than relying solely on post-hoc statistical inference. Accordingly, the constructed combinations of acceleration, speed, inferred gear state, and gradient are used as mass-sensitive proxy variables rather than as exact inversions of the longitudinal dynamics equations to recover true tractive force or precise vehicle mass. Gear ratios were inferred from the linear relationship between speed and RPM, allowing indirect identification of gear positions. Although this process is subject to real-world noise (e.g., clutch slip, variations in drivetrain efficiency), it still captures essential physical distinctions in tractive performance across loading states. These gear-related features were then used as input variables in a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN). While this differs from directly integrating equations into model architecture, as in Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs), it represents a “physics-guided” hybrid strategy that embeds mechanical principles into feature engineering under practical constraints53.

Based on this theoretical foundation, the study proceeded through four phases: (1) data collection, (2) preprocessing of DTG records, (3) identification of key attributes, and (4) construction of the loading status classifier (Fig. 2). In the data collection phase, DTG variables were combined with transportation document information, digital elevation models (DEM), and GIS-based road networks. The bill of loading data for the same vehicles served as ground truth for validation, while DEM and GIS databases enabled slope estimation, an important contextual factor for freight vehicle performance.

Research Implementation Process.

During preprocessing, the DTG records were segmented into trips, assigned IDs, annotated with loading status information, and enriched with derived gear ratios. Gear ratios, estimated from speed and RPM, provided a means to differentiate vehicle dynamics across empty and loaded states under comparable tractive conditions. Road gradient values were derived from DEM and trajectory information to account for slope resistance. Kernel density estimation (KDE) was applied to analyze acceleration distributions across gear–speed ranges, enabling systematic comparison between empty and loaded states.

Finally, a BNN classifier was developed using speed ranges, gear ratios, and average acceleration during the acceleration phase as explanatory variables. Model performance was evaluated using a confusion matrix to ensure robustness, and the results demonstrated reliable classification accuracy under real-world conditions.

Data collection and attribute identification

Data collection

DTG (Digital Tachograph) data, which include operational signals such as speed, acceleration, engine RPM, and brake usage, are regarded as a representative form of vehicle telematics data used in real-world freight operations. This study utilized the DTG data and bill of lading data obtained from one of the largest logistics companies in Korea as the primary datasets. The DTG data were collected over two days from September 10 to 11, 2020, and included operational records from 92 freight vehicles, each of which had a capacity of 25 tons. As detailed in Table 1, the data were recorded at three-second intervals, capturing parameters such as the instantaneous speed, RPM, GPS coordinates (latitude and longitude), date, time, and ignition status. This resulted in a dataset comprising 949,858 records.

DTGs were selected as the primary data source not because they provide the richest set of sensor channels, but because they are legally mandated for almost all heavy-duty freight trucks in Korea and constitute the only telematics system that can support nationwide deployment without retrofitting additional equipment. Focusing on DTG data therefore aligns the proposed framework with the institutional and technical constraints under which freight policies and monitoring systems must realistically operate.

The bill of lading data were explicitly gathered for the exact freight vehicles included in the DTG dataset, enabling the precise identification of vehicle loading status. This dataset provides comprehensive information on the driver and freight, including the vehicle ID, driver name and contact details, loading and unloading locations, dispatch time, commodity type, and gross vehicle weight. These data served as references to validate the loading status inferred from the DTG data.

This study employed DEM data and GIS road network databases to estimate road gradients. DEM data were sourced from the National Spatial Information Portal and covered the year 2020, comprising approximately 15.5 million grids with a resolution of 90 × 90 m. In addition, the 2021 Road Network GIS database provided by the National Transportation Database was used. This database includes detailed information on the physical and structural characteristics of roads nationwide and consists of 486,863 nodes and 625,674 links. Attributes such as the road grade, link length, and number of lanes were integrated into the analysis.

Road gradient information, which is essential for accounting for slope resistance, poses a significant challenge for South Korean Road management authorities, including the Korea Expressway Corporation, Regional Land Management Offices, and local governments, as they do not maintain gradient data for individual road sections. Consequently, this study adopted a method widely used in previous research54–56 that uses DEM data to estimate road gradients effectively. By integrating DEM and GIS data, this approach provided reliable estimates of gradient values for each road segment, ensuring an accurate representation of slope resistance in the analysis.

Identifying and estimating key attributes

DTG data preprocessing

The dataset was filtered to preprocess the DTG data by setting the speed range to 30–80 km/h and the engine RPM range to 800–1500 RPM. The lower speed limit of 30 km/h was chosen to exclude very low-speed, stop-and-go urban driving, where frequent braking, turning, and lane changes make longitudinal acceleration highly irregular and only weakly informative about loading effects. The upper speed limit of 80 km/h was selected to avoid near-maximum-speed regimes in which acceleration is constrained more by legal speed limits and surrounding traffic conditions than by available tractive effort. Similarly, the RPM band of 800–1500 excludes near-idle engine states and high-RPM transients around gear shifts, both of which tend to produce unstable acceleration and gear-ratio estimates that are not representative of steady cruising or typical uphill driving. This filtering excluded scenarios influenced by external factors unrelated to the loading status of the freight vehicle and eliminated irregular travel patterns, such as idling and excessive acceleration. In addition, the loading status of each freight vehicle was determined using the bill of lading.

As mentioned previously, this study was based on the premise that the acceleration characteristics of a freight vehicle differ depending on its loading status. Only the DTG records with acceleration values exceeding 0.0926 m/s2 were included in the analysis to focus on meaningful acceleration patterns. This threshold was established by considering that a 1 km/h speed change over 3 s could naturally occur without intentional acceleration. Consequently, the dataset was reduced from 949,858 to 57,543 records, ensuring that the data reflected relevant and consistent driving behaviors for the analysis. Although this filtering step removed a substantial share of the raw DTG records, exploratory checks indicated that low-speed idling, congested stop-and-go operations, and high-RPM transients around gear shifts accounted for the majority of the discarded records. These segments are only weakly informative about the steady longitudinal dynamics that differentiate empty from loaded operations. Because the objective of this study is not to reconstruct full vehicle trajectories but to learn load-dependent acceleration patterns under comparable operating conditions, the potential loss of information was judged acceptable in light of the gain in physical interpretability.

In the bill-of-lading records, each trip is associated with gross vehicle weight and freight information for operational management purposes. For the present study, these records were converted into a binary loading-status label that distinguishes between trips operating without freight (empty) and trips carrying any positive freight load (loaded). In other words, partially loaded operations and fully loaded operations are grouped into the same “loaded” class, because the primary policy question addressed here is whether a vehicle is contributing freight load to the network at a given time, rather than estimating the exact payload. Consequently, the proposed classifier should be interpreted as a coarse indicator of load presence (empty vs non-empty), rather than a fine-grained estimator of continuous payload magnitude or multiple loading levels.

Gear position (Gp) differentiation

The core premise of this study is that the acceleration characteristics of a freight vehicle depend on its loading status. Specifically, a loaded vehicle exhibits lower acceleration under the same force than does an empty vehicle. As the force applied by a vehicle is influenced by its gear position, it is essential to identify the position of the gear at each moment of operation. However, the DTG data do not directly provide gear position information.

Wefky et al.14 demonstrated that gear positions can be differentiated by analyzing the linear proportional relationship between the vehicle speed and RPM. Figure 3a illustrates this relationship, where the slope of the graph represents the gear ratio for each gear position. By observing the linear clusters of the speed and RPM, the range of each gear position can be identified, thus forming the foundation for the gear ratio calculation. The maximum and minimum RPM values corresponding to a specific speed are critical for calculating the gear ratio. As shown in Fig. 3a, the slope of the linear relationship determines the gear ratio and thereby enables the prediction of gear positions based on the speed and RPM data.

Possibility of Differentiating Gear Positions Using Speed and RPM.

Figure 3b presents the relationship between the speed and RPM derived from the DTG data collected in this study. Similar to the findings of Wefky et al.14, a clear linear relationship was observed between speed and RPM. Each linear cluster corresponds to a specific gear position, with multiple clusters resulting from gear shifts during vehicle operation. This study successfully differentiated gear positions by calculating gear ratios from DTG data, which is a critical input for analyzing the acceleration characteristics under different loading statuses.

The kernel density estimation (KDE) represents a data distribution as an overlap of kernel functions in which each kernel is centered at a specific data point. A Gaussian kernel was employed to smooth the distribution and facilitate the differentiation of the gear positions (Gp).

The probability density function (PDF) of the gear ratio (Gr) derived from the KDE visualizes the distribution of the gear positions and is divided into segments to identify extreme points. These extreme points are then used as reference markers to separate the gear positions. Subsequently, the appropriateness of the resulting gear separation boundaries was evaluated.

Figure 4 illustrates an example of the differentiation of the gear positions for a freight vehicle using KDE. Given that the kernel density functions possess the properties of a PDF, their values are always nonnegative, and the total area under the curve for all gear ratios is 1. To prevent underflow and improve computational robustness, an adjusted kernel density function was developed in this study.

Differentiation of Gear Position Utilizing KDE.

This involved the logarithmic and exponential smoothing of the kernel density function (Fig. 4b) along with the introduction of an adjustment coefficient to enhance computational efficiency. The adjusted kernel density function is differentiated with respect to the gear ratio (Gr) to locate extreme points. The second derivative was then used to determine whether these extreme points corresponded to the maxima or minima.

This process enables the classification of continuously distributed gear ratios (Gr) into discrete gear positions (Gp). The boundaries of each gear position were identified as points where the first derivative of the adjusted kernel density function was zero and the second derivative was positive, signifying local minima. This systematic approach ensured the precise differentiation of gear positions, enabling a clear and practical representation of the operational characteristics of freight vehicles. To evaluate the accuracy of differentiating the gear positions (Gp) of freight vehicles, the KDE results in Fig. 4 were visualized and compared with the speed–RPM graphs for each vehicle, as shown in Fig. 5.

Estimating road gradients

Road gradients were estimated using the methodology proposed by Tak57, which calculates gradients based on the average vector of terrain gradients and the driving trajectory vector of freight vehicles (for detailed methodology, refer to57). During this process, data from the downhill road segments were excluded from the analysis because of the frequent occurrence of idle driving and irregular braking patterns in freight vehicles. Consequently, only data from the flat or uphill road segments were included in the analysis. After filtering, a total of 40,280 records were retained for analysis from the initial 57,543 records.

Limitations in vehicle composition and control of external variables

This study focused on 25-ton freight vehicles manufactured by Hyundai Motor Company, which are widely used in Korean logistics operations. According to Article 55 of the Traffic Safety Act and Article 49–2 of its Enforcement Decree, vehicles with a gross weight exceeding 20 tons are mandated to be equipped with DTG devices, ensuring high coverage of real-world driving data. Focusing on 25-ton trucks is advantageous because their operating characteristics differ clearly between empty and loaded states, facilitating accurate labeling. Moreover, restricting the dataset to a single manufacturer minimized variation in key physical properties such as engine output and gear ratios, reducing intrinsic noise in the model and enhancing interpretability. Importantly, this restricted vehicle scope was a deliberate design choice to strengthen internal validity by isolating load-dependent dynamics under comparable specifications, rather than an attempt to claim universal applicability across heterogeneous fleets. While this homogeneity supports robust feature construction, it also limits the generalizability of the results to other vehicle classes. Lighter or heavier trucks (e.g., 10-ton or 40-ton vehicles) exhibit substantially different dynamics, which should be explicitly examined in future studies.

In addition, DTG data were collected under real-world operating conditions rather than in controlled laboratory environments, making it impossible to perfectly control for external variables such as weather, congestion, or surface conditions. Nevertheless, the dataset maintained analytical validity through careful control measures. Data were drawn from trucks operating on major expressways and national highways, where road quality is consistently high: according to the 2023 Road Network Status Report, Korea’s expressways and national highways are paved at a rate of 99.8%59. This ensures that grade resistance and rolling resistance effects were evaluated under relatively stable and comparable conditions. At the same time, using operational DTG traces from real drivers on real roads provides a more realistic basis for deployment-oriented evaluation than laboratory-only or purely simulation-based settings, while inevitably introducing environmental variability that cannot be fully controlled. Taken together, this strategy reflects a trade-off between generalizability and interpretability, which is considered appropriate for an early-stage empirical investigation with implications for future policy-oriented research.

From a generalization perspective, the homogeneous vehicle composition implies that the decision boundaries learned by the classifier are specific to 25-ton Hyundai trucks operating under the studied logistics environment. Although the physics-guided features are derived from general longitudinal dynamics and are conceptually applicable to other heavy vehicles, their empirical distributions and class-separating thresholds are expected to differ for 10-ton, 40-ton, or other truck categories with distinct engine characteristics, gear ratios, curb weights, and typical loading patterns. Therefore, the present model cannot be assumed to perform reliably on other vehicle classes without further adaptation. In practice, a broader deployment would require either (i) retraining separate models for each major vehicle class using labeled DTG and freight data, or (ii) developing a hierarchical framework that explicitly incorporates vehicle-class information and allows for partial parameter sharing across classes. Accordingly, the contribution of this study lies in demonstrating the feasibility of a DTG-minimal, physics-guided estimation framework, rather than asserting that a single trained model can be directly applied to all truck classes without retraining. In this sense, the current study should be regarded as a controlled proof-of-concept for 25-ton trucks, providing evidence of methodological feasibility rather than a fully validated nationwide model covering all heavy vehicles. Moreover, both model development and evaluation were conducted on DTG records from a single logistics environment, so the reported performance should be interpreted as case-study evidence rather than a universally generalizable benchmark. External validation using independent fleets, regions, or time periods is therefore an important direction for future work to more rigorously assess the transferability of the proposed framework.

Results and discussion

This study employed a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) model to estimate the loading status of freight vehicles. BNNs have been widely applied in transportation-related research due to their ability to capture predictive uncertainty and accommodate complex, heterogeneous datasets. For instance, Yuan et al.60 modeled collision risks associated with freight vehicles using a BNN and analyzed the influence of factors such as construction environments, demographics, land use, and road network structures. Dong et al.61 applied a BNN to predict traffic accident occurrences while explicitly quantifying associated uncertainties. Weng et al.62 utilized a BNN for transport mode classification to effectively address imbalanced data, and Ki et al.63 used a BNN to classify vehicles into five types using inductive-loop detector data. These studies highlight the flexibility and robustness of BNNs in addressing complex problems in traffic safety and operations.

Considering the inherent uncertainty and environmental variability present in DTG (digital tachograph) data—arising from factors such as road conditions, vehicle specifications, and driver behavior—the BNN model was selected for this study. Unlike deterministic models that produce only point estimates, BNNs characterize each prediction with a probability distribution. This structure allows the model to provide confidence information (e.g., variance, credible intervals), offering several practical advantages:

Estimation of freight vehicle loading status using record data

Dataset construction using record data

The dataset was collected from 25-ton commercial trucks operated by a private logistics company in Korea. DTG data were recorded at 3-s intervals, supplemented with bill of lading records that enabled precise labeling of loading status. Attributes such as speed range, gear position, and average acceleration during acceleration phases were used to construct the dataset. Analysis revealed an imbalance of approximately 7:3 between loaded and empty trips—reflecting real-world logistics operations where loaded trips are more frequent.

Such class imbalance can degrade the performance of machine learning classifiers by biasing predictions toward the majority class65. To mitigate this, random oversampling was applied to increase the number of empty-trip samples, expanding the dataset from 40,280 to 56,390 records (39,453 for training,16,917 for testing). This step improved model stability and classification performance without altering the core data structure66.

Nevertheless, the representativeness of empty-trip data remains a challenge, as freight loading information is often treated as sensitive business data by logistics companies. Access to large-scale labeled datasets is therefore limited. Despite these constraints, this study successfully collaborated with a private logistics operator to obtain verified DTG and bill of lading data, enabling a practical demonstration of data-driven load estimation under real-world conditions. This achievement not only strengthens the academic contribution of the study but also underscores its potential policy relevance in designing more efficient and transparent freight monitoring systems. In particular, it highlights the importance of fostering data-sharing collaborations between industry and public agencies to support evidence-based freight transport policy.

Bayesian neural network architecture

The Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) used in this study was implemented as an approximate Bayesian neural network using Monte Carlo dropout, following the approach of Gal and Ghahramani22. The model is a feed-forward architecture operating on the physics-guided feature vector described in Section "Identifying and estimating key attributes". The input layer received three standardized features (speed, acceleration, gear) for each DTG record, followed by a fully connected hidden layer with 16 units using a radial basis function (RBF) activation. A dropout layer with a dropout rate of 0.2 was inserted after the hidden layer to both regularize the network and enable approximate Bayesian inference. The output layer consisted of a single neuron with an RBF activation, producing a probability-like score for the loaded class in the range (0, 1]. Network weights were optimized using stochastic gradient descent with a batch size of 48 for 200 epochs, using binary cross-entropy as the loss function. Bayesian inference was approximated via Monte Carlo dropout by performing 50 stochastic forward passes for each input and aggregating the resulting predictions into posterior predictive means and standard deviations, with binary class labels obtained by thresholding the posterior mean at 0.5.

Enhancing the accuracy of freight vehicle loading status prediction using the multi-observation aggregation method

It is important to clarify that the multi-observation aggregation method is not an integral or required component of the core methodology proposed in this study. The primary approach estimates loading status from a single observation (i.e., a 9 s average acceleration segment) using the physics-guided features and the Bayesian Neural Network described earlier. The aggregation procedure is introduced as an optional post-processing enhancement to improve operational robustness by mitigating short-term variability and transient noise that may affect individual predictions in real-world driving environments.

To further improve the reliability of the operational predictions, we additionally evaluated a multi-observation aggregation method. Although single observation data, such as the 9 s average acceleration, can be easily collected and processed under real-time driving conditions, the prediction accuracy may be affected by temporary variability and noise in the data. This study proposes aggregating multiple observations to compile individual prediction results to mitigate these limitations and enhance the robustness of the predictions. The multi-observation aggregation method combines the prediction results from multiple 9 s average acceleration data points and determines the final prediction based on the most frequently observed outcome. This approach minimizes the impact of transient errors and enhances the reliability and accuracy of the predictions. Discussions and policy implications

Most prior studies on Vehicle Mass Estimation (VME) have focused on the precise estimation of vehicle mass using vehicle-dynamics equations11,67. However, such methods typically require additional sensors, access to in-vehicle CAN signals (e.g., driveline torque, acceleration, RPM), or data collected under constrained conditions such as test tracks or fixed gradients. While these approaches can achieve high technical accuracy, they face fundamental limitations in scalability and policy applicability at the national level. Accordingly, DTG-based loading status estimation should be viewed as complementary to sensor-rich vehicle mass estimation approaches: high-precision weight-sensor or CAN-based methods remain preferable when detailed instrumentation is available for individual vehicles, whereas the DTG-based framework provides broad coverage and scalability at the fleet and network levels using existing mandatory telematics.

In many countries, detailed load-status information is held exclusively by private logistics companies and is rarely shared due to its commercial sensitivity. National freight surveys or statistical frameworks—such as the U.S. Freight Analysis Framework (FAF5), which provides tonnage and ton-mile estimates by region and mode—offer only aggregate data and cannot capture vehicle-level loading conditions68. Likewise, although the digital tachograph became mandatory for new heavy goods vehicles in the EU on 1 May 2006 and Smart Tachograph Version 2 technical specifications were adopted in 20166970,, these systems are primarily designed for compliance and enforcement rather than open-access research applications.

In contrast, this study leverages the Digital Tachograph (DTG)—a legally mandated device for all heavy-duty trucks in Korea—to collect large-scale operational data without additional equipment, enabling scalable and near-real-time estimation of vehicle loading status. By embedding physics-guided features and quantifying predictive uncertainty through a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN), the proposed DTG-based framework strengthens interpretability and provides actionable insights for policy and operational decision-making. Moreover, it offers a practical and transferable approach for public agencies worldwide to infer freight O/D flows and loading patterns without relying on proprietary datasets, thereby enhancing both domestic and international policy relevance. Consistent with this policy-oriented objective and the structure of the available labels, the present framework intentionally formulates loading status as a binary empty versus non-empty variable, rather than attempting to estimate continuous payload magnitudes or multiple discrete loading levels.

Based on this framework, four major areas of policy and operational application are highlighted. First, enhancing freight demand analysis and logistics efficiency. By systematically identifying empty trips, the method provides a more accurate representation of freight flows, supporting demand-based dispatching, route optimization, and resource reallocation2. Empty-trip patterns derived from DTG data can be integrated into Vehicle Routing Problem (VRP) solutions for dynamic matching—such as pairing empty trucks with nearby freight demand or enabling mid-route pickups—which reduces empty running, improves driver working conditions, and enhances service levels3. Given that the empty-running rate of trucks in Korea remains as high as 41.3%1, the potential impact on fuel savings, emission reductions, and overall logistics efficiency is substantial.

Second, improving the consistency of freight O/D estimation. The Korea Transport Database (KTDB) currently estimates truck O/D flows by expanding samples from the National Freight Traffic Survey71, but the high share of empty trips is not fully reflected. By separating loaded and empty O/D flows, the proposed method helps reconcile discrepancies between commodity-based and truck-based O/D matrices, including directional imbalances observed between Seoul–Gyeonggi and Incheon–Gyeonggi, thereby enhancing the reliability of freight demand forecasting and policy formulation1.

Third, advancing road infrastructure management and load monitoring. Pavement deterioration and structural damage are strongly influenced by cumulative heavy loads from trucks8. DTG-based load-status information enables policymakers to identify high-load corridors and prioritize maintenance more efficiently and transparently, while spatial–temporal load heatmaps can guide the deployment of High-Speed Weigh-in-Motion (HS-WIM) systems and support mobile or risk-based enforcement strategies beyond fixed stations72. In addition, long-term monitoring of loaded versus empty operations can be integrated with pavement and bridge management systems to predict deterioration trends and optimize maintenance scheduling8. Fourth, improving traffic operations, safety, and roadway design. Although trucks represent a small share of total traffic, they exert a disproportionate influence on traffic-flow characteristics4, and passenger-car drivers often adapt their behavior—such as increasing headway or changing lanes preemptively—in response to truck movements73. DTG-based load-status information supports more accurate climbing-lane design by providing empirically grounded performance curves by grade and speed, strengthening the justification for lane installations (NCFRP Report 31). Load-status-based travel patterns can also help identify congestion-prone periods and corridors, informing targeted interventions such as dedicated truck lanes, ramp metering, and adaptive signal control4,74.

Finally, several implementation and modeling considerations should be noted. The proposed DTG-based framework complements traditional sensor-rich vehicle mass estimation methods by prioritizing scalability and accessibility over exact payload estimation, and it is intentionally formulated as a binary classifier (empty vs non-empty) in line with the available labels and the policy-oriented objective. At the same time, practical deployment must account for limitations related to unobserved environmental factors (e.g., weather, pavement condition, congestion) and partially loaded operations, which can induce deviations from idealized vehicle-dynamics behavior and lead to intermediate or high-uncertainty classifications. These issues, together with directions for extending the framework to heterogeneous fleets and multi-level payload estimation, are discussed in more detail in Section "Conclusion" (Conclusion).

Conclusion

This study proposed a novel methodology for estimating the loading status of freight vehicles using Digital Tachograph (DTG) data, supplemented with bill-of-lading, DEM, and GIS road network information. By integrating fundamental mechanics—including Newtonian acceleration, drivetrain characteristics, and driving resistance—into the feature engineering process, a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN) classifier was developed to distinguish between empty and non-empty vehicle states under real-world conditions. The results demonstrate that incorporating temporal context substantially improves predictive accuracy: from 76.4% using single-record data to 85.3% with 9-s averages, and over 90% in highway speed ranges. These findings highlight the practical potential of embedding vehicle dynamics into large-scale telematics analysis using legally mandated DTG signals that are already available in routine operations. Importantly, the contribution of this study is not to claim a universally applicable model for all truck types, but to demonstrate the feasibility of DTG-minimal, physics-guided load-status estimation under realistic operational constraints.

Beyond methodological contributions, the study underscores the broader policy relevance of DTG-based load estimation. First, distinguishing loaded from empty trips enhances the accuracy of freight O/D estimation, supporting demand-driven logistics planning and vehicle routing optimization. Second, the identification of recurring loaded operations offers a scalable basis for mobile or risk-based overloading enforcement, extending monitoring capabilities beyond the fixed locations of weigh-in-motion (WIM) stations. Third, mapping the spatiotemporal distribution of freight loads provides valuable input for predicting cumulative road wear, optimizing infrastructure maintenance priorities, and designing weight-restriction policies. More broadly, the ability to reduce empty running contributes to fuel efficiency and sustainability objectives, while enabling freight flow models that address inconsistencies in current O/D-based planning. Because the proposed framework produces probabilistic outputs via Monte Carlo dropout, it can also support risk-aware decision-making by allowing policymakers to prioritize actions on high-confidence classifications while treating low-confidence cases more conservatively.

This study has several limitations. The analysis was restricted to 25-ton trucks from a single manufacturer, which ensured interpretability but limits generalizability to other vehicle classes and load profiles. Moreover, external conditions such as pavement surface, weather, and congestion were not explicitly modeled, so deviations from the idealized Newtonian relationship between tractive effort, mass, and acceleration are absorbed into the data-driven component and reflected as increased predictive uncertainty. In addition, all trips with non-zero freight were labeled as “loaded” based on the bill-of-lading records, so the current model treats loading status as a binary variable (empty vs non-empty) rather than estimating continuous payload ratios or multiple discrete loading levels; partially or very lightly loaded trips are therefore expected to appear as intermediate-probability, higher-uncertainty cases near the decision boundary.

Future research should broaden the scope to heterogeneous fleets, integrate richer contextual variables (e.g., weather, pavement condition, congestion indicators), and access larger-scale labeled datasets through industry collaboration. As the framework is extended to diverse vehicle classes and operating environments, retraining or hierarchical adaptation of the model—together with a systematic analysis of how predictive uncertainty responds to adverse weather, pavement deterioration, congestion, and different degrees of partial loading—will be essential for ensuring reliable large-scale deployment. In particular, validating the model on independent datasets from other operators, regions, and time periods will be crucial to rigorously evaluate its generalizability beyond the current single-operator case study, and extending the formulation to multi-class or continuous payload estimation will be an important next step once detailed loading-ratio labels become available.

In conclusion, the research demonstrates that legally mandated DTG data—collected nationwide without the need for additional sensors—can be transformed into a scalable, practical, and policy-relevant tool. The proposed framework bridges engineering principles with real-world data to support evidence-based freight policies, improve logistics efficiency, strengthen overloading enforcement, and extend the longevity of transport infrastructure. Rather than a single fixed classifier, the study provides a transferable framework that can be applied to other vehicle classes through retraining with appropriately labeled DTG and freight data. As such, it contributes both a methodological innovation to the academic literature and a strategic decision-support instrument for policymakers and industry stakeholders.
