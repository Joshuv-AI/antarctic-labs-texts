Introduction

Stroke remains a leading cause of long-term disability worldwide, with approximately 795,000 new or recurrent cases annually in the United States alone [1]. Among the most debilitating consequences of stroke is upper limb motor impairment, affecting up to 35% of survivors and significantly limiting their ability to perform daily activities [2]. Despite advances in rehabilitation strategies, such as physical and occupational therapy, only 20–30% of patients achieve full arm motor recovery, showing the need for innovative interventions to enhance neuroplasticity and functional outcomes [3].

Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) is a promising neuromodulatory approach to enhance motor recovery after stroke [4]. Originally developed for epilepsy management, VNS involves the delivery of electrical impulses to the vagus nerve, which projects to key brain regions involved in neuroplasticity, including the cortex and brainstem [4]. Recent preclinical studies have shown that VNS, when combined with motor rehabilitation, enhances synaptic plasticity and promotes the reorganization of motor cortex representations in animal stroke models [4, 5]. These findings have spurred interest in translating VNS to human stroke populations, particularly for addressing arm motor deficits.

Clinical trials exploring VNS in stroke rehabilitation have reported encouraging results [6]. Similarly, non-invasive VNS, which utilizes transcutaneous stimulation of the vagus nerve, has demonstrated feasibility and potential efficacy in early-phase studies, providing a less invasive alternative [7]. However, the literature is heterogeneous, with variations in VNS protocols (invasive vs. non-invasive, stimulation parameters), study designs, and patient populations (acute vs. chronic stroke), necessitating a comprehensive synthesis to map the current evidence base.

Despite its potential, several challenges remain in understanding the role of VNS in post-stroke arm motor recovery. The optimal timing of VNS intervention (acute vs. chronic phase), the ideal stimulation parameters, and the synergy with conventional rehabilitation therapies are not well-established [8]. Additionally, patient-specific factors, such as stroke severity, lesion location, and baseline motor impairment, may influence VNS efficacy but are underexplored [8]. This scoping review maps the extent, nature, and characteristics of evidence on using VNS to improve arm motor recovery in stroke survivors. This scoping review uniquely integrates preclinical and clinical evidence up to March 2025, addressing gaps in prior reviews by including animal studies and focusing on clinical translation challenges.

Methodology

This scoping review was conducted to map the extent, nature, and characteristics of evidence for VNS for post-stroke arm motor recovery, identifying key findings, gaps, and implications for future research. The review followed the methodological framework proposed by Arksey and O’Malley (2005) [9], enhanced by Levac et al. (2010) [10], and adhered to the PRISMA-ScR guidelines [11].

Information sources

A search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. Grey literature was searched via ClinicalTrials.gov, the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform, and conference proceedings. Reference lists of included studies and reviews were hand-searched, and citation tracking was performed using Web of Science. The reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were hand-searched to identify additional studies. The search was conducted in June 2025.

Search strategy

The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms tailored to each database, focusing on three key concepts: VNS, stroke, and arm motor recovery. The PubMed search string, which served as the template, was: (“vagus nerve stimulation” [MeSH Terms] OR “vagus nerve stimulation” OR “vagal nerve stimulation” OR “transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation” OR “non-invasive vagus nerve stimulation” OR “invasive vagus nerve stimulation”) AND (“stroke” [MeSH Terms] OR “cerebrovascular accident” OR “ischemic stroke” OR “hemorrhagic stroke” OR “brain ischemia”) AND (“upper limb” OR “arm” OR “forelimb” OR “motor recovery” OR “motor function” OR “rehabilitation”). Risk of bias assessment

The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) assessed randomization, blinding, outcome data, and reporting for clinical RCTs. Non-randomized trials used ROBINS-I, focusing on selection bias and outcome reporting. Preclinical studies used SYRCLE, emphasizing randomization and blinding. Two reviewers (NA, GO) independently assessed risk, resolving conflicts via discussion or a third reviewer (EK). Results informed efficacy interpretations—supplementary file 2.

Data synthesis

Data were synthesized narratively to map the scope of evidence, focusing on the extent, nature, and characteristics of studies on VNS for post-stroke arm motor recovery. The synthesis was organized by study type (preclinical vs. clinical) and structured around four themes: intervention characteristics, efficacy for arm motor recovery, safety profile, and evidence gaps.

Preclinical studies

The four preclinical studies investigated invasive VNS in rat models of ischemic stroke, focusing on forelimb motor recovery as a proxy for human arm function. All studies employed controlled designs, comparing VNS paired with rehabilitative training to rehabilitation alone or sham stimulation, with small sample sizes (10–20 rats per group).

Intervention characteristics

VNS interventions were highly standardized, utilizing implanted electrodes on the cervical vagus nerve to deliver stimulation (0.8 mA, 100 µs pulse width, 30 Hz frequency, 500 ms bursts) during task-specific forelimb training [18–20, 22]. Rehabilitative tasks mimicked human arm movements, including rapid forelimb sequences, lever-pressing, and supination, administered over 5–6 weeks. Stroke was induced via motor cortex ischemia (e.g., endothelin-1 injection), targeting subacute or chronic phases. Hays et al. (2014) [18] explored VNS timing, comparing immediate stimulation during training to a 2-hour delay, highlighting the importance of temporal alignment with motor tasks. This consistency in stimulation parameters and task-specific pairing shows a mechanistic focus on leveraging VNS to enhance neuroplasticity. Table 2.

Task-specificity and generalization

Task specificity is critical in stroke recovery, as it targets neural circuits involved in specific movements, facilitating motor relearning [18]. VNS primarily enhances performance on trained tasks, indicating task-specific recovery. Tasks such as supination or lever-pressing significantly improved when paired with VNS [18–20]. However, evidence of generalization has also emerged. Meyers et al. (2018) [22] reported that VNS paired with supination training improved performance on an untrained isometric pull task, suggesting that VNS may promote broader motor network plasticity beyond the trained task.

Temporal factors

The timing of VNS delivery may influence recovery, with limited evidence suggesting immediate stimulation enhances outcomes. Hays et al. (2014) [18] found that immediate VNS during motor training resulted in 30–40% greater strength gains compared to delayed stimulation (e.g., a 2-hour lag), highlighting a time-sensitive neuroplasticity window that optimizes motor outcomes [1]. This temporal sensitivity highlights the importance of aligning VNS with rehabilitation to maximize the effective use of synaptic reorganization.

Safety profile

The safety profile of VNS in preclinical stroke models is favorable.

Adverse event frequency and severity

Across the reviewed studies, no adverse events were reported, reflecting the controlled laboratory settings and standardized stimulation parameters used in preclinical research. The absence of neurological, systemic, or other complications suggests that VNS poses minimal risk to animal models as applied in these studies. This consistent lack of adverse outcomes highlights the safety of VNS in the context of stroke rehabilitation research.

Tolerability and feasibility

VNS was well-tolerated by animal subjects, with no reported signs of distress, discomfort, or behavioral changes during or after stimulation. The seamless integration of VNS with motor training protocols further supports its feasibility for preclinical investigations. These findings indicate that VNS is a practical and well-accepted intervention in controlled research environments, paving the way for further exploration in clinical settings.

Evidence gaps

Despite the promising safety profile, several gaps in the preclinical literature limit the applicability of these findings. First, the studies primarily focused on ischemic stroke models, leaving the safety and efficacy of VNS in hemorrhagic stroke underexplored [18–20, 22]. Additionally, optimal VNS dosing parameters, including frequency, intensity, and duration, remain poorly defined, with limited data on dose-response relationships. Long-term safety outcomes beyond 6 weeks post-intervention are largely unreported, raising questions about the durability of VNS safety in chronic stroke models. The use of non-invasive VNS (e.g., transcutaneous auricular VNS, or taVNS) has not been adequately studied in animal models, limiting insights into its safety profile compared to invasive VNS. Additionally, the reliance on small sample sizes and forelimb-specific tasks restricts the generalizability of findings to human arm function, particularly for complex, multi-joint movements required in daily activities. Addressing these gaps is crucial for translating VNS safety data into human stroke populations and refining clinical protocols to improve patient outcomes. Tables 3 and 4.

Clinical studies

The six clinical studies evaluated VNS in human stroke survivors with arm motor deficits, primarily in chronic ischemic stroke. Five studies used invasive VNS, while one explored taVNS. Designs included randomized, blinded, sham-controlled trials (five studies) and a pilot study with control groups, with sample sizes ranging from 12 to 108.

Intervention characteristics

Clinical VNS interventions varied by delivery method but shared a focus on pairing stimulation with task-specific arm rehabilitation. Invasive VNS employed implanted devices that deliver standardized stimulation (0.8 mA, 100 µs, 30 Hz, 500 ms bursts) during movements such as reaching and grasping [13–17]. Rehabilitation involved 6 weeks of intensive in-clinic physiotherapy, with 90-minute sessions held three times weekly, and some studies also included home-based exercises [16, 17]. taVNS targeted the auricular vagus nerve, with Baig et al. (2020) [13] applying stimulation at maximum tolerated intensity during 300 + arm repetitions over 6 weeks, and Dickie et al. (2019) [14] using 4.5 mA, 300 µs, 30 Hz bursts before arm extension movements. Participants were chronic stroke survivors (3 months to 10 years post-stroke) with moderate to severe arm weakness (baseline FMA-UE scores ~ 20–40), though Baig et al. (2020) [13] included patients as early as 3 months post-stroke.

Key aspects of arm motor recovery

VNS significantly enhanced arm motor recovery, with subthemes of magnitude of motor improvement, task-specificity, generalization, and temporal factors.

Magnitude of motor improvement

Invasive VNS consistently produced improvements in arm motor function, with FMA-UE gains of 5.8–14.8 points in VNS-treated groups compared to 2.8–3.79 points in controls [15, 17], exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of ≥ 6 points for chronic stroke with minimal to moderate impairment. Smaller trials (N = 17–21) by Dawson et al. (2016) [15] and Kimberley et al. (2018) [21] reported mean FMA-UE gains of 8.63 points, while a larger trial (N = 108) by Dawson et al. (2021) [16] noted a 5.8-point increase, with 47% of VNS-treated patients achieving clinically meaningful improvements (≥ 6 points) [13, 17, 21]. Long-term follow-up data from Dawson et al. (2022) [17] demonstrated sustained gains, reaching 14.8 points 3 years post-intervention. In contrast, transcutaneous auricular VNS (taVNS) yielded smaller motor improvements, often as secondary outcomes. Baig et al. (2020) [15] reported modest FMA-UE gains, while Dickie et al. (2019) [14] observed enhanced biceps electromyography, suggesting less robust motor effects compared to invasive VNS.

Task-specificity and generalization

Invasive VNS primarily enhanced task-specific outcomes, with significant improvements in trained tasks such as reaching and grasping, as measured by the FMA-UE and Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT) [15, 17, 22]. These gains were consistent across studies, reflecting the targeted nature of VNS paired with rehabilitation. Evidence of generalization to untrained tasks was limited, though Dawson et al. (2022) [17] reported sustained WMFT improvements at 3 years, suggesting potential broader functional benefits. Conversely, taVNS effects were less task-specific, with motor improvements often secondary to primary outcomes, such as sensory function or reduction in spasticity [13, 14]. For example, Baig et al. (2020) [13] and Dickie et al. (2019) [14] noted motor gains but emphasized sensory or electrophysiological improvements, indicating a less focused motor recovery profile for taVNS.

Temporal factors

The timing and duration of VNS interventions significantly influenced motor outcomes. In multiple studies, invasive VNS demonstrated short-term efficacy, with significant FMA-UE gains observed within 6 weeks [14, 15]. Long-term follow-up revealed progressive improvements, with Dawson et al. (2022) reporting increasing FMA-UE scores over 3 years, highlighting the durability of invasive VNS effects [16]. In contrast, taVNS required extended protocols (e.g., 6 weeks) to achieve measurable motor benefits, as noted by Baig et al. [13]. The timing of stimulation delivery (e.g., during vs. before motor tasks) also varied for taVNS, with Dickie et al. [14] reporting unclear optimal windows for maximizing motor recovery. These findings suggest that invasive VNS benefits from immediate and sustained effects, while taVNS may require longer or more refined protocols to achieve comparable motor outcomes.

Safety profile

Adverse event frequency and severity

Invasive VNS was associated with mild and transient adverse events, including dysphonia, cough, and incision-site pain, reported in less than 10% of participants across studies [13–15, 21]. These events were typically self-limiting and did not lead to study withdrawal. One serious adverse event, vocal cord palsy, was reported in a larger trial (N = 108) by Dawson et al. (2021) [16], but no further serious complications were noted. taVNS) demonstrated an even milder safety profile, with only transient skin redness reported and no serious adverse events [13, 14]. Importantly, no studies reported neurological worsening or systemic complications, indicating a low risk profile for both VNS modalities in stroke rehabilitation.

Tolerability and feasibility

Both invasive VNS and taVNS were well-tolerated, with high participant adherence to intervention protocols across all studies. Invasive VNS required surgical implantation, but no procedure-related complications, such as infection or device failure, were reported, supporting its feasibility in clinical settings [14, 17]. The non-invasive nature of taVNS further enhanced its feasibility, particularly for outpatient or home-based rehabilitation, as noted by Baig et al. (2020) [13]. The ease of application and lack of significant discomfort with taVNS suggest it may be more readily scalable for broader clinical use.

Evidence gaps

Clinical studies revealed several gaps that limit the applicability of VNS safety data. First, the majority of studies focused on chronic ischemic stroke, with limited data on the safety of VNS in subacute or hemorrhagic stroke populations. Second, optimal taVNS parameters (e.g., stimulation frequency, intensity, and duration) remain poorly defined, with variability across studies hindering standardization. Also, long-term safety outcomes beyond 3 years are underexplored, leaving questions about the durability of VNS safety profiles. The clinical significance of reported motor gains (e.g., FMA-UE improvements) regarding functional independence is not fully elucidated, particularly for taVNS, where motor outcomes were often secondary to improvements in sensory function or spasticity.

Discussion

This scoping review synthesizes evidence on VNS as an intervention for post-stroke arm motor recovery. It highlights its potential as a neuromodulatory adjunct within the broader landscape of stroke rehabilitation strategies. Stroke rehabilitation aims to restore function and independence through strategies that leverage neuroplasticity, including physical therapy (PT), occupational therapy (OT), constraint-induced movement therapy (CIMT), robotic-assisted therapy, and non-invasive brain stimulation (e.g., transcranial magnetic stimulation [TMS] and transcranial direct current stimulation [tDCS]) [23]. These approaches primarily focus on repetitive, task-specific training to promote motor relearning, but their efficacy is limited, with only 20–30% of stroke survivors achieving full arm motor recovery [24]. VNS, by contrast, acts indirectly via vagal afferents to the brainstem and cortex [25], promoting widespread plasticity and yielding larger effect sizes in clinical trials (5.8–14.8-point FMA-UE improvements [16]) compared to TMS/tDCS, which report small to moderate effect sizes [23].

Comparison with conventional therapies

Conventional PT and OT, which involve repetitive exercises to improve strength and coordination, form the cornerstone of stroke rehabilitation but often yield modest gains in chronic stroke populations [26]. CIMT, which forces use of the affected limb by constraining the unaffected one, has shown efficacy but is resource-intensive and unsuitable for patients with severe impairments [26]. In contrast, VNS amplifies the effects of task-specific training, with preclinical studies reporting 50–100% greater forelimb recovery and clinical studies demonstrating 2–3 times greater FMA-UE improvements (5.8–14.8 points) compared to controls [15–17, 19, 20, 22]. Unlike early CIMT protocols, which required intensive training and were less feasible for severe impairments, VNS can be paired with standard rehabilitation tasks across a range of impairment severities [16, 17]. Modified CIMT protocols have since been adapted for broader populations, showing efficacy in similar cohorts [26]. However, VNS’s reliance on pairing with rehabilitation aligns it closely with PT and OT, suggesting it is best implemented as an adjunct rather than a standalone therapy.

Comparison with other neuromodulatory approaches

Non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), also aim to enhance neuroplasticity but differ from vagus nerve stimulation (VNS) in their mechanisms and applications [27]. TMS and tDCS directly modulate cortical excitability, with studies showing small to moderate effects on motor recovery [28, 29]. VNS, by contrast, acts indirectly via vagal afferents to the brainstem and cortex, promoting widespread plasticity and yielding larger effect sizes in clinical trials [16]. Preclinical evidence suggests VNS induces robust motor cortex reorganization [22], potentially offering sustained benefits, as seen in clinical trials with gains up to 3 years [17]. TMS/tDCS, being session-based, often requires repeated applications to maintain benefits [28, 29], though differing delivery methods limit direct durability comparisons. However, the invasive nature of VNS’s surgical requirement contrasts with the non-invasive nature of TMS/tDCS, although taVNS mitigates this barrier, aligning more closely with their accessibility [13, 14].

Strengths of VNS in rehabilitation

VNS’s strengths position it as a valuable adjunct to stroke rehabilitation. Its ability to enhance task-specific recovery, evidenced by preclinical gains and clinical FMA-UE improvements (≥ 6 points) [16, 17, 19, 20], complements conventional therapies like PT and OT, which may yield more modest gains in chronic stroke populations [26]. Also, its favorable safety profile, preclinical studies reporting no adverse events, and clinical studies noting only mild, transient issues (dysphonia, skin redness), support its feasibility across settings [13–17, 19–22]. The potential for generalization, as observed in preclinical studies where VNS improved untrained tasks [22], suggests broader motor network plasticity, a feature also seen in other interventions like CIMT and robotic therapy [24, 26]. taVNS’s non-invasive nature enhances accessibility, offering a scalable alternative to invasive VNS and aligning with the growing emphasis on home-based rehabilitation [13, 14].

Challenges and limitations in context

Despite its promise, VNS faces challenges within the broader rehabilitative landscape. The heterogeneity of VNS protocols (stimulation parameters, timing) mirrors variability in other neuromodulatory approaches, complicating standardization and clinical adoption [13–17]. Small to moderate sample sizes (12–108 participants in clinical studies) limit generalizability, a common issue in early-phase trials of novel interventions, such as robotics or VR [13–17, 24]. The focus on chronic ischemic stroke populations parallels limitations in TMS/tDCS research, leaving efficacy in acute, subacute, or hemorrhagic stroke underexplored [13–22]. Additionally, the clinical significance of FMA-UE gains, particularly for taVNS, remains unclear compared to patient-centered outcomes, such as functional independence —a critique also leveled at robotics and VR [3, 13, 14]. The surgical requirement for invasive VNS may limit accessibility compared to non-invasive therapies like CIMT or TMS/tDCS, though taVNS mitigates this barrier [13, 16].

Integration into rehabilitative frameworks

VNS’s synergy with task-specific rehabilitation suggests it could be integrated into multimodal rehabilitative frameworks to maximize outcomes. For example, combining VNS with CIMT could enhance forced-use training, leveraging VNS’s plasticity-enhancing effects to overcome CIMT’s intensity demands. Pairing VNS with robotic-assisted therapy could optimize high-repetition training, with VNS amplifying the neural response to precise movements. Similarly, integrating taVNS with VR-based rehabilitation could create engaging, home-based protocols, capitalizing on taVNS’s portability and VR’s motivational appeal. Such combinations could address the limitations of individual therapies, creating personalized, synergistic interventions tailored to patient needs.

The temporal sensitivity of VNS, particularly the need for immediate stimulation during training, aligns with principles of Hebbian plasticity underlying PT/OT and CIMT [18]. This suggests that VNS protocols should be carefully timed with rehabilitation sessions, a consideration also relevant for TMS/tDCS, where stimulation timing impacts efficacy [23]. Furthermore, VNS’s potential for long-term benefits, with clinical gains sustained up to 3 years, contrasts with the shorter-term effects of TMS/tDCS, positioning VNS as a durable adjunct for chronic stroke management [17].

Implications for clinical practice

In clinical practice, VNS could enhance existing rehabilitation programs, particularly for chronic ischemic stroke survivors with moderate to severe arm weakness. Invasive VNS, with its robust efficacy, may be prioritized for patients eligible for surgical intervention, integrated into outpatient PT/OT protocols to maximize FMA-UE gains [16, 17]. taVNS, given its accessibility, could be trialed in community or home-based settings, potentially paired with tele-rehabilitation or VR to increase access [13, 14]. Clinicians should weigh the benefits of VNS against its costs and logistical demands, particularly for invasive VNS, which requires surgical expertise and ongoing follow-up. Multidisciplinary teams, including neurologists, rehabilitation specialists, and therapists, will be crucial for implementing VNS effectively.

This scoping review has limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Including only English-language studies may have excluded relevant research published in other languages, potentially limiting the comprehensiveness of the evidence synthesis. In addition, the reviewed studies predominantly focused on chronic ischemic stroke populations, with limited data on subacute or hemorrhagic stroke, restricting generalizability to diverse stroke types and phases. Also, the heterogeneity in VNS protocols (stimulation parameters, timing) and the lack of long-term safety data beyond 3 years in clinical studies pose challenges for standardizing and implementing VNS in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This scoping review consolidates evidence from preclinical and clinical studies conducted between 2014 and 2023, establishing VNS as a promising neuromodulatory intervention for enhancing post-stroke arm motor recovery. Preclinical studies in rat models demonstrated that invasive VNS, paired with task-specific training, yields 50–100% greater forelimb recovery, often approaching pre-stroke levels, through synaptic reorganization and motor cortex plasticity mechanisms. Clinical studies in chronic ischemic stroke survivors corroborated these findings, with invasive VNS producing 2–3 times greater improvements in FMA-UE (5.8–14.8 points) compared to controls, with sustained benefits lasting up to 3 years. TA VNS, while less effective, can serve as a promising non-invasive alternative with preliminary motor gains, thereby enhancing its potential for broader application. The favorable safety profile of both modalities, characterized by minimal adverse events (mild dysphonia, transient skin redness) and high tolerability, supports their feasibility for stroke rehabilitation.
