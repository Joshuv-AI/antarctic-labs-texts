Coronary angiography with photon-counting detector CT—new options and some critical decisions
Authors: Victor Mergen, Matthias Eberhard, Hatem Alkadhi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe advent of photon-counting detector CT brought up various new interesting possibilities for coronary imaging including different scan modes and various post-processing options. The two main scan modes include spectral data acquisition with inherent energy-resolved imaging and ultra-high-resolution scanning with unprecedented high spatial resolution, both at the highest available temporal resolution. Post-processing options include the generation of virtual monoenergetic images at different levels and the generation of virtual non-calcium images. Many early studies indicated the potential of each of these options for coronary CT angiography but also show that much more work is needed to clarify the optimal scan mode for each individual patient and clinical setting.Keywords: computed tomography, coronary arteries, imaging, photon-counting detector, post-processing, dual-energy, spectral imaging

“Nothing is more difficult, and therefore more precious, than to be able to decide.”

—Napoleon Bonaparte

The recent introduction of photon-counting detector CT into routine clinical practice has been accompanied by a bouquet of new options for scan acquisition and image post-processing, not only but also for coronary CT angiography. These include two distinct scan modes for coronary CT angiography, both at the highest available temporal resolution of 66 ms: the spectral mode with a detector collimation of 144 × 0.4 mm, providing energy-resolved information with a maximum spatial resolution of 0.21 × 0.21 × 0.32 mm3, and the ultra-high-resolution (UHR) mode with a detector collimation of 120 × 0.2 mm, providing a maximum in-plane resolution of 0.11 mm and a maximum through-plane resolution of 0.16 mm.1 Since the two scan modes have distinct strengths, key decisions must be made both prior to the scan and after data acquisition.

Spectral scan mode

The spectral scan mode enables dual-energy-based post-processing, including the reconstruction of virtual monoenergetic images (VMIs) at various energy levels,2 virtual non-iodine images that resemble true non-enhanced images,3 and virtual non-calcium images with subtracted calcified plaques.4

VMI energy levels impact quantitative image information, as shown in studies evaluating their influence on coronary angiography with photon-counting detector CT.1 Notably, lowering monoenergetic levels closer to the k-edge of iodine enhances the contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR) in the coronary arteries.5 Cundari et al5 demonstrated that leveraging the higher CNR of low-energy VMI (at 45 keV) enables a contrast media volume reduction of up to 40% in coronary photon-counting detector CT angiography while maintaining diagnostic image quality. It must be kept in mind that monoenergetic levels influence stenosis severity measurements, since blooming artefacts of calcified and mixed coronary plaques are reduced at increasing monoenergetic levels, as shown in a study of Wolf et al.6 Sartoretti et al7 confirmed these observations using quantitative catheter coronary angiography as the reference standard. However, they proposed a different optimal monoenergetic level (65 keV) for calcified plaques7 than in the study by Wolf et al6 (100 keV). A recent phantom study elaborated on the decreasing iodine attenuation in coronary vessels at higher monoenergetic levels and provided a framework for how this decreasing attenuation can be counterbalanced with adjustments of the contrast media protocol.8 The rationale behind that study was that obtaining images with minimal blooming artefacts from calcified plaques requires high monoenergetic levels, while iodine contrast might not be sufficient at these levels.8 In addition, monoenergetic levels impact coronary plaque volume9 and pericoronary fat attenuation.10 Thus, when the spectral mode is chosen for coronary CT angiography, the optimal monoenergetic level must be carefully chosen, given its impact on the various constituents of coronary artery disease.

The virtual non-calcium technique represents another promising option for assessing calcified stenoses, where the inherent spectral information of photon-counting detector coronary CT angiography serves to separate calcified plaques from iodinated vessels. While virtual non-calcium images have shown potential to improve the quantification of calcified stenoses, current common sense is to not use them as a sole tool but as an adjunct to standard image reconstructions due to the limited robustness.4

Ultra-high-resolution scan mode

In distinction to the spectral mode, the UHR mode for coronary angiography with photon-counting detector CT has attracted a lot of attention because of its so-far unrivalled spatial resolution. Combined with high temporal resolution, the UHR mode yields reduced blooming artefacts in heavily calcified coronary arteries and provides superior delineation of adjacent vessel lumen.11,12

The UHR mode for coronary CT angiography undeniably opens up new diagnostic pathways in patients who were previously deemed unfeasible with conventional CT, that is, patients with a high calcium load13 and those with coronary stents.14 However, downsides of the UHR mode include longer scan times due to the narrower collimation, which can potentially lead to motion artefacts or necessitate adjustments to the contrast media protocol. Current studies applying the UHR mode for coronary CT angiography have used higher radiation doses compared with scans performed with the spectral mode.13 Whether higher radiation doses are truly necessary remains an area of ongoing research, and no study has yet proposed an optimal radiation dose for UHR acquisitions. Nevertheless, these potentially higher radiation doses may be negligible in older and frail patients, for whom the high diagnostic performance of this technique may replace invasive tests with their inherent periprocedural risks. In contrast, for younger and middle-aged patients with a lower pretest probability of coronary artery disease and without stents, the spectral scan mode usually provides sufficient diagnostic information.

Thoughtful choice of the image reconstruction kernel for UHR scans is indispensable, as the kernel sharpness level has a direct impact on coronary plaque characterization. Higher kernel strengths increase vessel sharpness but, on the counterpart, increase image noise resulting in lower CNR.15 Additionally, strength of the iterative reconstruction, field of view as well as matrix size need to be carefully selected to ensure that they do not limit the spatial resolution of the UHR images.15 Interestingly, coronary CT angiography in the UHR mode with a sharp image kernel improves coronary plaque characterization by providing superior visualization of fibrotic and lipid-rich plaque components,16 and enhances coronary stenosis grading by reducing blooming artefacts.11 The UHR mode was also found to be reliable for coronary in-stent lumen visualization and stent patency assessment, potentially avoiding unnecessary invasive downstream testing.14 Similar to lumen visualization adjacent to calcified plaques, in-stent lumen visualization is highly influenced by blooming artefacts and is therefore dependent on the optimal image reconstruction kernel.17 Currently, reported scan acquisition and image reconstruction parameters in the literature vary widely, which may contribute to differing findings. No universally accepted method has been established yet.

Precious decisions on the right scan mode

In coronary angiography with photon-counting detector CT, imagers are forced to make wise choices to maximize the diagnostic yield of each examination and optimize patient outcomes. The numerous options offered by the new technology constitute a challenge and we are not yet in a position to propose an ultimate decision tree that includes the optimal scan and reconstruction protocol for each clinical situation. Should the decision on the ideal scan mode be based on patient age, pre-test probability of coronary artery disease, or coronary calcium score? What are the optimal scan parameters, including radiation dose, and what is the most appropriate contrast media protocol? When opting for the spectral scan mode, which monoenergetic level and other post-processing options should be used, and how? On top, each decision may influence the results of advanced post-processing tools, such as CT fractional flow reserve measurements, quantification of coronary plaque components, or evaluation of pericoronary fat attenuation.

Currently, all centres adopting the photon-counting detector CT technology have developed their own decision trees for acquiring coronary angiography image data based on personal experience. Randomized, controlled multicentre clinical trials appear essential to identify the optimal imaging approach.

In the near future, a third coronary CT angiography scan mode, combining UHR and spectral information, is expected to become clinically available with a detector collimation of 96 × 0.2 mm. The narrow detector collimation is adjusted to accommodate hardware constraints. This scan mode could simplify the choice between the various options, and research is awaited on this topic to further clarify the issues mentioned above.

Conclusion

Photon-counting detector CT has undoubtedly made a considerable impact on coronary artery imaging, pushing boundaries. However, the challenging yet valuable decisions mentioned above require additional scientific work on coronary angiography with new photon-counting detector CT. The community is in debt to provide answers to these issues, hopefully in the near future.
