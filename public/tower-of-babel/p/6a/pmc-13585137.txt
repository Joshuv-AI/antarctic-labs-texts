Molecular Properties of a Novel Inward Proton Pump Rhodopsin, GhXeR
Authors: Nanako Hattori, Yuma Ito, Yuji Furutani, Tatsuro Nishikino, Hideki Kandori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Two kinds of proton pump functions are found in microbial rhodopsins: outward- and inward-directed pumps driven by light. While outward proton pumps convert light energy into proton motive force, the physiological role of light-driven inward proton pumps has not been well understood. In this study, we investigated the molecular properties of a xenorhodopsin (XeR) from the marine bacterium Guptibacillus hwajinpoensis (GhXeR). GhXeR works as an inward proton pump similar to many other XeRs when expressed heterologously in E. coli cells. The proton pump activity per unit time was similar between GhXeR and NsXeR, the known optogenetic tool, whereas GhXeR changed solvent pH for a much longer duration than NsXeR. This suggests that GhXeR is a superior optogenetic tool. Low-temperature UV–visible and FTIR spectroscopy revealed that the primary photoreaction of GhXeR is the retinal photoisomerization from the all-trans to 13-cis form. At 77 K, the chromophore is highly distorted after isomerization, and the peptide backbone structure is not largely changed, suggesting that light energy is stored by the distorted chromophore structure. The protonated Schiff base forms a hydrogen bond in GhXeR, and retinal isomerization weakens the hydrogen bond. Structural changes at 77 K include surrounding side chains such as protonated carboxylic acids and internal water molecules. Since GhXeR is the only rhodopsin in the cultivatable bacterium, GhXeR will be used for understanding the physiological role of the inward proton pump in addition to molecular mechanism studies.

Introduction

The first discovered rhodopsin from microbes was bacteriorhodopsin (BR), a light-driven proton pump found in halophilic archaea. About 30 years later, metagenomic analysis identified thousands of new microbial rhodopsins from marine bacteria. The new rhodopsin was called proteorhodopsin (PR), also a light-driven proton pump, and it was estimated that 50% of microbes in the photic zone possess PR genes. − Diverse functions of microbial rhodopsins have been so far revealed, including light-driven ion pumps, light-gated ion channels, light sensors, and light-activated enzymes, whereas light-driven proton pumps are the most abundant microbial rhodopsins on Earth. − Light-driven proton pumps such as BR and PR are all outward-directed, generating proton motive force across a biological membrane that is used for ATP synthesis (Figure a). Thus, light-driven proton-pumping rhodopsins contribute to light energy conversion in microbes. In optogenetics, light-driven proton-pumping rhodopsins have been used for neural silencing by cellular hyperpolarization, acidification of cell organelles, apoptotic cell death, etc. −

In this view, light-driven inward proton pumps are highly unlikely in nature as they compete with ATP synthesis (Figure a). In 2009, we engineered an inward proton pump by a single amino acid replacement (D217E) of Anabaena sensory rhodopsin (ASR), a photochromic light sensor. An inward proton pump by the ASR mutant enabled the alkalization of cell organelles in optogenetics. An optogenetic tool PhotonSABER was designed by using D217E ASR and was used to study long-term depression that is a synaptic plasticity model in various neuronal circuits. Interestingly, it was found that nature created inward proton-pumping rhodopsins such as xenorhodopsin from Parvularcula oceani (PoXeR) and Nanosalina xenorhodopsin (NsXeR). , PoXeR and NsXeR are bacterial and archaeal rhodopsins, indicating that XeRs are distributed in different domains of life. ASR also belongs to a family of xenorhodopsins (XeRs), although ASR is not an inward proton pump. In addition to XeRs, schizorhodopsins (SzRs) were identified as a rhodopsin family found in Asgard archaea, which were also light-driven inward proton pumps. , XeRs and SzRs thus constitute a new and unexpected rhodopsin family (Figures b and S1), though the physiological role of an inward proton pump has not been well understood. In optogenetics, light-driven inward proton-pumping rhodopsins have been used for neural excitation by cellular depolarization and the alkalization of cell organelles. ,,

Protein structures of conventional outward and novel inward proton-pumping rhodopsins resemble each other very well, ,− which attracts the understanding of their molecular mechanisms. Therefore, the molecular properties of inward proton pumps have been extensively investigated mainly by spectroscopic methods, whose targets were XeRs ,,,,− and SzRs. ,,,,− One of the factors discriminating the outward or inward direction is the number of counterions of the protonated Schiff base. It is known that outward or inward proton-pumping rhodopsins contain two or one negatively charged counterion(s), respectively (Figures c and S2). In BR, D85 and D212 act as the Schiff base counterions in the C-helix (TM3) and G-helix (TM7), respectively, with D85 accepting the Schiff base proton during the photocycle (Figure c,d). In contrast, the counterion in the G-helix or C-helix is neutralized in XeRs or SzRs, respectively (Figure c,e,f). Consequently, the single counterion cannot act as the proton acceptor of the Schiff base, and the released proton from the Schiff base is transferred through hydrogen bonds to the cytoplasmic side (inward proton pump). This is supported by the successful functional conversion of an outward into an inward proton pump by neutralizing a counterion. ,

A mechanistic understanding of the inward proton pump has been rapidly gained in this decade. However, the mechanism of the outward proton pump has been much more understood in the past 50 years. ,− In the case of XeRs, molecular properties were studied for four proteins, PoXeR, ,, NsXeR, ,− RmXeR, and BcXeR. Here, we focused on a XeR from the marine bacterium Guptibacillus hwajinpoensis (GhXeR). GhXeR is highly homologous to the inward proton pumps BcXeR (58.3% identity), PoXeR (47.8% identity), and RmXeR (44.4% identity) but also homologous to the sensor protein ASR (45.3% identity), while being less homologous to the inward proton pump NsXeR (29.3% identity) (Table ). It is thus important to examine first the proton-pump activity of GhXeR. In this study, we expressed GhXeR in E. coli cells, followed by measuring proton transport activity using a pH electrode. Then, GhXeR proteins were purified, and their molecular properties were studied by transient absorption spectroscopy at room temperature and by low-temperature UV–visible and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy at 77 K. The obtained data clearly showed GhXeR pumping protons inwardly, with activity similar to that of NsXeR and much higher than that of PoXeR. Spectroscopic studies provided both common and unique features of GhXeR. The molecular mechanism of GhXeR will be discussed on the basis of the present results.

Materials and Methods

Plasmid Construction for GhXeR Expression

Routine DNA manipulations were carried out according to standard procedures. To express GhXeR (NCBI protein accession number: TKD72086.1) from a plasmid named pHIDA10, an E. coli codon-optimized gene fragment encoding a C-terminal hexa-histidine (His6) tag for protein purification was cloned into the pET21a vector (Novagen) between the ATG start codon in an NdeI enzyme restriction site and the TAG stop codon using In-Fusion cloning.

Phylogenetic Analysis

Amino acid sequence alignments were generated by using MUSCLE. The optimal amino acid substitution model was selected in MEGA11 based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Phylogenetic trees were inferred using the maximum-likelihood method implemented in IQ-TREE, applying the LG + G substitution model in Figure b or the LG + G + F model in Figure S1. Branch support values were estimated from 1000 ultrafast bootstrap iterations. Sequence data for the other rhodopsins were obtained from the GenBank database.

Sample Preparation

A pHIDA10 plasmid was transformed into E. coli C41­(DE3) cells by a standard method using CaCl2. For preculture, cells were grown overnight at 37 °C with rotary shaking (180 rpm) in LB medium (1% (w/v) tryptone, 0.5% (w/v) yeast extract, 0.5% (w/v) NaCl). The culture was then transferred to 2 L of 2× TY medium (1.6% (w/v) tryptone, 1.0% (w/v) yeast extract, and 0.5% (w/v) NaCl) and incubated at 37 °C until an optical density of 0.4–0.6 at 660 nm was reached. Protein expression was induced by adding 1 mM isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and 10 μM all-trans retinal (ATR), followed by at least 4 h of incubation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 7 mL of buffer A (50 mM tris­(hydroxymethyl)­aminomethane (Tris)–HCl [pH 8.0], 5 mM MgCl2) per 1 g (wet weight) of the cells, and cells were disrupted using a French press (Ohtake). The membrane fractions were collected by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C and further sedimented using ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C. The membrane fragments were resuspended in the original volume of buffer S (50 mM 2-(N-morpholino) ethane-sulfonic acid (MES)-NaOH [pH 6.5], 0.3 M NaCl, 5 mM imidazole) and homogenized. Solubilization of GhXeR-His6 was carried out by incubating the suspension with 1% (w/v) n-dodecyl-β-d-maltopyranoside (DDM) at 4 °C overnight under constant stirring. The solubilized fraction was isolated by ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Purification was performed using a TALON Metal Affinity Resin (Takara) affinity chromatography column, pre-equilibrated with buffer S. The column was washed with buffer W (50 mM MES-NaOH [pH 6.5], 0.3 M NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 0.1% DDM), and GhXeR-His6 was eluted with buffer E (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 0.1 M NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 0.03% DDM). Excess imidazole was removed by dialysis against buffer D (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 0.3 M NaCl, 0.03% DDM). The final protein concentration was determined by measuring visible absorption at λmax (565 nm) using a UV–Vis spectrometer (V-730, JASCO).

Assay of Light-Driven Ion-Pumping Activity of GhXeR

The light-induced proton-pumping activities were measured according to previous reports. ,, E. coli C41­(DE3) cells expressing GhXeR were harvested by centrifugation (8000× g, 5 min), washed three times, and resuspended in a 100 mM NaCl solution. A 7.5 mL cell suspension was kept in the dark before illumination with >500 nm light (Y-52 cutoff filter) using a 1 kW tungsten–halogen projector lamp (Rikagaku, Japan) with a power density of 30.0 mW/cm2. Light-induced pH changes were monitored using a pH electrode (HORIBA, Japan). The assay was repeated under identical conditions following the addition of 2.5 μL of a protonophore solution (30 mM carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP)), yielding a final concentration of 7.5 μM. The ΔpH value was calibrated by adding 10 μL of 10 mM HCl after every light-induced pH change experiment.

Quantification of GhXeR Expressed in E. coli cells

E. coli C41­(DE3) cells expressing GhXeR were harvested by centrifugation (8000× g, 4 °C) and resuspended in buffer F (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0] and 100 mM NaCl) to a final volume of 2.4 mL. 0.3 mL of Lysozyme (10 mg/mL) was added, and the suspension was gently stirred at 4 °C for 1 h. The cells were then disrupted by sonication (TAITEC, Japan) and 0.3 mL of buffer G (50 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl, and 10% DDM) was added to solubilize the broken cells. The absorption change due to rhodopsin bleaching by hydroxylamine (HA) was measured using a UV–Vis spectrometer (Shimadzu, Japan) with an integrating sphere. HA was added to a final concentration of 200 mM, followed by illumination at >500 nm using a 1 kW tungsten–halogen projector lamp (Rikagaku, Japan) with a Y-52 glass filter (Toshiba). The molecular extinction coefficient of GhXeR (ε = 52,700 M–1 cm–1 at 565 nm) was determined by comparing the absorbance of purified GhXeR with that of retinal oxime (ε = 33,600 M–1 cm–1 at 360 nm) produced by HA bleaching. The expression level of GhXeR in E. coli was determined by measuring the absorbance of the bleached GhXeR, and proton transport activity was quantified from the initial slope of the pH change, normalized by protein expression levels.

Flash Photolysis Measurements

Purified GhXeR samples were resuspended in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 0.3 M NaCl, and 0.03% DDM. A 0.6 mL aliquot of each sample was placed in a cuvette (1 cm path length) held in a temperature-controlled holder (Flash300, Quantum Northwest, USA). The sample temperature was maintained at 25 °C using an embedded Peltier device and a circulating water jacket connected to a thermostat bath (Alpha RA8, Lauda). Each sample was illuminated with a nanosecond-pulsed Nd3+:YAG laser (λ = 532 nm, INDI40, Spectra Physics) at an energy density of 2.0 mJ/cm2 per pulse. The repetition interval was adjusted to be sufficiently longer than the photocycle time of each sample, preventing unintended photoexcitation of transient intermediates (15 s). The absorption changes following laser excitation were probed using monochromatic light at 380, 410, 480, 565, or 600 nm, generated by an Xe arc lamp (L9289-01, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). The wavelength of the probe light for the K intermediate and the dark state was selected to 600/565 nm for GhXeR. Probe light detection was performed using a photomultiplier tube (R10699, Hamamatsu Photonics) connected to a digital storage oscilloscope (DPO7104 or MSO22 2-BW-500, Tektronix, Japan) through a resistance of 500 Ω, 1 kΩ, or 5 kΩ. Time-resolved transient absorption data were analyzed using Igor Pro (version 9) and the Global Fit package, applying multiple exponential functions to determine decay time constants and pre-exponential factors.

Low-Temperature UV–Visible and FTIR Spectroscopy

The concentration of GhXeR was estimated from its retinal absorption for UV–Vis and FTIR spectroscopy. Proteins were reconstituted into a lipid membrane composed of 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-nolamine-3-phosphoethanolamine (POPE) and 1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-phosphatidylglycerol (POPG) (molar ratio 3:1) at a protein-to-lipid molar ratio of 1:20 by removing DDM with Bio-Beads (SM-2, Bio-Rad). The reconstituted samples were washed three times with 1 mM NaCl and 2 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8.0). The pellet was resuspended in the same buffer and adjusted to a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. A 60 μL aliquot was placed on a BaF2 window and dried using an aspirator. For low-temperature FTIR measurements, GhXeR films were hydrated with H2O, D2O, or D2 18O before analysis. The hydration levels were confirmed by O–H or O–D stretching vibrations as described previously. , The hydrated sample was mounted on a sample holder and placed in a cryostat (Optistat DN2, Oxford Instruments) attached to an FTIR spectrometer (Cary 670, Agilent Technologies Japan, Ltd.). The sample was cooled to 77 K using liquid nitrogen, with temperature regulation at ±0.1 K precision. To generate the K intermediate, GhXeR was illuminated at 77 K with 530 nm light (Toshiba KL54 interference filter) from a 300 W xenon lamp (Max-303, Asahi Spectra) for 2 min. The K intermediate was reverted to the dark state by exposure to >590 nm light (R-61 cutoff filter, Toshiba) for 2 min. For FTIR spectroscopy, interferograms were collected before and after photoreactions, each constructed from 128 scans. Multiple irradiation sequences were averaged: 40 spectra for H2O-hydrated GhXeR and 100 spectra for D2O- and D2 18O-hydrated samples.

Results

Inward Proton-Pump Activity of GhXeR

We tested the ion-transport functions of GhXeR using heterologous expression of C-terminally His-tagged proteins in E. coli. For GhXeR-expressing cells, we observed a light-induced pH increase but the signals disappeared in the presence of CCCP (Figure a). This indicates the inward proton transport function of GhXeR by light, similar to the well-known inward proton pump, NsXeR. GhXeR is homologous to a sensor protein ASR but functions as an inward proton pump.

To compare the inward proton pump activity of GhXeR with those of other inward proton pumps quantitatively, the initial slope of pH changes was analyzed according to the method recently applied for NsXeR. Figure b shows that the inward proton pump activity of GhXeR is comparable to that of NsXeR, and about 5 times larger than that of PoXeR (Table ). These results suggest the suitability of GhXeR as an optogenetic tool. Interestingly, the kinetic features of GhXeR and NsXeR were different, although both initial slopes were identical (Figure a). In the case of NsXeR, the pH change was saturated at 50 s after continuous illumination. In contrast, the pH change of GhXeR increased linearly, so that light-induced pH changes were larger after 150 s of illumination in GhXeR than in NsXeR (Figure a). In general, rhodopsin causes the initial pH change but is not associated with any subsequent process, and in Figure a, transport experiments were performed under identical conditions, except for expressing GhXeR or NsXeR in E. coli cells. The mechanism is unclear at present, while GhXeR is likely to be superior in optogenetics to NsXeR.

Photocycle of GhXeR

We next studied the photocycle of the dark-adapted GhXeR protein, where single-wavelength kinetics were measured after excitation at 532 nm. Figure a shows the absorption spectrum of the GhXeR sample (λmax = 565 nm) in the detergent, which looks purple. Upon photoexcitation, bleaching of GhXeR was observed within several microseconds, together with a transient absorption at 480 nm, which is characteristic of the blue-shifted L intermediate (Figure b). Then, the L-specific transient absorption at 480 nm decays, while the absorptions at 380 and 410 nm appear concomitantly. These are characteristic absorptions of the M intermediate containing a deprotonated Schiff base, whose formation time constant was 1.01 ms (τ2 in Figure b). The M intermediate decays in 27.8 ms (τ3 in Figure b), but the transient absorption does not return to zero after >1 s, indicating the presence of another long-lived intermediate state. The long-lived state returns to the initial state with the time constants of 3.3 s (τ4 in Figure b) and 8.8 s (τ5 in Figure b). Global analysis depicted an additional early time constant of 3.18 μs (τ1), although the data are noisy at the microsecond time scale (Table ).

Based on the results in Figure b, we summarized the photocycle model of GhXeR in Figure c. In the present transient absorption experiment, the red-shifted K intermediate was not clearly observed. The lack of the K intermediate is probably because of limited time resolution, and low-temperature UV–visible spectroscopy clearly shows the presence of the K intermediate below. The M intermediate is a key state in inward proton pumps, as the Schiff base deprotonation accompanies the proton transfer to the cytoplasmic region. In the case of NsXeR, complex kinetics were obtained for the M formation, where two rise components, 110 and 217 μs, were observed, together with an additional time constant at 2.48 ms. As a result, two traces at 380 and 410 nm differ significantly. Similarly, complex kinetics were reported for the formation and decay of the M intermediate in BcXeR. On the other hand, the formation and decay kinetics of the M intermediate in GhXeR can be described by a single time constant, yielding an identical shape of the time traces at 380 and 410 nm (Figure b). The M decay in 27.8 ms corresponds to the proton uptake from the extracellular region, and this value is slower than that of NsXeR (16.7 ms).

Photocycles of microbial rhodopsins are generally described by a single-sequential model. In the case of the inward proton pump PoXeR, however, the M decay was shown to be a branching reaction, one is directly reverted to the original state (43%), and another is converted into the PoXeR* state (57%) that presumably binds the 13-cis, 15-syn chromophore. A similar kinetic feature of GhXeR to those of PoXeR and NsXeR strongly suggests that GhXeR also has a branching reaction. The branching reaction is consistent with the fast repeated photoreaction of NsXeR observed in optogenetic experiments. It should be noted that this study did not determine the individual time constants of the M to GhXeR and M to GhXeR* processes, but the time constant of the M decay was determined (Figure c).

Low-Temperature FTIR Spectroscopy of GhXeR

Light-induced spectral changes were first examined by low-temperature UV–visible spectroscopy. The GhXeR protein was reconstituted into lipids, which were dried on the cell and rehydrated. Illumination of GhXeR at 530 nm for 2 min at 77 K caused a spectral red shift, as shown in the difference spectrum with the negative and positive peaks at 520/556 and 606 nm, respectively (black line in Figure ). This indicates the formation of the K intermediate, which is commonly observed in animal and microbial rhodopsins. , Subsequent illumination at >590 nm resulted in complete photoreversion to the original state as shown by the mirror-imaged difference spectrum (red line in Figure ). We then applied FTIR analysis under the same illumination conditions.

C–C stretching vibrations of the retinal chromophore appear at 1250–1100 cm–1. In this frequency region, GhXeR exhibits peaks at 1200 (−), 1195 (+), and 1173 (+) cm–1, which are characteristic of the isomerization from the all-trans to the 13-cis form. The primary photoreaction in GhXeR is retinal photoisomerization, forming the red-shifted K intermediate (Figure ). This is also the case in other inward proton pumps and BR. ,

Ethylenic stretching vibration (CC stretch) of the retinal chromophore appears at 1550–1500 cm–1. The negative band at 1532 cm–1 in GhXeR corresponds to the ethylenic stretching vibration of the all-trans chromophore, which is in good agreement with a well-known linear correlation with the absorption maximum. From the linear correlation, we expect a downshift of the positive peak upon formation of the red-shifted K intermediate, as is shown in BR (1530 cm–1 (−)/1514 cm–1 (+)). However, the K formation in GhXeR accompanies an upshifted positive peak at 1543 cm–1 together with a small downshifted peak at 1523 cm–1. This observation apparently contradicts the spectral red-shift in the UV–visible region (Figure ). Interestingly, this trend is common for other inward proton pumps, including PoXeR, NsXeR, and SzR1 (Figure ).

Figure S3 summarizes the published difference FTIR spectra at 77 K in this frequency region. Light-driven outward proton-pumping rhodopsins such as proteorhodopsin (PR) and gloeobacterrhodopsin (GR) exhibit a downshift like BR (Figure S3a), which was also the case for the light-sensor pharaonis sensory rhodopsin II (pSRII), enzyme rhodopsin Rh-PDE, and the light-driven sodium pump KR2 (Figure S3b). These rhodopsins contain two carboxylates near the Schiff base (Figure S3e), both of which act as Schiff base counterions. In contrast, light-driven inward proton-pumping rhodopsins exhibit an upshift (Figure S3c), which has a single carboxylate near the Schiff base (Figure S3c). Other microbial rhodopsins with a single carboxylate near the Schiff base (Figure S3e) are assembled in Figure S3d, including the light-driven chloride pumps HR and FR, the photochromic sensor ASR, TAT rhodopsin, and heliorhodopsin from Thermoplasmatales archaeon SG8-52-1 (TaHeR), whose functions remain unknown. An upshift was found for HR, ASR, and TaHeR, whereas FR and TAT show normal downshifted features. It should be noted that the light-driven chloride pumps HR and FR have a single carboxylate, but the Schiff base has two counterions because of chloride binding. Thus, there is a certain correlation between up/down shifts and the number of counterions, but this is not perfect.

In the 1500–1450 cm–1 region, an intense band appears only on the negative side (1448 cm–1 in GhXeR), which was similarly observed for PoXeR, NsXeR, and SzR1, but not for BR. Such a strong negative band was first observed in the light-induced difference FTIR spectra of ASR at 77 K. This frequency region corresponds to the imide II vibrations of proline, which originate from the unique backbone structure of proline. Proline is highly conserved near the retinal Schiff base in XeRs, which corresponds to the position of D212 in BR (Figure c). Nevertheless, its origin is not so simple, as summarized in Figure S4. The negative 1448 cm–1 band in GhXeR was H/D unexchangeable, as is expected for imide II vibrations of proline. On the other hand, the negative 1449 cm–1 band in PoXeR is upshifted to 1457 cm–1 in D2O, and the negative 1446 cm–1 band in NsXeR is slightly upshifted in D2O. Although SzRs have no proline near the Schiff base (Figures c and S2), a similar negative peak was observed at 1444 cm–1 for SzR1, which disappeared in D2O completely. These results suggest complex origins for these bands.

In Figure , difference spectra are compared for hydrogen-out-of-plane (HOOP) bands, where retinal distortion upon photoisomerization is signified as the positive bands. If peaks in D2O are shifted from those in H2O, they originate from HOOP vibrations close to the Schiff base because the protonated Schiff base only differs in the retinal chromophore between H2O (CNH) and D2O (CND). In GhXeR, H/D exchangeable bands were observed at 1001 cm–1 and 974 cm–1, while bands at 966 cm–1 and 956 cm–1 were H/D unexchangeable. Figure clearly illustrates different intensities of HOOP bands among rhodopsins. NsXeR exhibits small HOOP bands, which was also the case for SzR1. In contrast, GhXeR and PoXeR show strong positive HOOP bands as well as BR, an outward proton pump. This suggests that chromophore distortions depend on the local environments of each rhodopsin, not on the function of rhodopsin.

Figure compares difference spectra in the 1800–1580 cm–1 region, in which there are various vibrations from proteins such as the amide-I vibration (CO stretches of the peptide backbone). An exceptional chromophore vibration in this region is the CN stretch of the protonated Schiff base. The CN stretching and N–H bending vibrations of the Schiff base are coupled, and thus CNH stretch is up-shifted from CND stretch. Consequently, the frequency difference between H2O and D2O is a measure of the hydrogen-bonding strength of the N–H group in the Schiff base. In the case of GhXeR, the bands at 1646 (−)/1622 (+) cm–1 in H2O and at 1624 (−)/1608 (+) cm–1 in D2O are candidates for the CNH and CND stretches, respectively. The difference between the CNH and CND stretches in the dark state was 22 cm–1 for GhXeR, 19 cm–1 for PoXeR, 20 cm–1 for NsXeR, 15 cm–1 for SzR1, and 12 cm–1 for BR. Therefore, the hydrogen bond of the Schiff base is strong in XeRs. In the K intermediate, the frequency difference in GhXeR (14 cm–1) is also similar to those of PoXeR (13 cm–1) and NsXeR (13 cm–1), while those of SzR1 and BR are unclear. GhXeR weakens the hydrogen bond of the Schiff base upon retinal photoisomerization, which was also the case in PoXeR and NsXeR.

H/D unexchangeable bands at 1670–1620 cm–1 originate from amide-I vibrations that respond to secondary structural alterations. Although the presence of the H/D exchangeable CN stretch of the retinal Schiff base complicates the analysis of amide-I vibrations, bands at 1670–1640 cm–1 in D2O (red lines in Figure ) are probably owing to amide-I because of the downshifted Schiff base CN stretch in D2O. Spectral changes at 1670–1640 cm–1 in D2O are smaller in GhXeR, PoXeR, and NsXeR than in SzR1 and BR. It is likely that GhXeR does not change the peptide backbone structure, while the retinal chromophore is highly distorted.

CO stretching vibrations of protonated carboxylic acids appear at 1760–1700 cm–1 (Figure ). In the case of BR, the 1742 (−)/1732 (+) cm–1 peaks in H2O are down-shifted to 1727 (−)/1721 (+) cm–1 in D2O, and these bands were assigned to the CO stretches of D115. In GhXeR, a peak pair appears at 1741 (−)/1736 (+) cm–1 in H2O, and at 1733 (−)/1728 (+) cm–1 in D2O. Similar peaks were observed at 1740 (−)/1733 (+) cm–1 in PoXeR, and identified as the CO stretch of D108 using the D108N mutant. In NsXeR, an up-shifted peak pair at 1762 (−)/1758 (+) cm–1 was observed, and the negative band was previously assigned to E111. The corresponding residue in GhXeR is D117 (Figure S2), and it is thus likely that the band in GhXeR originates from D117. The lower frequency shift indicates a strengthened hydrogen bond, which is common for GhXeR, PoXeR, and NsXeR.

Difference spectra at 2800–2000 cm–1 contain the X–D stretching vibrations of protein and water molecules (Figure ). Vibrational bands differing between D2O and D2 18O identify O–D stretching vibrations of water molecules, which alter their O–D stretching frequencies when retinal isomerizes by light. Water bands are widely distributed from 2690 cm–1 to 2174 cm–1 (>500 cm–1) in BR, while they are limited in frequency in inward proton pumping rhodopsins. The obtained spectra of GhXeR showed clear water bands at 2681 (+), 2667 (−), 2657 (+), and 2594 (−) cm–1 (Figure ). On the other hand, spectral shifts caused by isotope water were unclear at <2500 cm–1 for GhXeR, which was also the case for NsXeR. In contrast, PoXeR has a strongly hydrogen-bonded water at 2277 cm–1.

By use of 15N Lys labeled protein, N–D stretching vibrations of the Schiff base were identified at 2475 (+) and 2236 (−) cm–1 in NsXeR, and at 2466 (+), 2174 (−), and 2124 (−) cm–1 in BR. , In GhXeR, we observed negative peaks at 2172 and 2127 cm–1, which are most likely attributable to the N–D stretching vibration of the Schiff base. Therefore, hydrogen bonding strength of the Schiff base is similar between GhXeR and BR, though the analysis of CN stretching bands suggested that the Schiff base hydrogen bond in GhXeR is stronger than in BR. The corresponding positive bands in GhXeR are located at >2300 cm–1, which indicates that the hydrogen bond of the Schiff base becomes weak upon retinal isomerization, as well as the case in other microbial rhodopsins. ,

Discussion

Light-driven inward proton-pumping rhodopsins are potential optogenetic tools for neuronal depolarization. In fact, NsXeR enabled fast, neural photostimulation with a firing success rate of 100% up to a frequency of 40 Hz. The time constant of the main recovery of NsXeR (16.7 ms) probably contributes to the fast response for optogenetics. In this sense, the slower recovery of GhXeR (27.8 ms) may be less useful as an optogenetic tool (Figure ). On the other hand, the proton pump activity measurements made using a pH electrode and E. coli cells provided interesting results. While the activity evaluated by the initial slope of pH change was similar between GhXeR and NsXeR (Figure b), such performance of pH change was maintained longer in GhXeR than in NsXeR (Figure a). The expression of each protein was similar (1.57 nmol GhXeR and 2.3 nmol NsXeR), and the amount was normalized (Table ), indicating that each performance could be quantitatively compared. The photocycle of GhXeR by single-photon excitation resembled that of NsXeR, and the photoreaction of GhXeR* containing the 13-cis, 15-syn retinal might affect the difference (Figure ). It should be noted that the inward proton-pump activity evaluated by the initial slope is higher in SzRs than in XeRs such as NsXeR and GhXeR. Nevertheless, the pH change was also saturated in a short time for SzRs. Thus, GhXeR is likely to be a potential tool for long-term optogenetics.

In NsXeR and BcXeR, the formation kinetics of the M intermediate are complex, described by multiple time constants as the rise components. , Consequently, their photocycles contain multiple M states, some of which are in equilibrium with other intermediates, such as L. , This suggests the presence of a complex proton release process from the Schiff base. Recent crystallographic structures of the L and M intermediates of BcXeR reported the presence of an internal hydrogen bond and their changes induced by light, forming proton wires from the Schiff base. GhXeR presumably contains a similar hydrogen-bonding network in the cytoplasmic region, which drives the proton release. A single time constant (1.01 ms) for the M formation in GhXeR suggests a simple mechanism for the proton transfer reaction. The M decay in GhXeR (27.8 ms) is slower than in NsXeR (16.7 ms) but faster than in BcXeR (70 ms), and these differences presumably originate from proton uptake on the extracellular side or thermal isomerization from the 13-cis form.

FTIR spectroscopy at 77 K provided further detailed structural information on GhXeR. GhXeR shows normal photoisomerization characteristics like many other rhodopsins, including all-trans to 13-cis isomerization (Figure ) and induced chromophore distortion (Figure ). The protonated Schiff base forms a hydrogen bond in the dark, which is weakened upon retinal isomerization, during which protein and internal water molecules are rearranged (Figures and ). While these features are common among many microbial rhodopsins, including inward proton pumps, unique features were observed in GhXeR. Chromophore distortion by photoisomerization probed by HOOP bands is larger in GhXeR and PoXeR than in NsXeR and SzR1 (Figure ). Spectral changes at 1670–1640 cm–1 in D2O monitored structural changes of the peptide backbone, which were larger in SzR1 than in GhXeR (Figure ). It is thus likely that GhXeR stores light energy in the chromophore distortion without altering the secondary structure at 77 K. On the other hand, chromophore distortion is considerably relaxed at 77 K, leading to alterations in the secondary structure.

One of the intriguing FTIR data points was the CC stretching vibration of the retinal chromophore. It is generally believed that the formation of the K intermediate accompanies a downshift of the CC stretch from the well-known linear correlation between absorption maxima and CC stretch frequency. It was known that the K formation accompanies an upshift in the CC stretch region in inward proton pumps, such as the proton-pumping ASR mutant, PoXeR, NsXeR, and SzR1. The present study also added an unexpected example. From the analysis of Figure S3, rhodopsins containing two carboxylates near the Schiff base show normally downshifted difference spectra in the CC stretching region. If a single carboxylate is located near the Schiff base, upshifted spectral features in the CC stretching region can be observed (Figure S3). Specific interaction in the K intermediate with a single counterion may lead to the observation of downshifted peaks, though spectral red shift is common in the UV–visible region. One problem with FTIR spectroscopy is that the amide-II vibration from the peptide backbone appears in the same frequency region, which complicates the analysis. Resonance Raman spectroscopy at 77 K is a more suitable method for this purpose.

Inward proton-pumping rhodopsins commonly display a strong negative peak at ∼1450 cm–1 (Figure S4). ,,, Since the frequency corresponds to the amide II vibrations of proline, and proline is highly conserved near the retinal Schiff base in XeRs, structural changes in amide II are tentatively interpreted. However, such a strong negative peak is observed in SzR1, which has no proline near the Schiff base (Figure ). In addition to inward proton pumps, a light-driven outward proton pump GR displays a similar negative peak (Figure S3a). Detailed spectral analysis in Figure S4 further complicates the interpretation. As these negative bands are strong, identification of these bands needs stable isotopes, which is our future focus.

Finally, the physiological aspect of inward proton-pumping rhodopsins is noted. As the inward proton pump competes with ATP production by ATP synthase, the presence of an inward proton pump was highly unlikely. That is why the engineered light-driven inward proton pump (D217E ASR) surprised researchers. More surprising was the discovery of natural inward proton-pumping rhodopsins such as XeRs , and SzRs. , Although the XeRs and SzRs family is growing and expected to serve as unique optogenetic tools, ,, the physiological role of inward proton-pumping rhodopsins is still unknown. Acidification of the cellular interior by light may provide some biological benefits, though this has never been proven. One reason is that XeRs and SzRs were studied by heterologous expression such as E. coli, and they have never been studied using natural cells. It should be noted that the marine bacterium Guptibacillus hwajinpoensis is cultivatable, and GhXeR is the only rhodopsin gene in this bacterium. This is the reason we studied GhXeR, and further analysis using natural Guptibacillus hwajinpoensis will uncover the real meaning of the presence of a light-driven inward proton pump in nature.

Conclusions

In this paper, we studied a novel inward proton-pumping rhodopsin, GhXeR. Light-driven inward proton pumps are interesting in view of both fundamental and applied research. In addition to the known rhodopsins, PoXeR, ,, NsXeR, ,− RmXeR, and BcXeR, the present study reports the molecular properties of GhXeR. The inward proton-pump activity of GhXeR evaluated by the initial slope of pH change was similar to that of NsXeR, a well-known inward proton pump used for optogenetics. On the other hand, the pH change is observed for a longer duration in GhXeR than in NsXeR, suggesting that GhXeR is a potential tool for long-term optogenetics. Upon light absorption by GhXeR, retinal photoisomerization occurs from all-trans to 13-cis form, by which the chromophore is distorted without altering the peptide backbone structure. The protonated Schiff base forms a hydrogen bond in the dark, which is weakened upon retinal isomerization, causing the protein and internal water molecules to be rearranged. As the marine bacterium Guptibacillus hwajinpoensis is cultivatable and GhXeR is the only rhodopsin gene in this bacterium, GhXeR will serve as a platform to answer the question of why nature created a light-driven inward proton pump.
