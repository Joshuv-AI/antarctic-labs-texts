A Huge Thoracic Aortic Aneurysm as a Rare Complication of Hyperimmunoglobulin E Syndrome
Authors: Leila Bigdelu, Ossama Maadarani, Ali Azari, Ali Heidari-Bakavoli, Zahra Oskoueyan, Zouhair Bitar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHyperimmunoglobulin E syndromes (HIES) are a heterogeneous group of primary immunodeficiencies sharing manifestations including recurrent lung infection, staphylococcal skin abscesses, eczema, and significantly raised serum levels of immunoglobulin E. Arterial aneurysms are among the rare vascular manifestations of HIES that may lead to devastating outcomes. We present a case of a 17-year-old girl who had a known history of HIES since childhood. She manifested a recurrent chest infection and skin rash, and it was discovered that she had a large thoracic descending aortic aneurysm, which required surgical repair to prevent devastating complications.Key Words: aorta, computed tomography, echocardiography

Hyperimmunoglobulin E syndrome (HIES) is a rare primary immunodeficiency disorder that involves combined humoral and cellular immunity deficiencies. Currently, in the literature, the number of documented cases is approximately 300 cases, with an incidence of <1 per million population. Various gene mutations may result in HIES by affecting the alteration of class switching of both helper type 17 and immunoglobulin in activated B cells. Elevated immunoglobulin E (IgE) is the hallmark of HIES. Our case demonstrated a rare vascular complication of HIES with a unique huge size of descending aortic aneurysmal dilatation that occupied the hemithorax of a young female.

History of Presentation

A 17-year-old girl was referred for investigation because of nonspecific left-sided chest and shoulder pain that was related to certain body positions. Initially, her symptoms were intermittent but gradually progressed to daily occurrences that interfered with her everyday activities.

Past Medical History

She was diagnosed with sporadic HIES in early childhood, during which she experienced recurrent pyogenic skin infections, persistent eczematous rash, and recurrent chest infection/pneumonia with pneumatocele, in addition to a persistently high IgE level with eosinophilia. There was no family history of similar manifestation.

Differential Diagnosis

The patient's vital parameters were stable: blood pressure, 120/65 mm Hg; heart rate, 80 beats/min; respiratory rate, 24 breaths/min; and peripheral oxygen saturation, 94%. Her body surface area measured 1.48 m2. Her clinical examination was unremarkable, apart from widespread eczematous-like rash. Her chest radiograph revealed a huge well-defined homogeneous opacity occupying the entire left hemithorax and raised the suspicion of a mass occupying the left hemithorax (Figure 1). The electrocardiography demonstrated sinus rhythm with no ischemic changes.

Investigations

During the transthoracic echocardiographic study, aneurysmal dilatation of the descending thoracic aorta was discovered, extending from the isthmus to the diaphragmatic level and measuring 90 mm at its maximum diameter. A circumferential thrombus was found inside the aneurysmal dilatation (Figure 2). A stenotic segment was found at the end of the diaphragmatic level with accelerated flow before joining the abdominal aorta (Figure 2). The diameter of the abdominal aorta was normal. Notably, an aneurysmal dilatation of the left anterior descending artery was also found. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography aortography and 3-dimensional reconstruction showed a huge aneurysm of the descending thoracic aorta (Figure 3).

Management

Because of the large size of the descending aorta and her daily symptoms, it was decided to perform an operative repair to relieve symptoms and prevent devastating complications. A left lateral thoracotomy incision was made between the ribs, and the fifth rib was resected to improve the exposure. The aneurysm occupied nearly the entire left hemithorax. The aorta was clamped above and below the aneurysm, and a 14-mm-diameter woven Dacron graft was used to anastomose the aorta from the beginning of the descending aorta to the diaphragmatic level (Figure 4). Operative specimens were investigated by a microbiologist, but no evidence to support a mycotic etiology was detected. The postoperative course was complicated with respiratory failure, requiring respiratory support with gradual improvement, and the patient was discharged home in good condition. A prophylactic long-term antibiotic was started and reviewed on an ongoing basis.

Discussion

HIES is considered a disease associated with a group of primary immunodeficiency diseases manifested with a unique combination of elevated IgE levels, recurrent pulmonary and skin infections, and eczematous rashes. HIES can be inherited genetically with autosomal dominant (AD), autosomal recessive (AR), and X-linked variants; however, most cases are sporadic.1

In 1966, Davis et al2 and in 1972, Buckley et al3 separately reported on 2 girls and 2 boys, respectively, who exhibited common symptoms of early-onset severe eczema, recurrent pulmonary infections, and staphylococcal skin cold abscesses. Buckley et al's group additionally found dysmorphic facial features and exceptionally high IgE levels in their 2 boys. The disease of the 2 girls was labeled as Job's syndrome, whereas that of the 2 boys was referred to as Buckley's syndrome. In 1974, Hill et al4 demonstrated that these 2 syndromes describe the same disease, which was then named autosomal dominant HIES (AD-HIES). Grimbacher et al5 thoroughly described the AD-HIES inheritance in 1999 as an AD multisystem disease with both immune and nonimmune manifestations, including skeletal, dental, and connective tissue abnormalities. In 2004, another type of HIES was also identified as autosomal recessive HIES (AR-HIES) by Renner et al.6 They reported patients in families who fulfilled the HIES clinical triad; however, they were distinct phenotypically owing to their susceptibility to cutaneous viral infections and by the absence of connective tissue and skeletal abnormalities.6 The cause for AD-HIES was established as the heterozygous mutations in the signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) gene.7 In contrast, the biallelic mutations in the dedicator of cytokinesis-8 (DOCK8) gene were identified as the most common cause for AR-HIES.8 In the past few years, the heterogeneity of HIES has expanded to other monogenic mutation disorders with the HIES phenotype. STAT3 is involved in many signal transduction pathways, including those of several proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory cytokines. This may explain the immunologic dichotomy of AD-HIES, where manifestations of excessive inflammation and an inadequate immune response coexist. Immunologic dysfunction leads to the following symptoms: eczematous rash, cold abscess, recurrent bacterial chest infection with fungal superinfection, and pneumatocele formation. The nonimmunologic features of the disease include musculoskeletal, dental, craniofacial, and vascular system abnormalities. The patients with AR-HIES usually share similar presentations; however, they are more prone to have increased viral skin infection, more neurologic symptoms, and autoimmunity, as well as the lack of connective tissue and skeletal abnormalities typically seen in AD-HISE. Elevated IgE is considered the hallmark of HIES. An IgE level > 2,000 IU/mL is often regarded as diagnostic;9 however, very low to nondetectable IgE levels may present in patients younger than 6 months. In more than 90% of patients with HISE, eosinophilia count was elevated, that is, >2 standard deviations above the normal range. Genetic testing was available for STAT3 and DOCK8 gene mutations.10 The diagnosis of HIES can be made based on a combination of clinical and laboratory findings for both AD-HIES and AR-HIES. One of the rare manifestations of HIES is vasculopathy and arterial aneurysms, with the most commonly reported cases of vascular abnormalities and aneurysmal formation associated with the coronary and intracranial arteries. The possible pathophysiological mechanisms of the vasculopathy in HIES are due to the effects of STAT3 on vascular remodeling resulting in arterial fragility, rather than an inflammatory process. Moreover, the vasculopathy in HIES can be explained by the reduction of vascular endothelial growth factor and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α in addition to the dysregulation of matrix metalloproteinases that implicated in aneurysm formation.11

The predominant coronary artery abnormalities are tortuosity, ectasia, and aneurysm.12 Moreover, cerebrovascular aneurysms present a vascular manifestation of HIES and may be silent or present symptomatically with rupture, causing significant mortality.13 The management of asymptomatic aneurysms poses a dilemma, as intervention confers considerable risk. The involvement of the aorta as a manifestation of HIES is very rare and limited to a few case reports. Two instances of aneurysmal dilatation of the ascending and descending thoracic aorta were prescribed by van der Meer et al14 in young adolescents as a vascular manifestation of AR-HIES, and both subjects died of respiratory failure. A case of ruptured thoracoabdominal aneurysm was reported in a 27-year-old with AD-HIES and underwent surgical repair.15 A single case of pulmonary artery pseudoaneurysm associated with HIES was described in the literature.16 Despite its rarity, understanding and accurately diagnosing HIES are crucial for effective management and treatment.

Given the silent nature of initial aneurysm development in patients with HIES, it is advisable to undergo an evaluation for possible vascular involvement17 in addition to follow-up imaging every 5 years to exclude aneurysmal dilatation.15

Treatment approaches typically involve a combination of preventive measures, such as prophylactic antibiotics, antifungal drugs, and immunoglobulin replacement therapy.

Our case highlights the successful surgical treatment of a very rare vascular complication of a quiet rare entity. The reported cases of surgical repair of aortic aneurysm as a manifestation of HIES counted as <5 cases.

This case report demonstrated a huge thoracic aortic aneurysm as a complication of HIES in a 17-year-old girl, where the size of the aneurysm reached approximately the size of the entire left hemithorax and led to pressure symptoms and was treated with surgical repair successfully.

Follow-Up

During the 1-month follow-up at the outpatient clinic, she reported being in good health with no symptoms. Her imaging results indicated that the graft position remains stable (Figure 5). She is currently being evaluated by a geneticist and has been confirmed to have a DOCK8 mutation.

Conclusions

Aortic aneurysm can be one of the rare vascular manifestations of HIES and may require surgical repair to prevent devastating complications.
