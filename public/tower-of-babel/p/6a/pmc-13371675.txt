Integrating untargeted metabolomics and machine learning to reveal an aberration of sphingolipid metabolism in cardiometabolic HFpEF
Authors: Xinxin Mao, Lu Zhao, Pengfei Liu, Yajiao Wang, Shuqing Shi, Qingqiao Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a high-risk phenotype primarily driven by metabolic syndrome, with a significantly increased incidence and risk of adverse outcomes. A fundamental reason for this is the lack of early clinical diagnosis. As a tool capable of accurately capturing pathophysiological states, metabolomics provides a critical entry point for addressing this issue; however, studies focusing on the metabolic characteristics of this population remain limited.MethodsThis study integrated a clinical cohort and untargeted metabolomics to compare serum metabolic profiles between patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF and those with metabolic syndrome (MetS). Baseline characteristics were balanced using propensity score matching (PSM). Differential metabolites were identified by untargeted metabolomics, followed by KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. Machine-learning approaches were further applied to screen candidate metabolites with potential diagnostic efficacy, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) together with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were used to evaluate phenotype association and feature contribution. In an independent clinical cohort, total sphingomyelin (SM) levels were assessed by ELISA as an external evaluation strategy based on clinical applicability.ResultsDifferential metabolites between the two groups were mainly enriched in sphingolipid metabolism and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathways. Through multi-method screening, C24:1 Sphingomyelin was identified as a candidate metabolite with potential diagnostic efficacy. The co-expression module containing C24:1 Sphingomyelin was significantly correlated with NT-proBNP, a key biomarker of heart failure, and SHAP analysis indicated that C24:1 Sphingomyelin contributed substantially to the classification model. In the external cohort, total SM levels were associated with disease status, suggesting the potential clinical association of sphingolipid-related signals.ConclusionThis study preliminarily characterized the metabolic features distinguishing cardiometabolic HFpEF from MetS alone, suggesting that sphingolipid dysregulation is associated with the development and progression of this phenotype. Among the identified metabolites, C24:1 Sphingomyelin was identified as a candidate metabolite with potential diagnostic performance, and SM showed potential clinical applicability. These findings provide new clues for biomarker discovery and preliminary clinical translational exploration in cardiometabolic HFpEF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-026-02237-6.

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) has become a major subtype of heart failure (HF), accounting for more than 50% of all HF cases [1, 2]. HFpEF is highly heterogeneous and involves complex pathophysiological processes, including systemic inflammation, coronary microvascular dysfunction, and multiple extracardiac comorbidities such as obesity, diabetes, and hypertension [1, 2]. Among these, cardiometabolic HFpEF is a major clinical phenotype, with metabolic syndrome (MetS) regarded as its "risk state" and a major driver; compared with other pathophysiological subtypes, patients with this phenotype tend to present with more severe symptoms and a worse prognosis [3]. As the prevalence of obesity and metabolic disorders continues to rise worldwide, the burden of HFpEF has become an increasingly important public health challenge [4].

The current clinical diagnosis of HFpEF relies primarily on the HFA-PEFF algorithm, which is heavily dependent on circulating levels of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) or N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT‑proBNP), together with echocardiographic findings [5]. BNP and NT‑proBNP are the standard clinical biomarkers for heart failure; in this study, NT‑proBNP is used as a benchmark for assessing the biological relevance of the newly identified metabolic signals. However, this framework has important limitations in patients with obesity or MetS. Obesity may lower circulating BNP/NT-proBNP levels through increased natriuretic peptide clearance, thereby increasing the risk of underdiagnosis [6]. In addition, the pathophysiological cascade of metabolically driven HFpEF often begins with low-grade systemic inflammation and insulin resistance, followed by coronary microvascular endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide bioavailability, cardiomyocyte stiffening, and interstitial fibrosis [7]. In contrast, structural and functional abnormalities detectable by conventional echocardiography, such as left ventricular hypertrophy and diastolic dysfunction, often appear relatively late [5]. As a result, when imaging abnormalities become evident, the disease may already be at a more advanced stage, and the opportunity for early intervention may have been missed. Consistently, a scientific statement from the Journal of the American College of Cardiology has emphasized that, despite advances in HFpEF research, clinical recognition remains insufficient and improved phenotyping is needed to enable more precise diagnosis and better outcomes [3, 8].

Metabolomics provides a promising systems-level approach for identifying metabolic abnormalities associated with HFpEF. Previous studies have shown that, compared with patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and those without HF, patients with HFpEF—especially those with obesity or diabetes—display distinct metabolic features, including impaired fatty acid oxidation, disrupted branched-chain amino acid catabolism, and reduced tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, collectively suggesting a state of metabolic inflexibility [9]. In addition, studies have shown that, even before clear structural abnormalities such as left ventricular hypertrophy become evident in patients with prediabetes, myocardial energetic reserve is already impaired, as reflected by a reduced phosphocreatine-to-ATP (PCr/ATP) ratio [10, 11]; this trend is further supported by findings from a systematic review [12]. These findings suggest that metabolic abnormalities may represent a key step in the progression from MetS to overt HFpEF. However, most previous metabolomics studies have compared HFpEF with healthy controls or HFrEF [8], while a more clinically relevant question remains insufficiently addressed: which specific metabolic alterations distinguish high-risk individuals with MetS from patients who have already developed cardiometabolic HFpEF?

To address this question, the present study used untargeted metabolomics to compare the serum metabolic profiles of patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF and those with MetS. By contrasting a “disease state” with a “risk state,” we aimed to minimize their shared metabolic background and preliminarily identify specific metabolic features associated with HFpEF, as well as candidate biomarkers with potential diagnostic efficacy. Machine learning, WGCNA, and SHAP were further integrated to prioritize differential metabolites and assess their biological relevance, together with pathway enrichment analysis to characterize the underlying metabolic alterations. In addition, an independent clinical cohort was used for a preliminary external evaluation based on clinical applicability. This study was designed to provide new clues for biomarker discovery and preliminary clinical translation in cardiometabolic HFpEF. Methods

This study included two independent clinical cohorts: one for untargeted metabolomics discovery analysis and the other for external validation based on clinical applicability. Detailed experimental procedures and supplementary methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Study design and population

In the discovery cohort, 44 patients with MetS and 44 patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF were enrolled, all of whom were recruited from Guang’anmen Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences, Beijing, China. The inclusion criteria are provided in the Supplementary Materials. To reduce potential confounding bias, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed at a 1:1 ratio to balance the baseline characteristics between the two groups. The matching variables included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), history of hypertension, dyslipidemia, and elevated blood glucose, serum creatinine (Scr) level, and the use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic medications. This cohort was used for untargeted metabolomics analysis and candidate biomarker screening.

Fasting serum samples were collected from all participants in the morning. After blood collection, the samples were allowed to stand for 2 h. Once serum separation was achieved, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 3500 rpm for 10 min. The serum was then collected and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis and metabolite identification

Untargeted metabolomics profiling was performed using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). The original raw data files were converted into mzXML format using ProteoWizard. Peak detection, peak alignment, and retention time correction were performed using XCMS, and peak areas were corrected using the support vector regression (SVR) method. Peaks with a detection rate of less than 50% within each group were excluded from further analysis.

Metabolite annotation was achieved by searching against an in-house database, integrated public databases, an AI-assisted database, and metDNA. Metabolite identification was based on accurate mass (MS1), retention time (RT), and MS/MS fragmentation information. Metabolites were classified according to identification confidence levels (four levels: Level 1, highest confidence; Level 4, lowest confidence). Only metabolites with confidence levels of 3 or higher (Levels 1–3) were included in subsequent analyses. To evaluate the overall distribution patterns of the samples, unsupervised principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for dimensionality reduction and visualization. Data were unit variance-scaled before PCA. Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) and Pearson correlation analysis were also conducted to assess the overall clustering patterns of samples and metabolites, and the results were visualized as heatmaps.

Preliminary screening of differential metabolites

For two-group comparisons, differential metabolites were initially identified using VIP > 1 and raw P < 0.05 (Student’s t test). VIP values were derived from the orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model implemented in MetaboAnalystR. Data were log2-transformed and mean-centered before modeling. A 200-permutation test was performed to assess model overfitting.

In addition, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied in the univariate analysis, and the corresponding FDR values were reported in the differential metabolite results table. As this was an exploratory study, FDR was not used as the primary threshold for the initial screening of differential metabolites, but FDR < 0.05 was applied as a stricter criterion in the subsequent machine-learning–based screening of candidate biomarkers.

KEGG annotation and enrichment analysis

Identified metabolites were annotated using the KEGG Compound database and subsequently mapped to the KEGG Pathway database. Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using a hypergeometric test to identify significantly enriched metabolic pathways between the two groups.

Machine learning-based screening of candidate biomarkers

To identify candidate biomarkers, this study employed two complementary feature selection methods, elastic net and random forest (RF), for the screening and robustness evaluation of differential metabolites. All metabolite quantitative data were log₂-transformed prior to analysis.

Training and validation set partitioning

Samples from the discovery cohort were randomly partitioned into a training set (n = 58) and a validation set (n = 30) at a ratio of 2:1. The training set was used for feature selection, while the validation set was used for internal evaluation.

Elastic net feature selection

The mixing parameter α was automatically optimized to 0.5 using the caret package. The penalty parameter λ was determined through tenfold cross-validation, and the more conservative lambda.1se (one standard error above the minimum λ) was adopted as the final model parameter to reduce model complexity and mitigate the risk of overfitting. To further enhance the stability of feature selection, the elastic net analysis was independently repeated 500 times. In each iteration, 80% of the training set samples were randomly drawn without replacement for model construction, and only metabolites selected in at least 90% of the iterations (i.e., frequency ≥ 450) were retained as stable features. This ensured that the elastic net feature set was derived from metabolites that consistently emerged across repeated analyses rather than from a single model run.

Random forest feature selection

In parallel, random forest analysis was performed using the R package varSelRF as a complementary feature selection strategy. Random forest constructs multiple decision trees based on bootstrap aggregating and evaluates model performance and variable importance using out-of-bag (OOB) error. Detailed parameter settings are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Determination and evaluation of candidate metabolites

To minimize methodological bias inherent to any single algorithm, the intersection of the stable feature set from elastic net and the feature set from random forest was taken as the final set of candidate metabolites. A logistic regression model was subsequently constructed, and its discriminative performance in the training and validation sets was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curve (AUC). In addition, weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was employed to explore the correlations between candidate metabolite modules and key clinical indicators, thereby providing supporting evidence for their biological relevance. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was used to quantify the contribution of each candidate feature to the classification decision, thereby enhancing model interpretability.

External validation based on clinical applicability

An independent external validation cohort was further included in this study, comprising 30 patients with MetS and 30 patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF. This validation cohort was independent of the metabolomics discovery cohort described above, and serum samples were collected using the same protocol as previously described.

To make the validation setting more reflective of real-world clinical practice, a simplified 1:1 PSM strategy was applied in the external validation cohort. The matching variables were limited to sex, age, BMI, and and the use of antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic medications. In addition, from the perspective of clinical applicability, total SM levels were measured in the independent clinical samples using an ELISA kit (Cat. No. ab287856, Abcam, USA) for a preliminary external evaluation of sphingolipid-related signals. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), and between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Untargeted metabolomics detected a total of 5,559 metabolic features. After MS/MS spectral acquisition and database annotation, 4,678 metabolites met the data quality requirements for subsequent analysis. According to the identification confidence levels, 1,056 metabolites were identified with high confidence (Level 1 or Level 2) and 3,622 with moderate confidence (Level 3). Overall, the detected metabolites mainly belonged to categories such as amino acids and their metabolites, benzene derivatives, and heterocyclic compounds (Figs. 2A and 2B).

Using VIP > 1 and P-value < 0.05 as the screening criteria, a total of 1,255 differential metabolites were identified, including 714 upregulated and 541 downregulated metabolites in the cardiometabolic HFpEF group relative to the MetS group. The relative abundance changes and statistical significance of these metabolites are shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 2D). After row-wise unit variance scaling of the raw relative abundance data, a hierarchical clustering heatmap was generated (Fig. 2E), showing a clear overall separation trend between the two groups.

Discussion

Using MetS as a risk-state comparator, this study systematically compared the serum metabolic profiles of cardiometabolic HFpEF and simple MetS after balancing baseline characteristics between the two groups by PSM. The results showed that patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF exhibited metabolic features mainly characterized by dysregulated sphingolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism. The related differential metabolites were primarily enriched in pathways including sphingolipid metabolism, sphingolipid signaling, glycerophospholipid metabolism, and arachidonic acid metabolism. Although C24:1 Sphingomyelin was annotated at confidence Level 3, it was retained by both elastic net and random forest among all Level 1–3 metabolites and ranked first in both random forest importance and SHAP contribution among the 22 candidate metabolites. Additionally, WGCNA showed that its module was significantly positively correlated with NT‑proBNP levels. Converging evidence from these multidimensional analyses suggests that C24:1 Sphingomyelin may represent a key candidate molecule linking metabolic dysregulation to cardiac involvement. Accordingly, it was prioritized as the candidate lipid signal of interest, and the subsequent discussion focuses on its potential significance in sphingolipid dysregulation and its clinical relevance as a candidate biomarker in cardiometabolic HFpEF.

Sphingolipid dysregulation: a potential pathway linking metabolic risk to cardiac dysfunction

Sphingolipids are important structural components of cell membranes and are widely present in cardiomyocytes. Ceramides (Cer), sphingosines, and their derivatives are involved not only in maintaining membrane structure but also in processes such as cell signaling, senescence, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and energy metabolism. Based on these biological functions, sphingolipid metabolic imbalance has been postulated as a potential pathological basis for cardiometabolic diseases [13] and is closely associated with cardiovascular disease and metabolic syndrome [14].

Previous studies have shown that elevated Cer levels and activation of related signaling pathways may be involved in pathological processes such as endothelial dysfunction, increased vascular tone, and atherosclerosis [15, 16]. In heart failure research, animal models have demonstrated increased synthesis of certain Cer species (such as Cer 18:1;O2/16:0, SM 18:1;O2/24:0) in failing myocardium [17]. Clinical studies have further shown that specific circulating Cer species are associated with heart failure risk and severity. For example, an elevated plasma Cer 18:1;O2/16:0 and Cer 18:1;O2/24:0 ratio has been associated with an increased risk of heart failure, reduced left ventricular ejection fraction, and worsened cardiac structure and function[17, 18]. In addition, the accumulation of sphingolipid metabolites in failing myocardial tissue [19] and altered expression of related metabolic genes [20] have provided indirect evidence, from different levels, for the association between sphingolipid dysregulation and myocardial involvement.

Notably, different sphingolipid species may have distinct or even opposite clinical implications. For example, in patients with HFpEF, elevated plasma Cer 18:1;O2/16:0 and Cer 18:0;O2/16:0 levels have been associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, whereas Cer 18:1;O2/24:0 has not shown a similar association [21]. This suggests that dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism is unlikely to represent a unidirectional alteration, but rather a complex network with bidirectional regulatory features. On the one hand, abnormal accumulation of lipids such as Cer and SM may be associated with enhanced oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and apoptosis, thereby potentially contributing to adverse cardiac remodeling. On the other hand, sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) and ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P) may exert protective effects by regulating vascular function and tissue remodeling [22].

In the present study, we observed that the differential metabolites in patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF were predominantly enriched in sphingolipid metabolism and its related signaling pathways, providing preliminary clues for further exploration of the potential role of sphingolipid dysregulation in this phenotype.

Sphingomyelin: a potential lipid signal in the transition from MetS to cardiometabolic HFpEF

SM is an important component of sphingolipid metabolism. Derived from ceramide, it is not only a major structural constituent of cell membranes but also a precursor of multiple bioactive lipid signals. Under the action of sphingomyelinase, SM can be hydrolyzed into ceramide, thereby participating in signaling pathways related to stress, apoptosis, and inflammation, suggesting that it may play a role in cardiovascular disease. Previous studies have shown that elevated SM levels are associated with the nondipper blood pressure pattern in patients with hypertension [23] and may contribute to atherosclerosis independently of cholesterol levels [24]. Animal studies further suggest that inhibition of SM synthesis can improve the lipid profile and attenuate the progression of atherosclerosis [25].

More importantly, large clinical cohort studies have shown that specific SM species and ceramides are associated with heart failure risk. Elevated levels of short-chain SM species (such as SM 18:1;O2/16:0) and Cer 18:1;O2/16:0 are positively associated with incident heart failure, whereas higher levels of long-chain saturated fatty acid–related Cer 18:1;O2/22:0 and SM 18:1;O2/20:0, SM 18:1;O2/22:0, and SM 18:1;O2/24:0 are associated with lower risk [26]. These findings suggest that different sphingolipid species may have distinct roles in cardiovascular diseases. And the direction of their effects may be influenced by structural features such as carbon chain length. On the other hand, some studies have reported that, after adjustment for traditional cardiovascular risk factors and NT-proBNP, the association between ceramides and disease becomes attenuated, suggesting that the specificity of certain sphingolipid markers may be influenced by clinical confounding factors [27]. In contrast, the corresponding performance of SM remains to be further clarified.

Against this background, the present study further showed that, after strict balancing of baseline characteristics between the two groups by PSM, sphingolipid-related signals were prominent among the differential metabolites identified in patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF. Among them, C24:1 Sphingomyelin, as one of the SM species, belonged to a co-expression module that was significantly correlated with NT-proBNP. These results suggest that SM-related metabolic abnormalities may be associated with the pathophysiological changes accompanying the progression from MetS to cardiac involvement, and provide preliminary clues for further investigation of C24:1 Sphingomyelin as a candidate lipid signal with potential diagnostic efficacy.

C24:1 Sphingomyelin: a candidate biomarker with potential diagnostic and biological relevance

Integrating evidence from dual feature selection, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, ROC quantification and SHAP contribution evaluation, we conclude that C24:1 Sphingomyelin is a reliable candidate biomarker. We further observed that C24:1 Sphingomyelin tended to be elevated in patients with cardiometabolic HFpEF, which may be related to dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism under conditions of metabolic stress. Previous studies have shown that sphingolipid metabolic imbalance may be associated with pathological processes such as myocardial fibrosis, cellular hypertrophy, and microvascular inflammation [28, 29]. Therefore, elevated C24:1 Sphingomyelin levels may indicate a potential association between metabolic dysregulation and cardiac involvement, and may provide a useful clue for distinguishing simple metabolic abnormalities from conditions in which cardiac dysfunction has already emerged, such as HFpEF.

In addition to its potential diagnostic efficacy, it may also serve as a potential therapeutic target. A multicenter randomized dietary intervention study conducted in Northern Europe showed that a healthy diet significantly reduced plasma ceramide and sphingomyelin levels in patients with metabolic syndrome and improved metabolic and inflammatory status [30], suggesting that modulation of sphingolipid levels may have potential for preventing the progression of HFpEF. Regarding direct intervention, preclinical studies have confirmed that modulating sphingolipid metabolism can improve cardiac remodeling. Enhancing acid ceramidase activity to promote ceramide degradation [31], or inhibiting its de novo synthesis using inhibitors such as myriocin [32, 33], can effectively reduce cardiac injury, alleviate myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, and improve cardiac function. Future research could further explore how to specifically regulate the activity of C24:1 Sphingomyelin and its related metabolic enzymes (e.g., sphingomyelin synthase or sphingomyelinase), or develop therapeutic strategies to correct specific harmful sphingolipid profiles (e.g., lowering Cer 18:1;O2/16:0 levels while increasing Cer 18:1;O2/22:0 levels), thereby opening new avenues for the prevention and treatment of cardiometabolic HFpEF.

Conclusion

Based on untargeted metabolomics and machine-learning approaches, this study preliminarily characterized the metabolic features that distinguish cardiometabolic HFpEF from simple MetS. The findings suggest that cardiometabolic HFpEF may be associated with metabolic alterations represented by dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism, among which C24:1 Sphingomyelin was identified as a candidate metabolite with potential diagnostic value, suggesting that it may help distinguish simple metabolic abnormalities from patients who have already developed cardiac involvement. This study was primarily designed for candidate biomarker discovery and a preliminary exploration of clinical translation based on clinical applicability. The current findings provide new clues for biomarker research in cardiometabolic HFpEF, but further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts, together with targeted LC–MS/MS detection and more rigorous external validation strategies, is still needed.

Limitations

This study was exploratory in nature and was primarily designed to identify candidate biomarkers associated with cardiometabolic HFpEF based on untargeted metabolomics, rather than to draw definitive conclusions regarding the clinical diagnostic performance of any single metabolite. Given the relatively limited sample size, although we attempted to minimize the risk of overfitting through training/validation set splitting, cross-validation, repeated resampling, OOB evaluation, and multi-algorithm cross-screening, the robustness and generalizability of the findings still require further validation in larger, multicenter cohorts.

In addition, only metabolites with identification confidence levels of 3 or higher (Levels 1–3) were included in the analysis. Although this strategy is consistent with common practice in exploratory untargeted metabolomics studies, some results were still based on medium-confidence annotations and may therefore involve a degree of uncertainty. In particular, C24:1 Sphingomyelin was identified at Level 3, and thus is currently more appropriately regarded as a candidate metabolite of potential interest rather than a definitively validated biomarker.

It should also be noted that accurate quantification of specific sphingomyelin species generally relies on targeted LC–MS/MS, which is not yet readily applicable to routine clinical testing. Therefore, in this study, ELISA-based measurement of total SM was used for a preliminary external evaluation from the perspective of clinical applicability. As a result, the external validation in this study was performed on total SM and does not constitute a species-specific direct validation of the main finding. This part of the results is therefore better interpreted as reflecting an association between sphingolipid-related signals and disease status, rather than as a direct validation of C24:1 Sphingomyelin itself. Future studies combining targeted LC–MS/MS and more rigorous external validation strategies are still needed to further validate C24:1 Sphingomyelin and assess its potential clinical utility.
