Iodide Mumps Following Facial Iodine Iontophoresis: A Case Report
Authors: Wangxiang Mai, Yuting Tang, Bing Feng, Zubiran Rouzhahong, Yingzi Huang, Zhuoming Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveIodide mumps, a rare form of sialadenitis, is thought to result from iodide accumulation in the salivary glands, leading to transient ductal obstruction and inflammatory swelling. This report emphasizes the potential risk of iodide mumps induced by facial direct current iodine ion introduction therapy, with the aim of raising awareness among clinicians, reducing misdiagnosis, and ensuring timely intervention.Case DescriptionA 61-year-old man with left-sided peripheral facial paralysis, type 2 diabetes, and necrotizing lymphadenitis developed bilateral earlobe swelling, pain, and fever during the fourth session of facial direct current iodide iontophoresis, after three uneventful treatments. Routine blood and organ function tests were normal, and no allergic history was reported. We present a unique case of iodide mumps that occurred in a patient with acute facial nerve palsy and co-existing necrotizing lymphadenitis, undergoing facial direct current iodine ion introduction therapy. The patient’s medical history, clinical presentation, and diagnostic assessments are described in detail.ResultsFollowing the cessation of iodide ion direct current import therapy and appropriate symptomatic treatment, the patient’s symptoms gradually improved, and during subsequent follow-up appointments, there were no further instances of parotid gland inflammation or enlargement.ConclusionAdverse reactions, such as iodide mumps, may occur following facial iodine ion import therapy. While such reactions have been predominantly reported in association with intravenous contrast administration, the present case illustrates that similar pathophysiological mechanisms can be triggered by iodide iontophoresis, even in patients without a documented history of iodine allergy.

Background and Purpose

Facial direct current iodide iontophoresis is a commonly used physical rehabilitation treatment method for peripheral facial paralysis. We report a case of iodide mumps that occurred in a patient with acute facial nerve palsy and concomitant necrotizing lymphadenitis undergoing this therapy. Iodide mumps, a rare form of noninfectious sialadenitis, is thought to be related to the salivary glands’ ability to concentrate and retain iodide. Excess iodide may transiently obstruct salivary ducts, cause mucosal edema, and trigger inflammatory swelling even in patients without a prior history of iodine allergy. Iodide-related adverse reactions, such as iodide mumps or iodide otitis media, have been most frequently reported after exposure to iodinated contrast agents. These conditions are characterized by transient, noninfectious swelling of the salivary or middle ear glands, typically occurring within minutes to days after exposure, and usually resolving spontaneously.1 Although the exact incidence is unknown, the reaction is considered rare, with only isolated case reports published to date. The pathophysiology is thought to involve the salivary glands’ and mucosal tissues’ ability to concentrate and retain iodide ions, leading to ductal obstruction, mucosal edema, and inflammatory swelling. In the context of iontophoresis, iodide ions penetrate the skin and gradually accumulate in local tissues; while the absolute absorbed dose is small (estimated 2–10% of the applied drug), their retention is prolonged due to slow clearance from lymphatic and salivary systems.2 This pharmacokinetic profile may explain why adverse reactions sometimes occur only after repeated sessions rather than immediately after the first exposure. However, there are limited epidemiological data on the frequency of such adverse events, underscoring the importance of reporting rare cases.

Case Description

A 61-year-old male was admitted to the hospital with a chief complaint of “crooked mouth with left cheek air leakage for more than 3 months”. He denied any history of allergic diseases. The admission diagnosis was as follows: 1. Peripheral facial paralysis (left side); 2. Type 2 diabetes; 3. Necrotizing lymphadenitis. Routine blood tests and liver and kidney function tests upon admission showed no abnormalities. Clinical treatment of left-sided facial nerve inflammation was initiated after admission, including facial direct current iodide ion import therapy on the left cheek once a day. The first three treatments had no adverse reactions. During the fourth treatment, swelling and pain were felt in the left earlobe after 2–3 minutes, followed by swelling and increased temperature in the right earlobe. After approximately 8 minutes of treatment, it became unbearable, accompanied by chills and fever, but without rash or airway compromise. Notably, this adverse reaction developed despite the absence of any known iodine allergy. The patient’s comorbid type 2 diabetes and necrotizing lymphadenitis may have modulated the local immune milieu and iodide handling. In particular, necrotizing lymphadenitis has been associated with circulating immune complexes, which could plausibly alter iodide clearance and favor transient ductal obstruction and swelling. Taken together, the concomitant lymphadenitis suggests a state of immune dysregulation, and—along with diabetes—may have influenced tissue retention and clearance of iodide during repeated iontophoresis sessions, predisposing to this delayed presentation at the fourth session.

Examination

On examination, the patient was febrile (39.6°C) with tachycardia (102 bpm) and normal blood pressure (130/80 mmHg). Bilateral parotid enlargement with tenderness and increased local temperature was evident, accompanied by limited temporomandibular joint mobility but no purulent discharge from Stensen’s ducts, as shown in Figure 1A. Post-treatment improvement is illustrated in Figure 1B. Ultrasonography demonstrated bilateral parotid thickening (right ≈ 29 mm, left ≈ 30 mm), reduced and heterogeneous internal echoes with patchy hypoechoic areas, and minimal intraglandular flow on CDFI, consistent with inflammatory change, as shown in Figure 2. Laboratory testing revealed elevated serum amylase (204.1 U/L), while the complete blood count, including white blood cells, neutrophils, and eosinophils, was unremarkable. Diagnosis and Treatment

The case was diagnosed as suspected sialadenitis directly induced by direct current iodide iontophoresis. The treatment involved immediate discontinuation of direct current iodide iontophoresis therapy, along with administration of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), prednisolone, calcium gluconate, and anti-allergic medications.

Outcomes

After treatment, the patient’s chills improved, and after 2 hours, the swelling and pain in both parotid glands decreased, and the texture became softer. After 8 hours, most of the swelling in both parotid glands had subsided (Figure 1B), with unrestricted temporomandibular joint movement and normal body temperature. The swelling and pain in the bilateral parotid glands had mostly disappeared the next day. During longitudinal follow-up via telephone at 3 months, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years post-treatment, the patient demonstrated no recurrence of sialadenitis or parotid gland enlargement.

Discussion

Facial direct current iodide ion import therapy is a commonly used physical rehabilitation treatment method for peripheral facial paralysis (Figure 3). This treatment method uses direct current to electrolyze potassium iodide into ions and import negatively charged iodide ions into the skin through the cathode, forming an “ion heap” mainly in the epidermis. It then gradually enters the lymphatic and circulatory systems through osmosis. The amount of imported drug is generally 2–10% of the total amount of the drug in the pad, so the amount of drug introduced into the body during each treatment is small.2 However, the drug remains in the body for a long time, resulting in long-lasting efficacy. Common adverse reactions during direct current ion import therapy include transient local erythema, skin burns, and burning sensations. Dryness and itching of the local skin may occur after repeated treatments, but there have been no reports of sialadenitis in adverse reactions to iodide ion direct current import therapy. In this case, bilateral parotid gland swelling occurred during the fourth iodide ion import treatment. The physical therapy was discontinued, and the swelling of the parotid glands quickly subsided after anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic treatment. Considering the elevated serum amylase and ultrasound results, it was considered sialadenitis induced by direct current ion import therapy. Differential diagnoses include acute thyroiditis, bacterial sialadenitis, allergic sialadenitis, and iodide mumps. The patient did not have any history of medication or infection, and there were no adverse reactions to iodine glycerin exposure during the first three iodide ion direct current import treatments and follow-ups. Therefore, the first three possibilities are not considered. Based on the patient’s disease progression and comparison with previous literature, we are more inclined to diagnose iodide mumps, which may be related to the accumulation of iodide ions in the salivary duct system and the patient’s specific immune status during treatment.3,4

Iodide mumps often refers to salivary gland swelling caused by the administration of iodine contrast agents.4–9 This is a rare adverse reaction, and its underlying mechanism is not completely clear. It may be related to hypersensitivity reactions or the deposition of iodides in salivary ducts leading to block reaction include rapid swelling of the salivary glands,10 usually without pain. However, due to the lack of specific symptoms such as rashes, vasogenic edema, difficulty breathing, and hypotension, it is often misdiagnosed. It usually occurs within a few minutes to 5 days after exposure.1 The parotid gland is known to actively concentrate iodide ions through the sodium-iodide symporter, similar to thyroid tissue, which explains its susceptibility to iodide accumulation and subsequent inflammatory swelling.3,4 The ductal system of the parotid gland may become transiently obstructed by iodide deposition and mucosal edema, resulting in upstream dilatation and pain. This physiological capacity for iodide retention provides an anatomical explanation for why iodide mumps occurs preferentially in the salivary glands. In the present case, the reaction occurred during the fourth iodide iontophoresis session. While the absolute absorbed dose per session is small (2–10% of the drug in the pad), the cumulative retention of iodide in salivary gland tissue may explain the delayed onset. The parotid gland, through the sodium-iodide symporter, can concentrate iodide similar to thyroid tissue. With repeated exposure, progressive accumulation may reach a threshold sufficient to obstruct ducts and provoke swelling.4,8 Additionally, the patient’s necrotizing lymphadenitis could have slowed clearance of iodide via lymphatic pathways, further predisposing to accumulation.1 Diabetes may have contributed to altered immune regulation and local tissue susceptibility. These factors together support a plausible pathophysiological explanation for the timing of the adverse reaction. From a clinical perspective, iontophoresis parameters may warrant adjustment in susceptible patients. Reducing the number of consecutive sessions, lowering the concentration of potassium iodide, or implementing monitoring after the first three sessions could minimize the risk of cumulative toxicity. While evidence-based guidelines are lacking, these considerations are supported by parallels from contrast-related iodide sialadenitis, where repeated exposure has been associated with delayed glandular swelling.1,8 Therefore, beyond recognizing iodide mumps as a rare but possible complication, clinicians should evaluate patient-specific risk factors (eg, immune status, comorbidities, renal/lymphatic clearance impairment) and adjust iontophoresis regimens accordingly. Further case accumulation will be essential to establish safe practice parameters.

Regarding management, while the reaction was self-limiting in this case,11 corticosteroids, antihistamines, and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs have been used successfully in previous reports.5,12–14 For patients with recurrent episodes or high risk of severe reaction, alternative physical therapy modalities such as ultrasound therapy, low-level laser therapy, or neuromuscular electrical stimulation could be considered for facial paralysis rehabilitation. These options avoid systemic iodide exposure and may be safer in predisposed individuals. Furthermore, no clear guidelines exist regarding preventive strategies, but some authors have suggested careful monitoring during repeated iodide exposures and considering pre-treatment screening in high-risk patients (eg, those with autoimmune disease, lymphadenitis, or renal impairment).1 Taken together, this case expands the clinical spectrum of iodide-induced sialadenitis by demonstrating that it can occur following iontophoresis, even in patients without iodine allergy, and highlights the importance of clinician awareness, judicious patient selection, and exploration of alternative rehabilitation options when necessary.

Conclusion

Through this case, we emphasize the potential risk of sialadenitis induced by facial iodide ion import therapy. Furthermore, the fact that symptoms appeared during the fourth iodide ion direct current import treatment supports the hypothesis of acute inflammatory diseases caused by iodine accumulation in the salivary glands. This case provides a new perspective and helps improve understanding of the mechanisms and clinical characteristics of iodine-related sialadenitis. It also highlights practical considerations for clinicians, including the need for monitoring patients at higher risk, considering iodine sensitivity testing prior to treatment, and adjusting iontophoresis protocols as appropriate. By increasing awareness of these strategies, clinicians may reduce misdiagnosis, promptly manage adverse reactions, and ultimately improve patients’ treatment experience and clinical outcomes. We also call for more case reports and systematic studies to better define the incidence, risk factors, and management strategies for iodide-induced sialadenitis.
