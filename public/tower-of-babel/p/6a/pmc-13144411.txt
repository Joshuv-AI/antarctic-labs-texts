Kinetic Human Movement Ontology: a semantic terminology model to symbolically represent physiological movement
Authors: Muhammad Amith, Vivian Ha, Eloisa Nguyen, Yang Gong, Ahsiya Lin, Cui Tao, Licong Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCataloging physiological and anatomical terminologies may provide a fundamental basis for bridging the involvement of muscles, skeletal, and other essential parts in human movement, in addition to understanding their role on human morbidity. We present a general formalized ontology model that focuses on anatomical and physiological entities that are active during human movement, called the Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO). We developed a model to represent human movement that links human postures using OWL2. We used the model to assemble various open-sourced controlled terminologies involved in human movement - anatomical entities, physiological terms, etc. KHMO presents 1954 classes, 42 properties, and 1921 logical axioms, and passed logical satisfiability and consistency tests. Compared to physical activity-related ontologies, KHMO exhibited high semiotic quality, including high domain coverage. KHMO is publicly available as an open-source resource on our GitHub repository, and we provide software for data management of our ontology model. We envision furthering the research of our work to interoperate with human data movement and for data curation and harmonization endeavors.Subject terms: Data integration, Standards, Physiology

Introduction

Human physiology is the study of how the human body functions through coordinated chemical and physical processes that sustain life1. The structure and anatomical positions of the human body are closely related to how it functions1. Thus, to better understand these functions, knowledge of anatomical terminology is crucial.

Exercise physiology, a subclassification of physiology, places emphasis on the human body’s response to physical stress along with the relationship between physical exercise and physiological function2. Examining how different anatomical foundations of the human body interact through sequences of physiological movement may help prevent injury and lead to further findings about disease etiology and prevention3.

Human movement and impact on health outcomes

Movement is a fundamental aspect of normal functioning for all organisms. It is achieved through complex interactions and movements of bones, muscles, ligaments, and joints within the musculoskeletal system3. In exercise physiology, physiological movements encompass the body’s dynamic movements through unsupported positions and its capacity to continually maintain these movements4. Modeling physiological movement is standardized by the usage of anatomical terminology and an understanding of causal relationships in movement5. Thus, modeling movement is essential in establishing effective and beneficial exercise plans and reinforcing healthy mobility.

Physiological movement is a significant and modifiable lifestyle change influencing the risk and progression of most cancer types6. Regular physical activity, e.g., aerobic exercise and muscle-strengthening activities, has been shown to reduce the onset of numerous cancers and mitigate side effects from anticancer treatments7,8. In murine studies, aerobic exercise has been shown to inhibit tumor growth in murine-derived tumor lines in part by modulating intratumoral angiogenesis and related vascular signaling9. Exercise perturbs whole-body homeostasis and elicits adaptive responses across the cell, tissue, and organ systems8. For example, aerobic exercise reduces excess adiposity10, and lowers growth-related hormones associated in rapid cell division, such as those elevated in obesity11. Through its effects on muscle and adipose tissue, aerobic exercise promotes the release of cytokines and growth factors into the tumor microenvironment, influencing apoptosis, cell proliferation, and cellular migration9.

While aerobic exercise has been the primary focus of many mechanistic and epidemiological studies, muscle-strengthening activities also contribute meaningfully to cancer-related outcomes. Aerobic exercise (moderate-to-vigorous) has been associated with a 28% decrease in total cancer mortality, whereas muscle strengthening activities are associated with a 13% decrease in cancer prevalence12. Notably, combining both forms of exercises promotes additional health benefits and reduces the risk of all-cause, cardiovascular disease, and cancer mortality13. Overall, lifestyle modifications which incorporate aerobic exercise and/or muscle-strengthening activities lowers the risk of various cancer types, including, but not limited to, colorectal, kidney, and endometrial cancer14.

Biomedical ontologies

Biomedical ontologies have become essential tools for data science and artificial intelligence research. Over the last few decades, biomedical researchers have increasingly leveraged semantic web technologies, specifically ontologies (and knowledge graphs) to better organize and represent biomedical data over other terminological systems, such as simple hierarchical taxonomies15. Biomedical ontologies offer a computable representation of data and information encoded using Resource Description Framework (RDF)16,17 and Web Ontology Language (OWL2)18. These computational representations of various biomedical domains open opportunities for software application and tasks to enhance analytical and data mining research to advance discoveries. This is further enhanced with machine-readable syntax of OWL2, Semantic We Rule Language19, Shapes Constraint Language (SHACL)20, and SPARQL21, which enable machine reasoning, data constraints, and querying of complex information. The Linked Data Cloud22, a visualization of multi-disciplinary ontologies linked across the web, shows the relatively large population of life science ontologies, as well as, the tightly coupled shared terminologies.

The aforementioned linked data feature helps to promote data harmonization across diverse datasets. It is presumed that voluminous and heterogeneous data sets generated from researchers and institutions at an exponential rate. The National Institutes of Health’s Office of Data Science and the National Academies acknowledge the role of ontologies to help harmonize big data sets23. However, domain coverage is crucial to enable the harmonization of heterogeneous datasets. Of relevance, data generated from wearables and from sensors that monitor physiological activities are needed to link health outcomes and variables to analyze the impact of human movement. This work focuses on addressing this potential need to describe human movement in a computable, machine-readable format.

Research objective

The domain space we intend to represent is human mobility and the physiological and anatomical parts that contribute to human body movements. In this paper, we developed the Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) that models the abstraction of physical movement, as well as, the anatomical parts that occur in motion. The contribution of this work is we provide a verified computational representation of human body motion using standardized open-sourced controlled vocabularies. This ontology provides a core model that was forged from past demonstration to model Tai Chi movements24, and also swimming movements – physical activities that promote human health. In addition, we provide KHMO-based software that facilitates management and generation of a knowledge graph from movement data. We assert that a computational representation of human physical movement and physiology can illustrate adequate coverage, sufficient term quality and semantic expressiveness.

Methods

We created our model based on how movement is abstracted through motion pictures or animation. In Fig. 1, we show an example of frames that capture a snapshot of movement. Within the span of this movement there exists a starting and ending moment that define an organism’s movement. Within the points there exist activities and parts that are involved to help propel an organism from the start to the end. In totality, the beginning and ending moments, the anatomical movement and parts that assist in the movement, define and express specific physical movement. Furthermore, when combining a series of movement, we can define a specialized activity or exercise.

Motion picture strip of showing points in movement, genesis of the KHMO model. Source from54.

For the Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO), we intended to utilize existing controlled terminologies from various open-sourced biomedical ontologies, however there is not a standard model that defines an interpretation or abstraction of an organism’s, specifically a Homo sapiens’, physical activity. To link the existing terminologies that help to describe movement, we define our aforementioned understanding of human physical activity using ontological formalism.

Definition 1 (Planned physical activity for KHMO). We define a planned physical activity L for humans as a type of planned process R (another formal definition originating from the Ontology for Biomedical Investigation, OBI_0000011). Each planned physical activity L has a set of human movement M that is a component of the activity.

Definition 2 (Human movement for KHMO). We presume for each atomic human movement M (a type of behavior B), starts with an initial stance Sn and ends with a realized stance Sn+1, where Sn precedes Sn+1. Both Sn and Sn+1 are a type of human stance S.

Definition 3 (Anatomical entities for KHMO). With the model, for every Sn and Sn+1 there are anatomical entities A that contribute to the human’s movement. An anatomical entity A enables S and participates in a physiological movement P.

Definition 4 (Physiological movement for KHMO). With the model, for every Sn and Sn+1 there are physiological body part movements P that contribute to the human’s movement. The physiological body part movement P has a participant(s) that are anatomical entities A and is a causally upstream of S.

Figure 2 shows a conceptual model of our definition for the central representation of KHMO. With the core model, we were able to devise a way to link some of the shared terminologies from existing biomedical ontologies, specifically from the Open Biomedical Ontology (OBO) Foundry25. From the simplified model that we show, we leverage the terms from the Basic Formal Ontology (BFO)26, Ontology for Biomedical Investigation (OBI)27, Neural Behavior Ontology (NBO)28, and the Foundational Model of Anatomy (FMA)29, to name a few. We identified terminologies using Tai Chi movements and swimming movements. For each movement, we identified the anatomical entities (sourced from FMA) and formal physiological terminology and reviewed the targeted ontologies we wanted to source from. We used the web-based application OntoFox30 to extract a subgraph ontology of NBO, OBI, and FMA. We authored the central representation of KHMO using Protégé, and linked the model with extracted subgraph ontologies using Protégé.

Evaluation of KHMO

We administered a quantitative evaluation of KHMO’s ontological quality based on31,32. The quality evaluation makes use of a theoretical framework of semiotic, which is a theory that describes the meaning of symbols on three aspects – syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic. The evaluation system produced an overall quality score based on the subscores that measures syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic measurements.

The syntactic score assesses the readability of the ontology artifact. It rates the fidelity to a syntax profile (OWL2) and the amount of syntax features (SubClassOf expressions, DisjointClasses expression, etc.). Semantic presents a score that measures the quality of the labels (rdfs:label) used for an entity. It assesses the consistency where terms are not used repetitively for different entities. Semantic also involves measuring the ambiguity of the labels (using the number of word senses) and the clarity (also based on a word sense count of the label). Lastly, pragmatic assesses the applied perspective of the ontology. This is indicated by the proportion of classes, properties, and instances to a total value (from a library of ontologies or from a selection of similar ontologies). Taken together these subscores and their values can be compared to a set of similar published ontologies or a library/repository of ontologies - acting as a baseline average. Theoretically, these calculations and their computed values are organized and expressed within the branches of semiotics to provide a symbolic quantifiable quality measure of an ontology.

The assessment of the overall score of KHMO will be compared against a group of other ontologies that share the same domain space. We compared KHMO with average scores of physical activity and human movement ontologies that were publicly available – Exercise Medicine Ontology (EXMO)33, Ontology of Physical Exercises (OPE)34, OPTImAL35, Physical Activity Ontology (PACO)36, and Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation (PMR) ontology37.The assessment of the overall score of KHMO will be compared against a group of other ontologies that share the same domain space. Data Record

This iteration of The Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) is deposited on the Zenodo repository40 as a Web Ontology Language artifact (OWL,.owl). Any latest iteration can be accessed using the artifact’s permanent URL (http://w3id.org/khmo) or visiting its GitHub workspace41. Reused terminologies sourced from the Foundational Model of Anatomy, Ontology of Biomedical Investigation, Neuro Behavior Ontology, etc. are also included in the repository.

Results

The Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) was encoded using the Web Ontology Language (OWL2) and reuses terminologies shared among the Open Biomedical Ontology Foundry ontologies, facilitated by OntoFox30 and Protégé42. KHMO has 1954 classes, 40 object properties, 2 data properties, and 1921 logical axioms. The ontology artifact and a merged version are hosted on our GitHub repository with a public permanent URL (http://w3id.org/khmo) to access and utilize the terminologies. For the semiotic evaluation of KHMO, we computed the scoring using computation metrics31,32 as described in our evaluation method section. The evaluation result is provided on Supplementary Table 1, with z-score values for clarifying the comparisons. In Supplementary Table 1 we also present measures for each individual branch (syntactic, semantic, and pragmatic) and their subcategories. The overall score for KHMO was 0.614 (z-score of 1.213), which was relatively higher than the collected average of 0.572 (σ = 0.033). In general, KHMO exhibited higher or near average across the various scores except for interpertability, which is a subscore of semantic, that measures percent of terms that are meaningful.

Lastly, to assess the satisfiability and the logical consistency of KHMO model, we executed software ontology reasoners – FacT++39 (i.e., JFact v5.0.343) and HermiT38. The results of the reasoners (executed through Protégé) yielded no inconsistency of the axioms.

Data and information management

In an effort to manage and link data of human movement with the Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) we developed an information processing pipeline to help generate and allow for reuse in software tools (see Use Cases and Future Direction) to transform physical activity data to machine readable knowledge graphs. We also enable the linkage of images of human movement to supplement the knowledge graph to support linked data.

The software imported a spreadsheet that categorizes human movement data (with semantic links to data based on the schema defined by KHMO). The software exports a knowledge graph (linked instance data) as an OWL2 artifact. The resulting artifact can be shared as a machine-readable resource to support software and data integration. The data management software is hosted on our GitHub repository41.

Discussion

The genesis of this work was initiated in our early attempt to model information from physical movements for Tai Chi24. The work presented here refines and expands the Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) to allow for a generalized model to detail the cataloging of anatomical and physiological terminologies within human movements, leading to the development of a computable knowledge base that details human movement towards chronic disease prevention. This computable knowledge base is linked to a greater online knowledge base of ontologies for medical knowledge through the use of shared terminologies of OBO Foundry ontologies.

Our quantitative evaluation of the ontology revealed that KHMO model is comparably better than average than the similar ontologies and taxonomies that represent human kinesiology and activity. The syntactic value for KHMO (0.782) was nearly above average, z-score 0.942 (μ = 0.719, σ = 0.067). This highlighted conformance to the OWL2 profile. The semantic rating of KHMO (0.978) was slightly above average, where the z-score was 0.495, where the collection of ontologies appear to have modest rating for semantics (μ = 0.949, σ = 0.059). The pragmatic score (0.077) which is essentially the comprehensive measure that indicate the coverage of the terminology was the highest rating among all the other examined ontologies (μ = 0.0495, σ = 0.037). Nonetheless, KHMO was shown to meet or exceed the baseline for the average ontology describing kinesiology and physical activity models.

EXMO, overall, was the most robust ontology for the domain of physical activity, based on the values shown on Supplementary Table 1. Differentiating from KHMO, EXMO is the classification of various types of exercises and physical activities while KHMO is focused primarily on the process of human movement – the mechanics and the stances to propel a human from one space to another. However, KHMO, with the overall score of 0.614 also demonstrated high quality (behind by a fraction of EXMO’s overall score). It is important to note that, both EXMO and KHMO are reusing OBO Foundry standard terminologies. This could allude to the importance of creating knowledge graphs and ontologies with existing, high quality ontology models. Generally, OBO Foundry ontologies have high standards and are likely to yield higher quality models. The weakest aspect of KHMO was the interpretability rating z-score of −0.480. The interpretability score is based on word sense of each term on the ontology. The score itself (0.945) is not deemed to be a drastically low score. All in all, as a first release, KHMO appear to be a promising biomedical ontology model when compared to its shared models.

Use cases and future direction

In a broader scope, KHMO can have further applications in sports science, rehabilitation, and even robotics, where the detailing of anatomical movement is crucial. The outcomes of these expansions would allow researchers to continue to analyze the impact of specific human activity involving distinct musculature and/or systems towards chronic disease prevention. For example, swimming is a muscle-strengthening and aerobic exercise performed in water that reduces joint stress, increases physical strength, and reduces body fat44. While additional information from swimming science has become possible with the help of swimming treadmills, computerized equipment, and automated diagnostics45, there is a relative lack of swimming research that is partly attributed to the monitoring of physiological movements in an aquatic environment46.

Skeletal muscle is heavily involved in swimming and other aerobic exercises. The preservation and maintenance of skeletal muscle strength can contribute to numerous factors that can prevent chronic disease47. At the molecular level, repeated periods of muscle contraction alongside exercise training can contribute to maximizing substrate delivery, mitochondrial respiratory capacity, and contractile function47. Various skeletal muscles can be studied in the context of cancer. An example of this can be seen in cachexia, a condition related to muscle loss and body weakness, found in patients with advanced pancreatic and lung cancer along with other cancer types48. In cachetic cancer patients, an increase in mitochondrial energy wasting can be found in their pectoralis major49. Furthermore, muscle mass in the context of muscle wasting was previously argued to be a potential marker or parameter linked to disease severity or progression in cancer and other chronic diseases50. MacDonald et al. have found quadriceps to be “potentially valid markers of muscle function,” which can be examined in the context of muscle wasting in cancer and other chronic diseases. Therefore, cataloging physiological and anatomical terminologies and components may provide a fundamental basis for bridging the involvement of various muscles in human movement and their effects on cancer prevention or progression.

In Fig. 7, we present an example of how our ontology can hypothetically describe the movement and anatomy across human postures for physical exercise and movement. We show the pull and back swimming movement between two points on the continuum – along with an image linking to a posture, showing heterogeneous data linking. Furthermore, ubiquitous sensor-based wearables and machine learning (e.g., OpenPose51, AlphaPose52) have enabled the recognition and tracking of human movement (i.e., kinematics and dynamics) using a skeleton annotation approach (See Fig. 8). The potential benefit of this work could help researchers identify and analyze specific bio-anatomy and label/classify formal human movement in data. Future work could investigate opportunities in this direction for analytical and organizing heterogeneous movement data.

With an extended set of terminologies and refined classifications with the inclusion of granular terminologies, KHMO has become a more versatile ontology following this study. Through this study, over 1600 terms were added for anatomical muscles, and over 700 movement data were generated through movements involved in aquatic exercise, a form of exercise that is difficult to physically assess. These data were compiled from each aquatic movement in a knowledge graph that simplifies muscle-function, computationally “dissecting” the human body to allow for better perception of involved muscles.

As alluded human movement is a complex machination of anatomical parts, and when combined with the aforementioned heterogeneous data, the information needs to be collated and processed in a systematized manner. With the informatics processing pipeline we developed, we can extend this to produce a web-based application with API services to enable the recording and cataloguing of human anatomical movement information. With the data and information aligned to a machine-readable ontological model (like KHMO), we could potentially annotate, reason, query complex anatomical and kinetic information of the human body.

Summary

We have developed and expanded a computable knowledge base that symbolically details anatomical movements that can be utilized by researchers to analyze and understand physical activity towards cancer prevention. The Kinetic Human Movement Ontology (KHMO) is a biomedical ontology that symbolically details anatomical movements and their relationship information on a movement-to-movement basis. The core model of KHMO links together developed terms with existing terms, and all curated data is available on GitHub41. This study comprises the KHMO framework being extended to include musculature terminology from the Foundational Model of Anatomy (FMA) Ontology, resulting in 1607 newly added terms for anatomical muscles involved in various forms of aquatic exercises. These data would allow for the examination of specific muscle involvement in aquatic exercise movements. Our future direction will include further expanding KHMO to encompass other forms of physical activity along with additional physiological and anatomical linkages. KHMO has allowed for a better understanding of human movement and specific entities involved in the coordination of these movements
