The Usefulness of Inclusion of Visual Field Tests Using Multiple Testing Strategies in Perimetric Rate of Progression Analyses
Authors: Yusuke Noda, Ryo Asaoka, Kazunori Hirasawa, Hiroshi Murata, Masaki Tanito, Nobuyuki Shoji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe purpose of this study was to validate the use of a mixture of strategies (Swedish Interactive Thresholding Algorithm [SITA] Fast and Standard) when conducting the mean total deviation (mTD) trend analysis.MethodsData were collected retrospectively from 128 eyes of 95 patients with open-angle glaucoma with at least 5 SITA Fast visual field (VF) followed by 5 SITA Standard VFs. First, the mTD of a fifth future VF in 5.3 years was predicted using various numbers (from 1–2 to 1–4) of SITA Standard VFs. Similar prediction calculations were performed simultaneously using the SITA Fast VFs before the SITA Standard VFs. Furthermore, this calculation was iterated by adjusting for the difference between the SITA Fast and SITA Standard.ResultsWithout SITA Fast VFs, the means of prediction errors were 10.40 and 4.44 decibels (dB) with 2 and 3 SITA Standard VFs, respectively. These values significantly decreased to 2.83 and 2.40 dB with SITA Fast VFs without adjusting for the difference between SITA Fast and Standard VFs. The values after adjusting for the difference between SITA Fast and Standard VFs were 2.88 and 2.39 dB, respectively, which were not significantly different. With 4 SITA Standard VFs, the prediction errors varied between 2.12 and 2.34 dB across the 3 methods; however, they were not significantly different from each other.ConclusionsIt is useful to add information from prior SITA Fast VFs when conducting mTD trend analysis with SITA Standard VFs.Translational RelevanceIt is clinically beneficial to include both SITA Standard and Fast VFs in mTD trend analysis.

Introduction

Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of blindness worldwide.1,2 In glaucoma treatment, appropriately controlling intraocular pressure can delay the progression of irreversible visual field (VF) damage.3–5 Therefore, future VF damage should be accurately predicted when making glaucoma treatment decisions, as medical and particularly surgical glaucoma treatments are often associated with ocular and general complications.6–10 One of the popular approaches to assess and predict future VF deterioration is applying ordinary least squares linear regression to global VF indices, such as mean deviation (MD), as implemented in the Guided Progression Analysis (GPA) software on the Humphrey Visual Field Analyzer (HFA; Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Dublin, CA). Conversely, VF data are inherently associated with measurement noise, making trend analyses inaccurate, particularly with short VF sequences. Previous studies have recommended that at least five11 or even more12,13 VFs are needed to acquire accurate results from an MD trend analysis.

A possible scenario in the clinical settings is performing VF measurements using different algorithms, such as screening and standard programs. For instance, in Japan with HFA, VF is most often measured using Swedish Interactive Threshold Algorithm (SITA) Fast or Faster program at clinics; however, it is often switched to the SITA Standard program once the patient is referred to a university hospital for intensive treatments. In such a case, at this moment, the VF progression analysis has to be performed using only the SITA Standard data obtained at the university hospital, following the recommendation that VF sensitivities with a mixture of strategies should be cautiously compared and that new baselines should be obtained when switching strategies for follow-up.14–16

This recommendation sounds reasonable because, in principle, VF thresholds measured with SITA Fast and Standard are not exchangeable due to the different measurement mechanisms (4 decibels [dB] single and 4-2 dB double cross-bracketing, respectively).17 Indeed, previous studies have indicated that an approximately 1 dB difference is observed between VF sensitivity with SITA Fast and SITA Standard.18,19 To overcome this problem, a recent study has recommended a nomogram for the exchange between the two strategies.19 However, this result has not been clinically available, and a possible caveat is that this conversion assumes the same formula to convert from one to another across patients. The higher sensitivity with SITA Fast compared with SITA Standard may be derived from the decrease in test duration and the consequent reduction in fatigue,20,21 and, hence, the magnitude of the difference should be different across patients. However, it is also true that global VF indices, such as MD, are calculated with reference to normative values of each strategy, good agreement between the indices of these strategies could be expected,20,22,23 which implies it may be beneficial simply to use the mixture of different strategies in the MD trend analysis. This study aimed to validate the use of a mixture of strategies (SITA Fast and Standard) when conducting mean total deviation (mTD) trend analysis and to construct and investigate the usefulness of a model to adjust for the individual differences in VF sensitivities between the two algorithms.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We retrospectively collected data from 128 eyes of 95 patients with open-angle glaucoma. The data sources were the medical records of the Japanese Archive of Multicentral Databases in Glaucoma (JAMDIG) dataset,24 Kitasato University Hospital, and Shimane University Hospital. This study was conducted following the tenets of the 2013 version of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of each institution provided ethical approval (#3306).

All patients were diagnosed with primary open-angle glaucoma with reproducible glaucomatous changes in the optic nerve head and glaucomatous VF defects identified by HFA 24-2 or HFA 30-2 SITA Fast and Standard programs. The inclusion criteria were (a) glaucomatous VF defects meeting the criteria of Anderson and Patella: (i) a cluster of ≥3 points in the PD plot within a single hemifield (superior or inferior) with p < 0.05, one of which should be p < 0.01, and/or (ii) Glaucoma Hemifield test results outside normal limits, and/or (iii) abnormal PSD with p < 0.05.25 The exclusion criteria were (a) previous ophthalmic surgery; (b) any ocular disease besides cataract and glaucoma affecting visual function; and (c) a history of cerebral or other ocular pathologies that could affect the VF results.

Visual Field Examination

We defined reliable VFs as fixation loss (FL) rate of <33% and false-positive (FP) rate of <33%, and false-negative (FN) rate of <33%. Eyes with five reliable VFs with SITA Standard, and also at least three reliable VF with SITA Fast before the initial SITA Standard VF measurement were collected. All patients had an experience of VF measurement before the baseline VF data.

We calculated the adjustment for the difference between the SITA Fast and Standard as follows: first, the mTD trend analysis was performed using all SITA Fast VF data, and the mTD value of the first SITA Standard VF was predicted by extrapolating the regression line. This calculation was conducted by initially adding −2 dB to all mTD values with SITA Fast. This process was iterated using various adjustment values between −2 and 0 dB with an interval of 0.1 dB. Subsequently, the adjustment value with the most accurate prediction of the mTD value of the first future SITA Standard VF was identified, and this value was used to adjust for the difference between the SITA Fast and Standard. It should be noted that this adjustment was made using the first future SITA Standard VF and prior SITA Fast VFs, assuming initial SITA Standard VFs are already obtained, in addition to prior SITA Fast VFs, at the clinical settings. All of the analyses described above were conducted using this adjustment plus newly obtained second (and thereafter) future SITA Standard VFs.

Finally, we predicted the mTD value of the final VF (fifth SITA Standard VF) by extrapolating the regression line with each method described above, using between 1 to 2 and 1 to 4 SITA Standard VFs with and without prior SITA Fast VFs. Then, we calculated the absolute prediction error against the true mTD value of the final VF (fifth SITA Standard VF) to be compared with each other by using the linear mixed model, where patients were the random effect (intercept) because of the inclusion of two eyes of the same patient. The measurement of two eyes from the same patient can be highly correlated. The linear mixed model adjusts for the hierarchical data structure modeling so that each patient is registered as having a random effect; that is, measurements are grouped within participants. By ignoring this measurement grouping, the standard errors of the regression coefficients would be underestimated.

Subsequently, we investigated the associations between the absolute prediction errors using the following factors: age, baseline mTD (the mTD value of the first SITA Standard VF), number of SITA Fast VFs, mean FL of SITA Fast VFs, mean FL of SITA Standard VFs, difference between the mean FL of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs, mean FP of SITA Fast VFs, mean FP of SITA Standard VFs, difference between the mean FP of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs, mean FN of SITA Fast VFs, mean FN of SITA Standard VFs, difference between mean FN of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs. In addition, we also investigated the associations between the magnitude of adjustment for the difference between SITA Fast and SITA Standard and the following values: age, number of SITA Fast VFs, mean FL of SITA Fast VFs, mean FL of SITA Standard VFs, difference between the mean FL of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs, mean FP of SITA Fast VFs, mean FP of SITA Standard VFs, difference between the mean FP of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs, mean FN of SITA Fast VFs, mean FN of SITA Standard VFs, and the difference between the mean FN of SITA Fast VFs and that of SITA Standard VFs.

In addition, we compared the difference in each slope value and that with the 1 to 5 SITA Standard VFs between each method.

Bonferroni's adjustment was used for multiple comparisons. All analyses were performed using the statistical programming language “R” (R software version 3.1.3; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Discussion

In the current study, the usefulness of using a mixture of VF measurement strategies (SITA Fast and Standard) in the mTD trend analysis was analyzed. Moreover, a model was constructed to adjust for the individual differences in VF sensitivities between the 2 algorithms, and its usefulness was validated using VF data from 128 eyes from 95 patients with glaucoma. As a result, it was suggested to use prior SITA Fast VFs when predicting a fifth future VF (SITA Standard) in 5.3 years (from the first future VF) using various numbers of SITA Standard VFs. Adjusting for the difference between SITA Fast and SITA Standard did not yield significantly better prediction. The same was true when analyzing the mTD slope using various numbers (from the first to the second to the first to the fourth) of SITA Standard VFs.

At least five11 or even more12,13 VFs are needed to acquire accurate results from an mTD trend analysis in previous studies. Thus, the absolute prediction error to predict the fifth future VF using four prior VFs was a relatively low level (2.34 dB on average). In contrast, this value sharply increased when smaller numbers of VFs were used for the prediction (4.44 and 10.40 with initial 3 and 2 VFs, respectively; see Table 2). In contrast, the prediction errors decreased to 2.39 and 2.88 dB with the initial 3 and 2 VFs, respectively, when the SITA Fast VFs were used with adjustment for the difference between the SITA Fast and Standard VFs. The prediction values with two SITA Standard VFs plus SITA Fast VFs were smaller than those with three SITA Standard VFs without SITA Fast VFs. Thus, using the mixture of VF data may be recommended (converted from SITA Fast to SITA Standard), rather than discarding all SITA Fast data, when the number of SITA Standard VF is relatively small, such as between 1 and 4. A very similar tendency was also observed in the analysis of the mTD slope estimation. Chauhan et al.26 comprehensively described recommendations when measuring clinically relevant rates of glaucomatous VF progression to help identify patients at risk for visual impairment. The recommendations included the statement of “use the same strategy (e.g. always SITA Standard) and test pattern (e.g. always 30–2 or always 24–2, and not a mixture of, for example, 30–2 and 10–2) is always much better for determining VF change.” Nonetheless, the current results suggested the benefit of using a mixture of VF measurement strategies (SITA Fast and Standard) in the mTD trend analysis. This may be because mTD is calculated with reference to normative values of each strategy, which makes the difference between strategies marginal, unlike raw visual sensitivities.20,22,23 Indeed, a previous report has indicated that a mean difference in mTD between SITA Standard and SITA Fast to be only 0.06 decibels, which is much smaller than the 1 dB difference in raw threshold values.27 Supporting this, in this study, an adjustment between −2.0 and 0 dB was applied to adjust for the difference between 2 strategies did not largely contribute to improve the prediction accuracy in general (see Tables 2, 3).

SITA Fast measures VF sensitivity through the 4 dB single bracketing, whereas it is the 4–2 dB double cross-bracketing with the SITA Standard, resulting in much shorter measurement duration with SITA Fast than with SITA Standard, such as 3.84 versus 6.56 minutes.28 The higher sensitivity with SITA Fast compared with the SITA Standard may have been derived from the decrease in this test duration and consequent reduction in fatigue.20,21,29 Another possible reason may be the increased error-related cutoff (i.e. the permitted uncertainty of the threshold estimate) of SITA Fast compared with the SITA Standard.17,27 However, the current results that show no association between the values of the prediction accuracy/magnitude of the adjustment for the difference between SITA Fast and standard VF, and the rates of FL, FP, and FN could suggest this is only a small or negligible part of the reasons. Notably, this result should be interpreted carefully because many previous studies have suggested the usefulness and uselessness of these indices when evaluating the reliability of the measured VF.30–35 Future studies are needed to shed light on this issue using other reliability information, such as gaze track record, which is a direct measure of the eye position at the timing of stimulus presentation.36-40 SITA Faster, which is now increasingly adopted in clinical practice, uses the same measurement algorithm as SITA Fast but omits catch trials. Future studies are needed to clarify whether comparable results can be achieved with SITA Faster.

Pham et al. reported that the tendency of higher VF sensitivity with SITA Faster compared with SITA Standard was significantly more exaggerated in eyes with advanced disease stage, possibly because of the shorter test duration.41 A positive association was found between the magnitude of adjustment for the difference between SITA Fast and SITA Standard, and baseline mTD, in the current study (see Table 5). Reflecting this, it was suggested that a more advanced disease stage was significantly associated with a more negative prediction error (S2). In addition, older age was significantly associated with a significantly larger prediction error (S2). This is probably because the effect of fatigue due to the longer VF test with SITA Standard is more exaggerated in such population. These results suggest careful consideration for the larger prediction error is needed in patients in older age and more advanced disease stage when utilizing this method. By contrast, these findings were not observed in the absolute prediction errors (see Table 4).

This study may be limited by the relatively small number of VFs used in the analysis. In addition, similar findings observed even with mTD values should be investigated in the future in other forms of programs, such as SITA Faster, Dynamic program in the Octopus perimetry42 (HAAG-Streit, Switzerland), or smart strategy in the AP7700 perimetry (Kowa Ltd., Nagoya, Japan).43,44 Moreover, the current results indicated that using prior data with SITA Fast is useful when conducting mTD trend analysis using SITA Standard VFs; it was particularly useful until data of large amounts of SITA Standard VFs were accumulated. Nonetheless, the current study lacks a comparison of the mTD slope only with SITA Standard VFs and with a mixture of SITA Standard and Fast VFs. A further study should investigate this respect, preparing VF sequences simultaneously measured with SITA Standard and Fast, unlike in the current study. The applicability to other populations is another limitation of the current study, as well as to more focal regression, such as a point-wise one.

In conclusion, our study indicated the usefulness of adding information from prior SITA Fast VFs when conducting mTD trend analysis with SITA Standard VFs. This approach was particularly useful when the number of SITA Standard VFs was small.
