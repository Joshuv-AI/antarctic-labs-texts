Introduction

Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) is a rare hereditary cancer predisposition disorder characterized by mutations in the TP53 gene, affecting an estimated 1 in 3500 to 1 in 5500 people [1,2]. Although it is typically inherited in an autosomal dominant manner, an estimated 7–20 % of patients with LFS result from de novo variants [3]. This genetic anomaly significantly elevates the risk of developing a spectrum of cancers, often at a young age [4]. First identified in 1969 by Drs. Frederick Li and Joseph Fraumeni [5]. LFS has since been a focal point for understanding the genetic underpinnings of cancer susceptibility [6].

Individuals with LFS face an increased lifetime risk of various cancers, including breast cancer, sarcomas, brain tumors, and leukemias, among numerous others [1]. The TP53 gene mutation impairs the function of the p53 protein, a crucial tumor suppressor involved in regulating cell division and apoptosis [7]. The discovery of LFS has revolutionized the approach to genetic screening and personalized medicine, particularly in young patients who develop cancers typically seen in older populations [8].

Genetic testing is critical in identifying LFS, allowing for early detection and proactive management of cancer risks [3]. Various criteria direct guidelines for genetic testing in patients at high risk for these genetic variants, such as the Chompret criteria, that may aid in early detection and prompt early treatment [9,10]. The lifetime risk of cancer in males is estimated to be about 70 %, while in females, the risk approaches 100% [2,11]. Thus, the importance of such testing cannot be overstated, as it informs tailored treatment plans and early surveillance strategies, improving patient outcomes and potentially saving lives [4,12]. Though there are high rates of prophylactic risk reducing mastectomies LFS patients, data in this population is extrapolated from BRCA1/2 studies. Long term survival and quality of life outcomes of risk reducing surgery in LFS remain unclear [[34], [35], [36]].

This article explores the multi-faceted aspects of Li-Fraumeni syndrome, from genetic testing and clinical management to patients' psychological and supportive care needs. By advocating for a multidisciplinary approach, we aim to contribute to improved care strategies and outcomes for individuals with this challenging hereditary condition.

Case presentation

Patient background

The patient is a 21-year-old healthy Hispanic female who presented with a palpable mass in her right breast. She reported no associated discharge, tenderness, or skin changes. There was no significant family history of breast or ovarian cancer, and she had not undergone any prior biopsies. Past surgical history includes a right salpingo-oopherectomy for ovarian torsion with a mature cystic teratoma. Her occupation is a receptionist.

Initial presentation and diagnostics

A 2 cm irregular mass was palpable in the right breast on physical examination. Diagnostic mammography and ultrasound confirmed the presence of a 2 cm irregular mass with indistinct margins and internal vascularity (Fig. 1), classified as BIRADS 4, indicating a suspicious abnormality (Fig. 2). Breast MRI showed a large enhancing mass in the lower outer quadrant of the right breast at the area of palpable mass (Fig. 3). No abnormal lymph nodes were visible within the axilla. Subsequent ultrasound-guided needle core biopsy revealed Grade 3 invasive ductal carcinoma (Fig. 4), low positive for estrogen receptors (ER) (Fig. 5), negative for progesterone receptors (PR), but positive for Her2 (3+) (Fig. 6). The Ki67 index was 84 %, indicating a high proliferation rate (Fig. 7).

Genetic testing and diagnosis

Given the patient's young age at diagnosis, genetic testing revealed a TP53 mutation (c.1009C > T, p. Arg337Cys), confirming the diagnosis of Li-Fraumeni syndrome. This finding necessitated a tailored and comprehensive management plan due to the high risk of multiple primary cancers associated with LFS.

Initial treatment

The patient was started on neoadjuvant chemotherapy with six cycles of docetaxel, carboplatin, trastuzumab, and pertuzumab (TCHP). She tolerated the treatment well and used cold caps to minimize hair loss during treatment. Follow-up imaging showed a good response to the chemotherapy, with the original mass no longer visible on ultrasound.

Surgical intervention

The patient underwent a bilateral skin-sparing, nipple-sparing mastectomy with right sentinel lymph node biopsy and immediate reconstruction with implant placement. Intraoperative findings were unremarkable for the bilateral breasts, and four sentinel lymph nodes were removed from the right axilla. Postoperative pathology confirmed a complete response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy, with no residual invasive carcinoma in the breast tissue or sentinel lymph nodes.

Complications and management

On the first postoperative day, the patient developed a hematoma, which required a return to the operating room for evacuation and washout. Approximately 100 cc of hematoma was evacuated, and good hemostasis was achieved. The implant was replaced, and the patient recovered without further complications. She was discharged in stable condition shortly after.

Adjuvant therapy and follow-up

The patient is following up with her medical oncologist, and the plan includes continuing trastuzumab and pertuzumab for 12 cycles, with the patient currently on cycle 2. She will have echocardiograms every three months to monitor cardiac function. Radiation therapy (XRT) is not indicated in her case as she had no malignant involvement in the sentinel lymph nodes following mastectomy, as well as having high susceptibility to radiation with LFS. Since there was a complete pathologic response (pCR), TDM-1 will not be pursued; trastuzumab and pertuzumab will continue for 52 weeks, with consideration of Neratinib for high-risk patients despite its added side effects. The patient qualifies for pertuzumab based on criteria from the KRISTINE trial due to her T2 lesion. Additionally, she will receive monthly Lupron injections for fertility preservation, administered every other treatment cycle.

Mental and psychological status

The diagnosis and treatment journey significantly impacted the patient's mental and psychological well-being. Initially, she experienced considerable anxiety and fear regarding her cancer diagnosis and the implications of Li-Fraumeni syndrome. However, with the unwavering support of her family, friends, and medical team, she was able to navigate these challenges. The compassionate care from her healthcare providers, along with the encouragement and practical support from her loved ones, played a crucial role in maintaining her mental resilience and helping her adhere to her treatment regimen effectively.

Discussion

Li-Fraumeni syndrome

Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) is a rare hereditary disorder caused by mutations in the TP53 gene, which significantly elevates the risk of developing various types of cancer, often at a young age [13]. This syndrome was first identified in 1969 by Drs. Frederick Li and Joseph Fraumeni noted a pattern of familial clustering of diverse cancers [5]. The TP53 gene mutation impairs the function of the p53 protein, a crucial tumor suppressor involved in regulating cell division and apoptosis [14,15]. As a result, individuals with LFS are predisposed to a wide range of cancers, including breast cancer, sarcomas, brain tumors, adrenocortical carcinomas, and leukemias [16]. Understanding the genetic basis and clinical implications of LFS is vital for developing effective management strategies and improving patient outcomes [17].

Clinical presentation and early genetic testing

Patients with LFS typically present with cancers at an earlier age than the general population and may have a history of multiple primary cancers [18]. The clinical presentation can vary widely depending on the types of cancers developed [7]. Early genetic testing is crucial for identifying LFS, especially in young patients with cancer [2,8]. Genetic testing for TP53 mutations should be considered in individuals diagnosed with cancers commonly associated with LFS, especially at an early age, or those with a family history suggestive of the syndrome [11,13]. Early identification of a TP53 mutation allows tailored treatment plans and proactive surveillance to manage cancer risk effectively [19].

Breast cancer in Li-Fraumeni syndrome patients

Breast cancer is one of the most common cancers associated with LFS, often presenting at a younger age and with more aggressive features compared to sporadic cases [12]. In LFS patients, breast cancer typically exhibits higher grades and higher proliferation rates [13]. A majority of breast cancers associated with LFS are estrogen receptor-positive and Her2 receptor-positive, which generally necessitates a more aggressive treatment approach [4,5]. Diagnosing breast cancer in LFS patients involves imaging studies such as mammography and ultrasound, followed by biopsy to confirm the histological type and receptor status [8,9]. However, as patients with LFS notably have high susceptibility to radiation, MRI with and without contrast is often used. Given the high lifetime risk of breast cancer and subsequent primary cancers, surgical options often include risk-reducing bilateral mastectomies [3,8].

Diagnosis and management

The diagnosis of breast cancer in LFS patients involves a combination of imaging and histopathological evaluation [20]. Mammography and ultrasound can identify suspicious masses, while needle core biopsy provides definitive histological diagnosis and receptor status assessment [21]. Management of breast cancer in LFS patients requires a multidisciplinary approach involving oncologists, surgeons, geneticists, and supportive care providers [17,20]. Treatment plans are tailored based on the cancer's characteristics and the patient's overall risk profile [8]. Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is often employed to shrink the tumor before surgery, provide chemosensitivity prognostic information, as well as help guide post-operative adjuvant therapy treatment [3].

Neoadjuvant therapy

Neoadjuvant chemotherapy is a common approach for managing breast cancer in LFS patients, aiming to reduce tumor size and eradicate micrometastatic disease, as a majority of these patients have chemo-sensitive HER2-positive breast cancers [22]. Common regimens for HER2-positive breast cancers include combinations of docetaxel, carboplatin, trastuzumab, and tertuzumab (TCHP), though the precise chemotherapy regimen may vary depending on tumor characteristics [23]. This approach not only facilitates surgical resection but also provides an early indication of the tumor's responsiveness to systemic therapy [24]. A favorable response to neoadjuvant therapy, as evidenced by the absence of residual disease on imaging and pathology, is a positive prognostic factor [25].

Adjuvant therapy

Adjuvant therapy is tailored based on the patient's response to neoadjuvant treatment and surgical outcomes [26]. For LFS patients with Her2-positive breast cancer, continued administration of trastuzumab and pertuzumab is common, along with periodic echocardiograms to monitor cardiac function [24]. In cases with no complete pathologic response, additional chemotherapy with TDM-1 may be considered [23]. Radiotherapy is also often considered in the management of breast cancer [27]. However, adjuvant radiotherapy is generally avoided in patients with LFS as these patients tend to be more radiosensitive and increase the risk of developing radiation-induced secondary cancer [28]. As LFS patients also have an increased risk of lung cancer, patients who underwent mastectomy generally still avoid postoperative whole breast radiation [9]. Occasionally, radiotherapy may still be utilized if the benefits of minimizing secondary malignancies outweigh the risks, and there is ongoing research on the roles of novel radiation treatments, such as particle therapy, in the management of these patients [[16], [17], [18]]. The overall goal of adjuvant therapy is to minimize the risk of recurrence and manage any residual disease effectively [26].

Surveillance and follow-up

Given the high risk of multiple primary cancers in LFS patients, rigorous and proactive surveillance is essential [29]. This includes regular imaging studies such as whole-body MRI, colonoscopies, upper endoscopy, and organ-specific screenings based on the patient's cancer risk profile [8]. Surveillance strategies aim to detect new cancers as soon as possible, facilitating prompt intervention. Follow-up care involves regular clinical assessments and imaging to monitor for recurrence and the development of new primary tumors [29].

Psychosocial support

The psychological and emotional impact of living with LFS and undergoing cancer treatment at a young age can be profound [30]. Patients often experience significant anxiety, fear of recurrence, and concerns about their future health and fertility [31]. Comprehensive psychosocial support is essential to address these challenges [32]. Empathy and compassionate care from healthcare providers can significantly enhance patients' ability to cope with their condition and adhere to their treatment plan. The role of family and friends in providing emotional support is equally important, contributing to the overall well-being and resilience of the patient [31]. Furthermore, understanding the many resources available to cancer survivors can further empower patients to improve their psychosocial well-being, including support groups, counseling, and procedural options, including breast reconstruction and cold caps to avoid chemotherapy-induced alopecia to improve self-image, as seen in this patient [32].

Conclusion

Managing Li-Fraumeni syndrome (LFS) and its associated cancers, particularly in young patients, necessitates a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach. Early genetic testing for TP53 mutations is crucial in identifying LFS, enabling personalized treatment plans and proactive surveillance strategies. This case demonstrates the importance of integrating oncology, surgery, genetics, and supportive care to address the complex needs of LFS patients. A cancer diagnosis and the challenges of a hereditary cancer syndrome demand empathetic and compassionate care, which is crucial for patient resilience and treatment adherence. Merging advanced medical treatments with strong psychosocial support enables healthcare providers to improve the quality of life and outcomes for individuals facing this difficult condition.
