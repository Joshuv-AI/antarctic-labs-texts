Use of biomolecular emulator for characterizing flexible proteins by small‐angle x‐ray scattering
Authors: Yoni Haitin, Bar Manori, Moshe Giladi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFlexible proteins populate heterogeneous conformational ensembles that are essential for their function. Small‐angle x‐ray scattering (SAXS) is widely used to study protein structure in solution and to characterize conformational heterogeneity. Yet, extracting ensemble information from SAXS remains challenging because many distinct conformational distributions can give rise to the same experimental scattering profile. The ensemble optimization method (EOM) addresses this by selecting SAXS‐consistent ensembles from large conformational pools, but commonly used pool‐generation strategies rely on user‐defined flexible regions and may include energetically unfavorable conformations. Here, we implement BioEmu, a generative deep‐learning biomolecular emulator, as a sequence‐based, physically informed prior for generating conformational pools in SAXS ensemble analysis. Using the flexible protein CLIC5 as a proof‐of‐concept, BioEmu sampled compact and rare elongated, interface‐exposed conformations ab initio. The unweighted BioEmu pool average did not reproduce the experimental scattering, indicating that EOM‐guided selection remained necessary. Convergence analysis across five distinct flexible protein systems showed that EOM fit quality plateaus at ~1000 BioEmu conformers pool, underscoring the feasible computational cost of this approach. Finally, we demonstrate the generalizability of BioEmu implementation for SAXS analysis on a diverse cohort of 20 SASBDB entries, ranging from intrinsically disordered to multi‐domain proteins. Together, our results support BioEmu as a scalable, sequence‐based prior for EOM‐based SAXS modeling, while underscoring that selected ensembles should be interpreted as SAXS‐consistent models rather than unique representations of equilibrium populations.

INTRODUCTION

Proteins are dynamic macromolecules that populate ensembles of interconverting conformations across a wide range of timescales (Zhuravlev & Papoian, 2010). These fluctuations are integral to their function, supporting substrate recognition, catalysis, and allostery (Astore et al., 2024). Moreover, perturbations that subtly reshape an ensemble (e.g., mutations, ligands, solution conditions) can have profound phenotypic effects (Ponzoni & Bahar, 2018; Tzeng & Kalodimos, 2011).

X‐ray crystallography, and to a lesser extent cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo‐EM), can provide high‐resolution snapshots of different protein conformations, yet suffer from inherent undersampling of the conformational space in its entirety (Banari et al., 2025). Moreover, since the structural dynamics describing the transition between discrete states are of mechanistic importance, much attention has been dedicated to resolving these transitions with dynamic structural approaches (Konermann et al., 2011; Meisburger et al., 2017; Trabjerg et al., 2018). Small‐angle x‐ray scattering (SAXS) is a particularly useful method in this context because it reports on ensemble‐averaged size and shape under near‐physiological conditions in solution, providing comprehensive structural information, including molecular shape, size, and structural variability (Dyer et al., 2014; Koch et al., 2003).

A prominent advantage of SAXS is that the protein structure and dynamics can be assessed ab initio, requiring no prior structural data for analysis (Dyer et al., 2014; Rambo & Tainer, 2011). However, SAXS data do not define a unique structural solution, because the measured scattering curve can be explained by different structural models (Weiel et al., 2019). Thus, structural interpretation requires additional constraints, such as physically realistic models, to avoid over‐interpretation (Ahmed et al., 2021). A widely used strategy for flexible systems is ensemble modeling, in which the experimental SAXS profile is explained as an average over several conformations, selected from a much larger conformational pool. The ensemble optimization method (EOM) is one of the most commonly used pipelines for this purpose (Bernadó et al., 2007; Tria et al., 2015). Briefly, flexible regions (e.g., interdomain linkers or intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs)) are predefined by the user. Next, a large pool (usually 10,000 structures) of stereochemically possible conformations is generated (using RANCH), with the flexible portions of the protein represented as chains of dummy residues. Finally, an ensemble with the best fit to the experimental data is selected from this pool using a genetic algorithm (implemented in GAJOE). In this procedure, each candidate solution (“individual”) encodes an ensemble as a set of conformers (often treated as the “genes”), the ensemble‐averaged theoretical scattering is computed, and its agreement with the experimental data is scored (typically by χ2). The genetic algorithm then iteratively improves the population through selection, crossover, and mutation, enriching ensembles that better fit the data.

While this approach has been proven useful and widely applicable, the quality and interpretability of any ensemble solution hinges on pool generation, which has several limitations: (i) structural biases may arise from the predefinition of flexible regions; (ii) while the flexible region models are stereochemically feasible, energetically unfavorable conformations (e.g., with exposed hydrophobic surfaces) may prevail (Zhu et al., 2017); (iii) the coarse‐grained representation of the flexible regions by dummy residues may limit the obtained structural insights (Sagar et al., 2021). Therefore, alternative approaches for pool generation, such as the use of atomistic pools derived from molecular dynamics (MD) simulations or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) studies, were explored (Debiec et al., 2018; Sibille & Bernadó, 2012; Thompson et al., 2017; Weiel et al., 2019). However, these approaches are technically demanding and computationally costly, precluding their high‐throughput implementation.

Biomolecular emulator (BioEmu) is a generative deep‐learning system designed to overcome the pool‐generation bottleneck by rapidly sampling sequence‐conditioned, approximate equilibrium ensembles at scale (Lewis et al., 2025). It couples an AlphaFold‐derived Evoformer (Jumper et al., 2021) representation of the input sequence to a denoising diffusion model that efficiently generates 3D structures, enabling the production of thousands of statistically independent conformations with substantially lower computational cost than conventional MD while capturing diverse heterogeneity (e.g., domain motions, local unfolding, cryptic pockets). Here, we use BioEmu to provide a fast, physically informed conformational pool for SAXS data analysis of flexible proteins (Figure 1). Across 25 SAXS datasets in our test cohort, BioEmu sampling, requiring only the protein sequence, captured the relevant conformational landscape with much smaller pools (≤1000 conformers) than required by standard RANCH generation. However, a downstream ensemble‐optimization step with GAJOE remained necessary, as the raw BioEmu sampling probabilities did not fully match the solution populations reported by SAXS. Overall, this workflow yields physically realistic ensembles while markedly reducing the cost and hand‐tuning of conformational sampling.

The BioEmu‐EOM workflow. BioEmu uses a diffusion‐based model to generate thousands of independent 3D backbone conformations directly from proteins' sequence. Side chains are added and models are filtered to ensure all‐atom structures are stereochemically feasible and free of steric clashes. Finally, a genetic algorithm selects the specific ensemble of conformers that best fit the experimental SAXS scattering curve.

RESULTS

Ab initio sampling of conformational pools by BioEmu

RANCH provides a practical computational route for generating conformational pools for SAXS analysis of flexible proteins, but it typically relies on user‐specified definitions of rigid bodies and flexible/disordered segments, which can introduce model bias and may overlook important physicochemical determinants of inter‐domain organization (e.g., electrostatic repulsion, unfavorable exposure of hydrophobic surfaces) (Zhu et al., 2017). BioEmu offers an alternative, ab initio route to generating physically plausible conformations that emulate equilibrium distributions directly from sequence (Lewis et al., 2025). BioEmu couples an AlphaFold‐derived Evoformer representation with a diffusion‐based generative model and is trained in stages—pretraining on a clustered/augmented AlphaFold database to promote structural diversity, followed by training on hundreds of milliseconds of all‐atom MD simulations to learn equilibrium ensemble statistics, thereby expanding the diversity of native‐like conformations (Singh, 2025).

The choride intracellular channel (CLIC) proteins are highly flexible in solution and are thought to interconvert between a compact, globular state and a more elongated conformation (Ferofontov et al., 2020; Manori et al., 2024). These GST‐Ω‐like proteins comprise two distinct domains: an N‐terminal thioredoxin domain and a C‐terminal α‐helical domain. In our previous work, the crystallographic structure of CLIC5 failed to reproduce the experimental SAXS profile, whereas EOM analysis assuming interdomain flexibility yielded a broad conformational distribution spanning radius of gyration (R g) ≈ 19–35 Å, from which an ensemble providing a good fit to the scattering curve could be selected (χ2 = 0.69) (Figure 2a,c,d) (Ferofontov et al., 2020). However, direct experimental evidence for such an elongated state, implying exposure of the interdomain interface, has remained elusive. Moreover, 3000 frames derived from all‐atom MD simulations totaling 3 μs of CLIC5 (Manori et al., 2024) sampled a very narrow conformational pool (Figure 2e,f) and produced a poor fit to the SAXS data (χ2 = 1.55), underscoring the difficulty of capturing the full conformational equilibrium within feasible MD timescales. We therefore reasoned that CLIC5 provides an ideal proof‐of‐concept system to test whether BioEmu can efficiently sample low‐population conformations relevant to SAXS‐based ensemble analysis of flexible proteins (Figure 2b,g,h).

The BioEmu‐generated pool, spanning R g ≈ 20–25 Å, markedly differed from the pool produced by RANCH. In contrast to the RANCH pool, which sampled the compact state only infrequently (Figure 2c), BioEmu assigned the highest probability to this conformation (R g ≈ 21 Å; Figure 2g), consistent with it being energetically favored. BioEmu also generated elongated conformations with exposure of the interdomain interface ab initio (Figure 2g,h), but these were comparatively rare, in line with the expected thermodynamic penalty associated with exposing hydrophobic surface area.

Because BioEmu favored compact CLIC5 conformations while still retaining rare extended states, we next asked whether this prior alone was sufficient to reproduce the experimental SAXS profile. This ensemble average, however, could not reproduce the data (χ2 = 71.0), indicating that reweighting was necessary (Figure 2b). We therefore used the BioEmu pool as input to the genetic‐algorithm selection step (GAJOE) employed in the original EOM framework. This EOM analysis with the BioEmu‐derived pool produced a modest enrichment of extended states and yielded an excellent fit to the experimental SAXS curve (χ2 = 0.66) (Figure 2b,g). Together, these results indicate that BioEmu provides an ab initio route to generate physically plausible conformational pools that efficiently explore the solution conformational landscape of CLIC5.

Convergence of BioEmu‐driven ensemble selection enables substantially smaller pools

Unlike RANCH, where conformations are produced by random sampling guided by user‐defined flexibility, BioEmu enriches physically plausible states, thereby potentially reducing the number of models needed to cover relevant conformational space. To test whether physically informed conformational sampling by BioEmu permits smaller, computationally tractable pools for SAXS ensemble analysis, we performed a convergence analysis on five representative proteins (Figures 3a–e and S1). For each target, we randomly selected an increasing number of conformers (50, 100, 1000, 2000, 5000) from the BioEmu‐generated pool in triplicate and applied GAJOE to select the ensemble that best reproduces the experimental SAXS curve.

We selected several example proteins previously characterized by us that exhibit pronounced conformational flexibility (Ferofontov et al., 2020; Giladi, Hiller, et al., 2013; Giladi, Michaeli, et al., 2013; Manori et al., 2024). These included CLIC5‐WT (Figure 3a); CLIC5‐F34D (Figure 3b), shown to stabilize the globular conformation; tandem C2AB domains of the calcium sensor DOC2B (Figure 3c), connected by a long and flexible linker; and the regulatory calcium‐binding domain tandem CBD12 from the sodium‐calcium exchanger, connected by a very short linker and exhibiting calcium‐dependent stabilization, analyzed in both the apo (Figure 3d) and calcium‐bound (Figure 3e) states. Across all five proteins, the quality of fit improved as pool size increased, but rapidly approached a plateau, indicating practical convergence at modest pool sizes (Figure 3a–e, rightmost panels). Notably, the smallest pools (50–100 conformers) sometimes yielded visibly less stable ensemble distributions and poorer fits (e.g., broader scatter and higher χ2 values), consistent with under‐sampling of low‐populated yet SAXS‐relevant states. In most cases, moving from 100 to 1000 conformers produced a clear reduction in χ2, whereas further expansion toward ~10,000 conformers yielded only marginal additional gains. As expected for SAXS ensemble modeling, individual selected conformers were not identical between runs (Figure S1), reflecting the non‐unique nature of SAXS‐consistent ensemble solutions. Nevertheless, the reproducibility of fit quality and ensemble‐level R g distributions (Figure S1) supports the practical robustness of the BioEmu‐GAJOE workflow. Our data support the premise that BioEmu reduces the reliance on brute‐force sampling because the pool is not assembled at random but samples thermodynamically and structurally reasonable regions of conformational space. In summary, the overall convergence trends demonstrate that BioEmu enables SAXS ensemble modeling with pools on the order of ~1000 conformers in the systems tested. This size remains computationally feasible for routine application while retaining the interpretability of the established EOM framework.

General applicability of BioEmu‐derived pools for EOM‐based SAXS ensemble selection

To assess the generalizability of the BioEmu‐EOM workflow, we re‐analyzed 20 flexible proteins with published SAXS ensemble models deposited in SASBDB, a curated repository of experimental SAS data and derived models (Valentini et al., 2015). This cohort included monomeric proteins up to 500 residues, ranging from IDPs to multi‐domain proteins (Table 1). Based on our convergence analysis (Figure 3), we generated a BioEmu pool of 1000 conformers for each entry from the primary sequence alone and subjected this pool to the GAJOE genetic‐algorithm selection to obtain a best‐fitting ensemble (Figures 4, S2, Table 1).

SASBDB benchmark demonstrates broad applicability of BioEmu for SAXS ensemble analysis. Twenty flexible‐protein SAXS datasets were selected from SASBDB (IDs shown) and re‐analyzed using a standardized workflow. For each entry, 1000 BioEmu conformers were generated from the primary sequence and used as the input pool for GAJOE selection. For each protein, the published R g distributions (as deposited in SASBDB) of the pool and selected ensembles are compared to those obtained using the BioEmu‐GAJOE workflow. Fit quality (χ2) is reported for the GAJOE‐selected BioEmu ensembles alongside the corresponding published values and colored according to the corresponding distributions.

Across the dataset, the unweighted BioEmu pool averages generally fit the SAXS data less well than the GAJOE‐selected ensembles, confirming that BioEmu should not be used as an unrefined population estimate (Table 1). However, after GAJOE selection, the BioEmu‐generated pools exhibited systematic differences from the published R g distributions (Figure 4), consistent with the notion that BioEmu provides a physically informed prior rather than reproducing distributions shaped by the original random pool‐generation strategy used in each study. Importantly, despite these inherent differences in the priors, GAJOE selection from up to 1000 BioEmu conformers was sufficient to recover ensembles that closely matched the experimental SAXS profiles, with fit quality comparable to the published analyses (Figure 4).

Together, these results support the wide applicability of our approach, as a modest pool size of up to 1000 BioEmu conformers is broadly sufficient to fit SAXS data across diverse flexible systems, minimizing computational costs while providing physically viable models for EOM analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this work, we propose an ensemble‐first SAXS workflow in which conformational heterogeneity is generated ab initio from sequence using BioEmu and then refined against experimental data using established GAJOE ensemble selection (Figure 1). Across a diverse benchmark of flexible systems, including multidomain proteins with long linkers, compact domain tandems with short linkers, and ligand‐stabilized states, we find that BioEmu provides conformational pools that are qualitatively different from those produced by RANCH (Figures 2, 3, 4). Importantly, these pools enable robust SAXS fits with substantially smaller pool sizes. This is particularly evident in our convergence analysis, where fit quality rapidly approaches a plateau around ~1000 conformers across multiple test proteins (Figure 3), supporting the practical feasibility of BioEmu‐driven pool generation for routine SAXS interpretation.

A central implication of these findings is that pool generation is not a neutral preprocessing step: it encodes strong assumptions about what conformations are even “allowed” to compete during ensemble selection. RANCH is a convenient and widely applicable stereochemical generator that has been successfully used for the ensemble analysis of numerous proteins, including multimers. However, it typically requires a priori designation of rigid bodies and flexible segments and may over‐represent geometrically feasible yet energetically implausible states (when the flexible region is treated as a generic dummy‐residue chain). By contrast, BioEmu offers a prior that often assigns high probability to compact, native‐like states while still sampling rarer elongated conformations. Notably, this effect is clearly observed in CLIC5, where interface‐exposed states are predicted at low frequency ab initio by BioEmu, consistent with the expected thermodynamic penalty (Figure 2). The shift from random geometric sampling to a learned equilibrium‐like prior helps explain why dramatically smaller pools can still capture SAXS‐relevant heterogeneity (Figures 3, 4) (Bernadó et al., 2007).

Accumulating evidence suggests that in multidomain proteins, linkers encode preferred states and propagation pathways rather than acting as passive, flexible tethers (Ma et al., 2011). In this view, linker sequence and its interactions with adjoining domains can bias the ensemble toward specific substates and lower barriers along functionally relevant transitions. This perspective helps rationalize why approaches that treat linkers as generic dummy residues may misrepresent both the population of states and the energetic accessibility of domain arrangements. It also suggests a compelling opportunity for emulator‐based priors. Since BioEmu conditions directly on sequence and is trained to emulate equilibrium ensemble statistics, it may be better positioned to reflect sequence‐encoded preferences in linker‐mediated inter‐domain organization than methods that predefine flexible regions without sequence‐resolved energetics. Importantly, pairing BioEmu‐derived all‐atom ensembles with SAXS constraints can yield experimentally testable hypotheses, such as predicted inter‐domain contacts or interface exposure that can be probed by orthogonal validation, underscoring that these models are best interpreted as useful priors for downstream experimental validation.

Even when BioEmu produces a plausible distribution, the unweighted pool‐average scattering can deviate substantially from experiment, necessitating a downstream selection/reweighting step as implemented by GAJOE (Figures 1, 2, and S2). The practical take‐home is that BioEmu is best viewed as a prior over conformations, while SAXS (via EOM/GAJOE) supplies the data‐driven update that yields an experimentally consistent posterior ensemble. Our results highlight that physically informed sampling does not eliminate the need for experimental reweighting.

A key consideration is the current scope of BioEmu, within which its predictions should be interpreted. BioEmu is principally an emulator for monomeric soluble proteins under fixed conditions (Lewis et al., 2025) and does not explicitly account for parameters such as pH or temperature, nor for ligands, membranes, or oligomerization states, factors that can strongly reshape conformational equilibria (Dyer et al., 2014; Rambo & Tainer, 2011). These limitations do not diminish BioEmu's utility as a fast, physically informed prior; rather, they motivate practical validation against experimental observables (as done here), treating BioEmu as a starting ensemble rather than a final population estimate, and pursuing future extensions that incorporate complexes, ligand conditioning, and longer‐sequence training. An especially attractive avenue is direct fine‐tuning or reweighting against experimental SAXS data, so that experimental constraints shape not only the selected ensembles but also the underlying emulator prior. In that sense, SAXS datasets like those analyzed here could become part of a virtuous cycle: emulators provide scalable, interpretable ensemble hypotheses, while experiments provide the needed constraints to systematically improve the fidelity of the learned equilibrium distributions.

In conclusion, our BioEmu‐guided SAXS workflow leverages sequence‐based ensemble generation to provide a user‐independent generation of conformational pools for SAXS analysis of flexible proteins. Using pools of up to 1000 conformers across in‐house targets and SASBDB benchmarks, this approach maintains sampling cost while reducing pool‐generation bias and providing physically viable structural models spanning the conformational space.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Conformational ensemble generation with BioEmu

Conformational ensembles were generated using BioEmu, a generative deep‐learning model that approximately samples from the equilibrium distribution of protein conformations (Lewis et al., 2025). For each protein, 1000–10,000 independent backbone conformations were sampled from the input amino acid sequence on a single GPU. BioEmu was run with the ‐‐filter_samples option enabled, which applies internal quality checks to the generated conformations and discards structures with steric clashes or other geometric violations. Thus, the final pools contain fewer conformers than the number initially requested. As BioEmu generates Cα‐trace (backbone‐only) structures, all‐atom models including side chains were reconstructed using PDBFixer, which adds missing atoms based on residue identity and optimizes their placement (Eastman et al., 2017). Each backbone frame was processed independently to yield a complete all‐atom PDB structure.

Pool average profile comparison

To evaluate if the raw BioEmu ensemble, without selection, recapitulates the experimental scattering data, theoretical SAXS profiles were calculated for each all‐atom conformer using CRYSOL (Franke et al., 2017) from the ATSAS software package (Manalastas‐Cantos et al., 2021). Next, the unweighted arithmetic mean of all individual CRYSOL profiles was computed and compared to the experimental data.

Ensemble optimization against experimental SAXS data

Theoretical SAXS profiles for the conformational pool were computed using ffmaker from the ATSAS software package (Manalastas‐Cantos et al., 2021). FFMAKER produces a matrix of theoretical intensity profiles and an accompanying size statistics file containing the Rg and maximum particle dimension for each conformer. Next, ensemble selection was performed using GAJOE from the EOM framework (Tria et al., 2015). GAJOE employs a genetic algorithm to select an ensemble of conformations whose population‐weighted average scattering profile best fits the experimental SAXS data in a least‐squares sense. The genetic algorithm was run for 1000 generations with ensemble sizes ranging from 2 to 20 conformers.

Protein expression and purification

Human CLIC5‐F34D was generated and expressed as previously described (Manori et al., 2024). For protein purification, cells were resuspended in CLIC buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris–HCl, and 1 mM tris(2‐carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP), pH 8.0, supplemented with 15 mM imidazole, 2.5 μg mL−1 DNaseI, 10 mg lysozyme (Fisher Scientific), and 2 mM phenylmethane sulfonyl fluoride (Acros Organics). Following cell lysis, the lysate was subjected to centrifugation at 40,000 × g for 45 min at 4°C. The supernatant was loaded onto a Ni2+ affinity resin column (HisTrap HP, Cytiva) and eluted using CLIC buffer supplemented with 300 mM imidazole. Next, the His‐tag was cleaved using TEV protease at 4°C overnight, followed by desalting (HiPrep 26/10, Cytiva) into CLIC buffer and subjected to a second Ni2+ affinity purification column. The flow‐through was concentrated and subjected to size‐exclusion chromatography (SEC) using a HiLoad 16/60 Superdex 75 column (Cytiva) equilibrated with gel filtration buffer containing 150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Na+‐HEPES, pH 7.5. Pooled fractions were concentrated, flash‐frozen in liquid N2, and stored at −80°C until use.

SAXS analysis

SAXS data were measured in gel filtration buffer supplemented with 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol at beamline BM29 of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF), Grenoble, France. Data were collected at 5°C with x‐ray beam at wavelength λ = 0.992 Å, and the distance from the sample to detector (Pilatus3 2 M, Dectris Ltd.) was 2.81 meters, covering a scattering vector range (q = 4 πsinθ/λ) from 0.007 to 0.55 Å−1. 10 frames were recorded for the buffer and sample, with an exposure time of 1 s/frame. The 2D images were reduced to 1D scattering profiles, followed by subtraction of the buffer scattering from the sample profile using the on site software. To account for possible inter‐particle effects, the sample was measured at three concentrations (Table 2). As no concentration‐dependent effect was observed, the highest concentration (4 mg/mL) was used for downstream analysis to preserve high signal‐to‐noise ratio at the higher angles (Table 2, Figure S3). The experimental R g was calculated from data at low q values in the range of qR g <1.3, according to the Guinier approximation using PRIMUS (Table 2 and Figure S3) (Manalastas‐Cantos et al., 2021). The D max values and the Porod volume were derived from the paired‐distance distribution function (PDDF or P(r)) calculated using GNOM (Table 2 and Figure S3) (Manalastas‐Cantos et al., 2021).
