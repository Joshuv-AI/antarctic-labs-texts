Synergistic Strategies of Biomolecular Transport Technologies in Transdermal Healthcare Systems
Authors: Jieun Han, Yi‐Jeong Choi, Seung‐Kyun Kang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTransdermal healthcare systems have gained significant attention for their painless and convenient drug administration, as well as their ability to detect biomarkers promptly. However, the skin barrier limits the candidates of biomolecules that can be transported, and reliance on simple diffusion poses a bottleneck for personalized diagnosis and treatment. Consequently, recent advancements in transdermal transport technologies have evolved toward active methods based on external energy sources. Multiple combinations of these technologies have also shown promise for increasing therapeutic effectiveness and diagnostic accuracy as delivery efficiency is maximized. Furthermore, wearable healthcare platforms are being developed in diverse aspects for patient convenience, safety, and on‐demand treatment. Herein, a comprehensive overview of active transdermal delivery technologies is provided, highlighting the combination‐based diagnostics, therapeutics, and theragnostics, along with the latest trends in platform advancements. This offers insights into the potential applications of next‐generation wearable transdermal medical devices for personalized autonomous healthcare.Keywords: biomolecular transport, biosensing, drug delivery, healthcare platform, theragnostics, transdermal technologies, wearable patch

Introduction

The aging population and pandemics have greatly amplified global concern about health. With the increased focus on health, smart wearable healthcare devices have gained significant attention as the next healthcare paradigm that can not only play an important role in addressing the emergency but also revolutionize personalized care.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] Along with this, the necessities and advantages of transdermal molecular transport systems are becoming increasingly evident in contrast to conventional methods such as hypodermic extraction, intravenous injection, and oral administration. Transdermal molecular transport is non‐invasive or minimally invasive, thus making the process painless and convenient for patients, including children or the elderly.[ 9 , 10 , 11 ] This method helps patients avoid side effects associated with the digestion and metabolism of drugs, offering a short diffusive pathway to the vasculature.[ 12 ] Often, this approach requires a lower dosage of drugs than oral administration since first‐pass metabolism is bypassed, which eliminates drug loss due to factors such as pH, enzymes, and bacteria.[ 13 , 14 ] Additionally, transdermal transport supports sustained drug release and enables continuous biomarker monitoring while attached to the skin surface.[ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ] As a result, the global wearable devices market has experienced significant growth and continues to expand rapidly. According to Market.US, the market was valued at ≈$40 billion as of 2023 and is expected to grow to $110 billion by 2030, at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of ≈16%.[ 21 ] Specifically, those related to drug delivery and biosensing via transdermal transport are experiencing significant growth, reflecting their implications, with a CAGR approaching 25%.[ 22 ]

However, transdermal molecular transport also has its limitations, primarily due to the barrier function of the skin. Specifically, the high resistance of the stratum corneum in the epidermis significantly impedes the transdermal diffusion of molecules.[ 23 ] Only small molecules with low molecular weights in the range of several hundred daltons and possessing lipophilicity can pass through the stratum corneum, making the transport of larger or hydrophilic molecules challenging.[ 24 ] Moreover, relying solely on simple diffusion for transport can compromise the reliability of transdermal transport in aspects such as controlling the onset time of drug release, variations in efficacy due to epidermal structure, and quantitative limitations in biomarker extraction.

As a result, a variety of methods aimed at improving the efficiency of transdermal molecular transport have long been extensively explored. It began with passive approaches, which promoted diffusion through the skin via nanocarriers and chemical enhancers.[ 25 , 26 , 27 ] Subsequently, external energy sources such as electricity, heat, and mechanical energy were employed facilitating molecular movement through the stratum corneum.[ 28 , 29 ] Through such active means, not only has transdermal therapy advanced, but also the sensing of biomarkers enabling diagnosis.[ 30 ] Extraction of ions, proteins, and other biomolecules within the body, or their quantification via minimally invasive electrochemical signals, became feasible.[ 31 , 32 ] Furthermore, the range of deliverable therapeutics and biomarkers expanded to large molecules, for example, from anticancer, analgesics to hormones, and from glucose to proteins and DNA, respectively (Figure 1 ).[ 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 ] Efforts to maximize delivery efficiency persist due to drawbacks such as skin irritation and high‐power consumption associated with single transport technology.[ 37 , 38 ] Therefore, research on the convergence of transport technologies is emerging,[ 39 , 40 ] aiming toward autonomous and multifunctional healthcare systems through integration with various modules and functional materials.[ 41 , 42 , 43 ]

Schematic diagram of transdermal molecular transport. Theragnostics enabled by drug delivery and biomarker extraction through wearable transdermal devices.

Currently, numerous studies actively pursued the combination of the aforementioned technologies to achieve synergistic effects,[ 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] thereby maximizing transdermal transport efficiency with enhancing therapeutic effects and diagnostic precision.[ 39 , 40 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 ] Even the patch enabling theragnostics within a single system via dual‐mode diagnosis and therapy has been developed.[ 41 , 42 , 54 , 55 ] Moreover, the consistent advancements of personalized healthcare platforms continue, encompassing patient convenience, safety, and on‐demand delivery.[ 41 , 56 , 57 ] Beyond increasing transport efficiency, progress is moving forward with diverse directions and strategies. These include the system for real‐time diagnosis to precisely release drugs via closed‐loop systems,[ 41 ] advancements in energy harvesting power sources for active transdermal transport,[ 48 , 57 ] and biodegradability that also account for patient safety and e‐waste.[ 43 , 58 , 59 , 60 ] Despite this active research, many reviews have been focused on single transdermal transport technologies for wearable healthcare systems,[ 61 , 62 , 63 ] while reports on the synergistic effects of the latest integrated technologies are lacking compared with their importance and effectiveness.[ 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 ]

Here, we highlight the latest progress in enhancing molecular transport and multi‐functionalities through various combinations of physical transdermal delivery technologies. The growing interest in personalized autonomous healthcare also emphasizes the state‐of‐the‐art in terms of next‐generation transdermal healthcare platforms. Although some reviews have reported on diverse aspects of transdermal patches,[ 7 , 8 , 68 ] the synergistic effects of active transdermal transport technologies with trends in platform development have been hardly addressed. This review presents a unique perspective on the recent advancements in transdermal theragnostic technology and platforms. We first explain the skin structure with each layer and summarize each physical transdermal delivery technology. Next, the utilization of each technology for therapeutic drug delivery and biomarker sensing is then introduced. The review concludes with an outlook on wearable transdermal healthcare devices that have potential applications for personalized devices that enable autonomous health management.

Skin as a Primary Barrier of Molecular Transport

The skin serves as a protective barrier against external elements, playing a crucial role in sensory perception, and maintaining hydration and insulation.[ 69 , 70 , 71 , 72 ] Figure 2 shows the structure of mammalian skin, composed of three main layers: the epidermis, the dermis, and the hypodermis.[ 73 , 74 ] The epidermis, the outermost and most dense layer, serves as a protective shield and includes the stratum corneum,[ 75 , 76 , 77 , 78 ] which poses a primary challenge to transdermal delivery due to its thickness of ≈10–20 µm.[ 79 ] The stratum corneum, with its multiple packed layers of dead skin cells, called corneocytes embedded in a lipid matrix, acts as a waterproof barrier and presents significant tortuosity of diffusion path, making it the most challenging barrier in transdermal permeation.[ 77 , 80 ] The dermis, below the epidermal layer, is the main target layer for transdermal delivery due to its many capillary vessels, enabling rapid drug absorption into the systemic circulation.[ 81 , 82 ] The hypodermis, or subcutaneous tissue, consists mainly of adipocytes and connective tissue.[ 83 ] Unlike capillaries in the dermis, arteries and veins in the hypodermis have thick walls, restricting molecular permeation.[ 84 ]

Schematic representation of human skin as a primary barrier. A full layer of skin (left), an enlarged view of the epidermis (right, top), and simplified anatomy of the stratum corneum with magnification (right, bottom).

Hence, the challenge in transdermal molecular transport systems lies in overcoming the barrier properties of the stratum corneum,[ 77 , 79 ] facilitating drug penetration into the skin tissue and navigating through the cellular and vascular tissue to reach the desired target.[ 80 , 82 , 85 ] One major limitation is that only a small amount of drugs can be transported across the skin.[ 84 , 85 ] To solve this problem, various physical technologies have been developed for enhanced transdermal molecular transport and have shown competitive advantages over other drug administration methods in terms of the dose delivered cost‐efficiency, and effectiveness in therapy.[ 86 , 87 , 88 ]

Transdermal Molecular Transport Techniques

Technologies for Transdermal Drug Delivery

Microneedle

Microneedles are micro‐sized needles generally characterized by a length of 10–1000 µm and a diameter of 10–100 µm that can penetrate the stratum corneum and reach the viable epidermis or dermis without causing pain or bleeding.[ 89 , 90 , 91 ] When microneedles reach the dermal layer, where nociceptors are densely distributed, pain is induced. As the skin depth increases, the density of nociceptors also increases, leading to greater pain with longer needle lengths.[ 92 ] Therefore, unlike hypodermic needles, microneedles must minimize pain while targeting effective skin layers to facilitate biomolecular transport and achieve efficient skin penetration. Consequently, factors such as the length of the microneedles, the number within an array, width, and tip angle are critical elements in the geometrical design of microneedles.[ 89 , 93 ] They are gaining attention for their low invasiveness, painless administration, and high drug bioavailability.[ 94 , 95 , 96 , 97 , 98 ] The microneedle does not require any external energy source, as it does not rely on stimuli such as electric fields, heat, or ultrasound, and therefore additional equipment is unnecessary for drug injection, which is favorable to combined with other methods.[ 39 , 40 , 41 , 50 , 51 , 60 ] They are versatile in material and dimension, allowing customization for specific diseases or drugs.[ 90 , 95 , 98 ] Microneedles can be fabricated from metals,[ 99 ] polymers,[ 44 , 58 ] silicon,[ 100 , 101 ] or sugars, each with unique advantages.[ 102 , 103 ] Ongoing research focuses on developing new materials or composites to enhance microneedle performance.[ 59 , 104 ] They are classified into solid microneedles for drug diffusion,[ 105 ] coated microneedles with drug coatings,[ 106 , 107 ] hollow microneedles for drug infusion,[ 108 ] and dissolving microneedles made of biodegradable materials that release embedded drugs after insertion.[ 109 ] Figure 3d shows an example of a microneedle patch for transdermal delivery of hydrophilic macromolecules.[ 44 ] The patch was composed of chitosan for a biocompatible, degradable, and nontoxic drug delivery platform. The chitosan microneedle patch was evaluated using bovine serum albumin (BSA) as a model protein drug and showed sustained release of BSA over at least 8 days with ≈95% of the drug released during this period. Also, the model drug could gradually diffuse to a depth of 300 µm in mouse skin using fluorescently labeled protein. The reported results provided a transdermal delivery platform for the sustained release of macromolecules. Nevertheless, microneedles using biodegradable materials such as chitosan predominantly rely on passive diffusion, leading to an initial burst release, making it difficult to control the release. Therefore, recent attempts have focused on strategies such as drug loading in nanocarriers for a stimulus‐responsive manner,[ 110 ] as well as integrating various power modules to retain drugs for periodic release.[ 41 , 111 ]

Laser Ablation

Along with microneedles, laser ablation also delivers drugs without passing through the densely packed skin cell layers.[ 45 , 112 , 113 ] It involves the use of a pulsed laser beam to selectively vaporize and remove or ablate a precise amount of tissue, which allows for the enhanced delivery of therapeutic molecules through the skin.[ 112 ] The laser energy is absorbed by the targeted tissue, leading to localized heating and the heat is used to achieve thermolysis of the skin.[ 112 , 113 , 114 ] This process removes stratum corneum, which creates microholes with a diameter of 10–100 µm and a depth of 10–20 µm for drug delivery, and it can be controlled by adjusting the laser parameters, such as wavelength, pulse duration, fluence, and spot size.[ 112 ] Among the various types of lasers available in clinical practice, pulsed carbon dioxide (CO2) laser and erbium:yttrium‐aluminum‐garnet (Er:YAG) laser are commonly used for ablating the stratum corneum barrier because the others have limited effectiveness due to minimal tissue absorption and thermal effects caused by their wavelengths.[ 115 , 116 ] The wavelength of mid‐infrared Er:YAG light with a wavelength of 2940 nm and a CO2 laser with a wavelength of 10 600 nm shows reduced damage even at deeper microholes and leads to an ideal light source for enhanced transdermal drug delivery.[ 116 ] Moreover, the fluence and the spot size affect the depth and diameter of the microholes, and the number and density of the holes, respectively, which are related to molecular size to be transported.[ 45 , 112 , 115 ] Figure 3e presents a representative example of a laser‐assisted transdermal drug delivery system.[ 45 ] The study investigated the use of ablative fractional CO2 laser to achieve transdermal delivery of hydrophilic drugs for three days to a week. Drug powder was coated onto reservoir patches and applied to laser‐treated skin, and water evaporated from laser‐generated skin microchannels gradually dissolved the drug powder, enabling multi‐day sustained drug delivery. Three different hydrophilic drugs, sulforhodamine b, zidovudine, and bovine serum albumin were tested as model drugs. Not only were the laser parameters optimized, but it was also confirmed that the drugs penetrated ≈30 times more in laser‐treated skin than intact one within a day. These results obtained a simple laser‐based delivery platform achieving high‐dose week‐long sustained transdermal delivery of hydrophilic drugs.

Sonophoresis

While laser ablation creates microchannels through precise tissue removal using pulsed laser energy, sonophoresis utilizes ultrasound waves to facilitate deeper drug penetration across the skin barrier.[ 117 , 118 , 119 ] There are several mechanisms by which sonophoresis enhances transdermal drug delivery.[ 46 , 120 , 121 , 122 ] One is that the acoustic pressure waves cause cavitation, which refers to the formation, growth, and collapse of microscopic bubbles within the skin and this cavitation generates microjets and shock waves that create temporary pores in the stratum corneum, facilitating drug penetration.[ 46 ] Another is increasing the fluidity and permeability of the lipid bilayers within the skin by sonophoresis.[ 122 ] The oscillating ultrasound waves create shear forces that disrupt the lipid structure, making it easier for drugs to diffuse through the lipid‐rich intercellular spaces.[ 120 , 122 ] Sonophoresis can be applied with low frequency (20–100 kHz) or high frequency (1–3 MHz), depending on the depth of penetration.[ 119 ] Since there is a trade‐off between penetration depth and damage to the skin, the applied frequency and intensity should be optimized according to the molecules to be transported.[ 123 ] The duration of ultrasound treatment can also vary depending on the specific application.[ 124 ] Figure 3f represents the wearable ultrasound patch for enhanced transdermal cosmeceutical delivery.[ 46 ] Yu et al. developed a conformable ultrasound patch that improves the transdermal absorption of niacinamide. The patch utilized piezoelectric transducers embedded in a soft elastomer to create localized cavitation pockets within the skin. Performance of the patch was characterized through simulation models of acoustic pressure fields and cavitation bubble dynamics, and the final system showed a 26.2‐fold increase in drug penetration after a 10 min ultrasound application, indicating the effective potential of the transdermal delivery device. Beyond the patchable sonophoresis without additional equipment, it is expected that it can be used as a user‐friendly transdermal delivery platform through the miniaturization and thin‐film processes of transducers and electronic modules.

Electroporation

Electroporation disrupts the stratum corneum using short pulses of high‐voltage electric fields to enhance skin permeability.[ 125 , 126 ] This process creates temporary pores in skin cell membranes, which significantly increase the conductivity of drugs.[ 125 , 127 ] Electroporation expands the range of deliverable compounds, especially large macromolecules and hydrophilic drugs that normally cannot penetrate tissues.[ 127 , 128 ] Critical parameters such as voltage, pulse duration, and frequency determine the effectiveness and safety of the method, tailored to specific skin and drug characteristics.[ 125 ] Low‐frequency pulses (1 Hz to hundreds of Hz) allow cells time to recover between pulses, minimizing damage and enhancing drug specificity, while high‐frequency pulses (several kHz) are used for rapid and efficient delivery.[ 126 ] Figure 3g is one of the examples of electroporation‐mediated enhanced transdermal drug delivery.[ 47 ] The study focuses on evaluating electroporation at different amplitudes of electric pulses (50–570 V) delivered between the electrode pin pairs, and 360 V for 100 µs duration pulse was optimized with fluorescently labeled molecules quantitatively. Non‐invasive multi‐array electrodes were utilized to achieve precise control of drug delivery both topically and transdermally.

Iontophoresis

Unlike electroporation, iontophoresis uses a continuous, low‐voltage current to propel charged drugs or ions across the skin.[ 129 ] Iontophoresis enhances the transport of charged molecules through the skin by electric field, and two electrodes and ionized drug formulations are commonly placed on the skin.[ 130 , 131 ] Molecules are transported across the skin by electrophoresis and/or electroosmosis, in which electrophoresis is the movement of charged particles in fluid medium under the influence of an electric field to electrodes of opposite charge by electrical attractive and repulsive forces.[ 132 ] On the other hand, electroosmosis is the movement of liquid or solvent through a porous medium under the electric field, where the charged surface of the medium attracts ions with opposite charges from the liquid to form a double layer at the interface, and this electrostatic interaction induces a net flow of liquid known as electroosmotic flow in the opposite direction the ion movement.[ 133 , 134 ] The function of iontophoresis depends on the degree of ionization and mobility of the drug, which is dependent on the pH, charge, and concentration of drug, as well as the potential applied to the electrode attached to both a drug reservoir and a counter electrode.[ 135 , 136 ] Generally, the voltage range is between 0.1–10 V, the current range is 0.1–2 mA cm−2, and the time to apply the electric field is usually between several minutes to hours.[ 130 , 135 , 137 ] Figure 3h shows the representation of the iontophoretic drug delivery patch that incorporates a magnesium (Mg) battery for enhanced transdermal medication.[ 48 ] Also, poly(acrylamide‐copolymerized‐p‐styrene‐bipyridine) hydrogels were used as drug reservoirs and also cathode, whose electrochemical properties were optimized with the amount of viologen. Specifically, the battery equipped with a 103 Ω resistor consistently produced an output voltage of 0.72 V and a current of 0.18 mA cm−2, thus the release efficacy increased by 40% for 3 h of application. This study demonstrated the effective treatment of imiquimod‐induced psoriasis in a mouse model with not only qualitative fluorescent image analysis for penetration depth but also quantitative concentration analysis. With its reliable energy supply, simplified configuration, and optimal electrical skin‐device interface, this battery‐powered iontophoresis patch achieved non‐invasive treatment for chronic epidermal diseases. Battery‐based iontophoresis offers the advantage of being capable of a conformal form without the need for bulky power supply equipment, but has limitations in that not suitable for long‐term or periodic drug delivery. Accordingly, various studies are conducted using self‐powered modules such as triboelectric nanogenerator (TENG) and biofuel in iontophoresis patches,[ 57 ] and employing near‐field communication (NFC) circuits to enable wireless periodic drug release via controlled current.[ 111 , 138 ]

Jet Injection

In contrast to iontophoresis, which uses an electric field to transport charged molecules through the skin, jet injection employs high‐pressure liquid jets to mechanically facilitate drug penetration into the dermis or subcutaneous tissue.[ 139 , 140 ] In jet injection, a high‐pressure device, commonly referred to as a jet injector, is used to generate a narrow and focused stream of liquid that may contain the drug of interest or be suspended in a suitable carrier solution.[ 141 , 142 ] The jet injector applies a high‐pressure force to the drug, which then forms a high‐velocity jet that penetrates the skin mechanically.[ 143 ] Jet injectors can be powered by different energy sources, such as springs and gas, and can deliver drugs in picoliter to microliter volume with precise control of penetration depth from micrometers to millimeters.[ 139 , 143 ] The design of the nozzle typically ranging from 50–300 µm in diameter, generates a high velocity jet with speeds of ≈100–300 m s−1.[ 144 ] Additionally, factors such as the viscosity, particle size, and chemical stability of a drug formulation can affect its ability to penetrate the skin and maintain the stability of the drug during processing.[ 145 ] Figure 3i presents temporal pressure methodology to enhance the penetration of drugs from small molecules to protein and nanoparticles by micropores (≈3 µm2) creation.[ 49 ] A 1 min treatment of 0.28 MPa was optimized for efficient delivery and allowed nanoparticles (up to 500 nm) and macromolecules (up to 20 kDa) to reach a depth of 430 µm into the dermal layer. Furthermore, the successful delivery of insulin into vascular circulation through topical application after the pressure treatment resulted in a significant decrease in blood glucose levels in diabetic mice.

Strategies for Transdermal Biosensing

In traditional medical services, blood has been the primary source of biomarkers used to assess an individual's health status. However, the collection of blood samples through methods like venipuncture, fingerstick, or arterial sampling often causes discomfort and involves complex procedures and specialized instruments for processing, resulting in time‐consuming and costly services. To overcome the limitations associated with blood‐based diagnosis, interstitial fluid (ISF) serves as an ideal site for transdermal biosensing of biomarkers as it consists of substances that leak out of blood capillaries and are known to contain biomarkers comparable to those found in blood (93.3% of protein, 79.3% of metabolites).[ 146 , 147 , 148 ] The field of transdermal biosensing encompasses three main aspects, sampling, delivery of diagnostic agents, and self‐sensing using microneedles.[ 149 , 150 , 151 , 152 , 153 , 154 , 155 ] In this context, reverse iontophoresis and microneedle technologies have emerged as primary methods for transdermal biosensing.

ISF Sampling

Both microneedles and reverse iontophoresis can be utilized for the extraction of skin ISF. Three types of microneedles, swellable, porous, and hollow forms enable external extraction of skin ISF for biomarker sampling.[ 149 , 156 , 157 , 158 ] The extracted ISF can be analyzed either using external devices or through devices attached to the microneedles.[ 159 , 160 ]

Figure 4a shows a hydrogel‐based swellable microneedle patch that effectively extracted skin ISF in a minimally invasive manner.[ 156 ] The patch utilized osmotic pressure, driven by maltose osmolytes, to enhance the diffusion of ISF into the methacrylate hyaluronic acid hydrogel matrix. The approach allowed for three times faster extraction compared to existing platforms, with the ability to collect 7.90 µL of ISF from pig skin ex vivo and 3.82 µL of ISF from mouse skin in vivo within just 3 min. The extracted biomarkers like glucose and insulin, were quantified using glucose quantification assay kits and insulin ELISA kits. The concentrations of these biomarkers were found to be correlated well with measurements obtained from tail vein blood samples in mice models. While this study demonstrates the efficacy of using swellable microneedles for rapid ISF extraction, it also presents certain limitations. The primary drawback is the need to centrifuge the swollen hydrogel to separate the ISF, which adds a layer of complexity and limits real‐time application. Although the study showed integration with glucose sensors without centrifugation, it is anticipated that the quantity of ISF extracted would be lower. This poses a challenge for continuous and non‐invasive monitoring systems. Therefore, future research directions for swellable hydrogel microneedles should focus on developing self‐separating hydrogels that can automatically expel ISF. Additionally, incorporating active transport mechanisms, such as electrically or magnetically driven pumps, can actively move ISF to the sensor, significantly enhancing efficiency and reducing reliance on passive diffusion. Swellable microneedles are increasingly being integrated with electrochemical sensors for immediate sensing of various biomarkers, leading to the development of flexible epidermal sensors. Recent studies, such as one involving a flexible microneedle patch coupled with an electrode array, have demonstrated in situ multiplexed detection of ions like Na+, K+, Ca2+, and pH in interstitial fluid, showcasing the potential for personalized health monitoring.[ 161 ] Also, recent research has combined swellable microneedles with colorimetric sensors for easy‐to‐operate, device‐free biomarker detection.[ 162 ] This technology holds the potential for precise biomarker detection without the need for complex devices, enhancing management strategies for chronic diseases like type 2 diabetes.

Porous microneedles can be composed of metals or ceramics sintered at high temperatures or formed at lower temperatures by incorporating pore‐forming agents like polyethylene glycol (PEG),[ 50 ] polyvinyl alcohol (PVA)[ 163 ] and water‐soluble salts,[ 164 ] also facilitate the sampling of ISF. The internal porous structure forms many continuous capillary channel networks, allowing porous microneedles to extract a substantial amount of ISF from the skin layer through capillary action. Figure 4b presents the aptamer‐decorated porous microneedle arrays of which the porous arrays are fabricated by replicating the negative molds comprising glass microspheres with a UV‐curable ethoxylated trimethylolpropane triacrylate (ETPTA).[ 165 ] By integrating the advantages of a porous structure and aptamers, the porous microneedle arrays exhibited a remarkable increase in specific surface area, reaching 6.694 m2 g−1. Consequently, a large quantity of stable aptamer probes, with a concentration of 0.9459 µM, can be effectively immobilized onto the microneedle arrays. The reported system enabled a highly sensitive and rapid detection of ISF endotoxin using a fluorometer in the concentration range of 0.0342 to 8.2082 EU mL−1, with a low limit of detection of 0.0064 EU mL−1. However, the porous microneedle arrays may face limitations due to the potentially weak extraction force generated by capillary action, which could hinder continuous use. Additionally, the biocompatibility of ETPTA has not been thoroughly validated. Addressing these issues will be crucial for the successful long‐term application and commercialization of the technology. In addition, hollow microneedles are also employed for rapid ISF extraction by capillary action owing to their structure or by negative pressure‐driven force in combination with the vacuum chamber.[ 147 , 166 , 167 ]

Reverse iontophoresis is a widely used method for extracting ISF from the skin by applying an electric field. It involves the placement of iontophoretic electrodes, anode and cathode, on the skin's surface which is typically separated by a hydrogel membrane.[ 31 , 32 ] When an electric field is applied (≈0.2–0.5 mA cm−2), it initiates an electroosmotic flow within the skin, facilitating the transport of ISF biomarkers toward the electrodes. Anions migrate toward the anode, while cations and neutral molecules move toward the cathode. As a result, biomarkers accumulate in the cathode as net electroosmotic flow is established toward the cathode. This process enables the non‐invasive extraction of biomarkers using reverse iontophoresis. Figure 4c reports a skin‐like glucose biosensor system that achieved an increase in ISF osmotic pressure by the repulsion of hyaluronic acid into the ISF under the paper battery anode.[ 168 ] This process promoted intravascular blood glucose refiltration and reduced reabsorption, as a result, driving glucose out of the vessel more efficiently. After the ISF extraction with paper battery, the ultrathin (≈3 mm) biosensor with multilayered sand dune nanostructures which exhibit a high sensitivity of 130.4 µA mM−1 was attached to the cathode‐contacting area for skin surface glucose measurement. The noninvasive measurement results showed a high correlation over 0.9 with clinically measured blood glucose levels. However, a drawback of this approach is the requirement for two separate steps, attaching the paper battery for hyaluronic acid delivery and ISF extraction, followed by attaching the biosensor. This dual‐step process may induce inconvenience, highlighting the need for developing a unified system integrating extraction and sensor functionalities to streamline the measurement process and enhance user convenience.[ 169 ] Among the factors influencing reverse iontophoresis, pH changes significantly affect the zeta potential, thereby altering the extracted glucose flux. A study addressing these aspects suggests the need for a system that can minimize pH variations to enhance the accuracy of continuous glucose monitoring.[ 170 ] Figure 4d shows the epidermal bio‐microfluidics technique based on flexible electronics for continuous blood glucose monitoring, ensuring more reliable measurements.[ 171 ] The system utilized a thermal activation method with a temperature of 37 °C to open hair pores and activate sweat glands, reducing extraction current density and time while improving the efficiency of ISF sampling. This method minimized skin irritation caused by the extraction process. For the temperature control component, gold wires were fabricated on the pretreated flexible polyimide film by inkjet printing. Additionally, the incorporation of the Na+ sensor and correction model played a vital role in accounting for individual variations, as they monitor Na+ concentration in the extracted ISF using a differential electrochemical sensor and reduce the possibility of inaccuracy due to the fluctuations in the amount of ISF extraction.

Delivery of Diagnostic Agents

In addition to their use in ISF sampling, microneedles can also serve as effective delivery tools for diagnostic agents, such as fluorescent or colorimetric probes, into the dermal layer of the skin. Figure 4e deals with the use of microneedles for delivering diagnostic agents into the skin's dermal layer specifically for lymphatic imaging.[ 172 ] By delivering indocyanine green (ICG) using microneedles, the lymphatic vessels in the dermal layer could be visualized and their clearance kinetics was determined using a near‐infrared camera. This painless procedure quantitively evaluated dermal lymphatic function in various conditions and assessed the effectiveness of treatments. The combination of poly(N‐vinylpyrrolidone) and ICG constituted microneedles and offered potential for rapid clinical translation and significant impact on preclinical lymphatic studies. Figure 4f shows a microneedle patch developed to create a colorimetric dermal tattoo biosensor for the multiplexed detection of health‐related biomarkers.[ 173 ] The microneedle was fabricated as a four‐area segmented structure that undergoes color changes in response to variations in biomarker concentrations, including pH, glucose, uric acid, and temperature. These color changes could be easily observed by the naked eye or captured by a camera for semi‐quantitative measurement. This colorimetric dermal tattoo biosensor demonstrates the ability to simultaneously detect multiple biomarkers in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo experiments and monitor biomarker concentration changes continuously for at least 4 days. However, colorimetric sensors may have limitations in sensitivity and accuracy, as small changes in biomarker concentrations might not produce noticeable color changes. Environmental factors, like variations in lighting and temperature, can affect the perceived color change, leading to inaccuracies. Future directions include improving dye stability, enhancing sensitivity and specificity, and developing biocompatible materials to minimize skin reactions.

Microneedle‐Mediated Sensor

The versatility of microneedles is highlighted not only in diagnostic agent delivery but also in direct sensing applications. Microneedle itself can be modified as a sensor for ISF biomarker detection. The microneedle‐mediated sensor usually undergoes surface modification for antibody‐antigen interaction, enzyme‐protein reaction, aptamer recognition, and nonenzymatic electrochemistry.[ 174 , 175 , 176 , 177 ] Microneedle sensor arrays are composed of reference, counter, and working electrodes to convert external biological signal changes into electric signals. As shown in Figure 4g microneedle biosensor toward rapid screening of skin melanoma was reported.[ 178 ] The 3 × 3 hollow microneedle arrays made of photocurable acrylate polymer material were filled with catechol‐coated carbon paste for assessing tissue tyrosinase (TYR) enzyme cancer biomarkers. In the presence of TYR, immobilized catechol underwent oxidation to form benzoquinone, allowing for amperometric measurement of the generated quinone. The wearable sensor was connected to a lightweight flexible electronic board, enabling wireless transmission of data to a mobile device. The performance of the sensing system was assessed using TYR‐containing agarose phantom gel and porcine skin. Figure 4h presents a high‐density silicon microneedle (≈ 9500 microneedles cm−2) preparing a three‐electrode patch for the electrochemical monitoring of glucose.[ 155 ] The working electrode was made of gold‐coated silicon microneedle arrays which were functionalized with ferrocene‐polyamidoamine and glucose oxidase as the biological recognition element after assembling of thiol groups on the gold surface. During the enzymatic reaction catalyzed by glucose oxidase, the presence of glucose led to a change in the current that is directly proportional to the glucose concentration. When evaluated in artificial ISF, the microneedle array glucose patch exhibited excellent selectivity, demonstrating a sensitivity of 0.1622 µA mM−1 cm− 2 and a detection limit of 0.66 mM. Another fabrication method, using digital light processing, has led to the development of wearable microneedle sensors. These sensors employ enzymatic reactions on gold‐coated electrodes to reliably and selectively detect β‐hydroxybutyrate, integrated with wireless analyzers for real‐time tracking, advancing medical and health monitoring applications.[ 179 ]

In general, the two types of techniques were applied simultaneously or sequentially to increase the depth at which the drug was delivered.[ 39 , 50 , 182 ] An integrated 3D printed ultrasonic microneedle array device consisting of hollow microneedles and an ultrasonic transducer has been proposed to overcome the limitation of microneedles inability to provide an additional driving force for drug transport in tissues (Figure 5a).[ 39 ] Hollow microneedles offer a means for drugs to penetrate the stratum corneum by creating channels in the skin, while the application of ultrasound through pores in the microneedles facilitates drug transport within the tissue. The results showed a two‐ to five‐fold increase in drug delivery in 2 h period when both techniques were used simultaneously compared to when either technique was used alone. Transdermal patches combining microneedle array and iontophoresis have been attracting attention for a long time, but controlled delivery of liquid macromolecules and easy self‐administration have been difficult challenges.[ 183 , 184 , 185 ] To address this, an iontophoresis‐driven porous microneedle array patch for the active delivery of charged macromolecular drugs was developed by integrating porous microneedle and iontophoresis into a single transdermal patch (Figure 5b).[ 50 ] Charged nanovesicles stored in the porous microneedle array were diffused and driven by iontophoresis through a constant current of 1–2 mA for 6–12 h. As a result, synergistic improvement in the released drug dose was achieved by 1.3 and 1.6 times at 1 and 2 mA current, respectively, compared to the porous microneedle only. In vitro and in vivo studies showed ≈99% skin penetration rate of microneedle, negligible cytotoxicity, excellent biocompatibility without skin irritation, and remarkable drug delivery efficiency was also demonstrated through animal experiments. It possessed higher controllability and efficiency compared to the single injection method through type 1 diabetic rat models and insulin. As a result, a powerful hypoglycemic effect was observed as they maintained normal blood glucose levels for ≈5.4 h. In brief, a platform for the charged liquid drug delivery in a controlled activity manner with advanced performance has been developed by combining microneedle and iontophoresis.

In order to overcome the limitations of the microneedle patch, where the variation in skin elasticity is large, the needles cannot be inserted depending on the amount of hair on the skin, and the molecules are not completely delivered, a study combining microneedles and jet injection has also been conducted.[ 186 ] Among them, “Microlancer”, a patchless, self‐administered micropillar‐based delivery system has been reported (Figure 5c).[ 51 ] It is capable of achieving enhanced delivery efficiency of a loaded drug within a second regardless of skin type or amount of hair while providing precise and consistent control of insertion depth. Only 46% of the microneedles penetrated porcine skin, whereas Microlancer reached 97%. Additionally, while only the microneedle patch did not achieve full insertion even when applied with a force of 16.4 N/array, Microlancer has worked effectively on a variety of skin types with complete insertion on all skin types. In vivo tests, insulin injected into diabetic rats through Microlancer rapidly lowered plasma glucose levels and showed therapeutic efficacy compatible with subcutaneous injection. Overall, Microlancer offers the possibility of being able to inject proteins, vaccines, or other biomolecules regardless of drug type, as well as deliver precisely constant amounts to targeted depths in a minimally invasive way. The system combining microneedle and jet injection can be expected to have an impact on the future of vaccine and drug delivery, which must provide optimal effects and patient convenience.

Studies have also been conducted to determine whether drug is more effectively delivered by increasing the mobility of the drug itself after destroying the barrier by removing the stratum corneum.[ 187 ] For example, the enhancement of the penetration ability of molecules with both low and high molecular weight through the combination of fractional laser ablation and iontophoretic transfer was evaluated (Figure 5d).[ 52 ] In this study, tape stripping which is a non‐selective technique, and epidermal laser ablation, which selectively removes a small part of the skin surface area, were used as approaches to physically remove the stratum corneum, and a series of microchannels directly connected to the epidermis were created. In addition, a constant current of 0.5 mA was passed for 6 h using Ag/AgCl electrodes and a power supply under iontophoresis conditions. The study examined the impact of surface damage area and the influence of mass or localized removal of the outermost layer of the skin on the iontophoretic transportation of each substance, demonstrating the superior permeability coefficient for small molecules (lidocaine>ketorolac>acetaminophen) with electromigration compared to electroosmosis. It was also concluded that delivery is beneficial when the ablation technique enhances passive penetration to compensate for the reduction in electron transport and the reduced electroosmosis favors cathodic transport. However, in some cases, the combination of the two technologies selectively produces a synergistic effect depending on drug characteristics.[ 182 ] It has been investigated whether more effective delivery was possible when both electroporation and iontophoresis are applied using the hydrophilic drug atenolol and the lipophilic drug timolol (Figure 5e).[ 182 ] The amount of drug penetrating the human stratum corneum was validated by applying 10 pulses of 400 V at 10 ms intervals in the electroporation, 0.25–0.5 mA for 3–9 h in iontophoresis. The outcome of the study revealed that the accumulation of the lipophilic cation timolol in the stratum corneum confirmed a decrease in electroosmosis, consequently reducing the electroporation‐based iontophoretic transport of the drug. In contrast, the iontophoretic transport of atenolol was significantly increased by electroporation since electroosmosis was not affected by atenolol. As a result, it has been demonstrated that the hydrophobicity and positive charge of drugs affect the electronic transport of drugs, leading to the conclusion that the physicochemical properties of drugs are essential in optimizing transdermal drug delivery through the combination of electroporation and iontophoresis techniques.

Moreover, a multifunctional system combining three technologies has recently emerged.[ 40 ] Figure 5f presents a high‐performance drug delivery system by integrating ultrasound, electric field, and microneedles for effective transdermal delivery as rapid local delivery.[ 40 ] Ultrasound induced the dissolution of hyaluronic microneedles through the vibration of acoustic pressure, while iontophoresis utilized the principle of enhancing electrostatic‐driven diffusion of rhodamine B as a cationic model drug. The drug release flux was analyzed with a gelatin hydrogel, and the frequency and voltage dependency of ion‐induced delivery were experimentally evaluated. Accordingly, the microneedle‐sonophoresis‐iontophoresis system demonstrated a shorter initial delivery time compared to delivery systems based solely on passive or single‐technology, thereby enhancing the efficacy of microneedles for drug penetration. However, to date, the increase in delivery efficiency has only been confirmed by the simple convergence of transport technologies, and there is no case of integration into a single system. In order to be used as an effective practical transdermal drug delivery system, both the ultrasonic transducer and the electrical source need to evolve into a patch without the need for separate bulky equipment. Integration with a transducer‐free sonophoresis patch or film‐type iontophoresis patch could potentially achieve enhanced drug delivery efficiency through multi‐stimuli within a unified system.[ 46 , 48 ]

Biosensing

ISF sampling methods for transdermal biosensing, including reverse iontophoresis and microneedle have been developed for biomarker extraction as previously described. Recently, a touch‐actuated glucose sensor that sequentially utilizes microneedles and reverse iontophoresis has been proposed (Figure 6b).[ 56 ] The sensor is composed of a solid microneedle array, a porous elastomer ring, flexible reverse iontophoresis on a printed circuit board, an impermeable ring, and agarose gel containing glucose oxidase. Upon compression of the sensor with a finger, the solid microneedle array effectively penetrated the agarose gel and the stratum corneum layer, resulting in the formation of temporary aqueous microchannels within the skin. Subsequently, the reverse iontophoresis extraction process took place, extracting glucose from the ISF into the agarose gel containing glucose oxidase. Finally, the electrochemical detection process quantified the glucose concentration in the agarose gel through chronoamperometry. This skin penetration‐reverse iontophoresis sampling strategy demonstrated a significant increase in glucose extraction flux of ≈1.6 times compared to reverse iontophoretic extraction alone, both in vitro and in vivo.

Research has also been conducted to enhance the extraction performance by improving the electroosmotic flow (Figure 6c).[ 193 ] The system features a solid polymer‐based ion‐conductive porous microneedle array patch which is fabricated by immobilizing negatively charged poly(2‐acrylamido‐2‐methylpropanesulfonic acid) (AMPS) on porous polyglycidyl methacrylate particles. Three essential functions of the ion‐conductive porous microneedle for iontophoresis applications included stabilizing skin resistance by partially breaking stratum corneum, facilitating molecular permeability through interconnected micropores, and generating significant electroosmotic flow by charge modification within the micropores. The microneedle used consisted of aggregated particles of ≈0.5 µm size, creating interconnecting pores of ≈1 µm size, which were sufficiently large to facilitate the transport of larger drugs including insulin (≈6000 Da) and vaccines. The glucose extraction from pig skin conducted using the 1.5 M AMPS‐modified porous microneedle validated its effectiveness as a practical sampling system. Consequently, these advancements in transdermal biosensing, combining reverse iontophoresis and microneedle technologies allow for efficient biomarker extraction and detection for continuous monitoring of ISF biomarkers, overcoming the limitations associated with individual variations.[ 56 , 192 , 193 ]

Theragnostics

The integration of diagnostics and therapeutics within a single platform, termed theragnostics, has emerged as a pioneering approach in transdermal healthcare systems.[ 194 , 195 , 196 , 197 ] These autonomous systems allow for real‐time monitoring of physiological parameters along with the administration of therapeutic agents enabling immediate treatment with timely care.[ 68 ] The instant feedback also allows for delicate treatments when precise doses of medication are required.[ 198 ] By streamlining treatment procedures and minimizing intervention, theragnostic systems enhance patient comfort and treatment efficacy.[ 68 , 199 ] The seamless combination of diagnostic sensors with drug delivery mechanisms enables personalized and adaptive therapies, revolutionizing patient care.[ 200 , 201 , 202 ] In particular, there has been a lot of research into facilitating theragnostics in transdermal healthcare devices.[ 41 , 42 , 54 , 55 , 201 ] Beyond the single‐time sensing and drug delivery patch initially developed,[ 42 ] healthcare devices that check various biomarkers at once with health conditions for precision diagnosis have been developed.[ 54 , 55 ] Moreover, with the development of the closed‐loop system for real‐time blood glucose control with insulin secretion,[ 41 ] transdermal devices that maximize patient convenience for diseases that require long‐term continuous monitoring, such as diabetes, are gaining attention as the direction in which transdermal theragnostic devices should be in advance.[ 42 , 54 , 55 , 200 , 201 ]

For transdermal theragnostic devices, microneedles are essential for effective ISF extraction with drug delivery.[ 64 , 65 ] Figure 7a shows a microneedle set consisting of glucose‐sensing and insulin‐delivery parts, that can release insulin according to blood glucose levels determined through colorimetric detection.[ 42 ] Each microneedle patch has microneedles of 860 µm base diameter and 950 µm height arranged in a 10 × 10 array. The sensing part detects abnormal glucose levels through interstitial fluid color changes, quantified using a smartphone. The sensing microneedle made of methacrylated gelatin, 3,3′,5,5′‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB), and glucose oxidase‐conjugated MnO2/graphene oxide nanozymes (GOx‐MnO2@GO) could extract ≈5.1 µL skin ISF within 3 min by hydro‐affinity of the gel and directly process biosensing. The glucose in the extracted ISF and GOx react to produce gluconic acid and H2O2, and the generated H2O2 combines with MnO2 to promote the oxidation of TMB, causing a color change from colorless to blue. The drug delivery system features branched poly(β‐amino ester) (bPAE) nanovesicles that encapsulate insulin. Similar to the sensing patch mechanism, ISF flows into the needle triggering glucose interaction with the bPAE nanovesicles. This interaction causes internal protonation of the bPAE leading to the breakdown of nanovesicles and subsequent insulin release. In vivo experiments demonstrated the effectiveness of the system in blood glucose regulation maintaining normal levels for up to 13 h and reducing the risk of hypoglycemia. While the disposable microneedle patch capable of both diagnosis and treatment has been developed, diseases such as diabetes and hypertension require continuous monitoring rather than one‐time diagnostics. Research is actively focusing on systems that allow real‐time monitoring throughout the duration of patch application, without the need for periodic reapplication for diagnosis.[ 41 , 193 ] Efforts are also underway to miniaturize sensor and circuit systems for integration into a single patch, accelerating the development toward an autonomous transdermal theragnostic system.[ 138 , 203 ]

A microneedle patch, featuring advanced dual‐sensing capabilities for biomarkers associated with inflammation and motion, has been developed to enhance diagnostic sensitivity.[ 54 ] Additionally, this patch enables more precise drug release, responding specifically to local temperature elevations linked to the disease, as illustrated in Figure 7b.[ 54 ] The biochemical sensor in this microneedle dressing quantifies the concentration of biomarkers such as lactate and calprotectin, which are increased due to the inflammatory response in chronic wounds. To bring high sensitivity, the fluorescence‐labeled antibodies against each biomarker were coated, and the concentration of the biomarkers was measured through fluorescence intensity. Additionally, the motion sensing part was integrated for monitoring the movement of the wounded area to minimize strenuous stretching and aid in a faster healing process. A Ti3C2Tx MXene‐based electrical circuit with metallic conductivity, high strength, and high stability was printed on the microneedle patch, and the relative resistance change was used to track the movement of the wound. The temperature‐responsive hydrogel achieved a controlled release of medications. Finally, in vivo experiments demonstrated the remarkable ability to expedite wound recovery utilizing diabetic mice. These findings highlight the promising potential of the theragnostic microneedle patch by simultaneous sensing of two disease‐related factors to increase diagnostic sensitivity, as well as suggesting improved treatment efficiency through precise drug release.

Multi‐factor monitoring is a key feature of the newly developed microneedle device,[ 66 , 67 , 204 ] which extends its use to daily healthcare with chronic, homeostasis‐related diabetes management.[ 55 ] This device allows for the real‐time tracking of various essential health factors from sweat, such as temperature, humidity, pH, and glucose levels (Figure 7c).[ 55 ] These advances in complexity and miniaturization using nanomaterials highlight their practical utility as routine medical devices and offer simplicity for patients with chronic diseases. Biochemical sensors fabricated from graphene with solid Ag/AgCl counter electrodes to monitor the multi‐factors exhibit heightened electrochemical activity, sensitivity, and selectivity when identifying crucial biomarkers within human sweat. Graphene‐hybrid interconnections and physical sensors efficiently transmit signals via a stretchable array and electrochemical sensors. The closed‐loop, point‐of‐care approach for diabetes management is achieved by orchestrating the monitoring of biomarkers and physiological cues alongside sweat monitoring and transcutaneous drug delivery. Detection of elevated humidity due to sweat triggers glucose sensing, rectified by simultaneous pH and temperature measurements. Then elevated glucose levels prompt embedded heaters to dissolve phase‐change materials, resulting in bioresorbable microneedles releasing Metformin for feedback‐driven transdermal drug delivery in glucose sensing.

Figure 7d proposes the fully closed‐loop theragnostic patch.[ 41 ] This on‐the‐spot diabetic sensing and treatment system consists of three components, a mesoporous microneedle‐reverse iontophoretic glucose sensor, a microneedle‐iontophoretic insulin delivery unit, and a flexible printed circuit board (FPCB) module serving as an integration hub including recording, signal processing, and circuitry for closed‐loop management. Porous microneedles painlessly penetrate the skin, enhancing glucose extraction and insulin delivery by iontophoresis with electrical regulation. The closed‐loop circuits within the FPCB module comprised two constant current sources, one to facilitate ISF extraction for sensing and the other to deliver the drug, with a 3.7 V lithium‐ion polymer battery as a power supply. These analog current signals, with a resolution of 1 nA, were converted to digital data by a 12‐bit microprogrammed control unit. For wireless transmission of the data, the Bluetooth chip CC2640R2F was used, which has a working distance of ≈100 m and was suitable for integration into wearable devices due to its high transmission rate and low power consumption. The transmitted blood glucose level data was finally filtered in the app by a Butterworth low‐pass filter with a cut‐off frequency of 1 Hz to remove noise and allow it to be displayed on the interface in real‐time. This demonstrated a fully integrated hardware and software system for diabetes management with a smartphone app. The system is successfully shown to accurately track variations in glucose levels and promptly deliver insulin as a response to elevated blood glucose levels in a diabetic rat model. Through the integration of the Internet of Things (IoT), the system not only facilitates real‐time monitoring and management of glucose levels but also allows for remote data access and control, significantly enhancing the overall efficiency and convenience of diabetes management for patients and healthcare providers.

Advanced Platform Technologies in Transdermal Healthcare System

In recent years, there has been a notable surge in research and the subsequent commercialization of medical devices that leverage transdermal transport enhancement technologies.[ 117 , 205 , 206 , 207 ] Transdermal healthcare electronics are developing for patient‐centered care in response to the escalating demand for convenience and simplicity.[ 2 , 208 , 209 , 210 ] Autonomous systems,[ 41 ] thin and lightweight patches capable of conformal contact,[ 46 , 211 , 212 , 213 ] and the development of new power sources to operate independently without external connection are evolving for patient convenience and efficiency improvement.[ 48 , 57 ] Furthermore, the use of biodegradable materials not only secures user safety but also promotes environmental development without electronic waste (e‐waste).[ 214 , 215 , 216 ] This part presents a comprehensive overview of these progresses and introduces the latest research, highlighting the system development with the utilization of wireless power sources and energy harvesting batteries, and biodegradability in terms of materials advancement.

Conformable Energy Harvesting Power Sources

Efficient power sources of transdermal healthcare devices play a pivotal role in ensuring the operation of the devices without imposing maintenance burdens. Previously, transdermal healthcare devices often utilized non‐rechargeable primary batteries for short‐term or one‐time operation of electric components.[ 217 ] Recently, rechargeable secondary batteries are generally used with the growing importance of long‐term drug delivery and health monitoring for health management.[ 50 , 56 , 218 ] However, rechargeable batteries represented by lithium‐ion batteries as a conventional power source not only possess limited battery capacity and require frequent recharging but also introduce safety concerns such as battery explosions and environmental pollution. Therefore, incorporating energy‐harvesting power sources into the system, such as wireless power sources capable of harvesting energy from external transmissions with the added advantage of external control for drug release or biomarker sensing, and self‐powered energy sources that can generate power without the need for external energy input, allows for the creation of user‐friendly and sustainable devices.[ 57 , 111 , 138 , 219 , 220 , 221 , 222 , 223 , 224 , 225 , 226 , 227 , 228 , 229 , 230 ]

Wireless power sources enable a continuous supply of power to transdermal devices by utilizing principles of electromagnetic resonance and radio frequency energy transfer. Close‐range power transfer is feasible through magnetic fields, while long‐distance power transfer can be achieved via electromagnetic waves. Moreover, they obviate the inconvenience of exposing the skin to frequent physical interfaces for power supply, addressing concerns related to skin irritation and infection. Figure 8a presents a skin‐interfaced drug delivery system utilizing electrically triggered, gated microneedles for on‐demand drug delivery powered by more stabilized NFC power transfer.[ 138 ] The system involves a thin gold layer (≈150 nm) coating on microneedles to encapsulate and protect drugs during standby. Applying a small electrical trigger (≈2.5 V, DC) for 30 s effectively disintegrates the gold coating, exposing the drug to initiation of delivery. The external power source, a signal generator, is linked to an inductive coil to deliver high‐frequency (≈MHz level) alternative current (AC), facilitating wireless power transfer through magnetic resonance coupling. The AC is converted to direct current using a full‐bridge rectifier, and a subsequent 2.5 V regulator ensures stable potential, coupled with a 0.1 µF capacitor to constitute a low‐pass filter, enhancing output stability. The final output power signal stabilizes at ≈2.5 V with a standard deviation of ≈0.1 V with an optimal signal input of ≈15 V peak‐to‐peak at 15 MHz, ensuring precise crevice corrosion control for initiating drug release. The system demonstrates its distinctive capabilities, featuring precise control over drug release in ultrafine spatial areas (< 1 mm2) from individual microneedles, effective real‐time management with high temporal resolution (< 30 s) of drug release, wireless operation, and comfortable wearability. However, wireless energy harvesting systems powered by NFC could suffer from short operation distances of up to ≈10 cm and the need for an external power source.

As an alternative, incorporating self‐powered energy sources facilitates the operation of the device through the natural body processes or external stimuli enhancing healthcare system sustainability. These self‐powering methods establish a continuous, self‐sustaining power supply, eliminating the need for frequent battery replacements or external power supplements, which have limitations in terms of distance. Furthermore, devices powered by themselves enhance the mobility of the patients by eliminating extra battery burdens or external power sources for power supply. Currently, glucose is predominantly used as a substrate for biofuel cells.[ 231 , 232 ] Figure 8b shows the integration of a self‐powered biofuel cell (BFC) into a glucose‐sensing microneedle array, offering a minimally invasive approach for subdermal glucose monitoring.[ 57 ] Glucose oxidation generates electrons and protons in a biofuel cell, which then move through an external circuit to produce electrical energy. Simultaneously, protons migrate to the cathode, where oxygen reduction occurs, completing the electrochemical reaction by combining with electrons to form water. The microneedle array combines a carbon paste/glucose oxidase/tetrathiafulvalene bioanode and a carbon paste/Pt black cathode with a total area of 0.006 cm2 for on‐body power generation from the transdermal fluid of the wearer. The system exhibits well‐defined power signals proportional to glucose concentrations, with power densities ranging from 3 to 5 µW cm−2 over the normal 5–10 mM range and a maximum response of ≈7 µW cm−2 at 25 mM glucose. Further improvements involve a metalized‐carbon‐fiber BFC microneedle configuration, achieving high power densities up to 200 µW cm−2 and expanding the upper glucose concentration range. The system showcases the advantage of continuous self‐energy supply utilizing glucose as an internal energy source, enabling sustained monitoring of the skin without the need for external power sources. Despite these advantages, the short lifespan of the biological components, such as glucose oxidase, and stability issues due to environmental variations and biofuel degradation over time, can pose challenges.[ 233 ] These factors can lead to reduced efficiency and frequent replacements, necessitating further advancements in enzyme stabilization and biofuel cell durability for long‐term use.

Figure 8c shows an iontophoretic drug delivery system that is self‐powered by TENG.[ 111 ] The operational principle of the TENG revolves around the generation of charges on the surfaces of two distinct materials with varying electron affinities and work functions. This phenomenon arises through the triboelectric effect and electrostatic induction, activated as these materials make contact and then separate. The outcome of this process yields a potential difference and subsequent flow of current, which holds the potential for energizing electronic devices or charging energy storage components. The proposed system consists of a wearable TENG made of polytetrafluoroethylene (10 cm × 10 cm × 50 µm) and aluminum (10 cm × 10 cm × 100 µm), which serves as the motion sensor and energy harvester, and a hydrogel‐based soft patch with side‐by‐side electrodes that enables noninvasive iontophoretic transdermal drug delivery. The developed pain‐relief transdermal drug delivery system for ankle injuries enables on‐demand drug release triggered by walking, harnessing electricity generated during foot movement. The insole TENG exhibits electrical characteristics with an open‐circuit voltage of 1200 V, short‐circuit charge transfer of 370 nC per cycle, and a maximum short‐circuit current of ≈20 µA at a 2 Hz operation frequency. TENG could convert body motions including both involuntary motions such as breathing and tremor, and intentional motions such as arm bending and walking into electricity that is used to power the iontophoresis process. Further development could involve optimizing TENG efficiency, refining wearable integration, and exploring its potential applications in personalized healthcare and portable medical devices.

Biodegradable Devices

The integration of biodegradable materials in transdermal healthcare devices not only enhances eco‐friendliness and patient safety but also synergizes with the development of sustainable power sources, thereby advancing the healthcare field toward environmentally conscious and patient‐centric healthcare solutions. Advances in materials have led to the development of biodegradable components for transdermal healthcare systems.[ 53 , 234 ] Initially, microneedles used for medical purposes consisted of hard materials, such as metals and ceramics for skin penetration.[ 90 , 99 , 101 ] However, the direction of using biocompatible polymers or freeze‐dried hydrogels has been developed due to side effects of residual needle parts in the body or skin inflammation.[ 102 , 104 , 109 ] In particular, biodegradable materials have gained attraction as they are infection‐free and eliminate the need for device detachment for convenience.[ 235 , 236 ] Biodegradable materials, hydrolyzed by moisture in body fluids, yield non‐toxic byproducts like carbon dioxide, nitrogen, and inorganic salts.[ 237 , 238 ] Beyond the microneedle patches, biodegradable transdermal medical devices minimize waste generation and the environmental impact, promoting safety, convenience, and eco‐friendliness by naturally degrading after intended use.[ 43 , 58 , 59 , 60 ] The integration of biodegradable materials in transdermal patches underscores the commitment toward eco‐friendly and patient‐centric healthcare solutions.[ 43 , 58 , 59 , 60 ]

Figure 9a presents a biodegradable microneedle patch for sustained drug release safely for a month after the drug‐loaded needles are inserted into the skin.[ 58 ] A microneedle patch that incorporates biodegradable needles, consisting of polylactic acid (PLA) and polylactic‐co‐glycolic acid (PLGA) was developed. The PLA or PLGA used in this study were confirmed to be suitable for microneedle materials because they have a modulus of several GPa that do not cause significant skin irritation but can tolerate > 0.15 N required to puncture the skin. Specifically, this microneedle patch features an air bubble between the microneedle and the back of the patch, and it allows for swift and straightforward separation of the microneedles from the backing upon insertion into the skin. The detached microneedles then undergo gradual biodegradation within the skin, facilitating the sustained release and systemic delivery of encapsulated levonorgestrel contraception for over a month. The microneedle patch, in rat experiments, was well tolerated with minimal visible traces and effectively maintained hormone levels in the bloodstream above the therapeutic range throughout the month. Continued enhancement of this rapidly detachable biodegradable microneedle patch could lead to its transformation into a drug delivery platform extending to self‐administered and long‐lasting drug release beyond hormones. The strategy of utilizing biodegradable materials has also been established not only for medication but also for microneedle sensors.

Figure 9b shows a biodegradable microneedle glucose sensor whose fluorescence intensity varies reversibly with the glucose level.[ 59 ] This allows for visual confirmation and continuous monitoring without the need for removal after use. In the needle, a glucose‐responsive fluorescent monomer (GF‐monomer) comprises two components: a diboronic acid moiety binding to glucose and an anthracene moiety emitting fluorescence. The interaction between boron and nitrogen intensifies the fluorescence emitted by anthracene as the glucose level rises due to the glucose‐diboronic acid‐binding. The glucose concentration was quantified by measuring the fluorescence intensity with the smartphone application. The needle layer of the patch, crafted from silk fibroin—a natural protein extracted from silkworm silk—exhibits high sensitivity owing to its hydrophilic properties. This enhances the probability of glucose contact in the ISF. Silk fibroin has ten times higher modulus than other biodegradable polymers, and a fracture force of 0.795 N, exhibiting sufficient mechanical properties for piercing the stratum corneum.[ 239 , 240 ] The key feature of the microneedle glucose sensor is its ability to be naturally absorbed by the body eliminating the need for device removal after usage for ≈40 days.[ 59 ] This study not only demonstrated the long‐term utility potential of this bioabsorbable device but also successfully verified the accurate detection of glucose levels based on changes in fluorescence intensity by implementing a tether‐free continuous glucose monitoring system. These characteristics significantly diminish the likelihood of infection and additionally, improve patient comfort and adherence positioning this system as a more favorable option for continuous glucose monitoring. As transdermal devices have evolved beyond simple patches that either contain drugs or sense ISF, additional modules have been added to improve delivery efficiency.[ 60 ]

The use of Mg, one of the biodegradable metals,[ 241 ] as an electrochemical electrode for iontophoretic microneedles was presented (Figure 9c).[ 60 ] Metal microneedles with modulus of around hundreds of GPa and high tensile strength of hundreds of MPa have been used in the past to ensure reliable skin penetration,[ 99 ] but have been replaced by other materials due to the inability of the drug capacity in the needle body and side effects on skin.[ 102 , 104 , 109 ] Mg is highlighted as a compelling choice for electrodes for iontophoresis and microneedle body for skin penetration, where the metallic properties of Mg allow for applying voltage to facilitate the diffusion of charged drug molecules without allergic reaction or infection. In addition, the applied voltage was optimized to 1.4 V because Mg electrode is degraded by generating hydrogen gas and magnesium hydroxide, and the higher voltage resulted in a more iontophoretic effect. This is because excessive hydrogen production can hinder the movement of drug molecules across the Mg microneedle surface and surrounding microchannels. Additionally, dye migration in a porcine skin model was examined to determine the optimal potential range that promotes enhanced transdermal drug delivery. The optimization of the drug delivery process requires precise control of the potential, and this study will serve as an opportunity to further expand the application of biodegradable metals to various electrochemical applications. This demonstrated the possibility of effective iontophoretic microneedle drug delivery by utilizing the needle material itself as electrodes, and all systems except the power supply are biodegradable, showing safety without infection issues.

Furthermore, biodegradable transdermal devices have been proposed to reduce e‐waste in response to growing environmental concerns.[ 214 , 215 , 216 ] Figure 9d shows a biocompatible and biodegradable electronic skin (E‐skin) patch rooted in flexible gelatin methacryloyl aerogel (FGA) without e‐waste.[ 43 ] FGA is synthesized into a highly porous structure at cryogenic temperature, characterized by flexibility, passive‐cooling effects, and ultra‐lightweight properties, which qualify for long‐term use. The FGA‐based E‐skin platform can monitor body temperature, hydration, and biopotentials, while simultaneously detecting glucose, lactate, and alcohol levels. The sensitivity of 2 mm glucose, 10 mM lactate, 10 µM uric acid, and 20 mM ethanol were each found to be selectively detected with amperometric responses within a single sensor. ECG signals were also recorded in high resolution and the temperature was reversibly changed in a few seconds by heating and cooling within the range of 30–60 °C. It adheres close to the skin, stimulating sweat excretion and extracting ISF through reverse iontophoresis for sensing. Also, human trials demonstrate its ability to detect dynamic responses to daily activities, such as food/alcohol intake and exercise, as well as predict abnormal vital sign changes early on. As gelatin methacrylolyl (GelMA) is well known to be a biodegradable material,[ 242 ] in‐vitro biodegradation test confirmed that bulk degradation and hydrolysis took place after 20 days incubation, with over 80% of the initial weight reduced after 30 days. The degradation period can be adjusted according to the thickness and concentration of GelMA, indicating that it can be controlled by usage. This research introduces an innovative materials approach for advanced next‐generation E‐skin platforms, overcoming environmental concerns of non‐recyclable electronic materials along with skin inflammation due to the limited water/gas permeability. The next generation of transdermal medical devices should therefore be fully biodegradable and biocompatible systems for long‐term use with effective transdermal delivery.

Summary and Outlook

This review highlights the physical technologies for transdermal delivery of biomolecules for theragnostics, a combination of technologies for increasing delivery efficiency, and the advanced latest research trends. First, the skin, consisting of the epidermis, dermis, and subcutaneous layers, provides a strong barrier to transdermal delivery due to the denseness and hydrophobic nature of the outer stratum corneum.[ 72 ] Overcoming the barrier is key to drug penetration into the target layer or to eliciting and sensing biomarkers present in body fluids such as ISF.[ 85 ] Various techniques have been developed to enhance transdermal molecular transport,[ 85 , 88 ] and we have focused on physical techniques.[ 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 ] In the following, representative physical techniques and examples to improve transdermal molecular delivery were introduced, classified into 1) bypassing the stratum corneum,[ 44 , 45 ] 2) stratum corneum modification,[ 46 , 47 ] and 3) drug mobility enhancement.[ 48 , 49 ] The mechanisms and characteristics of the strategies through different stimulus sources such as microneedle,[ 44 ] laser,[ 45 ] ultrasound,[ 46 ] electric field,[ 47 , 48 ] and pressure were investigated.[ 49 ] These techniques have been widely used in transdermal drug delivery and biosensing,[ 155 , 178 , 243 ] and furthermore, the trend of combining two or more stimuli to enhance molecular transport has been discussed.[ 39 , 40 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 182 ] Recently, the transdermal theragnostic system, which integrates diagnosis and treatment into a single platform, has also emerged.[ 41 , 42 , 54 , 55 ] The rapidly growing research and commercialization of medical devices using transdermal delivery enhancement technologies is driving a variety of research and development efforts. Advances include utilizing energy harvesting power sources for autonomous operation,[ 48 , 244 ] and the use of biodegradable materials, ensuring user safety and environmental sustainability.[ 43 , 58 , 59 , 60 ] These all aim to provide patient‐centered care with an emphasis on convenience and simplicity.

Transdermal medical patches have been actively developed,[ 2 , 68 , 207 , 208 ] but extensive further research is crucial for their practical applications. Transdermal biosensors integrated into biomarker transport systems require high sensitivity, accuracy, and the capability for continuous, user‐operated monitoring. To meet these demands, 5th‐generation technologies such as AI, 5G, and nanotechnology are increasingly being applied.[ 245 , 246 ] Nanomaterials like carbon nanotubes (CNTs), borophene, and MXene are used to enhance biosensor sensitivity. These materials increase the surface area for biomolecule capture and detection, enable greater functionalization, and improve electrical conductivity, resulting in higher sensitivity even at low analyte concentrations.[ 247 ] AI enhances biosensing accuracy through noise filtering, pattern recognition, predictive modeling, and multi‐analyte detection.[ 248 , 249 ] AI algorithms can filter out noise from sensor data, identify complex patterns, develop predictive models for analyte behavior, and handle simultaneous analysis of multiple biomarkers. Additionally, AI can dynamically adjust biosensor sensitivity based on real‐time data, ensuring optimal performance across various conditions. IoT technology further improves transdermal biosensors by enabling continuous real‐time monitoring and data transmission to cloud‐based platforms for advanced analysis.[ 175 ] IoT devices can automatically log data, provide real‐time alerts for timely interventions, and support personalized medicine through therapeutic drug monitoring and dose optimization. The application of these 5th‐generation technologies significantly enhances the performance of biosensors and the capabilities of transdermal monitoring systems, leading to more accurate and reliable health monitoring solutions.

Standardization in molecular transport methods, such as microneedles and iontophoresis we discussed above, is crucial yet faces several challenges. Variability in transport efficiency, influenced by different device designs, materials, and operational parameters, leads to inconsistent data and hinders the development of universally applicable theragnostic criteria. The lack of standardized protocols for parameters like application time, pressure into the skin, and electrical current intensity further complicate reproducibility and reliability. Additionally, individual differences like skin location for molecule transport, skin thickness, and age affect the efficiency of drug delivery and biomarker extraction, necessitating the calibration procedures to quantify transported molecules. To address these issues, several solutions and research directions are proposed. Developing adaptive algorithms and machine learning models tailored to individual skin properties and drug characteristics can enable personalized theragnostic methods. For instance, nonlinear machine learning models’ application in accurately predicting skin permeability for transdermal drug delivery highlights their superior performance, identifying key molecular descriptors and clusters among FDA‐approved drugs that offer valuable insights for drug design and formulation.[ 250 ] Especially for microneedles, an applicator device can ensure consistent penetration and improve the reproducibility of analyses, although it may increase fabrication costs.[ 251 ]

For commercialization, scale‐up production of high‐quality transdermal transport systems in a cost‐effective manner is essential. This objective seems achievable in the near future, given the market presence of cosmetic microneedles for some time, whose production can serve as a paradigm for the mass production of various transdermal transport devices. For instance, a device that utilized reverse iontophoresis for glucose monitoring, once commercially available, demonstrated the potential of this technology by allowing non‐invasive, continuous measurement of glucose levels through the skin, providing patients with an alternative to traditional blood sampling methods.[ 252 ] However, it faced issues with stability and skin irritation, leading to its eventual withdrawal from the market. Several challenges hinder the scale‐up production of these devices. Complex manufacturing processes, particularly those incorporating advanced materials like microneedles, make large‐scale production difficult. Investing in advanced manufacturing technologies, such as precision microfabrication and automation, can streamline production processes and improve scalability. Implementing rigorous quality control protocols and utilizing high‐throughput screening techniques can help ensure each device meets the required standards. Also, ensuring skin compatibility to avoid irritation and discomfort, especially for long‐term use, is necessary. Developing new material designs and optimizing device geometry to enhance comfort, along with employing methods that minimize pH changes and byproduct formation, can address these issues.

In order to expand the library of biomolecules that can be dealt with transdermally, such as nucleic acids, peptides, and macromolecules,[ 239 , 253 ] continuous research on enhancing delivery efficiency considering the size and charge of the target molecules is required. In addition, miniaturization and thin‐film technology through circuit simplification and increased power source efficiency for the patches would increase the convenience of disease management. Finally, the development of personalized medical patches, such as the periodic delivery of different drugs for each patient or disease,[ 254 ] would provide a new paradigm for the healthcare system. These endless approaches herald a transformative era in medicine, and personalized, innovative medical services are highly expected in the future.
