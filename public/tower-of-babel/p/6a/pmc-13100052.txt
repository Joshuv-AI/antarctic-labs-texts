Dietary advanced glycation products combined with chronic restraint stress induced anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors in male mice
Authors: Yebing Zhang, Kuiying Gu, Lili Xin, Yu Xia, Guoqi Yang, Jiejiao Cai, Zhilong He, Li-Qiang Qin, Zhongxiao Wan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dietary advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and chronic stress might be involved in psychiatric disorders. It remains unclear whether dietary AGEs and chronic stress work together to trigger anxiety and depression. Firstly, mice were subjected to dietary AGEs (12 weeks) combined with chronic restraint stress (weeks 9–12, with daily restraint in a 50-mL cylindrical plastic tube for 3–4 h), defined as CRA group. Combined exposure aggravated anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors, disrupted gut microbiota homeostasis, induced inflammation, shifted tryptophan (TRP) metabolism towards kynurenine (KYN) pathway, mainly characterized by elevated KYN and 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK), and induced ferroptosis in the hippocampus. Secondly, IDO1 inhibitor, 1-methyltryptophan (1-MT), restored the TRP-KYN pathway disturbance post CRA, and inhibited neuronal ferroptosis post CRA. Thirdly, the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 restored CRA-induced neuronal ferroptosis, but had no effect on the TRP-KYN pathway. KYN and 3-HK induced ferroptosis in the mouse hippocampal neuron cell line (HT22), consistent with in vivo experimental results. In conclusion, combined exposure may first disturb gut microbiota homeostasis, followed by intestinal inflammatory responses, subsequently triggering tryptophan towards kynurenine metabolism via the gut-brain axis. These pathological processes led to the abnormal accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites, i.e., KYN and 3-HK, within neurons, which then induced neuronal ferroptosis.

Introduction

Since the COVID-19 pandemic, the incidence of common mental illnesses such as depression and anxiety has increased by more than 25%, becoming a significant global health burden1. Dietary risk factors might be new emerging factors associated with the risk of mental illnesses. For example, high intake of ultra-processed food2 and western dietary patterns3 were associated with increased risk of depression and anxiety. Thus, clarifying the potential mechanisms how dietary risk factors affected the pathogenesis and development of depression and anxiety is of great necessity for the prevention and treatment of mental disorders.

Dietary advanced glycation end products (AGEs), mainly produced by the Maillard reaction (MR) during cooking or the autoxidation of fatty acids, were commonly found in highly processed food involving high temperature, such as grilling and frying4. Existing epidemiology evidence suggests that dietary AGEs might be a crucial factor in the onset and progression of depression and anxiety5. In a prospective study of the Nurses’ Health Study II, Samuthpongtorn et al.5 reported that ultra-processed food consumption, which was generally concomitant with increased AGEs intake, was associated with increased risk of depression among middle-aged females. Chronic stress is also considered an important factor in causing mental illnesses6. Stress can change a person’s eating patterns, causing a person to eat more high-calorie, high-fat, and sugary foods7, which is generally correlated with high amounts of dietary AGEs. It remains unclear whether dietary AGEs intake and chronic stress work together to trigger anxiety and depression, and what specific mechanisms by which dietary AGEs combined with chronic stress affect depression and anxiety.

Accumulating evidence suggested that gut dysbiosis and disturbance of the periphery-central tryptophan (TRP)-kynurenine (KYN) metabolism play a pivotal role as a “hub” linking dietary risk factors with the occurrence and development of depression and anxiety8. The kynurenine pathway is the main pathway for the metabolism of tryptophan in the body, accounting for approximately 95% of the metabolism of free tryptophan, where it undergoes conversion to KYN facilitated by the indoleamine-2,3-dioxygenase-1 (IDO1) and tryptophan 2, 3-dioxygenase 2 (TDO2)9. Subsequently, KYN is transformed into 3-hydroxykynurenine (3-HK) and kynurenic acid (KA) through the catalytic action of the kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO) and kynurenine aminotransferase (KAT), respectively10, while 3-HK serves as the precursor of quinolinic acid (QA). QA and KA are agonizts and antagonists of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDAR), respectively11. The intestinal KYN metabolic pathway is the most important catabolic pathway for mammalian TRP and is closely regulated by the gut microbiota via affecting IDO1 activity12 or encoding enzymes homologous to the eukaryotic KYN pathway, and is therefore capable of producing KYN and downstream metabolites such as 3-HK. Notably, 3-HK can readily cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB), elevate reactive oxygen species (ROS), induce oxidative stress, and ultimately promote neuronal cell death13. Intestinal epithelial barrier integrity, BBB and inflammation are also critically involved in regulating the homeostasis of the periphery-central TRP-KYN metabolism8,11. In the lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced depressive mouse model, pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1, IL-6, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) are activated, promoting the overexpression of IDO-1. This subsequently shifts TRP metabolism toward the kynurenine pathway14. Additionally, KYN could also activate the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling, resulting in neuroinflammation and depressive behavior in mice15. However, the effect of compositional changes in the gut microbiota, the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier and BBB, local and systematic inflammation, as well as the periphery-central TRP-KYN metabolism in response to combined exposure of dietary AGEs and chronic stress has not been elucidated.

There is evidence suggesting that neuronal ferroptosis may also be one of the key pathological mechanisms underlying the occurrence and progression of depression16. Several existing evidence suggest a complex interplay between ferroptosis and KYN metabolism17,18. On the one hand, Cui et al.18 reported that L-kynurenine triggered ferroptosis in natural killer cells within the gastric cancer tumor microenvironment. Xu et al.17 reported that knockdown of glutathione peroxidase-2 suppressed kynureninase-mediated KYN metabolism owing to ROS accumulation in gastric cancer cell lines. At high concentrations or under oxidative stress conditions, 3-HK is transformed into a pro-oxidant, significantly inhibiting the activities of complexes I, II and IV in mitochondria19, resulting in an excessive production of ROS production20, which might potentially trigger ferroptosis. On the other hand, 3-HK, as a precursor of QA, exhibits significant antioxidant properties at low concentrations, effectively scavenging ROS and thereby exerting protective effects on cells21. Thus, it is of great significance to further explore how the TRP-KYN pathway interacts with ferroptosis in response to combined exposure to dietary AGEs and chronic stress.

People in modern society are facing increased stress and a higher chance of exposure to dietary AGEs intake at the same time. Existing experimental evidence has demonstrated that chronic restraint stress (CRS) alone can induce depression in mice by promoting the shift of TRP metabolism toward the kynurenine signaling pathway22. Meanwhile, a close association between unhealthy dietary habits and psychological stress existed23. In order to better mimic the human situation, i.e., exposure to both stress and high intake of dietary AGEs, this study primarily aimed to explore the combined exposure of dietary AGEs with chronic stress on anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice, and elucidated the potential mechanisms. Firstly, male C57BL/6 mice were used to evaluate the potential effects of dietary AGEs combined with a CRS on the depression- and anxiety-like behavioral disorders, gut microbiome-periphery-central TRP-KYN metabolism and ferroptosis. Secondly, by using the IDO1 inhibitor and ferroptosis inhibitor in combined exposure-treated mice, the interactions between TRP-KP metabolism and ferroptosis post combined exposure of dietary AGEs and CRS were further determined. Additionally, HT22 cells were used as a model to further clarify the effects of KYN and 3-HK, key metabolites involved in TRP-KYP metabolism, on neuronal ferroptosis. Findings from this study will provide new insights into the potential roles of dietary AGEs combined with CRS in the pathogenesis of depression and anxiety and contribute to a better understanding of the detrimental effects of MR products in modern diets, especially under high stress conditions.

Results

Dietary AGEs combined with CRS exposure exacerbated anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors and neurofunctional damage in mouse

After 12 weeks of study intervention, the sucrose consumption ratio (Fig. 1A) [decreased by 38.8% (P = 0.049) and 37.3% (P = 0.04), respectively] and the frequency of entering the open arms in the EPM test (Fig. 1B) [decreased by 14.0% (P < 0.01) and 57.5% (P < 0.01), respectively] were significantly lower in the MCRS and MCRA groups compared with the MCON group. The immobility time in the FST (Fig. 1C) and TST (Fig. 1D) of mice from the MCRA group were significantly increased by 32.2% (P = 0.048) and 30.4% (P < 0.01), respectively, while the total activity distance in the OFT from the MCRA group was significantly decreased by 19.8% (P = 0.026), and the stay time in the central area has decreased by 44.5% (P = 0.028) (Fig. 1E–G). These results suggest that CRS exposure alone led to anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors in mice to some extent, and dietary AGEs intervention further exacerbated CRS-associated anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors. We further measured markers related to neural function from the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex (PFC). Compared with the MCON group, the protein expression levels of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) (P = 0.01) in the hippocampus (Fig. 1H) and its receptor TrkB (P = 0.048) in the PFC (Fig. 1I) were significantly reduced in the MCRS group. The expressions of BDNF (P = 0.047), TrkB (P = 0.046) and phosphorylated cyclic adenosine monophosphate response element binding protein (P-CREB) (P = 0.048) in hippocampus and PFC were significantly decreased in the MCRA group. Notably, P-CREB expression was further reduced in mice of the MCRA group compared to the MCRS group (P = 0.01). In the MCON group, the neurons in the dentate gyrus (DG), CA1, CA3 and PFC were arranged neatly and closely, and the Nissl bodies were clear. In MCRS and MCRA groups, the damage of neurons was aggravated, the arrangement of neurons was irregular, the distribution of neurons was sparse (black dashed circle), and the Nissl bodies were disintegrated, and vacuoles appeared in the nerve cells (red arrow) (Fig. 1J). The above results suggest that exposure to both dietary AGEs and CRS exacerbated neuronal damage in mice.

Dietary AGEs combined with CRS exposure disrupted the blood-brain barrier permeability proteins, exacerbated neuroinflammation, and disturbed TRP metabolism in the hippocampus

The exposure to dietary AGEs combined with CRS significantly reduced the protein expression of zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin, key proteins associated with the integrity of the BBB (Fig. 2A). The disruption of BBB integrity is closely related to chronic neuroinflammation24. Ibα-1 and GFAP proteins serve as markers for the activation of microglia and astrocytes, respectively25. NLRP3 plays a significant role in neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity. When stimulated, it assembles inflammasomes with ASC, activates caspase-1, and caspase-1 cleaves Pro-IL-1β to generate active IL-1β, initiating an inflammatory response26. Compared to the MCON group, the protein expression of Ibα1 (P = 0.047), GFAP (P = 0.048), NLRP3 (P = 0.049), and caspase 1 (P = 0.047) in the hippocampus were significantly increased from the MCRA group; also, the protein expression of caspase1 and ASC were significantly increased from the MCRA group compared to the MCRS group (Fig. 2A) (P = 0.049). The mRNA levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines including IL-1β (P = 0.047) and IL-6 (P = 0.047) in the hippocampus were significantly increased in the MCRA group compared to MCON group (Fig. 2B). IL-6 (Fig. 2C) and IFN-γ (Fig. 2E) secretion levels in the hippocampus [increased by 24.7% (P = 0.047) and 13.3% (P < 0.01), respectively] and serum [increased by 58.3% (P = 0.047) and 53.7% (P < 0.01), respectively] (Supplementary Fig. 3C) of mice from MCRA group were significantly higher than those in the MCON group and MCRS group. However, no significant changes in the protein contents of cleaved-IL-1β and pro-IL-1β in the hippocampus were observed. TRP-serotonin (SP) and TRP-KYN pathways are closely associated with anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors. In the SP, serotonin (5-HT) was metabolized by the MAOA enzyme into 5-HIAA. An increase in 5-HIAA is indicative of low 5-HT levels11. The level of 5-HT in the brain of patients with depression was reduced, leading to impaired emotional regulation and cognitive function27. TRP levels in the hippocampus (Fig. 2H) and serum (Supplementary Table 4) from MCRA group were significantly decreased by 16.3%% (P = 0.04) and 94.6% (P < 0.01), respectively compared to the MCON group. In SP pathway, compared with the MCON group, MAO A protein expression (P = 0.04) (Fig. 2G) and Maoa (P = 0.04) and Maob (P = 0.047) mRNA levels (Fig. 2F) were significantly increased in the hippocampus of mice from the MCRA group compared with the MCON group; also 5-HIAA levels were significantly increased by 62.0% (P < 0.01) and 52.7% (P < 0.01) and 5-HT levels were significantly decreased by 6.3% (P = 0.04) and 5.2% (P = 0.049) in the hippocampus of mice from the MCRS and MCRA groups (Fig. 2H). Serum 5-HT levels were significantly decreased by 64.4% (P = 0.03), and 5-HIAA levels were significantly increased by 90.7% (P = 0.02) from the MCRA group compared to the MCON group (Supplementary Table 4).

In the TRP-KYN pathway, IDO1 protein expression (P = 0.02) was significantly increased, and QPRT protein expression (P = 0.047) was significantly decreased in the hippocampus (Fig. 2G) and PFC (Supplementary Fig. 3A) of mice from the MCRA group compared with the MCON group. The mRNA expression of Ido1 (P = 0.047), Tdo2 (P = 0.05), Kmo (P = 0.01), Grin2a (P < 0.01) and Grin2b (P = 0.047) in the hippocampus of mice from the MCRA group were significantly increased compared with the MCON group, and the expression of Kat4 (P = 0.01) was significantly decreased (Fig. 2F). Compared with the MCON group, XA levels in the hippocampus (by 55.8%, P < 0.01) and KYN levels (by 73.1%, P < 0.01) in the serum were significantly higher and the ratio of 5HT/ KYN (P = 0.02) in the hippocampus decreased significantly in the MCRS group; the levels of KYN, XA and 3-HK in hippocampus were significantly increased by 24.8% (P = 0.048), 24.7% (P = 0.042) and 9.0% (P = 0.049) from MCRA group. Similar results were found from serum (Supplementary Table 4), while KA was significantly decreased by 13.0% from the MCRA group (P = 0.047) (Fig. 2H). In addition, in the hippocampus, the ratio of 5HT/KYN was significantly decreased (P = 0.047), and KYN/TRP was significantly increased (P < 0.01) in the MCRA group in comparison with the MCON group (Fig. 2H). We employed the Pearson correlation coefficient test to analyze the associations between three typical AGEs markers in the diet—free CML, protein-bound CML, and CEL-and TRP metabolites in serum and hippocampal tissue (Supplementary Fig. 4). The results revealed that in serum samples, these AGEs markers showed significant correlations with TRP, KYN, and 3-HK. Meanwhile, in the hippocampus, they exhibited a strong association specifically with 3-HK (P < 0.01).

The above results suggest that CRS exposure alone mainly led to TRP-SP pathway disorder, CRS exposure combined with dietary AGEs intervention resulted in TRP-SP and TRP-KYN pathway disorder, further indicating that TRP-KYN pathway disturbance was the critical pathway associated with anxiety-like and depression-like behavior post CRA intervention.

Dietary AGEs combined with CRS exposure disrupted intestinal barrier function, exacerbated intestinal inflammation and disturbed TRP metabolism

IL-6 (P = 0.047, P = 0.044) (Fig. 3B) and IFN-γ (P = 0.047, P < 0.01) (Fig. 3C) secretion level significantly increased in the colon of MCRS and MCRA group, respectively compared to MCON group. Dietary AGEs combined with CRS exposure induced gut microbiota dysbiosis

To investigate whether the combined exposure of dietary AGEs and CRS affects the gut microbiota, 16S rRNA sequencing technology was used to analyze the gut microbial composition of mice at 8 weeks (i.e., only dietary AGEs intervention was given, defined as the MCRA group for consistency), and 12 weeks (i.e., combined intervention). As shown in Fig. 4A, at week 8, the Shannon index (P = 0.047), Chao index (P = 0.046), and ACE index (P = 0.01) in the MCRA group mice were significantly decreased compared to the MCON group. By week 12, the Shannon index (P = 0.02, P = 0.047), Simpson index (P = 0.043, P = 0.043), Chao index (P < 0.01, P = 0.03), and ACE index (P < 0.01, P = 0.02) in both the MCRS and MCRA groups of mice were significantly lower than those in the MCON group. The β-diversity revealed significant differences between the MCRA group and the MCON group mice at both weeks 8 and 12 (Fig. 4B). At the phylum level, Bacteroidota, Firmicutes, Desulfobacterota and Actinobacteriota dominated the microbiota of all groups at both 8 and 12 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). By week 8, the MCRA group exhibited a marked increase in Proteobacteria (P < 0.01) abundance compared to the MCON group. Discussion

This study provided the very first evidence that dietary advanced glycation end products combined with chronic restraint stress aggravated the CRS-induced anxiety and depression-like behaviors in male mice. The underlying mechanism may involve gut microbiota dysbiosis and the induction of intestinal inflammation, and the activation of peripheral and central kynurenine metabolic disorders through the gut-brain axis. This led to the excessive accumulation of kynurenine metabolites, i.e., KYN and 3-HK in neurons, which further activated neuronal ferroptosis and exacerbated neuroinflammation and neurological dysfunction-associated protein expression, thus forming a vicious cycle. To our knowledge, this is the first to comprehensively evaluate detailed metabolic changes in the peripheral and central TRP-KYN pathway through the gut-brain axis, as well as its interaction with neuronal ferroptosis under conditions of dietary AGEs combined with CRS.

Chronic unpredictable mild stress is considered a reliable model for modeling depression in rodents29, but with different types of CUMS from multiple laboratories. In this study, by exposing mice to cylindrical plastic tubes for 3 to 4 h per day as one of the chronic stress, mice mainly exhibited abnormalities in SPT and EPM. When dietary AGEs intake was combined with CRS, mice exhibited notable anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors in a wide range of behavioral tests, also with a significant reduction in TrkB/BDNF/P-CREB protein expression. BDNF can promote the formation of neuronal synapses and the efficiency of synaptic transmission, also can enhance synaptic plasticity through the TrkB/P-CREB pathway30. Our findings are the very first to demonstrate that dietary AGEs might aggravate CRS-associated anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors, and neuronal dysfunction-associated protein. It is suggested that restricting dietary AGEs intake might be an effective strategy for controlling the occurrence of psychiatric disorders, especially for those under stress.

The role of TRP-metabolism in combined intervention-induced anxiety-like and depression-like behavior can be viewed through the KYN and serotonin pathway activity8,11,27,31. The pathophysiology of depression has been closely linked to low 5-HT levels27. TRP-KYN metabolism also plays a crucial role in psychiatric disorders8. Especially, the KYN pathway might compete with 5-HT, which may further contribute to the development of depression31. Han et al.32 reported that CUMS led to the central and peripheral imbalances of tryptophan metabolism, which is manifested with increased QA and KYN/TRP ratio in the hippocampus and plasma of rats. Ye et al.33 reported that one-week CRS led to TRP-KYN metabolism towards the KA, whereas four-week CRS shifted KYN metabolism towards the KYN-QA pathway, resulting in an accumulation of QA and 3-HK. Moderately increased KA can prevent neuronal hyperactivity and damage by inhibiting excessive glutamate-induced stimulation of NMDA receptors11. However, persistently elevated or excessively high KA levels may impair learning and memory by suppressing the α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptor34, which is critically involved in these cognitive processes. In patients with schizophrenia, elevated KA levels have been detected in both the prefrontal cortex and cerebrospinal fluid, accompanied by increased concentrations of L-kynurenine (the direct precursor of KA)35. In our study, 4 weeks of CRS elevated 5-HIAA and 3-HK, and reduced TRP, KA in the colon, also elevated 5-HIAA and XA, but reduced 5-HT in the hippocampus, which is partially consistent with previous studies32,33. When dietary AGEs were combined with CRS exposure, we found that the peripheral central TRP-KYN metabolic disorder was fully activated. This is mainly manifested by the significant increase in the levels of its metabolites, including KYN and 3-HK, and the increased protein expression of the synthetic enzymes IDO1 and TDO2, as well as reduced QPRT protein expression from both the hippocampus and colon. This was further confirmed by increased mRNA levels of the NMDA receptor subunits GluN2A/B in the hippocampus of mice exposed to combined intervention. Previously, it was reported that CRS induces TDO expression in the liver and cortex of mice, but it returns to baseline levels after 24 h36. Zhou et al.13 reported no differences in TDO expression in the hippocampus and colon of adolescent depression mice. Our results indicate that CRS intervention alone had no significant effect on TDO2 mRNA levels in the hippocampus and colon, which may be influenced by tissue type and detection time points. However, mice administered with combined intervention exhibited a significant increase in TDO2 mRNA levels. The potential involvement of TDO post CRA requires full clarification. Moreover, correlation analysis revealed a strong association between AGEs levels in feed and kynurenine metabolites in mice. Notably, protein-bound CEL showed significant differences before and after feed heating, suggesting it may be a key substance influencing KYN metabolism. Furthermore, as a classic competitive inhibitor of IDO1, 1-MT blocks the TRP-KYN metabolic pathway by directly suppressing enzyme activity37. Although Souza et al.38 reported that 1-MT possesses anxiolytic potential in neurodegenerative disease models and can reverse abnormal behaviors in chronically restrained mice22, the underlying mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated. In this study, following a 5-week 1-MT intervention, we found that the treatment effectively curbed the excessive shunting of TRP toward the kynurenine pathway, significantly improving key enzyme activities and metabolite profiles in both the peripheral and central nervous systems. Collectively, these findings indicate that peripheral-central TRP-KYN metabolic dysregulation, induced by the synergistic effects of dietary AGEs and CRS, serves as a core pathological mechanism underlying anxiety- and depression-like behaviors in mice. TRP is preferentially metabolized in an inflammatory environment along the neurotoxic TRP-KYN-QA pathway39. KYN could also activate AhR signaling, further involved in neuroinflammation and depressive behavior in mice15. Systemic inflammation also increases the influx of neurotoxic KYN and 3-HK into the brain via the impaired BBB, which is largely affected by the integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier40. The tight junction proteins, including ZO-1, occludin and claudin-1 situated between intestinal epithelial cells, are critical for maintaining intestinal epithelial barrier function41. Previous studies have shown that three weeks of CRS did not alter the permeability of the BBB in mice42. It has also been shown that chronic dietary intake of AGEs led to disruption of intestinal barrier integrity43,44. In the present study, we found that both CRS and combined intervention impaired the integrity protein expression of the intestinal epithelial barrier and BBB, but the damage was more severe post combined intervention. We further found the NLRP3 inflammasome, including NLRP3, Caspase 1, and ASC were fully activated from both hippocampus and colon post combined intervention, while no significant alterations in AhR protein expression was observed post MCRA intervention. It is likely that exposure to dietary AGEs combined with CRS may activate peripheral central TRP disturbances by disrupting intestinal epithelial barrier and BBB permeability and inducing inflammation. However, further studies are required to clarify the causal relationship between inflammation and TRP disorders post combined intervention.

The gut microbiota is also critically involved in the onset of mental illnesses via affecting the fluctuations in the levels of TRP-KYN pathway metabolism41. Gut microbiome disturbance has also been reported to impair gut barrier function and induce local or systemic inflammatory responses40. Deng et al.22 found that CRS disrupted gut microbiota profiles and the TRP-KYN metabolism in mice. Dietary AGEs have also been shown to adversely affect the microbiome composition44. In this study, both 8 weeks of dietary AGEs intake per se and 12 weeks of dietary AGEs intake combined with CRS elevated the phyla Proteobacteria, also [Acetivibrio]_ethanolgignens_group, Parasutterella at the genus level, while reducing Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Oscillibacter, UCG-009, and Anaerotruncus at the genus level. Also, 8 weeks of dietary AGEs differentially increased the genera Dubosiella, and combined intervention differentially elevated the genera Citrobacter and Desulfovibrio. It has been reported that high-fat diets may promote the expansion of Proteobacteria at the phylum level, upregulate inflammatory responses and IDO1 expression in colonocytes, and ultimately disrupt systemic TRP-KYN metabolism8. Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group is a butyrate-producing bacterium whose abundance levels are negatively correlated with the levels of metabolites in the TRP-KYN pathway, such as QA, in depression models45. A significant increase in Parasutterella abundance has also been reported in patients with social anxiety disorder and stress-induced mouse models46, potentially mediated by altered TRP-KYN metabolism that exacerbates intestinal inflammation47. Elevated Parasutterella abundance has also been found post high-fat diets and ultra-processed foods48. It has been reported that Dubosiella can produce L-lysine, which activates IDO1 and shifts the metabolism of TRP towards the KYN pathway49. Our findings suggested that 8 weeks of dietary AGEs intervention had perturbed gut microbiota balance, with elevated bacteria favoring TRP metabolism towards the KYN pathway. By the 12th week, after the intervention of dietary AGEs combined with CRS, the differentially abundant bacterium Citrobacter exhibited pro-inflammatory effects, potentially promoting TRP metabolism to produce metabolites like KYN50. It has been recently reported that Desulfovibrio generates hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) during metabolism51. Elevated H₂S levels exert cytotoxicity, which may indirectly affect TRP metabolism. Coriobacteriaceae UCG-002, belonging to the Coriobacteriaceae family, produces phenol and p-cresol compounds with cytotoxicity that impair intestinal barrier function52. Our correlation heatmap analysis further verified Proteobacteria, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group and Coriobacteriaceae UCG-002 might be the key microbiome in affecting the KYN pathway along the gut-brain axis post combined intervention. Taken together, it is speculated that exposure to dietary AGEs may first perturb gut microbiome homeostasis, which was further aggravated post CRS. This was followed with localized and systemic inflammatory responses, which facilitate tryptophan metabolism towards the KYN pathway in the gut, the translocation of toxic compounds, including KYN and 3-HK into the brain, consequently priming the neurotoxic TRP-KYN-QA pathway and the over-activation of NMDAR receptors, eventually participating the onset and progression of anxiety-like and depression-like behavior.

Ferroptosis, a cell death mode driven primarily by iron accumulation and lipid peroxides, is intricately linked to the onset of depression16. Previously, Jiao et al.53 reported that 6 weeks of CUMS induced the hippocampal ferroptosis pathway. In this study, CRS exposure alone only altered FPN1, NCOA4, and SLC7A11 protein expression, but iron loading was not observed, possibly due to tissue specificity or the type and degree of stress. When dietary AGEs combined with CRS, hippocampus ferroptosis pathway were fully activated, this is mainly manifested by alterations in key proteins regulating iron metabolism (i.e., FPN1, TFR1, transferrin, NCOA4), antioxidant system balance (i.e., SLC7A11/GPX4) and lipid peroxidation (PTGS2) in the hippocampus, as well as increased levels of ferrous and total iron, reduced GPX4 activity and typical ferroptosis morphological changes in mitochondria from the hippocampus. Studies have shown that AGEs can induce ferroptosis in non-neurological diseases. For instance, AGEs can induce ferroptosis in keratinocytes (HaCaT) and increase the level of malondialdehyde (MDA)54. This study further found that ferroptosis inhibitor (Fer-1) suppressed CRA-induced neuronal ferroptosis and improved neurological function and anxiety-depression-like behavioral performance in mice to some extent. Taken together, these studies suggest that dietary AGEs in combination with CRS further exacerbated iron overload, inhibited the SLC7A11/GPX4 pathway and broadly activated neuronal ferroptosis, which might also be involved in inducing anxiety-depression-like behavior in mice.

The complex interplay between ferroptosis and KYN metabolism has attracted researchers’ interests17,20. Our study is the very first to verify the interactions between KYN metabolism and neuronal ferroptosis in vivo post dietary AGEs intervention combined with CRS. In 1-MT-intervened mice, TRP-KYN metabolic dysfunction was broadly corrected, which was accompanied by improved neuronal ferroptosis; while in Fer-1-intervened mice, TRP-KYN metabolism was not improved in either hippocampus or colon. It is indicated that CRA-induced TRP-KYN metabolic dysfunction might be critical for eliciting neuronal ferroptosis. Metabolomics analyses showed that the levels of KYN and 3-HK, the core metabolites of KYN, were significantly elevated in both the hippocampus, colon and serum post combined intervention, and 1-MT significantly reduced the elevated KYN and 3-HK levels, suggesting that these two metabolites might be critical for the protective effect of 1-MT on ferroptosis. Previous studies have shown the pro-oxidant toxicity of KYN and 3-HK, which can trigger ROS production when there is an imbalance in the concentration of KYN and 3-HK in the neural microenvironment10. Notably, 3-HK and KYN each amplify oxidative stress by different molecular mechanisms: the former directly generates superoxide anion (O₂-) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) via autoxidation55, whereas the latter forms a dual pro-oxidative signal by synergistically activating the NADPH oxidase (NOX)-dependent O₂- generation pathway17. Using the HT22 neuronal cell line, we found that KYN and 3-HK treatment induced a significant up-regulation of ferric iron and ROS levels, accompanied by mitochondrial damage and aberrant expression of ferroptosis coregulatory proteins (i.e., TFR1, NCOA4, GPX4), further confirming that KYN and 3-HK could induce neuronal ferroptosis. Taken altogether, it is indicated that dietary AGEs combined with CRS induced periphery central TRP-KYN dysfunction, which is accompanied by elevated hippocampal KYN and 3-HK levels; these metabolites might further induce neuronal ferroptosis, eventually forming a vicious cycle, resulting in disorders of neuronal dysfunction-associated proteins, and finally resulting in anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors.

Finally, this study still has certain limitations. Firstly, although the method of generating AGEs by high-temperature heating of feed can simulate the formation process of dietary AGEs, the experimental design constrains the construction of a dose gradient, making it difficult to clarify the threshold and dose-response relationship between dietary AGEs and anxiety- and depression-like behaviors. Secondly, limited by current detection technologies, only quantitative analysis of free and protein-bound forms of CEL and CML in the feed can be achieved, failing to cover a more comprehensive detection of AGEs content. Third, the effects of interventions on behaviors in female mice were unexplored in this study. Future research will necessarily focus on including female animals to investigate potential sex differences in the model’s phenotype and underlying mechanisms. Fourth, the intraperitoneal injection method used in this experiment cannot precisely target the intestinal and brain regions, inadvertently contacting other organs such as the liver and kidneys. The resulting local effects may be confounded with systemic effects. Fifth, the direct effects of dietary AGEs on depression remain undetermined. Follow-up studies from our research group will answer this question. Finally, due to the limitations inherent in the initial experimental design framework, we have not yet established a definitive causal chain among AGEs, the gut microbiota, and depression. To address this limitation, we have prioritized functional validation as the core focus of our next study: we will conduct fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) experiments specifically targeting the significantly differential taxa—Proteobacteria, Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, and Coriobacteriaceae_UCG-002—in order to precisely pinpoint the key strains and verify their driving roles in disease pathogenesis.

In summary, exposure to dietary AGEs may first disturb gut microbiota homeostasis, this was further aggravated post CRS, followed with intestinal inflammatory responses and impaired intestinal epithelial barrier, subsequently triggering tryptophan towards kynurenine metabolism from the periphery to the central nervous system via the gut-brain axis. These pathological processes led to the abnormal accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites, i.e., KYN and 3-HK, within neurons, which then induced neuronal ferroptosis and associated functional impairment, ultimately manifesting as anxiety-like and depression-like behaviors. These findings provide novel insights and potential therapeutic targets for the early prevention of depression, which in turn will facilitate a deeper understanding of the pathogenesis underlying psychiatric disorders.

Animals and interventions

We primarily focused on male mice in the initial phase of the study for the following three main considerations. The first was to control for the significant confounding variable of hormonal fluctuations from the female estrous cycle, which is known to affect behavior, neuroendocrine responses, and drug metabolism59. Second, AGEs are typically highly enriched in ultra-processed foods (UPF). Recent human studies consistently confirm that UPF intake is significantly higher in adult men than in women. For instance, in South Korea, daily UPF intake among men is approximately 54% higher than among women60. In Canada, UPF contributes 49.4% of total energy intake in men, which is also significantly higher than the 47.6% observed in women61. Finally, it is necessary to follow the conventional approach, where most established models are based on male animals22,39, thereby ensuring our findings are directly comparable and compatible with existing research. Male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks) were purchased from the Shanghai Slac Laboratory Animal Co., LTD (Shanghai, China). All animal procedures followed the guidelines in the care and use of animals and were approved by the Animal Studies Committee of Soochow University (permission no. SUDA20250106A0). All mice were housed in standard plastic cage under specific pathogen-free conditions with appropriate temperatures of 20–25 °C, humidity of 50–60%, and a 12 h light–dark cycle. Animals were acclimated for 1 week before the experiments, the comprehensive experimental design employed throughout the study were presented in Supplementary Fig. 1.

(1) The first study aimed to explore the effects and underlying mechanisms of a diet enriched with AGEs, in conjunction with CRS, on anxiety and depression-like behaviors in mice. A total of 36 mice were randomly divided into three groups, with 12 mice in each group, and it was ensured that there was no significant difference in the initial body weight among groups. The weekly bodyweight changes and food intake over 12 weeks were shown in Supplementary Fig 2. These groups included the control group (MCON), which was administered with a daily AIN93-M diet over a 12-week period. The chronic restraint stress group (MCRS), which was administered with a daily AIN93-M diet over a 12 weeks period and underwent CRS from weeks 9 to 12. The mice were confined in a 50-mL cylindrical plastic tube (inner diameter 2.5 cm, length 10 cm) for 3 to 4 h daily, allowing limited mobility33. The third group, combining dietary AGEs with CRS (MCRA), was provided with a heat-treated (121 °C, 3 h) AIN93-M diet enriched in AGEs daily56 and CRS intervention as described above during the last 4 weeks of the study.

(2) The second study was to examine the impact of the IDO1-mediated KYN pathway of TRP metabolism on anxiety and depression-like behaviors induced by CRA. A total of 24 mice were allocated into three groups with 8 animals each: the MCON and MCRA group, which received the same treatment protocol as in the initial batch of experiments, accompanied by intraperitoneal injections of saline from the 7th to the 12th week. The MCRA-1-MT group received CRA intervention and an extra daily intraperitoneal injection of an IDO1 inhibitor, i.e., 1-methyltryptophan (1-MT), at a concentration of 3 mg/kg/day, from the 7th to the 12th week38.

(3) The third study was to investigate the inhibition of the ferroptosis pathway on anxiety and depression-like behaviors induced by CRA. A total of 24 mice were randomly assigned into three groups with 8 animals each: MCON, MCRA, and MCRA-Fer-1. The MCON and MCRA groups received the same treatment as in the first experiment, which involved intraperitoneal injections of saline from the 7th to the 12th week. The MCRA-Fer-1 group, building on the MCRA group, was further treated with the ferroptosis inhibitor, i.e., Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), at a dosage of 1 mg/kg/day, administered intraperitoneally from the 7th to the 12th week on a daily base62.

Behavioral evaluations

Following the 12-week intervention, the mice were subjected to an array of behavioral assessments, including the Sucrose Preference Test (SPT), Elevated Plus Maze (EPM), Open Field Test (OFT), Tail Suspension Test (TST), and Forced Swim Test (FST), adhering to the protocols described in previous studies22. The behavioral experiments were performed blindly and randomly by trained graduate students.

Sucrose preference test: Animals were individually housed and habituated with two bottles of 1% sucrose solution for 24 h, followed by one bottle of 1% sucrose solution and one bottle of water for another 24 h. Experimental mice were then exposed to two identical bottles for a 24-h test; one bottle contained 1% sucrose solution and the other contained water. The bottle positions were reversed every 12 h to prevent any positional biases from confounding the results. The sucrose consumption ratio was calculated by dividing the consumption of sucrose by the total consumption of both the sucrose solution and water. A decrease in the sucrose consumption ratio indicates anhedonia or a lack of ability to experience pleasure, which is a vital characteristic of depression-like behavior.

Forced swimming test: Mice were individually placed in a cylinder (height, 25 cm; diameter, 10 cm) filled with 10 cm deep water (23 ± 1 °C) to test for 6 min. The mouse was allowed to swim in a container for 6 min, with the first 2 min as adaptation, and the mouse’s movement situation in the subsequent 4 min was recorded. The immobility, climbing and swimming behaviors were recorded and quantified with VisuTrack behavior analysis software (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., LTD). The immobile time was considered as the time with no additional activity other than the required to keep the head above water. An increase in the duration of immobility is indicative of a depression-like behavior.

Tail suspension test: Mice were suspended by their tails using adhesive tape 1 cm from the tip of the tail and 15 cm above the ground. Small plastic tubes were attached to tails to make sure mice could not climb. The tests lasted for 6 min and the immobility time was measured and analyzed during the final 4 min by VisuTrack behavior analysis software.

Open field test: Animals were placed in the center of an arena (50 × 50 cm2) with a color-marked center square (25 × 25 cm2) for 6 min. An overhead video-tracking system (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., LTD) was used to record the animal’s location and total track length, and time spent in the center square. Between each trial, the open field was cleaned to avoid interference between individuals.

Elevated plus maze: The EPM apparatus consisted of a central platform (5 × 5 cm2) with four branching arms (30 × 5 cm2), where one pair of opposite arms was walled while the other was open. The equipment was about 70 cm above the floor. The time spent in the open and closed arms in a 6-min test was recorded automatically using a video-tracking system (Shanghai Xinruan Information Technology Co., LTD). To avoid olfactory cues between individuals, the apparatus was cleaned with ethanol between tests.

Sample collection

Following the completion of behavioral experiments, animals were fasted for 8 hours. On the subsequent day, mice were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of 0.3% pentobarbital sodium (weight-adjusted dose)63 and positioned in prone orientation for tissue collection. After incising the dura mater, the brain tissues were bilaterally dissected. The hippocampus and prefrontal cortex from one cerebral hemisphere were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently transferred to −80 °C storage. The contralateral hemisphere was immersed in pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution or electron microscopy fixative for fixation. Additionally, the posterior segment of the colon was excised and fixed in 4% PFA solution, while the remaining colon segments were flash-frozen with liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C.

Nissl staining and Immunofluorescent staining (IF) of brain tissue

The brain tissues of the mouse were sequentially fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin, and were cut with 5μm sections continuously via an automatic slicer. The sections were then stained with Nissl staining. Three fields of view in the CA1, CA3 and DG regions of the left side of the hippocampus and the prefrontal cortex (PFC) were randomly selected and captured using a DM3000 microscope (Phase Contrast, Japan).

The fixed hippocampal sections were also incubated with NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, then with Alexa 594-conjugated rabbit secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. After washing with PBS, DAPI nuclear staining was conducted, and fluorescence images of NLRP3-positive cells were captured using the confocal laser scanning microscope (C2, Nikon, Japan).

H&E staining and alcian blue-phosphoric acid schiff (AB-PAS) staining of colon tissue

The colonic tissue was stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) and Alcian blue, respectively. Subsequent microscopic examination was performed utilizing a DM3000 microscope (Phase Contrast, Japan) to facilitate image acquisition.

Determination of GPX4 activity and iron content in hippocampal tissue

The GPX4 activity, ferrous iron and total iron content in hippocampal tissue were measured via the colorimetry methods in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For the measurement of GPX4 activity, frozen hippocampal tissues were weighed and homogenized with the extraction working solution (1:9). Samples were then centrifuged at 1000 × g, 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for the measurement of GPX4 activity following the manufacturer’s instructions. The GPX4 activity was corrected by the protein content of the samples, and expressed as U/g protein. For the measurement of total iron and ferrous iron content in the hippocampus, 10 mg of hippocampal tissue was weighed, 0.5 mL of cold PBS was added, and samples were homogenized. Then samples were centrifuged at 1000 × g, 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was used for the measurement of total iron and ferrous iron content following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) for IL-6 and IFN-γ

The measurement of IL-6 and IFN-γ levels in serum, hippocampus, and colon tissues was measured via the quantitative ELISA kits. The hippocampus and colon tissues were weighed, and pre-cooled PBS were added at a volume five times the sample weight. Then, ultrasonic disruption of tissues using an ultrasonic device was performed, and finally, samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant is then collected for the measurement of IL-6 and IFN-γ secretion. All experimental procedures were carried out in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

LC-MS/MS measurement for TRP metabolites

Based on previous literature64, TRP metabolites in serum, hippocampus, and colon samples were measured via the AB Sciex QTRAP 5500 LC-MS/MS platform, combined with a Restek ultra-water C18 chromatographic column (100 × 2.1 mm2, 3 μm, produced in Bellefonte, USA). TRP, 5-HT, 5-HIAA, MLT, KYN, KA, XA, and 3-HK were diluted to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL using methanol or DMSO (dimethyl sulfoxide). A 500 ng/mL internal standard (IS) working solution using acetaminophen was also prepared. Mixed 10 μL of each standard stock solution (TRP, 5-HT, 5-HIAA, MLT, KYN, KA, XA, and 3-HK) to obtain a mixed standard solution (Mix 1). Then, 50 μL of the IS working solution, 80% methanol, and Mix 1 were combined to prepare calibration standards at concentrations of 50, 100, 250, 500, 1000, and 2000 ng/mL, and were stored at –20 °C until analysis.

For the measurement in serum, 100 μL of serum was added into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 20 μL of internal standard and 10 μL of trifluoroacetic acid (TFA). Samples were vortexed to mix thoroughly and then centrifuged at 20,000 rpm at 4 °C for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. For the measurement in the hippocampus and colon, the hippocampal tissue (10 mg) and colon tissue (30 mg) were weighed on dry ice and put into a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube, 100 μL of ultrapure water was added and then homogenized. A total of 100 μL of the homogenate, 20 μL internal standard and 10 μL TFA were vortexed and mixed thoroughly. The mix was then centrifuged at 20,000 rpm at 4 °C for 25 min, and the supernatant was collected for the further analysis.

TRP metabolites in serum, hippocampus, and colon samples were measured via the AB Sciex QTRAP 5500 LC-MS/MS platform, combined with a Restek ultra-water C18 chromatographic column (100 × 2.1 mm2, 3 μm, produced in Bellefonte, USA). In the chromatographic separation, the mobile phase A consisted of pure acetonitrile, and the mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid aqueous solution. The gradient elution program was set as follows: within 0 to 0.5 min, the proportion of mobile phase A was 5%; from 0.5 to 5 min, it increased from 5% to 80%; from 5 to 6 min, it remained at 80%; from 6 to 6.1 min, it decreased from 80% to 5%; and from 6.1 to 8 min, it remained at 5%. Mass spectrometry detection was performed using multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source operated in positive mode. The specific parameters were set as follows: electrospray voltage at 4500 V, ion source temperature at 500 °C, curtain gas at 30 psi, auxiliary gas at 40 psi, and nebulizer gas at 60 psi. The quantitative mass spectrometry parameters for tryptophan metabolites and their internal standards (including ion pairs, retention times, declustering potential (DP), collision energy (CE), etc.) are detailed in Supplementary Table 3. Data acquisition and processing were conducted using AB SCIEX Analyst software (version 1.6).

In vitro experimentation and cell viability assay

The HT22 cells derived from mouse hippocampal neurons were grown in a high-glucose DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Prior to treatment, HT22 cells were examined under a microscope and exhibited normal morphology, displaying neuron-like characteristics with relatively large cell bodies and distinct nuclei. No evidence of Mycoplasma contamination was detected during the observation. Cells were treated with KYN at 50, 100, and 200 μM and 3-HK at 10, 25, and 50 μM for 24 h. The untreated HT22 cells were used as the control group.

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay kit. After treating the cells with different concentrations of KYN and 3-HK for 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well. Then, the culture plate was incubated at 37 °C for another 1.5 h in a humidified cell culture incubator. Finally, the cell viability was evaluated by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm using a SYNERGY 2 microplate reader (Bio-Tek, Winooski VT, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of brain tissues and HT22 cells

The hippocampus and HT22 cells were subjected to overnight fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde. Subsequently, the tissue was prepared for coronal sectioning at a thickness of 60–80 nm and appropriately embedded. The morphology of the mitochondria was subsequently observed via transmission electron microscopy (H-7500, Hitachi, Japan).

Imaging by confocal microscopy for ROS and Fe2+ content in HT22 cells

DCFH-DA staining was used to measure the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the HT-22 cells. HT22 cells were treated with KYN (50, 100, 200 μM) and 3-HK (10, 25, 50 μM) for 24 h. Then cells were incubated with the probe solution at 37 °C for 20 min in a dark environment. After gently washing cells with PBS 3 times, the fluorescence images were captured under a FV1200MPE Laser Scanning Microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

The Fe2+ concentration within HT-22 cells by adding 1 µmol/L of FerroOrange working solution and incubating them at 37 °C for 30 min in a dark environment. Finally, the cells were observed, and images were captured via the FV1200MPE Laser Scanning Microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot

The total protein extraction and concentration determination of the hippocampus, prefrontal cortex, colon, and HT22 cell were performed using RIPA lysis buffer and BCA reagent. Subsequently, Western blotting analysis was conducted as previously described56. Based on the molecular weights of the proteins, different concentrations of SDS-PAGE gels were selected for protein separation, after which the separated proteins were transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were blocked with non-fat milk at room temperature for 1 to 1.5 h. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody and subsequently incubated at room temperature for 1 h with the secondary antibody. Finally, proteins were detected using an ECL reagent kit and exposed to a Syngene chemi-Imaging system (MD, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from the hippocampus, colon tissues of mice and HT22 cell utilizing TRNzol Universal Reagent. The isolated RNA was transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using HiFiScript gDNA Removal RT Master Mix. Real-time quantitative PCR was then conducted using 2×SuperFast Universal SYBR Master Mix and LightCycler 480 II PCR System (Roche, CH). The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal reference control. The primer sequences for qRT-PCR were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Microbial community profiling-16S rRNA gene sequencing

In the 8th (only individual AGEs intervention was carried out in the first 8 weeks) and 12th (CRS intervention was added in weeks 9 to 12) weeks of the experiment, fresh fecal samples from the mice were collected. These samples were quickly placed in sterile EP tubes and rapidly frozen on dry ice to maintain sample stability. All samples were stored at −80 °C to ensure the integrity of the DNA and the accuracy of subsequent analyses. Subsequently, after DNA extraction, fecal microbiota analysis was performed using paired-end 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform. The total DNA from the fecal samples was extracted using the GHFDE100 DNA Isolation Kit. After quantification, the extracted DNA was amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) to increase the copy number of the 16S rRNA gene for subsequent high-throughput sequencing. QIIME2 (Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2) and the R software package (version 3.2.0) were used for bioinformatics analysis of the amplified 16S rRNA gene sequences. First, the sequencing data were preprocessed using the QIIME software package, including the removal of low-quality sequences and assembly. Then, representative sequences were selected from each operational taxonomic unit (OTU) for species annotation. This step used the RDP classifier and the SILVA138 database for BLAST search to identify the taxonomic information of the microbes. To assess microbial diversity, this study employed α-diversity, β-diversity, and species richness analyses. The α-diversity indices, such as Chao1, ACE, Shannon, and Simpson indices, were used to measure the microbial diversity within individual samples. The β-diversity analysis, through the calculation of the UniFrac distance matrix and weighted UniFrac principal coordinate analysis (PCoA), revealed the differences in microbial communities between different samples. Additionally, this study also used PICRUSt (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) for gene function prediction analysis. PICRUSt predicted the possible metabolic pathways and functional genes in the microbial community through a reference database, thus providing a deeper understanding of the functions of the microbial community.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM and analyzed with the SPSS version 21 statistical analysis package. The Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene test were utilized to respectively verify the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Comparisons between different groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s or Tukey’s test for multiple comparisons. When non-parametric testing was required, the Kruskal-Wallis method was used for analysis, with Dunn’s method applied for post-hoc tests. T-tests were used for gut microbiota analysis at week 8. P < 0.05 was considered significant. Furthermore, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was applied to assess correlations between significantly altered gut microbiota profiles and behavioral tests and TRP metabolites, and to analyze the correlation between AGEs content in the diet and TRP metabolites, as well as the markers reflecting the iron metabolism level in the hippocampus.
