Vancomycin-induced Immune Hemolytic Anemia – A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Yilin Song, Meher Ali, Rukhsar Shaikh, Ashley Bersma, Harsh Y. Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia (DIIHA) is a rare complication of drug treatment. The clinical presentation involves rapid dropping of hemoglobin secondary to hemolysis leading to potentially fatal consequences. Over 100 drugs have been reported to cause DIIHA, most commonly cephalosporins. Here, we present a case of vancomycin-induced immune hemolytic anemia. The diagnosis was challenging due to the limited reports of hemolytic anemia induced by vancomycin in the setting of multiple antibiotic administration history. Hemolysis improved after vancomycin cessation and diagnosis was made with positive serological confirmation. Literature review was performed with four reported cases of vancomycin-induced immune hemolytic anemia identified and summarized. Our goal is to promote awareness of vancomycin as an uncommon cause of DIIHA for better management in clinical practice.

Introduction

Drug-induced immune hemolytic anemia (DIIHA) is a rare but potentially fatal complication of drug treatment. The prevalence of DIIHA was estimated to be 1–4 cases per million population per year caused by all medications worldwide. DIIHA accounted for approximately 12–18 % of immune related hemolytic anemia.1–4 Mortality among large sample publications was up to 23 % resulting from severe hemolysis or multi-organ failure.5 Common mechanisms of DIIHA are immune complexes formation, drug-adsorption of red blood cell (RBC) leading to IgG sensitization, RBC autoantibodies produced by drugs, and RBC membrane modification leading to non-immunological binding with drugs.6

Clinical presentation of DIIHA includes hemolysis after administration of the culpable drug, with a rapid drop in hemoglobin, and improvement after cessation of the drug. Diagnosis should be made with positive serological tests for drug-dependent antibodies and antiglobulin tests.4 Drugs which commonly reported inducing DIIHA shifted from methyldopa and penicillin in the 1970s to cefotetan and ceftriaxone in the recent decades.7 The number of drugs reported with DIIHA expanded rapidly to over 100 with serological evidence of antibodies detection.4,5,8

Vancomycin is an antibiotic widely used as part of empiric and/or targeted regimen for treatment of infections caused by Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). While thrombocytopenia induced by vancomycin has been reported and summarized previously,9 only a handful of cases of vancomycin-induced DIIHA are found in the literature. Here we present a case of vancomycin-induced immune hemolytic anemia with serological confirmation, together with a review of the previous reported cases.

Case report

A 58-year-old male was admitted due to severe right-hand injury and right leg pain after falling on the bathroom floor. Medical history was significant for hypertension, prostate cancer, hepatitis C infection, heart failure, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, ascending thoracic aorta aneurysm, and severe bilateral knee osteoarthritis status post right total knee replacement 10 years before admission. He did not have any known medication allergy history. It was unclear if he had been exposed to vancomycin previously. The patient was febrile and hypotensive on admission. The right knee was swollen and there was an open wound with signs of infection on the second right finger. Computed tomography scans of the right upper and lower extremities showed right knee joint deformation and effusion without any signs of osteomyelitis. Infectious disease was consulted who recommended orthopedic evaluation of the knee with source being potential hardware related infection of the knee or cellulitis. As per orthopedic surgery, surgical intervention was not indicated given there was no joint space to wash out and removal of fusion nail being a morbid and potentially catastrophic surgery. On admission, patient was started on vancomycin, cefepime and metronidazole along with intravenous fluids. On day 3, blood cultures returned positive for MRSA and patient was planned for 6 weeks of intravenous antibiotics for presumed hardware infection/septic arthritis. Regimen was changed to daptomycin monotherapy for ease of dosing/administration. However, due to lack of insurance coverage, patient was switched back to vancomycin on day 5. On day 11 (6 days after restarting vancomycin), the patient developed hematuria and generalized body pain. Scleral icterus, abdominal tenderness, and agitation were present on exam. There was an acute drop in hemoglobin. Indirect bilirubin and creatinine were elevated while haptoglobin was decreased. Laboratory values on day 1 and day 11 are compared in Table 1. Further workup revealed an elevated reticulocyte count and the presence of normochromic and normocytic RBCs, smudge cells, schistocytes, stomatocytes, pappenheimer bodies, and Howell Jolly bodies on peripheral smear. Direct Coombs test was positive and there was strong rouleaux formation at immediate spin crossmatch and at 37 °C, concerning for warm autoimmune hemolytic anemia. Prednisone was started; however, the patient did not respond and continued to have worsening hemolysis. On day 13, vancomycin was switched to ceftaroline. Hemoglobin nadir was 4.2 g/dL, thus our patient received 2 units of pRBC transfusion. He received methylprednisolone 1.8 mg/kg/day for 5 days followed by a 7-day taper course. Intravenous immunoglobin 400 mg/kg was also given for 5 days. Hemoglobin stabilized and the patient was discharged on day 30 with a hemoglobin of 13.6 g/dL. Vancomycin-dependent RBC antibodies were detected with the assistance of ARUP National Reference Laboratory through immuno-hematology serology and drug dependent RBC antibody study which ultimately confirmed the suspected diagnosis of vancomycin-induced hemolytic anemia.

Discussion and systemic review

DIIHA is a rare cause of hemolytic anemia presenting with sudden onset of hemoglobin drop after the administration of the culpable drug. First reported in 1953,10 the spectrum of drugs inducing DIIHA has expanded rapidly over the years.7 The most common drugs were methyldopa and penicillin in the 1970s, which shifted to cefotetan and ceftriaxone in recent decades.7 Subsequently, up to over 100 drugs have been reported with DIIHA with serological evidence of antibodies detection.4,5,8

To explore the previous reported cases of vancomycin-induced immune hemolysis anemia, literature review with the keywords of “Vancomycin,” “Drug-induced immune hemolysis anemia,” “Red blood cell,” “Hemolysis,” and “Hemolytic anemia” was performed using the databases of Google Scholar, PubMed, Ovid Medline, ScienceDirect, Scopus, and Cochrane Library. No time limitation was applied. In total, four previously reported cases were identified as vancomycin-induced immune hemolytic anemia.

The first case of vancomycin-induced hemolytic anemia was reported in 2003, where in-vitro antibody tests were performed among patients with AIDS who developed hemolytic anemia after drug exposure.11 Unfortunately, the detailed clinical information was not included. The other three cases were reported in the past 6 years with an indication of joint infection, which is the same as our case.12–14 Patients were exposed to either intravenous vancomycin or intraarticular spacers impregnated with different doses of vancomycin. The onset time of hemolysis was 3–14 days after vancomycin exposure, with nadir hemoglobin of 5.0–5.7 g/dL. Hemolysis improved after surgical removal of vancomycin spacer or cessation of intravenous vancomycin. The diagnosis was made with detection of vancomycin-dependent antibodies only in one of the three cases, while diagnosis was made with clinical presentations in the other two cases (Table 2). Our case shared similarities with the previous reported cases in terms of vancomycin indication, hemolysis onset time and clinical improvement after cessation of vancomycin. The diagnosis in our case was made with vancomycin-dependent antibodies detected on RBCs.

There are four mechanisms of DIIHA. The first mechanism was the formation of immune complex, where the drug acts as a hapten binding to RBC through drug-sensitive antibody, which leads to RBC destruction.15 This “immune complex” theory was proved in multiple DIIHA cases.16 Another theory of DIIHA is “drug-adsorption” where drug-induced non-immunological adsorption of proteins onto RBCs, leading to IgG sensitized RBCs with the presentation of the drug, and subsequently destructed by macrophages. This mechanism was first identified with penicillin and was later found among other drug families.3,17 The third mechanism was that some drugs produce RBC autoantibodies which leads to AIHA.18 The fourth mechanism, “membrane modification,” suggested some drugs affect RBC membrane which enable nonimmunological protein bounding to RBCs leading to AIHA.19 With the detection of vancomycin-dependent antibodies, the mechanism of the current case could be the “immune complex” theory.

Diagnosis of DIIHA includes clinical presentation of acute intravascular hemolysis after receiving the drug. Patients could have a history of receiving the drug without symptoms in the past and present with drug-induced antibody in the circulation leading to acute hemolysis presentation. Most reported cases of DIIHA were based on the occurrence of hemolytic anemia after drug administration and subsequent resolution after cessation of the drug. However, a positive serologic test is required for diagnostic confirmation.6 Unfortunately, two out of four previously reported cases were diagnosed by clinical course without confirmatory serologic test. In the current case, diagnosis was made by positive IgG on direct Coombs test and ultimately confirmed with the assistance of ARUP National Reference Laboratory.

To date, there is no efficient way of predicting antibodies to certain drugs as prevention of DIIHA. With the potentially fatal consequences of DIIHA, it is suggested that clinicians should be alert with the common drugs causing DIIHA. We presented this case together with the summary of previous reported cases to promote awareness of vancomycin as a rare cause of DIIHA given the widespread usage of the antibiotic. Diagnosis with serologic testing is recommended. The confirmatory antibody report in our case was available after 11 days. Therefore, when DIIHA is suspected, early recognition and stopping of the suspicious drug is crucial in ensuring a better clinical outcome.
