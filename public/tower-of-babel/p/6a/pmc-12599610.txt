Motor Unit Loss and Remodeling in Patients With Chronic Radiculopathy Using the EMG Filling Analysis
Authors: Javier Rodriguez‐Falces, Mathew Piasecki, Silvia Recalde‐Villamayor, Cristina Mariscal, Armando Malanda, Javier Navallas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsThe way in which the EMG signal is progressively filled with motor unit potentials during increasing voluntary contraction provides valuable information about the motor units (MUs) in a muscle. Here, we demonstrate the application of the EMG filling method to detect MU loss and remodeling in patients with chronic radiculopathy.MethodsConcentric needle EMG signals were recorded from the tibialis anterior of 19 radiculopathy patients and 18 healthy controls during voluntary ramped contractions increasing from 0 to maximum. The EMG filling process was analyzed by measuring the EMG filling factor and EMG amplitude during the contraction.ResultsThe way in which the filling factor increased with increasing force differed (p < 0.001) between healthy controls (exponential filling curve) and radiculopathy patients (linear curve). The extent to which the EMG envelope amplitude increased throughout the ramp contraction was markedly greater (p < 0.001) in healthy controls (6.1‐fold increase) compared to patients (0.5‐fold increase). The filling factor at maximal effort was significantly higher in healthy controls than in patients (0.53 ± 0.04 vs. 0.45 ± 0.09, p < 0.001). The EMG filling analysis revealed clear signs of MU loss and remodeling in 85% of patients.DiscussionThe EMG filling analysis is a fast and accessible approach to detect MU loss in radiculopathy patients. Moreover, it provides information about the MU remodeling process and the type of motor units that are predominantly lost in the neurogenic condition.Keywords: concentric needle, EMG filling, EMG interference pattern, filling factor, motor unit loss, motor unit remodeling, radiculopathy

Introduction

The loss of motor units (MUs) occurring in neurogenic diseases [1, 2, 3] is normally accompanied by the expansion of individual motor units (MU remodeling) [4, 5]. The electromyography (EMG) interference pattern recorded by needle electrodes is a widespread tool to study MU loss [6]. The reduction in MU numbers can be qualitatively assessed by observing the “fullness” of the EMG interference pattern recorded at maximal effort [7]. The result is listed in qualitative terms as a “full”, “reduced”, “incomplete”, or “discrete” pattern. The drawback of this qualitative visual evaluation of the EMG interference pattern is that it relies on the subjectivity of the physician. Moreover, the EMG interference pattern recorded at a single force level provides limited information on MU remodeling.

To obtain a more comprehensive picture of MU loss and remodeling, we study how the EMG signal is progressively filled with motor unit potentials (MUPs) as muscle force is gradually increased from rest to maximum (Figure S1). This filling process can be studied using a new analysis tool called the filling factor [8]. This index quantifies the degree to which an EMG segment is filled, and its value normally increases as volitional force is increased (Figure S1). The resulting filling curves may be useful to discriminate between normal and pathologic muscles. Filling factor analysis has been performed only in healthy individuals [9]. The filling factor is the inverse of the square of the form factor, an index introduced by Bostock and colleagues [10] to interpret motor unit number index (MUNIX) results, and subsequently utilized by Nandedkar and collaborators [11] to detect MU loss.

The shape of the EMG filling curve is mostly determined by: (1) recruitment of motor units according to their size with increasing force, (2) distribution of MU sizes, and (3) MU number. Because the distribution of MU sizes and MU number in each muscle is similar in healthy young individuals, in these subjects the filling curve is mainly determined by the first factor, namely Henneman's Size Principle [12]. Thus, the filling curves exhibit a similar (exponential) profile in healthy young subjects [9, 13, 14].

In patients with neurogenic disorders, it is reasonable to expect an alteration in the distribution of MU sizes relevant to their recruitment threshold due to two main processes. Firstly, a reduction in MU number, with a preferential loss of the larger, later recruited motor units [15, 16]. Second, remodeling of surviving MUs, with a conversion of MUs to those innervated by smaller motor neurons and containing more fatigue‐resistant muscle fibers [17]. Together, these two processes lead to a homogenization of MU sizes in neurogenic conditions, with the expectation that the filling curves would be altered compared to those of healthy individuals.

The objective of this study was to apply the EMG filling analysis to patients with radiculopathy and healthy subjects to explore differences in the filling curves between them.

Methods

Participants

We recruited patients with symptoms (pain, loss of strength or loss of sensation) in the lumbar region (L4‐L5‐S1) who attended the Clinical Neurophysiology Service of Navarre, between 2023 and 2025. Healthy controls were recruited as well. All patients underwent routine EMG and nerve conduction studies, as well as EMG filling studies, whereas the healthy controls underwent EMG filling studies only. All study participants provided written informed consent. The studies were approved by the medical ethics committee of the University Hospital and Reina Sofía Hospital of Navarre, Spain.

Tests Performed and Inclusion Criteria

Routine needle EMG analysis of the tibialis anterior and MRI of the lumbar spine were performed. The inclusion criteria were patients showing neurogenic MUPs (increased amplitude and duration) and L5 root involvement on MRI. The selected patients subsequently performed the EMG filling test.

EMG Filling Test

During the test, participants were comfortably seated on a stretcher‐bed with extended legs, with a trunk‐thigh angle of around 100°. Participants performed isometric ankle dorsiflexion by pushing the foot against the examiner's hand. They were asked to perform a ramp contraction of 15 s, during which force was increased progressively from 0 to maximum. The 15 s ramp contraction was repeated five times, with 1 min of rest between contractions. The concentric needle electrode was repositioned between contractions to ensure sampling of different MU populations. The force of contraction was not measured, but the electrophysiologist provided verbal guidance.

Recording Details for the EMG Filling Test

EMG signals were recorded using a 38 mm long concentric needle with a 0.07 mm2 recording surface (Ambu, Ballerup, Denmark), inserted into two different sites of the muscle belly. At each insertion point, the needle was repositioned after each ramp contraction by two actions: withdrawing the needle tip by around 3–5 mm and also rotating the bevel 180°. Intramuscular EMG signals were bandpass‐filtered (20 Hz to 10 KHz) and digitized (sampling frequency 20 KHz). The data were collected using a Medelec Synergy EMG machine (Viasys Healthcare, Surrey, UK).

EMG Filling Analysis

The filling factor is an indicator that objectively quantifies the degree to which an EMG segment is filled with MUPs. Its value is determined by the number and amplitudes of the MUPs in the EMG segment and their firing frequencies [8]. The filling factor was defined as the ratio of the square of the mean rectified EMG (MR EMG) to the square of the root mean square EMG (RMS EMG) [8].

The EMG filling curve was obtained by calculating the filling factor over successive non‐overlapping segments of the EMG signal, with a segment duration of 0.75 s [13]. Then, the filling factor values were plotted against the MR EMG amplitude, as done previously [11]. The filling factor was studied against MR EMG, instead of as a function of force because force monitoring adds complexity and is not always accessible [18].

The degree of EMG filling at the onset of the contraction was calculated as the average filling factor from the first two 0.75‐s epochs of the EMG signal (~ first 1.5 s). The degree of EMG filling at the end of the contraction (FFmax) was estimated as the average of the last two filling factor values (~ last 1.5 s). Each of these variables was averaged for all the repetitions performed by each participant. We proposed that the EMG interference pattern was full when the FFmax value was higher than 0.5 [9, 13].

We also assessed whether the shape of the filling factor (FF) curve approximated more closely an exponential or a linear shape (Figures 1a4 and 2b4). The filling curve shape was assessed as follows: First, the straight line between the start of the filling curve (average of the first two filling factor values) and the end of the filling curve (average of the last two filling factor values) was calculated. Second, the distance from each filling factor value of the curve to the straight line was computed (note that these distances could be positive or negative). Third, the sum of the distances (with signs) was calculated and referred to as “distance‐to‐linearity”. The distance‐to‐linearity threshold used to differentiate between an exponential and a linear filling curve shape was 1.5. Below this threshold, the filling curve was considered linear.

In the healthy participant, the EMG signal can be roughly divided into 2 sequential parts: An initial portion comprising small MUPs (black solid line in plot a1) and a second portion containing much larger MUPs (red dashed line in plot a1). In the first EMG portion, the successive recruitment of small‐amplitude MUPs caused the filling factor to increase markedly and rapidly from 0.1 to ~0.5 (y‐axis in plot a2), while it produced a modest increase in the rectified EMG amplitude (x‐axis in plot a2). An EMG amplitude analysis was also carried out. The EMG envelope amplitude and MR EMG amplitude were computed at the beginning and end of the contraction (average of the first two and last two 0.75‐s EMG epochs, respectively), as per [19]. The percentage increase in the EMG envelope amplitude from the beginning to the end of the contraction was also computed. Each of these variables was averaged for all the repetitions performed by each participant.

To enrich the analysis, our patients and controls were classified into four groups based on the combinations of two parameters: FFmax (whether this index was > 0.5 or < 0.5), and the filling curve shape (exponential or linear). Namely, group 1 (FFmax > 0.5 and exponential FF curve), group 2 (FFmax < 0.5, exponential FF curve), group 3 (FFmax > 0.5, linear FF curve), and group 4 (FFmax < 0.5, linear FF curve).

Statistics

Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests confirmed that each variable analyzed in the current study was normally distributed (p < 0.05). Differences in how the filling factor increased with increasing muscle force were investigated with a two‐way repeated‐measures ANOVA [contraction force (20%, 40%, 60%, 80%, and 100% of MR EMGmax) × participant condition (healthy, patient)]. Differences in the studied variables between patients and controls (filling factor, EMG envelope amplitude, and MR EMG amplitude) were examined using a one‐way ANOVA. When main effects or interactions were significant, Student–Newman–Keuls post hoc tests were conducted. For significant results, the Bonferroni adjusted pairwise test was used for post hoc testing. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

Patient Group Information

There were 26 patients (average age 46.3 ± 7.2 years (range 31–55)), and 25 healthy subjects (average age 43.8 ± 4.3 years (range 32–53)).

During the routine needle EMG analysis, morphologic MUP changes (increased MUP amplitude and duration) were found in 23 of 26 patients. Polyphasic MUPs were observed in 13 of these 23 patients. MRI findings showed evidence of L5 root involvement in 19 of these 23 patients (82%). After performing the EMG and MRI tests, 19 patients were diagnosed with L5 radiculopathy. The average duration of symptoms in these patients was 10 months, range 3–15 months.

Representative EMG Signals and Filling Factor Values

Figure 1 shows typical examples of EMG signals recorded from a healthy participant (plot a3) and a radiculopathy patient (plot b3) during the ramp contraction. For each participant, a detail of the initial EMG activity (plots a1 and b1) and final EMG activity (plots a2 and b2) is depicted at the top, whereas the filling factor values are shown at the bottom (plots a4, b4).

In the healthy participant, the amplitude of the EMG envelope increased progressively during the ramp contraction (plot a3), whereas in the neurogenic participant, the envelope amplitude remained roughly constant throughout the contraction. Note that in the healthy control, MUPs recruited at low forces (plot a1) had amplitudes approximately one‐tenth of those recruited at high forces (plot a2); whereas in the neurogenic participant, many of the MUPs activated at low forces (plot b1) had amplitudes comparable to the largest MUPs recorded at high forces (b2).

In the healthy participant (plot a4), the filling factor increased rapidly with force up to ~25% EMGmax, and then leveled off for higher forces, thus leading to an “exponential” filling curve. In contrast, in the patient (plot b4), the filling factor increased at the same rate throughout the whole force range, thus yielding a “linear” filling curve. Note that FFmax was above the 0.5 threshold in the healthy subject, but below 0.5 in the patient.

The Shape of the Filling Factor Curve Is Related to the Envelope of the EMG Signal

The next step is to show that the shape of the filling factor curve is intrinsically related to the shape of the EMG envelope amplitude during the ramp contraction (Figure 2). To illustrate this, in the healthy participant, let us divide the EMG signal into 2 sequential portions (Figure 2a1): a first portion comprising MUPs of small amplitude (black solid line in plot a1), and a second portion containing much larger MUPs (red dashed line). This first EMG portion generated the initial steep part of the filling curve, whereas the second EMG portion gave rise to the “flat” portion of the filling curve. In contrast, in the radiculopathy patient the EMG envelope had only one “amplitude level” (Figure 2b1). Thus, as force was increased, the successively recruited MUPs contributed to fill the EMG at this amplitude level (plot b1), thus leading to a linear increase in the filling factor (plot b2).

Discussion

This study shows the utility of the EMG filling analysis to distinguish between patients with radiculopathy and healthy controls.

Differences in the EMG Envelope Curve Between Healthy vs. Neurogenic Subjects

We found a large increase in EMG envelope amplitude from the onset to the end of the contraction in all healthy participants. This was mainly due to larger MUs being recruited as force was increased, although other factors, such as increasing summation of MUP waveforms due to higher firing rates, also contributed [20]. Indeed, for some healthy individuals, it was observed that some MUPs recruited at high forces were remarkably larger than at low forces. This large difference in MUP amplitude may indicate that healthy participants contain MUs that vary greatly in size: that is, the innervation ratio and fiber diameter of some high‐threshold MUs may be considerably larger than those of low‐threshold MUs [21].

By contrast, we found that in most radiculopathy patients the EMG envelope amplitude remained roughly constant throughout the ramp contraction. The lack of a marked increase in EMG envelope amplitude is attributed to MU loss and remodeling, which could be mediated by two distinct processes. First, the preferential loss of large motor units, an observation made in polio patients [15], ALS patients [16] and ALS mouse studies [17]. The prevailing hypothesis behind the loss of larger MUs is that these units are metabolically demanding, making them more susceptible to damage [22]. The second mechanism is the ability of small nerve fibers to sprout to form enlarged MUs, as observed in a mouse model with ALS [17, 23]. This may favor an activity‐dependent conversion of motor units to those innervated by smaller motor neurons [17]. In summary, many of the largest high‐threshold motor units were lost, while many of the surviving small units were enlarged, as occurs with aging [24]. This MU remodeling gave rise to a “homogenization” in the size of MUs, which explains the minimal increase in the EMG envelope throughout the contraction.

Differences in the EMG Filling Curve Between Healthy vs. Neurogenic Individuals

We found striking differences in the shape of the filling curve between healthy (exponential) and neurogenic (linear) individuals. The “exponential” shape of the filling curve in healthy participants was the result of the high heterogeneity of MU sizes in these individuals. As illustrated in Figure 2, the early recruited small‐amplitude MUPs generate the initial steep portion of the filling curve, whereas the large‐amplitude MUPs recruited at higher forces yield the final plateau of the filling curve. The presence of such a long plateau in the filling curve can only be the result of the recruitment of increasingly larger MUPs. This was substantiated in our previous study where we showed that, for the final plateau to appear, the later recruited MUPs should be 8–12 times larger than the early recruited MUPs [25]. Therefore, a wide range (heterogeneity) of MU sizes must exist to generate an exponential filling curve with a final plateau.

The “linear” shape of the filling curve observed in 85% of patients was due to MU loss and remodeling. The loss of the largest type II motor units was suggested by the absence of the final plateau in the curve. Homogenization of MU sizes explains the linear increase of the filling factor, as explained next. In the neurogenic condition, some of the MUPs recruited at the beginning of the contraction had already “large” amplitudes due to reinnervation. As force increased, the successively recruited MUPs were smaller, equal to, or slightly larger than these initial large‐amplitude MUPs. Therefore, as force was increased, the rectified EMG amplitude increased because the successively recruited MUPs progressively filled the baseline, and not due to the appearance of successively larger‐amplitude MUPs. As a result, the filling factor and rectified EMG increased in parallel, thus yielding a linear filling curve.

Classification of Healthy Controls and Patients Based on the EMG Filling Analysis

Classifying the subjects into four distinct groups helped to distinguish the cases with “no”, “moderate” and “severe” MU loss and remodeling. As expected, most patients (73%) fell into group 4 (FFmax < 0.5 and linear FF curve). Most large type‐II motor units were likely lost in these patients, as suggested by the absence of a final plateau in the filling curve. Moreover, the very low FFmax values encountered, together with the slight increase in EMG envelope and the low values of MR EMGmax, suggested severe losses of motor units and a homogenization of the size of the surviving units.

Interestingly, a few patients (12%) were found to have the group‐3 profile (FFmax > 0.5 and linear FF curve). The fact that FFmax > 0.5 may incorrectly lead to the conclusion that MU numbers were preserved. However, the absence of a final flat portion in the filling curve, combined with the slight increase in EMG envelope, indicated that most large type II motor units were lost. Therefore, it is likely that, although the large type II motor units were lost, the surviving units were sufficient in number to completely fill the baseline. Thus, for these few patients, we propose a moderate “selective” MU loss and remodeling.

Finally, there were a few patients (13%) with group‐1 characteristics (FFmax > 0.5 and exponential filling curve). Although these patients showed signs of radiculopathy (i.e., augmented “neurogenic” MUPs), the MU numbers seemed to be preserved.

Conclusion

In this study, we showed that the EMG filling analysis disclosed key significant differences between healthy controls and patients with radiculopathy, which helped to detect scenarios of MU loss and remodeling. In healthy individuals, the exponential shape of the filling curve and the large increase in EMG envelope indicated preservation of MU number and a large heterogeneity in MU size. In radiculopathy patients, the linear shape of the filling curve and the slight increase in EMG envelope suggested loss of the largest motor units, together with a homogenization of the size of the remaining MUs (MU remodeling). The EMG filling analysis offers a more comprehensive assessment of the neurogenic condition as it provides information on MU number, MU remodeling, and the type of motor units that are predominantly lost.
