High-density lipoprotein functionality in cholesterol efflux in early childhood is related to the content ratio of triglyceride to cholesterol
Authors: Akiko Futatsugi, Minoru Tozuka, Yuna Horiuchi, Ryunosuke Ohkawa, Tomoki Kosho
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCholesterol efflux capacity (CEC), commonly measured as a useful risk marker of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, depends on high-density lipoprotein (HDL) functionality and its concentration. We defined the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux, not influenced by HDL concentration, as the ratio of measured CEC to standardized CEC (stCEC) based on HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C) of each individual using the curve regression equation obtained from the correlation. HDL-C, CEC, and CEC/stCEC levels in the < 28-day-old participants (neonates) were significantly low compared to those of the ≥ 28-day-old participants, indicating that the low CEC levels in the neonates depend on not only lower HDL-C but also lower HDL functionality. The low level of CEC/stCEC was remarkable in neonates born at < 34 weeks of gestation and did not improved to the reference level (1.000) until the infantile period. The relatively low or high CEC/stCEC ratios in neonates and infants were associated with lower or higher HDL-TG and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio, respectively. However, no apparent effect of HDL-TG and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio on CEC/stCEC was observed in the ≥ 1-year-old participants, indicating that HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux could be associated with the various HDL particles with various lipid compositions, but not just with HDL-TG and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74699-5.Keywords: Cholesterol efflux capacity, High-density lipoprotein triglyceride, Gestational weeks, Childhood, Reverse cholesterol transportSubject terms: Biochemistry, Biomarkers

Introduction

High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is widely known to have the potential to prevent the development of atherosclerotic diseases such as cardiovascular disease (CVD)1. For over four decades, HDL-cholesterol (HDL-C) has been commonly measured as an indicator of the amount of HDL in clinical laboratories. Several drugs have been developed to increase HDL-C levels. However, almost none of these drugs have achieved the expected efficacy in the prevention of CVD development2–6, indicating that HDL functionality is important for estimating its anti-atherosclerotic ability. HDL plays several anti-atherosclerotic roles, including reverse cholesterol transport (RCT)7, antioxidant8, anti-inflammatory9, antithrombotic10, and anti-infection effects11. In these RCT, the transfer of cholesterol from foam cells in atherosclerotic plaques to the liver, followed by excretion in feces7, was suggested to have an extremely significant anti-atherosclerotic function. In the last decade, the cholesterol efflux capacity (CEC) of HDL, the first step in RCT, has been widely evaluated as a negative risk marker for CVD development12–14. Cardiovascular risk can be reduced by up to 67% in the highest quartile of CEC compared with the lowest quartile13. In addition, many recent reports have described the relationship between HDL function, including that of CEC, and various diseases. Chronic kidney disease15,16 and diabetes mellitus (DM)17,18 are known to be associated with elevated CVD morbidity and mortality rates. Patients receiving hemodialysis have reduced HDL-mediated CEC19. In contrast, DM affects CEC by changing the HDL composition20. Autoimmune diseases21 and cancer22 are considered to be related to the functions of HDL, including CEC. Studies have also described CEC in children. CEC, but not HDL-C, shows a significant negative correlation with adiposity during early childhood23. Obese children and adolescents show qualitative alterations in HDL levels, which could cause premature CVD24. Furthermore, it has been hypothesized that under nutrition in utero and during infancy, associated with infant growth, affects the development of coronary heart disease in adult life25,26. These observations give rise to an interest in whether the CEC of HDL in childhood, including neonates, plays a role as a predictor of CVD in later life. CEC might be a clinically useful biomarker for estimating the risk of CVD in not only adults but also children. However, the general measurement methods of CEC do not faithfully reflect HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux, because those depend on HDL concentration. Almost no study aimed at the HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux has been reported.

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux in children, including neonates and adolescents, expecting a dramatic change in the concentration and lipids composition of HDL. Apolipoprotein B-depleted serum (BDS), prepared from serum sample by the precipitation method13, was commonly used for CEC assay at 2.0% concentration as a serum. It means that BDS samples obtained from the participants contain various concentrations of HDL. The CEC obtained using BDS depends on both of HDL functionality and concentration. HDL is the complex consisting of many kinds of proteins and lipids and its concentration is universally expressed as HDL-C in clinical. To compensate the effect of various concentrations of HDL in BDS on CEC, the most suitable curve regression line (equation) was derived from the correlation between HDL-C and CEC obtained from 239 participants. The curve regression line indicates the standard CEC (stCEC) for each HDL-C concentration and the area above or below the line means to be higher or lower than the stCEC, respectively. The stCEC of each participant was obtained using the curve regression equation, indicating that CEC/stCEC ratio more or less than 1.000 means to be located in the area above or below the curve regression line, respectively. Consequently, CEC/stCEC ratio was defined as the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux not affected by HDL-C concentration. The factors affecting HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (CEC/stCEC ratio) were investigated.

Results

Validation of the CEC assay

The coefficients of variation (CVs [%]) of repeatability (intra-assay) and reproducibility (inter-assay) of the two samples were 2.6–5.5% and 1.6–6.3%, respectively. Samples containing more than 3.0 mg/dL of conjugated bilirubin were excluded because of their significant effect on CEC measurement. However, the effect of up to 11.6 mg/dL unconjugated bilirubin, which was the maximum value measured in the samples used, was within the range of measurement accuracy described above (Supplementary Fig. S1). This indicates that the CEC assay used in this study is applicable to samples obtained from neonates.

Definition of the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux

CEC revealed a strong positive correlation with HDL-C (R = 0.778). However, the correlation diagram clearly showed a non-proportional relationship, indicating that the CEC/HDL-C ratio is not suitable for evaluating HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (Fig. 1a). Therefore, a standardized CEC (stCEC) based on the HDL-C value of each participant was calculated using a curve regression equation (stCEC = 0.2475 × HDL-C0.3471) to fit the correlation diagram (R = 0.797). The ratio of measured CEC to stCEC (CEC/stCEC) in each participant was then defined as the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux, meaning that ratios above or below 1.000 (the reference level) indicated a relatively high or low HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux, respectively (Fig. 1b).

Comparison of CEC levels, CEC/stCEC ratios, and HDL lipids among four different age groups

CEC, CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-triglyceride (HDL-TG), and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio values obtained from the 239 samples were each compared among four groups divided by age (< 28-day-old, 28-day-old–3-month-old, 4–11-month-old, and ≥ 1-year-old groups) (Fig. 2). The CEC, CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio values of the < 28-day-old participants (neonates) were significantly lower than those of the other three age groups (Fig. 2a–e). Similar comparisons were carried out among the four age groups stratified by HDL-C levels, i.e., < 50 and ≥ 50 mg/dL, considering the average HDL-C concentration obtained from the 239 participants. Significantly low levels of CEC, CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio were observed in the < 28-day-old group with < 50 mg/dL HDL-C compared to those of the other three age groups (Fig. 2f–j); however, no significant difference was observed among the values from the four age groups with ≥ 50 mg/dL HDL-C (Fig. 2k–o).

Age-related distribution of CEC, CEC/stCEC, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and HDL-TG/HDL-C

Relationships between age and CEC, CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio were also investigated (Fig. 3). The average CEC at birth was 0.739 and increased to approximately the reference level (1.000) by 1 month of age; then, the reference level was maintained. HDL-C and HDL-TG levels showed similar fluctuations; they gradually increased during the neonatal period (< 28-day-old) and were maintained even after 1 year of age. The regression lines of the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratios were almost horizontal for all age groups; however, those of < 28-day-old participants were slightly lower than those of the ≥ 28-day-old participants.

Weak negative correlations between the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio and CEC were observed in all age groups, and the correlation coefficients gradually increased with increasing age (Fig. 5i–l).

Discussion

Validation of the CEC assay indicated its suitability for evaluating CEC in children. This method was used to measure the CEC in neonates with high serum levels of unconjugated bilirubin.

As expected, CEC showed a high positive correlation with HDL-C since apolipoprotein B-100 depleted serum (BDS) contains an amount of HDL proportional to that of serum. This indicates that the CEC obtained using BDS results from a comprehensive cholesterol efflux induced by the combination of HDL functionality and concentration. In addition, the correlation diagram was non-proportional, indicating that the CEC/HDL-C ratio was not suitable for estimating HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux. The ratio of the measured CEC to the standardized CEC (stCEC) in each individual was defined as the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux. This method could be reliable for evaluating the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux independent HDL-C level, because of no correlation between HDL-C and CEC/stCEC ratio, and for elucidating the factors affecting it, even though this was a single-institution study.

All CEC, the CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio were significantly lower in the < 28-day-old group (neonates) compared to all other participants (aged ≥ 28 days). These results indicate that low CEC levels in neonates were caused by both lower HDL-C concentrations and lower HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (CEC/stCEC). However, these differences were only observed in the participants with < 50 mg/dL HDL-C. HDL maturation is suppressed by the lower activity of lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT) in early neonates27, indicating the lower level of HDL-C in the < 28-day-old group including 25 participants aged ≤ 3 days out of 41 participants. Cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP), which catalyzes the exchange of cholesteryl ester in HDL and TG in apolipoprotein B-containing TG-rich lipoproteins, is also low in the neonates28. In this study, serum TG in the < 28-day-old group was significantly low compared to other three age groups (P < 0.001). In addition to low CETP activity, the low HDL-C and serum TG (meaning low TG-rich lipoproteins) as the substrates of CETP could be a cause of the low HDL-TG. These would induce the lower HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio in the < 28-day-old group compared to other three age groups. The increase in the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio from the neonatal to the infantile period could contribute to an increase in HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (CEC/stCEC). No significant difference in CEC, the CEC/stCEC ratio, HDL-C, HDL-TG, and the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio among four age groups with ≥ 50 mg/dL HDL-C would be caused by age composition in the < 28-day-old group, 13–27-day-old.

The comparison between the participants with < 50 mg/dL and ≥ 50 mg/dL HDL-C in each of the four age groups indicated that CEC/stCEC does not depend on HDL-C level, except the result of the 28-day-old–3-month-old group. The significantly low CEC/stCEC of the participants with ≥ 50 mg/dL HDL-C in the 28-day-old–3-month-old group and the change in the subject with the significant difference from HDL-TG to HDL-TG/HDL-C between neonatal and infantile period could indicate a dramatic change in lipid profile and size of HDL particles in that period.

CEC dramatically increased, accompanied by both HDL-C and HDL-TG levels, during the neonatal period (< 28-day-old participants), and remained high afterwards. However, the slight decrease in the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio during the neonatal period indicates that the increase in HDL-C and HDL-TG levels does not reflect a mere increase in HDL particle number. The relatively low CEC/stCEC ratio in neonates, especially in early neonates, could be the existence of extremely small HDL particles, due to low levels of LCAT activity27 and, consequently, low HDL maturation rates. The CEC of small HDL particles is lower than that of medium and large HDL particles29.

No statistical significance was observed among the CEC/stCEC ratios of the four age groups whose participants were born at ≥ 34 weeks gestation. In contrast, the CEC/stCEC ratios of the < 28-day-old participants born at < 34 weeks gestation were significantly lower than those of participants from the other three groups born at < 34 weeks gestation and the lower CEC/stCEC values did not improve until the infantile period.

The CEC/stCEC ratio in the ≥ 28-day-old participants born prematurely was rather high after the neonatal period compared to that in the participants born around 40 weeks gestation, possibly indicating a physiological reaction to compensate low HDL (HDL-C) levels. Actually, HDL-C in the participants born at < 34 weeks gestation was significantly lower than that in the participants born at ≥ 34 weeks gestation (P < 0.001). Similar results were observed for the CEC/stCEC ratios stratified by birth weight. This could be due to the strong correlation between gestational age and birth weight (R = 0.896). Both gestational age and birth weight showed low correlations with CEC/stCEC (R = 0.231 and R = 0.169, respectively) among < 28-day-old participants (neonates). However, the correlations between gestational age and CEC/stCEC in neonates that weighted less than 2500 and 1500 g at birth were relatively high (R = 0.404 and R = 0.491, respectively). The correlations between birth weight and CEC/stCEC in neonates born at < 37 and < 34 weeks gestation were relatively weak (R = 0.291 and R = 0.282, respectively). Gestational age may be more involved in relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (CEC/stCEC) than birth weight during the neonatal period.

HDL-C levels showed a strong correlation with CEC in all age groups as expected. A relatively strong correlation between HDL-TG and CEC observed in < 28-day-old (R = 0.640) was attenuated with age. These were reflected in the correlation between the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratios and CEC. Relatively high correlation coefficients between HDL-TG and the CEC/stCEC ratio were gradually decreased with age. A similar trend was observed in the negative correlation between the HDL-TG/HDL-C and CEC/stCEC ratios. These results indicate that HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux is strongly associated with HDL-TG level and HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio, especially in the < 28-day-old (neonates) and 28-day-old–3-month-old (early infants) individuals.

The relatively high HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux (> 1.000 CEC/stCEC) might be caused by a series of changes in the lipid composition of HDL. HDL-TG in the participants with > 1.000 CEC/stCEC was higher compared to that in the participants with < 1.000 CEC/stCEC and gradually moved in the direction of no significant difference with aging. These data strongly indicate an association of HDL-TG with the HDL-functionality in cholesterol efflux. Among the ≥ 1-year-old participants, the relatively high or low CEC/stCEC was not affected by the lipid composition of the whole HDL fraction, indicating that HDL particle size and lipids distribution are also important for high or low HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux. Similar comparisons were carried out using the median CEC/stCEC of each group as a boundary value, since the average CEC/stCEC ratios were obviously different among the groups (1.003, 0.948, 1.028, 1.027, and 1.007 for the participants from all ages, < 28-day-old, 28-day-old–3-month-old, 4–11-month-old, and ≥ 1-year-old groups, respectively) and CEC/stCEC distribution in each group was skewed. Although slightly different data were obtained, the association of HDL-TG including HDL-TG/HDL-C with HDL functionality in the participants < 28-day-old was confirmed.

HDL-C is believed to be negatively associated with the risk of developing CVD30. However, a few reports have indicated that high HDL-C levels induced by CETP deficiency are atherogenic rather than atheroprotective31,32. Healthy individuals with elevated CETP levels have a higher risk of developing CAD only if their TG levels are high33. In our results, no significant difference in lipid composition of HDL was observed between the ≥ 1-year-old participants with relatively high and low HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux. This indicates the importance of estimating the functionality of HDL in cholesterol efflux in addition to HDL-C, HDL-TG, and HDL-TG/HDL-C.

A limitation of this study is that it is impossible to compare our data to those of other studies because the CEC assay method has not yet been standardized and a reference serum obtained from healthy participants has been used as the reference. In addition, we measured CEC using the ILG method with BODIPY-cholesterol34, which is known to reflect different reactivities depending on HDL subclasses, such as HDL2 and HDL3, compared to those obtained using cultured cells and 3H-labeled cholesterol methods35. However, CEC/stCEC can be an available tool to evaluate the factors affecting HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux.

Conclusion

The CEC/stCEC ratio based on HDL-C has a potential to evaluate the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux. The relatively high functionality of HDL in cholesterol efflux observed during the neonatal and infantile periods could be induced by the high content rate of triglyceride determined in HDL. However, the relative HDL functionality in cholesterol efflux in ≥ 1-year-old children could depend on the various lipid compositions and/or sizes of HDL particles and not just on HDL-C, HDL-TG, and the HDL-TG/HDL-C ratio. This study provides insights into the types of HDL that should be increased and by what means to prevent atherosclerotic diseases.

Materials and methods

Serum samples

Residual serum samples from patients obtained for laboratory analyses at the Clinical Laboratory of Nagano Children’s Hospital were randomly used for this study. Serum samples (n = 239) of the participants without liver and lipoprotein metabolism diseases aged from 0 days to 24 years were stored at − 80 °C until use. The study design, including all experimental protocols, was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Nagano Children’s Hospital (No. 31-62). All experiments were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The need to obtain informed consent was waived by the Research Ethics Committee of Nagano Children’s Hospital.

Chemicals

All chemicals were purchased from FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation (Osaka, Japan) unless stated otherwise.

Measurement of serum lipids

The total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), HDL-C, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels were measured using enzymatic test kits (SEKISUI Medical Co., Tokyo, Japan).

Measurement of HDL-TG

LDL and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) were isolated from the serum samples using the heparin-MnCl2 precipitation method36 with some modifications. Briefly, 75 µL of serum was mixed with 18.9 µL of 1,000 u/ml heparin and 9.6 µL of 0.5 M MnCl2, and the mixture was vigorously vortexed and kept on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 11,000 × g for 20 min, the TG levels in the supernatant, including HDL, were measured using enzymatic test kits. HDL-TG levels were obtained by multiplying the dilution rate. The TC in the supernatant was also measured to compare to HDL-C concentration measured using enzymatic test kits (Supplementary Fig. S2). HDL was successfully separated from LDL and VLDL, since the number of samples within the limits of agreement was 96.7% using the Bland-Altman analysis.

Preparation of apolipoprotein B-100 depleted serum (BDS)

BDS was prepared as described previously34, with minor modifications. Briefly, 24 µL of 20% polyethylene glycol (PEG: 6000 MW) in 200 mM glycine buffer (pH 7.4) was added to 60 µL of serum, followed by mixing and incubation at 25 °C for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 9,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was isolated as the BDS.

Preparation of immobilized liposome bound-gel beads (ILGs)

In this study, we used a previously developed CEC assay using ILGs instead of cultured cells with slight modifications34,35. Briefly, egg lecithin (10.6 mg) and cholesterol (2.3 mg) were dissolved in 12 mL chloroform, and 60 µL of 2.5 mM 4,4-diflioro-4-bora-3a,4a-s-indacene labeled cholesterol (BODIPY-cholesterol; Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, AL, USA) in chloroform was added to the solution. ILGs were prepared as previously described36. Finally, 10 mL of the ILG suspension was prepared and stored in the dark at 4 °C.

Measurement of CEC

A cholesterol efflux assay was performed using a previously described ILG method with minor modifications34. Because, ILG method indicated good correlation with cultured cells method (R = 0.932)34 and had an advantage in terms of operability and reproducibility35. Briefly, after uniformly suspending the stored ILGs, 100 µL of the ILGs (in triplicate as a main test) or 10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) containing 150 mM NaCl and 1 mM Na2EDTA (Buffer A) (in duplicate as a background of a sample) were aliquoted into a 2-mL tube. Then, 150 µL of diluted BDS (3.33% concentration as a serum) was added to all tubes. In addition, 100 µL of ILGs was added to 150 µL of Buffer A in triplicate (as a background of ILG suspension). All mixtures were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 8 to 11 h. The mixture was resuspended by vortexing and then centrifuged. The fluorescence intensity of the supernatant was measured (excitation: 480 nm, emission: 530 nm), and the background of the sample and the ILGs suspension were subtracted from that of the main test. The values were normalized to those of the reference BDS obtained from a healthy adult (defined as 1.000 CEC)37, which was processed in the same manner as a sample in every assay. Consequently, this ratio is represented as the CEC of each sample.

Validation of the CEC assay

The repeatability (intra-assay) and reproducibility (inter-assay) of CEC assays were evaluated using two different serum samples. The effects of conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin on CEC values were evaluated using a series of serum samples containing different concentrations, which were prepared by mixing normal serum and Interference Check A Plus (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Conjugated and unconjugated bilirubin levels in the prepared samples were measured using test kits (FUJIFILM Wako Co., Osaka, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Pearson’s test was used for correlation analysis unless stated otherwise. Statistical significance was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and multiple comparisons with the Steel–Dwass method for comparisons of more than two groups and the unpaired Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
