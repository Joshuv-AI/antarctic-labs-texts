Chromium picolinate alleviates heat stress-induced breast muscle glucose and lipid metabolism disorders in broiler chickens
Authors: Miao Yu, Xiumei Li, Xin Zhao, Jinghai Feng, Minhong Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To date, extensive research has shown that heat stress disturbs glucose and lipid metabolism in broiler chickens. Recent evidence suggests that chromium supplementation influences metabolic regulation, particularly in glucose and lipid homeostasis in mammals. This study aimed to evaluate the effects of chromium picolinate supplementation on glucose and lipid metabolism in the breast muscle of broiler chickens under chronic heat stress. A total of 180 male Arbor Acres (AA) broilers (22 days old) were randomly assigned to three groups: a thermoneutral control group (21°C), a heat stress group (31°C), and a heat stress group receiving chromium picolinate (31°C + 400 μg/Kg elemental chromium). After 14 days, heat stress significantly impaired growth performance, induced insulin resistance, increased fat deposition, and suppressed the expression of key glucose and lipid metabolic genes. In contrast, chromium picolinate improved the average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and reduced feed conversion ratio (FCR). It also upregulated glucose metabolism genes (GLUT1, PI3K, GS) and lipid metabolism genes (PPARα, CPT-1, LPL) in breast muscle. Overall, chromium picolinate alleviated heat stress-induced skeletal muscle glucose and lipid metabolism disturbances in broiler chickens.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle is the main edible part of broiler chickens and a key economic trait (Wang et al., 2019). It is also the primary site of insulin-regulated glucose uptake and utilize (Meng, et al., 2017). Although genetic selection has improved the growth and skeletal muscle yield, it has increased broilers’ vulnerability to heat stress (Hosseindoust, et al., 2020). Heat stress raises insulin secretion, reduces insulin sensitivity, enhances glycolysis, and inhibits glycogen synthesis, thereby disrupting energy metabolism (Attia, et al., 2018; Baumgard and Rhoads, 2013; Lu, et al., 2019; Wang, et al., 2021). It also alters lipid metabolism, increasing fat deposition in visceral, subcutaneous, and intramuscular tissues (Lu, 2019; Piestun, et al., 2017). Our recent work confirmed that heat stress impairs glucose and lipid metabolism of skeletal muscle in broiler chickens (Li, et al., 2024), contributing to reduced muscle growth (Hara, et al., 2011). Therefore, strategies to counteract heat stress-induced glucose and lipid metabolic disruption are urgently needed.

Chromium supplementation is a safe and effective feed additive for improving the glucose and lipid metabolism. It enhances the glucose utilization, boots insulin sensitivity and reduced fat breakdown (Brooks, et al., 2016; Cupo and Donaldson, 1987; Jackson, et al., 2008). Organic chromium forms, such as chromium methionine, histidinate, picolinate, nicotinate and Chromium yeast, have higher bioavailability than inorganic types (Zha, et al., 2009). Prior studies showed that organic chromium reduces insulin resistance index, lowers triglyceride and glucose levels, and decreases abdominal fat (Piray and Foroutanifar, 2022; Sahin, et al., 2017; Wang, et al., 2021). However, the effects and mechanisms of chromium picolinate under heat stress remain unclear.

Given its metabolic benefits, chromium picolinate may help mitigate heat stress induced disorders in skeletal muscle. To test this, we exposed broilers to 31°C and evaluated the effects of chromium picolinate on glucose and lipid metabolism. This study offers a regulatory strategy and theoretical basis for foundation for managing skeletal muscle metabolic dysfunction in heat-stressed broilers.

Materials and methods

All experimental protocols and animal handling procedures were rigorously reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee for Experiment Animal Welfare and Ethics at the Institute of Animal Science, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS). This official authorization (Permit No. IAS 2020-112) ensures strict adherence to ethical standards and regulatory guidelines for animal research.

Birds and housing

A total of 180 22-day-old Arbor Acres (AA) male broilers of similar weight were randomly divided into three experimental groups, with six replicates per group and ten birds per replicate. The experimental groups included: (1) a thermal neutral (TN) group maintained at 21 °C; (2) a heat stress (HS) group exposed to a high temperature of 31 °C; (3) a heat stress group supplemented with chromium picolinate (HS+Cr) supplemented with 400 μg/kg elemental chromium in the form of chromium picolinate compound. The experiments were conducted in the artificial climate chambers (4.08 m × 2.88 m × 2.38 m) at the State Key Laboratory of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, and housed in single-layer flat cages (0.82 m × 0.70 m × 0.60 m). First, a 7-day acclimatization period was performed for 22 to 29 days old broilers according to Arbor Acres Broiler Management Manual to ensure consistency in feeding (Aviagen and Huntsville, 2018). Subsequently, a formal 14-day experiment was conducted with broiler chickens aged 29 to 42 days. Three cages were placed in each housing chamber to ensure consistent environmental exposure. Relative humidity was regulated with an accuracy of ±7 %, and additional controls were applied for air speed (<0.5 m/s), ammonia concentration (<5 ppm), and 24-hour photoperiod. Real-time monitoring was performed using a high-precision temperature and humidity recorder (Testo 174 H, Testo AG, Lenzkirchen, Germany), which recorded data every 10 min.

Growth performance

During the trail, daily feed intake was quantified by weighing remaining feed every 24 hours. Body weight (BW) was recorded at the beginning (Day 29) and end (Day 42) of the trial. Growth performance indicators such as average daily gain (ADG, calculated as ADG = [final body weight−initial body weight] ÷ 14), average daily feed intake (ADFI, calculated as ADFI = total feed intake ÷ 14), and feed conversion ratio (FCR, calculated as FCR = ADFI ÷ ADG) were calculated to assess broiler growth efficiency.

Sample collection

On days 7 and 14 of heat stress exposure, 12 broiler chickens (two per replicate) were selected from each group, with body weights close to the group the group mean. After a 12-hour feed deprivation period, to analyze glucose and lipid metabolism parameters in the broiler chickens, blood samples were first collected from the wing vein using a vacuum collection tube. Serum was then separated by centrifugation under specific conditions (3,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C). Finally, the serum was stored at −20°C to prevent degradation and ensure the reliability of subsequent testing results. After blood collection, the broiler chickens were humanely sacrificed by carbon dioxide asphyxiation. Abdominal fat was immediately collected and weighed, and abdominal fat percentage was calculated (abdominal fat percentage = abdominal fat weight ÷ Individual body weight ◊ 100). Breast muscle tissue samples were immediately collected after completion of these procedures. After being rinsed with sterile 0.9 % sodium chloride saline, the samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and then stored at −80°C pending metabolic parameter analysis. Notably, samples from the day 14 of the experiment were used to measure the expression of genes related to glucose and lipid metabolism. Liver tissue samples were also immediately collected, rinsed, frozen, and stored at −80°C until analysis of metabolic parameters.

Glucose metabolism analysis

Fasting blood glucose (FBG) was measured using a Roche glucometer (Accu-Chek Performa, Roche Diagnostics, USA). Fasting insulin (FINS) levels and glycogen content in breast muscle and liver tissues were assessed using commercial kits from the Nanjing Jiancheng Institute of Bioengineering (Nanjing, China). Insulin resistance was evaluated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) formula: HOMA-IR = FBG (mmol/L) × FINS (mU/L) ÷ 22.5.

Lipid metabolism analysis

Serum, breast muscle, and liver levels of free fatty acids (FFA) and triglycerides (TG) were measured using an automatic biochemical analyzer (Hitachi 7600, Hitachi, Japan). Crude fat in breast muscle and liver was determined via the Soxhlet method (GB 5009.6–2016), and fat content was calculated as (crude fat weight ÷ fresh sample weight) × 100.

Quantitative real-time PCR

The mRNA expression levels of glucose transporter type 1 (GLUT1), phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K), glycogen synthase (GS), peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors α (PPARα), carnitine palmitoyltransterase-1 (CPT-1), and lipoprotein lipase (LPL) in breast muscle was measured by quantitative real-time PCR. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and reverse transcribed with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed in triplicate on a LightCycler 96 System (Roche) using TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (Takara). The protocol included 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles at 95°C for 15 s and 65°C for 30 s, ending with a melting curve from 65°C to 90°C. Expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin as the internal control. Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from breast muscle samples using RIPA lysis buffer (AWB0136c; Abiowell, China). The extracted proteins were then denatured in SDS loading buffer (AWB0055; Abiowell, China) and boiled at 95°C for 10 min. Proteins were then separated using 12 % SDS-PAGE gradient gels (Quanshijin, Haidian District, Beijing, China) at an initial voltage of 80 V for 30 min and then at 120 V for 60 min. Finally, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane (0.45 μm; Millipore, USA) at 300 mA for 30 min to ensure compatibility for further analysis. The membrane was sealed with rapid blocking solution (AWB0214b; Abiowell, China) on a shaker at room temperature for 10 min and then washed three times with TBST (0.05 % Tween-20 in TBS; BioTec, China). Primary antibodies against GLUT1 (1:1000, AWA61570, Abiowell), PI3K (1:1000, AWA63394, Abiowell), GS (1:1000, AWA43660, Abiowell), CPT1 (1:1000, AWA63836, Abiowell), LPL (1:1000, AWA61104, Abiowell), PPARα (1:500, BS-23398, BIOSS) and β-actin (1:2000, AWA80002; Abiowell) were diluted in antibody diluent (AWB0200c; Abiowell) and incubated with the membrane overnight at 4°C. The membrane, which had been previously washed with TBST, was incubated at room temperature for 90 min before detection. Probe detection was performed using a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abiowell, AWS0002) diluted 1:5000 in Abiowell secondary antibody diluent (AWB0201c), a crucial step in subsequent protein detection. PPARα Protein bands were exposed to HyBlot CL Autoradiography Film (E3012 Denvill Scientific Inc.) for 30 seconds, followed by 2 min of development and mounting in the dark. Other protein bands were visualized by chemiluminescence using the Li-COR Odyssey system (Tanon-5200, Tianneng Technology, China). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (v1.53, NIH, USA), and relative expression was analyzed using β-actin as a standard.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 23.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). One - way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test was used to assess group differences, with replicate (n = 6) as the experimental unit. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Discussion

In our previous studies, we established a heat-stress broiler model at 31°C (Li, et al., 2021; Ma, et al., 2019; Tang, et al., 2015; Wang, et al., 2021; Xu, et al., 2024). In the present study, heat stress at 31°C disrupted glucose and lipid metabolism in breast muscle of broilers, as evidenced by increased insulin resistance, reduced glycogen storage, elevated fat accumulation, and suppressed the expression of glucose and lipid metabolism-related genes. Supplementation with chromium picolinate reversed these effects, improving glucose and lipid metabolism by reducing insulin resistance and fat accumulation while enhancing glycogen deposition and glucose and lipid metabolism-related gene expression.

Numerous studies confirm that heat stress impairs the growth performance of broiler chickens (Abdel-Moneim, et al., 2022; Li, et al., 2021; Ma, et al., 2019; Tang, et al., 2015; Wang, et al., 2021; Xu, et al., 2024; Zhai, et al., 2014). Consistent with previous findings, we observed lower ADFI and ADG, and higher FCR in heat-stressed broilers. Chromium picolinate supplementation improved these metrics, aligning with literature reporting growth benefits from dietary chromium (Bin-Jumah, et al., 2020; Kim, et al., 2023).

Heat stress is also induced glucose metabolism disorders in broiler chickens, with increased FBG, FINS and HOMA-IR, and decreased glycogen in liver and breast muscle in this study. As a key marker of insulin sensitivity, elevated HOMA-IR reflects impaired glucose regulation (Morgan, et al., 2014). Impaired insulin function reduces glucose utilization in muscle and increases hepatic glucose output, elevating both FBG (Yang, et al., 2018) and FINS (Rhoads, et al., 2013). Our results align with earlier studies reporting similar metabolic disturbances in heat-stressed broilers (Attia, et al., 2018; Baumgard and Rhoads, 2013; Lu, et al., 2019). Importantly, we examined gene level expression of GLUT1, PI3K, and GS to clarify these effects. GLUT1 mediates glucose uptake into muscle cells (Cantley, et al., 2014) via insulin-PI3K signaling pathway (Zhao, et al., 2014), and GS governs glycogen synthesis (Sun, et al., 2020). Their downregulation under heat stress further confirmed glucose metabolism disorder in broiler chickens. Chromium picolinate improved these glucose metabolism parameters, supporting its role in alleviating insulin resistance and restoring glucose metabolism, likely via enhanced insulin regulation pathway (Dalólio, et al., 2018).

Glucose metabolism and lipid metabolism disorders often co-occur in animals (Ding, et al., 2020). In this study, heat stress elevated FFA and TG levels, and increased fat deposition in liver, muscle, and abdominal adipose tissue. TG abnormal elevation signals impaired lipid metabolism (Lüdicke, et al., 2018), and increased FFA, produced from TG breakdown, contributes to fat accumulation and insulin resistance (He, et al., 2015). Additionally, heat stress downregulated PPARα, CPT-1, and LPL expression, which are essential for lipid metabolism. PPARα regulates fatty acid oxidation to dynamic regulates the glucose-lipid homeostasis (Li, et al., 2020), CPT-1 controls mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (Eaton, 2002), and LPL hydrolyzes TG and affects fat accumulation (Huang, et al., 2016). Our findings are consistent with previous reports linking heat stress to abdominal and intramuscular fat deposition (Lu, 2019; Piestun, et al., 2017). Chromium picolinate reversed these effects, lowering FFA and TG levels, reducing fat deposition, and upregulating PPARα, CPT-1, and LPL expression, indicating that chromium picolinate improved lipid metabolism under heat stress in broiler chickens.

Conclusion

In conclusion, supplementing 400 µg/kg chromium picolinate effectively alleviated heat stress-induced glucose and lipid metabolism disorder in broiler chickens. This was reflected by reduced insulin resistance, lower lipid accumulation, and up-regulated the expressions of GLUT1, PI3K, GS, PPARα, CPT-1, and LPL in breast muscle. These results support chromium picolinate as a safe and effective strategy to mitigate the adverse effects of heat stress on metabolic health and growth performance in broiler chickens.
