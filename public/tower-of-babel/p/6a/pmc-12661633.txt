Oral cheek tooth extraction and treatment of osteomyelitis in llamas and alpacas
Authors: Nora M Biermann, Eva Haltmayer, Matthias Jehle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdvanced dental disease with associated osteomyelitis is commonly encountered in New World camelids (NWC). In the past, the treatment of choice consisted of extraoral techniques, with complications occurring in up to 70% of cases. With advances in the treatment of dental disease in other species, oral extraction may offer a less invasive alternative, possibly associated with fewer complications. This report describes the diagnosis, treatment and long‐term outcome of NWC with dental disease.MethodsThis was a retrospective study of NWC with advanced dental disease undergoing oral cheek tooth extraction between September 2020 and November 2024.ResultsForty animals (20 alpacas and 20 llamas) undergoing oral extraction of one to four cheek teeth (median: 2, interquartile range: 1.1–2.9) were included. Eleven animals (27.5%) experienced complications. Most of these complications were mild, primarily consisting of tooth root fractures. The mean follow‐up duration was 24.1 months (standard deviation = 16.5 months). At the final follow‐up, 92.1% (35/38) of animals exhibited resolution of clinical signs.LimitationsRetrospective nature of study and large variation in severity of dental pathologies.ConclusionOral extraction of cheek teeth in NWC is a feasible alternative to extraoral approaches and carries a favourable prognosis. While dental equipment intended for other species can be used, species‐specific dental features should be taken into consideration when oral extraction is performed.Keywords: dental disease, large animal surgery, new world camelids, periapical tooth root infections, periodontal disease

INTRODUCTION

In a previous study of the oral health of New World camelids (NWC), more than 80% of the examined animals had varying degrees of dental pathologies, which included diastemata, wear abnormalities and periodontal disease, as well as fractured, malpositioned and missing teeth. 1 In particular, periodontal disease appears to be associated with peri‐apical infection and bony swelling. As dental examinations are currently not routinely performed in NWC, peri‐apical infections and associated osteomyelitis are commonly only diagnosed in advanced stages of disease. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 This often impedes the ability to employ tooth‐saving procedures, and dental extraction is frequently required.

NWC, particularly llamas, have long, narrow oral cavities, which were the reason that for a long time, oral tooth extraction was only considered a viable option in rostrally located teeth (up to the most caudal premolar tooth: P4). 5 But veterinary dental techniques have tremendously evolved in many species over the last decades, and today, oral extraction techniques have become the method of choice in most veterinary species. 6 , 7

Most of the literature on cheek teeth extraction in NWC, particularly in molar teeth, still describes extraoral approaches that require removal of the lateral alveolar bone plate to access these teeth. 3 , 8 , 9 Reports of this technique cite generally a good prognosis, but complication rates are high, particularly reinfection rates of more than 70% have been reported. 8

At this point, by adaptation of dental equipment and extraction techniques from other species (e.g., horse and small animals), oral check tooth extraction, including caudally located cheek teeth, in NWC has become available. However, while the cheek teeth of these species have some similarities with ruminants and horses, 1 they possess several unique features that are useful to consider in the process of dental care and particularly dental extraction. Some of these include that mesial and distal roots of adjacent cheek teeth are often in very close proximity to each other, particularly in older animals, 10 , 11 which makes the spread of dental disease to neighbouring teeth more likely. 11 Additionally, while the roots of young animals are relatively straight, with increased age the mesial and distal roots divert in opposite directions, 10 , 11 making extraction of these teeth more challenging. For a long time, it was propagated that the mesial and distal roots of the same cheek tooth do not communicate (separate pulp chambers), which would allow removal of just one root while preserving the others in cases of focal disease. 5 , 12 Recently, it was shown that, particularly in younger animals, the roots appear to communicate frequently by forming a common pulp chamber. 10 , 11 With increased age as more secondary dentin is laid down, the communication becomes less likely. However, the exact age when this happens has not been determined, and it appears that there are considerable inter‐individual differences. 10 , 11

These features, which in part are distinctly different from other commonly treated veterinary species, result in unique possibilities and challenges during dental treatment of NWC.

To date, no study has described oral dental extraction techniques, associated complications, and aftercare as well as long‐term outcomes in these animals.

The objective of this study was to describe the clinical presentation, diagnostic findings, treatment and outcome of NWC undergoing oral dental extraction of cheek teeth and surgical treatment of associated osteomyelitis.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Case selection

NWC with a diagnosis of cheek tooth infection (premolar and/or molar teeth) with or without associated osteomyelitis of the adjacent alveolar bone between September 2020 and November 2024 were included. This included animals presented for first opinion by the owners as well as cases referred by veterinary practitioners. Information was obtained from the electronic medical record system of the University of Veterinary Medicine Vienna. Animals were excluded from the study when the primary lesion involved incisors or canine teeth without concurrent involvement of one or more cheek teeth.

Upon admission of animals to the university hospital, owners’ consent was taken for anonymised clinical data to be used for scientific or educational purposes. Ethics approval is not required for retrospective case evaluations.

Medical record review

Information retrieved from medical records included signalment, clinical history, diagnostic procedures, surgical procedures, post‐operative care and complications (intra‐ and post‐operative). Follow‐up was either obtained by a re‐check visit and clinical assessment at the hospital or via a telephone questionnaire with the owners, obtained by one of the authors (NMB). A positive outcome was defined as complete resolution of clinical signs and return of the animal to its intended use.

Clinical examination

A complete physical examination, which included palpation of the head, was performed in each animal. An oral exam was performed either at the time of diagnostic imaging or after induction of anaesthesia immediately before surgery using an equine dental oroscope (Karl Storz Tele Pack vet X LED). When oral examination and diagnostic imaging were performed in the days before surgery, the animals were sedated using a combination of ketamine (2 mg/kg, intra‐muscular [IM], once; Ketamidor 100 mg/mL, Richter Pharma), butorphanol (0.08–0.12 mg/kg, IM, once; Butomidor 10 mg/kg, Richter Pharma) and xylazine (0.25–0.3 mg/kg, IM, once; Sedaxylan 20 mg/mL, Dechra). 13 The oral cavity was examined for signs of malpositioned teeth, wear abnormalities, periodontal disease (i.e., fistula formation with or without purulent discharge), diastemata with food packing and each tooth for pulp exposure, infundibular caries, peripheral caries and fractures of the clinical crown.

Diagnostic imaging

Radiographic examinations (Mobilett XP, Siemens) were conducted in some cases during initial examination, as well as intra‐ and post‐operative assessments.

Standard radiographic projections included dorsoventral, laterolateral and bilateral 30°–40° oblique views of mandibular and maxillary cheek teeth.

Particularly in the second part of the study period, animals exhibiting typical clinical signs immediately underwent computer tomography (CT) of the head (Somatom X Cite VA30, Siemens), without prior radiographic examination.

For peri‐ and post‐operative imaging, either radiographs or consecutive CT examinations were employed, with the attending clinician determining the modality for each case.

Surgical procedures, peri‐ and post‐operative care

Information on surgical procedures (e.g., affected tooth/teeth and number of teeth extracted, debridement of infected bone or soft tissue abscesses), number of surgeries required, duration of general anaesthesia and surgery (time from induction to extubation) and intra‐operative complications were reviewed.

Cheek tooth extraction and osteomyelitis treatment were performed as follows:

After anaesthesia induction and oral intubation, animals were positioned in sternal recumbency. Access to the oral cavity was facilitated using an equine Gunther speculum (Dachs Germany), with the animal's head elevated on an inflatable cushion (Vacuform 2.0, Microspace System) and the nose slightly pointing downwards. Before mandibular cheek tooth extraction, a modified intraoral inferior alveolar nerve block, as previously described in people and horses, 14 , 15 was performed using 1.5–3 mL of procaine hydrochloride (2%) (Procamidor 20 mg/mL, VetViva Richter). For maxillary cheek tooth extraction, periodontal and subgingival infiltration with 2–3 mL of local anaesthetic (procaine hydrochloride 2%) was performed at the level of the affected tooth/teeth.

Tooth loosening involved equine gingival elevators, incisor or molar spreaders, and a serrated equine extraction forceps, guided by a dental oroscope (Figure 1a). Care was taken to use limited force to maintain an intact clinical crown; particularly, the use of molar spreaders between teeth was limited and only performed when visual control of correct placement was possible (Figure 1b). After the tooth was loosened, a molar spreader was used to split the mesial and distal columns by applying pressure onto the enamel fold between the tooth columns until a transversal fracture occurred. This resulted in separate mesial and distal parts of the tooth (Figure 1c). In select cases, a high‐speed dental drill (iM3 GS Deluxe, iM3) was employed to pre‐drill the fracture line, ensuring fracture of the tooth in the intended position (Figure 1d).

(b) Use of molar spreaders between adjacent cheek teeth during the process of tooth loosening. (c) Molar spreader applied to a cheek tooth after initial loosening to split the tooth between mesial and distal columns. (d) Alternatively, a high‐speed dental drill can be employed to section the tooth between mesial and distal columns. (e) Sectioned (mesial and distal parts) during dental extraction. (f) Dental packing placed in the empty alveolus after tooth removal.

Following the separation of the tooth roots, these were further mobilised using different‐sized equine extraction forceps. Final extraction of the tooth, one root at a time, was either accomplished by extraction forceps or by use of dental elevators (Figure 1e).

In instances where fractured root remnants remained in the alveolar socket, several techniques were employed for fragment removal. Initially, dental elevators were utilised to lever the fragment from the alveolar socket. If unsuccessful, sectioning of the fragment was performed using a high‐speed dental drill to facilitate mobilisation with dental elevators and fragment forceps.

If bleeding from the alveolar bone occurred, a custom‐made suction device, comprising a blunt 16 G cannula (Suction needles, Medmix) bent at a 75°–90° angle, connected to a suction system (Ameda Egnell‐Universal), was used to allow for continued visualisation and subsequent removal of the fragments. In cases where bleeding could not be controlled sufficiently for continued visualisation of remaining fragments, these root fragments were left in place and removed during re‐examination or an additional surgical procedure.

When additionally present, infected alveolar bone and osteomyelitic lesions were debrided, followed by lavage and placement of dental packing material (Telaprep No 1–3, Hartmann) (Figure 1f).

External draining tracts or soft tissue abscesses underwent surgical debridement after sterile preparation, followed by application of a bandage. Animals recovered in sternal recumbency before being returned to their stalls.

Peri‐ and post‐operative care

All animals received perioperative nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory (Carprofen 1.4 mg/kg, subcutaneous, once a day or once every 2 days; Carprodolor 50 mg/mL, Dechra) and antimicrobial treatment (amoxicillin 15 mg/kg, IM, once every 2 days; Vetrimoxin 150 mg/mL, Ceva). In the early post‐operative phase, animals with extensive lesions and therefore more invasive procedures additionally received butorphanol boluses (0.05 mg/kg, intravenously [IV]/IM, every 4–6 hours) and/or ketamine constant rate infusion (CRI) (20–40 µg/kg/min, IV) to encourage early post‐operative feeding.

Re‐examination of the alveolar bone socket was performed every 3–4 days in sedated animals using the same sedation protocol as before surgery for diagnostic imaging and oral examination. During re‐examination, alveolar bone sockets were inspected, followed by lavage and, if necessary, further debridement of the alveolar bone. Animals were discharged once healing was progressing satisfactorily and dental packings were no longer required.

RESULTS

Animals, complaint, clinical history and presentation

A total of 40 animals, 20 alpacas (12 females and eight males) and 20 llamas (seven females and 13 males), with a mean age of 6.9 years (SD 3.8 years), with a range of 0.9–15 years, were included. Of the 19 female animals, seven were pregnant at varying gestational stages and six had nursing crias. Mean weight of alpacas was 60.0 kg (SD 13.0 kg) with a range of 22–82 kg and of llamas 128.7 kg (SD 24.8 kg) with a range of 78–188.2 kg.

Mean reported duration of clinical signs before admission was 87 days (SD 131 days). Eleven animals (27.5%) were previously treated by a referring veterinarian (rDVM), either with systemic antimicrobials alone (7/11) or in combination with nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatories (4/11). Additionally, one animal underwent both an unsuccessful attempt at an oral mandibular cheek tooth extraction (M1) and a mandibular cheek tooth (M2) repulsion procedure by rDVM. The latter resulted in the unintended removal of part of the third molar (M3) and the development of an orocutaneous fistula leading to food and saliva loss.

Upon admission, 75.0% (35/40) of cases presented with mandibular swelling of which 34.3% (12/35) had an associated draining tract. The remaining five cases involved the maxilla, and three of these animals had a bony swelling below one eye, with (2/3) or without (1/3) purulent discharge from the area. Among admitted cases, 55.0% (22/40) had a thin or very thin body condition, but only in five animals was weight loss either the primary or secondary presenting complaint (12.5%; 5/40). Two animals (5.0%) displayed either change in mastication and/or foaming upon presentation.

Before tooth extraction, oral examination findings revealed in most cases multiple dental pathologies (Figure 2a–d). These included severe periodontal disease with purulent discharge (60.0%, 24/40), purulent discharge associated with orally located sequestra (22.5%, 9/40), teeth with abnormal mobility (17.5%, 7/40), displaced teeth (12.5%, 5/40), diastema (10.0%, 4/40), and fractured teeth (7.5%, 3/40). In two cases each, an open pulp horn, severe infundibular caries and a generalised geriatric dentition were observed. No apparent changes were noted in 17.5% (7/40) cases, and extraction was based on other clinical signs (e.g., mandibular swelling and presence of draining tract) combined with diagnostic imaging findings, including one case where the affected M3 had not yet erupted through the oral mucosa.

(b) Open and discolored pulp cavity (arrow). (c) Diastema formation with food packing and purulent discharge (arrow). (d) Chronic tooth fracture and displacement of fractured fragments (arrow).

Radiographs and CT

Upon initial examination, radiographs were conducted in 52.5% (21/40) of cases, with CT examinations additionally performed in 20.0% (8/40) during hospitalisation, which added further information for complete diagnosis and surgical planning in seven of eight cases. Particularly later during the study period, most animals received only head CTs (47.5%;19/40) before surgery. Pre‐operative imaging showed in 20/40 (50.0%) cases a lesion localised to one tooth with focal bony changes and additional draining tract in six of 20 (30.0%) cases (Figure 3a). In seven of 40 cases (17.5%), two cheek teeth and the adjacent bone showed signs of infection including bony lysis and periosteal reaction (Figure 3b). Three or all four (five) cheek teeth of one arcade (mandibular or maxillary) were involved in 13/40 (32.5%), with severe change to the bony structure, 10 of these cases had also one or multiple sequestra and/or draining tracts (Figure 3c,d). Intra‐operative radiographs were performed in nine cases and post‐operative radiographs in 13 cases, while two animals underwent a post‐operative CT to assess proper healing (Figure 4).

Diagnosis

All cases were diagnosed with varying degrees of apical infections and bony involvement based on physical and oral examination, as well as radiography and/or CT findings (Figure 5).

Surgical procedure

A total of 78 cheek teeth were extracted (median = 2, interquartile range [IQR]: 1.1–2.9 teeth), of which 70 (89.7%) were mandibular cheek teeth and 8 (10.3%) were maxillary cheek teeth. M1 (23/78) was the most commonly extracted tooth, followed by M2 (22/78), P4 (17/78), M3 (14/78) and P3 (2/78).

In 10 animals with mandibular cheek tooth involvement, only one tooth root was removed after splitting the tooth as described earlier, as the other root showed no signs of infection.

The mean duration of anaesthesia and surgery was 79.3 minutes (SD 44.1 minutes), with a range of 25–185 minutes and resulted in complete extraction of the affected teeth in 29/40 (72.5%) of surgeries, with six cases (15%) requiring a second surgery. One case required complete tooth removal, while the remaining five cases involved removal of one or more tooth roots. One animal presented a second time for removal of M3 after the draining tract persisted following the previous removal of M2. The remaining five cases (12.5%) did not undergo a second surgery for tooth root removal. In three cases, tooth roots were removed during the first re‐examination after surgery, while in two cases, the tooth root was left in place due to rapid healing of the surgical site.

Recovery and postoperative treatment

Median duration of hospitalisation was 18 days (IQR: 12.3–23.8 days), with a range of 2–44 days. All animals received peri‐ and postoperative nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatories for a median of 4 days (IQR: 2.5–5.5 days) and systemic antimicrobial treatment for a median of 5 days (IQR: 1.5–8.5 days). Cases with severe osteomyelitis and extraction of three or four cheek teeth (13 cases) received antimicrobials for longer duration (11 days; IQR: 6.5–17.5 days) compared to cases with less severe changes (4 days; IQR: 2–6 days). Treatment length was based on satisfactory surgical site healing.

Most cases underwent multiple re‐examinations with dental packing changes under sedation during the median period of 12 days (IQR: 6.3–17.8 days), with a range of 1–38 days between surgery and discharge. In 11 cases, the dental packing was removed, and no further packing was placed during the first re‐examination after surgery. One additional packing was required in 16 cases, while two to three packing changes were necessary in nine cases. In four cases with severe osteomyelitis, additional debridement of external draining tracts and sequestrated bone was performed for several weeks until granulation tissue filled oral defects and the signs of infection resolved.

Complications

Complications associated with dental extraction and treatment of osteomyelitis were experienced perioperatively. Mild complications were encountered in 27.5% of cases (11/40), primarily consisting of one or more tooth root fractures (10/11). In two‐thirds of cases where tooth fragments were not removed immediately, this was due to moderate bleeding and resulting inadequate visualisation of remaining fragments. One case exhibited moderate transient emphysema of oral soft tissues following the use of pressurised air to remove blood pooling from an alveolus.

Severe complications were encountered in one animal (1/40; 2.5%) during general anaesthesia, manifested as cerebral hypoxia. The animal did not regain consciousness during recovery, and electroencephalography revealed decreased activity of parts of the brain, leading to the decision for humane euthanasia. Histopathological examination confirmed signs of hypoxia of multiple brain areas without other pathological findings.

Follow‐Up/Outcome

One animal was euthanased 6 weeks after surgery due to persistent depression and weight loss. Postmortem examination revealed normal progression of healing of previous dental extraction sites and no other findings that would have explained the clinical signs. For the remaining animals, mean follow‐up duration was 24.1 months (SD 16.5 months), which ranged from 1 to 52 months, and was greater than 3 months in all but two cases. In 10 cases (26.3%), this was based on re‐examination at the university clinic and in the remainder of the cases on owner feedback. At the final follow‐up, 92.1% (35/38) of animals exhibited resolution of clinical signs and returned to normal activity. One alpaca died 24 months after dental extraction for reasons unrelated to dental pathologies. Two animals, previously diagnosed with severe mandibular osteomyelitis, presented with recurrent draining tracts and continue to receive further treatment.

DISCUSSION

Even in animals with advanced dental diseases and associated osteomyelitis, the long‐term prognosis of NWC following treatment can be considered favourable. Compared to previously described extraoral techniques, oral dental extraction appears to have a low complication rate. Both the frequency and, particularly, the severity of complications were notably reduced. Intraoperative fractures of tooth roots were the most common complication, and a second surgery was necessary in some animals to remove these fragments, usually due to an inability to visualise them adequately because of blood pooling in the alveolar socket. Once inflammation had subsided, fractured roots could be more easily removed. Additionally, we built a custom‐made suction device to facilitate blood removal. Later in the study period, the need for a second surgery was reduced with the use of this suction device. Furthermore, with increased experience, it was noted that not all fractured tooth roots required removal. In cases where the alveolar bone was not infected at the level of the tooth root, healing of the site was rapid and remaining fragments were often already covered by healthy granulation tissue at the time of packing change. In such cases, no further removal was attempted.

In all cases, the bleeding encountered was mild to moderate and typically self‐limiting. When bleeding persisted toward the end of surgery, it was consistently manageable by applying dental packing to the alveolar socket.

While not counted as a complication, additional debridement of infected bone was performed in several animals. The continued presence of purulent material and soft bone indicated that further debridement was necessary. Similar to fractured tooth roots, once only healthy tissue remained, rapid granulation of the exposed bone was observed and, in our experience, is the best indication that no further debridement is required.

Various extraction techniques have been previously described, including tooth repulsion with associated risk of mandibular fracture 3 , 16 , 17 and a lateral mandibular approach involving removal of the lateral alveolar bone. 3 , 8 With the latter technique, a success rate of 84% for one tooth and 59% when more than one tooth had to be removed was reported. 8 Besides the markedly reduced prognosis, the cited complications were potentially more compromising (e.g., persistence of chronic draining tracts, salivary loss acidosis, mandibular fracture), especially when multiple teeth had to be extracted. 8

While our study's sample size precluded a definitive analysis of the impact of the number of teeth extracted, animals seemed to cope well, even when multiple teeth had to be removed, and complications did not seem to increase with the increased number of extracted teeth. Exceptions may include cases with severe osteomyelitis and extensive tooth involvement, where recurrent symptoms may necessitate further interventions. Regular follow‐up examinations seem crucial for early detection of potential recurrence in such cases. 18

Animals with extensive lesions and multiple tooth extractions usually required additional analgesia in the immediate postoperative period. Based on the invasiveness of the procedure, some animals immediately received a multimodal pain regimen, usually consisting of a ketamine CRI with or without butorphanol boluses to encourage food intake within a few hours after surgery. Animals that had partial tooth extractions or a low number of teeth removed usually did not require additional pain medication; however, if they had not started eating by the evening after surgery, additional analgesia was administered overnight.

The continuation of analgesic administration was determined based on normal mastication and food intake, which was additionally monitored through regular bodyweight checks and healing of the surgical sites during recheck examinations. None of the animals were discharged with pain medications, as it was considered crucial that they were comfortable before returning to their herd. While owners are often highly observant, NWC can hide pain well, and weight loss due to decreased food intake might go unnoticed for extended periods.

Although tooth roots were not removed in several animals, we did not observe these animals to be more painful in the post‐operative period. Pain and inflammation associated with retained tooth root fragments have been reported in other species and people, 18 , 19 but the recommendation has been made to decide on a case‐by‐case basis whether root fragments require removal. 19

Compared to other studies, 8 , 12 the median duration of antibiotic administration of 5 days was considerably shorter. However, animals with severe osteomyelitis received antimicrobials for a median duration of 11 days, with discontinuation primarily based on the resolution of signs of infection during re‐examination. Amoxicillin was chosen as a first‐line antibiotic due to federal regulations for prudent antimicrobial use, as well as restrictions on antibiotic use related to NWC being classified as food‐producing animals. As all treated animals showed resolution of clinical signs of infection, a change in antibiotic class was not considered necessary.

Oral dental extraction in NWC was performed primarily utilising equine dental extraction equipment and a (small animal) high‐speed dental drill. While custom‐made devices, such as extraction forceps with shorter grasping surfaces, may facilitate tooth loosening, the described procedures can be feasibly performed with standard equipment. Equine surgeons accustomed to equine cheek tooth extraction need to keep in mind that NWC teeth fracture much more easily and therefore require gentle handling. As NWC cheek teeth roots tend to diverge, particularly with increased age, 11 teeth are prone to fracture of the crown and roots during extraction. Tooth sectioning, which has also been reported in other species, for example small animals, 20 , 21 has facilitated removal after carefully loosening the teeth in our hands. Even when root diversion was mild based on prior imaging, we found that tooth sectioning increased the ease of extraction considerably. Either molar spreaders or a high‐speed dental drill can be used to perform sectioning. It was stated that sectioning of the roots is contraindicated in younger animals (≤5 years) as their teeth are more prone to fracturing secondary to forces exerted by the extraction forceps, as they have a larger pulp cavity and relatively thinner outer tooth walls. 11 So far, we have not experienced problems with premature crown or tooth fracture in younger compared to older animals, and we perform it regardless of the age routinely.

Earlier studies described partial extraction of either the mesial or distal root of a cheek tooth via a lateral extraoral approach. 22 However, recent research has questioned this practice due to the discovery of a common pulp chamber in younger cheek teeth, suggesting that sectioning the tooth could open these chambers, potentially leading to subsequent infection. 11 The exact time at which these common pulp chambers separate by laying down secondary dentin is currently still unknown and may vary considerably between individual animals. 10 , 11 We successfully sectioned and extracted only the affected part of a cheek tooth in several animals of different ages.

These animals presented with localised signs of infection at the apical aspect of only one root, which may suggest that at the time, there was no communication between pulp horns. However, in people, it has been shown that in multirooted teeth, healthy tissue can exist close to necrotic tissue in adjacent canals. 23 Further research is needed to determine whether this is also the case in NWC.

At the time of extraction, we did not identify any macroscopic communication between chambers; however, this does not entirely rule out the possibility. Although none of the animals with partially extracted teeth developed signs of infection during the follow‐up period, cases should be selected carefully. Additionally, close examination of the sectioned area for inadvertent opening of the pulp chambers, as well as gentle handling of the remaining tooth part to maintain healthy periodontal attachments, is crucial.

Similarly to other species, CT imaging proves to be superior to radiographs in NWC, 3 , 12 , 24 , 25 a finding that the experience in this study population would support where we utilised CT imaging in the majority of animals. It improved our ability to assess the extent of dental involvement more accurately. Particularly in chronic disease where multiple sequestra were present and several teeth were involved, the benefits of three‐dimensional imaging in identifying the exact extent and location can greatly improve treatment planning and execution. The other type of cases where CT imaging significantly improved diagnostic and treatment accuracy were those in which lesions were close to adjacent tooth roots and involvement could not be determined definitively by radiography alone. In our experience, in many cases where radiographs raised doubts about tooth root involvement, CT imaging revealed that the infection had likely not spread to adjacent teeth, enabling preservation of the neighbouring tooth.

Consistent with prior studies, mandibular cheek teeth were more frequently affected than maxillary cheek teeth, with the first and second mandibular molars being the most affected and extracted teeth. 8 Mandibular swelling and the presence of periodontal disease were some of the most frequent findings in this study population, which is consistent with the findings of others. 2 , 3 These findings also support the presence of advanced disease stages, where periodontal disease likely leads to bony infections and subsequent remodelling. 2 To prevent this, routine dental examination, common in other veterinary species, should be implemented and may allow for tooth‐saving procedures (e.g., endodontics) to be employed rather than dental extraction. Dental examination requires sedation of the individual animal, and owners might be reluctant due to the risk of adverse events associated with handling or sedation. While in the future, routine dental examination hopefully becomes more commonplace, routine palpation of both mandibles and frequent monitoring of bodyweight and body condition score can help identify at‐risk animals.

Major limitations of this study include the small sample size comprising animals with varying degrees of severity of disease, leading to significant variations in treatment duration and associated parameters such as post‐operative care, duration of drug administration and hospitalization.

Furthermore, consistent follow‐up through repeat clinical and diagnostic assessments was not ensured, often relying on owners' observations.

CONCLUSIONS

In conclusion, oral dental extraction of cheek teeth in NWC is a feasible alternative to extraoral approaches and carries a favourable prognosis with low complication rates. While dental equipment from other species can be used to facilitate extraction, several species‐specific dental features should be taken into consideration when oral extraction is performed. CT imaging offers valuable insights beyond radiographs, enhancing treatment precision and success, especially in cases of severe and chronic disease and when infected tooth roots are close to adjacent neighbouring teeth.
