Staurosporine Targets Mitochondrial Regulator VdAtuA3 to Disrupt Mitochondrial Homeostasis to Control Verticillium Wilt
Authors: Ruo‐Cheng Sheng, Huan Li, Jun Wang, Li‐Chao Wang, Hui‐Zi Liu, Xiao‐Feng Dai, Steven J. Klosterman, Jie‐Yin Chen, Krishna V. Subbarao, Feng‐Mao Chen, Dan‐Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMitochondria serve as cellular powerhouses that generate ATP via electron transport chain complexes and orchestrate metabolism‐apoptosis cross‐talk, yet genes maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis remain underexplored as antifungal targets. In this study, a previously uncharacterised protein VdAtuA3 was identified as a novel interactor with VdNuo1 (NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase 24‐kDa subunit), defining a regulatory axis for the mitochondrial respiratory chain in Verticillium dahliae. Notably, VdNuo1 is highly conserved across plants, animals, and humans, consistent with its essential role in respiration, whereas VdAtuA3 homologues in non‐fungal organisms share very low sequence similarity. Moreover, Y2H assays confirmed no interaction between Nuo1 and AtuA homologues from above organisms, indicating that this regulatory axis is specific to V. dahliae. The pathogen virulence factor VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1 co‐regulate oxidative phosphorylation and superoxide detoxification, thereby promoting mitochondrial homeostasis. Staurosporine (STS), a natural microbial product, targets VdAtuA3, inhibiting its function and reducing the interaction between VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction in V. dahliae, and suppressing its growth effectively controls Verticillium wilt. STS shows high efficacy with minimal off‐target toxicity and can be safely applied to cotton, zebrafish, and human cells at fungistatic doses. Its broad‐spectrum activity against multiple filamentous fungi, consistent with its high binding affinity to AtuA homologues from various filamentous fungi as demonstrated by molecular docking and functional complementation assays, further supports its potential as a promising antifungal agent. Our study provides a proof‐of‐concept for targeting pathogen‐specific virulence factors that regulate mitochondrial homeostasis as a novel strategy to manage fungal plant pathogens.

Introduction

The mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) establishes the proton gradient that drives oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), the primary process of ATP production in eukaryotic cells (Vercellino and Sazanov 2022). Electrons are sequentially transferred through complexes I/II → III → IV to oxygen, driving the pumping of protons (H+) across the membrane to establish an electrochemical gradient (Kohler et al. 2023). The proton flow from the matrix into the intermembrane space of mitochondria (IMS), powers adenosine triphosphate (ATP) synthases, which condense adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and phosphate to form ATP (Hirst 2013; Vercellino and Sazanov 2022). MRC is also functionally important in fungal pathogenicity, particularly in the context of adaptation to challenging environments (e.g., iron homeostasis, the response to stress, metabolic flexibility, antifungal resistance and phenotypic changes) (Black et al. 2021). The transcription factor Mig1 of Cryptococcus neoformans regulates the expression of genes encoding mitochondrial functions, and a mig1 mutant is sensitive to MRC inhibitors and oxidative stress (Caza et al. 2016). Functional defects in the component Ndi1p resulted in the abolishment of sporulation in Saccharomyces cerevisiae (Zhao et al. 2018). Knockdown of ndufs8 (23 kDa subunit of the I), modified hyphal architecture and compromising oxidative stress resistance in Monascus purpureus (Cai et al. 2022). Deletion of Nuo2, which is a subunit of MRC complex I inhibited hyphal growth and biofilm formation on non‐fermentative carbon sources like glucose in Candida albicans (Huang et al. 2017). The NADH:ubiquinone oxidoreductase 24‐kDa subunit (VdNuo1) of complex I mediates global transcriptional effects, including oxidation and reduction processes, as well as fatty acid, sugar, and energy metabolism in V. dahliae (Li et al. 2024). Genes associated with the mitochondria in fungi govern core life processes such as energy metabolism, apoptosis, and stress responses, and thus their functional integrity directly determines survival and virulence. Elucidating the roles of mitochondrial genes in fungal cellular processes promises to reveal untapped mechanisms and accelerate the discovery of mechanistically novel antifungal strategies.

A mono‐alkyl lipophilic cation (MALC), named C18‐SMe2 +, which inhibits oxidative phosphorylation by affecting nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) oxidation, induces damaging mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) at the level of respiratory complex I, triggering fungal apoptosis as well as plant defence (Steinberg et al. 2020). Rotenone acts on complex I, which blocks electron transfer from NADH to ubiquinone (CoQ), thereby inhibiting proton pumping and disrupting proton gradient formation (Heo et al. 2022). Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) inhibitors, such as fluopyram and benzovindiflupyr, bind to the ubiquinone‐binding site (Q‐site) of SDH to block electron transfer with low mammalian toxicity (Hervé and Themis 2010; Horsefield et al. 2006). Qo‐site inhibitors such as azoxystrobin and trifloxystrobin act on Complex III by blocking electron transfer from ubiquinol (QH2) to cytochrome c 1, consequently inhibiting proton pumping (Bartlett et al. 2002; Luo et al. 2010). Cyanide and azide, inhibitors of complex IV, bind to the heme a3‐Cuʙ active center to block electron transfer to oxygen and inhibit proton translocation (Solomonson 1981). Oligomycin inhibits ATP synthase (complex V) by binding to the F 0 subunit to suppress ATP synthesis (Abrahams et al. 1994). Conventional mitochondrial inhibitors are designed to interfere with the electron transfer activity of a single respiratory complex, a strategy that targets a specific step but overlooks the broader regulatory network that governs mitochondrial function. In contrast, factors that influence mitochondrial morphology, integrity, and the coordinated expression of multiple respiratory complexes operate at a global level. These regulatory components, largely unexplored in fungicide development, may offer a new paradigm for antifungal strategies.

Verticillium dahliae is a soil‐borne plant pathogenic fungus that causes vascular wilt on a broad range of herbaceous annuals and woody perennials, including cotton, lettuce, strawberry, sunflower, olive, and maple tree (Fradin and Thomma 2006; Klosterman et al. 2009; Chen et al. 2021; Li et al. 2017). Verticillium wilt is a challenging disease to control, given that the pathogen produces dormant structures capable of surviving in soil for more than 10 years (Klosterman et al. 2009). The control of V. dahliae relies on soil fumigation and deployment of plant disease resistance (Fradin and Thomma 2006; Klosterman et al. 2009; Chen et al. 2021), but increasing concerns on human health and environmental degradation associated with several fumigants have restricted their use, and development of host resistance is a long‐term undertaking. Some biologicals and fungicides have also been applied for control of Verticillium wilt, microbe‐induced gene silencing to boost crop protection, and the potential exploitation of hyperparasiticism to directly infect pathogenic fungi (Shan et al. 2024; Wen et al. 2025). The performance of biocontrol agents, however, is often compromised by variable field conditions that hinder the colonisation of pathogens and suppressive effects; resistance in plants may also be eroded due to the evolution of virulent variants of the pathogen (Hamrouni et al. 2025; Shikha et al. 2024). Consequently, there is an urgent need to develop novel control measures that are highly effective, environmentally benign, and economically viable, which are of paramount importance. In V. dahliae, the targeting of virulence factors that are central to mitochondrial function remains unexplored, representing a novel and viable strategy for eco‐compatible disease control.

This study examines the previously unannotated V. dahliae mitochondrial sensor‐VdAtuA3, which interacts with mitochondrial complex I subunit VdNuo1. ΔVdAtuA3 mutants exhibited aberrant mitochondrial morphology, severely impaired OXPHOS, and potentiated susceptibility to superoxide stress, indicating dysregulated superoxide anion metabolism and loss of virulence toward the host. Via its interaction with VdNuo1, VdAtuA3 forms a core regulatory module governing mitochondrial electron transport and superoxide anion metabolism and virulence in V. dahliae. Molecular docking identified staurosporine as a high‐affinity VdAtuA3 ligand, which suppresses fungal growth and virulence. Staurosporine treatment compromised the integrity of mitochondrial morphology and function in V. dahliae, thereby conferring robust protection to plants against Verticillium wilt. The broad‐spectrum growth suppression of staurosporine against numerous filamentous fungi revealed its potential to suppress multiple diseases. Critically, staurosporine exhibits minimal phytotoxicity and negligible effects on zebrafish and human cells at fungistatic concentrations.

Results

Identification of VdAtuA3 and Its Interaction With VdNuo1 in Verticillium dahliae

Our previous study had shown that the NADH: ubiquinone oxidoreductase 24‐kDa subunit (VdNuo1) of mitochondrial complex I regulates growth, microsclerotia development, stress tolerance, and virulence in V. dahliae (Li et al. 2024). To explore the regulating network of VdNuo1, we employed yeast two‐hybrid (Y2H) screening and identified that VdAtuA3 (VDAG_02121 in VdLs.17 (Klosterman et al. 2011)) as an interacting partner (Figure 1a). The VdAtuA3 gene comprises one intron and two exons (Figure S1) and contains two acyclic terpene utilisation (Atu) family protein A domains (13–408 aa), with a C‐terminal NAD(P)‐binding domain (451–510 aa) (Figure S1). Further bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and co‐immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays confirmed the interaction between them (Figure 1b,c).

Currently, AtuA domain‐containing proteins are reported only in bacteria, where they function as components of the gene cluster (atuR and atuABCDEFGH), a system dedicated to acyclic terpene utilisation (Atu), confirming their essential role in the Atu pathway that degrades citronellol to acetate, acetyl‐CoA, and acetoacetate (Förster‐Fromme and Jendrossek 2006) (Figure 1d,e). In contrast, homologues of atuABCDEFGH in V. dahliae are not organised into a gene cluster. Furthermore, database searches failed to identify a putative function for VdAtuA, unlike other homologues (Figure 1d). To investigate whether VdAtuA is required for the utilisation of citronellol, we constructed ΔVdAtuA3 and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains; all strains grew similarly on citronellol (Figure 1f,g). To determine potential regulatory relationships among VdAtu genes in V. dahliae, the other seven VdAtu genes were individually knocked out. Knockout of any given gene showed no consistent pattern of effects on the expression of other genes (Figure S2). Collectively, these results indicate VdAtuA3 does not act as a citronellol degrading factor and an absence of direct regulatory relationships between the atu gene homologues in V. dahliae.

VdAtuA3 Regulates Microsclerotia Formation, Melanisation, and Virulence in Verticillium dahliae

RT‐qPCR analysis revealed that VdAtuA3 expression was significantly induced during V. dahliae infection. Transcript levels peaked at 24 h post inoculation (hpi) in cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) and at 48 hpi in tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) (Figure 2a,c), indicating infection‐responsive induction. We drenched cotton and tobacco seedlings with conidial suspensions of WT, ΔVdAtuA3, and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains. Loss of VdAtuA3 caused defects in the virulence of V. dahliae and clearly decreased foliar wilting and vascular discoloration, and significantly reduced hyphal proliferation in infected cotton and tobacco plants (Figure 2b,d–g). To visualise ROS accumulation during infection, cotton roots inoculated with WT, ΔVdAtuA3, and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains were stained with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) and nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT), respectively. At 10 dpi, cotton roots infected with the WT and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains exhibited strong DAB and NBT staining, indicating sustained ROS production due to extensive colonisation and tissue necrosis. In contrast, ΔVdAtuA3‐inoculated roots showed markedly weaker staining, consistent with the impaired growth and failure to establish a persistent infection by ΔVdAtuA3 strains (Figure 2h,i).

The role of VdAtuA3 in growth and morphology was assessed on PDA and basal minimal medium (BMM) (Hu et al. 2013). The ΔVdAtuA3 mutants exhibited defective melanin accumulation on PDA (Figure 1f). On BMM medium: the microsclerotia density was significantly higher at 7 dpi (Figure S3a,b), and vegetative hyphae were nearly absent at 14 dpi (Figure S3a,b), with upregulated key genes involved in melanin biosynthesis in ΔVdAtuA3 strains (Fan et al. 2017; Li et al. 2023) (Figure S3c). VdAtuA3 showed persistent upregulation on PDA but consistent downregulation on BMM, indicating its critical role in nutrient adaptation (Figure S3d,e). Also, all strains penetrated equally at 7 dpi (Figure S3f). ΔVdAtuA3 strains exhibited increased sensitivity to membrane permeability (NaCl, KCl, and CaCl2) and oxidative stress, but enhanced tolerance to cell wall integrity (sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), Congo red (CR), and calcofluor white (CFW)) stresses (Figure S4). Collectively, our findings establish that VdAtuA3 regulates virulence, microsclerotia development, melanisation, and abiotic stress tolerance in V. dahliae.

VdAtuA3 Is Required for Normal Mitochondrial Function in Verticillium dahliae

To investigate the cellular localisation of VdAtuA3 in V. dahliae, a plasmid encoding a VdAtuA3‐GFP fusion protein was introduced into the WT strain AT13 (Li et al. 2017) via transformation. Confocal microscopy revealed that VdAtuA3 showed co‐localisation with the mitochondrial probe Mito‐Tracker Red CMXRos in mitochondria. This result demonstrates that VdAtuA3 localises to mitochondria (Figure 3a–c).

Given the central role of mitochondria in cellular metabolism and the specific interaction of VdAtuA3 with VdNuo1 (a subunit of respiratory Complex I), it is reasonable to propose that VdAtuA3 functions by targeting the MRC. To characterise the functions of VdAtuA3 in MRC, three respiratory chain inhibitors of complex I (rotenone) (Heo et al. 2022), II (carboxin) (Lucas et al. 2015), III (dimercaprol) (Huang et al. 2022), and V (oligomycin) (Shchepina et al. 2002) were introduced to assess the effects of VdAtuA3 deletion on the MRC in V. dahliae. Deletion of VdAtuA3 conferred significant sensitivity to complex I‐III inhibitors and more tolerance to the complex V inhibitor (Figures 3d–g and S5). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that ΔVdAtuA3 cells exhibited altered inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) organisation (Figure 3h). This phenomenon was exacerbated by treatment with complex I‐III inhibitors, and the swollen, disorganised cristae and the mitochondrial distortion were evident (Figure 3h). These results demonstrate that VdAtuA3 deletion severely compromises mitochondrial morphology and function in V. dahliae, suggesting its critical role in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and respiratory chain homeostasis.

VdAtuA3 Is Involved in Both Oxidative Phosphorylation and Superoxide Anion Metabolism Scavenging in Verticillium dahliae

Mitochondrial membrane potential is essential for mitochondrial integrity and was assessed using tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE) assay. Robust TMRE fluorescence was observed in WT and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains, whereas ΔVdAtuA3 strains lost fluorescence, consistent with dissipation of the mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial depolarisation, which is similar to that after exposure to carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP, MRC inhibitor) (Heytler 1979) (Figure 4a). Mitochondrial ATP production depends on maintaining the proton gradient across the inner membrane; membrane depolarisation directly impairs ATP synthesis (Mitchell 1961). Accordingly, ATP levels were significantly reduced in ΔVdAtuA3 but elevated in OE VdAtuA3 extracts (Figure 4b). Fungi possess a “branched respiration chain”, where electrons are provided by complex I and fungal‐specific alternative NADH dehydrogenases (Affourtit et al. 2000). NADH oxidation in ΔVdAtuA3 extracts was ~40% lower than those in WT and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains (Figure 4c), suggesting impaired electron entry. Transcriptional analysis confirmed reduced expression of most respiratory complex genes in ΔVdAtuA3 mutants (Figure S6).

Beyond ATP production, the MRC generate ROS (Spinelli and Haigis 2018). On menadione (generates O2·− through redox cycling) (Singh and Husain 2018) containing medium, ΔVdAtuA3 mutants showed ~15% greater growth inhibition than WT or ECΔVdAtuA3 strains, indicating heightened sensitivity (Figure 4d,e). Menadione treatment exacerbated membrane disruption and dissolution of mitochondrial contents (Figure 4f). Superoxide dismutase (SOD) initiates superoxide anion detoxification by converting superoxide to H2O2, which is then eliminated by Peroxidase (POD), completing a two‐step defence against oxidative damage (Ryder et al. 2013). SOD activity was reduced in ΔVdAtuA3 (~36% vs. WT; ~38% vs. EC) and increased in OE VdAtuA3 (~39% vs. WT; ~58% vs. EC) strains (Figure 4g), and VdSOD genes were downregulated in ΔVdAtuA3 strains (Figure 4h). Mitochondrial SOD activity followed a similar pattern: WT 124.08 U/g; ΔVdAtuA3 decreased ~50% (50.39 54.50 U/g); complemented strain restored it to near WT (129.44130.60 U/g); and OE VdAtuA3 strains increased to 222.07342.34 U/g (Figure S7b). Similarly, POD activity decreased in ΔVdAtuA3 (~55% vs. WT; ~47% vs. EC) and increased in OE VdAtuA3 (~46% vs. WT; ~55% vs. EC) (Figure 4i), with most POD genes downregulated (Figure 4j). Also, ΔVdAtuA3 mutants showed heightened sensitivity to H2O2 (Figure S7c,d) supporting its role in maintaining mitochondrial redox homeostasis.

VdAtuA3 Is Essential for VdNuo1‐Mediated Regulation of Mitochondrial Homeostasis in Verticillium dahliae

To elucidate the functional relationship between VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1, WT_ OE VdNuo1 and ΔVdAtuA3_OE VdNuo1 strains were constructed (Figure 5a,b). Intriguingly, VdNuo1 overexpression in WT V. dahliae increased ATP levels and NADH oxidation capacity. In stark contrast, this enhancement was abolished in ΔVdAtuA3 mutants (Figure 5c,d). Consistently, mitochondrial membrane potential dynamics and morphological alterations mirrored this pattern (Figure 5e,f). ΔVdAtuA3_OE VdNuo1 strains showed significant depolarisation and altered morphological changes (Figure 5e,f), confirming that the absence of VdAtuA3 affects the function of VdNuo1 in mitochondria. Superoxide anion sensitivity (induced by menadione) was increased in ΔVdAtuA3 and VdNuo1 mutants, and WT_OE VdNuo1 strains partially restored resistance, whereas ΔVdAtuA3_OE VdNuo1 strains remained sensitive (Figures 5g and S8a). In planta, ΔVdAtuA3 and ΔVdNuo1 strains showed significantly reduced fungal biomass in infected cotton plants, and ΔVdAtuA3_OE VdNuo1 strains remained severely hypovirulent (Figure 5h,i). Taken together, despite VdNuo1 overexpression, the mitochondrial and virulence defects of the ΔVdAtuA3 mutant remained, suggesting an irreplaceable role for VdAtuA3 in these processes.

Our study investigated that VdNuo1 recruits mitochondria VdSODs to maintain superoxide anion homeostasis (H. Li et al. 2025). Given the functional link between VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1, we investigated whether VdAtuA3 acts as a regulatory component in the VdNuo1‐VdSOD superoxide metabolic pathway. Most VdSOD genes were significantly upregulated in the WT_OE VdAtuA3 strains (Figure S9c,e), whereas VdAtuA3 expression remained stable in ΔVdNuo1 and ΔVdSOD2 strains (Figure S9d). To explore this further, we generated the ΔVdAtuA3_VdSOD2 and ΔVdAtuA3_OE VdSOD2 strains. The ΔVdAtuA3_VdSOD2 strain showed menadione inhibition similar to ΔVdSOD2 strains but greater than the ΔVdAtuA3 strains (Figure S9f,g). Overexpression of VdSOD2 in the ΔVdAtuA3 strain partially rescued the inhibitory phenotype, and this functional recovery was uncoupled from transcript levels (Figure S9b,f,g).

We further assessed mitochondrial functional parameters. Deletion of VdSOD2 alone caused only minor changes in NADH oxidation capacity, ATP synthesis, and mitochondrial morphology compared with the WT strain (Figure S9h,i,j). In contrast, ΔVdAtuA3 strains exhibited pronounced defects in all these parameters, and ΔVdAtuA3_VdSOD2 strains phenocopied ΔVdAtuA3 strains (Figure S9h,i,j). Importantly, overexpression of VdSOD2 in the ΔVdAtuA3 strain failed to restore NADH oxidation, ATP production, or mitochondrial morphology to WT levels, despite partially rescuing superoxide sensitivity (Figure S9h,i,j). Collectively, VdSOD2 specifically compensates for the superoxide‐detoxification defect but cannot correct the broader mitochondrial dysfunctions governed by VdAtuA3. Thus, VdAtuA3 functions upstream of VdSOD2 in the superoxide pathway while also controlling additional mitochondrial processes beyond VdSOD2‐mediated superoxide clearance.

Staurosporine Binds to VdAtuA3 to Disrupt Mitochondrial Homeostasis and Virulence in Verticillium dahliae

To identify fungal inhibitors targeting VdAtuA3, we performed molecular docking and identified staurosporine with high binding affinity (binding energy: −11.0 kcal/mol) forming hydrogen bonds with SER‐321 and THR‐354 (Figure 6a). The MST assay confirmed a strong binding ability (Figure 6b). Moreover, MST assays showed that the dissociation constant (K d ) between VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1 was 0.14 ± 2.92 μM, which increased to 2.05 ± 2.65 μM (Figure 6c) following the addition of staurosporine, indicating a weakened interaction. As a control, tricyclazole (weaker binding by molecular docking, Figure S10) did not affect the interaction, with a K d of 0.26 ± 3.97 μM. Notably, staurosporine impaired mitochondrial function: TEM showed disorganised cristae, TMRE fluorescence decreased (depolarisation), and ATP content and NADH oxidation capacity were reduced (Figure 6d–g). Most members of MRC genes were downregulated in the staurosporine‐treated WT strains (Figure S11). The ΔVdAtuA3 strains exhibited enhanced tolerance to staurosporine (~10% inhibition) compared with WT and ECΔVdAtuA3 strains (~60% inhibition) (Figure 6h,i), while the WT_OE VdAtuA3 strains showed ~70% inhibition (Figure 6h,i). These results demonstrate that VdAtuA3 plays a crucial role in staurosporine‐mediated suppression of V. dahliae, and the inhibition of staurosporine on mycelial growth of V. dahliae was concentration‐dependent, with the half‐maximal effective concentration (EC50) and 90% effective concentration (EC90) values of 1.60 and 10.36 μM, respectively (Figure S12a–c). Moreover, staurosporine demonstrated inhibitory activity against a range of V. dahliae isolates, including Vd991, JR2, DK186, DK178, and VD8 (Figure S12d,e). To validate the target, we introduced point mutations in predicted binding residues of VdAtuA3. The mutant‐complemented strains showed tolerance to staurosporine, confirming VdAtuA3 as the functional target (Figure 6j,k). To examine whether staurosporine can be explored as an antifungal compound to manage Verticillium wilt, we applied staurosporine at 1.60 μM on cotton plants inoculated with V. dahliae (Figure 6l,m). These results demonstrate that staurosporine has the potential to disrupt the VdAtuA3‐dependent virulence of V. dahliae. Phylogenetic analysis showed VdAtuA3 is closely related to homologues in multiple filamentous fungi (Figure S13a). Staurosporine exhibited broad‐spectrum activity: Magnaporthe oryzae (EC50 = 0.62 μM, EC90 = 5.30 μM), Botrytis cinerea (2.07, 7.44 μM), Colletotrichum fructicola, Colletotrichum gloeosporioides, Fusarium oxysporum, Fusarium solani (EC50 = 1.61–2.68 μM; EC90 = 10.46–14.27 μM), and Fusarium graminearum (1.65, 21.63 μM) (Figure S13b–j). Molecular docking revealed high affinity of staurosporine to AtuA homologues from these fungi, with binding free energies of −8.1 to −11.2 kcal/mol (Figure S14a). Heterologous expression of these homologues in the ΔVdAtuA3 mutant restored staurosporine sensitivity, confirming functional conservation of AtuA proteins as staurosporine targets across filamentous fungi (Figure S14b,c).

ADMETlab 3.0 predictions (Lipinski 2004) indicated that staurosporine complies with the Lipinski's Rule of Five (Figure S15a). No significant toxicity was observed in seed germination, root length, fresh weight, or plant height of the staurosporine‐treated cotton seedlings (Figure S15b–g). Discussion

In this study, we investigated the interaction of the mitochondrial regulatory factor VdAtuA3 with VdNuo1 (complex I subunit). Their coordinated regulation of mitochondrial integrity and functional stability underpins the virulence of V. dahliae. Computational screening pinpointed staurosporine as a VdAtuA3‐targeting antifungal agent that blocks V. dahliae virulence via mitochondrial sabotage (Figure 7). Crucially, there was no observed toxicity to plants, zebrafish, human cells, or biocontrol bacteria at fungistatic doses. The extensive activity of staurosporine against filamentous fungi underscores its potential as a broad‐spectrum fungal inhibitor.

A proposed model of VdAtuA3 function in the virulence of Vericillium dahliae. Acyclic terpene utilisation family protein VdAtuA3 is located in the mitochondrial matrix and functions in NADH oxidation, ATP synthesis and superoxide anion metabolism associated with the mitochondrial respiratory chain. VdAtuA3 interacts with VdNuo1 to form a core regulatory module governing mitochondrial electron transport and superoxide anion metabolism and virulence in V. dahliae. Staurosporine as a high‐affinity VdAtuA3 ligand, specifically binds to VdAtuA3 to inhibit its function, which suppresses fungal growth by inhibiting the integrity of mitochondrial morphology and function, and confers robust protection against Verticillium wilt.

We previously identified VdNuo1 (complex I) as a mediator of mitochondrial homeostasis (Li et al. 2024). VdAtuA3 was its binding partner (Figure 1a–c). Unlike P. aeruginosa , where AtuA is essential for acyclic terpene utilisation (Atu) (Förster‐Fromme and Jendrossek 2006), VdAtus are dispersed across multiple chromosomes without clustering or operonic linkage, and ΔVdAtuA3 mutants show no growth defect on citronellol (Figures 1d,f,g and S2). Instead, loss of VdAtuA3 disrupted virulence, stress tolerance, and microsclerotia development (Figures 2, S3, and S4), suggesting a distinct functional niche in virulence or host adaptation. Mitochondrial protein defects disrupt OXPHOS, a critical mitochondrial function (Wen et al. 2025). VdAtuA3 localises to mitochondria, its deletion increases sensitivity to complex I‐III inhibitors but confers tolerance to the complex V inhibitor (Figures 3 and S5), reflecting impaired upstream electron transfer, and causes mitochondrial depolarisation and impairs ATP production (Figure 4a,b), and inhibits NADH oxidation at complex I, which contains fungal‐specific subunits (Wirth et al. 2016) (Figure 4c). Coordinated downregulation of MRC complex genes in ΔVdAtuA3 mutants explains these defects, positioning VdAtuA3 as a master regulator of mitochondrial bioenergetics (Figure S6). These findings suggest that VdAtuA3 may exert pleiotropic effects on mitochondrial function by influencing the expression of multiple respiratory chain complexes, and may have additional targets beyond VdNuo1, a key question for our future studies. Superoxide anions derive mainly from the MRC and are key signalling and oxidative stress mediators (Zhao et al. 2019). VdAtuA3 also regulates superoxide anion metabolism (Figure 4d–f) and impairs ROS‐scavenging enzymes (SOD/POD) and suppresses gene expression (Figure 4g,h,i,j). Together, these results establish VdAtuA3 as essential for mitochondrial homeostasis and respiration.

The regulatory networks of MRC complexes remain largely unexplored in V. dahliae (Li et al. 2024). Overexpression of VdNuo1 failed to rescue the mitochondrial defects caused by VdAtuA3 deletion, indicating that VdAtuA3 plays an irreplaceable role (Figures 5 and S8). VdNuo1 is a manipulator of VdSOD, both of which participate in superoxide anion catabolism (P. Li et al. 2025) and mtSODs localise to the supercomplex for ROS scavenging (Suthammarak et al. 2013). VdAtuA3 acts as a pleiotropic regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis, and its positive effect on VdSOD2 expression is likely indirect, mediated through global mitochondrial dysfunction or downstream signalling rather than direct transcriptional control (Figure S9). Although VdSOD2 compensates for the superoxide detoxification defect caused by VdAtuA3 deletion (Figure S9f,g), it fails to restore broader mitochondrial dysfunctions such as NADH oxidation, ATP synthesis, or morphological integrity (Figure S9h,i,j), indicating that VdAtuA3 controls a broader network. To further dissect the underlying mechanism, future studies should investigate whether VdAtuA3 influences the expression or activity of known transcription factors that regulate VdSOD genes. Additionally, protein–protein interaction screens may uncover bridging factors that link VdAtuA3 to the transcriptional machinery, thereby enabling indirect regulation without direct DNA binding. These approaches will help clarify the indirect regulatory cascade connecting VdAtuA3 to VdSOD expression. While our results establish that a mitochondrial‐localised protein functions in concert with MRC complex I subunit to maintain mitochondrial homeostasis and virulence, future studies will dissect whether VdAtuA3 directly modulates VdNuo1 enzymatic activity, affects the cellular redox state into complex I, or acts through post‐translational modifications.

Mitochondria are valuable targets for fungicide development due to their roles in ATP production, lipid homeostasis, and programmed cell death (Kulkarni et al. 2019). Following targeted compound screening, we identified staurosporine as exhibiting strong binding affinity to VdAtuA3 and effectively inhibiting the growth and mitochondrial homeostasis of V. dahliae, with an EC50 value (1.60 μM, ~0.75 μg/mL) (Figure S12a–c) that are lower than those of numerous available fungicides against Verticillium wilt, including triadimefon (59.55 μg/mL), kresoxim‐methyl (1.10 μg/mL), benzovindiflupyr (0.07–11.28 μg/mL), tebuconazole (0.11–4.86 μg/mL), and difenoconazole (90 μg/mL), prochloraz (30 μg/mL), and azoxystrobin (71.95 mg/L) (Hu et al. 2025; K. Li et al. 2013; Kurt et al. 2003; Mihajlović et al. 2021) (Figures 6a–i and S12). Staurosporine attenuates VdAtuA3‐VdNuo1 binding (Figure 6c). Molecular docking revealed that the VdAtuA3‐VdNuo1 binding interface (e.g., THR‐68, ILE‐202, ASP‐128) is distinct from the staurosporine‐binding site (SER‐321, THR‐354), with comparable binding free energies (−11.0 and −11.2 kcal/mol) (Figure S16). Mutations at SER‐321 and THR‐354 abolished staurosporine sensitivity without disrupting VdAtuA3‐VdNuo1 interaction, indicating that staurosporine does not compete for the same site (Figure 6j,k). We propose that staurosporine binding induces a mutually exclusive state through subtle conformational adjustments or steric interference at adjacent interfaces, thereby weakening VdAtuA3‐VdNuo1 interaction, which disrupts mitochondrial morphology, NADH oxidation, ATP synthesis, and superoxide anion metabolism, thus effectively protecting against Verticillium wilt (Figures 6d–g and S11).

Notably, staurosporine exhibited strong binding affinity to AtuA homologues from multiple filamentous fungi, and heterologous expression restored staurosporine sensitivity in the ΔVdAtuA3 mutant (Figures S13 and S14), supporting its potential as a broad‐spectrum antifungal agent. Some mitochondrial inhibitors are confined to laboratory research due to toxicity concerns and are unable to achieve widespread application as fungicides in agriculture (Abrahams et al. 1994). Staurosporine is a well‐known broad‐spectrum protein kinase inhibitor (Gani and Engh 2010); it shows no detectable toxicity to cotton, zebrafish, human cells, or biocontrol bacteria at fungistatic concentrations (Figure S15b–j). This fungal specificity is explained by: (1) Mechanistic Basis: Molecular docking revealed that staurosporine bound to the protein kinase with a binding free energy of −9.1 kcal/mol, lower than its binding affinity for VdAtuA3 (−11.0 kcal/mol) (Figure S17a). Our molecular docking and sequence analyses reveal that its interaction with VdAtuA3 involves distinct binding residues that are not conserved in human kinases (Figure S17). (2) Phylogenetic Distribution: VdAtuA3 and its filamentous fungal homologues are evolutionarily distant from potential orthologs in non‐fungal organisms (Figure S13a), and the sequence conservation between VdAtuA3 and its counterparts in plants, animals, and humans is also very weak (Figure S19). In contrast, the substantial recovery of staurosporine sensitivity in V. dahliae strains heterologously expressing AtuA homologues from other filamentous fungi (inhibition rates of 46.37%–53.53%) confirms that the antifungal activity is specifically mediated through targeting of AtuA proteins, which are not conserved in plants and animals (Figure S14b,c). (3) Dosage Window: Staurosporine effectively inhibited V. dahliae at a low concentration (EC50 = 1.60 μM). Notably, no toxicity was observed in cotton, zebrafish, human cells, or biocontrol bacteria at this concentration, nor at a higher dose of 10.36 μM (EC90). These results indicate a wide therapeutic window, wherein the fungistatic dose remains well below the levels that would impact animal kinases or plant cellular processes.

Interestingly, we found that VdNuo1 is highly conserved across plants, animals, and humans, consistent with its essential role in mitochondrial respiration (Figure S18). Sequence alignment revealed that VdNuo1 shares a conserved 2Fe‐2S cluster‐binding domain with the bovine 24‐kDa subunit (NDUFV2), including strict conservation of the core cysteine residues that coordinate the Fe‐S cluster (Zu et al. 2002; Vinothkumar et al. 2014). These structural similarities, together with their established role in electron transport chain homeostasis and redox regulation, support the evolutionary conservation of this essential complex I component from mammals to fungi (Zhu et al. 2016). In contrast, VdAtuA3 homologues in non‐fungal organisms share low sequence similarity with the fungal protein and lack the key residues required for staurosporine binding (Figure S19). Y2H assays confirmed that no interaction occurs between Nuo1 and AtuA3 homologues from G. hirsutum , N. benthamiana, Danio rerio , or Homo sapiens (Figure S20). These findings indicate that the VdAtuA3‐VdNuo1 regulatory axis is specific to V. dahliae and likely represents a fungus specific mechanism, providing a molecular basis for the selective activity of staurosporine, and supporting its potential as a safe antifungal agent. As a microbial natural product originally isolated from Streptomyces species, staurosporine may have limited environmental persistence due to catabolic pathways in soil microbial communities (Tamaoki et al. 1986; Amichot et al. 2025). While detailed degradation kinetics remain to be determined, its natural origin suggests potential for biodegradation that could limit accumulation (Romdhane et al. 2018). The environmental fate of a candidate fungicide depends on multiple factors, including soil type, organic matter content, pH, and microbial activity, and such studies typically require microcosm or field‐scale experiments (Chen and Edwards 2001; Campan et al. 2024). Future investigations into soil persistence, transformation pathways, and impacts on soil microbial community structure will be essential to fully evaluate the ecological safety of staurosporine.

Materials and Methods

Bioinformatics Analysis and Toxicity Prediction

Acyclic terpene utilisation A (Pfam ID: PF07287) in V. dahliae was predicted using the National Center for Biological Information (NCBI) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and SMART (http://smart.embl‐heidelberg.de/). The VdAtuA3 and its homologous sequence was analysed by the Verticilli‐Omics (https://db.cngb.org/Verticilli‐Omics/database) and UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org/). A phylogenetic tree was constructed by MEGA 5.1 with the NJ algorithm (http://www.megasoftware.net/). The database ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) was used for the assessment of drug‐likeness.

Microbe Cultivation Conditions

The fungal strains were grown on potato dextrose agar (PDA, 20% potato, 2% glucose, 1.5% agar). The bacteria were grown on Luria‐Bertani (LB: 1% tryptone, 0.5% yeast extract, 1% NaCl) medium containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin or 50 μg/mL ampicillin at 37°C. Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain AGL‐1 (preserved at Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences) was cultured in LB medium containing 50 μg/mL rifampicin at 28°C. Yeast strains for Y2H (preserved at Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences) were incubated at 30°C.

Generation of Gene Deletion, Complementation and Overexpression Mutants

The 5′‐and 3′‐flanking regions of target gene from WT strain were inserted into the KpnI‐ or XbaI‐linearised pGKO2 plasmid (Gui et al. 2018) for generating the deletion recombinant vector. To produce complementary transformants, the fragment for the target gene with its native promoter (0.8–1.2 kb) and terminator (0.5–0.8 kb) was inserted into the XbaI/EcoRI‐digested pCOM vector (Zhou et al. 2013). To produce transformants overexpressing select genes, the coding sequence was inserted into the XbaI/SacI‐digested pCOM‐T0161 vector (with TrpC promoter) (Zhou et al. 2013). A. tumefaciens ‐mediated V. dahliae transformation was performed (Liu et al. 2013) and transformants screened by PCR. All primers used are shown in Table S1.

Fluorescence Microscopy

The CDS of VdAtuA3 was cloned into a XhoI‐digested PYF11 vector and transferred into protoplasts of the WT strain (Li et al. 2023). The fluorescence signals were observed utilizing a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscopy system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) for GFP channels (excitation wavelength (Ex) 450–490 nm, emission wavelength (Em) 500–550 nm). Mito‐Tracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1049B‐50) was used as the mitochondrial dye and observed using RFP channels (Ex 540–552, Em 575–640).

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) of fungal cells was as described (Steinberg et al. 2020) using a Hitachi Limited HT7800 transmission electron microscope, operated at 80 kV. Images were taken with a digital camera (ES 1000 W, Gatan, Abingdon, UK). The samples were cut into 80–100 mm slices using a Leica EM UC7 Ultramicrotome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and adhered to a nickel mesh; the slices were redyed by 2% lead citrate and 2% uranyl acetate.

Phenotypic Characterisation, Evaluation of Penetration and Microsclerotium Formation

Hyphal blocks (1 × 1 mm) were inoculated on PDA plates containing 1 M KCl, 0.4 M CaCl2, 0.8 M NaCl, 0.02% SDS (Solarbio, Beijing, China, S8010), 200 μg/mL Congo red (Solarbio, Beijing, China, C8450), 20 μg/mL calcofluor white (CFW) (Sigma, Germany, 1890), H2O2 (5, 7.5 and 10 mM), 30 μM menadione (Solarbio, Beijing, China, V8170), citronellol (Aladdin, Shanghai, China, C420494) and staurosporine (MAKLIN, Shanghai, China, S817469) was examined to investigate the response to them. To observe microsclerotia formation, 20 μL of a conidial suspension (1 × 106/mL) was dropped onto a cellophane membrane (Ø = 70 mm, Solarbio, Beijing, China, YA0620) overlaid on basal minimal medium (BMM) (5 g glucose, 0.2 g NaNO3, 0.52 g KCl, 0.52 g MgSO4·7H2O, 1.52 g KH2PO4, 3 μM vitamin B1, 0.1 mM vitamin H, 15 g agar, pH 7.5 in 1 L). The development of microsclerotia was observed after 3, 5, 7, and 14 dpi and ImageJ software (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/) was used to calculate the grey value. In penetration experiments, strains were incubated on MM plates covered with cellophane membranes for 48 h and all plates were incubated for another 5 days after membranes were removed.

Interaction Assays

Yeast Two‐Hybrid Assays

Bait (pGBKT7‐VdNuo1) and prey (pGADT7‐VdAtuA3) vectors were co‐transformed into Y2H gold (Takara, Beijing, China, 630 439). pGBKT7‐53/pGADT7 and pGBKT7‐Lam/pGADT7 were used as positive and negative controls. Interactions were assessed on SD‐Leu‐Trp (DDO) and SD‐Leu‐Trp‐His‐Ade (QDO) (with 20 μg/mL X‐α‐Gal (Coolaber, Beijing, China, CX11922) and 200 ng/mL ABA (Coolaber, Beijing, China, CX11922)) medium.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) Assays

Full‐length CDS was cloned into pCOM‐T0161 (Zhou et al. 2013) with Flag tag and HA tag, respectively. Total proteins were incubated with anti‐HA tag‐mAb (agarose conjugated) affinity beads (Abmart, Shanghai, China, M20031S). The samples were subjected to immunoblot analyses with the antibodies α‐HA (26D11) mouse mAb (Abmart, Shanghai, China, M20003S) and α‐FLAG DYKDDDDK‐Tag (3B9) mouse mAb (Abmart, Shanghai, China, M20008S) (diluted 1:5000 in TBST).

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BIFC) Assays

VdAtuA3‐YFPC and VdNuo1‐YFPN were co‐introduced into the WT strain (Zhang et al. 2020). Transformants were selected with 50 μg/mL hygromycin (Coolaber, Beijing, China, CH6361) and 250 μg/mL zeocin (Thermo, Shanghai, China, R25001), stained with Mito‐Tracker Red CMXRos (Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C1049B), and the YFP signals (Ex 510–560 nm, Em 500–550 nm) were observed by Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscopy (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Filamentous Fungal Mitochondrial Isolation Assay

Fungal samples were collected from PDA medium at 7 dpi, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground into a fine powder. Then, 50–100 mg fungal powder was mixed with 500 μL extract solution. The mitochondria of V. dahliae were isolated using a BBproExtra Filamentous Fungal Mitochondrial Isolation Kit (bestbio, Shanghai, China, BB‐36018).

SOD and POD Enzyme Activity Assays

The enzyme activities of SOD and POD were measured using a SOD Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, BC0170) and a POD Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, BC0095), respectively. Fungal samples were collected, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen, and ground into a fine powder. Then, 0.1 g fungal powder was mixed with 1 mL extract solution. After centrifugation, the supernatants were used for enzyme activity measurement in a 96‐well plate with a Flexstion 3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA) at the requisite wavelengths (560 nm for SOD; 470 nm for POD).

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Assay

Mitochondrial membrane potential was visualised with tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRE; Beyotime, Shanghai, China, C2001S). A 10 μL conidial suspension (1 × 104 spores/mL) was cultured at 25°C for 12 h to induce conidiation. TMRE was added to 1 mL of conidial suspension and incubated at 25°C for 20 min. The fluorescence signals were observed using a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscopy system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and emission of 510 nm. The conidial suspension of WT treated with carbonyl cyanide m‐chlorophenylhydrazone (CCCP) was included as the control.

Test for the Activity of NADH Oxidases and ATP in Cell Extracts

Cells of the WT, ΔVdAtuA3, ECΔVdAtuA3 , and OE VdAtuA3 strains were grown for 72 h at 18°C, at 200 rpm, in CM liquid medium. The activity of NADH oxidases and ATP was detected using the NADH‐Lite Assay Kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China, A114‐1‐1) and ATP‐Lite Assay Kit (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China, A095‐1‐1), respectively.

Virulence Assays

Virulence assays on cotton ( Gossypium hirsutum L.) and tobacco (Nicotiana benthamiana) cultivars were performed using root‐dip and root‐drench inoculation (Li et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2019). Disease was quantified following the method of Santhanam and Thomma (2013); 20 cotton and 5 tobacco plants were inoculated with each strain. The disease severity on cotton was scored into four categories based on defoliation percentage (Grade 0: 0%–25%, Grade 1: 25%–50%, Grade 2: 50%–75%, and Grade 3: 75%–100%) (Fan et al. 2017). Verticillium wilt symptoms were recorded at 14 dpi for tobacco or at 21 dpi for cotton. To further test the applications of staurosporine against Verticillium wilt, two treatment methods were employed: (1) a mixture of the compound and fungal conidia suspension was applied as a soil drench to the cotton roots; (2) the conidia suspension was first drenched onto the roots, followed by the staurosporine solution 5 days later.

Histochemical Detection of Superoxide

3,3′‐diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining (H. Li et al. 2013) and nitrotetrazolium blue chloride (NBT) staining (Wohlgemuth et al. 2002) were used to detect ROS in cotton stems at 10 dpi. Stems were incubated in 0.5 mg/mL DAB (pH = 3.0–3.8) and 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8) containing 0.1% NBT for 8 h, boiled in 95% (v/v) ethanol, then photographed using a Leica DM2500 confocal microscopy system (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany).

Molecular Docking

The three‐dimensional structure of the target protein was obtained from the RCSB PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/) and optimised using Discovery Studio 2019, which included removal of water molecules, addition of hydrogen atoms, charge completion, and side‐chain refinement. The ligand structure was downloaded from PubChem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and energy‐minimised using Discovery Studio 2019. Both protein and ligand were converted to PDBQT format with AutoDockTools 4.0. Docking simulations were carried out using AutoDock Vina 1.2.6. Visualisation and interaction analysis were performed with PyMOL 3.1 and Discovery Studio 2019.

Microscale Thermophoresis (MST) Assays

Recombinant VdAtuA3 (GST‐tagged) and VdNuo1 (His‐tagged) were separately expressed in E. coli ArcticExpress competent cells (Agilent Technologies, USA). Protein expression was induced with 0.2 mM IPTG at 15°C overnight. VdAtuA3 and VdNuo1 were purified using GST and Ni‐IDA affinity chromatography, respectively. Purity was confirmed by SDS‐PAGE, showing single bands at the expected molecular weights. The target protein was labelled using the RED‐tris‐NTA kit (NanoTemper, Beijing, China, MO‐L018) and a labeling efficiency of 0.8–1.0 mol dye/mol protein was achieved. MST measurements of recombinant GST‐VdAtuA3 with VdNuo1 or staurosporine were performed on a Monolith NT.115 Label Free instrument. Labeling efficiency and aggregation were evaluated by pretesting diluted samples (10–150 nM in assay buffer +0.05% Tween‐20). Labelled proteins were incubated with serial dilutions of VdNuo1 or staurosporine, loaded into standard capillaries, and measured at 20% LED and 40% MST power. Data were analysed using NanoTemper software (v.1.5.41) to calculate K d values.

Safety Assessment of Staurosporine

Seed Germination Assays

Cotton seeds were placed on water agar containing staurosporine and incubated in the dark at 25°C. Germination rates were recorded at 48 hpi from three independent plates per treatment.

Phytotoxicity Assays

Staurosporine concentrations (0 , 1.60, and 10.36 μM) were applied as the roots of cotton seedlings (50 mL/pot, 20 seedlings per treatment) three times at 5‐day intervals. The fresh weight, seedlings height and root length were measured at 20 dpi.

Acute Toxicity Analysis of Zebrafish

Ten zebrafishes were placed in 2 L of dechlorinated water containing concentrations of 0, 1.60, and 10.36 μM staurosporine. Each treatment had three parallel water tanks, and the mortality of each tank was calculated after 96 h of exposure to staurosporine. The acute toxicity assay was carried out with the approval of the independent animal ethics committee of Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences.

Cytotoxicity Assay of Human Cells

Human colon cancer SW620 cells (1 × 105 cells/mL) were inoculated in 96‐well plates at 37°C, treated with staurosporine (0, 1.60, and 10.36 μM) for 24 h, and cell proliferation was assessed with CCK‐8 assay (Solarbio, Beijing, China, CA1210) following the manufacturer's protocol (Lin et al. 2022).

Bacterial Toxicity Assay

The cytotoxicity of staurosporine toward reported biocontrol strains, including KRS005, KRS009, KRS010, KRS015, and KRS022 was assessed. Each strain was cultured in LB medium containing dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), 1.60 or 10.36 μM staurosporine. Colony morphology and OD600 were measured at 48 hpi.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription‐Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated after all strains were cultured on PDA medium or PDA supplemented with the required substances in the experiment and grown at 25°C for 7 days. To determine the expression profile of VdAtuA3 during infection, cotton and tobacco were inoculated with 1 × 109 conidia/mL suspension of WT strains and collected at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, 72, 120, 168, 216, and 336 h post‐inoculation (hpi) for RNA isolation. RT‐qPCR analysis for the expression of VdAtuA3 was carried out using the V. dahliae VdEF‐1α for normalisation according to the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen 2001). Fungal biomass in infected plants was assessed by qPCR using the cotton 18S rDNA gene and N. benthamiana NbActin (Zheng et al. 2019) as internal plant reference genes. The DNA of V. dahliae was quantified using the target of elongation factor 1α gene VdEF‐1α (Li et al. 2023). All primers used are shown in Table S1.

Statistical Analysis

At least three biological replicates were employed for each experiment. Each result is presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three replicated measurements. The significance of differences between treatments was statistically evaluated using SDs and one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) in Microsoft Excel 97–2003. Treatment significance was assessed using the F‐test in the ANOVA. Means were compared using the Fisher's protected LSD and significance was evaluated at *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01 or ***p < 0.001.
