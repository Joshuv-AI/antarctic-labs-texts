Potential role of calcium sulfate/β-tricalcium phosphate/graphene oxide nanocomposite for bone graft application_mechanical and biological analyses
Authors: Yung-Chang Lu, Ting-Kuo Chang, Tzu-Chiao Lin, Shu-Ting Yeh, Hung-Shih Lin, Qiao-Ping Cheng, Chun-Hsiung Huang, Hsu-Wei Fang, Chang-Hung Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone grafts are extensively used for repairing bone defects and voids in orthopedics and dentistry. Moldable bone grafts offer a promising solution for treating irregular bone defects, which are often difficult to fill with traditional rigid grafts. However, practical applications have been limited by insufficient mechanical strength and rapid degradation.MethodsThis study developed a ceramic composite bone graft composed of calcium sulfate (CS), β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) with/without graphene oxide (GO) nano-particles. The biomechanical properties, degradation rate, and in-vitro cellular responses were investigated. In addition, the graft was implanted in-vivo in a critical-sized calvarial defect model.ResultsThe results showed that the compressive strength significantly improved by 135% and the degradation rate slowed by 25.5% in comparison to the control model. The addition of GO nanoparticles also improved cell compatibility and promoted osteogenic differentiation in the in-vitro cell culture study and was found to be effective at promoting bone repair in the in-vivo animal model.ConclusionsThe mixed ceramic composites presented in this study can be considered as a promising alternative for bone graft applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-024-05142-8.Keywords: Calcium sulfate, Β-tricalcium phosphate, Graphene oxide, Mechanical strength, Degradation rate, Critical-sized calvarial defect model

Background

Bone grafts are extensively used for repairing bone defects and voids. Bone defects typically take about six to eight weeks to heal [1], but may be influenced by the patient’s age, health, and the location of the bone defect. Although bone has the capability to self-heal after injury, large segmental bone defects resulting from trauma, cancer or osteoporosis can only be repaired by bone grafting [2]. Autografts are considered the gold standard for bone repair. However, due to limited quantity and donor site morbidity, various bone graft substitutes have been developed [3]. Biodegradable synthetic bone grafts illicit a negligible immunological reaction, do not pose a risk of disease transmission, and are readily available on demand, meaning they are a practical cost-effective solution for repairing bone defects.

Synthetic bone grafts are available in various forms, and moldable products such as paste [2, 4] or putty [4, 5] can be shaped to fill irregular bone defects. Calcium sulfate (CaSO4, CS) and β-tricalcium phosphate (Ca3(PO4)2, β-TCP) are ceramic-based synthetic bone graft materials which have been widely used as for injectable and moldable bone grafts over the past decade, both as a pair and individually. Both CS and β-TCP have been demonstrated with excellent biocompatibility and osteo-conductivity to promote bone regeneration [6]. In addition, β-TCP is the major inorganic material in natural human bones [7, 8], releasing calcium and sulfate ions as substrates for bone growth. Ideally, the degradation rate of biodegradable materials should match to the growth rate of new bone. However, the different resorption rates of CS and β-TCP (CS:4–12 weeks, β-TCP:18 months) [9, 10] means that CS is absorbed in vivo up to 30 times faster than β-TCP [11]. Therefore, β-TCP demonstrates a relatively balanced degradation rate in relation to new bone formation [12].

Furthermore, the compressive strength of CS (Wet CS: 10–15 MPa; Dry CS: 20–30 MPa) is considerably greater than cancellous bone (5–10 MPa) [13]. On the other hand, β-TCP has a considerably lower compressive strength of 2.23 to 3.57 MPa [14], which may fracture under compression and would offer little resistance to fatigue loading. Thus, a composite of CS and β-TCP that combines the properties of both materials may overcome the above-mentioned limitations. However, a CS and β-TCP composite is still be unsuitable for weight-bearing applications as it would be susceptible to scaffold collapse or internal fracture [12]. Consequently, currently available CS/β-TCP moldable bone grafts are primarily used in low-weight-bearing osseous defects, such as filling voids in the pelvis, facial region and in the wrist [4]. Advances in material sciences and fabrication techniques may broaden the potential applications of these moldable materials.

Graphene oxide (GO), composed of highly oxidized graphene derivatives, has been reported with outstanding mechanical strength [15, 16], an ability to promote cell attachment and proliferation [17, 18], and with better osteogenic differentiation and mineralization capability than graphene [17, 19]. The structure of GO also provides a large surface area for loading biomolecules and medicine for drug delivery, bacterial resistance, and gene therapy [20, 21]. Our previous animal study also found that, while GO nano-particles do not increase the risk of osteolysis, they also offer potential benefits of promoting bone formation [22]. Studies have shown that graphene and its derivatives have potential applications as orthopedic biomaterials, such as being additives biodegradable polymers [16, 23, 24], hydroxyapatite (HA) [25–27], chitosan [16, 28–30] and gelatin/alginate [31, 32].

To the authors’ knowledge, no studies to date have evaluated how GO nanoparticles influence the mechanical behavior and biological properties of CS/β-TCP composite. Hence, this study investigated whether incorporating GO nanoparticles in a CS/β-TCP ceramic composite bone graft could compensate for the insufficient mechanical strength and rapid degradation rate [5, 10]. The biological responses and bone regeneration capability of the bone graft with/without GO were determined via an in vitro cell culture model and in vivo in a rat critical-sized calvarial defect model.

Methods

Preparation of moldable bone graft

Bone graft powders were created using a mixture of calcium sulfate hemihydrate (CaSO4·0.5H2O), β-TCP (Cas No. 7758-87-4, Sigma-Aldrich) and hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC, Cas No. 9004-65-3, Sigma-Aldrich) in an eppendorf tube. Table 1 shows the amount of calcium sulfate and β-TCP in the different groups. The CS/β-TCP in each group had a ratio of 1:1 [33]. Hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) was used as a plasticizer to improve the handling characteristics of the moldable bone grafts [33, 34]. The mixtures were vortexed for 120 s, then 70wt% Glycerol and 30wt% ddH2O were added and mixed for 1 min. This process formed the moldable bone graft. GO nanoparticles (single layer graphene oxide, flake size: 0.3–0.7 μm, thickness: 1.1 ± 0.2 nm, Graphene Supermarket, Graphene Laboratories, NY, USA) were suspended in ddH2O under sonication (Q700, QSONICA, Newtown, CT, USA). Then, the GO aqueous dispersion was frozen by placing in a freeze-drying apparatus for 2 days, resulting in a fluffy GO powder (Fig. 1A). Ceramic composite bone grafts were mixed with 0 wt%, 0.25 wt%, 0.5 wt%, 0.75 wt%, and 1.0 wt% GO (Table 1). The control group (0 wt%) was named ‘putty’, and the rest of the groups were named according to the GO composition (P0.25GO, P0.5GO, P0.75GO and P1.0GO).

Characterization of the bone grafts

The microstructure and distribution of GO in the samples was measured by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-7610 F, JEOL, Japan) using energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). An X-ray diffraction meter (XRD, x’pert3 powder, Malvern Panalytical, x’pert3, UK) was used for the phase identification of crystalline materials. The JCPDS numbers were calcium sulfate (JCPDS #33–0311), β-TCP (JCPDS #09-0169) and the peak of GO was according to previous literature [35, 36]. The functional groups of the samples were analyzed by Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Spotlight 200i Sp2, Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) in the range of 400–4000 cm-1.

Mechanical strength of bone grafts

The determine the mechanical strength of the bone grafts, the samples (N = 5/group) were prepared using the method detailed by Gao et al. [14]. The graft material was shaped into a cylindrical stainless steel mold (6 mm in diameter and 12 mm in height) and then removed from the mold after 3 days in ddH2O at room temperature. Sandpaper was used to smoothen the surface of the samples before testing. The maximum compressive strength and Young’s modulus of each sample were measured using a Material Testing System Acumen electrodynamic machine (MTS systems corporation, Minnesota, USA) with a 250 N load cell at a cross-head speed of 1.0 mm/min [33].

In vitro studies

Samples for the in-vitro study were first sterilized by gamma irradiation (Dose of 25 kGy, China Biotech Corporation, Taichung, Taiwan). Cells from a MC3T3-E1 murine pre-osteoblast cell line (ATCC CRL-2594, Manassas, VA, USA) were cultured in minimum essential medium α (α-MEM) (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) and 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Cells were then harvested using a 0.05% trypsin ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Gibco) for 10 min and washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (Gibco). Viable cells were counted using trypan blue dye exclusion before seeding.

Cell proliferation

According to ISO/EN 10993-12 [37], cell proliferation was assessed using an MTT assay (Cell Proliferation Kit I, 11465007001, Roche, Swiss). The samples (solid-to-liquid ratio of 0.2 g/mL [37]) were immersed in 10% culture medium at 37 °C for 24 h, and then the extract medium was obtained. A total of 1 × 104 cells were seeded in 100 µL of medium in a 96-well plate for 24 h. The medium was then removed from each well and replaced with 100 µL of 10× diluted extract medium (α-MEM added to positive controls, and 50% DMSO + 50% α-MEM added to negative controls). After incubating for 3 days, the medium was removed and 50 µL (1 mg/mL) MTT reagent was added into each well and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. The solution was then discarded and 100 µL of DMSO was added in each well and incubated for 15 min. The absorbance of each sample was measured by a microplate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) at a wavelength of 570 nm.

Osteogenic differentiation

The samples were immersed in an osteo-inductive medium (50 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 10mM β-glycerophosphate and 100 nM Dexamethasone) at 37 °C for 24 h, and the extract was obtained. A total of 1 × 104 cells were seeded in a 48-well plate and incubated for 24 h. The medium in each well was then replaced with 400 µL of 10× diluted extract medium (osteo-inductive medium was added to the positive control and α-MEM was added to the negative control). The medium was renewed every 4 days. After incubation for 14 days, an alkaline phosphatase assay kit (ab83369, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was used to assess the ALP activity.

Degradation test

An evaluation of in-vitro degradation of the bone graft was performed in phosphate buffered saline (PBS, pH = 7.4) at 37 °C according to ASTM F1635 [38]. The samples were shaped in a cylindrical mold (6 mm diameter and 3 mm height) and were incubated in ddH2O at 37 °C for 3 days and then dried. The samples were weighed and soaked in PBS solution (Sample: Solution = 1:100) for 7, 14, 21 and 28 days (N = 5 at each time point). The PBS solution was refreshed every week. In-vivo animal studies

Critical-sized calvarial bone defect model

The protocol for this experiment was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of MacKay Memorial Hospital [MMH-A-S-111-44], where the study was performed. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the guideline for the care and use of laboratory animals by the Council of Agriculture, Executive Yuan, Taipei, Taiwan. Animals were kept in a room at 24 °C and 50% humidity, with a 12 h light/dark cycle (light from 7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.). Thirty-two male Sprague-Dawley rats (body weight ranging from 300 to 350 g) were used for the critical-sized calvarial defect model. Animals were randomly divided into 2 groups: (1) Putty group: defect filled with putty (n = 16), and (2) Putty/GO group: defect filled with putty containing graphene (n = 16). Half of the animals in each group were sacrificed at the 4 weeks and 8 weeks post-surgery.

The critical-sized calvarial bone defect model was established [40–42]. The rats were anesthetized with 1.5-2% isoflurane after being administered with 5 mg/kg carprofen by subcutaneous injection. An electric shaver was used to remove the hair of the head. A longitudinal incision on the scalp was performed to expose the calvarium. The periosteum was gently divided and pushed laterally with a periosteal elevator. A critical bone defect was created on the midline of the two parietal bones with an 8-mm diameter dental trephine (Hager & Meisinger GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany). An elevator was used to free the dura from the underside of the bone and then raise the calvarium off the dura. The surgical site was washed with sterile saline and a bone graft 8 mm in diameter and 1 mm in height was placed into the defect without applying pressure to the underlying brain. The periosteal layer was closed with a 4 − 0 bioabsorbable suture (Novosyn®, B.Braun, Tuttlingen, Germany) and the incision was closed with a 3 − 0 non-absorbable suture (Dafilon®, B.Braun, Tuttlingen, Germany).

Micro-CT imaging

Micro-CT was used to analyze the formation of new bone in the defects. The animals were sacrificed after 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery. The skulls were dissected and fixed in 10% buffered formalin for 72 h and then transferred to 70% ethanol before micro-CT scanning. The micro-CT (Skyscan 1076, Bruker Micro-CT, Kontich, Belgium) was set with a 2048 × 2048 pixel matrix (resolution: 9 μm, VOI diameter: 8 mm, height: 0.7 mm). The percentage of new bone area in the defects (VOI diameter: 8 mm) were calculated from the superficial view using the software ImageJ [43].

Histological analysis

The calvaria were decalcificated in 10% EDTA for 4 weeks and then split into two sections along the coronal plane through the median line of the defect. The samples were dehydrated in ethanol and then embedded in paraffin. Cross-Conclusion μm thick were collected and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE stain) and Masson’s trichrome stain. The sections were observed by light field microscopy equipped with a tissue scanner (TissueGnostics GmbH, Vienna, Austria) and the images were captured at 100× magnification.

Statistical analysis

The results were reported as mean ± SD. A two-sided Student’s t-test was performed for independent samples to analyze the difference in means between two groups. The results were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to show the difference between groups. Multiple comparisons were adjusted with a Bonferroni post hoc test. p-values less than 0.05 were considered significantly different.

Results

Characterization of bone grafts

The microstructure of bone grafts with 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, and 1.0 wt% GO were observed by SEM (Fig. 1). XRD analysis was used to determine the crystalline properties of the bone grafts (Fig. 1C), and found that the crystalline structure of the bone grafts with GO (the diffraction peak of GO appears at 2θ = 10°-12°) was not noticeably different than the control group (Fig. 1D). The FTIR spectrum of bone grafts with different concentrations of GO also showed similar peaks to the control group (Fig. 1D), with adsorption bands for the aromatic C = C (1622 cm− 1), C = O (1686 cm− 1) and -OH (3547 cm− 1) [44–46]. The FTIR analysis found there was no change in the function groups of the composites between the groups with and without GO.

Mechanical strength

The results showed that the addition of GO significantly improved the mechanical strength of the graft. The compressive strength was significantly increased in all grafts with GO in comparison to the control group (Fig. 2). The stress-strain curves showed good reproducibility (Fig. 2B). The graft with 0.5% GO had the highest compressive strength and was 135% that of the control group (4.07 ± 0.48 for P0.5GO group, 1.74 ± 0.09 for control group, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2C). The Young’s modulus increased by 265% in the P0.5GO group in comparison to the control group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D). However, mechanical strength dropped as the concentration of GO was higher than 0.5%; showing that 0.5% would be the most optimal concentration among all preliminary tests (Fig. 2C).

In-vitro biocompatibility and osteogenic differentiation

To investigate whether GO influences biological responses in vitro, the cytotoxicity of the bone grafts with different concentrations of GO was measured by MTT assay (Fig. 3A). After 3 days of incubation, all groups with GO showed significantly higher viability than the control group (0.35 ± 0.04 for P0.5GO group and 0.20 ± 0.07 for control group, p < 0.001), indicating that GO nanoparticles may reduce the cytotoxicity of the graft. There was no significant difference among groups with different GO concentrations.

ALP activity was used to determine the osteogenic differentiation capability of the grafts (Fig. 3B). The results showed that ALP activity in the P0.5GO group was significantly greater (136%) than the control group after being incubated for 14 days (1.68 ± 0.29 for P0.5GO group and 0.71 ± 0.17 for control group, p < 0.05). However, increasing the concentration of GO beyond 0.5% resulted in lower and more unstable ALP activity than the control group.

Degradation test in-vitro

Based on the results of the mechanical test and in-vitro cell study, the 0.5%GO graft was chosen for further analysis. The degradation rate was determined by weight loss after the graft was immersed in PBS (N = 5). The results showed that the graft containing GO had a significantly lower weight loss rate than the control group (13.0%, 14.8% and 25.5% lower than the control group after immersion for 2, 3 and 4 weeks, respectively, p < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Overall, the total weight loss of the putty/GO material after 4 weeks was 52.7%, compared to 78.2% for the control group.

In vivo animal study

Critical-sized calvarial defect rat model

A critical-sized calvarial defect model was used to study the in vivo osteogenic performance of the moldable bone grafts (Fig. 5). Two animals died after the initiation of anesthesia and the remaining 30 animals survived until sacrificed during the study. The wounds healed well within 1 week and no obvious changes in animal behaviors were observed for the two groups for the duration of the study.

Micro-CT analysis

From the reconstructed micro-CT images (Fig. 6), new bone formation was observed around the edge of the defect in both groups at 4 weeks. At 8 weeks post-surgery, new bone formation was evident in the center of the defect in the putty/GO group, but there was little evidence of such bone formation in the control group. There was no significant difference in bone formation parameters at 4 weeks post-surgery (BMD, BV/TV and Tb.Th.) (Fig. 6B). However, BMD and Tb.Th. were significantly greater in the putty/GO group at 8 weeks post-surgery. The BMD was 75.6% greater in the putty/GO group than the control group (0.07 ± 0.03 for putty/GO group versus 0.04 ± 0.04 for the control group, p < 0.05), and Tb.Th. was 13.3% greater (0.31 ± 0.03 for putty/GO group versus 0.27 ± 0.03 for the control group). In addition, the percentage of bone regeneration area showed significantly increased in putty/GO group at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery, compared to the control group. These results demonstrate the potential for GO to promote new bone formation.

Histological analysis of bone regeneration

HE staining showed no obvious pathology or inflammatory reactions around the calvarial defect area in both groups (Fig. 7). Four weeks after surgery, moderate new bone tissue was observed close to the edges of the defect boundary, and there was obvious bone formation in the defect area at 8 weeks post-surgery. A thick fibrous tissue layer and blood vessels were detected in the putty/GO group at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery, but not in the control group. GO particles were detected (yellow arrow) with the fibrous tissue and blood vessels surrounding the new bone tissue and boundary.

Masson’s trichrome staining was used to visualize connective tissues and bone tissue (Fig. 7B). The blue dye is used to identify collagen in fibrous tissue and primary mineralized bone depending on tissue types and color intensity, while the red dye indicates mature mineralized bone tissue. The result of the staining showed obvious blue dye in new bone tissue separated by the defect boundary at 4 weeks post-surgery in the both groups. Furthermore, the red dye showed that the area of dense mineralized bone tissue increased after 8 weeks post-surgery.

Discussion

An ideal biodegradable bone graft material should have a porous microstructure for cell adhesion and proliferation, good biocompatibility, and a degradation rate similar to the growth rate of new bone, as well as mechanical properties resembling those of the implant site [47, 48].

Calcium sulfate (CS) and β-TCP have been extensively used as bone graft materials. CS exhibits excellent biocompatibility and osteoconductivity. Calcium ions are released during the dissolution of CS, which combines with phosphate ions, leading to the precipitation of calcium phosphate and stimulating osteoblast differentiation [49]. Moreover, the pH of the surrounding tissue decreases as the CS degrades, which stimulates the release of growth factors and promotes the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells and the mineralization process, ultimately resulting in the formation of new bone [50]. β-TCP, a porous osteoconductive bioactive ceramic compound which is similar to the inorganic phase of natural bone tissue, promotes osteoblast differentiation, angiogenesis and regulates the release of growth factors by releasing ions such as calcium ions and phosphate ions [51]. Additionally, it possesses osteoconductive and osteoinductive properties [52]. However, the lower mechanical strength [14] and mismatched degradation rate [9] of contemporary grafts may not provide effective long-term mechanical support to the defect site [53, 54].

The most important finding of this current study is that the mechanical strength and degradation rate of the moldable graft were significantly improved by 135% and 25.5%, respectively, after the addition of 0.5 wt% GO nanoparticles. Graphene is a nanomaterial with a high Young’s modulus of 1 TPa and intrinsic strength of 130 GPa [55]. GO, a strongly oxygenated and hydrophilic-layered nanomaterial derived from graphene, has a Young’s modulus than can range from 380 to 470 GPa as the coverage of oxygen groups changes [56]. Previous research has demonstrated that the optimal tensile properties of polyethylene materials increase with the content of GO, up to a maximum content of 0.5 wt%, after which the tensile strength decreases as more GO is added [57]. Similarly, in magnesium phosphate bone cement, the addition of 0.04% GO increased compressive strength by 8.2%, but the strength decreased as the GO concentration was increased further [15]. When 0.5% GO was added to a HA/gelatin scaffold, the compressive strength and flexural strength increased by 15% and 22%, but the compressive strength decreased with 1% GO [25]. The compression tests in this current study showed that adding GO nanoparticles significantly improved the mechanical strength of the material, increasing the compressive strength and Young’s modulus by 135% and 265%, respectively (Fig. 2). It is possible that the concentration of GO nanoparticles plays an important role in regulating the porosity of composite materials [42], suggesting that a certain amount of the nanoparticles might help to reduce the pore size and densify the microstructure, thus affecting the mechanical behavior. However, excessive concentrations might increase the viscosity and porosity of the composite and have a negative impact on the mechanical performance. A related study showed found that the particle size of the composite decreased as the GO concentration was increased [23].

Currently, part of biodegradable bone graft materials exhibit rapid degradation [58]. The degradation process of ceramics is generally considered to consist of buffer erosion and a hydrolysis reaction [59]. The higher the porosity, the larger the contact area between the samples and the buffer, leading to a hydrolysis reaction. As described above, the addition of GO nanoparticles may affect the porosity of the ceramic composite and decrease the contact area with the buffer. Additionally, the change in porosity could be attributed to the large surface area and the presence of oxygen functional groups in GO that enhance the binding with the materials, resulting in improved stability and a more regulated degradation rate. Furthermore, the layered structure of GO can create a physical barrier within the material, improving the enzyme resistance of the material to body fluids, thereby further slowing the degradation rate [28]. Previous studies have reported similar findings that adding 0.5–1.5% GO to agarose/chitosan scaffolds reduces the degradation rate by 10–12% [28]. The degradation rate decreased by 1.9% in polyhydroxybutyrate-chitosan scaffolds with 0.5% GO [16]. Gelatin-alginate composite scaffolds were reported to degrade slowly by about 30% when blended with 0.1–0.3 w/v GO, compared to the pure gelatin-alginate group [31]. Similar results have been reported with nano-HAp reinforced nanocomposite scaffolds [26, 32]. In this current study, the degradation rate decreased by 25.5% in comparison to the control group (Fig. 4). The oxygen-containing functional groups can form strong bonds with the composite material, which increases the physical strength and improves the adsorption of calcium and proteins [60]. These characteristics make GO an alternative additive to improve the performance of CS/β-TCP composites and extend their application in bone tissue engineering.

Several studies have demonstrated that GO improves the biocompatibility and increases the osteogenic differentiation ability of various materials such as polymers [24], chitosan [16, 28–30], and ceramics [17, 19, 32, 42]. Hakimi et al. [27] cultured MC3T3-E1 cells and evaluated the biocompatibility of GO-HAp nanocomposite scaffolds using an MTT assay, and reported lower cytotoxicity compared to HAp alone. Purohit et al. [31] found increased ALP activity and osteogenic protein expression in MG-63 osteosarcoma cell line when GO was added to gelatin-alginate scaffolds. Wang et al. [20] tested GO toxicity on human fibroblast cells and showed that concentrations below 2 wt% were non-toxic, while concentrations above 5 wt% affected cell attachment. The results of the in vitro tests in this current study showed similar trends, whereby the addition of 0.5% GO to the ceramic composites exhibited no cytotoxicity and improved cell compatibility and ALP activity (Fig. 3).

The critical-sized calvarial defect model is useful for evaluating the bone regeneration capability of biomedical materials. Previous studies utilizing the rat calvarial defect model have found that GO promotes the transformation of connective tissue into new bone and vascularization into chitosan-HAp [30], biphasic calcium phosphate (BCP) [19] and calcium phosphate (CaP) scaffolds [42]. Although the mechanism of GO as a biomaterial for bone repair is still unclear, Xue et al. found that GO regulates through the NF-κB pathway in RAW 264.7 macrophages [61]. This was found to promote the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and stimulate angiogenic differentiation in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). Considering these similar findings from literature and our previous research [22], it can be inferred that GO nanoparticle in CS/β-TCP composites promote vascularization and bone formation in the surrounding tissue. The micro-CT results in the current study demonstrated the presence of significant mineralized tissue in the bone defects of the putty/GO group after 8 weeks of implantation (Fig. 6). The presence of GO particles was observed in the histological sections at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery in the GO group, and no inflammatory responses were identified. Masson’s trichrome stain confirmed that the mineralized tissue observed on micro-CT represents newly formed bone tissue, with GO particles distributed around blood vessels within the connective tissue or new bone tissue (Fig. 7). The new bone areas in the defect for the control group and the putty/GO group were 33.25% and 46.26% at 4 weeks post-operative, while at 8 weeks post-surgery they were 32.19% and 62.36%, respectively.

Because graphene oxide cannot be absorbed by the body and is a potential toxin if used as a graft material, biosafety assessments should be carefully performed during the development phase [41, 62, 63]. Studies have shown that the toxicity of graphene and its derivatives depends on the size of the flakes [64]. Hoyle et al. indicated that GO particles of a specific size (lateral size: 30 nm to 3 μm; thickness:1–7 nm) can display anti-inflammatory properties when interacting with bone marrow-derived macrophages, leading to the activation of the transcription factor nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2), which inhibits the expression of cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6 [63]. This may explain why the GO in the current study showed good biocompatibility in the in vitro and in vivo studies.

Additionally, our previous study on a 3D-printed graphene-reinforced polycaprolactone scaffold showed no significant difference in cell viability in comparison to the pure scaffold group. However, early signs of aging such as hair graying and hair loss in the mice were found after implantation of the graphene-containing scaffold [41]. In the current study, the wounds healed well after surgery in each animal and no signs of inflammation were identified in the histological analysis. To confirm the biosafety of the graphene, we also performed a complete blood count test at 4 and 8 weeks post-surgery [see Additional file 1] and the results did not identify any abnormalities in blood cell counts compared to normal rats of the same strain and age [65, 66]. This indicates that local implantation of GO into the calvarial defect did not induce inflammation or allergic reactions in mice.

Furthermore, our previous study also found that graphene-derived polymer scaffolds induced specific electroencephalography (EEG) signals in a mouse calvarial defect model [41], possibly because the electrical conductivity of graphene affected the electrical activity in the brain. To investigate whether GO in putty influenced brain activity in this study, frequency bands of resting state EEG was recorded by applying electrodes to the scalp [See Additional file 2]. The results showed no significant differences in the relative power parameters compared to the control group, and also the values in both groups were similar to results for normal rats from literature [67].

There were some limitations to this study. First, only early osteogenic activity was analyzed using ALP activity. Future studies may investigate the presence of osteogenic markers such as Runx2, ALP, COL-1, and OCN in RNA and protein levels. Other methods such as alizarin red, PCR, and Western blotting, which are helpful to verify the material’s impact on osteogenic cells, were also not tested in this study. Second, the optimal concentration of GO was demonstrated from mechanical and in vitrotests. Therefore, only a single concentration of GO was tested for degradation and implantation. Future studies may consider a wider range of concentrations, and develop more comprehensive methods for investigating thein-vitro degradation rate and assess how changing the pore size of the microstructure affects the degradation rate in-vivo. Finally, the in-vivo study was conducted only up to 8 weeks. The long-term risks of GO in-vivo should be carefully considered before using for clinical applications. Except for the above limitations, this study demonstrated that the addition of graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles to ceramic composites can significantly alter the physical and mechanical characteristics of bone grafts.

Conclusions

This study investigated calcium sulfate/β-tricalcium phosphate/graphene oxide nanocomposite grafts for bone tissue engineering applications. The findings indicated that 0.5% concentration of GO nanoparticles in a ceramic composite can improve the compressive strength of the bone graft and regulate the degradation rate. The GO nanoparticles also improve cell compatibility and promotes osteogenic differentiation in in vitro cell cultures and have the potential to facilitate bone repair in vivo. The mixed ceramic composites presented in this study can be considered as a promising alternative for bone graft application.
