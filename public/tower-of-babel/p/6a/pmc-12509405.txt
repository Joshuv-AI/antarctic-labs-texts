Patterns of lipid profile and genetic variations in South Asians
Authors: Mahdi Rahmanian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AimsCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Dyslipidemia is a primary risk factor for CVDs. South Asians (SAs) are proven to be more at risk of dyslipidemia and the development of CVDs. In this review, we will provide an overview of dyslipidemia and its various types, followed by a closer examination of the pattern of dyslipidemia in SAs and their associated genetic variations.MethodsA PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science search using the relevant keywords was conducted without any time limitation. Afterwards, the articles were screened based on their title and abstract. The remaining articles were selected for full-text review.ResultsLDL levels have been found to be similar or lower in SAs than in white Europeans. However, it seems that compared with white Europeans, LDL leads to CVDs at lower serum levels in SAs. Denser and smaller LDL particles in SAs are more atherogenic. In addition, SAs have higher levels of lipoprotein (a) than white Europeans. Also, the rate of hypertriglyceridemia in SAs, affecting as high as 70% of their population, is significantly higher than that of white Europeans. A higher level of HDL is associated with a weaker protective effect in SAs compared with other ethnicities. SAs have variations in genes related to lipid metabolism, such as apoliprotein H, lipoprotein lipase, MBOAT7, SGPP1, SPTLC3, PCSK9, and CELSR2.ConclusionSAs are more prone to dyslipidemia, leading to a higher risk of CVDs compared with other ethnic groups.

Introduction

The term “South Asia” refers to the region in the Indian subcontinent and, as defined by the United Nations, includes India, Bangladesh, Bhutan, the Maldives, Nepal, Pakistan, and Srilanka. South Asia is the home of three out of the ten most populous countries and is the most populous region in the world, comprising approximately 25% of the world’s population [1, 2]. The population of immigrant South Asians (SAs) and their offspring is significantly growing in Europe and North America. It is estimated that SAs comprise as high as 10% of the population in the UK and more than 2.5 million in North America [3, 4].

Accounting for approximately one-third of all deaths, CVDs are the leading cause of death worldwide, which imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems [5]. It is revealed that the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and metabolic syndrome is higher among SAs [6]. Over the past few decades, the rate of CVDs has almost doubled in SAs living in Western countries and remained significantly higher compared with other ethnicities, especially White Europeans [7]. After adjustment for traditional risk factors, SA ethnicity is associated with an increased risk of CVDs and is considered an independent risk factor for CVDs [8].

Among the traditional risk factors for CVDs, including smoking, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, obesity, sedentary lifestyle, alcohol consumption, dyslipidemia, and psychosocial factors, dyslipidemia has been proven to be the most substantial risk factor for CVDs in SAs, and the prevalence of dyslipidemia can reach around 90% among SAs [9]. Even children with SA ancestry have higher levels of cholesterol than their European counterparts [10].

It is evident that the distinct lipid profile pattern of SAs is rooted in their specific genetic polymorphisms. However, few studies have discussed the genetic causal inferences of the lipid profile pattern in SAs. Hence, in this article, we review dyslipidemia and its subtypes. Then we investigate the pattern of lipid profile in SAs and try to shed more light on the genetic polymorphisms and mutations leading to the specific lipid profile pattern.

Search strategy

A PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science search using keywords “South Asian, South asian population, Asian, Lipids, Lipid profile, dyslipidemia, Cholesterol, Gene, and Genetics” was done without any time limitation. Afterwards, the articles were screened based on their title and abstract. The remaining articles were selected for full-text screening. In the end, the most relevant articles describing the pattern of at least one protein, lipoprotein, enzyme, receptor, or gene involved in the human lipid pathway in SAs were included in the manuscript. Additionally, in case of multiple articles with the same information, only the most recent one was included in the article. Both original and review articles were included in the study. However, it is noteworthy that only review articles that provided a novel insight into the subject were included in the study.

Dyslipidemia

Overview

Cholesterol and triglycerides are the two major forms of lipid circulating as lipoproteins in the human body. A Lipoprotein is composed of a central hydrophobic core of lipids surrounded by a hydrophilic membrane of phospholipids, free cholesterol, and apolipoproteins [11].

Based on their characteristics, including density, electrophoretic mobility, size, and content of lipids and protein, apolipoproteins fall into five major classes, including chylomicrons, very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL), intermediate-density lipoproteins (IDL), low-density lipoproteins (LDL), and high-density lipoproteins (HDL) [12]. A standard lipid profile, measured after 12-hour fasting on blood specimens obtained by venopuncture, includes total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, and triglycerides [13]. A global public health concern with the estimated prevalence of 20% to 80% based on various definitions, dyslipidemia refers to the abnormal metabolism of lipids and can be primary or secondary. Primary dyslipidemias include familial hypercholesterolemia, chylomicronemia, familial combined hyperlipidemia, and familial dysbetalipoproteinemia [14]. Primary dyslipidemia

Familial hypercholesterolemia is clinically presented by a high level of LDL cholesterol. There are three primary genetic mutations (autosomal dominant) in familial hypercholesterolemia, resulting in a defect in the LDL receptor (over 1600 gene mutations detected), a defect in apolipoprotein B, or a defect in proprotein convertase subtilisin-kexin type 9 (PCSK9) [18]. Unlike familial hypercholesterolemia, which is a common disorder that affects approximately one in 250 adults above 20, chylomicronemia is a rare form of severe hypertriglyceridemia caused by defects in the lipoprotein lipase (LPL) pathway. LPL pathway genes include LPL, lipase maturation factor 1 (LMF1), glycosylphosphatidylinositol anchored high density lipoprotein binding protein 1 (GPIHBP1), apolipoprotein C2, and apolipoprotein A5. The loss-of-function mutations in these genes, inherited as an autosomal recessive trait, lead to chylomicronemia [14, 19]. The familial combined hyperlipidemia is a common disorder with up to 4% prevalence. Familial combined hyperlipidemia is a complex disorder with a wide variety of clinical manifestations from an isolated increase in apolipoprotein B to hypercholesterolemia with or without hypertriglyceridemia. Although its genetic component is not fully understood, hepatic overproduction of lipoprotein particles containing apolipoprotein B-100 might cause Familial combined hyperlipidemia [20]. Familial dysbetalipoproteinemia is a genetic disorder with both homozygous (90% of cases) and heterozygous (10% of cases) inheritance patterns. It is caused by a mutation in the apolipoprotein E gene, leading to the formation of apolipoprotein E2, a defective isoform of apolipoprotein E3. The formation of apolipoprotein E2 is associated with poor adherence of apolipoprotein E to the LDL receptor, leading to the accumulation of lipoproteins due to impaired remnant clearance [14, 21].

Secondary dyslipidemia

Secondary dyslipidemia is caused by poor lifestyle or medical conditions affecting the level of lipids in patients’ blood. These conditions include alcohol consumption, smoking, obesity, diabetes mellitus, hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and impaired liver function. Also, some medications, including corticosteroids, beta blockers, oral contraceptives, and antiretroviral agents, can lead to dyslipidemia [22]. Alcohol abuse decreases the oxidation of fatty acids by the liver and is associated with an increase in VLDL and triglyceride levels [23]. The chronic oxidative stress caused by smoking impairs the synthesis of HDL cholesterol and increases the level of LDL and triglycerides [24]. Obesity is associated with the rise of VLDL and decreased liver clearance of chylomicrons, leading to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, high LDL, and low HDL cholesterol [25]. The insulin resistance and hyperglycemia associated with diabetes mellitus impair the lipolysis of triglycerides and LDL uptake [26]. The low levothyroxine hormone level results in decreased expression of LDL receptors and LPL, leading to high LDL cholesterol and triglyceride levels [27]. Chronic kidney disease is associated with defective catabolism of lipoproteins containing apolipoprotein B and reduced activity of LPL and hepatic lipase, leading to high LDL cholesterol, high triglyceride levels, and low HDL cholesterol levels [28]. Impaired liver function leads to defective synthesis and secretion of lipoproteins and bile acids, resulting in impaired transport and excretion of cholesterol and triglycerides from the liver [29].

Pattern of lipid profile in South Asians

LDL cholesterol

LDL level, a known traditional risk factor for CVDs, has been found to be similar or lower in SAs than in white Europeans. However, it seems that compared with white Europeans, LDL leads to CVDs at lower serum levels in SAs [30, 31]. Denser and smaller LDL particles in SAs can be more atherogenic, leading to a higher prevalence of CVDs in SAs compared to white Europeans. Also, higher levels of apolipoprotein B for any LDL level in SAs, as a result of increased particle burden, may be the reason for a higher chance of atherogenicity compared with other ethnic groups [32, 33]. Also, it has been revealed that LDL is more susceptible to aggregation in SAs, which can lead to increased LDL accumulation in the arterial wall and, consequently, a higher risk of CVDs. This aggregation has been attributed to high levels of sphingomyelin in the structure of LDL in SAs [34].

Lipoprotein (a) is a variant of LDL and is associated with an increased risk of CVDs. SAs have higher levels of lipoprotein (a) than white Europeans [35].

HDL cholesterol

HDL, a mediator of reverse cholesterol efflux function that leads to regression of atherosclerotic plaques and reduction of their stability, has cardiovascular protective effects [36]. Moreover, HDL stimulates the production of nitric oxide by stimulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase, resulting in vasodilatation and reduced risk of CVDs [37]. Although a higher level of HDL is associated with a lower risk of CVD development, it is associated with a weaker protective effect in SAs compared with other ethnicities [38, 39]. It is known that the function of HDL is of much greater importance than its serum level in CVD risk reduction. Dysfunctional HDL is not only ineffective in reducing CVD risk but also acts as a pro-inflammatory agent, leading to an increased risk of CVDs [40, 41]. Another factor that plays a key role in the function of HDL is its particle size. HDL is composed of heterogeneous particles. Bigger HDL particle size, especially the HDL2b subfraction, is associated with greater cardiovascular protective effects [42, 43]. A study comparing the functionality of HDL in SAs and white Europeans revealed that SAs had impaired anti-oxidant and anti-inflammatory HDL function [44].

Additionally, the serum level of HDL is lower in SAs compared with other ethnic groups [45]. Also, several studies have reported that the mean level of HDL is significantly lower in SA men and women compared with white Europeans [7]. Moreover, there seems to be an association between low HDL levels and deteriorating beta-cell function in the pancreas and insulin resistance in SAs [46].

Triglycerides

High level of triglycerides is commonly seen among SAs and are one of the main risk factors for a higher rate of CVDs among this ethnic group. The rate of hypertriglyceridemia in SAs, affecting as high as 70% of their population, is significantly higher than that of white Europeans, with a prevalence of approximately one-third [7].

Genetics

Whole genome sequencing in SA participants revealed variations in genes related to lipid metabolism, insulin signaling, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [47]. Apolipoprotein H, also known as β2-glycoprotein I, is a component of several classes of lipoproteins and is involved in the activation of LPL. It is evident that apolipoprotein H downregulation can affect cholesterol-related metabolic pathways and upregulation of de novo lipogenesis genes. Apolipoprotein H downregulation also disturbs the metabolism of lipid and lipid-like molecules, including fatty acids, glycerolipids, glycerophospholipids, and sterol lipids. In SAs, variations in the apolipoprotein H gene can cause elevated LDL and triglyceride levels [48].

Another genome-wide analysis of plasma lipid metabolites in SAs revealed variations at the membrane-bound acylglycerophosphatidylinositol O-acyltransferase (MBOAT7), lipase C, apolipoprotein E, apolipoprotein C1, apolipoprotein C2, apolipoprotein C4, sphingosine-1-phosphate phosphatase 1 (SGPP1), and serine palmitoyltransferase long chain base subunit 3 (SPTLC3) [49]. MBOAT7 encodes an integral membrane protein with acyltransferase activity. The downregulation of MBOAT7 is associated with metabolic-associated fatty liver disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, CVDs, and dyslipidemia [50, 51]. The downregulation of Lipase C is characterized by elevated total cholesterol, LDL, VLDL, and triglycerides, leading to premature atherosclerosis and CVDs [52, 53]. Polymorphisms in apolipoprotein E, apolipoprotein C1, apolipoprotein C2, and apolipoprotein C4 genes influence plasma lipid levels, including triglycerides, LDL, VLDL, and HDL, and are associated with premature CVDs [54]. SGPP1 catalyses the degradation of Sphingosine-1-phosphate, a bioactive lipid mediator involved in CVDs, obesity, and type 2 diabetes mellitus [55, 56]. SPTLC3 encodes a subunit of the serine palmitoyltransferase complex, which is involved in the biosynthesis of sphingolipids. Polymorphisms in SPTLC3 are associated with dyslipidemia and ischemic cardiomyopathy [57, 58].

PCSK9 plays a substantial role in plasma cholesterol metabolism. Its polymorphism leads to familial hypercholesterolemia and higher levels of plasma LDL, and subsequently, premature atherosclerosis and CVDs. It is found that PCSK9 has a significantly weaker effect on LDL in SAs than Europeans [59, 60].

The cadherin EGF LAG seven-pass G-type receptor 2 (CELSR2) is a subgroup of adhesion G protein-coupled receptors that regulate many human biological processes, such as planar cell polarity, nervous system development, and cardiovascular function. Polymorphisms in the CELSR2 gene have been linked with coronary artery disease in SAs [60].

Conclusion

In conclusion, SAs have a genetic susceptibility for dyslipidemia that can lead to a higher risk of CVDs compared with other ethnic groups, especially white Europeans. Overall, SAs have a higher LDL particle burden with smaller and denser particles that have a greater tendency for aggregation, lower levels of HDL that can be dysfunctional, and higher levels of triglycerides. It is recommended that SA ethnicity be considered as a separate risk factor for CVDs and metabolic syndrome. Also, it seems necessary that treatment approaches are adjusted for SAs based on lower serum lipid cut-off points that are tailored for this ethnic group. Moreover, it is recommended that SAs with dyslipidemia undergo genetic evaluation for better targeted treatment. Additionally, due to limitations of current knowledge about the genetic variations leading to dyslipidemia in SAs, more genomic research should be conducted in the future.
