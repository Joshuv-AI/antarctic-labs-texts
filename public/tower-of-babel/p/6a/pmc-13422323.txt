Structural Evolution of Hydrated Alkaline-Earth Metal Ion Clusters via Molecular Face Theory with Kohn–Sham One-Electron Potential
Authors: Runqiang Yu, Mingyang Sun, Jing Zhang, Xinmeng Liu, Yuanfeng Zhu, Jian Zhao, Dongxia Zhao, Lidong Gong, Zhongzhi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alkaline-earth metal ions exhibit distinct hydration behaviors that evolve with coordination number and govern their chemical reactivity, transport properties, and biological functions, yet a unified description linking hydration structure, electronic effects, and steric hindrance remains incomplete. In this work, the Molecular Face Theory (MFT) based on the Kohn–Sham one-electron potential (KSpot) was applied to systematically investigate the structural evolution of gas-phase hydrated clusters M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, and Ba, n = 1–15). In addition, KSpot was employed to quantify electron density distribution, atomic partitioning, charge transfer, and steric effects during hydration. Across the series, the total water binding energy decreases monotonically with increasing ionic radius (Be2+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+ > Sr2+ > Ba2+), reflecting the dominant role of ionic charge density. The depth of the KSpot at the saddle point along a chemical bond (D pb ) for the metal–oxygen coordination bond exhibits a strong negative correlation with bond length (R = 0.98) and a positive correlation with binding energy (R = 0.99), establishing it as a quantitative descriptor of coordination stability. With the increasing hydration number, the first ionization potential decreases continuously, while the molecular face surface areas and volumes display clear inflection points at n = 6–8, elucidating saturation of the first hydration shell. Charge distribution analysis reveals efficient electrostatic screening by the first-shell water molecules, followed by a pronounced saturation behavior upon shell completion. Stereoselective analysis of calcium ion clusters indicates that seven to eight water molecules mark the structural transition from a monolayer to bilayer hydration, where steric hindrance and electrostatic interactions jointly determine conformational stability and promote second-shell formation. These results provide a unified electronic and stereoselective perspective on alkaline-earth metal ion hydration and its shell-by-shell structural evolution.

Introduction

Alkaline-earth metal cations are essential in chemical and biological systems, serving both as counterions to maintain ion concentration gradients and as fundamental elements in a range of enzymatic processes. − In aqueous environments, the behavior of these ions is dominated by not only their intrinsic electronic properties but also interactions with the surrounding solvent. Water, through its extended hydrogen-bonding network, provides a highly structured yet adaptable medium; the introduction of metal cations locally reorganizes this network by strong electrostatic interactions between the positively charged ion and the oxygen atoms of neighboring water molecules, giving rise to well-defined hydration structures. , Frank et al. noted that, depending on the metal ionic charge, ionic radius, and valence, hydration structures exhibit a pronounced shell-like organization, with highly charged ions capable of sustaining up to three distinct hydration shells. Specifically, the ionic radius directly determines the number of water molecules accommodated in the first coordination shell and their binding strength. Once this inner shell is saturated, additional water molecules are incorporated predominantly through hydrogen-bonding interactions with the inner-shell waters. By eliminating the complexities of solid or bulk environments yet retaining key details of ion–water and water–water interactions, the gas-phase metal ion–water cluster model has become a fundamental and widely adopted model in both experimental and theoretical studies. −

Experimentally, a variety of techniques have been employed, including X-ray diffraction, − Extended X-ray Absorption Fine Structure (EXAFS), , and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry etc. Ončák et al. systematically investigated the photochemical behavior of gas-phase Mg­(H2O) n clusters (n = 1–5) at 130 K using Fourier-transform ion cyclotron resonance mass spectrometry (FT-ICR-MS) coupled with tunable laser photodissociation, in which the hydrogen atom elimination leading to MgOH+[(H2O) n−1], constitutes the dominant photodissociation pathway across all cluster sizes, while simple water evaporation plays only a minor role. For n = 1–3, H atom loss was shown to require multiphoton excitation, reflecting the intrinsic stability of the Mg–water coordination in the ground state. High-level excited-state calculations reproduced the experimentally observed red-shifted spectral features and provided a consistent interpretation of the multiphoton dissociation mechanism. Bush et al. characterized gas-phase Ca­[(H2O) n ]2+ clusters (n = 11–69) using infrared multiple photon dissociation (IRMPD) spectroscopy and observed that the coordination number in the first hydration shell of Ca2+ increases from 6 to 8 around n ≈ 12. With increasing cluster size, larger aggregates progressively display bulk-like water features, including a more ordered surface and an internal hydrogen-bonding network that approaches that of liquid water. Peschke et al. measured stepwise hydration reactions of Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+ over the temperature range of 130–420 K using electrospray ionization combined with gas-phase hydration equilibrium methods, together with high-level theoretical calculations. Their results determined the structures and thermodynamic parameters of the first hydration shell at a coordination number of 6 for Mg2+, Ca2+, and Sr2+, whereas Ba2+ can accommodate up to 8–9 water molecules. Nevertheless, precisely resolving the detailed interactions between Ca2+ and water molecules remains experimentally challenging, underscoring the essential role of theoretical approaches in achieving a molecular-level understanding.

As for theoretical research, hydration structures and dynamical properties of alkaline-earth metal ions in water have been studied using a range of electronic structure and multiscale approaches, including the quantum mechanics (QM), , molecular mechanics (MM), − and QM/MM, etc. These studies have highlighted the sensitivity of ion hydration to both coordination environment and solvent representation. Delgado et al. performed a systematic analysis of Mg2+ hydration in Mg­[(H2O) n ]2+ clusters (n = 1–6) and in large aggregates incorporating a second hydration shell (with up to 10 or 12 water molecules total) using QM calculations combined with local vibrational mode analysis. Their results showed that the Mg2+–O bond strength weakens as the coordination number increases within the first hydration shell of Mg2+, whereas inclusion of a second shell slightly enhanced the Mg2+–O interaction through increased charge transfer from oxygen lone pairs into vacant Mg2+ orbitals. Notably, the preferred coordination number was found to depend strongly on how the solvent environment is modeled. Five-coordinate structures can be favored in explicit solvent, while six-coordinate ones become dominant when a complete second shell (12 water molecules) is present or when implicit solvation is employed. Extending such theoretical analyses to heavier alkaline-earth ions, Shi et al. performed a global exploration of the potential energy surfaces of Ca­[(H2O) n ]2+ clusters (n = 10–18). The coordination number of the first hydration shell increases from 6 to 7–8 around n ≈ 12 and fluctuates with cluster size, indicating strong competition between the first and second hydration shells. Furthermore, the interaction between Ca2+ and water molecules is primarily mediated by charge transfer from the oxygen lone-pair orbitals in the first shell into the vacant Ca2+ orbitals. Moreover, Velásquez et al. employed QM and the stochastic search algorithm ASCEC to comprehensively explore the potential energy surfaces of Sr­[(H2O) n ]2+ and Ba­[(H2O) n ]2+ (n = 1–6) clusters. The results revealed that the interactions between Sr2+/Ba2+ and water molecules cannot be adequately described by a simple electrostatic model, but instead display a mixed ionic–covalent character. At larger cluster sizes, Zhu et al. systematically examined the Sr­[(H2O)1–25]2+ clusters using QM calculations and found that the first hydration shell of Sr2+ saturates at a coordination number of 8, while the second shell can accommodate up 9 additional water molecules. When the cluster size exceeds the capacity of the second shell (n ≥ 18), the incremental binding energy of added water molecules approaches that of the water–water hydrogen bond, indicating that the direct interaction between Sr2+ and outer-shell water molecules become negligibly weak. Schwenk et al. investigated the hydration structure of Ca2+ in aqueous solution using classical MD with three-body corrections and QM/MM molecular dynamics simulations based on Hartree–Fock (HF) and B3LYP functional. The average coordination numbers in the first hydration shell of 7.1 (classical), 7.6 (HF), and 8.1 (B3LYP), respectively. Using a polarizable force-field approach, Piquemal et al. employed the AMOEBA model to study the precise solvation dynamics of calcium and magnesium ions in water, which achieves excellent agreement with both experimental observations and Car–Parrinello MD simulations. Berglez et al. combined experimental measurements with molecular dynamics simulations to examine the competitive adsorption of alkaline-earth metal ions (Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, Ba2+) on TiO2 nanoparticles, revealing that adsorption affinity increases with ionic radius (Mg2+ < Ca2+ ≈ Sr2+ < Ba2+) and is strongly modulated by hydration structure and the concentration of background electrolyte.

Despite these advances, a complete understanding of the structural stability and interaction mechanisms of alkaline-earth metal ion–water clusters require more than atomic positions and coordination numbers alone. A quantitative description that explicitly captures the overall molecular geometry and electronic boundary of the hydrated system remains essential. In molecular science, shape, size, and boundaries are fundamental descriptors. Different conformations lead to distinct molecular shapes, sizes, and boundaries, which in turn govern molecular recognition and interaction. Molecular shape reflects not only the external contour of a system but also the collective manifestation of internal electronic and nuclear interactions, making it a core concept in chemical structure theory. − To address these aspects, Yang et al. − proposed the concept of Molecular Face Theory (MFT), which provides a quantitative description of molecular shape using Molecular Intrinsic Characteristic Contour (MICC). − Within the framework, electron density is further employed to characterize molecular polarization, reactivity, and related properties. − To date, MFT has been successfully applied to a broad range of chemical problems, including the analysis of dynamic changes in molecular contours, − the determination of molecular face volumes (MFV) and surface areas (MFSA), and the prediction of reaction orientation and strength in noncovalent interactions. − In its original formulation, MFT is built upon the Potential Acting on one Electron in a Molecule (PAEM) developed by Yang et al., from which the Kohn–Sham (KS) equation , can be derived to gain the KS one electron potential (KSpot) and corresponding molecular face. , However, the PAEM-based MFT involves numerous and two-electron integral terms, leading to high computational cost and steep scaling with system size, which limits its applicability to large molecular systems. To overcome this limitation, the PAEM is replaced by the KSpot, substantially reducing the computational cost and enabling efficient generation of KSpot even for large molecules.

In this work, MFT is applied to investigate the structural evolution of hydrated alkaline-earth metal ion clusters M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba; n = 1–15), constructed by sequentially adding water molecules around each alkaline-earth metal ion. Using KSpot molecular face, the evolution of molecular geometry and electronic boundaries upon hydration is studied to examine how these features correlate with key energetic and electronic descriptors, including first ionization energy, MFSA and MFV, metal-centered regional volume (MFVm), charge distribution, and charge transfer. To quantify coordination stability, the relationship between the depth of the KSpot at the saddle point along a chemical bond (D pb ) and both binding energy and bond length is analyzed. Furthermore, calcium ion hydrates are examined as a representative case to elucidate regioselective and stereoselective hydration behavior, where steric force analysis is integrated with KSpot molecular face descriptors to resolve the interplay between electronic stabilization and spatial constraints.

Methodologies

Kohn–Sham One-Electron Potential

Kohn and Sham addressed the problem of determining the external potential E 0 of an interacting many-electron system as a functional of the electron density ρ by introducing an auxiliary system of noninteracting electrons that reproduces the same density. Within this framework, the total energy functional is decomposed into the kinetic energy of the noninteracting reference system, the electron–nucleus attraction energy, the classical electron–electron Coulomb repulsion energy, and the exchange–correlation energy, which accounts for all remaining many-body effects. QM Calculations

The alkaline-earth metal ion–water clusters studied in this work were constructed sequentially, starting from the M­[(H2O)]2+ complex (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba). Each optimized structure served as the starting geometry for the addition of one water molecule to generate the next cluster in the series. All geometries were fully optimized using the Gaussian 09 program package at the PBE0-D3­(BJ)/def2-SVP − level of theory. Harmonic vibrational frequency calculations were subsequently performed at the same level to confirm that the optimized structures correspond to true minima on the potential energy surface, as indicated by the absence of imaginary frequencies. Subsequently, single-point energy calculations were carried out at the PBE0-D3­(BJ)/def2-TZVP level of theory to obtain the first vertical ionization energies and binding energies of the most stable conformers. The KSpot was calculated by Multiwfn 3.8 (dev), , whose input files were extracted from Gaussian formatting checkpoint files, and plotted by VESTA 3.5.8.

Binding Energies

In this work, the total water binding energy (E BE) and successive binding energy (ΔE) are defined as 10 EBE=E(M2+)+nE(H2O)−E[M(H2O)n2+] 11 ΔE=E[M(H2O)n−12+]+E(H2O)−E[M(H2O)n2+] where E(M2+), E(H2O), and E[M(H2O)n2+] denote the electronic energies of the isolated metal ion, isolated water molecule, and the hydrated cluster, respectively. Positive values indicate exothermic binding, and larger values correspond to stronger metal–water interactions. This sign convention follows that used by ref .

Prior to analyzing the structural evolution of M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba; n = 1–15) clusters, we validated our computational protocol through systematic benchmark calculations (details in Table S1). These tests confirm that the chosen PBE0-D3­(BJ)/def2-TZVP protocol provides reliable binding energies and that our qualitative conclusions are robust to reasonable variations in functional and basis set choices.

Results and Discussion

Geometries

Figures and S1, together with Table S2, summarize the optimized geometries and E BE of M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba; n = 1–15), while the successive binding energy is tabulated in Table S3. Overall, the results clearly elucidate that ionic charge density is the dominant factor governing both the magnitude of the binding energy and the evolution of hydration structures. Across the alkaline-earth series, the total water binding energy decreases systematically in the order Be2+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+ > Sr2+ > Ba2+. This trend directly reflects the increasing ionic radius, which leads to a progressive reduction in the local electric field strength, consequently, weaker electrostatic interactions with surrounding water dipoles. Owing to its exceptionally small radius and corresponding high charge density, Be2+ exhibits the strongest binding energy to water, whereas Ba2+ shows the weakest interaction. For all metal ions, the binding energy increases monotonically with the number of coordinated water molecules. However, the incremental gain in binding energy diminishes markedly as the first hydration shell approaches saturation, indicating a well-defined capacity limit for the first hydration shell. Upon the addition of second-shell water molecules (e.g., in the 10 (6,4) configuration), the E BE increases further. This behavior highlights that, although outer-shell water molecules do not interact directly with the metal ion, they significantly enhance the overall stability of the cluster through the formation of an extended hydrogen-bonding network. Such cooperative hydrogen-bonding effects therefore make a non-negligible contribution to the energies of hydrated alkaline-earth.

Schematic illustrations of the stable hydrated structures for alkaline-earth metal ion within their first hydration shell.

Analysis of the structural parameters provide further insight into the fine-tuning mechanism of hydration shells. The average metal–oxygen bond length (R M···O) systematically increases with the ion radius of the central metal, ranging from 1.539 Å for Be2+ to nearly 2.8 Å for Ba2+, consistent with Coulombic expectations. For a given metal ion, R M···O slightly elongates as the number of first-shell water molecules increases, reflecting growing ligand–ligand steric repulsion and partial electrostatic screening by the coordinated water molecules. Interestingly, in clusters containing a second hydration shell, R M···O is often slightly shorter than in pure first-shell clusters. The effect is attributed to polarization of the first-shell water molecules by second-shell waters via hydrogen bonding, which concentrates negative charge on the oxygen atoms and thereby enhancing their interaction with the metal ion.

Hydrogen-bonding descriptors, including the average O···H distance (R O···H) and the average ∠O–H···O) angle, further illustrate the structural order of the second hydration shell. The average hydrogen bond length is shorter than that in the gas-phase water dimer, and the angle is close to linear, indicative of strengthened intermolecular interactions. These features demonstrate that the strong polarization of the metal ion not only stabilizes the arrangement of first-shell water molecules but also reinforces intermolecular hydrogen bonds, thereby promoting the formation of a well-organized secondary solvation structure. Collectively, these observations support a hierarchical description of alkaline-earth metal ion hydration: the inner shell is dominated by strong electrostatic coordination, while the outer shell is stabilized by an enhanced hydrogen-bonding network.

A uniform color scale is employed, in which blue represents regions of high electron density and red corresponds to low electron density, with intermediate colors representing a continuous decrease in density. Across all clusters, the regions around the oxygen atoms of water molecules are distinctly blue, whereas the hydrogen atoms and metal-ion sites are predominantly yellow-green to orange-red. This distribution demonstrates that the electron density around oxygen atoms is significantly higher than that in the vicinity of the metal ions. This behavior originates from the much higher electronegativity of oxygen compared to the alkaline-earth metal ions, which endows oxygen with a stronger ability to attract electrons. Consequently, a polarization effect arises in the interaction between the metal ion and water molecules, leading to a shift of electron density from the metal ion toward the oxygen atoms of the water ligands. Notably, the high electron density in the blue regions does not stem from the metal ions themselves but results from the polarization of the lone-pair electrons on the oxygen atoms oriented toward the metal center. This feature unambiguously identifies the interaction between metal ion and water molecules as fundamentally an ion–dipole coordination bond, rather than a covalent bond.

Maps of KSpot molecular face for Ca­[(H2O) n ]2+ (a) and Mg­[(H2O) n ]2+ (b), where n is an integer from 1 to 15 and MFED represents frontier electron density. (Note that n = 7­(6,1) represents 7 water molecules in total, of which 6 are in the inner shell and 1 is in the outer shell.).

As the coordination number increases, the color of the metal ion region gradually shifts from red to orange and then to yellow, while the water molecule regions transition from deep blue toward green. This trend reflects the progressive polarization of the oxygen lone pairs toward the metal ion as the first hydration shell develops, which increases the effective electron density around the metal center while slightly diminishing the local electron density on the oxygen atoms. In clusters containing two hydration shells, the inner M2+–O coordination region consistently appears deep blue to cyan, exhibiting significantly higher electron density than other regions, and further confirming the dominant role of ion–dipole interactions in the inner-shell coordination. In contrast, the O–H···O hydrogen-bonding regions between outer-shell water molecules are predominantly green, yellow, or even orange, indicating markedly lower electron density and much weaker interaction strength compared to the inner-shell ion–dipole coordination. This hierarchical distribution of electron density clearly distinguishes the strong-interaction core from the weak-interaction periphery, providing an intuitive visualization of the relative strengths of different types of intermolecular interactions.

Figure a–e shows the variation of the KSpot along the M2+–O bond direction for the M­[(H2O)]2+ clusters, plotted as a function of electron coordinate. Between adjacent atomic nuclei corresponding to neighboring potential wells, a saddle-shaped surface is formed. Along a given bond direction, the highest point on this surface defines the intrabond saddle point, which represents a maximum along the bond axis and a minimum in the direction perpendicular to the bond. The energy difference between the KSpot value at the intrabond saddle point and the zero-energy reference level represents the barrier that an electron must overcome to move from the region of one nucleus, pass through the bonding region, and enter the basin of the other nucleus. This energy barrier, denoted as D pb , serves as a measure of chemical bond stability. A larger D pb corresponds to a stronger and more stable chemical bond, whereas a smaller value indicates weaker bonding. ,, Table tabulates the D pb values for the clusters alongside the corresponding M2+–O bond lengths and E BE. Figure f presents the associated linear regression plots. The results reveal that D pb is negatively correlated with the M2+–O and length (R = 0.98), indicating that a larger D pb corresponds to a shorter and stronger bond. Simultaneously, D pb exhibits a strong positive linear correlation with the binding energy (R = 0.99). Moreover, across the isoelectronic series from Be2+ to Ba2+, all physical quantities display a clear monotonic trend. As the ionic radius increases, D pb decreases, bond lengths increase, and E BE diminishes. This behavior fundamentally stems from the decreasing ionic charge density. Be2+, with the smallest ionic radius, possesses the highest charge density, and forms short and strong coordination bonds. In contrast, Ba2+ has a large radius and diffuse charge distribution, resulting in weak electrostatic attraction to water molecules, longer bonds, and significantly lower E BE. To comprehensively validate the physical significance of D pb as a descriptor of metal water coordination strength, we systematically examined its correlations with three independent theoretical descriptors across the M­[(H2O)]2+ clusters. As illustrated in Figure S5 and Table S4, D pb exhibits an excellent linear correlation with the electron density at the B from QTAIM analysis (R = 0.98), confirming that larger D pb values correspond to greater electron density accumulation at the metal oxygen bonding region and thus stronger coordination interactions. Simultaneously, D pb shows a strong negative correlation with the metal oxygen bond length (R M···O) (R = 0.98), further validating its reliability as a geometric indicator of bond strength across the entire alkaline earth series. To probe the orbital interaction component, Natural Bond Orbital (NBO) analysis reveals a positive correlation between D pb and the quantity of charge transfer from water molecules to alkaline earth metal ions (R = 0.96), demonstrating that D pb implicitly captures the covalent character of ion–water coordination. This multifaceted nature indicates the potential of D pb in characterizing ion–water coordination, as it complements conventional descriptors by providing a more holistic perspective on the interactions between metal ions and water molecules. The current validation of D pb against E BE, QTAIM, and NBO analysis is presented for the monohydrated complexes [M­(H2O)]2+ as a proof-of-concept benchmark. In future, we will consider applying it to larger clusters to validate the methodological rationality in such systems.

The First Ionization Energy, Molecular Face Surface Areas, and Volumes

Understanding the interaction mechanism between metal ions and their surrounding water molecules is essential for elucidating their physicochemical behavior. Through a systematic analysis of hydrated clusters of five representative alkaline-earth metal ions over a range of water coordination numbers (n = 1–15), we examined how hydration modulates the electronic structure and geometric configuration of the metal ions from four key perspectives, including the first ionization energy (IE), molecular face surface area (MFSA), total volume (MFV), and metal-centered regional volume (MFVm). The corresponding data are presented in Figure and Table S5. Data points marked with an asterisk in Figure denote clusters that comprise second-shell water molecules. This analysis further uncovers the intrinsic rules governing the structural evolution of alkaline-earth metal hydration shells.

Variation of the first ionization energies (a), molecular face surface areas (b), molecular face volumes of the M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (c), together with molecular face volumes of M2+ ions in the M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (d). Here M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, and Ba, with n = 1–15.

Figure a shows that, for all metal ions considered, the IE decreases monotonically as the number of water molecules n increases. For Be2+, the IE decreases from 1.0697 au at n = 1 to 0.5899 au at n = 12. For Ba2+, it decreases from 0.8441 au to 0.5658 au over the same range. This trend arises from the effective screening of the metal ion positive charge by surrounding water molecules. The oxygen atoms of water donate lone pairs to the metal ion via coordination bonds, which increases the local electron density around the ion and stabilizes its valence electrons. At a fixed n, the IE decreases with increasing atomic number, following the order Be2+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+ > Sr2+ > Ba2+. This trend reflects the increase in ionic radius and the corresponding decrease in charge density. Smaller ions generate stronger electric fields, leading to more compact hydration shells and stronger binding of valence electrons, which results in higher ionization energies. More detailed structural annotations reveal fine features in the evolution of the IE. For Ca2+ and Sr2+, when n = 6 corresponding to a saturated first hydration shell, the rate of decrease in IE becomes to slow down. Upon further addition of second-shell water molecules, the IE continues to decline but with a reduced slope, indicating that the first hydration shell is largely complete. Subsequent water molecules no longer significantly perturb the inner electronic environment, leading to a diminished rate of reduction in IE.

Figures b and c displays the variation of the MFSA and MFV of the hydrated clusters as a function of the number of water molecules n. Both quantities increase monotonically with n, although the growth is clearly nonlinear. Distinct inflection points appear at n = 6–8 for the five alkaline-earth metal ions, signaling the saturation of the first hydration shell. For Ca2+, the MFSA increases from 134.155 au2 at n = 1 to 1023.097 au2 at n = 15, while the MFV grows from 73.233 au3 to 836.930 au3 over the same range. A similar trend is observed for Mg2+, for which MFSA rises from 110.763 au2 to 990.567 au2 and MFV from 85.727 au3 to 809.274 au3 as n increases from 1 to 15. Notably, at any fixed n, both MFSA and MFV follow a strict ordering of Ba2+ > Sr2+ > Ca2+ > Mg2+ > Be2+, which directly reflects the systematic increase in ionic radius. For instance, at n = 5, the MFV for Be2+, Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+ are 245.605, 266.492, 294.512, 316.977, and 346.377 au3, respectively. These results confirm that ionic radius is the dominant factor governing the overall size of the cluster. Moreover, structural annotations allow the respective contributions of the first and second hydration shells to MFSA and MFV to be quantified. For Ca2+, the MFV increases from 339.399 au3 at n = 6 to 393.412 au3 at n = 7 in the (6,1) configuration, corresponding to an incremental increase of only 54.013 au3. This increment is significantly smaller than the average volume increase observed from n = 1 to n = 6. This demonstrates that second-shell water molecules reside on the periphery and contribute less to the overall cluster size, consistent with their role in forming an outer solvation layer rather than directly coordinating the metal ion.

Figure d presents the variation of the metal-centered regional MFVm, which reflects the spatial extent occupied by the metal ion itself and its innermost, strongly bound hydration shell. For Be2+, Mg2+, and Ca2+, MFVm remains essentially constant, or even slightly decreases, once the first hydration shell is saturated. For instance, the MFVm of Be2+ stabilizes at approximately 15.157 au3 beyond n = 6, while that of for Mg2+ stabilizes around 47.175 au3 after n = 6. This behavior indicates that, after completion of the first hydration shell, the inner coordination environment of the metal ion becomes structurally rigid. Additional water molecules primarily populate the outer solvation layers without significantly altering the volume of the inner core.

The inflection points observed at n = 6–8 in Figure b and c serve as quantitative geometric indicators for first-shell completion. For Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+, the first inflection point appears at n = 6, while saturation occurs earlier at n = 4 for the smaller Be2+. This size-dependent coordination number reflects the ionic radius and the spatial requirements for optimal ion–dipole interactions. Beyond these critical n values, additional water molecules primarily occupy the second hydration shell, increasing the overall cluster size while exerting a reduced influence on the inner electronic structure. This behavior is supported by the decreased slope in IE (Figure a) and the plateau in MFVm (Figure d). The consistency of these features across multiple descriptors highlights the discontinuous and hierarchical nature of ion hydration. This hierarchical transition is fundamentally governed by a competition between long-range electrostatic ion–dipole attraction and short-range steric repulsion. In the early stage (n ≤ 6), the strong electric field of the bare metal ion dominates, resulting in rapid charge transfer and the formation of a compact first hydration shell. As the n approaches 6–8, ligand–ligand crowding increases sharply, as evidenced by the inflection in molecular face surface area and volume, as well as the slowdown in IE reduction. Beyond this threshold, the system relieves steric strain by forming a second hydration shell through hydrogen bonding, rather than further direct coordination to the metal center. To quantitatively capture this mechanistic crossover, the saturation of the first hydration shell is operationally defined as the hydration number n at which (1) MFSA and MFV exhibit a distinct inflection in their growth trend, (2) MFVm stabilizes within ±2%.

In summary, these results not only quantify the impact of hydration on the electronic structure and geometric configuration of alkaline-earth metal ions, but also reveal the hierarchical nature of hydration-shell evolution. Saturation of the first hydration shell represents a critical structural transition, shifting from formation of a stable inner coordination core to expansion of the outer solvation layers. This insight provides a microscopic foundation for understanding ion hydration in solution and has important implications for modeling ion transport and selectivity in biological ion channels.

Charge Distributions and Charge Transfer

Figure and Table S6 present the charge distribution on the alkaline-earth metal ions and the corresponding charge transfer in clusters with varying numbers of water molecules. As shown by the left Y-axis in each subplot, the net positive charge on the metal ion consistently decreases as the n increases. This trend directly reflects the strong polarizing effect of water as a highly polar solvent. The lone pairs on the oxygen atoms form coordination bonds with the metal ion, leading to a redistribution of electron density toward the metal center. Although no real electron transfer occurs in a strict quantum-mechanical sense, this redistribution manifests as an effective charge transfer arising from polarization of the electron cloud. For example, at n = 1, Be2+ retains a charge of approximately 1.46|e|, which decreases to about 0.71|e| at n = 6. Under the same conditions, Ba2+ shows a much smaller reduction with charge decreasing from 1.81|e| to 1.69|e|, indicating significantly weaker charge screening compared to Be2+. This difference stems from their distinct ionic radii and charge densities. Be2+ exerts the strongest polarization on surrounding water molecules with the smallest radius and highest charge density, resulting in more pronounced electron density redistribution. In contrast, the larger and more diffuse Ba2+ has much weaker interaction with water, leading to a gradual change in its charge distribution.

Charge distributions and charge transfer of M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba; n = 1–15) by KSpot. (a) Be­[(H2O) n ]2+; (b) Mg­[(H2O) n ]2+; (c) Ca­[(H2O) n ]2+; (d) Sr­[(H2O) n ]2+; (e) Ba­[(H2O) n ]2+; (f) Schematic diagram of charge transfer between alkaline-earth metal ion and water molecules.

The right Y-axis (red curves) shows the charge transfer quantity that is defined as the equivalent number of electrons effectively transferred from water molecules to the metal ion, which further quantifies the interaction strength. This value increases monotonically with n but exhibits a clear slowdown or even plateaus beyond a certain point (marked by axis breaks in the plots, n = 5 (*) for Be2+, n = 7 (*) for Mg2+, Ca2+, Sr2+, and Ba2+). This inflection aligns precisely with the saturation of the first hydration shell identified in earlier geometric analyses (at n = 6–8), indicating that the inner shell has largely completed its encapsulation of the metal ion, achieving maximal charge shielding efficiency. This correlation between geometric saturation and charge transfer saturation is not coincidental. Each inner-shell water molecule donates a lone pair oriented toward the metal center, enabling efficient charge transfer. Once all primary coordination sites are occupied, additional water molecules are forced into the second shell, where its lone pairs are oriented tangentially to participate in hydrogen bonding instead of direct metal coordination. As a result, the directionality and efficiency of charge transfer are diminished, leading to a natural upper limit in the metal’s charge-accepting capacity. Additional water molecules that populate the second hydration shell are located farther from the metal center and therefore contribute much less to further charge redistribution, resulting in diminished increments in the charge transfer value. For Be2+ and Mg2+, the charge transfer quantities show slight fluctuations or even minor decreases at n larger than 8. The incorporation of second-shell water molecules can subtly perturb the spatial arrangement of the first-shell ligands, thereby modulating their coordination strength and the degree of electron density polarization associated with the metal-water interactions.

A clear hierarchy emerges across different metal ions. The overall charge transfer capability follows the order Be2+ > Mg2+ > Ca2+ > Sr2+ > Ba2+, which directly mirrors the increase in ionic radius and corresponding decrease in charge density. This result provides direct microscopic support for the classical solvation evidence, where ions with higher charge density exhibit stronger hydration, and offers insight into ion selectivity in biological systems. For instance, calcium channels in cell membranes can specifically recognize Ca2+ over Ba2+ in part, because Ca2+ exhibits stronger hydration and a greater tendency for charge transfer, thereby requiring a higher energy barrier to shed its hydration shell upon entering the channel.

It is worth noting that clusters containing second-shell water molecules consistently show a marked reduction in the rate of charge transfer increase, which highlights the layered nature of ion hydration. The first hydration shell dominates charge screening and electronic stabilization, whereas the second and subsequent shells primarily influence the overall geometry and dynamical behavior of the hydrated cluster rather than its electronic structure. This distinction has important implications for multiscale solvation modeling, where the first hydration shell may be treated as an electronically active core and outer-shell water molecules can be approximated as an environmental screening medium.

Collectively, the charge distribution and transfer analyses demonstrate that the intrinsic properties of metal ions, such as ionic radius and charge density, determine the strength of their interactions with water molecules, while the number of hydration shells governs the saturation of these interactions.

Stereoselectivity and Steric Effects

Steric hindrance is a fundamental concept in chemistry, but it has traditionally been described in qualitative terms, mainly to identify atoms or functional groups subject to steric crowding. Based on conceptual density functional theory, Liu et al. introduced the notion of steric force as a quantitative descriptor of spatial congestion. A larger steric force value indicates stronger steric hindrance, which in turn reduces the accessibility for approaching reactants. Within this framework, our group has mapped steric force onto the MICC surface, enabling both qualitative and quantitative visualization of steric repulsion distributions within cluster structures and providing insight into their stereoselectivity. The theoretical foundation and detailed derivations are presented in ref .

In the steric force maps, red denotes regions of strong steric repulsion and blue corresponds to regions of weak steric hindrance. The KSpot molecular face shows that the calcium ion acts as a strong electrophilic center, while the oxygen atoms of surrounding water molecules serve as nucleophilic centers. Newly added water molecules preferentially bind to regions of lower electron density and greater spatial accessibility. In monolayer structures, the incoming water molecule coordinates directly to the Ca2+. As for bilayer structures, additional water molecules instead attach via hydrogen bonds at interstitial sites of the first hydration shell.

The steric force maps elucidate the stereoselectivity underlying this growth process. As the number of first-shell water molecules increases, spatial crowding within the coordination sphere becomes progressively more severe. Further addition of water molecules therefore leads to a sharp increase in steric repulsion, which reduces the successive binding energy and drives the system toward the formation of a second hydration shell. This structural reorganization alleviates steric strain and enhances overall stability.

For M­[(H2O) n ]2+, the monolayer configuration forces the seventh water molecule into an already crowded coordination sphere, resulting in significant geometric distortion and high steric repulsion. Although electrostatic interactions remain strong, the successive binding energy (ΔE = 20.02 kcal/mol) is relatively low. In contrast, the bilayer isomer sacrifices part of the direct ion–dipole interaction but gains stabilization through hydrogen bonding with the first shell. It also occupies a spatially open region above the equatorial plane, where steric repulsion is minimal, with steric force value as low as 0.0020 au Consequently, the bilayer structure exhibits a higher successive binding energy of 23.85 kcal/mol and greater thermodynamic stability. Thus, the seven-water cluster resides at the critical transition point between monolayer and bilayer structures.

A similar trend is observed for clusters containing eight water molecules. A similar trend is observed for clusters containing eight water molecules. In the monolayer arrangement, eight water molecules are forced into a compact cage-like geometry, leading to severe geometric distortion, elongated Ca2+–O bonds, distorted coordination angles, and markedly elevated steric repulsion. This configuration displays the lowest binding energy among all isomers, with a value of 18.61 kcal/mol, rendering it energetically unfavorable. By contrast, the bilayer structure enables the formation of a more complete hydrogen-bonding network. The two additional water molecules experience minimal mutual repulsion and reside above the first hydration shell, maintaining low steric hindrance and achieving a substantially higher binding energy of 23.23 kcal/mol. Consequently, the bilayer architecture becomes the dominant configuration at this cluster size.

These KSpot-derived descriptors provide a direct mechanistic rationale for the gas-phase IRMPD experiments of Bush et al. Their IRMPD spectra of M2+(H2O)7 (M = Mg, Ca, Ba) reveal the onset of second-shell formation through the emergence of bonded–OH bands (<3520 cm–1) and spectral signatures indicative of coexisting n = 6 and 7 populations. The present steric force mapping quantitatively resolves this observation: the monolayer (7,0) configuration forces the seventh water into a congested coordination sphere, elevating steric repulsion and yielding a lower successive binding energy (ΔE = 20.02 kcal/mol), whereas the bilayer (6,1) isomer minimizes steric strain (0.0020 au) and achieves greater stability via hydrogen-bonding compensation (ΔE = 23.85 kcal/mol). Overall, the KSpot molecular face theory can provide a reasonable theoretical basis for investigating the hydration shell evolution of hydrated alkaline-earth metal ion clusters.

In summary, clusters with six or fewer water molecules favor monolayer structures due to strong electrostatic attraction and low steric repulsion. At seven waters, steric crowding in the monolayer begins to substantially destabilize the system, allowing the bilayer form to compete effectively with its lower steric cost. By eight water molecules, steric strain in the monolayer becomes prohibitive, and the bilayer structure becomes energetically preferred. The coexistence of both structural motifs in the seven- and eight-water clusters arises precisely because electrostatic attraction and steric repulsion reach a delicate balance at these sizes.

By integrating molecular face analysis with steric force mapping, this study provides both qualitative insight and quantitative predictive capability or the conformational stability of hydrated alkaline earth metal ion clusters. This combined framework establishes a robust theoretical basis for understanding the structural evolution and stereoselectivity of M­[(H2O) n ]2+ clusters over a wide range of hydration levels. The Ca2+ is used as a representative example. The evolution of hydration shells is not merely a geometric packing process, but a thermodynamically governed balance between electronic stabilization and steric relief. The KSpot molecular face captures the progression of polarization and charge screening, while steric force mapping quantifies the buildup of spatial congestion. The crossover at n = 7–8 marks the point where steric repulsion overcomes the electrostatic driving force for further coordination, resulting in a transition from a single-layer to a double-layer hydration structure. This electronic-steric interplay provides a consistent and unified explanation for the discontinuous hydration behavior observed across the entire alkaline-earth series. Nevertheless, it should be noted that the structures reported here correspond to local minima identified via a sequential growth protocol; while representative competing isomers (e.g., monolayer vs bilayer for Ca2+ at n = 7–8) were examined, a comprehensive global search will be conducted in future as the benchmark for gas-phase metal ion hydration clusters.

Conclusions

Within the framework of Molecular Face Theory (MFT) based on the Kohn–Sham one-electron potential (KSpot), this work systematically elucidates the structural, electronic, and energetic evolutions in hydrated alkaline earth-metal ion clusters, M­[(H2O) n ]2+ (M = Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, and Ba, n = 1–15). The results demonstrate that ionic charge density is the central factor governing hydration behavior. In detail, the total water binding energy, metal–oxygen bond strength, and first ionization potential all decrease significantly as the ionic radius increases, reflecting a diminishing trend in electrostatic interactions. In light of KSpot molecular face and atomic partitioning, it is clearly identified that the inner-shell coordination is dominated by ion–dipole interactions, whereas the outer shell is stabilized primarily by hydrogen-bonding networks, exhibiting distinct hierarchical differences in electron density distribution. The strength of metal–water coordination is quantitatively encoded in the depth of the KSpot at the saddle point along a chemical bond (D pb ) at the M2+–O direction. Its strong correlations with both bond length (R = 0.98) and binding energy (R = 0.99) elucidate that D pb captures variations in coordination stability more directly than geometric measures alone. From this perspective, the monotonic weakening of hydration from Be2+ to Ba2+ emerges naturally as a consequence of decreasing charge density rather than as an empirical trend.

Hydration shell formation is further shown to be intrinsically discontinuous. Inflection points in surface area, volume, and metal-centered regional volume consistently identify the saturation of the first hydration shell, beyond which additional water molecules contribute primarily to hydrogen-bonded networks instead of enhancing metal–water coordination. The analysis reveals a rapid buildup and subsequent saturation of electrostatic screening in the first hydration shell, which provides a clear electronic marker for shell completion via charge partitioning. The calcium ion clusters illustrate how structural transitions arise from the competition between electronic stabilization and steric repulsion. For seven and eight water molecules, monolayer coordination becomes increasingly penalized by steric hindrance, despite favorable ion–dipole interactions. The emergence of bilayer structures at this size therefore reflects an energetic compromise, where reduced direct coordination is offset by relief of steric hindrance and enhanced hydrogen bonding. This transition is simultaneously resolved in both the KSpot molecular face and steric force maps, demonstrating that electronic and steric effects cannot be separated in the shell-closure region.

It should be noted that the structural and energetic trends reported here are derive from gas-phase M­[(H2O) n ]2+ clusters and thus reflect intrinsic ion–water interactions in the absence of bulk solvent effects. While these results provide valuable benchmarks for coordination preferences, charge-transfer mechanisms, and hydration-shell saturation, their direct extrapolation to aqueous solution or biological environments should require caution. In particular, factors such as dielectric screening, entropic contributions, dynamic solvent exchange, and the presence of counterions or macromolecular constraints may substantially influence the observed behavior. Future studied that integrate the present KSpot with explicit or implicit solvation models, as well as molecular dynamics, are expected to further connect gas-phase clusters insights with condensed-phase systems.

Beyond the gas-phase benchmark, the present results have several important implications. The present results have several important implications. The identified interplay between charge screening and steric effects provides a microscopic basis for ion selectivity, particularly in distinguishing ions of different sizes and charge densities. In addition, the combined Molecular Face and Steric Force analyses offer a practical framework for predicting preferred interacting regions, which is relevant to ligand binding, nucleation, and crystal growth processes. The descriptors developed in this work further establish a unified connection between electronic structure and hydration-shell evolution, and are expected to be transferable to more complex systems, including solution environments and interfacial chemistry.

Put together, the studied results show that alkaline-earth metal ion hydration is governed by a hierarchy of physical constraints that can be consistently resolved using KSpot molecular face and steric force analysis. This framework gains insight into the understanding of the microscopic hydration mechanisms of alkaline-earth metal ions and offers a new theoretical perspective for ion solvation, biomolecular recognition, and materials design. Furthermore, KSpot will be extended to multicomponent ionic systems or interfacial environments, combined with ab initio or classical molecular dynamics simulations, enabling direct analysis of the coupling between electronic structure and conformational evolution under dynamic hydration conditions.
