Global microplastic pollution poses a disproportionate reproductive threat to critically endangered marine fishes
Authors: Chunhui Liu, Xiangang Hu, Ruiqi Wang, Xiaofan Li, Peng Deng, Qian Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroplastic pollution is ubiquitous across the global ocean, threatening marine ecosystems. While environmental baselines often treat contamination as a temporally uniform stressor, the reproductive period represents the most physiologically vulnerable window in the life cycle of marine fishes. However, it remains unknown how seasonal reproductive aggregations spatiotemporally interact with microplastic accumulation hotspots and vector-driven co-contaminants at a global scale. Here we show that the spatiotemporal overlap between peak spawning and pollution intensity poses a disproportionate threat to marine fish communities, particularly critically endangered species. Integrating 6327 seawater microplastic observations and reproductive traits of 992 species across 65 large marine ecosystems, we reveal that microplastic-mediated vector effects for polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons and perfluorooctane sulfonate peak precisely during the critical spring spawning window, amplifying synergistic bio-risks by up to 42.3%. This reproductive-period vulnerability is exacerbated by climate-driven warming and hypoxia, which synergistically maximize microplastic bioconcentration factors, leading critically endangered fishes to endure compound vector exposures nine times higher than least-concern taxa. With 7.8% of global critical spawning grounds currently exceeding ecological safety thresholds, these findings fundamentally challenge existing static conservation paradigms. Explicitly incorporating these seasonal life-cycle dimensions into global marine management is imperative to safeguard threatened biodiversity and fisheries sustainability.Keywords: Microplastics, Reproductive period, Fish, Spawning habitat, Trophic level, Threatened species

Introduction

Microplastic (MP, plastic fragments smaller than 5 mm in diameter) pollution has been widely recognized as a major threat to aquatic ecosystems [1], especially coastal ecosystems [2]. Reproductive exposure to MPs induces multiple adverse effects in aquatic organisms [[3], [4], [5]], including oxidative stress, endocrine disruption, reduced fecundity, and embryonic abnormalities (e.g., gonadal histopathology, impaired fertilization, and developmental malformations). However, assessments of microplastic pollution frequently overlook how reproductive strategies and the spatial heterogeneity of spawning habitats modulate these effects [6]. The reproductive period represents a critical yet consistently understudied window of vulnerability across marine fish species [7]. Prior research, largely confined to controlled laboratory experiments and short-term field surveys, has been insufficient to elucidate how interspecific variation in reproductive traits interacts with spatiotemporal gradients of MP pollution during spawning [8]. Moreover, the field lacks both global-scale, long-term observational data to identify reproduction-specific MP burden patterns and multifactorial mechanistic analyses of the drivers underlying such variation [9].

The spawning habitats of marine fish are typically located in nutrient-rich nearshore waters [10], which often serve as major convergence zones for terrestrial MP inputs [11]. However, it remains unclear whether peak spawning activity coincides with seasonal pulses of MP pollution. Environmental conditions (e.g., temperature and dissolved oxygen) and anthropogenic activities (e.g., shipping and wastewater discharge) vary throughout reproductive periods and likely influence fish physiological and metabolic processes [12], thereby altering MP biotransfer and accumulation patterns. However, the relative importance of these factors remains poorly understood. Microplastics have been detected in 34 threatened fish species (classified as critically endangered, endangered, or vulnerable) [13]. These species are particularly susceptible to anthropogenic stressors due to their restricted geographic ranges, reduced population resilience, and obligate habitat dependencies [14]. Despite this vulnerability, evidence quantifying their MP exposure risk during reproductive periods is lacking, particularly because many threatened species occupy coastal refugia that may overlap with pollution accumulation zones.

MP particles are highly efficient vectors for persistent organic pollutants in marine environments [15]. Among these contaminants, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) [16] and per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances [17], particularly long-chain perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS) [18], have emerged as priority pollutants due to their persistence, hydrophobicity and toxicity. MPs adsorb PAHs through hydrophobic interactions [19] and bind PFOS through electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions [20], forming stable pollutant–vector complexes. The transfer of PAHs and PFOS via MPs can increase contaminant burdens and prolong pollutant residence times in fish tissues [21], effects that may be particularly pronounced during energy-intensive reproductive periods [22]. However, direct spatiotemporal evidence demonstrating that the restricted spawning ranges of threatened species disproportionately overlap with pollutant hotspots remains lacking.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study integrated 6327 in situ seawater MP observations, 3959 fish MP burden samples, and in situ water sampling data for PAHs and PFOS, encompassing reproductive parameters for 992 marine fish species across 65 large marine ecosystems (LMEs). These data were used to develop a global-scale assessment framework for evaluating MP risk during fish reproduction. Spatiotemporal dynamic modeling, statistical analyses, and data-driven approaches were used to quantify the spatiotemporal coupling characteristics of global marine fish MP exposure during reproductive periods in the water, organism, and vector dimensions. This framework enabled the identification of reproduction-specific MP stress, characterization of the spatiotemporal dynamics of pollutant vector effects, and evaluation of exposure risks among threatened species. This work provides a crucial scientific foundation for marine ecosystem conservation and sustainable fisheries management.

Materials and Methods

Data inventory compilation

In this study, we established a multidimensional data integration framework to compile a comprehensive dataset to assess the global effects of marine MPs on fish reproduction. The framework integrates marine environmental pollutant concentrations, fish MP burden data, reproductive ecological parameters, and environmental drivers. Rigorous data selection criteria were applied to ensure data quality, including the availability of precise spatiotemporal coordinates, standardized analytical methods, comprehensive quality control procedures, detailed methodological descriptions, and adequate sample sizes.

Marine environmental pollutant data were compiled through systematic literature searches of the Web of Science Core Collection and China National Knowledge Infrastructure databases using the keywords “marine” and “microplastics.” Studies were limited to in situ monitoring of marine surface waters (0–5 m depth) published between 1998 and 2023. Following strict screening procedures, we retained 6327 observations of marine surface MP concentrations. To quantify MP vector effects, we used the same search strategy to obtain 528 PAH records and 1891 PFOS concentration records in marine environments, providing a basis for evaluating the synergistic effects of MPs and associated pollutants (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Fish MP burden data were obtained through systematic searches of the Web of Science Core Collection for studies published between 2010 and 2023 using the keywords “fish” and “microplastics.” Eligible studies included marine fish species, field-collected samples, quantitative individual particle content data expressed as items per individual (items ind−1) based on gastrointestinal tract (GIT) content, and reported MP types. This work focused on microplastics in gastrointestinal contents across the most widely cited reports. To avoid bias arising from tissue differences, we excluded data on non-gastrointestinal tissues (e.g., muscle, gills, and liver) from relatively few reports. Of the methodologically documented samples, approximately 68.5% underwent polymer-level identification via Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (67.0%) or Raman spectroscopy (1.6%; percentages are rounded), while the remaining were identified by morphological criteria alone, including stereo-microscopy or transmitted polarized light microscopy (20.1%) and hot-needle testing (11.4%). Morphological identification without spectroscopic confirmation is susceptible to false positives from biogenic particles, which may introduce upward bias into burden estimates.

We standardized the GIT tissue to minimize interspecific variability associated with tissue-specific accumulation patterns. GIT contents primarily reflect direct environmental ingestion and the primary route of MP uptake in fish, but do not necessarily represent chronic bioaccumulation in target tissues. To assess potential analytical bias arising from the limited availability of paired tissue samples and interspecific variation in tissue-to-GIT concentration relationships, we extracted paired measurements from studies reporting both GIT and alternative tissue concentrations (i.e., muscle or gill) and evaluated these relationships using regression fitting and statistical significance testing (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The final dataset comprised 3959 valid samples representing common coastal fish species worldwide, providing broad coverage across ecological groups. Given the complexity of marine ecosystems and the heterogeneous spatial distribution of available data, we adopted 65 global LMEs as the primary analytical units. Fish spawning locations were subsequently mapped within this framework (Supplementary Fig. S3 and Table S1).

Reproductive ecological parameters were compiled from multiple authoritative sources, including spawning season and trophic-level information from FishBase, spawning distribution data [23] (Supplementary Text S1), species occurrence records from the Ocean Biodiversity Information System [24,25], and conservation status classifications from the International Union for Conservation of Nature Red List [26] (Supplementary Fig. S4).

To quantify variation in MP transfer across the life cycle, we calculated the bioconcentration factor (BCF) as the ratio of MP burden in organisms to MP abundance in the surrounding medium. BCFs were calculated separately for each species during reproductive and non-reproductive periods. We then defined the BCF ratio as the reproductive-period BCF divided by the non-reproductive-period BCF to compare transfer efficiency; values greater than 1 indicate enhanced transfer efficiency during reproduction. For spatial analyses, species-level BCFs were averaged within each 0.5° grid cell for each period.

The marine fish species included in this study represent the major groups targeted by global commercial fishing operations, including anchovies, cod, flatfish, herrings, Perciformes, salmonids, and tuna, among others [27]. These species are distributed across LMEs in all major ocean basins (Supplementary Table S1). Although the dataset focuses on commercially exploited species rather than the full diversity of marine fish, this emphasis aligns with our objective to assess the impacts of microplastics on fisheries sustainability and food security and reflects the availability of globally comparable spawning and burden data.

Environmental driver data were obtained from monthly global oceanographic datasets provided by the European Copernicus Marine Service [28]. Variables included sea surface temperature (SST), salinity, and current velocity at a spatial resolution of 0.5° (Supplementary Table S2). Indicators of anthropogenic activity intensity were derived from Global Fishing Watch shipping data [29], including vessel locations, vessel types, and activity duration, which were aggregated to the same spatial resolution (0.5°). Riverine plastic discharge data were incorporated using spatial interpolation of point-source discharge data [30] using an improved Eulerian weighted-advection model [31].

Outliers were identified using the interquartile range method. All variables were standardized through unit conversion (MP: items m−3; PAHs: ng g−1; PFOS: ng L−1; fish burden: items ind−1). Duplicate sampling locations were consolidated using weighted averages calculated with 64-bit double-precision arithmetic. To address differences in spatial resolution among datasets, we used bilinear interpolation to standardize all variables to a common 0.5° × 0.5° spatial grid. To mitigate multicollinearity, we combined variance inflation factor analysis with recursive feature elimination to optimize the model. All data processing was conducted in Python 3.9 using NumPy and SciPy, yielding a high-quality multidimensional dataset suitable for global-scale analysis of MP impacts on fish reproduction.

Ecological threshold assessment framework

To quantify the ecological risks of MP pollution to marine fish communities, we employed the species sensitivity distribution (SSD) approach to develop an ecological threshold assessment framework based on global observational data. The primary objective of this method is to establish dose–response relationships between pollutant concentrations and the proportion of potentially affected species by statistically characterizing interspecific variation in tolerance to environmental contaminants [32].

First, species-specific MP exposure intensities were determined using spawning ground distribution data and corresponding environmental MP concentration measurements. We defined exposure intensity as the maximum MP concentration observed within each species’ spawning habitat, representing the highest level of exposure under which the species has been observed to maintain reproductive activity in the wild [33]. Species-specific exposure intensities were then ranked in ascending order to characterize the relationship between MP concentration gradients and the cumulative proportion of affected species. At each concentration level, the potentially affected fraction (PAF) was calculated as the proportion of species with exposure intensities no greater than that concentration. PAF therefore represents the fraction of the species assemblage potentially subject to adverse effects.

Spatial modeling and analysis

To quantify the spatial distribution of global marine MP concentrations and predict pollution risks in fish spawning habitats, we developed a machine learning framework based on the random forest algorithm [36]. The framework focused on MPs as the primary pollutant while incorporating additional pollutant indicators, including PAHs and PFOS. Integrating marine environmental variables, anthropogenic activity intensity indicators, and in situ pollutant observations, the framework employed grid search optimization to develop a global marine pollution risk model focused on MP prediction. Pollutant concentration data were log10-transformed prior to modeling to reduce skewness. Model performance was evaluated using R2, mean squared error, root mean squared error, mean absolute error, and explained variance score, providing a comprehensive assessment of model fit, predictive accuracy, stability, and explanatory power (Supplementary Text S2 and Fig. S5).

We used the TreeExplainer algorithm to compute SHAP (Shapley additive explanations) values to quantify feature importance in both the reproductive-period and full life-cycle datasets [37]. To characterize nonlinear responses of key environmental variables, we used generalized additive models [38] to model relationships between SHAP values and feature variables [39]. Nonlinear relationships were modeled using the LinearGAM model from the pygam package, where s(0) denotes a spline term fitted to the first predictor variable in the model matrix. We used piecewise linear regression to identify threshold responses of MP concentrations to environmental variables. The piecewise-linear fitting algorithm [40] estimated breakpoint locations by minimizing the sum of squared residuals across segments. We generated bivariate partial dependence plots [41] to evaluate the interactive effects of SST and O2 on BCF ratios by predicting responses across a grid of feature-value combinations while marginalizing over all other environmental variables.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal overlap of microplastics and spawning habitats

Marine fish reproduction is subject to substantial MP pollution pressure, with pronounced spatial heterogeneity across the 65 LMEs examined (Fig. 1a). Exposure levels within spawning grounds were positively associated with both spawning species richness and MP concentration, a pattern that may reflect the co-occurrence of nutrient-rich environments and anthropogenic terrestrial inputs. Species sensitivity distributions revealed clear ecological thresholds across observed MP exposure levels (Fig. 1b). The derived ET20, ET50, and ET80 values were 0.7, 3.6, and 18.2 items m−3, respectively. Approximately 7.8% of spawning habitats across global LMEs exceeded the ET20 threshold, indicating widespread exposure risk within critical reproductive habitats.

MP concentrations in economically and ecologically important spawning grounds consistently exceeded global background levels. The Baltic Sea (LME 23) and Gulf of Thailand (LME 35) exhibited mean concentrations greater than 2.5 items m−3, approximately 7.8 times the global average of 0.3 items m−3. Although mean concentrations at the LME scale remained below the ET50 threshold, strong spatial heterogeneity in pollutant accumulation generated localized hotspots with substantially elevated concentrations. This pattern underscores the vulnerability of key spawning habitats to MP contamination.

Experimental studies employing food-dilution approaches have identified a range-median effect concentration that represents the threshold for observable reproductive toxicity in marine fish [42]. The 0.1% level of this experimental threshold (4.0 items m−3) fell between our field-derived ET50 and ET80 values, providing independent support for the species sensitivity distribution approach applied to spawning populations. Although current environmental concentrations remain below laboratory-derived toxicity thresholds, the chronic nature of exposure and the potential for cumulative effects suggest that existing pollution levels already represent a meaningful ecological concern. Regional risk stratification based on ecological thresholds revealed pronounced clustering of high-risk areas along densely populated coastlines and major shipping corridors (Fig. 1c), consistent with previous studies [43]. Approximately 89.1 km2 of global spawning habitat exceeded the 0.1% experimental threshold, with the greatest concentrations occurring in the South China Sea (6.0% of LME 36) and Baltic Sea (15.6% of LME 23) (Supplementary Fig. S6). These findings indicate that reproductive stress is already occurring in some of the world's most productive fishing grounds.

Long-term monitoring revealed a marked increase in MP stress within spawning environments (Supplementary Fig. S7). We estimate that the global mean concentrations increased from 0.2 items m−3 in 1998 to 0.3 items m−3 in 2023, representing a 69.0% increase. During the same period, spawning habitats exceeding the ET20 and ET50 thresholds increased by 72.4% and 184.0%, respectively. The relationship between MP concentration and the number of exposed species also strengthened significantly, with the slope of the linear regression increasing from 17.2 species per log10(items m−3) in 1998 to 25.6 species per log10(items m−3) in 2023 (i.e., for every tenfold increase in MP concentration, the number of co-occurring exposed species increased by approximately 17 species in 1998 and 26 species in 2023). These results indicate that fish community exposure to MP pollution has become increasingly sensitive over time (p < 0.05).

The spatiotemporal patterns of MP exposure differed significantly among species in different threat categories (Fig. 1d and e). Nearly all species experienced significantly greater MP exposure during reproductive periods than during non-reproductive periods (Fig. 1d; p < 0.001). Critically endangered species exhibited the largest reproductive-period effect size (Fig. 1e; Cohen's d = 1.2, p < 0.05). These populations, already under substantial conservation pressure, appear particularly vulnerable to the additional burden of MP pollution. The association between MP exposure and conservation status suggests that elevated contamination may further exacerbate conservation challenges.

Reproductive-period species diversity and MP concentrations showed a seasonal synchrony in spring (Fig. 1f). Shannon diversity peaked in March–April (3.4–3.5), coinciding with relatively elevated MP concentrations (0.11–0.10 items m−3). Chao1 richness exhibited a similar spring peak (105–104), overlapping with periods of greatest MP exposure. These results suggest that marine fish communities may experience their highest MP exposure during the spring spawning period, when both reproductive activity and species diversity are at their peaks. Because spawning represents one of the most physiologically vulnerable phases of the fish life cycle [44], this temporal overlap may amplify the negative effects of MPs on reproductive success at the community level.

Environmentally associated microplastic burden during reproduction

MP burden differed significantly between reproductive and non-reproductive periods across the species included in this study (Fig. 2a; p < 0.05). The reproductive-to-non-reproductive MP burden ratio ranged from 0.2 to 5.8, highlighting substantial interspecific variation in physiological and ecological responses. Body length was evaluated as a potential explanatory factor given its association with gut retention capacity [45]. Although body length was statistically significant, it explained less than 2% of the variance in MP burden, indicating a weak association at the population level (Supplementary Fig. S8; R2 = 0.01, p < 0.01).

The analysis of global in situ samples confirmed distinct MP burden patterns between reproductive and non-reproductive individuals (Supplementary Fig. S9; p < 0.05). Reproductive individuals contained higher MP burdens (1.9 items ind−1) than non-reproductive individuals (1.7 items ind−1). Long-term analysis from 2010 to 2021 further revealed that the continuous increase in marine microplastic concentrations led to widespread increases in MP burden levels in fish populations (Supplementary Fig. S10). Notably, MP burden increased slightly faster during reproductive periods (slope: 0.22 items unit−1 year−1) than during non-reproductive periods (slope: 0.21 items unit−1 year−1), suggesting a growing threat to reproductive individuals and population recruitment.

Reproductive-period MP burden also varied among species of similar body size. For example, Gobius niger, a parental care species, exhibited markedly elevated MP burden during reproduction, whereas Engraulis encrasicolus, a pelagic broadcast spawner, showed the opposite pattern. These contrasting responses suggest that reproductive strategy may contribute to variation in reproductive-period MP burden.

To explore this possibility, species were classified into four reproductive strategy groups: parental care species, demersal spawners, pelagic broadcast spawners, and viviparous and ovo-viviparous species (Supplementary Table S3 and Fig. S11) [46]. Viviparous and ovo-viviparous species exhibited the highest reproductive-period MP burden (3.2 items ind−1), exceeding values observed during the non-reproductive period (1.9 items ind−1; p < 0.001). This pattern may reflect increased energetic demands associated with gestation, coupled with migration into nearshore nursery habitats characterized by elevated MP burdens. Parental care species similarly exhibited significantly higher MP burden during the reproductive period (3.0 versus 2.0 items ind−1; p < 0.001). This increase may reflect prolonged residence in benthic and coastal microhabitats, which are recognized as preferential zones for MP deposition [47]. Increased feeding intensity is a less likely explanation because parental males often reduce active foraging during the care period and experience declines in appetite-regulating hormones [48]. In contrast, pelagic broadcast spawners and demersal spawners exhibited no significant reproductive-period differences in MP burden, consistent with the absence of major habitat shifts or systematic changes in feeding behavior during spawning.

We used the SHAP method and elasticity analysis [49] to quantify the relative contributions of anthropogenic and environmental drivers during reproductive periods and across the full life cycle of marine fish (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. S12). SHAP values were used to assess the direction and relative importance of each predictor, whereas elasticity coefficients quantified the sensitivity of marine fish responses to incremental changes in these factors. Anthropogenic drivers, including shipping activities and riverine plastic discharge, showed consistent associations across the entire life cycle, reflecting persistent pressure on marine fish populations. Underwater noise from shipping activity may be an additional stressor affecting reproductive behavior. In contrast, environmental variables, including biogeochemical and water quality parameters, exhibited substantially stronger associations during reproductive periods.

SST and net primary productivity (NPP) exhibited pronounced nonlinear threshold responses during reproductive periods, with R2 values increasing from 0.4 to 0.8 for both SST and NPP relative to full-life-cycle patterns (Fig. 2c and d). These thresholds were derived empirically from in situ observations rather than predefined criteria, and breakpoint locations were confirmed by segmented regression analysis (Davies test, p < 0.001). At suboptimal temperatures (5–15 °C), the association between SST and MP burden was weak or negative, consistent with hypothesized reductions in metabolic rates and feeding activity. In contrast, a strong positive association emerged within the 20–30 °C range, potentially reflecting the convergence of peak vitellogenesis, increased foraging demand, and elevated environmental MP concentrations. Previous studies on seasonal variations in coastal waters similarly highlight the importance of temperature-dependent productivity in shaping MP burdens [50].

Structural equation modeling revealed potential multiple pathways associated with MP burden during reproductive periods (Fig. 2e). Given the complexity and spatial heterogeneity of global observational datasets, the estimated path coefficients should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Relative to full-life-cycle patterns (Supplementary Fig. S13), the absolute values of most path coefficients were greater during reproductive periods, suggesting that environmental controls on MP burden may be amplified during reproductive periods. SST and O2 were positively associated with MP accumulation, potentially through effects on metabolic activity and feeding frequency [51]. Nutrient availability was also associated with elevated NPP levels, which may increase the abundance of biological vectors and transfer pathways for MPs. Among anthropogenic pathways, the effect of coastal plastic discharge on MP burden was notably stronger during reproductive periods (β = 0.3) than across the full life cycle (β = 0.1). These patterns were consistent with the independent SHAP and generalized additive model analyses and suggest that microplastic burden during reproductive periods is associated with a combination of environmental drivers and anthropogenic pressures.

Trophic magnification of microplastic transfer during reproduction

The BCF ratio was used to quantify life cycle-dependent variation in MP transfer. MP transfer efficiency differed significantly among life cycle stages (Fig. 3a). BCF ratios across LMEs ranged from 0.1 to 4.7, and approximately 45.5% of LMEs exhibited enhanced transfer efficiency during reproductive periods. This enhancement was particularly pronounced in low-latitude regions (Fig. 3b). The Central American coastline (LME 11) exhibited the greatest reproductive-period enhancement, with a BCF ratio of 1.3 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.24–1.30), representing a 20.2% increase above the global average (1.1). Similar results were observed in the Guinea Current (LME 28, 1.2; 95% CI: 1.22–1.27) and Caribbean Sea (LME 12, 1.2; 95% CI: 1.18–1.27).

Under optimal temperature (8–15 °C) and dissolved oxygen (250–350 mmol m−3) conditions, the BCF ratios remained relatively low (0.9–1.0), indicating minimal differences in microplastic uptake between the reproductive and non-reproductive periods. This result aligns with the minimal differences in MP uptake between reproductive and non-reproductive periods. This finding is consistent with the optimal spawning temperature range reported for many fish species [52]. In contrast, under warmer and more hypoxic conditions (>28 °C and <210 mmol m−3), BCF ratios increased substantially, indicating greater physiological vulnerability during reproduction and enhanced MP biotransfer relative to non-reproductive periods. Under the most extreme conditions, when the SST was 31.3 °C, and the O2 concentration was approximately 211.0 mmol m−3, the BCF ratio reached a peak value of 1.1 (Fig. 3c).

Elevated temperatures are associated with increased metabolic rates and feeding activity in reproductively active fish [53]. When combined with the heightened energetic demands of vitellogenesis, these conditions may increase MP ingestion through the gastrointestinal tract. Hypoxia-induced cortisol responses can suppress gastrointestinal mucosal immunity [54], potentially reducing xenobiotic clearance and increasing MP retention times. The high-temperature, low-oxygen threshold identified here closely matches conditions characteristic of eutrophic coastal waters and seasonally hypoxic regions (Supplementary Fig. S14–S15), suggesting that climate warming and ocean deoxygenation may act synergistically to extend periods of environmental stress on fish during reproductive periods.

The analysis of trophic structure revealed a significant food web position dependence in reproductive-period MP transfer enhancement (Fig. 3d). Fishes with trophic levels below 3.0 (primarily filter-feeding and herbivorous species) exhibited relatively low BCF ratios (0.98 ± 0.08, p < 0.001), indicating limited enhancement of MP transfer during reproductive periods. BCF ratios increased progressively with trophic level, reaching 0.99 ± 0.10 in low-to-mid trophic species (3.0–3.5), 1.01 ± 0.09 in mid-to-high trophic species (3.5–4.0), and 1.02 ± 0.10 in apex predators (>4.0; p < 0.001). These results indicate that higher trophic-level fishes experience greater MP biomagnification pressure during reproductive periods.

This trophic magnification pattern is consistent with field observations showing a trophic magnification factor of 1.0 in commercial fish species, supporting the occurrence of MP biomagnification through marine food webs. This phenomenon may stem from increased demands for high-energy prey by higher-trophic-level species during reproduction [55,56], thereby amplifying MP accumulation through dietary pathways. The longer lifespans and slower pollutant-metabolism rates of apex predators may further contribute to this pattern.

Enhanced microplastic vector effects during reproduction

Based on the estimated vector effects of MPs, the MP–polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (MP–PAH) interaction index was 42.3% higher during fish reproductive periods than during non-reproductive periods, whereas the MP–perfluorooctane sulfonate (MP–PFOS) interaction index was 16.2% higher (Fig. 4a and b and Supplementary Fig. S16). The spatial distribution exhibited pronounced hotspot clustering and clear offshore gradients, with the highest values concentrated in the eastern North Atlantic, Mediterranean Sea, and Southeast Asian waters. These patterns closely correspond to major industrial centers and densely populated coastal regions [57].

Compared with MP–PAH thresholds, MP–PFOS thresholds were 7.6% higher in the South China Sea region, whereas MP–PAH thresholds were 7.05% higher in the Mediterranean Sea (Supplementary Fig. S17). Growing evidence indicates that MP can substantially amplify the toxicity of co-occurring contaminants in fish. Relative to single-pollutant exposure, combined exposure to MP-PAHs increased embryonic malformation rates and inhibited growth, reducing body length to approximately 90% of that observed in control groups [58]. Similarly, co-exposure to MP-PFOS decreased survival and hatching rates in fish embryos [59]. PAHs and PFOS, under the carrier effect of microplastics, enhanced oxidative stress and induced cellular apoptosis and tissue damage [60,61].

The magnitude of these vector effects depends on factors such as gut residence time and contaminant desorption kinetics. Under some conditions, MPs may sequester contaminants rather than release them, thereby reducing their bioavailability and absorption [62]. Nevertheless, these findings highlight the importance of accounting for vector effects when assessing reproductive risks to fish, particularly in heavily polluted coastal waters in industrialized, densely populated regions.

Threatened species generally exhibit lower population resilience and greater sensitivity to pollutant stressors [63]. Susceptibility indices increased progressively across threat categories, with critically endangered species exhibiting the greatest sensitivity to the synergistic effects of MP–PAHs and MP–PFOS. Average synergistic risk indices for critically endangered species were 9.0 and 13.3 times higher than those of least concern (LC) species, respectively (Fig. 4c and d). These effects were significant during reproductive periods (p < 0.001) but not during non-reproductive periods (Supplementary Fig. S18). The spatial and temporal overlap between the habitats of threatened species, highly polluted coastal environments, and reproductive activity likely contributes to the elevated exposure risk posed by MP-associated contaminants.

Monthly synergistic risk analyses based on reproductive data from marine fish species revealed pronounced seasonal patterns (Fig. 4e). Although May had the greatest number of spawning species (440), synergistic pollutant risk peaked in March (normalized value, 1.0), declined to its lowest levels during summer (June–August; 0.3–0.5), and gradually increased again during autumn and winter. These findings suggest that the combined effects of increased pollutant bioavailability and heightened physiological vulnerability are greatest during spring. Management approaches based solely on single-pollutant assessments or species diversity metrics may therefore fail to identify periods of greatest ecological vulnerability [64]. The interaction between MP vector effects, seasonal environmental cycles, and fish reproductive cycles may pose a substantial threat to reproductive success and the long-term sustainability of marine fish populations.

Uncertainties in risk assessment

Existing laboratory toxicological data are derived predominantly from a limited number of model species, particularly zebrafish (Danio rerio) and medaka (Oryzias latipes). Consequently, the reproductive toxicity thresholds reported for these species should be regarded as reference benchmarks rather than representative thresholds for global fish communities. Interspecific differences in metabolic physiology, reproductive strategy, and habitat use can lead to substantial variation in sensitivity to MP exposure. Considerable caution is warranted when extrapolating laboratory-derived reproductive toxicity thresholds [42] to global field-based risk assessments, and conclusions drawn from such extrapolations should be interpreted within the broader context of multispecies toxicological evidence.

Although the SSD approach partially accounts for interspecific variability, the SSD model developed in this study (R2 = 0.99) was parameterized using field-derived exposure intensities rather than dose–response toxicity data. This distinction is conceptually important because the relationship between the resulting ET20–ET80 ecological thresholds and toxicologically defined effect concentrations has not yet been empirically established. In addition, laboratory studies typically use uniform, smooth-surfaced plastic microspheres, whereas environmental MPs undergo extensive weathering through photo-oxidative degradation, biofilm colonization, and mechanical abrasion. These processes can substantially alter their physicochemical properties relative to pristine laboratory materials and may introduce systematic bias into laboratory-based risk estimates.

Furthermore, the complex co-exposure scenarios arising from MPs carrying organic contaminants such as PAHs and PFOS cannot be fully captured by a single-contaminant toxicity framework. The ecological thresholds and reproductive toxicity thresholds presented here should therefore be viewed as conservative indicators of potential risk based on the best available data, rather than definitive toxicological boundaries.

Conclusion

This study provides a global assessment of MP risks during marine fish reproduction. Elevated MP burdens during reproductive periods were associated with multiple interacting factors, including metabolic physiology, feeding behavior, environmental threshold responses, and contaminant vector effects. These patterns were particularly pronounced among threatened species and higher trophic-level fishes.

The spatial overlap between major spawning grounds and pollution hotspots, together with the combined pressures of climate warming and ocean deoxygenation, suggests that current marine MP management frameworks may underestimate risks during reproductive periods by treating contamination as a temporally uniform stressor. Our findings challenge the fundamental paradigm of existing marine pollution management, demonstrating that MP pollution management frameworks must explicitly incorporate the reproductive period as a critical vulnerability dimension. More broadly, the results demonstrate that risk amplification during the relatively brief reproductive window has likely been systematically underestimated in conventional assessment frameworks. This issue extends beyond the reproductive success of individual species and has important implications for the maintenance of marine biodiversity, the resilience of fish populations, and the long-term sustainability of global fisheries.
