Disseminated mucormycosis leading to a fatal gastrointestinal perforation in a pediatric case of EBV-associated Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis
Authors: Yingyi He, Rong Hu, Li Yuan, Weiqiang Xiao, Xiaoling Su, Li Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Disseminated mucormycosis is a rare and often fatal infection, primarily affecting immunocompromised individuals. Its diagnosis is extremely challenging, as definitive identification of organisms in tissue by histopathology with culture confirmation can be invasive, slow, and insensitive. We present the case of a 12-month-old female with Hemophagocytic Lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), triggered by a high Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) viral load and complicated by an underlying NK-cell perforin deficiency. Despite rapid HLH-specific and maximal supportive care, the patient developed life-threatening mucormycosis with extensive gastrointestinal involvement. Clinicians should note insidious signs, such as a black eschar and hypodense lesions on MRI imaging findings, indicative of tissue infarction from hyphal vascular invasion in these susceptible hosts. Prompt invasive testing of involved organs is critical upon suspicion.

Introduction

Invasive fungal infections (IFIs) have become a more significant threat over the past two decades, representing one of the leading causes of death in patients with high-risk hematologic malignancies [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Yeasts and molds are the most common causative agents. Mucormycosis, a severe IFI caused by ubiquitous filamentous fungi of the order Mucorales, is less common than aspergillosis but carries a particularly poor prognosis. The most frequently encountered genera are Rhizopus, Mucor, Lichtheimia, and Rhizomucor. Based on the involved organ system, mucormycosis is classified into several forms: pulmonary, rhinocerebral, cutaneous, gastrointestinal, and central nervous system infections. While most often reported in immunocompromised patients, an increasing number of cases are now seen in immunocompetent individuals. Despite early diagnosis and aggressive combined surgical and medical therapy, the prognosis remains poor due to rapid disease progression and frequent delays in diagnosis. The estimated mortality rate ranges significantly, from 32 % to 70 %, with cutaneous mucormycosis being a notable exception that rarely disseminates [5].

Hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) is a severe, life-threatening hyperinflammatory syndrome characterized by persistent fever, hepatosplenomegaly, pancytopenia, and the presence of hemophagocytosis in the reticuloendothelial system [6]. HLH is broadly categorized into two forms, primary and secondary. Primary HLH is caused by underlying genetic mutations that impair the cytotoxic function of T lymphocytes and Natural Killer (NK) cells, typically presenting in early childhood. Secondary HLH is triggered by severe infectious, malignant, rheumatologic, or metabolic conditions. Primary HLH and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-associated HLH are the predominant subtypes in children [7,8]. A nationwide survey in China identified EBV-HLH as the most common form, accounting for approximately 44.1 % of all pediatric cases, followed by genetic and other infection-associated forms [9]. Despite this classic distinction, emerging evidence suggests that many patients with presumed secondary HLH may have an underlying genetic predisposition. We present a case of secondary HLH in a child, initially triggered by a high EBV viral load, which was further complicated by disseminated mucormycosis. The fungal infection progressed rapidly to a fatal gastrointestinal perforation. This case highlights the critical need for vigilance regarding mucormycosis as a potentially lethal complication in these susceptible hosts, particularly in the setting of persistent neutropenia and profound immunosuppression.

Case presentation

A 12-month-old female was admitted to our hospital with a two-week history of persistent fevers up to 104°F. She subsequently developed progressive complications of capillary leak syndrome, manifesting as pleural effusions, breathlessness, oliguria, hypoalbuminemia and generalized edema. Family history was unremarkable. Upon initial evaluation, the patient was diagnosed with HLH. This diagnosis was supported by obvious hepatosplenomegaly and laboratory findings including worsening neutropenia(0.4 × 109/L, normal range 2.0∼7.2 × 10^9/L), moderate anemia(hemoglobin 78g/L, normal range 105∼145g/L), borderline thrombocytopenia(85 × 10^9/L, normal range 140∼440 × 10^9/L), hypofibrinogenemia(0.83g/L, normal range 2.00∼4.00 g/L), hypertriglyceridemia(7.94mmol/L, normal range <1.7mmol/L), elevated transaminases(aspartate transaminase 301 IU/L, normal range 7∼30IU/L), elevated soluble interleukin-2 receptor(>7500 U/mL, normal range 223-710U/mL), reduced NK cell activity(0.95 %, normal range ≥4 %) (Figure 1),and a rising ferritin level that peaked about 16,500ug/L(normal range 4.63-204ug/L). Work-up for infectious causes identified a high Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) viral load(1.67 × 105 copies/mL, normal range < 200 copies/mL); other bacterial and fungal cultures were unremarkable. Flow cytometry confirmed a profound cytotoxic defect in NK cells, characterized by undetectable perforin expression(0 %, reference range≥81 %) and severely deficient granzyme B expression(21.79 %, reference range ≥71 %). Bone marrow biopsy confirmed prominent hemophagocytosis. Flow cytometry revealed an activated T-cell population (14.3 % of total cells) with numerous CD38high/HLA-DR+CD8+ cells. T-cell receptor gene rearrangement analysis demonstrated clonality, raising strong suspicion for an underlying T-cell lymphoma driving the HLH. A planned cervical lymph node excisional biopsy was deferred due to severe coagulopathy. The patient received maximal supportive care, including mechanical ventilation, continuous veno-venous hemodiafiltration (CVVHDF). Empiric broad-spectrum antibiotics (imipenem/cilastatin 15 mg/kg q6h combined with vancomycin 15 mg/kg q6h) were administered promptly. A provisional diagnosis of primary HLH was strongly suspected given the patient's age (< 2 years), impaired NK-cell cytotoxicity, and severe presentation. Consequently, immunochemotherapy based on the HLH-2004 protocol (etoposide/dexamethasone) was urgently initiated on day 3 after admission. After a temporary improvement, the patient's condition worsened on day 8, marked by a returning fever and frequent maroon-colored stool. Physical examination revealed abdominal distension, and ultrasound demonstrated pelvic fluid and bowel gas. The patient also experienced a neurological decline and the neutrophil count reached a nadir of zero by day 5 of HLH-2004 protocol therapy. At the same time, blood cultures revealed the presence of the bacteria Staphylococcus haemolyticus and the fungus Candida parapsilosis. Additional symptoms included a black eschar on the nose on day 11, however this clinical sign was neglected at that time. Given her clinical decline, HLH therapy was discontinued, prompting an escalation of the anti-infective regimen to voriconazole (8 mg/kg q12h, with a subsequent therapeutic drug monitoring trough of 5 mg/L) plus Caspofungin (70 mg/m2/day) and a switch from linezolid to vancomycin. On day 19, the patient developed neurological abnormalities, specifically depressed consciousness and hypertension. Lumbar puncture showed intracranial hypertension(opening pressure >330 mmH2O; normal: 80-180 mmH2O), and inflammatory cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) findings: pleocytosis (42 × 106/L; normal range <5 × 106/L), markedly elevated protein (3.13g/L, normal range 0.23∼0.38g/L), and hypoglycorrhachia (1.43 mmol/L; normal range 2.5-4.4 mmol/L). CSF culture and mNGS were negative. Brain MRI identified an irregular, poorly marginated patchy lesion in the left parieto-occipital lobe. The lesion was isointense/hypointense on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and mildly hyperintense on T2-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR). Its center exhibited a linear, markedly hyperintense T2WI signal that appeared to communicated with the left lateral ventricle(Figure 2). On day 21, the patient developed rapid-onset hypotension requiring transfusion support and abdominal rigidity, raising strong suspicion for bowel perforation. An emergency laparotomy revealed extensive and unresectable mucormycosis involving the entire gastrointestinal tract from the stomach to the ileum. Histopathology confirmed diffuse, full-thickness gastric necrosis with complete loss of mucosal architecture. Mucorales hyphae were identified in the tissue as broad (3-25 μm in diameter), thin-walled, aseptate, and folded, with twisted, compressed, and irregularly branching forms (Figure 3). Despite a subsequent escalation to high-dose liposomal amphotericin B(10mg/kg/day) and emergency surgery, the patient's condition deteriorated with recurrent hypotension. Discussion

The delayed diagnosis of mucormycosis remains a major clinical challenge, attributable to several interrelated factors that impede early detection and intervention. First, the disease's initial presentation is often non-specific and site-dependent, involving the cutaneous, pulmonary, abdominal, or neurological systems. Furthermore, even classic clinical and radiological signs are frequently subtle in early stages. The pathophysiological basis of mucormycosis —angioinvasion by hyphae, leading to thrombosis, tissue infarction, and necrosis—was illustrated in our case by an early necrotic lesion on the nasal bridge, a finding not initially recognized as fungal in origin. These challenges are particularly pronounced in gastrointestinal involvement, where non-specific symptoms such as abdominal pain and gastrointestinal bleeding, as seen here, frequently progress to catastrophic complications like bowel infarction and hemorrhagic shock before a diagnosis can be established [10]. A third critical barrier is the lack of reliable non-invasive diagnostic tools. Currently, no effective serological tests or serum biomarkers exist for early detection [11,12]. As a result, diagnosis continues to rely on tissue biopsy and histopathology as the gold standard [13,14]. However, this approach is invasive, time-consuming, and subject to sampling error, further delaying confirmation. Consequently, the diagnosis is often made only during surgery or postmortem, underscoring the ongoing difficulty in achieving timely and accurate detection.

Children with HLH are profoundly immunosuppressed, a state resulting from both the immune dysregulation inherent to the disease and the intensive chemotherapy used for treatment. This profound vulnerability makes them highly susceptible to opportunistic infections, particularly IFIs [15]. Evidence from a single-center retrospective study of 18 children with primary HLH underscores this lethal risk: an infectious agent was documented at initial presentation in five patients, and significant infections occurred during therapy in 10 of 18 patients (56 %). Furthermore, among the 12 fatal cases, invasive infection caused death in eight children, with six of these deaths attributable to IFI [16]. The severity of this risk was also evident in the current case, where prolonged and severe neutropenia (<500 cells/μL for >7 days) throughout the disease course represented a high risk factor for a life-threatening infection. Beyond acquired immunosuppression, patients with primary HLH are believed to have fixed defects in cytotoxic cell function. While an infectious trigger may initiate HLH in these patients, the specific immunologic cause may not always be apparent. Currently, Natural Killer (NK) cell cytotoxicity is considered a better predictor of a patient's immune status than other indicators defined in the HLH-2004 criteria, given that NK cell function was impaired in the majority of these FLH cases [17]. In our specific patients, the confluence of deficient NK cell activity, severe systemic symptoms at diagnosis, and a trajectory of deterioration despite initial immunosuppressive therapy collectively raised a strong clinical suspicion of an underlying immunodeficiency, even with negative whole-exome sequencing results. Given these factors, these patients face a clear risk of HLH recurrence, making hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) mandatory for long-term survival [18].

Conclusion

In conclusion, we present a pediatric disseminated mucormycosis case with EBV-associated HLH in the setting of an underlying immunodeficiency. Physicians should keep in mind that the mucormycosis cannot be neglected in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent patients. A definitive diagnosis of mucormycosis requires a comprehensive clinical and radiological imaging, in addition to mycological and histological evidences. Once mucormycosis is suspected, a biopsy of the affected tissues and investigation of the other involved organs must be promptly conducted.
