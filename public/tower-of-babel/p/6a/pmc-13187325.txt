PAK5 drives vascular remodeling in hypoxic pulmonary hypertension via Drp1-dependent mitochondrial midzone division
Authors: Junming Zhang, Huimin Yan, Yan Wang, Yuwen Dai, Chenfei Zhao, Yukun Gan, Limin Liu, Qiong Liu, Lan Wang, Zhichao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial dynamics play an indispensable role in maintaining the proliferation-apoptosis balance of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), a process closely linked to hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH). P21-activated kinase 5 (PAK5) has been implicated in cancer-related mitochondrial dysfunction to induce cell proliferation/apoptosis and pulmonary hypertension (PH) shares numerous similarities with cancer, yet its pathophysiological role in HPH, and its mechanistic connection to mitochondrial fission remains unclear. Here, we demonstrated that PAK5 expression was upregulated in the lungs and pulmonary vasculature and mitochondrial fission was dysregulated following HPH injury. In tissue samples from PH patients, PAK5 expression positively correlated with pulmonary vascular remodeling. In vivo, PAK5-silenced mice exhibited significantly improved hemodynamic parameters and right ventricular function compared to control mice. In vitro, hypoxia-induced upregulation of PAK5 promoted dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) activation, leading to the occurrence of mitochondrial midzone division, reduced expression of the mitochondrial fusion protein Mfn1, and enhanced expression of proliferative proteins including PCNA, Cyclin A, and Cyclin D. Pharmacological or viral-induced PAK5 deficiency resulted in enhanced Mfn1 expression, reduced levels of Drp1, Mff, PCNA, Cyclin A, and Cyclin D, and consequently attenuated mitochondrial midzone division and excessive PASMC proliferation. Our findings indicate that PAK5 participates in the development of HPH by driving mitochondrial fission through Drp1, and this study provides a new potential therapeutic target for HPH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46809-y.Keywords: HPH, PASMCs proliferation, Midzone division, PAK5, Drp1Subject terms: Cancer, Cell biology, Diseases, Molecular biology

Introduction

Hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH) is an ongoing vascular disease characterized by excessive remodeling of the pulmonary vasculature and collagen deposition, culminating in right heart failure and high mortality rates1,2. Pathologically, pulmonary vascular remodeling, a hallmark of HPH, is driven by the hyperplasia and apoptosis resistance of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs). However, the exact mechanisms underlying excessive proliferation and apoptosis resistance of PASMCs remain unclear. Therefore, it is urgent to investigate the mechanisms underlying hypoxia-induced biological changes in PASMCs that contribute to the pathogenesis of HPH.

Mitochondria, renowned as “the powerhouse of the cell”, are increasingly recognized for their unique importance in vascular homeostasis and the pulmonary circulation system disease3. On the one hand, mitochondria in PASMCs act as oxygen sensors in the pulmonary circulation4,5. On the other hand, metabolic dysregulation within mitochondria contributes to disease pathophysiology by promoting excessive proliferation and resistance to apoptosis in vascular cells6. Normally, mitochondria exist in the form of filamentous networks, but they are fragmented in HPH, which is an emerging hallmark of the condition that reflects an imbalance in mitochondrial fission/fusion (favoring fission). This imbalance is largely driven by the activation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), mediated by its post-translational modifications7, upregulation of its binding partners8, and downregulation of mitofusin 1 (Mfn1)9. In HPH, the rate of mitochondrial fission is accelerated, which facilitates rapid cell cycle progression and enhanced resistance to apoptosis7,10. However, the upstream signaling mechanisms regulating this increase in fission are not yet fully understood.

p21-activated kinase 5 (PAK5), the most recently identified member of the PAK II subfamily, plays a crucial role in cell survival and cytoskeletal stability11. Accumulating evidence indicates that PAK5 acts as an oncoprotein and is expressed in various pathological tissues, including lung cancer, breast cancer, and liver cancer, etc12–14. It has been reported that PAK5 regulates cardiovascular diseases by modulating the β-catenin signaling pathway15. Furthermore, PAK5 is primarily involved in mitochondria-related functions. Research has shown that knocking out PAK5 inhibits mitochondrial membrane permeability and mitochondrial membrane potential, thereby slowing down the pathologic process16. Conversely, overexpressing PAK5 enhances camptothecin-induced apoptosis and initiates caspase-8 and effector PARP cleavage17. However, the precise role of PAK5 in HPH remains unclear.

Nowadays, PAK5 remains largely unexplored and poorly defined in cardiovascular diseases, particularly PH. Notably, PH shares key pathological hallmarks with malignancies, including aberrant cellular proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and metabolic reprogramming18. Our preliminary findings reveal significant PAK5 upregulation in the remodeled pulmonary arterial medial layer of both rodent models of HPH and human PH specimens (Fig. S1). Given PAK5’s mitochondrial predilection and HPH’s core characteristic, we hypothesize that PAK5 drives HPH progression by accelerating mitochondrial fission-dependent PASMC hyperproliferation. In this translational study aims to: (1) determine the PAK5 expression patterns in clinical PH cohorts; (2) validate its pathological role through genetic manipulations in HPH models; (3) elucidate PAK5-mediated mitochondrial fission mechanisms in PASMC proliferation. This research seeks to clarify the role and mechanism of PAK5 in the progression of HPH, while providing new therapeutic targets.

Results

Hypoxia induced PAK5 upregulation in vivo/vitro

In order to investigate the potential effect of PAK5 in HPH, we first established the HPH rat model as previous paper, and its vascular remodeling and hemodynamic indexes were measured. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stains results showed that after 28 days of hypoxia treatment, the media of pulmonary blood vessels in rats was significantly thickened (Fig. S1A). Meanwhile, hypoxia induced the increasing of the ratio right ventricle/(left ventricle plus septum) (RV/(LV + Sep); Fig. S1B), right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP; Fig. S1C) and mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP; Fig. S1D ). All these indicate that the HPH model was successfully constructed. To gather further evidence, lung tissues from interstitial lung disease (ILD, control patients, ILD-non-PH and PH patients, ILD-PH) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, control patients, COPD-non-PH and PH patients, COPD-PH) were collected for histological examination. H&E results showed significant thickening of the pulmonary vascular media in ILD-PH patients and COPD-PH patients compared with non-PH patients (Fig. S1E).

Also, we observed the changes of PAK5 expression in animal model of hypoxia. Western blot and qPCR results showed that both protein and mRNA expression levels of PAK5 were significantly increased in hypoxic lung tissues compared to the normoxic group (Fig. 1A, B). To visualize this phenotype change, immunohistochemical analysis were performed (Fig. 1C). The positive results of PAK5 mainly appeared in the mesial layer of pulmonary blood vessels undergoing hypoxia treatment. Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that PAK5 expression is significantly elevated in α-SMA positive area (specific marker of PASMCs ), indicating that PAK5 was mainly expressed in the smooth muscle cells of pulmonary blood vessels (Fig. 1D). And it was observed in Fig. 1C and D that the expression of PAK5 was significantly up-regulated after hypoxia treatment. Positive results for PAK5 in the pulmonary vascular media were significantly increased in both PH patients with ILD and COPD (Fig. 1E).

To obtain more solid evidence, we used PASMCs to explore PAK5 and HIF-1α expression at the posttranslational levels in the hypoxic environment (Fig. 2A). The results showed that the expressions of PAK5 and HIF-1α both significantly increased under hypoxic stress. And the mRNA level of PAK5 in PASMCs was also markedly elevated after 48 h of hypoxia stimulation (Fig. 2B). Subsequently, the immunofluorescence analysis was performed to observe the distribution of PAK5, revealing co-localization PAK5 with mitochondrial outer membrane protein Tom20 could be colocalized. Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) was significantly greater than 0.7 in hypoxia. This indicated that PAK5 could localize to mitochondria under hypoxia conditions and its function was closely related to this organelle (Fig. 2C). Also, mitochondrial dysfunction was verified in PASMCs by immunofluorescence analysis. Mitochondrial morphology analysis by mean network size (degree of mitochondrial fission) and mean branch length were quantified measurements. As shown in Fig. 2D, hypoxia exposure was prone to mitochondrial fragmentation shortening. Moreover, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that hypoxic-treated PASMCs showed phenomenon of intermediate folding, which was called midzone division of mitochondria (Fig. 2E). These data indicated that PAK5 deregulation may be able to prevent the pathological process of HPH, aberrant PASMCs proliferation and mitochondrial fission may have synergistic effect on vasculopathy.

PAK5 mediates hypoxia-induced proliferation of PASMCs in vitro and in vivo

The hallmark pathological change of HPH is vascular remodeling, is primarily driven by the persistent proliferation of PASMCs19. Therefore, it’s necessary to investigate the effect of PAK5 on the proliferation of PASMCs. The proliferative capacity of PASMCs under hypoxic stimulation was detected by EdU assay (the transfection efficiency of PAK5 shRNA and the effect of PAK5 inhibitor were shown in supplemental materials: Fig. S2B, C). Compared with the normoxia group, the red fluorescence intensity of EdU–DNA complexes was significantly enhanced in the hypoxia group, whereas it gradually decreased in PASMCs with silenced or inhibited PAK5 (Fig. 3A). Proliferating cell nuclear antigen (PCNA) and cell cycle regulatory proteins (Cyclin A and Cyclin D) were determined by western blot (Fig. 3B). Multiple lines of evidence showed that once PAK5 was silenced or inhibited, the proliferation-related proteins showed a significant decrease trend. In addition, we examined the effect of PAK5 overexpression on proliferation-related indicators under normoxic conditions (the transfection efficiency of overexpressed PAK5 shRNA was shown in supplemental materials: Fig. S2D). As shown in Fig. 3C and D, overexpressed PAK5 in normoxic PASMCs promoted abnormal cell proliferation, exhibiting a phenotypic trend similar to that of PASMCs stimulated by hypoxia. Finally, we employed the TUNEL assay to evaluate apoptosis in PASMCs under hypoxic conditions (Fig. 3E). The results revealed a noticeable decrease in apoptotic cells in the hypoxic group compared to the normoxic group, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. And a significant increase in apoptosis was observed in the shPAK5 group and GNE group compared with the hypoxia group.

To further investigate the role of PAK5 on pulmonary vascular remodeling in vivo, we constructed an adeno-associated virus vector 6 (AAV6) to silence PAK5 and exposed it to a hypoxic environment to establish a mouse model of HPH using this vector. The silencing expression efficiency of PAK5 in lung tissue is shown in Figure S2E. Pulmonary arterioles from each group were isolated and subjected H&E staining. The results revealed significant medial thickening of the small pulmonary arteries in the hypoxia group, which was effectively attenuated by AAV6-PAK5 (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, C and D, after four weeks of hypoxia stimulation, the RV/LV + S, RVSP and mPAP in the hypoxia group were significantly higher than the normoxia group, further confirming the successful establishment of the HPH model. Compared with the hypoxia group, silencing PAK5 significantly reduced RV/LV + S, RVSP and mPAP. Subsequently, we used α-SMA, PCNA and PAK5 antibodies to further observe the analysis of pulmonary vascular morphology. As clearly shown in Fig. 4E, silencing PAK5 significantly alleviated the medial thickening of the pulmonary vessels marked by α-SMA. The PCNA-positive results also confirmed the above results in Fig. 4F. These findings indicated that PAK5 promoted the progression of HPH, while its knockdown effectively mitigated the disease process.

Hypoxia-activated midzone division mediated by PAK5 through Drp1 in PASMCs

Although it’s clear that PAK5 played its function and was closely related to mitochondria, the specific mechanism of how PAK5 stimulated cell proliferation in hypoxia remained unclear. Early reports suggested that mitochondrial fission mainly regulated proliferative phenotype of PASMCs. To investigate the regulatory role of PAK5 in mitochondrial fission, the mitochondrial morphology of PASMCs was firstly observed by immunofluorescence using specific antibodies to mitochondrial outer membrane proteins Tom20. The results showed that mitochondria in the normoxia group were mostly elongated and in network shape, while those in the hypoxic group were mostly in short rod shape or fragment shape, suggesting the occurrence of mitochondrial fission (Fig. 5A). Under normoxic conditions, overexpression of PAK5 also affects mitochondrial morphology (Fig. S3A). Transmission electron microscopy further revealed that hypoxia-induced mitochondrial midzone division was effectively suppressed after silencing or inhibiting PAK5 (Fig. 5B). Subsequently, Drp1-related proteins were measured. Figure 5C showed that the expression of mitochondrial Drp1 (mito-Drp1) was significantly increased in the hypoxic group, while the relative expression level of Drp1 in the cytoplasm (cyto-Drp1) was significantly decreased. This phenomenon was reversed in the context of silencing or suppressing PAK5. Simultaneously, overexpression of PAK5 reproduced the aforementioned phenomenon observed in hypoxia-treated PASMCs (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, fluorescence co-localization analysis of Drp1 and Tom20 demonstrated that Drp1 co-localized with Tom20 under normoxic conditions when PAK5 was overexpressed, consistent with the Western blot results mentioned above (Fig. S3B, C). To further investigate the relationship between PAK5 and Drp1, fluorescence assays were performed for co-localization analysis. The results revealed a high degree of co-localization between PAK5 and Drp1 under hypoxic stimulation. Conversely, inhibition of PAK5 expression led to a corresponding reduction in their co-localization (Fig. 5E, F). At the same time, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) was performed using antibodies corresponding to PAK5 and Drp1 (Fig. 5G). The results of Fig. 5G elucidated that there was a one-way direct binding between PAK5 and Drp1, and PAK5 was directly combined with Drp1. Based on these findings, we inferred that PAK5 may mediate mitochondrial fission by influencing Drp1 expression.

Midzone division is a newly discovered mode of mitochondrial fission, primarily regulated by Drp1-Mff and Drp1-Fis1 pathways, both of which are involved in modulating distinct cellular fate processes20. To further elucidate the mode of mitochondrial fission, we examined proteins involved in the regulation of mitochondrial fission. As shown in Fig. 6A, silenced PAK5 reversed the increased expression of Drp1-related mitochondrial fission receptor Mff associated with hypoxia, while no significant changes were observed in Fis1, Mid49, or Mid51 following either hypoxia treatment or PAK5 inhibition. Notably, compared to the hypoxia group, mitochondrial fusion protein Mfn1 content was significantly upregulated after PAK5 inhibition, indicating that silencing PAK5 promoted protective mitochondrial fusion (Fig. 6A). The result related to mitochondrial fission was consistent with Figure. S3D. It could be seen from Figure. S3C that once overexpressed PAK5, the relative expression of Mff and Mfn1 would show similar results with those observed under hypoxia stimulation. These results demonstrated that PAK5 regulated mitochondrial fission via the Drp1-Mff mediated midzone division.

In order to clarify the upstream-downstream regulatory relationship between PAK5 and Drp1, we added Drp1 inhibitor (Mdivi-1) to PAK5-overexpressed PASMCs, thereby inhibiting the mitochondrial fission-related behaviors. The protein assays showed that the addition of Mdivi-1 significantly reduced the expression level of Drp1, but has no effect on PAK5 even if the expression of PAK5 was expanded. Drp1 mediates the amplification of mitochondrial fission signaling through phosphorylation at its Ser616 residue21. As shown in the Fig. 6B, the expression level of phospho-Drp1 (p-Drp1, Ser616) was effectively suppressed following the administration of the Mdivi-1. Meanwhile, Mdivi-1 also increased the expression of Mfn1. The results further confirmed that PAK5 activated the mitochondrial midzone division regulated by Drp1-related midzone division.

PAK5 regulation of PASMC proliferation via Drp1-mediated mitochondrial midzone division

To conclude, to investigate the effect of PAK5/Drp1/Mff on PASMCs, we further investigated the effects of inhibiting mitochondrial midzone division on PASMCs. By labeling the outer mitochondrial membrane with Tom20, we found that the enhanced mitochondrial fission and mitochondrial network extension caused by PAK5 overexpression were weakened after disrupting mitochondrial fission (Fig. 7A). Meanwhile, it was clearly observed in Fig. 7B that overexpression of PAK5 was promoted the occurrence of midzone division. After adding the Drp1 inhibitor, the midzone division decreased. In addition, fluorescence experiments showed that inhibition of Drp1-Mff regulated mitochondrial midzone division, and the co-localization signal of Drp1 and Tom20 significantly decreased (Fig. 7C). Fluorescence measurements further indicated that inhibiting mitochondrial midzone division could down-regulate the co-localization of PAK5 and Drp1 (Fig. 7D). Subsequently, the aberrant proliferation function of PASMCs was measured. The results clearly showed that as the process of mitochondrial midzone division was attenuated, the promoting effect of PAK5 overexpression on proliferation-related proteins significantly weakened, and the fluorescence intensity of the EdU-DNA complex also significantly decreased (Fig. 8A, B). These results clearly confirmed that PAK5 affected the proliferation of PASMCs through the Drp1/ Mff-mediated mitochondrial midzone division pathway.

Discussion

To date, only one study by Georges et al. based on targeted interaction network analysis, has suggested PAK5 as a potential therapeutic target for HPH22. However, little is known about the role of PAK5 in HPH. In this study, we found that hypoxia leaded to a significant increase of PAK5 in the membrane layer of pulmonary vessels, causing excessive proliferation of PASMCs and eventually resulting in a certain degree of vascular remodeling. This process required the activation of Drp1, which induced the binding with Mff and reduced the level of Mfn1, thereby promoting the occurrence of mitochondrial midzone division and accelerating the expression of proliferative proteins. These results enhance our understanding of the molecular mechanism by which PAK5 promotes the occurrence and development of HPH.

The PAK protein family comprises six members and is divided into two classes (Class I: PAK1, 2, 3; Class II: PAK4, 5, 6). As a member of the PAK II subfamily, PAK5 is the most recently identified member of this family23. Accumulating evidence has indicated that PAK5 is involved in the pathological and physiological processes of cancer24. Although it has been extensively studied, its precise function has not yet been fully elucidated. The pathophysiology of HPH involves persistent pulmonary vasculopathy, endothelial cells (ECs) injury, abnormal proliferation of PASMCs, vascular interstitial fibrosis, remodeling, and inflammation25–27. In this study, we investigated the mechanism by which PAK5 regulated HPH through clinical samples and both in vivo and in vitro experiments. For the first time, we discovered that PAK5 is significantly increased in the medial layer of small pulmonary arteries (PAs) in patients with respiratory-related pulmonary hypertension and HPH rodent models. Notably, PAK5 expression is markedly upregulated in various malignant tumors, where it mediates the cell cycle and regulates cell proliferation28,29, suggesting a positive correlation between PAK5 and cell proliferation. Therefore, our current finding is consistent with previous research results. Additionally, during the experimental process, pentobarbital was used as an anesthetic in animal experiments. Although it may influence RVSP measurements in a way, the same dosage and administration method were used in each experiment to minimize this interference as much as possible.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is considered a key pathological feature of HPH30. It has been reported that PAK5 is predominantly localized in mitochondria. PAK5 directly phosphorylates BAD at Serine 112, retaining it in the cytoplasm and preventing the release of cytochrome c from mitochondria, thereby mediating an anti-apoptotic mechanism31. This seemingly elusive link between PAK5, mitochondria, and HPH led us to reasonably hypothesize that PAK5 might regulate HPH through mitochondrial pathways. Our results were consistent with this hypothesis; silencing or inhibiting PAK5 expression significantly alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction. Furthermore, under pathological conditions such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage triggers dynamic fission processes, which in turn impair tissue and organ development32. Mitochondrial fission, characterized by mitochondrial fragmentation, has been identified as a hallmark of PH and represents a potential therapeutic target for HPH33. Drp1 is the main regulator of mitochondrial fission, it requires interaction with some mitochondrial fission receptors on the mitochondrial membrane, including Mff, Fis1, Mid49, and Mid5135,36. However, in our study, we found that upon PAK5 knockdown, the Drp1-associated mitochondrial fission receptors did not change, except for Mff, which showed a synchronous alteration. This further indicated that PAK5 triggered Drp1-Mff-mediated mitochondrial midzone division. Recently, Rajarshi et al. provided a detailed analysis of mitochondrial fission patterns from different perspectives and thoroughly examined the role of mitochondrial fission in cell proliferation/apoptosis34. They pointed out that Drp1-Mff-mediated mitochondrial fission, termed midzone division, regulates cell proliferation35; whereas Drp1-Fis1-mediated mitochondrial fission, termed peripheral division, regulates cell apoptosis. This aligns with our experimental results and further clarifies the involvement of mitochondrial midzone fission in regulating the pathological progression of HPH. While Lian et al. have reported that Drp1-Fis1 could directly act on monocrotaline-induced pulmonary hypertension (PAH)36, Zhou et al. Fis1 showed no significant change after hypoxia treatment, instead implicating its SUMOylation in the pathogenesis of HPH37. The underlying reasons for this discrepancy require further elucidation in future research.

In summary, our study provided compelling evidence that PAK5 was a central player in the pathophysiology of HPH, mediating mitochondrial fission and excessive proliferation of PASMCs through the Drp1/Mff-dependent midzone division pathway. Our study suggested that PAK5 may be necessary for the occurrence of developmental processes in mitochondrial biology, ameliorating right heart injury and pulmonary vascular remodeling. These findings not only enhanced our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying HPH but also proposed PAK5 as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating the pathological remodeling associated with this condition. Future studies aimed at delineating the specific signaling pathways involved in PAK5 regulation would be essential to further validate its role in HPH and could pave the way for innovative therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and antibodies

Antibodies against Tom20, Mff, HIF-1α (Catalog numbers 42406, 84580, 36169) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Boston, MA, USA). Antibodies against PCNA, GAPDH, α-SMA, CyclinA, CyclinD, Mid49 and Mfn1 (Catalog numbers 10205-2-AP, 60004-1-Ig, 67735-1-Ig, 18202-1-AP, 26939-1-AP, 28718-1-AP,13798-1-AP) were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China). Antibodies against PAK5, p-Mff, Drp1, p-Drp1, Mid51 and Fis1 (Catalog numbers YT3573, YP1403, YT1414, YM8563, YT6538, YN5292) were purchased from ImmunoWay Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Midiv-1 and GNE 2861 (GNE) were obtained from MedChemExpress (NJ, USA).

Animals

The rats (Sprague-Dawley, male, 250–300 g) and mice (C57BL/6, male, 6–9 weeks old) were purchased from the Chengdu Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. All animal procedures were approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the Medical Department of Northwestern University and were conducted in accordance with the ethical guidelines for the use of experimental animals. After the completion of in vivo experiments (4 weeks of hypoxia exposure), mice were euthanized humanely in accordance with the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020). Euthanasia was performed by intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of 3% pentobarbital sodium (dosage: 150 mg/kg body weight). The rat/mice were closely monitored until the absence of vital signs (including cessation of breathing, loss of corneal reflex, and no heartbeat) was confirmed, ensuring a painless and humane end.

SD rats were kept in a house with a temperature of 21–25℃ and a relative humidity of 55–70%, and the light/dark cycle was maintained for 12 h/12 h. The HPH model was constructed by exposing SD rats to a low-pressure anoxic chamber (10% O2) for 28 days, while the control group was supplied with normal indoor oxygen for 28 days. At the same time, the pod was opened for 30 min every day to supplement water and food for SD rats to maintain normal physiological function.

In order to achieve specific gene knockout of vascular smooth muscle cells in lung tissue in vivo, a smooth muscle cell-specific gene transduction system was adopted, using an AAV6-mediated gene delivery system and operated under the control of a vascular smooth muscle cell-selective SM22α promoter. (HanBio Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). For lung tissue vascular PASMCs-selective PAK5 knockdown, AAV6-SM22α-ZsGreen-mir30-shPak5 particles with SM22α promoter were generated (The core advantage of AAV6 lies in its high transduction efficiency to the lungs, while maintaining a relatively low immunogenicity). The specific steps are as follows: The mouse was immobilized in a supine position on an operating board, with its neck moderately extended to expose the trachea. The tongue was gently pulled out using forceps, and a cold light source endoscope was used to illuminate the oral cavity. At the moment the glottis opened, a flexible intravenous catheter (with the needle core removed) was gently inserted into the trachea, after which the liquid was slowly injected into the mouse’s trachea. The total injection volume is 50µL (virus titer > 1.9*1012 vg/mL). Each experimental mice, weighing approximately 25 g, was randomly divided into the normoxia group (Nor, 6 mice), the hypoxia group + AAV6-negative shRNA group (Hyp + Nc, 6 mice), and the hypoxia group + AAV6-shPAK5 group (Hyp+PAK5, 6 mice). After one week adaptation period, the AAV6 vector drug was intratracheally instilled into each mouse in the experimental group. Three weeks later, the mice began to undergo modeling in both normoxia and hypoxia environments. After 4 weeks of hypoxia exposure, the hypoxia model was completed. The surgical operation was performed under intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital anesthesia and with efforts to minimize pain.

Clinical specimens

Human samples were obtained with the Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital Clinical Research Center, and all human studies were approved in accordance with the guidelines of the Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital Ethical Review Committee (Approval number: L20-211) and carried out in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were given informed consent before the study began. The diagnosis of PH was confirmed by echocardiography and right heart catheterization. Detailed characteristics of non-pH donors and PH patients are shown in Table S1. The collected lung tissue samples were fixed using formalin and subsequently stained and immunohistochemically for histological examination.

Right ventricular systolic pressure (RVSP) measurement

The RVSP of rats in each group was measured after four weeks of continuous treatment with low-pressure oxygen chamber. First, the rats were intraperitoneally injected with pentobarbital sodium anesthetic and fixed on the endotherm fixation plate in the supine position. After disinfection, the right external jugular vein was dissociated, the distal end was lapped and the proximal end was blocked with an artery clamp. The cannula was connected and connected to a pressure transducer and a tuned PowerLab signal acquisition system. The tube is inserted into the blood vessel and slowly advanced to the right ventricle while observing the waveform of the signal acquisition system. When the waveform with blunt tip and large amplitude appears, indicating that the intubation has penetrated into the right ventricle, the intubation can be fixed and the waveform can be recorded steadily for 5 min, in order to obtain continuous records of RVSP.

Ventricular hypertrophy index measurement

After performing RVSP, the heart was dissected. The right ventricular tissue was cut along the margin of the interventricular septum, and the right ventricle (RV), the left ventricle and its septum tissue (LV + S) were weighed respectively. The ratio of RV/ LV + S was the right heart hypertrophy index.

Histology and immunohistochemistry on tissue

Fresh lung tissues were f paraffin-embedded and sliced. Next, lung tissue was sliced thinly (5 μm) and stained with H&E for vascular morphometry analysis. For immunohistochemistry, after deparaffinization and dehydration, lung sections were treated with 3% H2O2 for 30 min, and were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Then, the slices were covered with primary antibodies, i.e. anti-PAK5, anti-α-smooth muscle (α-SMA) actin, anti-Drp-1, anti-PCNA overnight at 4℃ and subsequently incubated with the secondary antibodies (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, the slices were stained by 3,3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit (Proteintech, PR30010) and hematoxylin. Brown and yellow colors indicated positive stains, which was evaluated by Image Pro-Plus Software (BioTek Synergy HTX, USA).

PASMCs isolation and culture

Primary PASMCs were isolated from adult male SD rats using improved enzymatic hydrolysis38. In summary, under stereomicroscopy, the pulmonary arterioles are detached from the lung lobes. After the vascular outer membrane was peeled off and the vascular endothelium was destroyed, the vessels were sheared (1–2 mm) and settled in Hank’s Worthington Biochemical Corporation containing 0.1% collagenase for elimination into single cells at 37℃ for 1 h. Subsequently, the cell suspension was transferred to a centrifuge tube containing fetal bovine serum. PASMCs were collected, centrifuged 200 g for 5 min and re-suspended in DMEM medium containing 15% fetal bovine serum. A week later, PASMCs were observed under microscope, and the experiment was carried out with PASMCs of 2–5 generations. PASMCs were maintained in a DMEM culture medium (Hyclone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco). Cells were cultured under either normoxic conditions (37℃, 21% O2, 5% CO2) or hypoxic conditions (37℃, 5% O2, 5% CO2). PASMCs were identified using α-SMA antibodies (Fig. S1A).

shRNA design and transfection

For silence PAK5, PASMCs were transduced with lentiviral particles at the befitting density, which were designed and synthesized by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). The vector alone was deemed to be a negative control (Hyp + WT). After transduction for 72 h, PASMCs were used for subsequent experimental processing. PAK5 overexpression vector (adPAK5) is designed and synthesized by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China). The adPAK5 vector alone was used as a negative control (Nora). After transduction for 24 h, PASMCs were used as required follow-up experimental treatment.

Western blotting analysis

Lung tissue and cells were collected and broken by sonication, containing 1% protease inhibitor on ice. All of the harvested lysis buffer was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4℃, the supernatant was collected and stored in -80℃ for western blotting experiment. Protein extracts (30 µg) were separated using 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. After blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk, the membranes were incubated with the specific antibodies at 4 °C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-PAK5 (1:1000), anti-Drp1 (1:1000), anti-Mff (1:2000), anti-p-Mff (1:1000), anti-Mfn1 (1:1000), anti-PCNA (1:1000), anti-Cyclin A (1:1000), anti-Cyclin D (1:1000), and anti-GAPDH (1:4000).

Immunofluorescence

PASMCs was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. 0.05% Triton X-100 were permeated the slices at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin at room temperature for 1 h, and then incubated overnight with antibodies against PAK5 (1:50), Drp1 (1:300) and Tom20 (1:400) at 4℃. The cells were washed with PBS 3 times and incubated with red/green coupled secondary antibody (1:500) at room temperature for 1 h away from light. After that, the tablets were cleaned 3 times with PBS and sealed with an anti-fluorescence quencher containing DAPI. Images were taken using confocal laser scanning microscope (AF6000; Leica).

For mitochondrial morphological analysis, Mean Network Size: This refers to the number of clusters “clusters” (connected components) that make up the entire network, as well as the average size of these clusters. It is a measure of the overall connectivity of the network. Mean Branch Length: This focuses on the average distance within a single mitochondrial network, from one “node” (branch point) to another node, or from one node to the end of the network. It is a measure of the extent of individual extension of the network. When the mean branch length decreases, it can be highly suspected that the mitochondrial fission activity has intensified. If both decrease simultaneously, it strongly suggests that the mitochondrial network is undergoing a fragmentation process caused by excessive division.

Co-immunoprecipitation

PASMCs was collected and purified with cold PBS. IP lysis buffer with PMSF (1:100) was added to cell culture dish and the cells were collected. Cell lysate was collected and slowly shaken for 30 min on a shaking table at 4℃. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm at 4℃ for 20 min to collect the supernatant, add 3 µg target antibody or IgG, incubate at 4℃ for 2 h, and add protein A/G agarose beads overnight at 4 °C on a rocking platform. On the second day, the antibody-protein complex was washed with PBS, then the complex was collected, resuspended in the 1 × loading buffer, and western blotting was performed on the sample.

EdU incorporation assay

PASMCs were treated with 50µM 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU, C10310, RIBOBIO), and incubated in complete medium at 37 °C for 2 h without light. PASMCs were soaked in 4% formaldehyde for 20 min. And PASMCs were incubated with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 5 min, which were performed as described manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were purified with cold PBS three times, then apollo and nuclear staining were performed. Edu-positive PASMCs were evaluated under fluorescence microscopy (Olympus FLUOVIEW FV 1000). Fluorescence intensity was quantified by Image J software.

TUNEL assay

PASMCs was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. 0.3% Triton X-100 were permeated the slices at room temperature for 5 min. Following the manufacturer’s instructions, the TUNEL (TdT-mediated dUTP Nick-End Labeling, C1089, Beyotime) reaction mixture was prepared and applied to the cell samples at the recommended volume. The samples were then protected from light and incubated at 37 °C for 60 min.

RNA isolation and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from the lung tissue and cells using Trizol® reagent (Accurate biology, CHN). Next, the extracted RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA according to reverse transcription procedures as specified by the manufacturer described protocol. The quantitative expression of 1 µl cDNA was evaluated by one-step RT-PCR kit using SYBR Green (Accurate biology, CHN) as fluorescent marker. GAPDH was chosen as an internal control. The sequences of PCR primers were described as follows:

PAK5-F (Rat): ACACCCATACAACTGGCTCC;

R: TTCTTCGGAGTGGCCTTGAA.

GAPDH-F (Rat): GACATGCCGCCTGGAGAAAC.

R: AGCCCAGGATGCCCTTTAGT.

Cell counting kit 8 (CCK8) assay

PASMCs were inoculated in 96-well plates and cultured in hypoxic environment for 48 h. Then, 5 mg/mL CCK8 reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) were added and incubated for 2 h at 37 °C following the manufacturer’s instructions. Finally, the absorbance at 450 nm was detected by a microplate reader (Bioteke, Beijing, China).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

PASMCs samples were collected under normal and hypoxia conditions for TEM structure analysis. Cell samples were fixed at 4 °C with 2% glutaraldehyde in a 0.1 mol/L sodium cacodylate buffer and postfixed for 1 h on ice with 1% osmium tetroxide. After sectioning, the staining was observed under Hitachi-7800 electron microscope (Japan).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data are presented as means ± SD from ≥ 3 independent biological replicates. Normality and variance homogeneity were assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For normally distributed data with homogeneous variance: parametric tests were applied (two-tailed Student’s t-test for two-group comparisons; one-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test for ≥ 3 groups). For non-normally distributed data or heterogeneous variance: non-parametric tests were applied (Mann-Whitney U test for two-group comparisons; Kruskal-Wallis H test with Dunn’s post-hoc test for ≥ 3 groups). A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
