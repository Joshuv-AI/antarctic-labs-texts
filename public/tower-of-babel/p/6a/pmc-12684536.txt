Outcomes Analysis of Transcatheter Closure of Atrial Septal Defects
Authors: Zakhia S Saliba, Lea Bou Karam, Nelly Sleiman, Linda Daou, Raymond N Haddad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAtrial septal defect device closure is routine, yet clinical success and long‐term outcomes are often overlooked. We aim to identify predictive factors for failure and describe long‐term outcomes.MethodsA single‐center retrospective review (2006–2021) of 400 patients undergoing transcatheter atrial septal defect closure was conducted. Primary end point was 6‐month composite clinical success, including technical success (implantation and device retention), closure success (trivial/no residual shunt), absence of 30‐day serious adverse events, and no device removal/reintervention. Secondary end points were technical success, procedure success (technical success with less than moderate residual shunt), closure success (clinically insignificant/absent residual shunt), and safety.ResultsThe cohort included 43.2% males, with a median age of 14.5 years [Q1–Q3: 8.3–37] and weight of 47.5 kg [25–68]. At baseline, the median stop‐flow defect diameter was 24 mm [18.9–29]; 22.3% had a diameter ≥30 mm, 4.9% had a diameter‐to‐weight ratio of 1.5–1.8, 17.7% were multi‐fenestrated, 39.3% had margin deficiency, and 10.7% had an atrial septal aneurysm. A single device was used in 98.8% of procedures (75.7% Amplatzer, 23.0% Occlutech). Median difference between device size and balloon‐sizing diameter was 1 mm [0–2]. Implantations were pulmonary vein–assisted in 26.8%, guidewire‐assisted in 2.5%, and balloon‐assisted in 3.8%. Technical success at hospital discharge was 95.8%, and procedural success was 94.8%. Closure success was 98.7% (377/382) at 6 months and 98.9% (193/194) at 96 months. Freedom from SAEs was 95.5% at 30 days. Freedom from device events was 97.4% at 6 months. Six‐month composite clinical success rate was 89.3%. Balloon‐assisted implantation (odds ratio [OR], 4.494, [95% CI 1.471–13.729]), guidewire‐assisted implantation (OR 6.559 [95% CI 1.759–24.454]), and smaller device‐to‐balloon sizing difference (OR 0.8000 [95% CI 0.663–0.967]) were independent predictors of failure.ConclusionsTranscatheter atrial septal defect closure shows high success, with balloon‐ and guidewire‐assisted implantations and smaller device‐to‐balloon‐sizing differences as predictors of clinical failure.Keywords: atrial septal defect, complications, device closure, outcomes, risk factorsSubject Categories: Congenital Heart Disease, Treatment, Quality and Outcomes, Complications, Cardiovascular Disease

Since the introduction of the King‐Mills device in 1976, technological advancements have made transcatheter device closure the preferred method over surgical repair for secundum atrial septal defects (ASDs) in both children and adults. 1 , 2 , 3 The Amplatzer septal occluder (ASO) and similar nitinol‐based devices have initially been favored for suitable anatomies. 4 Various implantation techniques and procedural maneuvers have since been developed to expand percutaneous treatment options for a wider range of defect types. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Although transcatheter closure is typically safe and effective, procedural failures and complications still occur, with patient‐ and anatomy‐related risk factors remaining debated and underexplored. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Flexible delivery systems 15 , 16 and, more recently, softer, or bioresorbable device designs 17 , 18 have been introduced to reduce the risk of adverse events. However, the debate on their effectiveness continues in the literature. 19 , 20 This study builds on existing knowledge to identify predictive factors for procedural failure and serious adverse events (SAEs), as well as to assess long‐term follow‐up outcomes.

METHODS

The raw data supporting the conclusions of this article will be available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request, subject to approval by all coauthors, to any qualified researcher.

Study Design

We retrospectively reviewed clinical data from all consecutive pediatric and adult patients with hemodynamically significant ASDs, regardless of age or gender, who were sent for transcatheter device closure at Hôtel‐Dieu de France University Medical Center between 2006 and 2021. No patients were excluded at the time of data collection. Patient demographics and clinical data from baseline to the latest follow‐up were collected. Following anonymous data collection, one author (R.N.H.) had exclusive access to the merged data set, ensuring its integrity and overseeing the analysis. The data were analyzed comprehensively, with a focus on standard safety and outcomes.

All procedures followed institutional guidelines and adhered to the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. The study was approved by an institutional review committee, and the subjects or their legal guardians gave informed consent. The article adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. No sex‐, race‐, or ethnicity‐based differences were present.

Study Purpose

This study aims to identify the predictive factors for procedure failure and SAEs and to describe long‐term follow‐up outcomes.

Patient Selection and Preprocedure Ultrasound Evaluation

Details of the procedural planning are provided in Data S1, Patient Selection and preprocedure ultrasound evaluation.

Anatomically challenging defects were defined as (1) extra‐large defects (diameter ≥30 mm or ASD diameter/patient weight ratio ≥1.5); (2) large defects (≥20 mm or ratio ≥1.2 to ≤1.4) with absent or deficient (<5 mm) anterosuperior (aortic, retro‐aortic) margin; (3) defects with deficient (<5 mm) and or floppy inferior (posteroinferior) or superior (posterosuperior) caval margins; and (4) multifenestrated defects with mobile or aneurysmal septal tissue extending to any margin.

Exclusion criteria for transcatheter closure included (1) defect diameter >40 mm by stop‐flow balloon sizing; (2) absent inferior or superior caval margin; (3) the presence of multiple congenital anomalies or significant cardiac valve or coronary abnormalities requiring surgical intervention; (4) severe pulmonary arterial hypertension with systolic pulmonary artery to systolic systemic artery pressure and resistance ratios of more than two thirds or a net right‐to‐left shunt; (5) pregnancy; (6) debilitating stroke; (7) active infection; (8) uncontrolled arrhythmia; (9) contraindications to acetylsalicylic acid or heparin; and (10) body weight ≤12 kg.

Study Procedure

Details of the procedure are provided in Data S1, Study procedure. The intervention was considered complex when advanced technical maneuvers had to be applied to achieve successful closure, including deployment of the left atrial disk within the left or right upper pulmonary vein, the use of modified or steerable delivery sheaths, balloon‐assisted, snare‐assisted, or guidewire‐assisted implantations.

Data Collection

Data collection encompassed baseline patients’ demographics, clinical presentations, defect anatomical characteristics and associated anomalies, procedural and technical data, adverse events, device deficiencies, and follow‐up outcomes.

Outcome Measures

Subjects were considered enrolled when the device was introduced into the body. The primary study end point was a 6‐month composite nonhierarchical clinical success, including technical success (device successfully deployed and retained at procedure end), closure success (complete occlusion or trivial shunt through 6 months), no SAEs through 30 days, and no device events (embolization, erosion, removal, or reintervention) through 6 months. Patients who underwent surgical or snare‐mediated device retrieval, whether elective or emergent, due to device embolization or SAEs, were classified as having experienced technical failure.

Secondary end points included technical success (device successfully deployed and retained postindex procedure), procedure success (achieving technical success and ≤2 mm residual shunt post procedure), closure success (complete occlusion or trivial shunt) at 6, 36, and 96 months, and safety (freedom from SAEs and device events) at 6 months post procedure.

Residual shunting by ultrasound was categorized as trivial (grade 0 or 1, <1.0 mm), mild (grade 2, 1.1–2.0 mm), moderate (grade 3, 2.1–4.0 mm), or severe (grade 4, >4.0 mm). SAEs were defined as any adverse event related to the procedure or device that resulted in death, life‐threatening situations, hospitalization or prolonged hospital stay, disability, or the need for catheter or surgical interventions.

Follow‐Up

Details of the procedural planning are provided in Data S1, Follow‐up.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS, Version 22.0 for Macintosh (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables are presented as median (first quartile [Q1]–third quartile [Q3]), and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Candidate predictors were initially assessed using univariable logistic regression. Continuous variables were evaluated for linearity, and in cases of collinearity, the most clinically relevant variable was retained. Multivariable models were adjusted for patient weight and balloon‐sized defect diameter, selected as key confounders based on clinical relevance and event distribution. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was applied to reduce bias associated with small sample sizes or rare events. Both unadjusted and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% CIs are reported. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all reported P values are 2 sided.

Procedure

The procedural data are summarized in Table 3. The intervention was performed via the internal jugular vein in 3 patients with inferior vena cava interruption and azygos continuation. Twenty‐seven patients (6.8%) underwent concurrent diagnostic or interventional procedures, including 11 balloon pulmonary valvuloplasties, 3 diagnostic coronary angiograms, 12 ASD balloon occlusion tests (2 of which also involved diagnostic coronary angiograms and 1 included percutaneous coronary intervention), and 1 balloon dilation of aortic coarctation. A single device was used in 98.8% of cases, with the Amplatzer septal occluder used in 72.5%. Advanced technical implantation maneuvers were necessary for 118 patients (29.5%), which included 10 cases using guidewire‐assisted implantation and 15 cases employing balloon‐assisted techniques. Among these patients, 89 had defects measuring 25 mm or larger, 77 presented with deficient rims, 21 were multifenestrated, and 12 had atrial septal aneurysms. The overall median diameter difference between the device and the balloon‐sized defect was 1 mm (0–2).

End Point Analysis

End points are presented in Table 4. Composite clinical success (technical success, closure success, no SAE at 30 days, and no device events through 6 months) was achieved in 89.3% of patients (357/400). Reasons for clinical failure included technical failure in 8 (2%), 30‐day SAEs in 16 (4%), 6‐month device events in 10 (2.5%), and clinically significant residual shunt in 9 (2.2%) at 6 months. Univariable analysis identified several predictive factors for 6‐month composite clinical failure, including larger 2D or balloon‐sized defect diameters, a higher balloon‐sized defect‐to‐weight ratio, technical implantation maneuvers, and a smaller device‐to‐balloon‐sized defect diameter difference (see Table 5). Multivariable analysis confirmed that a smaller diameter difference between the device and balloon‐sized defect (OR, 0.800 [95% CI, 0.663–0.967]), balloon‐assisted implantation (OR, 4.494 [95% CI, 1.471–13.729]), and guidewire‐assisted implantation (OR, 6.559 [95% CI, 1.759–24.454]) were significant predictors of failure.

Technical Success

Transcatheter device closure was attempted in all subjects, with successful implantation in 98% (392/400). Device release was withheld in 1 case due to significant residual shunting, in 6 cases due to inability to achieve stable positioning, and in 1 case due to complete heart block unresponsive to medical therapy. All 8 patients subsequently underwent successful surgical ASD closure.

Procedure Success

At discharge, the procedure was successful in 376 out of 400 patients (94%). Failures were due to technical failure in 8 patients, early device embolization in 9, and residual shunts over 2 mm in 7.

Device Events

In 392 patients with implanted devices, embolization occurred in 9 (2.3%): 6 within 1 hour post wakeup and three discovered the following day. Three devices embolized to the descending aorta and were snare recaptured, followed by surgical ASD closure. Surgical inspection revealed no rim deficiency. Three embolized to the pulmonary artery, 2 to the right atrium, and 1 to the tricuspid valve—all were surgically removed with concurrent defect closure. Surgical inspection revealed rim deficiency: posteroinferior in 3 cases, posterosuperior in 1, and anterosuperior in 1. In a 59‐year‐old patient with a history of large pericardial effusion requiring drainage, the device was surgically explanted 50 days post implant due to concerning symptoms and computed tomography findings indicating recurrent pericardial effusion with peripheral contrast enhancement. However, intraoperative findings revealed a well‐positioned device and an intact pericardium. Overall, freedom from device‐related events was 97.4% (382/392) at 6 months.

Closure Success

Clinical closure success and residual defects are detailed in Table 4. At 6 months, the closure success rate was 98.7% (377/382), with 99.2% (374/377) of patients achieving complete closure, and 0.8% (3/377) exhibited a clinically insignificant leak as confirmed by ultrasound. Five patients (1.3%) had residual defects (2 mild and 3 moderate) and were classified as clinical closure failures. These patients were closely monitored, with no further intervention planned. Closure success was 98.3% (292/297) at 36 months and 98.9% (193/194) at 96 months.

Adverse Events

Eighteen (4.5%) SAEs were reported within the first 30 days (see Table 6). Following device implantation, 5 patients developed complete heart block requiring atropine, which resolved to sinus rhythm after intravenous prednisolone treatment: 1 patient in 1 hour, 2 in 24 hours, and 2 in 48 hours. One patient developed Mobitz type II second‐degree atrioventricular block that spontaneously regressed to first‐degree block and returned to sinus rhythm within 36 hours. Two patients experienced postprocedure supraventricular tachycardia; 1 resolved with adenosine and 1 required cardioversion. Three patients developed moderate pericardial effusion that resolved with observation over 1 week, and 1 large circumferential effusion required pericardiocentesis 11 days post implant. One right femoral pseudoaneurysm required surgical correction 2 weeks post implant, and 1 patient developed grade 2 device‐related mitral valve regurgitation. Device‐related thrombus was observed in 4 patients (1 right‐sided, 3 left‐sided), all treated with 48 hours of intravenous heparin and resolved with 6 months of oral anticoagulation. Freedom from SAE at 30 days was 95.5% (382/400). No erosion, perforation, neurological events, or deaths were observed. No additional arrhythmias or other SAEs were noted during follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

As transcatheter closure has become the standard of care for secundum ASDs, most existing literature stems from large multicenter studies in the United States or Europe, characterized by diverse operators, techniques, device types, and patient populations. 2 , 3 , 18 , 21 , 22 In contrast, our study provides a uniquely consistent perspective, representing one of the largest single‐operator, single‐center series to date, with 400 pediatric and adult patients treated over 15 years using nitinol devices and a standardized approach to device selection and implantation. This methodological consistency enhances internal validity and sharpens the interpretation of procedural outcomes, device performance, and complication profiles. Notably, this is also one of the first high‐volume studies from the Middle East, a region largely absent from the global interventional cardiology discourse. By capturing the demographic and clinical nuances of this setting, our findings bridge a geographic evidence gap and provide locally relevant insights to inform practice and shape procedural standards in comparable health care systems.

Clinical Success and Procedural Efficiency

The study underscores that although transcatheter ASD closure boasts a high success rate, it is not without challenges, particularly in anatomically complex cases. With a procedural success rate of 94%, technical difficulties were encountered in 29.5% of patients, necessitating advanced maneuvers such as guidewire‐ or balloon‐assisted implantation, especially in cases with large, multifenestrated defects or deficient margins. The 6‐month composite clinical success rate of 89.3%, coupled with a closure success rate of 98.9% at 96 months, underscores the safety and efficacy of transcatheter interventions, consistent with outcomes reported in other large‐scale studies. 18 , 22 , 23

Predictive Factors for Clinical Failure

The study highlights key predictors of clinical failure, particularly the impact of anatomical and technical factors. Despite high technical success, a smaller diameter difference between the device and balloon‐sized defect increased the likelihood of 6‐month clinical failure, emphasizing the importance of optimal device sizing for better outcomes. A limited oversizing margin may fail to address anatomical challenges such as rim deficiencies, septal mobility, or large fenestrated defects, leading to underestimation of the defect’s biomechanical demands. Mechanistically, insufficient oversizing reduces the radial force exerted by the device on septal tissue, compromising anchoring stability and increasing the risk of residual shunting, malposition, or embolization over time, as observed in our cohort. 13 In contrast, moderate oversizing facilitates implantation, improves rim apposition, enhances tissue capture, and supports endothelialization—key factors for durable closure. However, it also carries risks such as erosion, valve interference, and conduction disturbances. 12 , 24

To balance these concerns, our approach relied on balloon sizing, carefully avoiding overstretching by using the “stop‐flow” technique or halting inflation upon visualizing the small balloon waist on fluoroscopy. 25 Devices were then selected to match or slightly exceed (by 1 or 2 sizes) the largest balloon‐measured diameter, following best practices that advocate moderate oversizing. However, the ongoing debate between echocardiographic (2D/3‐dimensional [3D]) and balloon sizing methods remains central in the literature. Advocates of balloon sizing emphasize its ability to reduce measurement errors and better account for floppy edges when performed correctly using the “stop‐flow” technique. 26 In contrast, proponents of 2D/3D ultrasound sizing prioritize avoiding overstretching in cases with thin rims and undersizing when using “stop‐flow” for aneurysmal septa. 27 , 28 , 29 They typically add 3 to 6 mm to the largest 2D/3D or color Doppler diameter for device selection. In our data, significant discrepancies between these 2 methods were relatively rare: the baseline median 2D echocardiographic defect diameter was 21.5 mm, and the median balloon diameter was 24 mm, with differences typically ranging from 0 to 3 mm. Our preference for balloon sizing ensures safe device anchoring within anatomical boundaries. Larger discrepancies usually indicate dynamic or distensible defects or floppy rims—features that static echocardiography may underestimate. Balloon sizing provides a physiological assessment of the defect’s maximal distention under pressure, offering a more accurate reflection of its biomechanical behavior during device deployment.

Univariable analysis found that extra‐large defects with deficient rims were significant predictors of adverse outcomes, underscoring the challenges of anatomically complex defects. 30 Additionally, balloon‐ and guidewire‐assisted implantations emerged as predictors of failure. Both techniques are typically reserved for anatomically complex ASDs—such as those with deficient rims (particularly aortic or posterior), maligned or floppy septa, or very large defects—where maintaining device alignment and stability is technically challenging. Although the balloon and guidewire aid delivery, they may distort device orientation, reduce septal contact, and impair symmetric expansion, increasing the risk of inadequate anchoring, embolization, or residual shunting, especially once removed. Excessive wire manipulation may also traumatize the left atrium or pulmonary veins, potentially causing postoperative pericardial effusion. Thus, balloon and guidewire use reflects both a procedural adaptation and a surrogate marker of high‐risk anatomy predisposing to failure.

Adverse Events, Device Safety, and Long‐Term Follow‐Up

The study reports a low rate of SAEs, with only 4.5% of patients experiencing complications within 30 days, aligning with findings from other contemporary studies. Importantly, no life‐threatening issues, such as erosion, perforation, or neurological events, were observed, even among a cohort where 44.5% had anatomically challenging defects. Erosion, though rare and potentially occurring late, 31 has no identified risk factors strongly associated with contraindications. 10 , 24 Counseling families about patient characteristics linked to erosion, such as absent rims, and emphasizing careful device sizing to avoid oversizing is essential.

The most common complications included transient complete heart block (1.3%), which was reversible with medical therapy, and device embolization (2.3%), effectively managed through snare or surgical retrieval. These findings highlight the importance of procedural expertise and multidisciplinary readiness. Softer devices and more delivery systems may reduce embolization risk, 15 , 16 , 17 though ongoing postmarket surveillance remains crucial for evaluating the long‐term safety profiles of newer‐generation devices. 18

Transient arrhythmias, postprocedural migraines, and pericardial effusions were classified as minor adverse events in our cohort, but their clinical impact, particularly on early postimplantation comfort and quality of life, should not be overlooked. Most pericardial effusions resolved conservatively, though 1 case required device removal without evidence of erosion. 30 Transient atrial arrhythmias, while generally self‐limiting and responsive to short‐term therapy, caused symptoms like palpitations, dizziness, and anxiety that affected daily activities and well‐being in the weeks following the procedure. The need for temporary medication and monitoring adds a burden to patients and caregivers, highlighting the importance of prompt recognition and management. Postprocedural migraines, mainly in adolescents and young adults, likely result from transient neurovascular responses to device implantation, possibly linked to microemboli or inflammation. 32 , 33 These episodes caused headaches, visual disturbances, and nausea, disrupting normal activities such as school or work. Though resolved without long‐term effects, their impact on patient quality of life and satisfaction calls for careful counseling and supportive care.

No deaths or new arrhythmias were observed during follow‐up, reaffirming the safety of the procedure once the early postoperative period is successfully navigated. However, studies on long‐term outcomes of ASD closure indicate an increased risk of major comorbidities, such as atrial fibrillation and heart failure, especially when the closure is performed after age 60. 34 , 35 Another study reported less than 1% incidence of atrial arrhythmias, primarily in young children, occurring after a median of 6 months post procedure. 22 Additional delayed complications included isolated cases of pulmonary hypertension, linked to preexisting conditions or initial patient selection, 36 as well as rare instances of transient ischemic strokes and atypical chest pain.

The absence of cardiac arrhythmias in our cohort can be attributed to its younger population, with two‐thirds under 25 years of age. Because ASD‐related atrial fibrillation and heart failure typically manifest between the third and sixth decades of life, a follow‐up of at least 30 years is necessary to determine whether early childhood transcatheter ASD closure reduces the incidence of atrial fibrillation later in life. 37

Limitations and Areas for Future Research

Although this study provides a comprehensive analysis of transcatheter ASD closure, several limitations should be addressed in future research. The retrospective design, although useful for evaluating long‐term outcomes, may introduce biases due to missing data or variations in procedural techniques over time. We acknowledge attrition at long‐term follow‐up, a common limitation in elective procedures for relatively straightforward conditions such as ASD closure. However, true attrition—patients missing scheduled follow‐up and never reconsulting—was low (see Table 4). Clinically stable patients often discontinue visits within 2 years, a pattern consistently reported in similar studies, with reconsultation usually triggered by symptoms or occasional subsequent routine checks. In our setting, missed visits were mainly due to logistical, financial, or personal factors (eg, relocation, transportation difficulties, costs, or perceived lack of necessity). Loss of records, administrative errors, or death were extremely rare, particularly as most patients eventually reconsulted. Moreover, as the national referral center for congenital heart disease and such interventions, our institution is systematically involved in any significant late complication or adverse outcome. This reduces the likelihood of missing clinically relevant events, even among those formally lost to follow‐up. Although complete long‐term data would strengthen our conclusions, the absence of reported late events and transparent reporting support the safety and durability of the intervention.

Regression models were adjusted for patient weight and balloon‐sized defect diameter as key confounders; however, residual confounding from unmeasured factors—such as margin quality, or operator skill level—cannot be excluded.

Although a single senior operator ensures consistency in technique and decision‐making, enhancing internal validity, it may limit the external generalizability of our findings. Operator‐specific proficiency may not reflect skill variability across centers, potentially overestimating efficacy, or underestimating complications in less experienced hands. The lack of preprocedural magnetic resonance imaging for volumetric analysis and pulmonary/systemic flow ratios may limit insights into certain risk factors, particularly in complex cases with coexisting pulmonary hypertension or systemic anomalies. Although 3D echocardiography offers enhanced spatial resolution and is particularly useful for complex or fenestrated defects, its added value is most relevant in centers relying solely on echocardiographic sizing. 28 In our protocol, detailed 2D imaging was consistently paired with balloon sizing, which provides a physiological assessment of the defect’s maximal diameter and accounts for dynamic or compliant margins. This mitigates some limitations of 2D imaging. However, the absence of routine 3D imaging may limit anatomical detail in select cases and constrain generalizability to settings using advanced imaging modalities. Moreover, as device technology continues to advance, evaluating newer devices and techniques in prospective trials will be crucial to assess their safety and effectiveness compared with older‐generation ASO and ASO‐like nitinol devices.

CONCLUSIONS

In this cohort of 400 patients, transcatheter ASD closure demonstrated high technical success, with a 6‐month composite clinical success rate of 89.3%. Key predictors of failure included smaller device‐to‐balloon defect diameter difference, as well as balloon‐ and guidewire‐assisted implantations. Serious adverse events were rare, with no deaths or permanent complications. Long‐term closure success was excellent, achieving 98.9% at 96 months.
