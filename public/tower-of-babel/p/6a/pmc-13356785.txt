From underwater physiology to intensive care: oxygen tolerance unit at the bedside
Authors: Sylvain Diop, Maxime Théo Aparicio, Antoine Premachandra, Roman Mounier
Published: 2026

To the editor,

Finding the optimal oxygenation target for critically ill patients has attracted considerable attention over the past two decades. More than ten randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared restrictive and liberal oxygenation strategies in the intensive care unit (ICU), using either PaO2 or SpO2 based targets, with inconsistent results [1, 2]. Despite the absence of a difference in clinical outcomes between the two strategies in the largest RCT to date, which enrolled 16,500 patients (16,394 analyzed) [3], an even larger trial targeting 40,000 patients is currently underway (ACTRN12620000391976). Given these conflicting findings, it is reasonable to question whether PaO2 and SpO2 are the most appropriate variables for defining oxygenation targets in critically ill patients. Accordingly, after briefly reviewing why neither PaO2 nor SpO2 adequately reflects the risks associated with excessive O2 administration, we propose revisiting an old—but largely overlooked—concept for monitoring potential pulmonary O2 toxicity in ICU patients.

Physiological considerations

Hyperoxia and O₂ toxicity

Hyperoxia refers to excessive cellular O2 exposure beyond physiological requirements, whereas O2 toxicity denotes the resulting biological injury, predominantly mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS) [4, 5]. ROS are physiological byproducts of mitochondrial oxidative metabolism and play a variety of metabolic and regulatory roles, including intracellular signaling, angiogenesis, and both anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial effects [4, 6]. ROS-mediated oxidative stress is frequently invoked to support the rationale for restrictive rather than liberal oxygenation strategies [2]. Although O2 toxicity is commonly considered a ubiquitous phenomenon, a clear distinction should be made between pulmonary and systemic O2 toxicity. The lungs are the only organs with a direct blood-gas interface and are therefore uniquely exposed to O2 toxicity, the risk of which is proportional to the amount of supplemental O2 administered. Under normobaric conditions, systemic O2 toxicity may depend more on large fluctuations in mitochondrial O2 availability—for example during ischemia–reperfusion—than on an excess of dissolved O2 in the blood itself [4, 5, 7].

O2 physiology

Under normobaric conditions, the respiratory system is the only organ directly exposed to high O2 concentrations during supplemental O2 therapy. In contrast, other organs are relatively protected because of the low solubility of O2 in aqueous biological fluids and the presence of hemoglobin (Hb), which serves as the principal O2 carrier [7, 8]. Hb enables the transport and controlled release of the large quantities of O2 required to meet metabolic demands, thereby overcoming the limited O2-carrying capacity of dissolved O2 [8, 9]. The concentration of dissolved O2 in arterial blood is primarily determined by alveolar O2 partial pressure, the concentration of dissolved O2 in mixed venous blood, ventilation-perfusion matching, the magnitude of pulmonary shunt, the surface area and thickness of the alveolar-capillary membrane, cardiac output, and the solubility coefficient of O2 in plasma, which itself depends on plasma composition and temperature. O2 diffuses from the blood to the tissues at a rate determined by blood flow, blood viscosity, and the blood-to-cell O2 gradient. Additional factors, including the diffusion properties of O2 across phospholipid membranes and the existence of specific O2 transporters, may also influence regional O2 diffusion according to the physical, chemical, and functional characteristics of individual cells and tissues [9, 10]. Hb passively releases O2 in response to the local dissolved O2 concentration, as described by the oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve, and to H+ concentration through the Bohr effect [8, 9].

Assessing tissue O2 exposure

RCTs comparing restrictive and liberal oxygenation strategies have primarily relied on PaO2 and SaO2 targets, with SaO2 generally assessed noninvasively by SpO2. However, because of the unique characteristics of O2 transport in blood, defining an "optimal" oxygenation target solely on the basis of a PaO2 or SaO2 threshold is physiologically imperfect. PaO2 reflects the concentration of dissolved O2 in arterial blood and therefore may be considered a marker of hyperoxemia rather than hyperoxia. In contrast, SaO2 reflects the proportion of arterial Hb saturated with O2 and therefore cannot quantify hyperoxemia per se, although it may help prevent it when a conservative target (e.g. 94–96%) is used. Of note, SaO2 should always be interpreted in conjunction with pH and temperature, two major determinants of Hb-O2 affinity. In most organs, O2 is delivered through the circulation, where Hb-bound O2 accounts for the vast majority of O2 transport, whereas dissolved O2 represents only a small fraction of total blood O2 content [7, 9]. At the tissue level, only dissolved O2 molecules diffuse across biological membranes toward the cells according to the local O2 gradient generated by cellular O2 consumption [7, 9]. Hb passively releases O2 in response to the local dissolved O2 concentration [7, 9]. Consequently, only the arteriovenous O2 difference reflects the amount of O2 actually extracted by the tissues, and previous studies have shown that cells tightly regulate O2 uptake according to their metabolic requirements [10–13]. For a given Hb concentration, PaO2 and SaO2 primarily reflect the amount of O2 carried within the blood rather than the effective number of O2 molecules reaching the intracellular compartment [7, 9]. Extracorporeal mechanical life support may provide a unique opportunity to distinguish between pulmonary and systemic O2 toxicity. Patients supported with venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) are frequently hyperoxemic, with markedly elevated PaO2 values [14]. In these patients, blood is oxygenated directly through the ECMO membrane, thereby bypassing the lungs. Consequently, patients may be profoundly hyperoxemic while the amount of O2 delivered to the lungs remains relatively low. Nevertheless, several observational studies have reported an association between VA-ECMO-related hyperoxemia and worse clinical outcomes, which has been attributed to systemic O2 toxicity [14]. To date, only one RCT has compared liberal and restrictive oxygenation strategies in patients receiving VA-ECMO [14]. No significant differences in major clinical outcomes were observed between the two groups, arguing against a clinically relevant detrimental effect of ECMO-driven hyperoxemia, as acknowledged by the authors themselves [14]. Interestingly, the mean ventilator FIO2 remained below 60% in both groups, representing a relatively low pulmonary O2 exposure. This may provide a physiological explanation for the absence of harm associated with severe hyperoxemia in the liberal group: despite markedly elevated PaO2 values, pulmonary O2 toxicity was likely limited because the lungs were exposed to relatively modest FIO2 levels.

Lessons from underwater physiology

Exposure to supraphysiologic O2 levels causes time-dependent lung injury in both animal models and humans [8]. Because divers are exposed to very high alveolar O2 partial pressures, pulmonary O2 toxicity has been extensively investigated in diving physiology.

Several tools have been developed to quantify pulmonary O2 toxicity. The most widely used are the Unit Pulmonary Toxic Dose (UPTD), which corresponds to 1 min of exposure to 100% O2 at 1 atmosphere absolute (ATA; sea level), and the O2 Tolerance Unit (OTU), which quantifies the cumulative pulmonary O2 dose over consecutive exposures. Both indices share the same unit, with the OTU corresponding to the cumulative sum of successive UPTDs [15].

The OTU provides a practical means of quantifying cumulative pulmonary O2 exposure over time [15]. These exposure limits were established empirically from studies involving prolonged exposure to high O2 concentrations (e.g. hyperbaric workers). They represent the maximum cumulative O2 dose considered acceptable under specific operational conditions [15]. It should be emphasized, however, that individual tolerance depends on both the underlying clinical condition and susceptibility to O2 toxicity [15, 16].

Clinical application in the ICU

To our knowledge, this approach has never been applied in the ICU to investigate cumulative pulmonary O2 toxicity in patients receiving supplemental O2 for several days with an FIO2 above 50%. Integrating OTU monitoring into routine practice could provide valuable information to guide FIO2 titration in addition to conventional oxygenation variables.

For example, a patient receiving mechanical ventilation with an FIO2 of 70% for 24 h accumulates 673 OTUs, which remains below the maximum daily exposure recommended for divers. If the same FIO2 is maintained for the following two days, the cumulative exposure reaches 2,019 OTUs, exceeding the recommended cumulative exposure limit (Table 1). At an FIO2 of 100%, the daily maximum exposure (850 OTUs) is reached after approximately 14 h. However, these thresholds were established in individuals with healthy lungs and therefore cannot be directly extrapolated to critically ill patients. Indeed, such thresholds are likely too high for ICU patients, who often present with multiple pulmonary and systemic insults related to their underlying disease.

Several research strategies could be considered to evaluate the potential role of OTU monitoring in the ICU. First, standard care could be compared with an OTU-guided strategy aimed at limiting daily OTU exposure, with outcomes including ventilator-induced lung injury, respiratory mechanics (e.g. driving pressure and respiratory system compliance), and pulmonary biomarkers of oxidative stress. Second, the impact of a continuous automated OTU display—including projected OTU exposure over the next 24 to 48 h and the cumulative duration of exposure above an FIO2 of 0.5—could be evaluated on clinicians’ O2 prescription practices and total O2 exposure. Such a tool could increase clinicians’ awareness of cumulative O2 exposure and encourage earlier FIO2 reduction while maintaining a predefined oxygenation target (e.g. SaO2/SpO2). Third, disease-specific OTU targets could be developed according to the underlying pathology (e.g. pneumonia, lung transplantation). Finally, automated FIO2 titration could be guided by combined OTU and SaO2/SpO2 targets to minimize unnecessary O2 exposure.

OTU monitoring offers several potential advantages. It is simple to calculate at the bedside and captures cumulative O2 exposure throughout the ICU stay, a dimension of O2 therapy that is not reflected by PaO2 or SaO2. Automated OTU displays could facilitate FIO2 titration and help reduce unnecessary O2 exposure. However, this approach also has important limitations. Lung recovery occurring during periods of reduced O2 exposure is not accounted for by the OTU calculation. Moreover, in critically ill patients, OTU provides only an indirect estimate of potential pulmonary O2 toxicity, and data correlating OTU values with objective physiological, mechanical, or biological markers of lung injury remain lacking. Finally, OTU thresholds specific to different critical illnesses have yet to be established.

Conclusion

From a physiologic standpoint, toxicity of O2 is mostly related to the dose of O2 delivered to the lung overtime. PaO2 or SpO2 variables do not allow to directly evaluate the potential excessive exposure to O2. Directly monitoring the dose of O2 delivered overtime to the airways by calculating the OTU and determining specific threshold might be an interesting area of investigation for future studies.
