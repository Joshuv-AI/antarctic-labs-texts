Advocacy for health rights with a group of patients with chronic diseases in a low-income neighbourhood in Ecuador
Authors: Sergio Morales-Garzón, Elisa Chilet-Rosell, Jessica Pinto Delgado, Francisco Barrera-Guarderas, Marta Puig-García, Mª José Sanchís-Ramón, Ana Lucía Torres-Castillo, Lucy A Parker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWe describe an advocacy process developed with a diabetes patient club from a low-income area, using participatory research methods aimed at securing governmental support and recognition of peer networks as a fundamental principle of health promotion for chronic patients.ResultsThis participatory process, led by local facilitators from the Chimbacalle Diabetes Patients’ Club, followed three key steps: social diagnosis (using participatory tools such as a sociogram and SWOT analysis), development of a community narrative through digital storytelling, and process evaluation. Through this approach, participants identified support groups as essential connectors between patients and the healthcare system and highlighted their potential in prevention and health promotion, while also emphasizing the need for institutional support, infrastructure, and accessibility. By centring lived experiences, such as environmental stressors, emotional well-being, and peer support, storytelling helped surface issues often overlooked in clinical guidelines. The experience illustrates how participatory methodologies and storytelling can enhance communication, foster empathy toward the often-invisible challenges of living with Type 2 diabetes, and help drive advocacy strategies that translate evidence into context-sensitive actions rooted in social realities and community needs.Keywords: Participatory research, Health advocacy, Storytelling, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus

Introduction

In Ecuador, Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) affects nearly 5% of the population, with notable gender disparities in mortality [1]. The current health care model, including the Model of Integrated Family, Community and Intercultural Health Care (MAIS-FCI) [2] and the T2DM Clinical Practice Guideline [3] emphasise the importance of social collaboration and community participation. The work of chronic disease networks and support groups is a fundamental principle of health improvements and chronic conditions management [4, 5].

At the Chimbacalle Primary Health Centre (PHC) in Quito, managed by the Ecuadorian Ministry of Public Health, the diabetes care programme has been promoting patient involvement through community participation and social collaboration in the “Patients with Diabetes Club” (PDC) since 2007 [6]. However, the program faces potential closure due to limited governmental support and the ongoing national crisis. Furthermore, the COVID-19 pandemic has significantly disrupted non-communicable disease (NCD) services globally, disproportionately affecting individuals requiring long-term care [7].

In this research note, we describe a participatory process aimed at advocating for support from governmental institutions for peer-to-peer networks and as a cornerstone of health promotion among people living with T2DM. The action described in this paper is part of the Contextualizing Evidence for Action in Diabetes in low-resource settings (CEAD) [8] project which sought to stimulate context-led health systems innovations to improve diabetes care and reduce inequity [9].

Methods

Study design

We designed a participatory process to develop an advocacy action in health district 17D06 with a group of T2DM patients. Activities were conducted by local facilitators together with PDC volunteers.

Setting

The experience was conducted with the PDC at Chimbacalle Health Centre, located in a parish of 324,000 inhabitants in the south of Quito, Ecuador. This area is characterised by diversity and a predominantly low-income population.

Participants

We organized different working groups:

Local facilitators, who invited community participants and organized workshops. They were community leaders with experience in advocacy and coordination. Five PDC members agreed to participate, including the president and vice-president.

Community participants, PDC members willing and available to participate during the process.

Academic team, responsible for assessing and supporting the process. Team members were from Pontificia Universidad Católica del Ecuador (PUCE) and Universidad Miguel Hernández (UMH).

Participatory workshops

The participatory process aimed to ensure active involvement of members across the three working groups, fostering horizontal decision-making with participatory methods and frameworks. The main activities took place from May to November 2023, as part of the CEAD project that had been working in the district for five years, during which partnerships and contextual knowledge had been extensively developed. For this process, the academic team organized a meeting with facilitators (11 May 2023) to discuss the stages of the action and the relevance of social determinants of health. This meeting also served as training. Afterward, facilitators organised the next three steps of the process using the PCD’s WhatsApp group.

Social diagnosis

Sociogram

A sociogram is a diagram used to represent relational situations between individuals, groups, and stakeholders at a given point in time. The sociogram provides a visual representation of key relationships, highlighting points of interest for network analysis and illustrating connections between the group and its broader context [10]. Our sociogram began with a map of the parish in which facilitators placed the PDC. Participants were then asked to identify other stakeholders in three levels of distance from the PDC activities (distant, intermediate and close) and the relationships between them.

Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities and threads (SWOT) analysis

A SWOT analysis is a strategical method that provides both internal and external information about an organisation´s situation, based on the perspectives of its members. The SWOT analysis allows us to compare internal resources and capabilities with external events. The central question is: do we have the resources and capabilities to withstand threats and take advantage of opportunities in our environment? It involves the following steps: 1) Formulate external events as opportunities or threats; 2) Formulate internal resources and capabilities as strengths or weaknesses; 3) Compare strengths and weaknesses with opportunities and threats; 4)Use the results to formulate strategic options. In this study, the SWOT analysis was conducted to understand the PDC’s situation and to define realistic strategic guidelines in line with its capacity to influence public opinion and generate government action commensurate with the PDC’s potential and highlight areas such as multidimensional support needs [11].

This situational analysis started with “The group of chronic patients is not at its best because there is no continuous support from the institutional power and the professionals are not able to assist patients, what are the SWOT of the PDC in this situation?”

Dear health minister

During the development of the social diagnosis, facilitators introduced another participatory activity called ‘Dear Health Minister” which involved asking participants what they would say if the health minister asked them to suggest policy actions to improve the management of their disease. The aim of this activity was to clarify the messages to be developed in the storytelling activity. Each participant prepared a short sentence in response to this question which they then shared with the group. All three activities were carried out on 13 May 2023.

Storytelling

Digital storytelling is a valuable participatory method that synthesizes individual or group narratives into a video format, transforming personal experiences into collective concerns and fostering empathy with those sharing their stories. Storytelling encourages deeper community involvement by creating meaningful opportunities for participation in identifying and addressing local health concerns [12]. The script and key messages of the storytelling were developed in a workshop on 20 May 2023 together with the participants, the academic team and a professional video team. Building on ideas collected from the previous workshops, personal reflections were shaped into messages that included the reflections and feelings of the group, and could connect with the audience through everyday activities and scenarios. For example, to highlight the need for primary health care, participants decided to show images of the PHC and the challenges associated with accessing it. To ensure natural delivery, the main ideas and scenarios were used as prompts, allowing actors to avoid memorising the script word for word.

Process evaluation

After filming the video, the facilitators, supported by the academic team, organised a workshop on 3 June 2023 to present the results of the digital storytelling process to the PDC. The workshop included a video presentation followed by a group discussion in which participants proposed changes or alternative ways to share the messages. We also used the dartboard method to evaluate the process [13]. This participatory and visual tool consists of concentric circles, where the centre represents the most positive rating, and the circle is divided into segments that correspond to different aspects under evaluation, to capture participants’ perceptions in a rapid and accessible way. In our case, the segments reflected group participation, communication with facilitators and the academic team, workshop results, methodology, time investment, alignment with expectations, and overall planning.

Results

Social diagnosis

Sociogram

The social diagnosis was conducted by facilitators and 27 PDC members aged 50–75 years old. In the sociogram discussion, participants identified the Ministry of Public Health and politicians at the distant level, highlighting complex and conflictive support relationships in developing shared activities, particularly after government changes and the COVID-19 crisis. Participants expressed distrust of politicians and felt medical care was guided by interests other than health. They also noted the lack of basic treatment supplies. At the intermediate level, participants identified local healthcare centres and doctors who fostered a close, dynamic relationship between PDC members and hospitals, often through informal contacts. Participants stated that their main support was the Chimbacalle PHC, with many activities organized with health professionals’ assistance. They also recognized the existence and potential for collaboration with other chronic patient groups, although no such relationships existed and, in some cases, rivalries in health activity organization and participation were evident.

SWOT analysis

In the SWOT analysis, participants highlighted the social support provided by the PDC as a strength, which encompassed disease management and everyday life activities, creating spaces for self-management beyond clinical approaches. Participants also emphasised their experience in political activism, such as organising protests to demand increased funding for medication, reinforcing the PDC’s role in advocating for health rights. As weaknesses, participants noted fragmentation and lack of interest among members, with participation sometimes difficult to secure, and professionals attempting to link participation to medical follow-up. Internal governance was also a challenge, with leadership conflicts emerging. Regarding opportunities, participants noted that their activist experience enabled support and recognition from health professionals and, in many cases, politicians, giving them social power to drive change. Finally, threats included limited government support and external pressures due to their capacity to mobilise people in addressing social inequities.

Dear health minister

As a result of this activity, participants highlighted specific aspects related to their relationship with the Ministry of Public Health and, more generally, with the administration of health services. They expressed feeling alone in managing their chronic conditions due to breakdowns in the supply of medicines, the frequent turnover of health professionals, and the need for social and economic strategies to address the environment in which they live. They also pointed out the lack of communication between decision-makers and those responsible for implementing these decisions. For example, physicians had to care for patients while also performing other tasks, such as telemedicine, health promotion activities, and participating in preventive actions, such as vaccination campaigns throughout the year.

Storytelling

Although facilitators and club members were invited to storytelling workshops, only two participants of previous activities attended the first workshop. The script design began with participants, facilitators, the academic team, and the professional recording team. The narrative sought to illustrate the needs of people living with T2DM in Chimbacalle and to highlight the importance of support groups using personal stories, that revealed how patients felt abandoned by institutions and politicians. During the workshop, participants included everyday activities of the characters and representations of life with diabetes, combining audio recordings with images of the local setting. Images featured two older women facing difficulties walking, eating, or caring for themselves due to family responsibilities, combined with interviews of PDC participants and leaders. These narratives addressed the impact of diagnosis on daily life, a sense of abandonment resulting from care interruptions during the pandemic and structural reforms at Chimbacalle PHC, and how lack of institutional support can undermine social support groups. Five videos were produced with the PDC: one compiling all stories, three short videos for social media, and one documenting the behind-the-scenes process. These videos are available on the CEAD project website (https://cead.umh.es/storytelling-participatory-research-2/).

Evaluation

A total of 32 people participated in the evaluation workshop. Participants highlighted aspects not addressed in the main video and provided suggestions for improvements. These included low engagement among members conditioned by insecurity, gaps in the national law protecting individuals with chronic illness, frequent changes of health professionals, and their perceived lack of empathy with T2DM patients. The activity included messages emphasising the importance of the group as social therapy and the need to motivate both patients and professionals. The dartboard showed that participants valued the methodology as one of the most positive aspects. After including all suggestions, we organised a presentation of the final videos with all PDC members and another chronic disease group (7 and 9 Nov 2023). Although one objective was to organise a meeting with institutional representatives to facilitate dialogue between PDC members, policymakers and social leaders, the meeting was cancelled due to upcoming local elections, institutional changes and political instability.

Challenges and recommendation for future advocacy projects

When conducting health advocacy projects, it is necessary to have a thorough knowledge of sociodemographic and social organization of the target group [14]. In the initial stages, the leadership of the health professional coordinating the PDC proved crucial, providing a source of trust that enabled patients to work together. Engaging those who play a central role in bringing the group together and promoting shared decision-making through a horizontal approach is recommended. We prioritised creating a space for open discussion where information exchange was grounded in mutual learning about the neighbourhood and experiences with community action. The activities undertaken during the social diagnosis phase enabled us to contextualise relational dynamics and gain deeper insight into power structures, in line with the sociogram-based approach described by Biardi et al. [15]. Although patient support groups are not typically conflictive, participants described tensions between the PDC and policymakers stemming from previous advocacy actions, which risk straining institutional relationships and undermining the sustainability of actions led by the group [16].

Ecuadorian legislation mandates that all health centres have a support group for chronic patients, with the collaboration of a clinician to develop non-pharmacological management strategies and health promotion activities. A key finding of our advocacy process was that club activities depended on the predisposition of individual professionals and revealed limited connection between chronic patient groups from other centres. This fragmentation may reduce the effectiveness of activities and weaken prevention efforts [17, 18]. The SWOT analysis identified low participation in PDC activities as a weakness, despite participants being T2DM patients monitored through the group. As highlighted in literature, a major challenge in working with disadvantaged populations lies in fostering meaningful participation [19, 20].

During the process, discussions focused on individual behaviours and motivations. A key concern was lack of motivation among PDC members, often framed as personal failing rather than linked to structural barriers. The unplanned activity “Dear Health Minister” was introduced to encourage a more collective perspective, but the demands continued to reflect a biomedical view of health, focused on access to medication and attendance at consultations. This reflects broader critiques of the biomedical model, which overlooks social and contextual factors that determine health and individual behaviour [21].

Digital Storytelling proved to be an effective method for communicating information and a powerful tool for uncovering context-specific knowledge that might not emerge through traditional research [22]. It allows participants to articulate lived experiences, highlight structural barriers, and raise issues relevant to their communities. This makes it especially valuable in participatory research and advocacy, where understanding grassroots perspectives and priorities is essential. Filming deteriorated streets or traffic jams emphasized environmental factors beyond what is typically addressed in T2DM clinical guidelines.

Another finding was the emphasis on familiar support. PDC members requested that storytelling include messages about self-esteem and emotional well-being, highlighting psychosocial dimensions of chronic disease management and the role of peer groups. Storytelling showed how relevant the PDC was as a space to escape duties and responsibilities while caring for well-being. One of the strengths of the initiative was the involvement of other support groups and health professionals in reviewing the videos. This feedback enriched the material, broadened its reach, fostered new connections, and laid the groundwork for future initiatives.

The participatory activities and their results highlight the importance of support groups and their impact on emotional well-being. Our primary goal was not fully achieved, as the difficult political context prevented joint work and advocacy with local and national politicians. Nevertheless, the process was successful in raising awareness, creating spaces for discussion, and forging alliances with health professionals to advocate for chronic patients’ right to health through collective action. Future initiatives should focus on flexible strategies that adapt to shifting political and social contexts, incorporating multiple channels of advocacy.

We experienced difficulties in achieving participation during the first storytelling workshop. Although we offered transport, it was challenging to encourage participants to move from their familiar setting to the university, this was resolved during the video recording phase because we moved into community. Despite participation of club members and clinicians, the short-term design limited capacity to generate advocacy outcomes. Trust is built over time, so it may be advisable to design projects with medium- to long-term implementation to foster more effective outcomes [23].

Baseline indicators were not collected and as a result, we were unable to assess the before-and-after impact of the actions. Although participatory research has proven effective in addressing health disparities, our process was not designed to measure impact on this dimension. At the outset of the storytelling process, we were mindful that power and leadership dynamics within patient clubs and social organisations could affect participation. Since the club´s establishment in 2007, the executive committee, composed of a president and a vice-president, has been developed by the same individuals along with itinerant members. This non-selective model, effective in their case due to the representative’s influence, shaped how members interacted with the club. Two influential leaders decided not to join the activities, which had a negative effect on participation. While we aimed to construct a collective community narrative, this excluded certain voices. The outcomes reflect the shared history of the PDC according to the participants, but not necessarily the full diversity of experiences. We also must acknowledge the imbalances between community members, health professionals and researchers due to differences in status, privilege, or historical contexts [24]. In our participatory process, we proposed addressing these by emphasizing equitable partnerships, empowerment, shared decision-making, and trust.

Conclusions and call to action

The participatory process, which combined social diagnosis with storytelling, highlighted support groups as critical bridges between patients and the healthcare system, as well as essential assets for prevention, health promotion, and peer support that empower individuals to manage their health. Storytelling also underscored the importance of situating health challenges within their social and environmental contexts, moving beyond purely biomedical perspectives. Future advocacy efforts could be strengthened by flexible participatory approaches and dedicated human resources. Building community capacity may be especially useful in low-income settings to address health system shortcomings and advance the right to health.
