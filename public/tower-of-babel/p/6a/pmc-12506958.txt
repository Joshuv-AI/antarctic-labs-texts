Earthquake frequency-magnitude distribution at Mount Etna sheds light on magma ascent in the volcano’s plumbing system
Authors: Marco Firetto Carlino, Rosalia Lo Bue, Matteo Taroni, Mauro Coltelli, Flavio Cannavò, Ornella Cocina, Graziella Barberi, Luciano Scarfì
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Active volcanoes in densely populated areas are generally monitored through geological observations, geophysical, and geochemical data, which provide information on magma movement in the uppermost crust, allowing only short-term predictions of volcanic activity. However, deep magma recharge processes remain poorly understood. Here, we show that temporal variations in the slope of earthquake frequency-magnitude distribution (b value) at Mount Etna, a proxy for crustal stress changes, can track magma movements from deep to shallow crustal levels. Analyzing two decades of seismicity (2005–2024) across three crustal sectors, we distinguished magma recharge from the mantle, its transfer and storage at intermediate depths, and final ascent to the surface. Moreover, we found that b value time variations can precede geochemical anomalies from magma ascent and its eruption by a few months. Our results suggest that b value monitoring could have anticipated volcanic crises, supporting its integration into multiparametric surveillance systems for medium and long-term volcano monitoring.

INTRODUCTION

The emplacement of magmatic bodies within the crust induces substantial changes in chemical and physical properties of the wall rock, which can be remotely measured to monitor the state of volcanoes and assess potential eruptions. In addition to geodetic (1–4) and geochemical (5–8) methods, seismology is widely used for volcano monitoring, as (i) magma movement within the conduits and storages produces low-frequency vibrations of the volcanic edifice [i.e., volcanic tremor and long-period events; (9–13)] and (ii) intruding magma loads the wall rock triggering volcano-tectonic earthquakes due to fracturing processes or the reactivation of pre-existing faults (11, 14, 15).

In general, during periods of unrest, the number of earthquakes increases, and sometimes the space-time distribution of hypocenters may track dyke propagation (16–18). In addition, magma movement along volcanic plumbing systems has also been shown to modify the rheology of the crust, influencing the mechanical response of a crustal volume at storing and releasing elastic energy (14, 19–24). This aspect can be investigated by detecting changes in the slope of the Gutenberg and Richter frequency-magnitude distribution (FMD) of earthquakes (25): log 𝑁 = 𝑎 − 𝑏𝑀, where 𝑁 is the cumulative number of seismic events with magnitude greater or equal to 𝑀, 𝑎 represents the productivity, and the slope b is commonly referred to as the b value.

The b value expresses the proportion of small to large earthquakes and is thus inversely dependent on the mean earthquake magnitude. In volcanic settings, crustal volumes hosting active magma storages generally exhibit higher b values (11, 14, 20–22, 24, 26, 27) than average [around 1 on a global scale; (28, 29)]. Here, the wall rock is indeed characterized by poor mechanical properties, being repeatedly intruded by magmatic fluids and intensely fractured by mechanical and thermal stresses (26, 30), leading to a dense network of relatively small-scale fractures and conduits. This results in a high fractal dimension of seismicity (19, 31, 32), with a preferential occurrence of frequent low-magnitude earthquakes at the expense of larger ones (i.e., high b value), further facilitated by magmatic volatile pressure in the wall rock, which locally reduces the normal effective stress (21, 22, 27, 33). Conversely, higher-energy shocks are more likely associated with homogeneous crustal sectors and/or stress concentration zones, such as asperities along fault planes (28, 33–35). Under such conditions, elastic energy can be stored for longer periods, promoting the propagation of larger cracks (i.e., low b value).

Changes in the b value over time can be considered a proxy for variations in average crustal stress (36–39). The exsolution and diffusion of magmatic volatiles around magma storage zones may temporarily increase fluid pressure within fractures and fault surfaces, reducing differential stress and favoring slip (14, 21, 22, 27, 33). This mechanism results in a temporary higher proportion of small seismic events and, thus, in increased b values. Conversely, a reduction in b values is associated with increasing differential stress, either due to tectonic loading or the rapid ascent of magma that pressurizes the system without releasing considerable quantities of gas (22). This leads to the closure of microcracks, their propagation, and coalescence into larger fractures (37), which may also favor dyke propagation.

To examine whether the b value can track magma movement from deep crustal sectors to the surface and potentially serve as an eruption precursor, we use Mount Etna (southern Italy) as a test site (Fig. 1). Etna basaltic volcanism occurs in the collisional geodynamic context of the convergence between the African and European plates. The volcano lies atop the Apenninic-Maghrebian fold-and-thrust belt (orogenic domain) and the external portion of the underthrusting Hyblean Plateau carbonate succession (foreland domain), which flexes northwestward beneath the accretionary wedge [Fig. 1A; (40)]. A strike-slip fault system trending WNW to NNW, originally associated with the opening of the Tyrrhenian basin (41) but now controlled by a NNW-oriented maximum horizontal stress field (42), cuts the Etna area (Fig. 1A), facilitating magma migration through the crust (43). Here, the stress exerted by magma ascent and emplacement in the crust, along with background regional tectonic forces, strains the wall rock, triggering numerous seismic events.

Our results show that monitoring the b value over time offers a valuable opportunity to track magma movements from deep to shallow portions of the plumbing system and could be integrated into Etna’s multiparametric surveillance system to improve the assessment of impending eruptions.

RESULTS

Establishing temporal variation of the b value along the Etna’s plumbing system

To analyze how magma ascent influences earthquakes production, we examined the local earthquake catalogs of the Istituto Nazionale di Geofisica e Vulcanologia, Osservatorio Etneo (INGV-OE; Catania, Italy), covering the period from 1 January 2005 to 31 December 2024 (44–48). This catalog has been relocated using the tomoDDPS software (49) and a three-dimensional (3D) velocity model derived from the integration of passive and active seismic data [see Firetto Carlino et al. (14) and references therein].

For monitoring purposes, it is essential to track magma transfer from the deep crust to the surface. On the basis of the existing knowledge on Etna’s plumbing system (6, 8, 14, 50), we considered three distinct pivotal seismogenic volumes (Fig. 1). For each, a global b value was estimated. The completeness magnitude (Mc) was determined by testing the exponentiality of the magnitudes using a Lilliefors test (51, 52). Particular attention was given to the temporal variability of Mc, which can lead to biased estimation (53).

The deep volume (comprising 2594 earthquakes, represented as blue dots in Fig. 1) extends from depths of 10 km to over 30 km below sea level (b.s.l.). It consists of a NE-striking and NW-dipping seismogenic zone characterized by a high anomaly in P-wave velocity relative to a reference model [high dVp; (50)] and a b value of 1.00 (σ = 0.03), typical of purely tectonic environments. This volume has been interpreted as part of a remnant of the continental Hyblean foreland units, underthrusting beneath the accretionary wedge, which is in turn overlain by Etna volcanics (14, 50). It forms a mechanical boundary that constrains an aseismic volume characterized by lower dVp (50), which is likely related to a mantle plume. A discontinuity in the foreland units has been detected in seismic tomographic data (50) and interpreted as a tear, likely of tectonic origin, which facilitates the ascent of basaltic magma.

At shallower depths, on the basis of volatile concentrations in melt inclusions, Corsaro et al. (8) identified a deep magma storage, where a volatile-rich, subaphyric, and poorly differentiated basaltic magma is stored, between approximately 10 and 12 km b.s.l. On the basis of gas geochemistry, Paonita et al. (6) constrain the deep storage to a depth between 7 and 12 km b.s.l. At this depth range, recent seismic tomographic studies have identified a low dVp region, interpreted as a deep magma storage (50, 54). Its location is still the subject of debate, and we found that these crustal depths are characterized by low seismic productivity (beige-dashed ellipse in Fig. 1); therefore, a reliable b value time series cannot be calculated there. The regions described so far characterize the deep portion of Etna’s plumbing system. Here, magma is still poorly degassed (55), and its ascent from the mantle would temporarily increase differential stress within the wall rock, leading to a decrease in b value (22).

From the deep plumbing system, magma propagates to a network of multiple interconnected storage zones, occasionally activated before eruptive events (8, 56). This storage system is centered at 5 to 7 km b.s.l. (8), likely extending upward to less than 3 km (6), and its presence has also been inferred from geophysical data (14). Here, magma degassing is enhanced (55). We consider the seismogenic volume beneath the central crater area, extending from sea level to a depth of 8 km (5840 earthquakes, shown as yellow dots in Fig. 1), representative of the crustal sector hosting this intermediate portion of Etna’s plumbing system. This depth range also coincides with that of a high P-wave velocity body located in the central-southern part of the volcano [see Lo Bue et al. (50) and references therein], believed to be the expression of high-density cumulates, fractionated by magma during its ascent, stored and solidified at depth. As expected, a higher b value of 1.70 (σ = 0.04) was estimated, reflecting a heterogeneous, fractured, and fluid-intruded sector, where frequent low-magnitude earthquakes preferentially occur over larger ones.

Last, to investigate the final stages of magma transfer to the surface, we examined the shallow plumbing system (6, 8), where an additional magma storage zone is located within a seismogenic volume extending from directly beneath the summit of Etna to a depth of 2 km b.s.l. (1.604 earthquakes, displayed as red dots in Fig. 1). This region exhibits a b value of 1.31 (σ = 0.06). The global frequency-magnitude distributions for the three seismogenic volumes are shown in Fig. 1B, using survivor functions for easier comparison.

For each volume (Fig. 1), b value time series with their associated SEs (Fig. 2) were also estimated by modifying the weighted likelihood approach of Taroni et al. (57) to account for variations in Mc over time. We considered the entire seismic catalog (1 January 2005 to 31 December 2024); however, we started the observation period in January 2016 to ensure a significant number of seismic events (Fig. 2).

Tracking Etna volcano activity through temporal changes in the b value

The main phases of Etna volcano activity from January 2016 are related to variations in the b value over time, as outlined below. We refer to the three distinct time series as time series – deep (TS-D), time series – intermediate (TS-I), and time series – shallow (TS-S), (Fig. 2, A to C), which investigate the deep, intermediate, and shallow portions of the plumbing system, respectively (Fig. 1).

After a period of intense yet discontinuous paroxysmal explosive activity at the summit craters that began in January 2011, volcanic eruptions at Mount Etna have notably diminished from mid-2016, becoming characterized by only moderate Strombolian eruptions and associated small lava flows (58). This substantial reduction in erupted magma volume led to abnormal overpressurization of the plumbing system starting in 2017 (6, 14, 18).

During this period, variations in the TS-D trend can be observed, leading to a slight increase in the b value in December 2016 (1 in Fig. 2A), followed by a marked decrease on 25 January 2017 (2 in Fig. 2A). The rising trend could be attributed to the migration of magma from the deep plumbing system to shallower crustal levels, temporarily reducing differential stress by unloading the host rock. However, as it falls within the 2-σ error range, intrinsic stochastic variability cannot be excluded. Instead, the subsequent drop in TS-D is notably sharp, potentially indicating the replenishment of magma from the mantle plume, which presses on the bounding foreland units without releasing considerable amounts of gas, thereby increasing stress.

The drop in b values in TS-D is followed by a marked increase in TS-I at the beginning of February 2017 (3 in Fig. 2B), peaking at 2.6 in March (4 in Fig. 2B). Subsequently, TS-I remains relatively stable, with minor fluctuations, until the end of 2017. This unique rising trend, followed by stable values, aligns well with an extraordinary buildup of magmatic volatile pressure within the wall rock hosting the intermediate magma storage. This process likely reduced differential stress along fractures and fault surfaces, resulting in the preferential occurrence of many relatively smaller-than-normal earthquakes [i.e., increased b values; see also Firetto Carlino et al. (14)]. The rise in TS-I precedes by about 2 months the progressive increase in 3He/4He isotope ratio in the gaseous emissions measured at the base of the volcano (6), as well as the active inflation of the Etna edifice (59), both of which began in April 2017. The increase in 3He/4He isotope ratio lasted until the summer of 2018 and was interpreted as being related to magma recharge and pressurization between 7 and 12 km b.s.l. (6).

Starting in December 2017, we observe a gradual drop in TS-I (5 in Fig. 2B), which approaches the average b value for the entire intermediate seismogenic volume (1.70) by mid-September 2018 (6 in Fig. 2B). This downward trend may result from the ascent of a sequence of poorly degassed magma batches within the intermediate storage. Such a process would increase differential stress, promoting the coalescence of microcracks into larger fractures and favoring dyke propagation, thereby temporarily shifting seismicity toward relatively higher magnitudes [i.e., decreased b values (22,37)]. This process likely favored the migration of magmatic volatiles toward the shallow storage, in turn increasing b values in TS-S (5 in Fig. 2C). The consequent reduction in volatile pressure around the intermediate storage further contributed to the progressive decrease in b values in TS-I until mid-September 2018. Our interpretation aligns well with the increased CO2 flux from the soil between June and October 2018 (6), which has been attributed to the ascent of either magma batches or CO2-rich fluids from the deep toward the intermediate and shallow portions of the magmatic system. From December 2017 to mid-September 2018, TS-D does not show significant variations (Fig. 2A), suggesting that no relevant stress changes occurred within the deep seismogenic volume (Fig. 1).

Considering the entire period examined thus far, magma batches rising from the mantle between December 2016 and January 2017 (1 and 2 in Fig. 2A) may have temporarily accumulated in the deep magma storage (Fig. 1), which lies in a region with scarce seismicity, and then progressively transferred to the intermediate and shallow storages between February 2017 and September 2018 (3 to 6 in Fig. 2, B and C).

A clear sign of unrest was identified in October 2018 (18), based on intense seismicity affecting the intermediate and shallow seismogenic volumes (Fig. 1). From November 2018 onward, the decrease in CO2 emissions from Etna’s flanks and in the 3He/4He ratio, together with high SO2 fluxes and CO2/SO2 ratios, were interpreted as evidence of reduced pressure in the deep magmatic system and the injection of poorly degassed magma into shallower sectors (6). We observe an increase in b values in TS-I approximately 2 months earlier, starting in mid-September 2018 (6 in Fig. 2B), accompanied by nonsignificant fluctuations in TS-D. This trend may be related to (i) the temporary unloading of the wall rock hosting the intermediate storage due to magma transfer from the mid to the shallow plumbing system and/or (ii) an increase in magmatic volatile pressure. One or a combination of these processes would locally reduce differential stress, thereby increasing the b values. The nearly steady trend in TS-S (Fig. 2C) during magma recharge may reflect an open conduit condition in the shallower part of the Etna’s plumbing system. From this perspective, b values in this crustal sector remained unchanged because, as seismicity is exclusively triggered during episodes of magma intrusion, the frequency-magnitude distribution of earthquakes remains nearly constant over time.

These conditions preceded one of Etna’s major unrest phases, which occurred between 24 and 27 December 2018, when the magmatic system had accumulated a considerable amount of CO2 that could not be released by inter-eruptive degassing (6). It was characterized by a remarkable release of the seismic strain (18), abrupt variations in the FMD of earthquakes (14), a rapid increase in volcanic tremor, intense ground deformation (59, 60), and enhanced degassing (58). This unprecedented unrest was triggered by the intrusion of an estimated ~30 × 106 m3 of magma, which stalled its ascent at approximately sea level (61). Nevertheless, Etna’s magma storage was not substantially discharged, as the unrest event was followed only by a short-lived fissure eruption near the summit, during which just ~3 × 106 m3 of magma reached the surface (58, 61, 62). The stress exerted by the intrusive episode triggered a relatively strong earthquake (ML = 4.8) on 26 December, causing severe damage to several towns on Etna’s lower eastern flank (18). The December 2018 unrest is marked by an abrupt drop in b values in TS-I (7 in Fig. 2B). This observation is consistent with an increase in mean magnitudes during dyke propagation leading up to the eruption (14, 18). The nearly steady trend in TS-D and TS-S suggests no substantial variations in the deep and shallow portions of the plumbing system (Figs. 1 and 2, A and C).

Afterward, volcanic activity remained characterized by episodes of moderate Strombolian eruptions at the summit and associated small lava flows until 13 December 2020, when a sequence of lava fountains erupted from the summit craters (63, 64). Before the onset of lava fountains, significant variations in the b value were observed in the deep seismogenic volume. Specifically, an increase in TS-D is observed in early October 2020 (8 in Fig. 2A), approximately 2 months before the sequence began, followed by a sharp drop from 7 December until the end of the year (9 in Fig. 2A). As described above, we interpret the rising trend as the upward migration of primitive magma from depth to shallower crustal levels, while the subsequent drop represents the replenishment of magma from the mantle in the deeper part of the magmatic system. A further increase in TS-D was observed in mid-January 2021 (Fig. 2A). Magma migration throughout Etna’s plumbing system is also consistent with geochemical, petrological, and ground deformation data monitored by INGV-OE (see Data and materials availability).

During this paroxysmal explosive activity, which continued until 21 February 2022, b values remained close to the average in TS-I (Fig. 2B), suggesting no considerable stress variations in the intermediate plumbing system. Conversely, a slight increase is observed in TS-S (10 in Fig. 2C), consistent with augmented volatile pressure around the shallow storage at the onset of the lava fountaining sequence. Overall, our observations align with a nearly open conduit system, likely established following the December 2018 unrest.

Lava fountaining events discharged a volume of magma on the order of 100 × 106 m3 (65), causing the most intense deflation of the entire volcanic edifice recorded in the past 20 years (3). During the sequence of lava fountaining, seismic production diminished, especially in the mid- and shallow-crustal sectors, and the b value time series do not show significant variations (Fig. 2, B and C).

In the following period, eruptive activity diminished, and seismicity primarily affected the deep volume in the western and northern sectors of the volcano. Strombolian activity resumed at the summit craters on 18 May 2023. On the same day, the occurrence of a seismic swarm within the shallow seismogenic volume (Fig. 1), along with ground deformation data, suggested a shallow magma intrusion. Strombolian activity then evolved into a lava fountaining episode on 21 May 2023. A further seismic swarm occurred on 28 May 2023, just east of the summit craters, within the intermediate seismogenic volume (Fig. 1; for details, see Data and materials availability). Until the end of 2024, Etna’s volcanic activity was characterized by episodic Strombolian eruptions and lava fountaining from the summit craters (e.g., the sequence of six lava fountains between 4 July and 15 August 2024; for details, see Data and materials availability). With the exception of a drop in TS-I and TS-S due to the seismic swarms (11 in Fig. 2, B and C), the b value time series show no significant variations. This observation, coupled with the sporadic occurrence of summit eruptions, suggests no substantial obstacles in the transfer of magma along the whole Etna’s plumbing system.

DISCUSSION

This study highlights the potential of the b value as a key indicator for investigating different stages of magma migration throughout Etna’s plumbing system over the medium and long term. The most likely physical causes of b value variations are summarized below and illustrated in Fig. 3.

Conceptual model of Etna’s plumbing system, showing the main structural features of the region and magma storages. The most likely physical causes of b value time variations in the deep and intermediate seismogenic volumes (Fig. 1) are reported, while the nearly steady frequency-magnitude distribution of earthquakes around the shallow storage reflects an almost open conduit condition (see text for details).

In particular, the recharge of magma from the mantle is consistent with a drop in b values over time at depths greater than 10 km b.s.l., as it would temporarily increase differential stress, shifting seismicity toward higher magnitudes. Conversely, magma outflow to shallower sectors unloads the host rocks, leading to an increase in this parameter (Fig. 2A).

In the crustal volume enclosing the intermediate storage (between sea level and 8 km depth), where magma degassing is enhanced, a decrease in b values points to (i) the injection of poorly degassed magma from deeper levels and/or (ii) a reduction in volatile pressure. Both processes would increase differential stress, promoting the coalescence of microcracks into larger fractures and enhancing dyke propagation. An increase in b values, on the other hand, is indicative of augmented volatile pressure due to enhanced degassing and/or magma transfer to shallower crustal levels (Fig. 2B).

Last, the minor b value variations around the shallow storage likely reflect a relatively stable open conduit system. Here, as seismicity is only triggered during magma intrusion episodes, the frequency-magnitude distribution of earthquakes remains nearly constant over time (Fig. 2C).

The overall trend of the three b value time series (Fig. 2) aligns well with the different phases of Etna volcano activity and with the expected sequence of magma recharge from the mantle, its transfer and storage at intermediate depths, and its final ascent to the uppermost part of the system. This reinforces the potential of the b value as a monitoring tool and conceptually supports our interpretation. Furthermore, by analyzing the long-term behavior of the volcano, we identified an initial period of inhibited magma transfer, which culminated in the major unrest of December 2018. This was followed by a phase in which no substantial obstacles hindered magma ascent and then eruption, primarily characterized by Strombolian activity and lava fountaining from the summit craters. The nearly open conduit system was likely established following the major unrest.

However, the reliability and resolution of temporal b value analysis strongly rely on accurate and continuous seismic monitoring, as it allows for the detection of even the least energetic earthquakes, lowering Mc and mitigating artifacts related to systematic mislocation and magnitude estimation (66, 67). To this end, the future implementation of an automated earthquake detection and location method based on artificial intelligence would increase the number of events in earthquake catalogs and enhance location reliability. This will strengthen b value estimations, making them even more suitable for near real-time monitoring.

In conclusion, this methodology enables the investigation of temporal changes in crustal stress conditions along volcanoes’ plumbing system, provided that a sufficient number of earthquakes is available. Its application to Mount Etna allowed us to shed light on magma movements even in the deep crust, unlike other methods, which provide reliable data only for the intermediate-to-terminal parts of the magmatic system. Our observation aligns well with other monitoring data and, in particular, has been shown to precede geochemical signals by a few months, as information travels at the speed of seismic waves and is not subject to the latency of gas phases migrating through the crust. From this perspective, integrating temporal b value analysis into volcano monitoring could enhance our understanding of magma dynamics, help identify phases of unrest, and detect in advance anomalous conditions that may lead to volcanic eruptions. The detection of signals related to magma transfer along different sectors of Etna’s plumbing system, before they are identified by early warning systems working at the INGV-OE control room, will improve the forecasting of impending eruptions. Last, the b value could be used as an eruption precursor for other volcanoes where a sufficient number of earthquakes is available, ultimately leading to better management of volcanic hazard and public safety.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Earthquake 3D relocations

In this study, we analyzed the seismicity of Etna volcano from 01 January 2005 to 31 December 2024 (44–48). Volcano-tectonic earthquakes were recorded by a seismic network of over 30 stations, equipped with three-component broadband seismometers, and located daily by a team of experienced analysts in the seismological division of the INGV-OE. The initial dataset consisted of about 20,500 seismic events with ML in the range 0.1 to 4.8, selected with at least six observations, root mean square time residuals at the stations of 0.50 s or less, and uncertainties in both horizontal and vertical directions of 5.0 km or less.

This dataset was relocated using the tomoDDPS software (49) and a 3D velocity model derived by the integration of passive and active seismic data (14), to improve accuracy in hypocenter locations. The algorithm has the advantage of using a combination of both absolute and differential arrival-time readings between pairs of closely spaced earthquakes, which share the same velocity profile along the ray path between the source and a common receiver. Since the velocity paths are nearly identical, the difference in arrival times can be reasonably attributed only to the spatial offset between the seismic events. We obtained a final dataset of 20,465 relocated earthquakes with improved locations, while the remaining events could not be reliably relocated due to insufficient data quality or constraints in the velocity model.

The 3D relocations resulted in better clustering of the earthquakes and reduced the residuals between the observed and theoretical arrival times by approximately 37%, with an average value of 0.014 s. The final locations are affected by average uncertainties of less than 0.8 km in both the horizontal and vertical directions.

Magnitude of completeness estimation

The Mc, i.e., the threshold above which an earthquake catalog can be considered complete (68), is a fundamental parameter for an unbiased b value estimation (53). In general, short-term variations in Mc can occur due to incompleteness caused by the strongest events in the catalog (69). These increases in Mc are referred to as short-term aftershock incompleteness (STAI) and are caused by a rapid surge in the number of seismic events shortly after stronger earthquakes, which makes it difficult to detect smaller events in the seismograms (69). However, volcanic zones are characterized by additional factors, as the high background seismic noise (e.g., volcanic tremor), that can temporarily increase Mc. Therefore, a detailed study of Mc variations is necessary in these regions.

Here, we used the exponentiality test approach to determine the general Mc value (70). Specifically, we applied the Taroni (52) method for testing the exponentiality of magnitudes, as this method is helpful when dealing with a catalog potentially affected by b value variations. To account for the potential underestimation of the Mc due to the magnitude binning (71), we added 0.1 (i.e., the magnitude binning of the seismic catalog) to the estimated Mc values. To capture short-term increases in Mc, we used the graphical method of Zhuang et al. (72). Figure S1 shows the variations of Mc for the three crustal volumes.

b value estimation

To estimate the b value in the presence of temporal variation in Mc, we used the Taroni (73) approach. In this method, the b value and its uncertainty (σ) can be estimated using the following formulasb^=N−1Nln(10)[∑i=1N(Mi−MC,i)N+∆M2](1)σ^=b^N(2)where N is the number of seismic events in the complete part of the catalog, Mi is the magnitude of the ith event, MC,i is the magnitude of completeness related to the ith event, and ∆M is the magnitude binning (in this case, 0.1).

Since variations in Mc are not solely due to incompleteness caused by the strongest events in the catalog (STAI) but also to peculiarities of the volcanic environment (e.g., volcanic tremor), the maximum likelihood estimation of b value with the correction proposed by Taroni (73) is preferable to the b-positive method (74). The b-positive approach is most effective for large seismic sequences, a context that differs markedly from the cases investigated in this study.

For the b value time series, we modified the method of Taroni et al. (57) to account for Mc variations. This approach constructs the time series using a weighted likelihood estimation, rather than the classical moving window method based on a fixed number of events within a time window. This approach is particularly suitable for the seismic catalogs where the earthquakes occur with uneven interevent times. It also avoids the subjective choice of the number of events to be included in the moving window.

To test the differences in the b values, we used the classical Utsu test (75), which can also be applied in the case of a variable Mc, provided that Xi = Mi − MC,i is used as the observation (73) (see table S1). Finally, the dynamic range used in the b value estimation (76), defined as max {Mi − MC,i}, is also reported in table S1.
