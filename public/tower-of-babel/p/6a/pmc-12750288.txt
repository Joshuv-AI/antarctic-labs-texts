Addressing Migrant Worker Housing—From Structural Neglect to Strategic Policy Action
Authors: Schawanya K Rattanapitoon, Natnapa H Padchasuwan, Nav La, Nathkapach K Rattanapitoon
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

The recent article by Samant et al [1], "Europe’s Overlooked Health and Hygiene Issues in Migrant Worker Housing: A Call for Action," brings long-overdue scholarly attention to a structural determinant of occupational health that remains strikingly absent in policy discourse: housing.

The authors rightly underscore the multidimensional consequences of substandard living conditions for migrant workers—ranging from heightened infectious disease transmission to psychological distress and cumulative occupational risks. This intersectional perspective reflects the evolving understanding of the “social determinants of occupational health,” where work-related exposures do not end at the factory gate but extend into the domestic sphere [2].

To strengthen the occupational hygiene perspective—central to the journal section in which the original article appears—it is important to emphasize that migrant worker housing should be considered a workplace-adjacent environment requiring systematic hygiene inspection protocols, sanitation and waste management standards, and coordinated cross-sector interventions involving labor inspectorates, public health authorities, and municipal housing agencies. Routine inspections, backed by enforceable standards, are essential for mitigating environmental health hazards such as inadequate ventilation, pest infestations, and unsafe food preparation facilities.

However, what deserves further elaboration is the disjunction between regulatory frameworks and their practical enforcement. While countries such as Norway are cited as examples of conscientious regulatory efforts, evidence from labor inspections in southern and Eastern Europe suggests that enforcement remains fragmented and under-resourced [3]. The situation is even more precarious for posted workers and informal laborers, whose legal status often precludes them from invoking protective entitlements [4]. In such contexts, precarious accommodation—unstable, unsafe, or insecure housing—becomes not only a health hazard but also a barometer of labor precarity. Similarly, segregated accommodations—housing arrangements that spatially and socially isolate workers from the host community—can intensify occupational and social vulnerabilities.

Moreover, the paper’s discussion on psychosocial harms could be strengthened by linking it to the concept of precarious housing as a chronic stressor with effects akin to job insecurity [5]. Research from migrant farmworker camps in North America demonstrates that overcrowded and segregated accommodations function as sites of racialized spatial exclusion, reinforcing structural vulnerabilities and social isolation [6]. These psychosocial dimensions warrant a more prominent place in occupational health frameworks, especially when advocating for integrated mental health strategies.

I strongly support the authors’ call for expanded research but would argue for a shift toward participatory and ethnographic methods that capture the lived experiences of migrant workers, which often elude survey-based studies. Collaborative research involving workers’ organizations, trade unions, and local health authorities could offer more actionable insights for a policy reform.

Cross-regional comparisons further demonstrate the global pervasiveness of this issue. In Southeast and South Asia—especially in countries with high outbound labor migration such as the Philippines, Nepal, and Bangladesh—migrant workers are frequently housed in employer-controlled dormitories with minimal regulatory oversight. Evidence from Singapore and the Gulf States shows that dense, high-risk accommodations contributed to large-scale COVID-19 outbreaks among migrant laborers, exacerbated by limited access to health care and precarious legal status [7,8]. These cases illustrate lessons for European regulators, including the importance of proactive, rather than reactive, housing inspections, and the integration of emergency public health measures into labor housing codes.

In North America, undocumented farmworkers and meatpacking employees in the United States face similar risks stemming from overcrowded, informal housing and fear-based avoidance of medical systems [6,9]. These parallels reinforce the need for European policies that address not only physical safety but also legal and social access barriers. In Australia, studies among seasonal workers in agriculture and the Pacific Labour Mobility Scheme have documented exploitative conditions and housing that fails to meet minimum standards, despite legal protections [10]—underscoring that legislative frameworks alone are insufficient without robust enforcement, a challenge also faced in parts of Europe.

Meanwhile, in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, internal and cross-border migrants employed in mining, construction, and informal markets often live in makeshift or temporary settlements lacking sanitation and basic utilities, highlighting the need for hygiene-oriented housing policies that address water, waste, and environmental safety as occupational health priorities, which aligns with calls for migration-aware health systems in the region [11].

These parallels across continents underscore the need to reconceptualize housing not as a secondary labor issue but as an integral element of occupational health systems. Global cooperation is necessary—but so is localization—to ensure that interventions reflect regional legal, social, and economic contexts.

Samant et al [1] have made an important contribution by situating migrant worker housing as an urgent occupational health concern. Their work opens the door for a truly global conversation—one that must integrate perspectives from the Global South, build on comparative evidence, apply cross-regional lessons to European contexts, and transform temporary accommodations into dignified spaces safeguarded by international labor and occupational hygiene frameworks.
