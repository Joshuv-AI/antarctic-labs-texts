Salivary proteomic signatures in severe dental fluorosis
Authors: Patcharaporn Gavila, Penpitcha Ajrithirong, Supoj Chumnanprai, Nuttiya Kalpongnukul, Trairak Pisitkun, Soranun Chantarangsu, Kanokwan Sriwattanapong, Junji Tagami, Thantrira Porntaveetus
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe relationship between dental fluorosis and alterations in the salivary proteome remains inadequately elucidated. This study aimed to investigate the salivary proteome and fluoride concentrations in urine and drinking water among Thai individuals afflicted with severe dental fluorosis. Thirty-seven Thai schoolchildren, aged 6–16, were stratified based on Thylstrup and Fejerskov fluorosis index scores: 10 with scores ranging from 5 to 9 (SF) and 27 with a score of 0 (NF). Urinary and water fluoride levels were determined using an ion-selective fluoride electrode. Salivary proteomic profiling was conducted via LC–MS/MS, followed by comprehensive bioinformatic analysis. Results revealed significantly elevated urinary fluoride levels in the SF group (p = 0.007), whereas water fluoride levels did not significantly differ between the two cohorts. Both groups exhibited 104 detectable salivary proteins. The NF group demonstrated notable upregulation of LENG9, whereas the SF group displayed upregulation of LDHA, UBA1, S100A9, H4C3, and LCP1, all associated with the CFTR ion channel. Moreover, the NF group uniquely expressed 36 proteins, and Gene Ontology and pathway analyses suggested a link with various aspects of immune defense. In summary, the study hypothesized that the CFTR ion channel might play a predominant role in severe fluorosis and highlighted the depletion of immune-related salivary proteins, suggesting compromised immune defense in severe fluorosis. The utility of urinary fluoride might be a reliable indicator for assessing excessive fluoride exposure.Keywords: Dental fluorosis, Fluoride, Hypomineralization, Mass spectrometry, SymbiosisSubject terms: Diseases, Pathogenesis, Proteomics

Introduction

Fluoride is found in various sources such as water, food, and dental products, and it is often regarded as a double-edged sword. When consumed in appropriate amounts, fluoride offers protection against dental caries, but a chronic and excessive intake of fluoride can lead to dental and skeletal fluorosis1. More than 500 million people in at least 25 countries live in endemic fluorosis areas with excess fluoride in water source and biosphere, resulting in public health problem2. In Thailand, fluoride is naturally present in groundwater, and regions with high fluoride content are typically identified within geological formations known as fluoride belts3. Severe dental and skeletal fluorosis have been observed in individuals residing in the northern and western parts of Thailand, particularly among those who commonly consume water from underground wells and springs4.

An excessive ingestion of fluoride during tooth development, particularly during the transition and early-maturation stages of enamel formation, can lead to dental fluorosis5. The effects of severe dental fluorosis result in a range of dental abnormalities, including discoloration, pitting, and enamel deterioration. Individuals with severe dental fluorosis may experience tooth sensitivity and compromised oral health. Furthermore, the impact of severe dental fluorosis extends to the oral proteomic environment, where changes in saliva composition can be observed. The altered proteomic profile may reflect the physiological response to enamel degradation, involving variations in the expression of specific proteins.

Proteomic methods serve as a potent tool for identifying proteins with differential or unique expressions, uncovering modifications in signaling pathways. The investigation of the salivary proteome has found applications in diverse biomedical fields, spanning genetics, molecular biology, medicine, and dentistry6. It offers a comprehensive identification and quantification of total proteins and their posttranslational modifications, playing a vital role in maintaining oral health. Moreover, salivary proteomics serves as valuable indicators for disease detection, allowing for a deeper understanding of the molecular signatures associated with health and potential oral pathologies7. In dentistry, the saliva proteome has been investigated in several conditions, such as periodontitis, oral squamous cell carcinoma, caries, and molar-incisor hypomineralization8–10.

To date, our understanding of the salivary proteome in individuals affected by dental fluorosis remains limited. Further exploration in this area could contribute significantly to our understanding of the molecular alterations associated with dental fluorosis and pave the way for potential diagnostic and therapeutic advancements. The hypothesis of this study was that severe dental fluorosis is associated with specific alterations in the salivary proteome and that these alterations can provide insights into the underlying mechanisms and potential biomarkers of severe dental fluorosis. The objective of this study was, therefore, to investigate the salivary proteomic profiles in Thai children and adolescents (ages 6–16 years) with severe dental fluorosis (SF) compared to those without dental fluorosis (NF) to gain a better understanding of the effects of fluoride in this age group.

Results

Participant characteristics

Subjects were categorized into two groups according to their TF fluorosis scores. The SF group (n = 10, TF scores 5–9) consisted of 5 males and 5 females (Fig. 1a–c). The NF group (n = 27, TF score 0) comprised 11 males and 16 females (Fig. 1d–f). There was no statistically significant difference in sex distribution between groups (p = 0.614). The mean age of the SF group was 10.40 ± 2.36 years, while in the NF group was 10.70 ± 2.77 years. There was no significant difference in age between groups (p = 0.256). The mean urinary fluoride of the SF group was 5.44 ± 3.39 ppm with the range of 0.70–11.40 ppm while in the NF group was 2.49 ± 1.80 ppm with the range of 0.50–6.78 ppm. The urinary fluoride in the SF group was statistically significantly higher than the NF group (p = 0.007). The water fluoride in the SF group was 1.93 ± 2.40 with the range of 0.17–5.90 ppm while in the NF group was 0.97 ± 1.82 ppm with the range of < 0.10–6.10 ppm. The water fluoride between groups was not significantly different (p = 0.191). General characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 1. Salivary proteomic profiles of the SF and NF groups

A total of 166 proteins were successfully identified and quantified. After filtering out potential contaminants and failed labeling reactions, the number of proteins was narrowed down to 157 (Supplementary Table S1). Proteins that were found in less than 3 cases in either the SF or NF group were filtered out to prevent bias associated with the exclusive presence of a protein in each group. As a result, 146 proteins remained. Of those, 110 proteins were detected in both SF and NF groups while 36 proteins were exclusively found in the NF group (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Table S2). By employing a t-test to evaluate significant differences among the 110 proteins present in both SF and NF groups, applying criteria of a ratio-fold change exceeding 1.5 and a p-value below 0.05, we identified six proteins as differentially expressed. Among the six proteins, five proteins were significantly upregulated in the SF group. These included (1) isoform 3 of L-lactate dehydrogenase A chain (LDHA), (2) ubiquitin-like modifier-activating enzyme (UBA1), (3) S100 calcium binding protein A9 (S100A9), (4) histone H4 (H4C3), and (5) plastin-2 (LCP1). Meanwhile a protein, leukocyte receptor cluster member 9 (LENG9), was significantly upregulated in the NF group compared with the SF group (Table 2, Fig. 2b–g). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicates that LDHA is associated with lactate and pyruvate metabolism, contributing to energy production, particularly in conditions of limited oxygen availability. UBA1is an enzyme plays a pivotal role in the ubiquitination process, essential for protein turnover and various cellular processes. S100A9 functions as a calcium-binding protein involved in inflammatory and immune responses. H4C3 assists in nucleosome assembly, contributing to chromatin structure and gene regulation, and LCP1 is involved in the organization of the actin cytoskeleton, playing a role in cellular structure and motility (Supplementary Table S3).

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

Among the 36 proteins exclusively found in the NF group, 11 activities within the Biological Process category exhibited a high significance, with a p-value less than 0.001. These included adaptive and innate immune responses, immunoglobulin production, Ig-mediated immune response, complement activation, B cell activation, phagocytosis, and defense response to bacterium. Similarly, in the Molecular Function category, both immunoglobulin receptor binding and antigen binding demonstrated a high degree of enrichment (p < 0.001). Regarding the Cellular Component category, the immunoglobulin complex, as well as extracellular space, secretory granule lumen, extracellular exosome, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, and plasma membrane, showed very significant enrichment (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table S4). These findings underscore that the proteins enriched in the NF group exhibit a particularly strong association with immune defense mechanisms. Furthermore, the diverse locations where these proteins were identified suggest a comprehensive involvement in various stages of immune response, ranging from extracellular interactions to intracellular processes.

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was performed on 36 identified proteins exclusive to the NF group using three pathway databases. In alignment with the GO analysis, the markedly significant pathways (p < 0.001) encompassing bacterial (Staphylococcus aureus) infection (KEGG Pathway), innate immune response, neutrophil degranulation, immune system, post-translational protein phosphorylation, insulin-like growth factor transport and uptake (Reactome Pathway), and the complement system (WikiPathways) were predominantly associated with immune response (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Table S5). These findings suggest that the saliva of the NF group demonstrated higher activity of immune-related proteins compared to the SF group.

Protein–protein interaction and network analysis.

Protein–protein interactions among the 36 identified proteins were established through STRING analysis, however, only 25 proteins were detected. Notably, HSPA8, YWHAZ, and C3 exhibited more than 5 interacting partners each, while ARPC4, IGKVID-33, SERPINA1, and APOA1 demonstrated interactions with 4 partners (Fig. 3c). Of these 7 proteins, HSPA8, YWHAZ, C3, IGKVID-33, and SERPINA1 are implicated in a diverse range of cellular behaviors and responses to various physiological challenges, including stress response (HSPA8), cell cycle regulation and signal transduction (YWHAZ), immune defense (C3 and IGKVID-33), and protease activity regulation (SERPINA1).

Regarding the six differentially expressed proteins between the SF and NF groups, STRING analysis did not reveal any protein–protein interactions. However, network analysis using Cytoscape uncovered that the five proteins significantly upregulated in the SF group—LDHA, UBA1, S100A9, H4C3, and LCP1—were connected to an ion channel, CFTR (Fig. 4). This suggests a potential association or regulatory relationship, implying that these proteins may influence or be influenced by CFTR.

Discussion

This study investigated the salivary proteome, as well as the fluoride levels in urine and drinking water among individuals with severe dental fluorosis residing in Thailand. The mean fluoride concentration in drinking water for both groups exceeds the Thailand's national standard of 0.7 mg/L11. Some children ingested water with fluoride levels of 3–5 mg/L, which exceeds the WHO recommended guideline maximum level of 1.5 mg/L for drinking water12. It is concerning that children are still being exposed to such high levels of fluoride in the water, leading to lifelong effects on their teeth. Accurately assessing fluoride intake can be challenging due to diverse sources such as water, foods, and dental products. In contrast, urinary fluoride levels have proven to be a more reliable biomarker for measuring fluoride exposure13. Moreover, a study in an endemic area demonstrated sustained high urinary fluoride levels for years after a change in the water supply system, indicating continued fluoride accumulation and release from bones and soft tissues14. The WHO recommends an 'optimal' urine fluoride level at 0.8–1.2 ppm for preventing dental issues, regardless of age15. Our study revealed significantly higher urinary fluoride levels in the SF group compared to the NF group, aligning with previous findings14. These findings affirm the reliability of urinary fluoride measurement as a biomarker for monitoring excessive fluoride exposure.

Saliva is integral to oral immune defense through antimicrobial proteins and salivary immunoglobulins16,17. Our proteomic analysis identified enriched immune-related activities in the saliva of the NF group, contrasting with the SF group where these activities were absent, indicating a compromised oral immune defense mechanism in the SF group. This observation is consistent with current knowledge regarding the effects of excessive fluoride exposure that can diminish immune function18,19.

We noted a significant upregulation of five proteins, namely LDHA, UBA1, S100A9, H4C3, and LCP1, in the SF group compared to the NF group, which could shed light on potential molecular pathways implicated in dental fluorosis. LDHA plays a crucial role in the metabolic shifting from the oxidative phosphorylation toward the aerobic glycolysis and high expression of LDHA has been observed in highly glycolytic human cancers20. A recent study in dental fluorosis mouse models demonstrated a correlation between the glycolysis/gluconeogenesis pathway and dental fluorosis21. These findings suggest a potential link between altered glycolytic processes, exemplified by the upregulation of LDHA, and the pathogenesis of dental fluorosis. UBA1, a multifunctional regulator, influences cellular processes such as gene transcription, DNA repair, and apoptosis22. An upregulation of UBA1 induces ubiquitylation, potentially impacts gene transcription, DNA repair, immune signaling, and apoptosis23. S100A9 plays a significant role in modulating inflammatory processes including periodontitis and the early stages of cancer development through mechanisms such as autophagy and apoptosis24–26. In a fluoride-endemic region in Kenya, a study revealed a positive correlation between esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) and dental fluorosis, accompanied by an upregulation of S100A927,28. The upregulation of S100A9 found in our SF group suggests its potential as a biomarker for inflammation in fluorosis, aligning with its previously observed associations with periodontitis and ESCC. Additionally, an increase in LCP1 has been associated with periodontal inflammation29, reinforcing the connection between the modified salivary proteins in SF and oral inflammation. Additionally, chronic exposure of fluoride has been shown to induce DNA hypermethylation, affecting epigenetic process, and compromised DNA repair machinery30–32. H4C3's involvement in chromatin structure, histone modification, and gene regulation suggests a possible link between fluoride exposure and disruptions in gene expression and epigenetic regulation during tooth development33. Our results underscore the complex molecular interconnections associated with dental fluorosis, urging further research to delineate their exact roles and implications.

Our network analysis revealed a significant association between five upregulated proteins in the SF group and the CFTR protein, an ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporter acting as an ion channel34. Notably, CFTR is expressed in various exocrine glands, including the salivary glands, as well as in ameloblasts, odontoblasts, and bone cells35,36. A previous study has demonstrated that high-dose and chronic fluoride exposure can induce oxidative stress, alter gene expression in submandibular glands, and compromise DNA integrity in animal models37. Additionally, transplacental fluoride transfer in pregnant rats has resulted in reduced antioxidant capacity and morphological changes in offspring salivary glands38. These findings suggest that high fluoride exposure might affect salivary gland function even in the absence of clinically apparent disease. Individuals with cystic fibrosis, characterized by biallelic mutations in the CFTR gene, often exhibit enamel anomalies, with reported incidences reaching up to 56%36,39. Similar enamel defects have been observed in Cftr knockout mice40. This suggests a potential link between CFTR dysfunction and enamel abnormalities. Given the identified association between the upregulated proteins (LDHA, UBA1, S100A9, H4C3, and LCP1) and CFTR, excessive fluoride exposure may potentially alter CFTR function. This interaction could occur through mechanisms such as altered gene transcription, epigenetic modifications, and DNA repair pathways, as previously discussed. These changes could disrupt enamel formation by affecting ameloblasts and/or impact salivary gland function, leading to alterations in salivary protein homeostasis. Such disturbances could, in turn, trigger the aforementioned mechanisms related to the development of dental fluorosis. To comprehensively understand the complex relationship between these identified salivary proteins and dental fluorosis, further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and explore potential therapeutic interventions related to fluoride-induced enamel defects.

Our study acknowledged several potential limitations. Firstly, the participants were exposed to multiple fluoride sources, including drinking water, food, and groundwater used for irrigation, making it challenging to isolate the specific impact of fluoride from drinking water on salivary proteomic changes. Additionally, the duration of exposure to high fluoride levels during tooth formation, a critical period, could have influenced the observed proteomic changes. Furthermore, while we aimed to investigate the correlation between salivary proteomic changes and dental fluorosis, other immune or epigenetic factors associated with high fluoride exposure could also contribute to the observed proteomic alterations. Finally, the study was conducted in a specific population with high fluoride exposure, necessitating further research to confirm the generalizability of these findings to other populations with varying fluoride levels and exposure patterns. Despite these limitations, the salivary proteomic findings presented in this study highlight the significant potential of saliva-based proteomics as a valuable tool for evaluating the impact of dental fluorosis and its clinical applications. This approach holds promise for revolutionizing how we diagnose, monitor, and treat dental fluorosis. First, saliva-based proteomics could serve as a valuable diagnostic tool for differentiating dental fluorosis from other causes of tooth discoloration, enabling more accurate treatment planning. Secondly, this method could facilitate early detection and monitoring of dental fluorosis, potentially allowing for timely interventions to prevent more severe cases. Finally, understanding how saliva protein expression is affected by different fluoride levels could inform the development of personalized fluoride supplementation strategies, tailored to individual patient risk factors and susceptibility to fluorosis. By recognizing the potential of salivary proteomics in these areas, this research contributes to a more comprehensive understanding and management of dental fluorosis, ultimately improving patient care. Additionally, our study emphasizes the significance of urinary fluoride levels as surveillance tools for public health. The study was conducted in areas where public water supply systems are inadequately organised, and residents rely on natural water sources for drinking, cooking, and plant irrigation. This is particularly relevant for marginalized communities lacking proper water supply infrastructure, affecting impoverished populations, and heightening the risk of fluoride toxicity. Addressing this issue is crucial not only for safeguarding public health but also for mitigating disparities in access to clean water. Furthermore, targeted health literacy efforts should be promoted to enhance awareness and understanding of the potential risks associated with fluoride exposure.

In summary, our study revealed significant changes in salivary proteins in the SF group, supporting our hypothesis that severe dental fluorosis is associated with distinct alterations in the salivary proteome. These findings suggest a possible compromise in the immune response and the potential involvement of CFTR-related proteins. The study proposes a role for ion channels and potentially implicates epigenetics, DNA repair, and gene regulation in severe fluorosis.

Materials and methods

Subject recruitment and study design

Participants were recruited from Lamphun and Ratchaburi provinces, which are fluoride-endemic areas of Thailand, using a systematic and targeted approach during December 2021 and January 2022. Initially, schools and community health centers in these provinces were contacted to identify potential participants aged 6–16 years. Information sessions were held to explain the study's purpose, procedures, and potential benefits to both parents and children. Following these sessions, informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians, and assent from the children. Oral examination was conducted to identify individuals with and without severe dental fluorosis (SF and NF groups). The inclusion criteria for the case group in this study comprised individuals from the Thai population exhibiting severe dental fluorosis, as determined by the Thylstrup & Fejerskov (TF) fluorosis index ranging from 5 to 9, referred to as the "SF" group. A consensus score for TF index was given for each subject by two examiners (P.G. and P.A), the final score for TF index was recorded directly by the interface excel file. The inter-observer reliability test for the TF index involved two examiners (P.G. and P.A.), who independently evaluated all participants. The intra-observer reliability test was conducted by having both examiners re-evaluate all participants at a minimum 2-week interval. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was utilized to assess both inter- and intra-observer reliability and the ICC scores of 0.989 for inter-observer reliability, 0.999 for intra-observer 1 reliability, and 1.000 for intra-observer 2 reliability were observed, indicating excellent agreement and high consistency in the measurements. The criteria for excluding subjects from this study encompassed individuals with clinical dental caries, systemic conditions, amelogenesis imperfecta, a recent history of antibiotic, antifungal, antiviral, or steroid usage, or mouthwash use within the 3 months preceding sample collection. Furthermore, participants were excluded if they exhibited signs of periodontitis or mucosal lesions, were smokers or alcohol consumers, or had used narcotic drugs. The control group, denoted as the "NF" group, consisted of healthy children within the same age range who did not exhibit dental fluorosis (TF index of 0). Participants were selected based on these records and their willingness to participate.

Ethical aspects and sample collection

The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Dentistry, Chulalongkorn University, Thailand (HREC-DCU 2021-061, approved on 1 October 2021) and in accordance with the 1964 Helsinki declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consents were obtained from children and their guardians prior to participation. Saliva and urine samples were then collected during scheduled visits to the schools, ensuring a controlled and consistent sample collection process.

Sample collection and fluoride quantification in urine samples

24-h urine samples were collected from each participant in a 2.7 L urine container. On the morning of the collection, the subject voided the first urine and then urinated into the wide-necked collection container, and then the urine was poured into the screw-capped collection bottle using the funnel. During transportation of urine, the collection bottle was kept at 4 °C. The amount of fluoride was evaluated using a fluoride electrode (4-star benchtop, Orion, USA) and TISAB III, containing CDTA (cyclohexylenedinitrilotetraacetate), sodium hydroxide, sodium chloride, and acetic acid (ethanoic acid) dissolved in deionized water at the Intercountry Centre for Oral Health, Chiangmai, Thailand.

Sampling and fluoride analysis in water samples

The participants were asked to collect the most consumption drinking-water in their household in 60 ml clean bottles for approximately 5/6 full. The samples were transported to the laboratory at Intercountry Centre for Oral Health, Chiangmai, Thailand for fluoride measurement. The amount of fluoride was measured using TISAB III and the fluoride electrode (4-star benchtop, Orion, USA).

Saliva collection

The subjects were instructed to refrain from all oral hygiene procedures, eating, drinking, and brushing in the morning of collection day. The collection was taken between 8 to 10 A.M., 12 h after the last meal. The participants were instructed to rinse their mouth with 10 ml of distilled water for 30 s to remove debris and moisturize the mucosa. Saliva samples, approximately 3 mL each, were collected in sterile tubes, placed in ice cooling containers, and stored at − 20 °C within 3 h after collection.

Mass spectrometry

Saliva samples were centrifuged at 14,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. Proteins from 1 mL of supernatant were precipitated with 100 μL of 100% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) on ice for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 20,000×g for 20 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was washed with 500 μL of acetone, vortexed, and then centrifuged at 20,000×g for 10 min. This washing procedure was repeated twice. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 300 μL of 8 M urea in 100 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) containing Halt protease inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA).

Protein samples (100 μL) were combined with 10 μL of dithiothreitol (DTT) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Following this, samples were treated with 40 μL of 100 mM iodoacetamide (IA) in a dark environment at room temperature for 30 min. Afterward, 40 μL of 100 mM DTT was added and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for a minimum of 15 min. The samples were then diluted with 100 mM TEAB to reduce urea concentration from 8 M to below 1 M. Subsequently, trypsin was added at a 1:50 (w/w) ratio to the samples, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The samples were added with 100% TFA to final concentration of 0.5% TFA, incubated for 15 min at room temperature, and dried using Speed-Vac. The quantity of tryptic peptides was measured with the Pierce Quantitative Fluorometric Peptide Assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). The samples were labeled with TMT10plexTM Isobaric Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Labeled peptide samples were fractionated using peptide fractionation by a High pH Reversed-Phase Peptide Fractionation Kit (Pierce). Peptide samples were dried and stored at − 80 °C. Before analysis, the subjected were resuspended with 0.1% formic acid (FA) and analyzed using liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using the EASY-nLC1000 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA), connected to a Q-Exactive Orbitrap Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA) equipped using a 25 cm EASY-Spray C18 column, 75 μm internal diameter. The flow rate during the gradient was kept constant at 300 nL/min. Peptides were injected and eluted by a 90 min gradient (0–5% B in 0 min, 5–20% B in 60 min, 20–40% B in 23 min, 40–95% B in 2 min, and 95% B in 5 min). The electrospray voltage 2.0 kV was applied. Orbitrap precursor spectra (350–1,400 Da) were recorded using a resolution of 70,000 with a AGC target at 3 × 106 ions and a maxIT 250 ms. The 10 most abundant ions per scan were selected and fragmented by normalized collision energy N(CE) 32 for MS/MS at 35,000 resolutions with AGC target of 1e5 and max ion injection time of 100 ms.

Data analysis and visualization

SPSS software version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed for statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics were used to report demographic data and fluoride concentrations in water and urine samples. The normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Nonparametric Mann–Whitney U tests were utilized to identify differences of urine and water fluoride concentrations between the SF and NF groups, while an independent t-test was employed to indicate differences of age between two groups. The Chi-square test was applied to analyse differences in proportion of sex. Significance was set at a threshold of p-value < 0.05.

For proteomic analysis, the mass-spectrometry raw files were searched against the human Swiss-Prot Database, and a list of common protein contaminants, such as keratin, was included. The data were processed using Proteome Discoverer™ Software 2.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mass tolerances for precursor and fragment ions were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da. The false discovery rates (FDR) based on Q-values of the identified peptides were set to 1%. The reporter ion intensity ratios for the SF group versus the NF group were transformed to log2. The p-values were calculated via independent t-tests based on at least three valid log2 values per group. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (FDR) was used to control the false discovery rate when analyzing the differentially expressed proteins (p-adj value). Proteins with a ratio-fold change > 1.5 and p-adj value < 0.05 were considered statistically different.

For functional interpretation of the discovered proteins, the selected proteins underwent analysis using the online resource database, DAVID Bioinformatics Resources (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) and Cytoscape (Version 3.10.0, https://cytoscape.org/). This analysis focused on examining Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment, exploring functional terms that describe the biological process, molecular function, and cellular component41. Pathway analysis was performed using KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) (https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.htmlX)42–44, Reactome (https://reactome.org/), and Wiki Pathway (https://www.wikipathways.org/) platforms. The STRING (https://string-db.org/) and Cytoscape (V3.10.0) resources were used to investigate protein–protein interactions (PIPs), and network analysis, respectively.
