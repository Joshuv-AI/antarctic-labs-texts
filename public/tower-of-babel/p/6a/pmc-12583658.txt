The association between serum uric acid and homocysteine is mediated by metabolic risk factors
Authors: Linlin Zhao, Hui Zhou, Zhilin Xiao, Xinjian Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have revealed a link between serum uric acid (SUA) and homocysteine (Hcy) levels, but it is unclear whether such relationship can be mediated by metabolic risk factors. This study evaluated the relationship between SUA and Hcy levels through a mediation analysis, examining the extent to which metabolic risk factors mediate this association. A total of 16,870 participants aged 20–60 years was consecutively enrolled at the Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Data on health behaviours and medical history were collected through self-reported health questionnaires. Laboratory examinations were conducted by trained personnel via standardized methods. Logistic regression models were used to evaluate the associations between SUA and Hcy levels. Mediation analysis was conducted to explore the possible impact of metabolic risk factors on the SUA-Hcy relationship. There was a significant dose‒response relationship between higher SUA quartiles and increased hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy) risk (P for trend < 0.01). Mediation analysis indicated that the relationship between SUA and HHcy was partially mediated by triglyceride (TG, 10.78%), diastolic blood pressure (DBP, 10.12%), systolic blood pressure (SBP, 7.63%), body mass index (BMI, 6.02%), and total cholesterol (TC, 0.56%); high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) negatively mediated this association, accounting for 13.03% of the effect. SUA is positively associated with HHcy risk and potentially mediated by metabolic factors such as HDL-C, TG, DBP, SBP, and BMI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22270-1.

Introduction

Homocysteine (Hcy), a nonessential amino acid with a sulfhydryl group, is produced during methionine metabolism. Plasma Hcy levels exceeding 15.0 μmol/L are classified as hyperhomocysteinemia (HHcy), reflecting potential disturbances in methionine–homocysteine metabolic pathways. Various factors can affect Hcy metabolism, including deficiencies in dietary folate and vitamins B12, B6, and B2, as well as genetic mutations or polymorphisms in enzymes involved in Hcy metabolism1. Elevated plasma Hcy levels are linked to significant health risks, such as cardiovascular diseases and neurodegenerative disorders2. Hcy induces oxidative stress, causing organ damage and apoptosis by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and forming disulfides with thiol-containing molecules3. Furthermore, recent studies have highlighted the role of Hcy in promoting colorectal cancer by impairing DNA damage repair mechanisms4. The early identification of modifiable risk factors is essential.

Recent studies have concentrated on the link between serum uric acid (SUA) and Hcy, emphasizing their common metabolic pathways and impact on cardiovascular health. SUA is recognized for its dual role as both an antioxidant and a pro-oxidant, affecting various physiological and pathological processes5; it is produced through the metabolism of purines and is primarily excreted by the kidneys. Several studies have investigated the relationship between SUA and Hcy. A cross-sectional study of 17,692 adults revealed that SUA levels are positively correlated with Hcy levels, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was 0.11(P < 0.01) in men and 0.23 (P < 0.01) in women6. This relationship has also been observed in patients with gout7 and adolescents8. The pathway connecting SUA to Hcy is not yet fully understood. SUA is linked to several metabolic risk factors, including blood pressure, lipids, glucose, and obesity9,10. This association raises the question of whether SUA may indirectly contribute to elevated Hcy levels through these metabolic risk factors.

This study examined, via mediation analysis, the association between SUA and Hcy levels in individuals undergoing health checkups to determine the extent to which metabolic risk factors mediate this relationship. Understanding these mechanisms is essential for the development of targeted interventions aimed at mitigating the health risks associated with elevated Hcy levels.

Materials and methods

Participants and procedures

A total of 16,870 individuals aged 20–60 years participated in routine physical examinations at the Third Xiangya Hospital, Central South University, Changsha, China, from January 2020 to January 2021. Individuals with severe renal or liver diseases, as well as those on SUA- or Hcy-lowering medications, were excluded from the study. To avoid duplicate entries, the first test record from each year was chosen for participants with multiple annual tests. Figure 1 provides a flowchart detailing the participant selection process. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Third Xiangya Hospital (Approval No. 2019-S451). Informed written consent was obtained from all participants, who did not receive any form of compensation.Fig. 1Flow diagram for patient inclusion.

Anthropometric and laboratory measurements

Anthropometric measurements and laboratory analyses followed the methodology of a prior study11, whereas self-reported health questionnaires were used to gather data on health behaviours, medical history, and medication use through an online platform. Participants were classified into never smokers (those who had quit for more than a year or never smoked) and current smokers on the basis of smoking status. The categorization of drinking status was delineated into two groups: nondrinkers, defined as individuals who abstained from alcohol consumption for over one year or had never consumed alcohol, and current drinkers. Physical activity was categorized as regular if it was performed three or more times weekly and nonregular if it was performed less frequently. Sleep quality was evaluated and classified into three levels: good, fair, or poor. Fasting morning blood samples were obtained from the antecubital vein following an overnight fasting period of 8 to 12 h. Hcy was performed by the enzyme circulation method and Mike kit (Mike Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China), with HHcy defined as Hcy levels exceeding 15 µmol/L in various analyses. Additionally, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), fasting blood glucose (FBG), albumin (ALB) were measured using reagents from Leadman Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China); Serum urea nitrogen (BUN), SUA, serum creatinine (SCr) were measured using reagents from Mike Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was measured using reagents purchased from Meikang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Ningbo, Zhejiang, China). These indicators were measured using a Hitachi 7170S autoanalyzer (Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Other clinical data collection

Body weight and height were measured using a computer human scale (SK-L09B, Shenzhen Shuangjia Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Guangdong, China), with precisions of 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm. Height and weight were measured without shoes and with light clothing. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated using BMI = weight (kg)/height2 (m2). After a 15-min rest, skilled medical personnel measured seated blood pressure using an electronic sphygmomanometer (Omron 9020), and the average of three readings was recorded for analysis.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed via SPSS (Version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The participants were categorized into hyperuricaemic and nonhyperuricaemic groups. Data were evaluated for skewness, kurtosis, and normality using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Continuous variables that fit a normal distribution are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) and were analysed via independent t-tests, while those that did not are presented as medians and interquartile range (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and were analysed via Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies (percentages) and were analysed via the chi-square test. The relationships among SUA, metabolic risk factors, and Hcy levels were examined via Spearman correlation analysis. Additionally, logistic regression models were utilized to evaluate the associations between SUA and Hcy. Prior research has demonstrated a strong association between age, sex and obesity with SUA13,14 and Hcy15,16 levels. Furthermore, lifestyle factors, including smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep patterns, and physical activity, have been significantly correlated with SUA17 and Hcy18. In the present study, we concentrated on the mediating role of metabolic indices in the relationship between SUA and Hcy. Consequently, we controlled for age, sex, BMI, current smoking and drinking status, physical activity, systolic blood pressure, FBG, TC, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, history of hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, and medication use. Trend tests were performed in logistic regression models by using the median SUA values for each quartile as continuous variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for the risk of HHcy linked to a one standard deviation (SD) increase in SUA.

Given the potential nonlinear relationship between SUA, metabolic factors and Hcy, standard linear models may lead to model misspecification. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were chosen to flexibly capture potential nonlinear patterns without overfitting. We analyzed the nonlinear associations between Hcy and various factors, including SUA, BMI, SBP, DBP, FBG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG. In RCS, four knots were used, located at the 5th, 35th, 65th and 95th percentiles, as previously suggested19. A restricted cubic spline analysis was conducted utilizing Harrell’s Regression Modeling Strategies (rms) package. Linear regression was used to analyse the associations between SUA and metabolic risk factors, whereas logistic regression was used to evaluate the relationships between these risk factors and Hcy. After confirming the link between SUA and Hcy, a mediation analysis was performed to assess whether metabolic risk factors mediated this relationship. Mediation analysis was performed via PROCESS version 3.5 for SPSS, adjusting for the specified covariates. A P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

STROBE guidelines

The study adheres to the STROBE guidelines.

Results

Population characteristics

The final analysis included a total of 16,870 participants, comprising 10,376 men and 6,494 women. The participants were divided into two groups according to the presence or absence of HHcy. Among the study participants, 24.45% (n = 4125) met the diagnostic criteria for HHcy, with 3676 (35.43%) being men and 449 (6.91%) being women. This study examined the associations between SUA and Hcy levels. The dependent variable was whether the participant was diagnosed with HHcy (Hcy > 15 μmol/L). SUA quartiles were defined as the independent variables (using the first quartile as the control group). Table 3 shows that higher SUA quartiles were correlated with an increased risk of HHcy in Model 1, indicating a dose‒response relationship (P for trend < 0.01). Compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1, < 278.00 μmol/L), participants in the highest SUA quartile (Q4, > 399.00 μmol/L) had a significantly increased risk of HHcy (OR 6.00; 95% CI 5.33–6.77). After adjusting for age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, SBP, BMI, FBG, TG, medication use, and disease in Model 3, the risk remained significant, albeit slightly lower (OR 1.74; 95% CI 1.50–2.02), with a trend P value < 0.01. In adjusted Model 3, each one-standard deviation increase in SUA was correlated with a 1.26-fold increase in the risk of HHcy (95% CI: 1.20–1.32). To confirm the robustness of these findings, we investigated the potential nonlinear relationship between SUA and HHcy risk. As seen in Fig. 2A, RCS models revealed a significant nonlinear positive correlation between SUA and HHcy risk (P overall < 0.01; P nonlinear < 0.01). The characteristic of the nonlinear relationship between SUA and HHcy was a smooth curve that rises without a clear peak or trough (Fig. 2). SBP (Fig. 2C) and DBP (Fig. 2D) showed similar trends. Higher plasma levels of SBP and DBP were similarly linked to an elevated risk of HHcy. SUA < 340 mmol/L, SBP < 120 mmHg, and DBP < 80 mmHg were protective factors for HHcy (OR < 1). The relationships between BMI (Fig. 2B), TC (Fig. 2F), LDL-C (Fig. 2G), and HHcy showed S-shaped patterns. The OR for developing HHcy tended to increase with increasing BMI and TC levels when these levels were lower (BMI < 29.93 kg/m2, TC < 6.70 mmol/L), while a negative correlation with HHcy risk occurred when the levels were higher (BMI ≥ 29.93 kg/m2, TC ≥ 6.70 mmol/L Fig. 2B, F). LDL-C displayed an inverse trend; a negative correlation was observed in its lower and higher ranges, respectively (Fig. 2G). FBG and HHcy showed an approximately U-shaped relationship, with the OR value being lowest when the FBG level was 5 mmol/L (Fig. 2E). The nonlinear relationships between HDL-C, TG, and HHcy showed a clear peak or valley. Discussion

This study revealed that participants with HHcy had elevated SUA levels and that higher SUA quartiles were correlated with increased HHcy risk. The dose‒response relationship between SUA and HHcy risk was depicted via restricted cubic splines. The underlying mechanisms connecting these two metabolites may involve oxidative stress20,21, inflammation5,22, endothelial dysfunction, and insulin resistance (IR)23,24. Research indicates that hyperuricaemia can lead to oxidative stress, which may contribute to endothelial dysfunction and subsequently influence Hcy metabolism25. A previous study indicated that the deletion of CR6 interacting factor 1 (CRIF1) in endothelial cells leads to increased Hcy production, an effect that is mainly due to disrupted dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR) expression25. Hcy and increased SUA levels are also associated with IR. Elevated SUA is identified as a risk factor for IR. Research indicates that higher SUA levels are correlated with increased IR and metabolic syndrome, particularly in overweight and obese individuals26,27. Research has indicated that allopurinol treatment enhances insulin sensitivity and decreases systemic inflammation in patients with asymptomatic hyperuricaemia28. The relationship between IR and Hcy metabolism is complex. For example, elevated Hcy levels can inhibit insulin receptor cleavage, thereby exacerbating IR29. Additionally, HHcy has been linked to increased inflammation and oxidative stress, which are also key factors in the development of IR30. Furthermore, specific metabolic pathways, such as those involving betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase and cystathionine β-synthase, are significantly altered in individuals with IR, leading to decreased efficiency in Hcy metabolism31. Additionally, elevated Hcy levels are linked to impaired renal function32, affecting SUA excretion and potentially creating a feedback loop between these metabolites. Moreover, diet and genetics can affect SUA and Hcy levels33–35. Gene polymorphisms related to urate transport and metabolism can alter SUA and possibly Hcy levels36. The intake of B vitamins, which are important for Hcy metabolism, can also impact both Hcy and UA levels37.

Several studies have identified a correlation between SUA and Hcy, with evidence suggesting that this association exhibits gender-specific differences. One potential explanation for these differences is the influence of estrogen, which is inversely related to both SUA and Hcy levels38,39. Estrogen facilitates the metabolism of Hcy by enhancing the activity of betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase40. Furthermore, estrogen plays a crucial role in renal protection by inhibiting the urate reabsorptive transporter (URAT1) in the kidneys, thereby promoting increased SUA excretion and subsequently reducing SUA levels41.

The current study shows that higher SUA quartiles were correlated with an increased risk of HHcy. To mitigate concerns about potential bias arising from unmeasured confounding, we conducted a sensitivity analysis employing the E-value methodology. The computed E-value was 3.04, this suggests that a moderately strong, unmeasured confounder would be necessary to invalidate the observed association, indicating that our observed results exhibit a degree of robustness against potential unmeasured confounding. However, it is important to acknowledge that the cumulative impact of multiple unmeasured variables could potentially account for this association.

Various metabolic factors that could mediate the relationship between SUA and Hcy. In this study, a mediation analysis was conducted to examine whether metabolic factors mediate the associations between SUA and Hcy. These findings indicate that TG, TC, HDL-C, BMI, SBP, and DBP are essential in these pathways. Elevated SUA levels are associated with lipid metabolism disorders, as hyperuricaemic patients have significantly higher serum TG levels than healthy individuals do, with 47% of hyperuricaemic patients experiencing hypertriglyceridaemia compared with 5% of individuals with normal SUA levels experiencing hypertriglyceridaemia42. The mechanisms involve IR, fat accumulation, and inflammatory responses. Excessive SUA production and oxidative stress can result in liver inflammation and damage, leading to hepatic lipid metabolism dysfunction. Interestingly, we did not find a correlation between SUA and LDL-C. This finding aligns with multiple studies examining the link between SUA levels and lipid profiles, especially LDL-C43. For example, research on overweight and obese Chinese adults indicates that there are no significant associations between elevated SUA and metabolic syndrome components, including LDL-C44. With respect to lipids and Hcy, in the current study, all the significant correlations indicated that increasing Hcy levels were linked to lipid dysfunction; The mediation analysis revealed that HDL-C (mediation proportion = 13.03%) and TG (mediation proportion = 10.78%) had greater mediation effects than did TC (mediation proportion = 0.56%) and LDL-C (mediation proportion = 0.06%). HDL-C exhibits anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-thrombotic effects45, potentially reducing Hcy levels. The association between TG and Hcy may arise from inflammation, oxidative stress, and IR mediated by TG46.

With respect to FBG, correlation analysis and regression analysis revealed that an association existed between FBG and both SUA and Hcy, all of which reached significance. However, the mediation effect failed to reach significance. The results of this study indicate that the ability of FBG to elucidate the relationship between SUA and Hcy is limited, necessitating further investigation for possible explanations. Numerous studies have established a link between SUA and the risk of hypertension47. The higher the SUA level is, even when it is within the normal range, the greater the risk of developing hypertension. Elevated SUA contributes to hypertension by activating the renin‒angiotensin system (RAS), which reduces renal blood flow and causes endothelial dysfunction48. Additionally, an animal study demonstrated that increased SUA levels in rats activate the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC) in the distal nephron, decreasing renal sodium excretion and subsequently increasing blood pressure49. Previous studies have also shown a significant association between elevated blood pressure and Hcy50. It is widely recognized that chronic hypertension can lead to a decline in renal function. This decline may not only impair the renal clearance of homocysteine due to reduced plasma flow but also influence homocysteine metabolism by affecting its remethylation, a principal pathway in homocysteine degradation. It is widely recognized that chronic hypertension can lead to a decline in renal function51. This decline may not only impair the renal clearance of Hcy due to reduced plasma flow but also influence Hcy metabolism by affecting its remethylation, a principal pathway in Hcy degradation52,53. BMI, an indicator of adiposity, was linked to increased obesity risk due to high SUA levels in a longitudinal clinical trial involving 4411 obese subjects54. Elevated SUA levels have been linked to increased oxidative stress, a primary mechanism contributing to obesity55. Obese individuals exhibit various indicators of oxidative stress, such as elevated ROS levels and diminished antioxidant defences56. In individuals with obesity, SUA can become an oxidant, directly contributing to fat proliferation and oxidative stress. Additionally, oxidative stress can also promote homocysteine transsulfation and glutathione oxidation which could lead to increased Hcy levels and related complications57,58.

Limitations

Firstly, the cross-sectional design of the study prevented us from establishing a causal link between SUA and Hcy levels. Second, the study did not include measurements of folate, vitamin B6, or vitamin B12 levels, which are known to influence Hcy levels. Additionally, the study population was derived from a population undergoing physical examinations, and the lack of external validation necessitates cautious interpretation when generalizing the findings to broader populations. Finally, the health behaviors and medication use were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias.

Conclusions

In summary, the study revealed a significant link between SUA and Hcy, which can aid in preventing and managing cardiovascular diseases. The findings revealed that metabolic factors, including HDL-C, TG, and DBP, need to be an area of focus because of their mediating effects.
