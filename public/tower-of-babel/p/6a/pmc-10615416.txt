Air tamponade in vitrectomies for primary rhegmatogenous retinal detachment caused by superior breaks
Authors: Lin Zheng, Chan Wu, Mingyue Luo, Xinyu Zhao, Nan Zhao, Youxin Chen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The effectiveness of filtered air tamponade for superior retinal breaks was well established. This study was performed to compare the treatment efficacy of pars plana vitrectomies (PPV) with filtered air and silicone oil (SO) for patients with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD) caused by superior breaks with no or mild proliferative vitreoretinopathy. Patients of RRD with superior breaks who underwent PPV with filtered air (Group A) and SO (Group S) tamponade were reviewed retrospectively. Age, gender, laterality, lens status, duration of symptoms, macular status, proliferative vitreoretinopathy grade, use of perfluorocarbon liquid, early and late postoperative complications, follow-up duration were acquired. The primary anatomic reattachment after the first surgery and the final rate of successful reattachment was compared as the main outcome. Secondary outcomes were long-term postoperative best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), rate of deferred cataract removal, surgical complications and total surgery number. The primary anatomic success rate was 88% (14/16 eyes) in Group A and 100% (16/16 eyes) in group S, which was not significantly different (P = .484). Both groups achieved 100% final anatomic success. The rate of cataract removal was 57.1% and 100% (P = .016), and the duration from first surgery to cataract surgery was 231.38 ± 241.23 and 156.36 ± 110.09 days (P = .428) for group A and group S, respectively. The rate of postoperative epiretinal membrane was 21.4% vs 25.0% (P = 1.000). Postoperative BCVA was associated with preoperative BCVA after multiple linear analysis. The primary and final anatomic success rate for PPV with air tamponade and SO in treating RRD with superior breaks were not statistically different. The rate of deferred cataract removal was higher in patients with SO as tamponade.

1. Introduction

In cases of rhegmatogenous retinal detachment (RRD), intraocular tamponade after air-fluid exchange at the end of pars plana vitrectomies (PPV) serves as a barrier preventing fluid tracking through retinal breaks until chorioretinal adhesion is established. The choice of tamponade includes inert expensile gas, filtered air (FA) and silicone oil (SO), which depends on the extent of detachment and whether the case is complicated with severe proliferative vitreoretinopathy (PVR), giant retinal tears and trauma.[1,2]

Compared with expansile gas, FA tamponade stays for a shorter time, shortening the period of postoperative forced positioning and visual recovery, thus is favored in less complicated cases. Indeed, various studies have explored the efficacy of air tamponade (AT) in PPV for RRD patients. AT demonstrated high anatomic reattachment rate in RRD with inferior breaks in a non-comparative[3] and a randomized controlled study,[4] while Tan et al[5] showed AT had a significant lower primary success rate compared expansile gas in RRD involving the inferior quadrants, thus recommended AT for superior RRDs only. Although its use in inferior RRD remains controversial, the application of AT in superior breaks is well-established. Zhang et al[6] retrospectively compared the anatomic and functional outcomes of partial and whole AT in RRD with superior breaks, and found partial AT was no less effective in anatomic success rate, with quicker postoperative vision improvement, and lower rate of postoperative cataract surgery. All these findings supported the use of AT in RRD with superior breaks.

In 2016, a batch of perfluoropropane with quality problems caused permanent vision loss or blindness in over 50 patients receiving PPVs in China, which subsequently resulted in national shortage of expansile gas for about 3 years. During this period, options of intraocular tamponade in PPV for Chinese vitreoretinal surgeons were limited to SO and air. In this period, we did PPVs for patients of RRD caused by superior retinal breaks with no or mild PVR but not fit for scleral buckling or encircling such as those with vitreous hemorrhage and extensive RD. This study aimed to compare the treatment efficacy of PPVs with FA and SO for these patients.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study. The protocol was approved by the Peking Union Medical College Hospital Review Board (S-K1907) and was conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The electronic medical records of all patients with RRD admitted into Peking Union Medical College Hospital and later treated by a single surgeon (L.Z.) were collected. Cases from January 1, 2016 to December 30, 2020, were reviewed retrospectively. If the patient had cataract that impeded the observation of posterior segment, cataract surgery in combination with vitrectomy was strongly suggested. The surgical options, especially the difference of FA and SO as well as the necessity to combine with cataract surgery were fully informed and the final decision was made by the doctor after discussion with the patients. Written informed consent for surgical treatment was obtained from each patient. All patients underwent a standard, 3-port, 23/25-gauge pars plana vitrectomy with whole FA tamponade (Group A) or SO (Group S, Oxane 5700, Bausch & Lomb, Kingston-upon-Thames, UK). To ensure AT was adequate, pars plana injection of FA was performed if IOP was low with digital tonometry after incision suture until IOP was normal. Severity of PVR was classified according to the Retina Society 1983 classification.[7] The inclusion criteria were: eyes with primary RRD caused by a single superior retinal break (Fig. 1) or multiple breaks within 10 to 2 o’clock (Figs. 2 and 3) and treated by PPV with AT or SO as tamponade; PVR grade A to B. The exclusion criteria were: RRD caused by retinal breaks in other quadrants; PVR grade C to D; prior intraocular surgeries other than routine cataract surgeries; comorbid with other serious eye diseases; follow-up period <3 months; incomplete data.

2.2. Measurements and study endpoint

All eligible subjects underwent comprehensive ophthalmic examinations prior to surgery and during follow-up, including preoperative Snellen best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), non-contact tonometry (Canon TX-20P), slit-lamp microscopy, dilated funduscopic examination. Age, gender, laterality, lens status, duration of symptoms (acute-onset floaters or visual field defect), location of the retinal break, macular involvement, PVR grade, the use of perfluorocarbon liquid, early and late postoperative complications, reattachment state of the retina and long-term postoperative Snellen BCVA were also recorded. For statistical analysis, the Snellen BCVA was converted to a logarithm of the minimal angle of resolution (logMAR). The main outcomes were the primary and final anatomic success rates. Secondary outcomes were long-term postoperative BCVA, rate of deferred cataract removal, surgical complications and total surgery number.

2.3. Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed with SPSS 22 (IBM, Inc., Chicago, IL). Normally distributed continuous data were presented as the mean and standard deviation or as the median and interquartile range (IQR) in the case of abnormal distribution. Categorical variables were assessed using Fisher exact test. Continuous variables were assessed with t test or Mann–Whitney U test in case of abnormal distribution. Variables with a P value < .05 in univariate analysis were included in multivariate linear regression model. A P value < .05 was considered significant in all aforementioned analysis.

3. Results

Thirty-two eyes of 32 patients were included in the study, with 16 and 16 eyes each group. The follow-up period was 487.88 ± 306.36 and 457.31 ± 244.39 (P = .757) for Group A and Group S respectively.

The primary anatomic success rate was 88% (14/16 eyes) in Group A and 100% (16/16 eyes) in group S; this difference was not statistically significant (P = .484, Fisher exact test). Both the 2 redetachment cases of Group A underwent secondary PPV with SO tamponade and a third surgery to remove SO after the retinal condition became stable.

Complications within and after 3 months since the initial surgery were summarized as early and late complications in Table 2. Two patients of Group A experienced RD recurrence about 1 month after the initial surgery, and 1 patient experienced mild vitreous hemorrhage without RD. No patients of either groups experienced postoperative intraocular hyper- or hypo-tension. All patients successfully reattached the retina and SO was successfully removed in subsequent surgeries. One patient in Group A and 2 in Group S had phacovitrectomies, and one in Group A was pseudophakic, which left each group 14 phakic eyes for postoperative observation of cataract development. In terms of late complications, 8 of 14 cases (57.1%) of Group A required cataract removal after initial PPV, compared to 14 of 14 cases (100%) of Group S (P = .016, Fisher exact test). The duration of first PPV to cataract removal was 231.38 ± 241.23 days for group A and 156.36 ± 110.09 days for group S (P = .428, t test). The duration of SO tamponade for group S was 152.00 ± 95.79 days. The rate of postoperative epiretinal membrane (ERM) was 21.4% (3/14 eyes, 2 eyes with preoperative ERM) in Group A and 25.0% (4/16 eyes) in Group S, and this difference was not statistically significant (P = 1.000, Fisher exact test).

4. Discussion

Gas tamponade provides surface tension and buoyant force, allowing functional closure of the retinal breaks.[8] The tamponading period depends largely on the volume and absorption rate of the gas bubble. Meanwhile, retinal laser photocoagulation increases adhesive force at the sites of laser burns beyond normal within 24 hours[9] and by twice normal between 3 days to 4 weeks,[10] thus, it is crucial to deliver enough laser photocoagulation to prevent fluid from inflow into subretinal space through surrounding area. In order to extend the tamponading period, we did pars plana injection of FA until IOP was normal with digital tonometry in case of air leakage during trocar withdrawal and incision suture.

Due to its nonexpansile property and shorter half-life, AT causes less disturbance to the lens, decreasing the incidence of visual-disturbing cataracts, which was in line with our results that Group A had fewer patients that needed cataract removal compared to Group S. Besides, AT achieves visual rehabilitation sooner, shortens the period of prone position, and the gas bubble lasts long enough to achieve a chorioretinal adhesion without the need of a secondary surgery for removal.[4,8] In SO injected eyes, complications such as increased IOP, band keratopathy, anterior chamber oil accumulation and glaucoma are not uncommon,[11] though they were not observed in our study. Besides, cataracts of various degree are almost unavoidable in SO injected eyes.[12] In our study, the rate of developing vision-disturbing cataracts that required removal in Group S was 100%, significantly higher than group A, although the period between the primary surgery and cataract surgery was not significantly different. In terms of postoperative vitreoretinal proliferation, Riedel et al[12] reported a 40% rate of reproliferation of epiretinal and subretinal fibrous membranes in 415 consecutive patients with SO injection. While in our study, the rate of postoperative ERM was not significantly different.

In this study, the preoperative BCVA of Group A and Group S were significantly different, which we considered could be partially attributed to macular on/off status and cataract severity, although these 2 factors were not statistically significant. There could also be bias that surgeons tended to choose SO as tamponade for patients with poorer BCVA.

The 2 cases in Group A experiencing postoperative RD were both with multiple superior breaks that lied over 3 clocks from each other (three in total). Axial length, quadrants of RD, macular on/off status and the distance of breaks were analyzed with multiple linear regression for patients with multiple breaks in Group A, and we found breaks that lied over 3 clocks distance might be a possible factor that contributed to retinal redetachment (P = .050), though not statistically significant. We speculated that in this scenario, despite strict postoperative positioning, AT couldn’t tamponade these breaks simultaneously long enough to allow the breaks to securely heal. Tiny amount of fluid entered the subretinal space, leading to redetachment. Thus, we appeal to use AT for RD with single superior break or superior breaks in proximity.

This study has the inherit limitations as a retrospective study. Besides, the sample size was relatively small. In addition, at the outset, the composition ratio for lens status of the 2 groups were distributed unevenly, with less clear lenses in Group S, which may potentially contribute to the observed early cataract development in the postoperative period. However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies had compared the efficacy of SO and AT in treating RRD caused by superior breaks with no or mild PVR. Our study provides insights in the selection of tamponade to treat these patients, especially when expansile gas is not available.

5. Conclusion

In conclusions, the primary and final anatomic success rate for AT and SO in treating patients of RRD caused by superior breaks with no or mild PVR was not statistically different. Postoperative BCVA was associated with preoperative BCVA. The rate of deferred cataract removal was higher in patients with SO as tamponade.
