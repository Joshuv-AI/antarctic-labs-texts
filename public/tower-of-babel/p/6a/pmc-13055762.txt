Monosialoganglioside‐Containing Nanoliposomes Protect Against Acute and Chronic Ischemic Stroke Injury
Authors: Saif Ahmad, Takuma Maeda, Jessica Contreras, Nina Karamanova, Kaleb T. Morrow, Sudhanshu Raikwar, Naseem Akhter, Paulina Iacoban, Maria Lozoya, Justin Snider, Volkmar Weissig, Andrew F. Ducruet, Raymond Q. Migrino
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNeuroprotective strategies as primary or ancillary treatment could ameliorate the significant mortality and disability associated with ischemic stroke. Nanoliposomes composed of monosialoganglioside, phosphatidylcholine, and cholesterol (NLGM1) were previously shown to ameliorate brain injury when given before arterial occlusion in mice. We aimed to test the hypothesis that NLGM1 given following transient intraluminal filament and photothrombotic (PT) middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO) will attenuate acute and chronic stroke injury in mice.MethodsTwelve‐week‐old C57BL/6 mice underwent MCAO for 45 minutes and were then injected with 1 or 3 doses of saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg IV) after occlusion. Neurologic deficit score and brain infarct area were measured after 48 hours. Separate mice underwent PT occlusion of the distal lateral middle cerebral artery trunk followed by injection of 1 or 2 doses saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg IV) postocclusion. Motor and behavior testing was performed at 21, 30, 45, and 90 days after PT MCAO.ResultsFollowing intraluminal MCAO, single‐dose NLGM1‐treated mice exhibited reduced neurological impairment (neurologic deficit score 3.6±0.2, 2.6±0.3, 2.4±0.3 at 0, 1, 2 mg, respectively; P<0.05 versus control) and reduced infarct area (29.8±2.9%, 15.0±1.5%, 13.0±1.2%; P<0.001 versus control) with similar results with multiple dosing. Following PT MCAO, day 21 latency to fall (rotarod test), day 30 corner test, day 45 adhesive tape removal, and day 90 novel object recognition were improved in single‐dose NLGM1‐treated mice versus controls with improved corner and adhesive tape removal tests with multiple dosing. There were no significant differences in outcomes between 1 versus 2 mg NLGM1 treatments.ConclusionsTreatment of mice with NLGM1 resulted in reduced brain infarct size acutely following intraluminal MCAO and improved motor and behavior function acutely and chronically following intraluminal and PT MCAO. NLGM1 represents a promising novel neuroprotective agent in stroke.

Ischemic stroke is one of the leading causes of mortality and morbidity worldwide with incidence projected to increase to 89.32 per 100 000 population in 2030. 1 Despite development of standard‐of‐care reperfusion therapies, either pharmacologic or endovascular, ischemic stroke still caused 3.29 million deaths worldwide in 2019. 1 Existing challenges associated with reperfusion therapies include lack of availability, a narrow therapeutic window, numerous contraindications, treatment‐related complications, and lack of efficacy in a substantial number of patients. 2 Post‐ischemic brain injury still results in significant long‐term disability and increased health care costs in the large population of stroke survivors. 3 Therefore, the Stroke Progress Review Group of the United States National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke has called for the development of adjuvant therapies to protect against reperfusion injury as well as to extend the therapeutic window of reperfusion therapy. 4 Neuroprotective therapeutics that could be given early, even at first point of medical contact, before thrombolytic or endovascular therapy and which also prevent reperfusion injury, have the potential to improve clinical outcomes in ischemic stroke. To date, >1000 neuroprotective agents showed promising results in preclinical models, and >200 neuroprotection clinical trials have been completed or are ongoing, but none of these agents have achieved success. 5 Therefore, there is urgency to find a pipeline of novel neuroprotective drugs for ischemic stroke that could be developed for human translational testing. Neutralizing oxidative stress in stroke is a promising adjuvant or primary therapy in stroke, because the ischemic brain is susceptible to oxidative damage due to high oxygen consumption and low antioxidant capacity. 6

We previously showed that nanoliposomes composed of 75% phosphatidylcholine, 20% cholesterol, and 5% monosialoganglioside (NLGM1) protected endothelial cells against oxidative stress induced by amyloidogenic light chain 7 and medin 8 proteins through nuclear factor erythroid 2‐related factor 2 (Nrf2)‐induced activation of antioxidant enzymes including heme oxygenase‐1 (HO‐1), NAD[P]H quinone dehydrogenase 1 (NQO1), and superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1). In vitro, NLGM1 prevented endothelial and neuronal cell death following exposure to 20 hours of oxygen–glucose deprivation (OGD) through the same Nrf2‐dependent antioxidant response. 9 In vivo, C57BL/6 mice undergoing intraluminal middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO)‐reperfusion ischemic injury given NLGM1 before occlusion and a second dose before reperfusion had reduced acute neurological impairment and brain infarction size when compared with saline‐treated controls. 9 It remains unknown whether NLGM1 given after cerebral artery occlusion confers acute and chronic/long‐term benefit. The aims of this study were to test the hypotheses that (1) NLGM1 given after intraluminal MCAO injury will reduce infarct size and improve acute neurologic function versus saline controls and (2) NLGM1 given after photothrombotic (PT) MCAO will improve subacute and chronic neurologic function versus saline controls. We show that NLGM1 given after intraluminal and PT MCAO resulted in reduced brain infarct size and improved acute and chronic neurologic function when compared with saline control.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Materials

All liposomes were prepared using the lipid film hydration method. 10 Briefly, all lipids were dissolved in chloroform, followed by complete removal of the solvent in a rotary vacuum evaporator (Yamamoto‐USA, Clifton, NJ) at 40 °C. The resulting lipid film was hydrated with HEPES buffer (5 mmol/L HEPES, 150 mmol/L sodium chloride pH: 7.42) to obtain a final lipid concentration of 10 mg/mL. The hydrated lipid suspension was sonicated with a probe sonicator (Sonic Dismembrator Model 100; Fisher Scientific, Pittsburgh, PA) in an ice bath for about 1 hour until the formation of an opaque solution could be observed. To remove titanium particles shedded from the probe sonicator, liposomes were centrifuged at 1500g in a Lynex 6000 centrifuge (Fisher Scientific) for 15 minutes at 4 °C. Finally, liposomes were sterilized by filtration using a 0.22 μm membrane. The size distribution of all liposome preparations was determined via dynamic light scattering using a Malvern Zeta Sizer (nano series; Malvern Panalytical, Westborough, MA). Phosphatidylcholine, PC‐d31 (phosphatidylcholine‐d31), cholesterol, and ganglioside GM1 (GM1) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids, Alabaster, Alabama, and ganglioside GM1‐d3 (GM1‐d3) was obtained from (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI). All nondeuterated liposomes were prepared at the molar lipid ratio phosphatidylcholine:cholesterol:GM1=70:25:5. The molar lipid ratio for the deuterated formulations was cholesterol:PC‐d31:cholesterol:GM1:GM1‐d31 = 63:7:25:4.5:0.5. The size distribution for the deuterated liposomes was determined to be 59.5 nm (polydispersity index 0.203), whereas the nondeuterate liposomes had a size distribution of 47.6 nm (polydispersity index 0.282). NLGM1 with deuterated lipid components was used for pharmacokinetic/brain biodistribution studies, whereas the nondeuterated NLGM1 was used for the rest of the experiments. There were 2 formulations of NLGM1 produced: 10 mg/mL and 5 mg/mL (lipid mass/volume), and mice received either 2 or 1 mg NLGM1. There are 0.22 mg and 0.11 mg GM1 in 2 and 1 mg of NLGM1, respectively.

Mice, Experimental Plan, and Procedures

Institutional Animal Care and Use Committees of St. Joseph's Hospital and Medical Center approval was obtained for this study, and the procedures followed were in accordance with institutional guidance. The study adhered to the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments 2.0 guidelines. C57BL/6 wild‐type 12‐week‐old male and female mice (24–26 g) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, Maine). For pharmacokinetic studies, 3 male C57BL/6 mice were used for each method of injection (intravenous or intraperitoneal). For more rapid delivery to the brain, intravenous dosing was selected for mouse injections. For stroke models, mice were randomly assigned to either sham operation or intraluminal MCAO stroke (acute cohort) or sham operation and PT MCAO stroke (chronic cohort) and treated either with vehicle or NLGM1. Each surgical day had saline and NLGM1 treatments with order randomly assigned. The PT injury model was used for the chronic cohort because the model has minimal mortality that allows long‐term follow‐up. 11

Pharmacokinetic Study and Brain Biodistribution

Study nanoliposomes were extracted from plasma using a pseudo Folch extraction adapted from Huang. 12 Plasma was added to a 2:1 mix of chloroform:methanol; 300 μL of water and 50 μL of 2 μmol/L internal standard mix were then added. Samples were vortexed and placed at −20 °C for 1 hour to aid in protein precipitation. After centrifugation, the supernatant was dried and brought back to volume in 50 μL acetonitrile+0.1% formic acid. Extract was injected onto a Sciex 7500 Qtrap tandem Agilent 1290 ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography system.

Chromatographic separation was achieved on an Agilent1290 ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography system with a Peeke Scientific C‐8 Spectra 3u 150×3 mm column. Analysis of GM1 and deuterated phosphatidylcholine were performed as separate injections. GM1 analysis used mobile phases composed of water containing 0.1% formic acid (phase A) and methanol containing 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 500 μL/min. Chromatographic gradient elution began at 90% phase A and remained there for the first minute, proceeding to 1% phase A at 10 minutes, and remaining there for 4.5 minutes, before returning to 90% mobile phase A over 0.5 minutes and remaining there until the end of the 20‐minute run. Mobile phases were adapted for phospholipids, although the gradient remained the same. Mobile phase A consisted of water:methanol:acetonitrile (1:1:1) with 0.1% acetic acid, whereas mobile phase B consisted of isopropanol with 0.1% acetic acid at a flow rate of 500 μL/min. The Sciex 7500 Qtrap was operated in positive ion mode in multiple reaction monitoring mode with a mass transition of 791.8 m/z/184 m/z/41CE and a 200 ms dwell for the analyte PC‐d31. The internal standard, phosphatidylcholine16:0‐d9/16:0, used a mass transition of 743.4 m/z/184 m/z/41 collision energy. The spray voltage was set at 5500 V, and source gases were at gas 1 to 50, gas 2 to 70, collision gas −9, with a source temperature of 450 °C. To quantify levels of 1‐palmitoyl‐d31‐2‐oleoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (Avanti Polar Lipids), 1‐palmitoyl‐d9‐2‐palmitoyl‐sn‐glycero‐3‐phosphocholine (Cayman Chemical) was used as an internal standard. Calibration standards are prepared with Folch solvents and extracted directly with 50 μL of internal standard. The calibration range for PC‐d31 is 4 to 11 000 μg/mL. Liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry data were processed using ScieX Analyst software. Processed data were analyzed for pharmacokinetic parameters using a PKSolver Excel plugin. 13 Informed by the pharmacokinetic data, the investigators opted for an intravenous NLGM1 administration route.

To evaluate NLGM1 brain biodistribution, tissue samples were normalized to tissue weight before glass bead homogenization in the FastPrep system using 1:1 chloroform:methanol mix (lipidomics) or methanol (metabolomics). Next, lipid metabolites were isolated from tissues and homogenized via a Bligh‐Dyer extraction containing 50 μL of 2 μmol/L internal standard mix. Samples were vortexed and placed at −20 °C for 1 hour to aid in protein precipitation. After centrifugation, the supernatant was dried and brought back down to volume in 50 μL acetonitrile+0.1% formic acid. Extract was injected onto a Sciex 7500 Qtrap tandem Agilent 1290 ultra‐high performance liquid chromatography system. Chromatographic, mass spectrometry, and quantification conditions were identical to the pharmacokinetic study. Sample values were normalized to grams of tissue extracted.

Intraluminal MCAO (Acute Stroke Cohort)

Adult mice (C57BL/6) were anesthetized with isoflurane (3% for induction followed by 1.5% for maintenance) in a mixture of 30% oxygen and 70% nitrogen. A silicon‐coated 6‐0 nylon suture was introduced into the external carotid artery and advanced up to the internal carotid artery to occlude the middle cerebral artery for a period of 45 minutes followed by removal of the filament to restore perfusion. Laser speckle contrast imaging (PeriCam PsI system; Perimed, Stockholm, Sweden) was used to assess cerebral blood flow as previously described, 10 and animals that failed to show a 60% reduction in cerebral blood flow were excluded. A cohort of mice (all males, n=8 per treatment group) underwent single tail vein intravenous dosing of either saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg) just after occlusion. A separate cohort of mice (n=8–10 males and n=7 females for each treatment group) underwent a dosing regimen of either saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg) at 3 time points: immediately postocclusion (tail vein intravenous), before reperfusion, and 2 hours postocclusion (intraorbital intravenous). Neurologic deficit scoring was done 48 hours postocclusion followed by euthanizing and histologic analyses.

PT MCAO (Chronic Stroke Cohort)

Adult mice (C57BL/6) were subjected to PT MCAO by using Rose Bengal dye and laser light. Briefly, a modified PT‐stroke model produces a fibrin‐rich clot as previously described 14 but with significant modifications such as a lateral‐side surgical approach without craniotomy, laser beam source, and route of injection. The PT injury model was used for the chronic cohort because this model has minimal mortality that allows long‐term follow‐up. 14 We previously confirmed that this technique caused reduction in cerebral blood flow. 15 Briefly, mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (1.5%–2% mixed with room air) using a low‐flow gas anesthesia system (Somnosuite; Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT). Mice were then transferred to a stereotaxic frame integrated with an angle device (Stoelting, Wood Dale, IL) and a 532 nm laser source (4.5 mW; beam diameter 2.5–3 mm; ThorLabs, Newton, NJ). The head was rotated to face right side up, hair was shaved, the temporal muscles under the skin were blunt dissected, and the skull bone above the zygomatic arc was exposed. Rose Bengal dye (50 mg/kg in 200 μL of 1× sterile saline; Sigma) was then injected via the tail vein. Using the laser light, the distal trunk of the middle cerebral artery was visualized underneath the bone, and the laser was lowered down to the skull bone, illuminated, and exposed for 15 minutes. Thrombotic occlusion of the distal lateral trunk of the middle cerebral artery was confirmed using laser doppler flowmetry (>70% drop in focal cerebral blood flow). Finally, the wound was closed carefully using 3M VetBond tissue glue (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA). Mice were then transferred to a clean warm cage for recovery. A cohort of mice (all males, n=5–7 per treatment group) were given a single tail vein intravenous dose of saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg) immediately following PT MCAO. A separate cohort of mice (males, n=4 per treatment group or females, n=3–4 per treatment group) received 2 intravenous doses of saline or NLGM1 (1 or 2 mg) at 2 time points: immediately postocclusion and 2 hours postocclusion.

Motor and Behavior Tests

For the acute stroke cohort, behavioral outcomes were evaluated 48 hours postocclusion using a modified Bederson scale as per prior methods. 9

For the chronic stroke cohort, behavioral assessment was performed at 21 days (rotarod), 30 days (corner turn test), 45 days (adhesive tape removal) and 90 days (novel object recognition test [NOR]) poststroke by an investigator blinded to the identity of groups. We evaluated the animals for different tests on different days to avoid the stress and fatigue of 1 of these tests influencing another test.

The rotarod test (LE205 number 76‐0770 Panlab; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA) examines equilibrium behavior and locomotor ability in rodents. 16 , 17 Before testing, animals were trained to perform the task. Mice were placed on the rod without rotation for 30 seconds, followed by a constant slow (4 rpm) rotation. After the training session, animals were placed on the rod for the testing trial, in which the rod rotates with an increasing speed from 4 rpm to 40 rpm, thereby producing constant acceleration. The trial ended when the mouse fell off the rotarod. Latency to fall from the rotarod and the rotation speed were recorded for each mouse.

The corner turn test was performed to assess sensorimotor and postural asymmetry as previously described. 18 , 19 Briefly, 2 boards were placed at a 30° angle to form a narrow walk, and the mouse was placed in between the boards facing the corner. As the animal reaches the corner, it turns either right or left to exit the corner. For each mouse, this test was repeated 10 times at 30‐second intervals, and the percent of right turns was calculated.

The adhesive tape removal test was also performed to evaluate sensorimotor function and tactile extinction. 20 Briefly, mice are placed into a 15 cm×25 cm transparent box, and adhesive tape (≈6.35×3 mm) is attached to each forepaw. The amount of time it takes to contact and remove the stimuli was recorded.

The NOR was used to assess learning and recognition memory 21 as described previously. 22 Briefly, mice were placed in a hard plastic chamber (40 cm×40 cm×40 cm) in which they explored an open field for 10 minutes (habituation phase). The training phase began 24 hours following the habituation phase and involved exposing mice to 2 identical objects placed at diagonally opposite corners for 10 minutes to acclimate. The testing phase began 24 hours post‐training phase, involving exploration of the open field in the presence of familiar and novel objects for 10 minutes. Preferential exploration of the novel object over the familiar object was defined as successful recognition or discrimination. All behaviors were recorded using a video tracking system and analyzed using Noldus EthoVision XT 16 software (Noldus, Wageningen, the Netherlands). The discrimination index was then calculated: (time spent with novel object−time spent with familiar object)/(time spent with novel object+time spent with familiar object).

Histology

For the acute stroke cohort, following deep anesthesia with isoflurane (2%–3%), mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Brain coronal sections (2 mm thickness) were cut using a mouse brain matrix (Roboz Surgical Instruments, Gaithersburg, MD) and stained with 1% 2,3,4‐triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (Sigma‐Aldrich) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Using ImageJ, infarct volume was calculated indirectly to correct for edema. The PT model was previously shown to have minimal infarct by histological analysis after day 14, 11 and so brain infarct size was not quantified for the chronic cohort.

Oxygen–Glucose Deprivation Cell Experiments

We previously showed that NLGM1 reduces oxidative stress and enhances cellular viability via Nrf2‐dependent upregulation of antioxidant enzymes such as HO‐1 in light chain‐ 7 and medin amyloid 8 ‐mediated oxidative stress and when given before OGD injury. 9 To confirm that NLGM1 given after hypoxic injury results in upregulation of HO‐1, we exposed a coculture of SH‐SY5Y neuroblastoma (passages 10–13) and human brain microvascular endothelial cells (HBMVECs, passages 7–12) in transwell plates (ThinCert Tissue Culture Inserts; Greiner Bio‐One, Monroe, NC) to either room air with regular media (Complete Classic Media catalog number 4Z0‐500; Cell Systems, Kirkland, WA) for 20 hours or to 3 hours of OGD (1% oxygen, 5% carbon dioxide in a Cytation 5 Cell Imaging Multimode Reader; Biotek, Winooski, VT and DMEM media with no glucose, no glutamine, no phenol red catalog number A1443001; Gibco, Waltham, MA) followed by 17 hours of room air and regular media. OGD cells were treated 15 minutes following OGD onset with either vehicle or NLGM1 300 μg/mL. We measured and compared gene expression of HO‐1 by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (Prime Time qPCR Primers Hmox1 catalog number Hs.PT.58.45340055; Reference Sequence NM_002133 (1), exon location: 2–3) and protein expression of HO‐1 by Western blot (using anti‐human monoclonal HO‐1 antibody, catalog number 43 966 from Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA; dilution 1:1000, normalized to β‐actin detected using 8H10D10 Mouse mAb isotype Ig[immunoglobulin]G2b; Cell Signaling Technology; dilution 1:1000) similar to prior methods. 9 In separate experiments, we performed similar treatments but harvested the cells after 3 hours of OGD, separated the nuclear and cytosolic cellular compartments (NE‐PER Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagent Kit, catalog number 78835; Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA) as per prior methods 8 and measured Nrf2 in the nuclear and cytosolic compartments by Western blot (anti‐human Nrf2 antibody, NRF2(D1Z9C) XP, isotype‐rabbit IgG in dilution 1:1000). The ratio of nuclear to cytosolic Nrf2 relative to value in no OGD control was compared between vehicle and NLGM1‐treated cells.

To assess cell viability, a 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay was performed using CyQUANT, the 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide assay (catalog number V13154; Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). HBMVECs and SH‐SY5Y were seeded in 96‐well plates in quadruplicate. After OGD treatment (without or with NLGM1 300 μg/mL given 15 minutes following initiation of OGD) and 20‐hour recovery, 100 μL of 1mM of 3‐(4,5‐dimethylthiazol‐2‐yl)‐2,5‐diphenyltetrazolium bromide in cell culture media was applied to each well and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 2 hours. Then, 100 μL of sodium dodecyl sulfate‐hydrochloric acid solution was added to each well to solubilize the produced formazan and incubated at 37 °C and 5% CO2 for 1 hour. Plates were then put on a rotary plate shaker at 37 °C and 600 rpm for 10 minutes. Absorbance was measured for each well at 570 nm on a SpectraMax 190 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA) and then averaged for each treatment group. Corresponding cells not exposed to OGD served as controls.

Statistical Analysis

Power Calculation Assumptions

Based on our previous study on NLGM1 given before MCAO and assuming similar or better outcomes, a sample size of n=8 per treatment arm (NLGM1 or saline) will have 80% power (α=0.05) to detect 8.5% difference in infarct size with standard deviation of 5.3% in each treatment.

Data are expressed as means±SEMs. Following a test for normality, group treatment outcomes were compared using one‐way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise comparison using Holm–Sidak (GraphPad Prism 9.1.0; GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). To tease out relative contributions of treatment and sex, two‐way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise comparison using the Tukey method was performed. When there was heavy‐tailedness in the outcome distribution, natural log transformation was done, and transformed values were used for the analyses. Nuclear to cytosolic Nrf2, viability, and HO‐1 gene and protein expression relative to no OGD control were compared between OGD and OGD‐NLGM1‐treated cells using a paired t test. P value was set at 0.05 (2‐sided). No completed data point was excluded. R.Q.M. had full access to all data in the study and takes responsibility for their integrity and data analyses. S.A. was aware of group allocation during conduct of experiment, outcome assessment, and data analysis. Measurements were done blind as to treatment allocation.

RESULTS

Pharmacokinetic Assessment

The half‐life following single intravenous administration of NLGM1 (2 mg) averaged 6.4 hours and following intraperitoneal injection averaged 4.9 hours, with similar peak dose achieved between the 2 modes of administration (Figure 1). Deuterated phosphatidylcholine‐labeled NLGM1 was detected in brain tissue using mass spectrometry following intravenous injection (4, 8, and 24 hours: 559±148, 263±50, and 322±151 μg/mg brain tissue).

Intraluminal MCAO Neurologic Assessment

Intraluminal filament occlusion of the middle cerebral artery caused reduced cerebral blood flow (Figure 2A) and resulted in brain infarcts (Figure 2B). Forty‐eight hours after occlusion, there was reduced neurologic deficit score, infarct volume and brain edema in NLGM1‐treated versus saline‐treated mice (Figure 2C through 2E). There was no significant difference in neurologic function between mice receiving 1 versus 2 mg of NLGM1.

Intraluminal MCAO Stroke Histology

Staining with triphenyl tetrazolium chloride showed significant reduction in infarct volume in single‐dose NLGM1‐treated versus saline‐treated mice following MCAO (absolute reduction of −14.8±4.2% and −16.8±3.3% for 1 or 2 mg dose, respectively; relative reduction of −49.6±13.9% and −56.4±11.2% for 1 or 2 mg dose, respectively; Figure 2B and 2C). There was corresponding reduction in brain edema in NLGM1‐treated versus saline‐treated mice (Figure 2D). There were no significant differences in infarct size and brain edema among mice who received 1 or 2 mg of NLGM1. Multiple dosing of NLGM1 also showed reduction in infarct volume (absolute reduction of −12.1±7.4% and −12.4±6.4% for 1 or 2 mg dose, respectively; relative reduction of −31.8±19.5% and −32.6±16.9% for 1 or 2 mg dose, respectively), edema, and neurologic deficit score with NLGM1 treatment versus saline treatment but no difference in outcomes between 1 versus 2 mg NLGM1 (Figure 3A through 3D; data organized by treatment and sex). Two‐way ANOVA showed no significant differences between male and female outcomes (Figure 3). Analysis of variance testing show that the percent reduction of infarct size with single‐dose 2 mg NLGM1 was higher when compared with multiple dosing of either 1 or 2 mg of NLGM1 (Figure S1A). There were no significant differences in percent reduction of edema among the various treatment regimens (Figure S1B). The percentage neurologic impairment change compared with saline was higher in multidose NLGM1 2 mg versus single‐dose NLGM1 1 or 2 mg (Figure S1C).

Photothrombotic Stroke MCAO

In a cohort of mice tested for single intravenous dosing of NLGM1, compared with sham control, mice undergoing PT injury that received saline showed worse performance on the rotarod test (day 21), corner test (day 30), adhesive tape removal test (day 45), and NOR test (day 90) (Figure 4A through 4D). Treatment of PT‐injured mice with either 1 or 2 mg of NLGM1 prevented impairments in the rotarod, corner, adhesive tape removal, and NOR tests when compared with saline treatment and with no significant differences when compared with sham control. There were no differences in response between a 1 versus 2 mg NLGM1 dose.

DISCUSSION

Our results show that NLGM1 given after intraluminal MCAO stroke in mice reduced neurologic impairment, brain infarct, and edema size when compared with saline‐treated controls. Additionally, NLGM1 given after PT MCAO injury reduced motor and behavioral impairment in the subacute and chronic periods.

Following cerebral artery occlusion, the goal of acute stroke therapy is the salvage of the ischemic penumbra or region of hypoperfused yet still viable tissue surrounding the ischemic core. 6 Despite remarkable progress in reperfusion therapy using thrombolytic or endovascular approaches in specialized stroke units, ischemic stroke still remains a leading cause of mortality and disability. 6 Neuroprotective drugs that address key mediators of ischemic cell death during stroke such as excitotoxicity, oxidative/nitrative stress, and inflammation are promising agents as adjuncts to reperfusion therapy or as primary treatments for those not eligible for reperfusion, 6 , 23 but clinical trials to date have failed to come up with a neuroprotective drug that is effective and safe for clinical use. 6 , 24 Thus, the search for novel neuroprotective drugs continues.

Acute ischemia increases production of free radicals and reactive oxygen species leading to neuronal damage. 25 In the early phase of ischemia–reperfusion, mobilization of antioxidants is triggered, but the antioxidant capacity might be limited by chronically low baseline availability. 26 , 27 The decreased availability of antioxidant defense mechanism in stroke 28 led to efforts to find neuroprotective agents that increase scavenging or degradation of reactive oxygen species, although no agent or drug has to date been validated to be clinically useful. 29

We showed in prior work that NLGM1 given before cerebral occlusion reduced infarct size in mice, 9 but this scenario limits its usefulness for conditions with prodromal events such as transient ischemic attacks, which occur in only 15% to 20% of patients who have a stroke. 30 The current study expands on our previous finding by showing benefit when NLGM1 is given following arterial occlusion and before reperfusion. We previously showed that pretreatment with NLGM1 induces gene and protein expression of antioxidant enzymes through Nrf2‐dependent signaling in endothelial and neuronal cells in vitro with prevention of cell death by NLGM1 following OGD conditions. 9 We confirmed in this study that gene and protein upregulation of HO‐1 also occurs when NLGM1 is given following exposure of endothelial and neuroblastoma cocultured cells to OGD conditions, congruent with our in vivo temporal conditions. Following ischemic stroke, the ischemic brain is highly susceptible to oxidative damage owing to its high consumption of oxygen, its rich content of iron and unsaturated lipids, and its relatively low endogenous antioxidant capacity, 6 so the Nrf2‐activating antioxidant effects of NLGM1 could be a promising approach. There are planned future experiments to measure the time course and magnitude of HO‐1 expression and Nrf2 nuclear translocation in vivo with NLGM1 treatment following MCAO injury to confirm whether our in vitro results also apply to in vivo conditions.

It is also relevant to show that NLGM1 affords longstanding functional benefits in the chronic phase of stroke recovery. The intraluminal MCAO stroke model induces large infarct size that is often not compatible with long‐term survival in the mouse, so we also performed a PT MCAO stroke model that results in smaller ischemic injury 11 but low mortality to test NLGM1 efficacy long term. We confirmed that NLGM1 treatment did impart functional improvement in motor and behavior functional outcomes in the early, subacute, and chronic phases following PT MCAO injury. As far as dosing is concerned, although there was a trend toward greater improvement in acute and chronic functional outcomes with 2 versus 1 mg NLGM1 dose, the differences were not significant. The half‐life data we obtained (6.4 hours intravenous and 4.9 hours intraperitoneal) suggest that greater than single dosing might be beneficial. However, our results show that multiple intravenous dosing of NLGM1 is not superior to single dosing in terms of brain infarct and edema, although a 2 mg dose given at 3 time periods showed improved neurologic deficit score compared with a single dose of either 1 or 2 mg. A single dose of 2 mg NLGM1 showed greater reduction in infarct size (relative to saline treatment) when compared with multiple dosing of NLGM1. Whether dosing >2 mg will have incremental structural and functional improvement over the doses tested here will be empirically tested in the future.

GM1, a glycosphingolipid, given at a dose of 10 mg/kg, showed reduction in infarct volume following MCAO in rats, 31 but a meta‐analysis of 12 human clinical trials in ischemic stroke did not show any clinical improvement with this monolipid treatment. 32 It is important to note that there are critical differences in the properties of GM1 alone and GM1 mixed with other lipids to form nanoliposomes. Due to its amphiphilicity, GM1 is easily soluble in a mixture of chloroform and methanol (2:1), but only slightly in water, where it forms micelles with a critical micelle concentration of around 28 μM, 33 making it poorly soluble in circulation in vivo. To increase the bioavailability of GM1 in vivo, it can be incorporated into pharmaceutical nanocarriers such as nanoliposomes. Nanoliposomes are artificial phospholipid vesicles with a diameter of ≈≤100 nm that are being used in numerous pharmaceutical applications, because they are known to be nontoxic, nonimmunogenic, fully biodegradable, and structurally versatile. 34 , 35 Thus, GM1 incorporated into nanoliposomes does not form micellar insoluble aggregates in water solutions unlike naked GM1. Future translational studies should test whether nanoliposomal formulations of GM1, such as NLGM1, would have similar benefit in human stroke as our preclinical stroke models. Similarly, future translational investigations of NLGM1 should test effects on an expanded battery of cognitive tests to fully assess the functional domains affected by NLGM1.

We showed sex difference in adverse outcomes following intraluminal MCAO (in terms of infarct size) but not with PT MCAO (motor and behavior tests). The difference was mainly the greater infarct size in females compared with males treated with saline. In humans, women have more severe strokes than men after the age of 60 years, although they are more likely to survive. 36 We showed that despite this sex difference, the beneficial effects of NLGM1 in mitigating intraluminal and PT MCAO injury were similar between sexes. There was variability in the rotarod test scores of sham‐treated mice that could be experimental variability from environmental factors among cohorts tested at different time points. Furthermore, differences in numbers of male and female mice between single and multiple dosing cohorts could possibly explain some of the variability of results between dosing regimens.

In conclusion, we show for the first time that NLGM1 given following MCAO resulted in reduced brain infarct and edema size and improved neurologic function acutely and chronically. The results support further translational testing of NLGM1 as a primary or adjunctive therapy for ischemic stroke.
