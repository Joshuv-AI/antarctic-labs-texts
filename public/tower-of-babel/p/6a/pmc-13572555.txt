miR-140-5p Inhibition alleviates LPS-induced cholangitis in rats and is associated with modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway
Authors: Qingjian Wang, Ao Luo, Wenjuan Huang, Yuhan Zhao, Yalu Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cholangitis is characterized by persistent inflammatory injury of biliary epithelial cells (BDECs), which is a key driver of biliary fibrosis, cholestatic cirrhosis, and cholangiocarcinoma. Nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) is the central mediator of the inflammatory cascade in cholangitis. miR-140-5p has been reported to regulate inflammatory diseases via modulating NF-κB signaling, but its role and regulatory mechanism in cholangitis remain completely unelucidated. This study aimed to investigate the functional role of miR-140-5p in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced cholangitis in rats, and its potential interplay with the NF-κB signaling pathway. Thirty specific pathogen-free (SPF) male Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats were randomly divided into 5 groups (n = 6 per group): Control group, LPS-induced cholangitis model group, LPS + negative control (NC) adenovirus group, LPS + miR-140-5p inhibitor group, and LPS + pyrrolidine dithiocarbamate (PDTC, NF-κB specific inhibitor) group. The cholangitis model was established via retrograde injection of LPS (5 μg/mL, 0.9 mL) into the common bile duct. Interventions were administered before modeling. Biliary tissues were harvested for transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to evaluate BDEC ultrastructural injury, and immunohistochemistry (IHC) to detect NF-κB protein expression. Serum levels of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interleukin-18 (IL-18) were measured by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The mRNA expression of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), NF-κB, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and miR-140-5p was quantified by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Protein levels of TLR4, NF-κB, and TNF-α were determined by Western blot. The rat cholangitis model was successfully established. Compared with the Control group, the LPS model group showed significant BDEC ultrastructural damage, severe inflammatory infiltration, elevated serum IL-1β and IL-18 levels, and upregulated mRNA and protein expression of TLR4, NF-κB, and TNF-α (all P < 0.01), accompanied by a significant downregulation of miR-140-5p expression (P < 0.01). No significant differences in all indicators were observed between the LPS + NC adenovirus group and the LPS model group (*P* > 0.05). Compared with the LPS model group, the LPS + miR-140-5p inhibitor group exhibited markedly alleviated BDEC injury, reduced serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and downregulated TLR4/NF-κB/TNF-α signaling activation (all P < 0.05). The LPS + PDTC group showed almost complete reversal of LPS-induced inflammatory injury, and a significant upregulation of miR-140-5p expression compared with the LPS model group (P < 0.01). This preliminary in vivo study demonstrates that miR-140-5p expression is downregulated in LPS-induced cholangitis in rats, and that pharmacological inhibition of miR-140-5p significantly alleviates biliary epithelial cell injury and inflammatory response, concomitant with suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB/TNF-α signaling pathway. Additionally, NF-κB inhibition by PDTC upregulated miR-140-5p expression, suggesting a potential negative regulatory relationship between NF-κB activation and miR-140-5p transcription. While these findings identify miR-140-5p as a potential therapeutic target for cholangitis and provide a testable hypothesis for the miR-140-5p/NF-κB regulatory interplay in biliary inflammation, direct mechanistic proof remains to be established in subsequent studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-54662-2.

Introduction

Cholangitis is primarily characterized by intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile duct injury accompanied by persistent inflammatory infiltration1,2. It is mainly triggered by endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) released from injured cells, or exogenous pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) derived from the intestinal tract or systemic circulation3. Upon activation by these stimuli, cholangiocytes recruit immune, vascular and mesenchymal cells via intercellular crosstalk, and secrete chemokines, cytokines and angiogenic factors to initiate and amplify cholestatic inflammation4–6. Long-term exposure of biliary epithelial cells (BDECs) to a microenvironment enriched with cytokines and chemokines from innate and adaptive immune cells drives sustained cellular damage7,8. Persistent inflammatory stimulation induces aberrant tissue repair, leading to excessive scar deposition around injured bile ducts and subsequent biliary fibrosis. This pathological process further increases the risk of cholestasis, and is a well-documented high-risk factor for bile duct stone formation and cholangiocarcinoma9.

Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), the canonical pathogen recognition receptor expressed on the surface of cholangiocytes, undergoes receptor oligomerization and conformational changes upon recognition of PAMPs (such as lipopolysaccharide, LPS, from Gram-negative bacteria). By recruiting adaptor proteins including myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88), interleukin-1 receptor-associated kinases (IRAKs), and TIR-domain-containing adapter-inducing interferon-β (TRIF)10, TLR4 transduces signals via MyD88-dependent or TRIF-mediated independent pathways11. This signaling cascade ultimately activates nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB), promotes the biosynthesis and release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and drives the initiation and progression of cholangitis12.

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are a class of endogenous, highly conserved non-coding small RNAs of approximately 20–24 nucleotides in length13. They regulate post-transcriptional gene expression by specifically binding to the 3′-untranslated regions (3′-UTRs) or 5′-UTRs of target mRNAs14, and are widely involved in core physiological processes including cell proliferation, apoptosis and lipid metabolism15. Accumulating evidence has confirmed that miRNAs regulate the pathogenesis of hepatobiliary diseases via multiple signaling pathways. For instance, miR-29a-3p suppresses hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation and alleviates liver fibrosis in mice by inhibiting bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) expression16; miR-513 inhibits the translation of the immune checkpoint molecule B7-H1 in cholangiocytes, and plays a key regulatory role in primary biliary cholangitis (PBC) and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)17.

miR-140-5p has been demonstrated to be closely associated with inflammatory responses and cellular injury in various disease models, and modulates these processes by targeting downstream genes and signaling pathways18,19. For example, miR-140-5p overexpression aggravates insulin resistance via directly targeting GYS1 and PPP1CC in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells20. In a mouse model of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis, miR-140-5p promotes pro-inflammatory factor secretion and tissue damage by activating the NF-κB pathway via targeted inhibition of Smad3, demonstrating its pro-inflammatory role in inflammatory progression21. However, in other disease models including intervertebral disc inflammation and diabetic nephropathy, miR-140-5p directly targets TLR4 and inhibits NF-κB signaling activation22,23.

These findings indicate that the inflammatory regulatory function of miR-140-5p is highly tissue- and microenvironment-specific, and its specific effector mechanism is closely related to target gene profiles, pathway activation status and cell types. To date, NF-κB has been well established as the core driver of cholangitis initiation and progression. Given the regulatory effect of miR-140-5p on the NF-κB pathway in multiple inflammatory models, this study aimed to investigate the regulatory relationship between miR-140-5p and the NF-κB pathway, as well as their functional roles in cholangitis progression, using an in vivo LPS-induced rat cholangitis model.

Materials and methods

Experimental animals

Thirty SPF-grade healthy adult male SD rats (6–8 weeks old, body weight 200 ± 20 g) were provided by the Animal Experiment Center of Guangxi Medical University (Production License No. SCXK (Gui) 2020–0004). Rats were housed in a temperature-controlled (22 ± 2 °C) environment with a 12 h light/dark cycle, 50 ± 5% relative humidity, and free access to sterile chow and water. All rats were acclimatized for 7 days before the experiment.

All animal experiments were performed in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Guangxi University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. DW20221024-203). All efforts were made to minimize the number of animals used and their suffering.

Animal grouping and model establishment

Rats were randomly allocated into 5 groups (n = 6 per group) using a computer-generated random number table: Control group: Rats underwent the same surgical procedure, with an equivalent volume of sterile normal saline injected into the common bile duct, and received normal saline via tail vein and intraperitoneal injection.LPS model group: Rats were subjected to retrograde injection of LPS into the common bile duct to establish the cholangitis model, and received normal saline via tail vein and intraperitoneal injection.

LPS + NC adenovirus group: Rats received a single tail vein injection of negative control adenoviral vector (2 × 108 pfu/100 μL) 2 days before modeling, followed by LPS-induced cholangitis model establishment.

LPS + miR-140-5p inhibitor group: Rats received a single tail vein injection of adenoviral vector carrying miR-140-5p inhibitor (2 × 108 pfu/100 μL) 2 days before modeling, followed by LPS-induced cholangitis model establishment. LPS + PDTC group: Rats received an intraperitoneal injection of PDTC (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# P8765; 100 mg/kg body weight) 1 h before modeling, followed by LPS-induced cholangitis model establishment. The adenoviral vectors were constructed and verified by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with a viral titer of 2 × 109 pfu/mL, and the knockdown efficiency of the miR-140-5p inhibitor was confirmed by pre-experiment.The cholangitis model was established via retrograde common bile duct injection of LPS (Escherichia coli O55:B5, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat# L2880; 5 μg/mL, total volume 0.9 mL). Briefly, rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water before surgery, and anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% sodium pentobarbital (0.3 mL/100 g body weight). Laparotomy was performed under sterile conditions to fully expose the common bile duct, and the distal end of the common bile duct was temporarily ligated with 4-0 silk suture. LPS was slowly injected into the common bile duct using a 30G micro-syringe, and the ligation was maintained for 5 min after injection to ensure sufficient retention of LPS in the biliary tract. Successful injection was confirmed by diffuse distension of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts without fluid leakage. The ligation was then released, and the abdominal cavity was closed layer by layer. The Control group underwent the same surgical procedure, with an equivalent volume of sterile normal saline injected into the common bile duct.

Sample collection

All rats were housed for 7 days after modeling to stabilize the model, and their general conditions (food intake, activity, mental status) were monitored and recorded daily. At the end of the experiment, rats were fasted for 12 h with free access to water, anesthetized as described above, and euthanized by cervical dislocation. For serum collection: 4 mL of whole blood was collected from the abdominal aorta, left to stand at room temperature for 2 h, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The serum supernatant was aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent ELISA detection. For biliary tissue collection: The liver and biliary tract were dissected immediately after blood collection, and the biliary tree was isolated with hepatic parenchyma removed. Part of the biliary tissue was fixed in 4% neutral buffered formalin for TEM and immunohistochemistry analysis. Another portion of the biliary tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for qPCR and Western blot analysis.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Fresh biliary tissue was cut into 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm fragments on ice within 3 min, fixed in electron microscopy fixative (Servicebio, Cat# G1102), and stored at 4 °C. Samples were rinsed 3 times with pre-cooled 0.1 M phosphate buffer (PB, pH 7.4, 15 min each), post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at 4 °C in the dark, and rinsed again 3 times with pre-cooled 0.1 M PB. Gradient dehydration was performed with anhydrous ethanol and acetone at 4 °C in the dark (20 min per step), followed by gradient infiltration and embedding with 812 embedding medium (SPI Supplies, Cat# 90529-77-4). Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were prepared, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed under a HT7800 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi, Japan).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) for NF-κB

Biliary tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C, dehydrated with gradient ethanol, cleared with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial Sects. (5 μm thick) were cut, mounted on poly-L-lysine-coated slides, and baked at 60 °C for 2 h.

Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated with gradient ethanol, and subjected to microwave antigen retrieval in 0.01 M EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). After cooling to room temperature, sections were washed 3 times with 0.01 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature in the dark to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and washed again 3 times with PBS. Sections were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in a humidified chamber at 37 °C for 30 min.The blocking solution was discarded, and diluted primary antibody (rabbit anti-rat NF-κB p65 monoclonal antibody, Affinity, Cat# AF5006; 1:200 dilution) was added to the sections, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C in a humidified chamber. After 3 washes with PBS, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Boster, Cat# BA1054; 1:500 dilution) at 37 °C for 30 min, and washed again 3 times with PBS. DAB chromogenic solution (Boster) was added to the sections, and color development was monitored under a light microscope for 3–5 min, followed by rinsing with tap water to terminate the reaction. Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, followed by dehydration, clearing, and mounting with neutral balsam.Sections were observed under a light microscope, and semi-quantitative analysis was performed in a single-blinded manner using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). The integrated optical density (IntDen) of NF-κB positive staining was calculated to evaluate the protein expression level.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Serum levels of IL-1β and IL-18 were detected using commercial rat ELISA kits (Jianglai Bio, Cat# JL20236, JL20874) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, serum samples were thawed on ice and centrifuged at 4000 g for 5 min at 4 °C. The microplate was equilibrated at room temperature for 20 min, and standard wells, sample wells (10 μL serum + 40 μL sample diluent), and blank wells were set up in duplicate. 100 μL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody was added to all wells except the blank wells, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 60 min. After 5 washes, 50 μL of substrate A and 50 μL of substrate B were added to each well, and incubated for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The reaction was terminated by adding stop solution, and the optical density (OD) value at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentrations of IL-1β and IL-18 were calculated according to the standard curve.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from biliary tissues using TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, Cat# 15596026) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA purity and concentration were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and only RNA samples with OD260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.1 were used for subsequent experiments. RNA integrity was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

For mRNA reverse transcription: Genomic DNA was removed using a gDNA removal kit, and reverse transcription was performed using a HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (Vazyme, Cat# R323-01) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For miR-140-5p reverse transcription: Stem-loop RT primer was used for reverse transcription using a miRNA 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme, Cat# MQ101-01). The resulting cDNA was diluted fivefold and stored at − 20 °C.

qPCR amplification was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Cat# Q711-02) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The primer sequences are listed in Table 1. β-actin was used as the internal reference for TLR4, NF-κB, and TNF-α, while U6 was used as the internal reference for miR-140-5p. The stability of internal reference genes was verified by pre-experiment, and the amplification efficiency of all primers was between 95 and 105%. Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from biliary tissues using pre-cooled RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, Cat# P0013B) supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail (Servicebio, Cat# G2006) and phosphatase inhibitor (Beyotime, Cat# P1045). Tissues were homogenized on ice, incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, and protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, Cat# P0010).

Protein samples (20 μg per lane) were separated by SDS-PAGE with 5% stacking gel and 10% separating gel, and transferred to methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Millipore, Cat# IPVH00010) at a constant voltage of 120 V for 60 min. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature for 1 h, and washed 3 times with TBST (10 min each).

Membranes were incubated with the following primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C: rabbit anti-rat TLR4 monoclonal antibody (Affinity, Cat# AF7017; 1:1000), rabbit anti-rat NF-κB p65 monoclonal antibody (Affinity, Cat# AF5006; 1:1000), rabbit anti-rat TNF-α monoclonal antibody (Affinity, Cat# AF7014; 1:1000), and rabbit anti-rat β-actin monoclonal antibody (Affinity, Cat# AF7018; 1:1500). After 3 washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Boster, Cat# BA1054; 1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h.

Chemiluminescent detection was performed using an ECL kit (Millipore, Cat# WBKLS0500), and band images were acquired using a ChemiDoc XRS + gel imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). The grayscale value of each band was quantified using ImageJ software, and the relative protein expression was calculated as the ratio of target band grayscale to the internal reference β-actin band grayscale. Original uncropped blots are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA). Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). The normality of data was tested by Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was tested by Levene’s test. Multi-group comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), with LSD test for pairwise comparisons when variance was homogeneous, and Dunnett’s T3 test for pairwise comparisons when variance was heterogeneous. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Inhibition of miR-140-5p alleviates BDEC ultrastructural injury in LPS-induced cholangitis

TEM was used to observe the ultrastructural changes of BDECs in each group (Fig. 1). In the Control group, BDECs were arranged neatly with tight intercellular junctions, intact cell and nuclear membranes, regular nuclear morphology, intact mitochondrial bilayer membrane, dense matrix with neatly arranged cristae, and no dilation of the endoplasmic reticulum. In the LPS model group, BDECs showed typical pathological damage: significantly widened intercellular spaces, disrupted cell and mitochondrial membranes, irregular nuclear morphology, dissolved mitochondrial matrix, fractured and disappeared cristae, and markedly dilated endoplasmic reticulum. The pathological changes of BDECs in the LPS + NC adenovirus group were almost identical to those in the LPS model group, with no significant difference. In contrast, BDEC ultrastructural injury was markedly alleviated in the LPS + miR-140-5p inhibitor group and LPS + PDTC group compared with the LPS model group, with intact cell membrane structure, improved mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum morphology, and reduced intercellular space widening.Fig. 1Morphological changes of bile duct cells in each group. Discussion

Cholangitis is a progressive inflammatory disease of the intrahepatic and extrahepatic bile ducts, with a pathological core of structural damage and functional impairment of BDECs. Upon activation by exogenous stimuli, BDECs undergo pathological phenotypic changes and secrete large amounts of inflammatory mediators including cytokines and chemokines, which further recruit and activate infiltrating immune cells24. Activated immune cells such as neutrophils and macrophages can directly induce BDEC injury, and compromise the structural integrity of the biliary tract12. In this study, we successfully established a rat model of LPS-induced cholangitis, and observed marked inflammatory infiltration in the biliary tissues of the LPS group. Transmission electron microscopy revealed typical pathological alterations of BDECs in model rats, including widened intercellular spaces, disrupted cell and mitochondrial membranes, irregular nuclear morphology, dissolved mitochondrial matrix, fractured and disappeared cristae, and dilated endoplasmic reticulum. Immunohistochemistry and molecular detection further showed enhanced NF-κB protein expression, significantly elevated serum IL-1β and IL-18 levels, and upregulated mRNA and protein expression of TLR4, NF-κB and TNF-α in the LPS group. These findings confirm that NF-κB plays a critical regulatory role in cholangitis-related inflammatory responses, which is consistent with the results reported by Hu et al25 and Zhang et al26, and further corroborates the central role of NF-κB in the inflammatory cascade of cholangitis. Intervention with the NF-κB-specific inhibitor PDTC significantly reduced the expression of IL-1β, IL-18, TLR4 and TNF-α compared with the LPS group, further supporting a positive correlation between NF-κB activation and inflammatory response in this rat cholangitis model.

As a highly conserved small non-coding RNA, the inflammatory regulatory function of miR-140-5p exhibits significant tissue and microenvironment specificity, with diametrically opposite effects reported in different disease models27. Previous studies have shown that miR-140-5p exerts anti-inflammatory and protective effects by directly targeting the TNF-α signaling pathway and TLR4 receptor, thereby inhibiting NF-κB nuclear translocation, reducing the release of downstream pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-8, and ultimately blocking the uncontrolled positive feedback loop of inflammation28. In osteoarthritis models, exogenous miR-140-5p mimic significantly promotes chondrocyte proliferation and autophagy by inhibiting NF-κB p65 activation, and effectively delays joint degeneration29. In LPS-induced myocardial injury, miR-140-5p binds to TLR4 mRNA to promote its degradation, negatively regulates excessive activation of the TLR4/NF-κB pathway, and alleviates myocardial inflammatory injury29,30. This cross-tissue protective effect suggests that miR-140-5p may be a potential universal therapeutic target for regulating the cellular inflammation-repair balance. However, miRNAs exhibit significant functional heterogeneity in biliary tract diseases and related hepatic metabolic disorders. For example, miR-381-3p promotes HSC activation and biliary liver fibrosis by targeting Klf631, while miR-140-5p directly targets glycogen synthase 1 (GYS1) and protein phosphatase 1 catalytic subunit gamma (PPP1CC) to downregulate their expression and aggravate insulin resistance in insulin-resistant HepG2 cells20. Therefore, the specific regulatory role of miR-140-5p in biliary tract diseases remains to be elucidated, which is the core scientific question addressed in this study.

The results of this study showed that, compared with the LPS group, inhibition of miR-140-5p significantly reduced serum IL-1β and IL-18 levels, and markedly alleviated the ultrastructural injury of biliary tissues in rats, indicating that miR-140-5p expression is associated with the severity of cholangitis. Further molecular detection revealed that miR-140-5p inhibition was accompanied by downregulation of the mRNA and protein expression of TLR4, NF-κB and TNF-α in rat biliary tissues compared with the LPS group, suggesting that the anti-inflammatory effect of miR-140-5p inhibition may be related to modulation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.Consistent with previous published studies, miR-140-5p may indirectly regulate the NF-κB pathway by targeting and inhibiting negative regulators of the NF-κB cascade, which provides a theoretical basis for the in vivo findings of this study. The proposed miR-140-5p/TLR4/NF-κB regulatory axis is supported by published evidence in other cell types: miR-140-5p has been demonstrated to directly target TLR4 in renal tubular epithelial cells22, bronchial epithelial cells28, and intervertebral disc cells23. These findings provide a plausible mechanistic basis for our observations. However, the direct binding and transcriptional regulatory relationship between miR-140-5p and its target genes has not been verified by gold-standard assays such as dual-luciferase reporter assay and RNA pull-down in this study, and the specific regulatory mechanism remains to be further clarified in subsequent experiments.

We also observed no statistically significant differences in all detected indicators between the LPS + NC adenoviral vector group and the LPS group, which confirmed that the inflammatory phenotypic changes in this study were specifically mediated by miR-140-5p inhibition, excluding the non-specific interference of the adenoviral vector itself on the experimental results. Notably, miR-140-5p inhibition resulted in a downward trend of total NF-κB protein expression that did not reach statistical significance, which may be attributed to two main reasons: first, the tissue collection time point in this study was 7 days after modeling, which is the late stage of acute inflammatory response, and the change in total NF-κB protein level may be weakened at this time point, while its phosphorylation and nuclear translocation (the key steps of pathway activation) may still maintain a high level; second, miR-140-5p may regulate the NF-κB pathway indirectly via targeting upstream molecules such as TLR4, rather than directly targeting NF-κB itself, which may lead to a more moderate change in total NF-κB protein level.

In addition, this study found that PDTC-mediated NF-κB inhibition significantly upregulated miR-140-5p expression in rats compared with the LPS group, suggesting a potential negative regulatory relationship between NF-κB activation and miR-140-5p transcription. Based on these findings, we hypothesize a potential negative feedback regulatory loop between miR-140-5p and NF-κB in cholangitis progression: LPS-induced NF-κB activation drives the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and aggravates biliary injury; on the other hand, activated NF-κB negatively regulates miR-140-5p transcription, leading to downregulated miR-140-5p expression, which acts as an endogenous negative feedback mechanism to limit excessive NF-κB pathway activation and inflammatory amplification. This pattern is consistent with our experimental observations: miR-140-5p expression was paradoxically downregulated in the LPS-induced cholangitis group, while miR-140-5p inhibition alleviated inflammation. The combination of our miR-140-5p inhibitor experiment and PDTC experiment provides bidirectional indirect evidence supporting this proposed regulatory loop.

This potential regulatory loop provides a new perspective for understanding the inflammatory self-regulation mechanism in cholangitis, but the direct transcriptional regulatory relationship needs to be further verified by ChIP assay in subsequent studies.

It is important to acknowledge that cholangitis is fundamentally a multicellular pathological process involving dynamic crosstalk among biliary epithelial cells, immune cells, and mesenchymal cells within the biliary microenvironment. Our retrograde LPS injection model preserves these critical cell‑cell interactions, which cannot be fully recapitulated in a simplified monoculture system. Several recent studies investigating miRNA‑mediated regulation of biliary inflammation have been published based primarily on in vivo evidence, including studies on miR‑381‑3p (Gao et al., Regen Ther 2025), miR‑513 (Gong et al., J Immunol 2009), and β‑catenin‑NF‑κB‑CFTR interactions in cholangiocytes (Hu et al., eLife 2021). These precedents support the feasibility of publishing in vivo exploratory studies in this field. Nevertheless, in vitro validation (including LPS‑stimulated BDEC models with miR‑140‑5p modulation) remains a critical next step to dissect direct molecular mechanisms without the confounding factors of an in vivo system, and further studies will incorporate complementary in vitro experiments.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that need to be explicitly clarified. First, this study is a preliminary in vivo exploratory study, and we only verified the phenotypic effect of miR-140-5p inhibition on cholangitis, but did not clarify the direct molecular mechanism. The specific target genes of miR-140-5p in BDECs have not been verified by dual-luciferase reporter gene assay and RNA pull-down, and the direct transcriptional regulatory relationship between NF-κB and miR-140-5p has not been confirmed by ChIP assay, so the causal relationship of the miR-140-5p/NF-κB regulatory axis is not fully established. Second, this study only used a miR-140-5p inhibitor for intervention, and did not perform miR-140-5p overexpression experiments and in vivo/in vitro rescue experiments, so the bidirectional regulatory function of miR-140-5p in cholangitis cannot be fully verified. Third, we only detected total NF-κB p65 protein, but not phosphorylated p-p65 level and its nuclear translocation, which cannot accurately reflect the activation status of the NF-κB pathway. Fourth, this study only conducted in vivo experiments in rats, lacking in vitro mechanism verification at the BDEC level, and the sample size of each group was relatively small (n = 6). Finally, this study only observed the short-term effect of miR-140-5p inhibition in the cholangitis model, and its long-term effect on chronic cholangitis, biliary fibrosis, and even cholangiocarcinoma still needs further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this preliminary in vivo study demonstrates that miR-140-5p expression is downregulated in LPS-induced cholangitis in rats, and that pharmacological inhibition of miR-140-5p significantly alleviates biliary epithelial cell injury and inflammatory response, concomitant with suppression of the TLR4/NF-κB/TNF-α signaling pathway. Additionally, NF-κB inhibition by PDTC upregulated miR-140-5p expression, suggesting a potential negative regulatory relationship between NF-κB activation and miR-140-5p transcription. However, it is important to note that the present study provides correlative in vivo evidence rather than direct mechanistic proof. The specific target genes of miR-140-5p in biliary epithelial cells, the direct binding relationship, the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation status of NF-κB p65, and the bidirectional regulatory function of miR-140-5p (i.e., gain-of-function effects) remain to be elucidated by in vitro cellular experiments and direct molecular assays (dual-luciferase reporter assay, ChIP, and immunofluorescence for p65 nuclear translocation) in subsequent studies. Nonetheless, this study identifies miR-140-5p as a potential therapeutic target for cholangitis and provides a testable hypothesis for the miR-140-5p/NF-κB regulatory interplay in biliary inflammation, warranting further mechanistic investigation.
