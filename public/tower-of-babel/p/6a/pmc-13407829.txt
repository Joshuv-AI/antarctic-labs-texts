Artificial intelligence and transforming cancer care
Authors: Aneesha Mallu Reddy, Gurleen Kaur, Vincent Sean D Ribaya, Elizabeth Laurize A Ribaya, Mallu Chenna Reddy, Tariq Shah, Dheeraj Shinde, Gurparsad Singh Suri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial Intelligence (AI) is reshaping oncology by addressing key limitations in traditional cancer care and enabling data-driven, personalized approaches from diagnosis to treatment. This review explores the transformative role of AI across the cancer care continuum, highlighting its contributions, challenges, and future directions. AI has significantly advanced cancer detection and diagnosis by improving the interpretation of medical imaging (CT, MRI, PET scans, digital pathology) and liquid biopsies, allowing for early and accurate identification of tumors and biomarkers. In genomics and molecular profiling, AI facilitates the analysis of large-scale sequencing data to uncover actionable mutations and support targeted therapy decisions. This review also examines AI-powered prognostic models that integrate clinical, genomic, and electronic health record data to predict outcomes such as survival rates and recurrence risks, allowing for more precise treatment planning. In the therapeutic landscape, AI aids in optimizing radiation dosing, guiding surgical interventions, and predicting individual responses to chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted treatments, thereby reducing uncertainty and improving outcomes. Key limitations, such as data privacy concerns, algorithmic bias, model opacity, and integration hurdles are discussed, along with strategies to address them, including explainable AI, standardized validation, and clinician training. Looking ahead, innovations like federated learning, generative AI for drug discovery, and multimodal data integration are poised to enhance precision oncology further. By synthesizing current developments and emerging trends, this review underscores the potential of AI to drive equitable, efficient, and personalized cancer care on a global scale.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Cancer care, Oncology, AI in healthcare, Machine learning, Precision medicine

Background

Responsible for close to 10 million deaths annually, cancer continues to be a major global health challenge, profoundly affecting healthcare systems, economies, and societies around the world [1]. Despite significant advances in prevention, early detection, and treatment, the inherent complexity and heterogeneity of cancer continue to confound conventional therapeutic approaches. Tumors vary not only across patients (inter-tumor heterogeneity) but also within individual tumors (intra-tumor heterogeneity), driven by diverse genetic mutations, epigenetic changes, microenvironmental influences, and variable metastatic potential [2, 3]. As a result, standard “one-size-fits-all” therapies such as chemotherapy and radiation often yield variable patient responses, provide limited survival benefits, contribute to unnecessary toxicity, and foster drug resistance and delivery challenges [4].

These challenges have catalyzed a paradigm shift toward personalized, or precision, medicine, an approach that seeks to tailor treatment strategies to the unique clinical, molecular, and genetic characteristics of each patient [5]. Enabled by high-throughput sequencing, biomarker discovery, and multi-omics integration, precision oncology has led to the development of targeted therapies against actionable mutations, such as HER2 (Human Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor 2) in breast cancer or EGFR (Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor) in non-small cell lung cancers [6, 7]. However, the translation of personalized medicine into widespread clinical practice is hindered by the complexity and scale of data generated from genomic, proteomic, radiologic, electronic health record (EHR) and other such sources.

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly seen as a powerful tool for addressing these challenges. AI represents a broad class of computational methods enabling machines to perform tasks requiring human intelligence. Machine learning (ML), a subset of AI, includes algorithms that learn patterns from data without explicit programming, while deep learning (DL) a subfield of ML uses multi-layered neural networks for complex, hierarchical data representations (Fig. 1). This review employs precise terminology contextually, with "AI" signifying broader computational intelligence [8]. These tools are advancing the frontiers of oncology by enabling predictive modelling of treatment response, stratification of patient subgroups, identification of novel drug targets, and optimization of combination therapies [9, 10]. AI is also enhancing radiomics, digital pathology, drug repurposing, and also the design of adaptive clinical trials [11, 12].

Challenges in traditional cancer treatment

Traditional cancer therapies like chemotherapy, radiation therapy, surgical resection, and systemic treatments have long formed the cornerstone of cancer care, yet their efficacy is constrained by a multitude of biological, technical, and systemic challenges that collectively contribute to suboptimal patient outcomes [14]. A primary limitation lies in the non-specific cytotoxicity of many conventional therapies, which target rapidly dividing cells without discriminating between malignant and healthy tissues. Chemotherapeutic agents, for instance, induce acute side effects such as myelosuppression, mucositis, alopecia, and neuropathy. Long-term sequelae, including secondary malignancies, cardiotoxicity, and organ dysfunction, further complicate survivorship care [15, 16]. Radiation therapy similarly inflicts collateral damage on adjacent healthy tissues, causing acute dermatitis, mucositis, and chronic fibrosis, while its cumulative effects may predispose patients to secondary cancers [17, 18]. The physical and psychological burden of these adverse effects often prompts dose reductions or treatment interruptions, eventually undermining the therapeutic efficacy [19, 20].

The emergence of drug resistance represents another critical barrier, driven by the adaptive capacity of cancer cells to evade therapeutic interventions. Resistance mechanisms include genetic mutations altering drug targets, upregulation of efflux pumps that expel chemotherapeutic agents, enhanced DNA repair pathways that counteract radiation-induced damage, and activation of compensatory signalling cascades [21, 22]. Furthermore, intrinsic resistance rooted in tumor heterogeneity drives both inherent and acquired resistance, as diverse cancer cell subpopulations with distinct molecular profiles survive therapy and evolve under treatment pressure [22, 23]. This genetic, epigenetic, and phenotypic variability creates subclones with differing growth, metastatic, and drug-response traits, ultimately limiting the efficacy of targeted and conventional treatments [3, 24]. Late detection and widespread metastasis further exacerbate these challenges [25, 26]. Most cancers are diagnosed symptomatically, after dissemination, limiting curative options like surgery or radiation and rendering systemic treatments less effective [27, 28].

Systemic inequities and economic burdens further impede equitable cancer care. Disparities in access to timely diagnosis and advanced therapies disproportionately affect low-resource and rural populations, exacerbating survival gaps across socioeconomic and geographic groups [29]. The high cost of treatment imposes financial toxicity on patients and healthcare systems, limiting access to newer, more effective therapies in resource-constrained settings [30]. Traditional "one-size-fits-all" approaches to treatment selection often overlook individual variations in tumor biology and patient-specific factors, leading to unnecessary toxicity and suboptimal responses [31, 32]. Despite advances in genomic, proteomic, and imaging technologies, their integration into clinical decision-making remains underutilized, delaying the realization of precision oncology. [31] These multifaceted challenges underscore the urgent need for transformative approaches, including the integration of artificial intelligence in cancer treatment, which must overcome both scientific and systemic barriers to deliver equitable, personalized, and effective cancer care.

AI in cancer detection and diagnosis

AI, particularly ML and DL, is transforming cancer detection and diagnosis, offering significant potential to improve patient outcomes through earlier detection, more accurate diagnosis, and personalised treatment strategies. As of 2025, meaningful developments have been made, showcasing AI's ability to analyse vast datasets, interpret complex medical images, and predict patient outcomes.

Medical imaging in early diagnosis

Medical imaging has been a leading application area for AI in oncology. Digital modalities like radiographs, CT, MRI and whole-slide histopathology generate large, structured datasets well-suited for DL. Addressing the traditional limitation of subjective image interpretation and interobserver variability, AI tools such as Sybil leverage low-dose CT scans to predict lung cancer risk with 80–95% accuracy, often detecting tumors before human radiologists discern visible changes [33]. Similarly, the CHIEF model, trained on millions of histopathology images, achieves 94% accuracy in diagnosing 19 cancer types and predicts patient survival by analysing tumor microenvironments [34, 35]. In breast cancer screening, AI has helped improve detection rates by 29% while reducing radiologist workload by 44%, as demonstrated in the landmark MASAI trial [36]. A recent study in Nature demonstrated that AI system outperformed radiologists in detecting breast cancer from mammograms, reducing false positives by 5.7% and false negatives by 9.4% (U.S.A study) [37]. Similarly, Campanella et al. trained a deep network on > 44 000 whole‐slide images (WSIs) of prostate, skin and breast biopsies to achieve AUC (areas under the curve) > 0.98 for cancer detection, permitting pathologists to safely exclude 65–75% of slides while retaining 100% sensitivity [38]. AI assistance in biopsy grading has also boosted performance: Bulten et al. reported that pathologists aided by a deep‐learning Gleason grader significantly raised agreement with expert consensus and outperformed either unassisted readers or the AI alone [39]. Commercial tools (e.g. the FDA-cleared Paige Prostate suite) similarly highlight suspicious regions on prostate biopsies, improving diagnostic accuracy and efficiency.[40] In cervical cancer screening, a recent AI pipeline (AICCS) achieved very high metrics (prospective AUC 0.947, sensitivity 0.946, specificity 0.890) and in a randomized trial increased cytopathologist sensitivity by ~ 13% [41]. AI is also used in breast imaging (e.g. ProFound AI in mammography), where it flags subtle lesions with high sensitivity to improve early detection and workflow efficiency [42]. Another AI model, LYNA (Lymph Node Assistant), achieved 99% accuracy in identifying metastatic breast cancer in lymph node biopsies [43]. Similarly in lung cancer: The National Cancer Institute’s NLST (National Lung Screening Trial) data trained an AI model that detected lung nodules with 94.4% sensitivity, surpassing radiologists’ performance [44]. Koh et al. similarly note that many promising imaging-AI tools exist, but widespread clinical translation remains limited by data and validation bottlenecks [45]. These studies agree that to harness AI’s power, large well-curated image repositories (mammography, CT, pathology slides) and multidisciplinary development (radiology, pathology, data science) are essential [45].

AI in genomics and molecular profiling

Beyond imaging, AI especially ML and DL is increasingly applied to molecular and multi-omic data in cancer. Advances in sequencing and multi-omics have created “big data” in oncology (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, epigenetics) that traditional statistics struggle to handle. AI methods can integrate these diverse datasets to improve early detection and subtype classification [46]. For instance, AlphaMissense an AI model, built on AlphaFold, predicts pathogenicity in missense mutations across the human genome, aiding early cancer risk stratification [47, 48]. Similarly neural networks trained on cancer gene expression profiles can predict tumor origin or prognosis with high accuracy. For example, Ozaki et al. recently reviewed 89 recent studies on AI in cancer prognosis and note a growing emphasis on integrating multi-omics data (clinical records, radiomics, pathomics and lab data) thus effectively combining imaging phenotypes with molecular and clinical features for diagnosis [49]. To enhance early cancer detection, advanced genomic tools are demonstrating significant promise. DeepVariant, for instance, improves the identification of cancer-driving mutations by leveraging high-precision variant calling, which refines genomic analysis and strengthens the foundation for effective early screening strategies [50]. The emergence of single-cell multi-omics technologies has revolutionized our understanding of tumor heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution. Single-cell sequencing approaches encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, proteomics, and spatial omics enable dissection of tumor complexity at single-cell resolution with multi-layered depth, illuminating tumor biology, immune escape mechanisms, treatment resistance, and patient-specific immune response mechanisms [51]. AI frameworks leveraging these technologies are particularly powerful for non-invasive early cancer detection. For example, iCanTCR harness deep learning to analyse T-cell receptor repertoires in peripheral blood, offering a non-invasive method to detect cancer at earlier stages by identifying tumor-reactive T-cell clonotypes without requiring tissue biopsies.[52] Together, these technologies highlight the growing role of computational approaches in advancing oncology diagnostics. Despite these breakthroughs, challenges persist, including algorithmic bias, data privacy concerns, and the need for robust validation across diverse populations [53].

AI in cancer prognosis

In clinical oncology, a substantial body of research is dedicated to predicting patient responses to therapy and estimating prognostic outcomes. Accurate prognostic assessments enable the delivery of more effective, individualized treatment strategies, thereby enhancing patient outcomes. Addressing the traditional limitation of imprecise prognostication using conventional staging systems, AI has emerged as a pivotal tool in this domain, capable of processing complex, multi-dimensional datasets derived from diverse clinical evaluations. By leveraging such data, AI systems can generate more precise predictions. Numerous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of AI models in forecasting key prognostic indicators, including survival outcomes, the risk of disease recurrence, and responses to specific treatment modalities. For example, recently Kawakami et.al developed a machine learning framework using pretreatment blood tests and age to predict key clinical parameters (stage, histotype, residual disease, prognosis) in ovarian cancer. Ensemble methods, particularly Random Forest, outperformed conventional analyses, achieving high accuracy (e.g., 92.4% for distinguishing malignant from benign tumors and 69.0% for staging). The system accurately predicted specific histotypes and surgical outcomes and identified early-stage subgroups with differing survival [54]. Similarly, Researchers trained a deep neural network on 129,450 skin lesion images to classify skin cancer. When tested against 21 dermatologists on critical tasks (identifying common and deadly cancers), the AI achieved performance on par with the experts demonstrating the potential for AI, particularly to provide widespread, low-cost dermatologist-level skin cancer prognosis and diagnosis [55]. Furthermore, Wang et. Al. showed, an AI model (CHIEF) that demonstrates significant promise by extracting robust histopathological features from whole-slide images to predict patient outcomes (pretrained on 60,530 slides), it substantially outperforms existing methods (by up to 36.1%) in prognostic tasks, exhibiting strong generalizability across diverse populations and laboratory protocols in international validations (19,491 slides from 24 hospitals) [35]. Another study by Bai et.al. developed an AI algorithm (using QuPath) to automatically quantify tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) from standard H&E slides in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). Validated across 749 patients in multiple cohorts, the AI-derived TIL metrics were robust, independent prognostic factors, significantly associated with improved overall survival even after accounting for standard clinical factors. The tool offered an objective method to overcome observer variability in prognosis [56]. Similarly, zhang et. al. developed an AI systems using nasopharyngeal MRI to diagnose and predict prognosis in natural killer/T-cell lymphoma (NKTCL). The prognostic AI predicted survival outcomes more effectively than clinical models and crucially identified that only high-risk early-stage patients significantly benefited from early radiotherapy (hazard ratio = 0.414, p = 0.022). demonstrating AI's potential for optimizing risk-stratified treatment in NKTCL [57]. Another group of researchers developed an AI-derived genomic mutation signature (GMS) to predict prognosis in gastrointestinal cancer patients receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors. The GMS reliably stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups across multiple cohorts, outperforming conventional biomarkers, and correlated with distinct immune microenvironments. The AI approach also identified UMI-77 as a potential therapeutic compound showing higher sensitivity in high-risk cell lines [58]. Similarly in a large international PI-CAI (Prostate Imaging Cancer Artificial Intelligence) study, an AI system demonstrated superior diagnostic accuracy (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [AUROC] 0.91 vs 0.86) compared to 62 radiologists using PI-RADS 2.1 for detecting clinically significant prostate cancer (csPCa) on MRI, detecting 6.8% more csPCa at the same specificity. While comparable to the real-world multidisciplinary standard of care, the AI significantly reduced false positives. This highlights AI's strong potential as a supportive diagnostic tool to improve prostate cancer prognosis [59].

AI initially used for non-medical purposes, are now significantly transforming healthcare, particularly in oncology. They hold immense potential for improving cancer prognosis, prediction, and treatment, moving towards a more effective care. Despite AIs potential to combat cancer, further research is essential to establish the clinical value and validity of these algorithms.

AI in treatment and therapy personalization

Recent advances in AIs are enabling more personalized and effective cancer treatment. Traditional approaches often apply the same treatment approach, which proves ineffective for many patients. For instance, without careful selection, immunotherapies benefit only about 20–40% of recipients [60, 61]. More recent data indicate that while eligibility for immune checkpoint inhibitors has expanded to 55.47% of patients, the estimated response rate is 19.60% overall, with considerable variation depending on specific tumor types (such as non-small cell lung cancer, hepatocellular, and renal cell carcinoma) and biomarker status [62]. This underscores the critical need for AI-driven patient stratification. AI models have the potential to overcome these challenges by analysing patient data (medical history, genetic information, scans etc.) to stratify patients based on their likelihood of response. This spares non-responders from unnecessary toxicity directing them toward alternative treatments. AI's applications in cancer therapy can be broadly subdivided into key areas: radiation oncology, surgical oncology, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy. We will explore each of these areas in detail below.

AI in radiation oncology

Fuelled by AI, radiation oncology has entered an era of unprecedented technological transformation. Addressing the traditional challenge of time-consuming treatment planning and interobserver variability in contouring, the field is well-positioned to harness AI, leading to major improvements across the entire radiotherapy process from treatment planning to outcome prediction. Beyond enhancing technical efficiency, AI holds the potential to tackle key challenges in personalized, precision medicine. For example, a study by Yalamanchili et. al. evaluated ChatGPT-3.5's responses to 115 radiation oncology patient questions against expert answers. The LLMs (large language models) matched or exceeded expert correctness in 94% of responses and completeness in 77%, with minimal risk of harm (only 2 responses flagged). Demonstrating strong potential for accurate, comprehensive patient communication [63]. Similarly, a recent study developed and comprehensively evaluated a deep learning model for auto-segmentation of OARs and CTVs in cervical cancer radiotherapy. The model achieved clinically acceptable geometric accuracy (DSC > 0.80 for most structures) and significantly reduced contouring time by 30 min while decreasing inter-physician variability, as validated by a Turing test and multi-institutional physician assessment. Though not replacing humans, the model demonstrated strong clinical utility as an efficient tool for real-world implementation [64]. Another such study by Cambridge researchers implemented an AI system within the NHS (National Health Service) to automate the time-consuming contouring of healthy organs on radiotherapy planning scans for head/neck cancer patients. This reduced contouring time per patient from approximately 2 h to just 30 min, freeing up over 80 clinical hours per week. Consequently, the AI significantly accelerated treatment pathways, reducing waiting times for patients awaiting radiotherapy [65]. Other commercial platforms Varian's Ethos Therapy[66, 67] and Elekta's MOSAIQ Plaza[68, 69] leverage AI to transform radiation treatment planning. They automate critical workflow elements, employ advanced modelling to optimize dose delivery, and dynamically adjust treatment plans as needed throughout therapy to account for anatomical variations or evolving tumor dimensions. In conclusion AI is reshaping radiation oncology from a tech-focused field into an information science. While current uses revolve around workflow management and imaging, the integration of AI with multi-omics can pave the way for personalized radiotherapy. Achieving this will require equitable access, high-quality data and transparent models.

Surgical oncology

Advancements in AI technologies, including ML, computer vision, and DL, are deeply altering surgical oncology practice. Overcoming traditional surgical limitations including subjective preoperative assessment and intraoperative decision-making uncertainty, these technologies are providing novel capabilities for preoperative planning, intraoperative guidance, and postoperative assessment, resulting in surgical interventions that are more precise, personalized, and data-driven. For example, in epithelial ovarian cancer, machine learning models utilizing routine blood biomarkers were able to preoperatively predict tumor stage, histotype, residual disease burden, and prognosis, supporting personalized surgical strategies [54, 70]. For prostate cancer, a support vector machine-based model demonstrated superior performance over artificial neural networks in predicting advanced disease based on biopsy data, aiding surgical risk assessment [71]. Similarly, in gastric cancer, a decision support system leveraging CT radiomics and Clinicopathologic data effectively predicted lymph node metastasis preoperatively, outperforming conventional staging criteria [72]. For intraoperative guidance a recent study presented a near-real-time intraoperative diagnostic tool for brain tumors that integrates stimulated Raman histology (SRH) with deep convolutional neural networks (CNNs). Trained on over 2.5 million images, the CNNs classify tumor types in under 150 s, significantly faster than traditional pathology. A multicentre clinical trial (n = 278) showed CNN-based accuracy (94.6%) comparable to expert pathologists. The approach enabled rapid, automated diagnosis and tumor margin identification without reliance on standard pathology labs [73]. Similarly In thoracic oncology, an AI system based on 3D convolutional networks assessed pulmonary nodules (≤ 3 cm) intraoperatively, showing AUCs of 0.80–0.88 for malignancy and invasiveness prediction, and 97% specificity for invasive adenocarcinoma, closely aligning with frozen section performance (0.69 concordance) [74]. Furthermore, shi et. al. showed, in case of metastatic spinal tumors, intraoperative planning was supported by a machine learning model (XGBoost), which predicted massive blood loss (≥ 2,500 cc) aiding surgical preparedness and resource allocation [75]. Similarly In colorectal surgery, AI-enhanced near-infrared (NIR) imaging using indocyanine green (ICG) is enabling real-time tissue characterization, offering explainable digital biopsies and perfusion-based margin assessment with accuracy approaching 80%, with future applications targeting intraoperative T-staging and molecular profiling [76].

Building on this preoperative and intraoperative foundation, AI, is also extending its utility into the postoperative phase, where it supports outcome prediction, complication monitoring, and personalized recovery planning [77, 78]. By integrating intraoperative data with postoperative clinical metrics, AI systems are being developed to anticipate complications such as infections, anastomotic leaks, and recurrence risks, thereby optimizing follow-up protocols and improving long-term oncologic outcomes [79, 80]. This seamless transition from preoperative to intraoperative decision-making to postoperative assessment marks a new era of continuous, AI-guided surgical oncology care. Collectively, enhancing the precision and reliability of surgical planning across diverse cancer types.

Chemotherapy and personalized therapy

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone of cancer treatment even today, but its effectiveness is often hindered by major challenges such as drug resistance, systemic toxicity, narrow therapeutic windows, and significant variability in patient response. These limitations frequently result in suboptimal outcomes and unnecessary patient harm due to trial-and-error treatment strategies [81, 82]. Addressing these traditional limitations, AI is rapidly emerging as a powerful tool to overcome these challenges. By harnessing machine learning models trained on complex biological datasets, AI holds transformative potential in areas like predicting individual treatment responses, identifying drug resistance, reducing toxicity, and accelerating drug discovery and repurposing. This data-driven approach represents a shift away from traditional methods, enabling truly personalized chemotherapy regimens that optimize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. For instance, a recent study by Li et. al presented a non-invasive multitask AI system designed to predict residual cancer burden (RCB) early during neoadjuvant chemotherapy in breast cancer. Using pre- and mid-treatment MRI data from over 1000 patients, the model integrates radiomics and deep learning features to classify RCB groups with high accuracy (AUC up to 0.976). Validated across multiple cohorts, the system enables early identification of poor responders (RCB III), potentially guiding treatment adjustments [83]. Similarly, a deep learning-based CT model demonstrated good performance in diagnosing signet-ring cell carcinoma in gastric cancer (AUC 0.786, sensitivity 77.3%, specificity 69.2%) and predicting chemotherapy benefit, with AI-identified low-risk patients achieving significantly longer survival (54.4 vs. 31.0 months; p = 0.036) [84]. Another study by Finkelstein et. al. showed that a machine learning model for chemotherapy symptom trajectory prediction achieved 81% accuracy and 87% precision in 3-day intervals, allowing early interventions to improve patient quality of life [85]. Furthermore, in metastatic colorectal cancer, a meta-analysis of 26 studies showed AI models reached pooled AUCs of 0.90 (training) and 0.83 (validation), with some achieving up to 99% sensitivity and 100% specificity using delta-radiomics and gene signatures [86]. While in biliary tract cancer, AI-powered spatial analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes effectively stratified patients for anti-PD1 therapy, with the “inflamed” group showing markedly better responses (ORR 27.5% vs. 7.7%) and survival outcomes (OS 12.6 vs. 5.1 months; p = 0.002). These studies collectively affirm AI’s growing role in tailoring chemotherapy decisions, predicting toxicity, and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [87].

Moving further deep towards personalized therapy, AI has been a game changer. The integration of multi-omics data, such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and radiomics with AI has enabled an unprecedented level of treatment customization. This combination of big data and advanced analytics are yielding highly accurate predictive models, offering insights that were previously unattainable. For instance, CURATE.AI, a small-data phenotypic platform, is coming up as a promising alternative to traditional, population-based approaches. Unlike big-data models constrained by limited and variable datasets, CURATE.AI uses individualized patient data to optimize both drug choice and dosage dynamically, improving therapeutic outcomes while reducing side effects [88, 89]. In the PRECISE CURATE.AI feasibility trial (NCT04522284), 10 patients with advanced solid tumors received dynamically modulated chemotherapy. The AI-informed dosing led to a 20% (± 13.8%) average reduction in prescribed capecitabine doses, with CURATE.AI recommendations accepted in 26 out of 27 cases where considered. These results suggest that AI-powered dynamic dosing may significantly enhance treatment personalization, warranting further investigation in larger, randomized trials [88, 89]. Furthermore, these AI models (CURATE.AI and Hartung) are also aid in real-time dosing and toxicity prediction in nanomedicine [90]. In prostate cancer, AI has helped enhance nanocarrier-based drug delivery by optimizing design and pharmacokinetics, enabling safer, targeted, and patient-specific treatments despite ongoing challenges with data and regulation [91]. A case study on temozolomide revealed that AI-driven personalized dosing protocols reduced tumor mass by an average of 67.2 g over 12 cycles while lowering toxicity rates, underscoring AI’s clinical potential in tailoring cancer therapies [92].

These above advancements collectively highlight how AI is reshaping cancer therapy enabling more precise, efficient, and adaptive treatment strategies that accounts for individual variability.

Immunotherapy

In the case of immunotherapy, the story is analogous. AI is rapidly transforming this field, offering novel approaches to enhance the precision, efficacy, and personalization of treatments. Immunotherapy, which harnesses the body’s immune system to fight cancer, has demonstrated remarkable success in certain malignancies. For example, Rakaee, et. al developed and validated a deep learning model to predict immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) response in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) using H&E-stained histology images. The model outperformed or matched conventional biomarkers like PD-L1, TMB, and TILs in both internal and external cohorts, showing an AUC of up to 0.75. It was also independently associated with improved progression-free and overall survival. Combining the AI model with PD-L1 further enhanced predictive performance, suggesting its potential for improving personalized immunotherapy strategies [93]. Building on these histopathology-based approaches, recent AI tools have demonstrated remarkable performance using routine clinical data. The LORIS (Logistic Regression-Based Immunotherapy-Response Score) model, developed by NIH researchers and published in 2024, uses six readily available clinical features (like patient age, cancer type, history of systemic therapy, blood albumin level, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and tumor mutational burden) to predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Validated across 2881 patients with 18 solid tumor types, LORIS accurately predicted response likelihood and survival outcomes, notably identifying patients with low tumor mutational burden who could still benefit from immunotherapy [94, 95]. More recently in 2025, the SCORPIO model demonstrated even greater predictive accuracy (72–76% performance) in distinguishing patients more or less likely to benefit from immune checkpoint inhibitors, using similar routine clinical parameters and showing superior performance to FDA-approved biomarker tests [96]. These AI tools represent a paradigm shift toward accessible, cost-effective patient stratification that does not require expensive molecular sequencing [96].

Similarly in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), researchers developed an AI-based stemness-related gene signature (SRS) using single-cell RNA sequencing and a combination of machine learning and deep learning methods. The SRS accurately stratified patients into high- and low-risk groups for prognosis and immunotherapy response. High-risk tumors showed lower immune cell infiltration, and knockdown of the key gene CKS1B significantly reduced cancer cell proliferation and invasion [97]. Furthermore, in gastrointestinal cancers, an AI-derived genomic mutation signature (GMS) predicted overall survival at 6, 12, and 24 months with high accuracy across three public datasets. Low-risk tumors had more active immune environments, and the small molecule UMI-77 showed stronger effects on high-risk cells, suggesting therapeutic potential [58]. Additionally, a recurrent neural network model simulated the interaction between radiotherapy (PULSAR) and PD-L1 immunotherapy in a mouse model, revealing how treatment timing influences tumor response and supporting the development of optimized combination therapies [98]. Moreover, a deep learning model analysed 8,646 tumors from 29 cancer types and identified four distinct genomic clusters with different immune profiles. When applied to melanoma datasets, one cluster with high microsatellite instability but poor immune activity showed limited benefit from immunotherapy [99]. Finally, a Bayesian network model built from clinical trial data in metastatic renal cell carcinoma (mRCC) predicted survival outcomes with AUC values of 0.74, 0.71, and 0.68 at 12, 24, and 36 months, improving to 0.86 when 12-month treatment response data were included. External validation confirmed its robustness, outperforming standard clinical risk scores [100]. Together, these studies highlight the value of AI in predicting outcomes, and guiding personalized cancer immunotherapy strategies using integrative, data-driven approaches.

Challenges and limitations

As discussed earlier, AI present numerous opportunities in cancer care. It holds immense potential to improve cancer detection, diagnosis, and treatment planning. However, its widespread adoption is hindered by a complex interplay of data-related, technical, ethical, regulatory, biological, economic, and implementation challenges [101, 102]. First, the lack of large, well-annotated datasets particularly for rare cancers, early-stage tumors, and underrepresented groups leads to class imbalances that bias models toward common cases. For instance, a melanoma diagnostic AI trained predominantly on dermatological images from Caucasian cohorts often exhibits higher misdiagnosis rates for lesions in patients with darker skin types, [103, 104] just as algorithms trained mainly on prevalent lung cancer subtypes might misclassify rare neuroendocrine tumors. [105, 106] Data heterogeneity, stemming from variations in imaging hardware (MRI, CT, histopathology scanners) and protocols (staining techniques, resolution settings), further complicates feature representation, while annotation costs, interobserver variability, and inconsistent labelling standards introduce noise and undermine ground‐truth reliability [101, 102]. Limited access to sufficiently large, high-quality multicentre datasets often results in incomplete or noisy clinical records and imaging archives, further constraining model robustness and external validity. Technically, most AI models have only been evaluated retrospectively in single‐centre studies, and prospective trials demonstrating improved patient outcomes such as longer overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) remain scarce. To address this validation gap and build clinical trust, AI algorithms should incorporate clinical endpoints like OS and PFS as evaluation indicators from the design stage onwards, with multicentre prospective trial protocols developed following established frameworks such as the PI-CAI consortium. Without such rigorous validation, clinician trust and willingness to integrate AI into workflows remain limited; integration itself requires substantial infrastructure upgrades to EHRs, picture archiving and communication systems (PACS), and robust DevOps pipelines, as well as extensive staff training to prevent alert fatigue or workflow disruption [107, 108]. Algorithms that overfit to specific training data often suffer performance degradation when exposed to scans, lab results, or patient populations from different centres, and the “black‐box” nature of deep learning models exacerbates trust issues, making it difficult for physicians to understand or validate AI‐driven diagnoses and treatment recommendations [101, 109].

Processing large, high‐resolution images or combining multimodal data (imaging, genomics, proteomics, and EHRs) places heavy demands on computing resources and network security, requiring interoperable standards and secure data management to handle sensitive medical information in real time [110, 111]. On the regulatory front, fragmented approval pathways from FDA clearances in the United States to CE markings in Europe and CDSCO oversight in India lack standardized benchmarks for safety, efficacy, and continuous post‐market surveillance, especially for adaptive AI systems that evolve over time. Unclear reimbursement policies and intellectual property tensions between proprietary models and open‐science approaches further impede validation and adoption [107, 108].

Biologically, intrinsic tumor heterogeneity, both within individual tumors and across patient cohorts and the dynamic nature of tumor evolution limit model robustness and generalizability, as models trained in one environment often falter when deployed elsewhere due to domain shifts in imaging protocols, sequencing platforms, or clinical workflows [102, 112]. Static AI models, in particular, suffer from performance degradation during long-term follow-up as they cannot adapt to the evolving biological characteristics of tumors. To overcome these limitations, dynamically updatable AI models based on tumor heterogeneity-temporal evolution theory have emerged as a promising solution. From a technical implementation standpoint, these models typically utilize continuous learning algorithms such as elastic weight consolidation, Bayesian updating, or online reinforcement learning which allow the architecture to incorporate new longitudinal data without suffering from catastrophic forgetting of baseline features [113–115]. These adaptive frameworks integrate tumor evolution operators that process real-time data inputs and facilitate regular parameter adjustments, enabling the models to continuously recalibrate in response to temporal changes in tumor biology and maintain predictive accuracy throughout disease progression [116–118]. Crucially, the clinical validation of such dynamic systems requires specialized protocols; this includes initial "shadow-mode" deployment parallel to standard clinical workflows to monitor calibration drift [119], followed by prospective, multi-center adaptive clinical trials to continuously validate sensitivity, specificity, and safety across evolving patient cohorts [120]. Furthermore economically, high costs associated with data annotation, model development, infrastructure, and ongoing maintenance pose formidable barriers, particularly for resource‐constrained institutions and proving long‐term cost‐effectiveness by demonstrating reductions in overall cancer care costs or measurable improvements in patient outcomes remains challenging [101, 110, 112]. Finally, clinician and patient acceptance hinge on education that clarifies how AI complements rather than replaces human expertise, and on explainable outputs that facilitate informed consent discussions; Building model transparency and fostering clinical trust necessitates the integration of explainable AI algorithms, such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations), and Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping), alongside visual case analyses that demonstrate how AI-derived insights inform and support medical decision-making processes [121]. Without transparent reporting, standardized validation guidelines, and collaborative, interdisciplinary efforts to build diverse, privacy‐preserving datasets, adapt regulatory frameworks, and develop cost‐effective deployment strategies (such as federated learning, cloud‐based solutions, and open‐source platforms), the transformative promise of AI in oncology will remain constrained by these multifaceted limitations. While these technical, biological, and implementation barriers are substantial, they intersect with equally important ethical concerns related to data governance, fairness, transparency, and accountability, which are examined in detail in the following section on Ethical Considerations.

Ethical considerations

The integration of AI into cancer care marks a paradigm shift, but it also raises serious ethical issues, that necessitate systematic evaluation across multiple domains encompassing privacy, data governance, algorithmic equity, interpretability, and legal accountability frameworks [107, 108]. In structuring this discussion, we draw on the framework for AI ethics in oncology proposed by Hantel et al., which delineates key domains such as data governance, algorithmic equity, interpretability, and legal accountability, thereby helping to distinguish ethical concerns from purely technical limitations [122]. AI development requires extensive patient data utilization, raising concerns regarding secondary data use beyond standard clinical consent parameters, with current HIPAA regulations inadequately addressing de-identified health records used for AI training, creating gaps in patient privacy protection. Moreover, strict privacy laws (such as HIPAA, the General Data Protection Regulation [GDPR], and related national frameworks) and institutional data silos impede multi‐centre data sharing, constraining the development of diverse, representative training cohorts and amplifying concerns around data governance and patient autonomy. Notable data breaches, including the 2024 exposure of 113,789 oncology patient records, underscore the vulnerability of large-scale AI datasets and the imperative for robust security protocols [123, 124]. Training dataset composition significantly influences model performance across demographic subgroups, as recent analyses of melanoma detection algorithms revealed substantial performance degradation on darker skin phenotypes (Fitzpatrick IV-VI), with models trained on predominantly Caucasian datasets (≈95% light skin) demonstrating reduced precision and recall for underrepresented populations, while clinical surveys indicate 76.5% of oncologists recognize their responsibility to mitigate biased AI implementations [104, 122]. The "black box" nature of deep learning models poses additional ethical challenges in clinical decision-making, with survey data demonstrating 84.8% of oncologists requiring explainable AI systems before clinical adoption, as regulatory frameworks increasingly emphasize interpretability requirements for AI-enabled medical devices [122, 125]. Furthermore, current medico-legal structures lack specific AI-related liability provisions, and while 90.7% of surveyed oncologists attribute primary responsibility to AI developers, existing tort law maintains physician accountability under standard-of-care doctrines, creating ambiguity in clinical implementation and professional liability frameworks [122]. While these ethical issues are substantial, they are not insurmountable, provided that stakeholders commit to collaborative problem-solving and evidence-based solution development.

Future perspectives

As AI technologies evolve, one of the most transformative advances will be the fusion of diverse data streams, medical imaging, genomic and proteomic profiles, digital pathology, EHRs, and patient-reported outcomes into unified multi-modal models that deliver a comprehensive picture of each patient’s cancer. By synthesizing these varied inputs, AI systems will enable more accurate diagnostics, finer risk stratification, and truly personalized treatment planning [102, 126]. At the same time, privacy-preserving approaches such as federated learning will become standard practice, allowing AI models to learn from data across institutions without ever exposing sensitive patient information, thereby enhancing both data security and model robustness across diverse populations. Generative AI will further drive breakthroughs by designing novel drug candidates, predicting molecular interactions, and simulating treatment effects, while also identifying new biomarkers and neoantigens to power next-generation immunotherapies [126, 127]. Crucially, lightweight AI algorithms deployed on mobile or cloud platforms will democratize cancer screening and diagnostics in low-resource settings, helping to close global gaps in care. Explainable AI techniques will be integrated at every level to ensure that clinicians and patients understand model outputs, promoting trust and enabling shared decision-making [127, 128]. Looking ahead, adaptive AI systems that continuously learn from real-time clinical data such as wearable devices, liquid biopsies, and ongoing imaging will support dynamic treatment adjustments tailored to each patient’s changing condition [102, 129]. Successfully bringing this vision to life will depend on close collaboration among researchers, clinicians, industry partners, regulators, and ethicists, all working together to establish flexible regulatory pathways, interdisciplinary training programs, and ethical guidelines. Central to this endeavour is the need to quantify AI ethics and fairness through robust evaluation systems that incorporate metrics such as demographic parity and equal opportunity, ensuring these measures are integrated into the model training process itself. By embedding fairness assessments at the foundational level, we can proactively address biases and disparities that may otherwise compromise equitable care delivery. In this way, AI will not only drive technological innovation but will also build the trustworthy, patient-centred infrastructure needed to transform cancer care worldwide.

Conclusion

AI is poised to redefine oncology, offering transformative potential across the cancer care continuum from early detection and diagnosis to prognosis, treatment personalization, and drug discovery. By leveraging large-scale, heterogeneous biomedical data through advanced learning algorithms, substantial progress has already been achieved in medical imaging, liquid biopsies, genomic profiling, digital pathology, and clinical decision-making. Across these domains, AI systems have demonstrated improved accuracy, scalability, and workflow efficiency compared with traditional approaches, enabling more precise risk stratification and increasingly individualized patient management.

At the same time, the translation of AI tools into routine oncology practice remains complex. Data privacy and security concerns, algorithmic bias, limited interpretability of black-box models, regulatory uncertainty, and challenges in embedding AI within existing clinical workflows all pose significant barriers. Robust fairness evaluation, rigorous external validation, high-quality and representative datasets, and careful attention to clinical context are essential to ensure that AI applications are reliable, generalizable, and equitable across diverse patient populations.

Realizing AI’s full impact in oncology will depend on sustained interdisciplinary collaboration among clinicians, researchers, industry stakeholders, and regulators supported by clear governance and accountability frameworks. Human-AI collaborative models, continuous performance monitoring, and explainable, transparent systems will be crucial to building and maintaining trust among clinicians and patients. By prioritizing ethical implementation, transparency, clinician education, and inclusive design, AI can serve as a powerful catalyst for a new era of precision oncology one that is more accurate, efficient, and accessible to patients worldwide.
