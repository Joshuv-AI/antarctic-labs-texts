Universal thiourea coupling enhances electron and ion transport in quinone cathodes for aqueous zinc batteries
Authors: Hu Hong, Xinru Yang, Yiqiao Wang, Zhiquan Wei, Dedi Li, Xun Guo, Yanbo Wang, Qingshun Nian, Shaoce Zhang, Shixun Wang, Shengnan Wang, Shimei Li, Dechao Zhang, Qi Xiong, Hui Yang, Chunyi Zhi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Organic electrode materials, despite their elemental abundance, environmental friendliness, and design flexibility, often suffer from limited electronic and ionic conductivities, which restrict their practical applications. Here, we present a universal thiourea coupling strategy that improves both electron and ion transport in quinone-based organic electrodes. Taking phenanthrenequinone as a representative example, thiourea incorporation increases the electron density of the quinones and improves the overall electronic conductivity of the electrode material. Meanwhile, thiourea establishes continuous proton-transport pathways, enabling proton-dominated redox reactions via a Grotthuss-type hopping mechanism. As a result, zinc batteries employing the coupled electrode exhibit stable cycling behavior over 6000 cycles at a low conductive carbon content (10 wt%) and maintain reliable operation in pouch-cell configurations under high mass loading conditions of 20 mg cm–2. In addition, the applicability of this molecular coupling strategy is demonstrated across multiple quinone systems, paving that path towards practical organic electrode materials.

Introduction

Developing high-performance rechargeable batteries to store and regulate the intermittent output of solar and wind energy is of critical importance. Batteries employing organic electrodes present an attractive electrochemical energy storage solution, as organic compounds offer advantages such as lightweight, environmental friendliness, sustainability, and design flexibility1. The capacity contribution of organic electrode materials primarily stems from their redox-active functional groups, which can be categorized into various types, including quinones, organic sulfur compounds, organic radicals, imides, nitriles, and azo compounds2–11. Among them, quinone compounds are the most extensively studied class of organic electrode materials, as they often exhibit favorable reaction kinetics, enabling rapid proton insertion/extraction, making them particularly suitable for aqueous battery systems8,12. The combination of quinone-based organic electrodes with high-capacity and low-cost zinc metal negative electrodes has been considered one of the most promising aqueous organic energy storage systems, and has been widely reported9,12–19.

Except for a category of conductive polymer, which generally exhibit low capacity, most other organic molecules have conductivities below 10–9 S cm–1 and therefore act as insulators. The insulating feature of organic materials typically requires a large amount of conductive carbon additives (often 30 wt% to 70 wt%) for battery electrode fabrication. However, this high content of inactive carbon additives limits the achievable specific energy of the electrode, straying far from the commercial lithium-ion battery electrodes, in which conductive carbon additives are typically less than or equal to 10%17.

To tackle this issue, several strategies were adopted, including the polymerization and extension of the π-electronic conjugated structure of phenyl rings, involving both one and two-dimensional extensions11,14,20–22. These methods, based on intrinsic molecule design and modification, rely on specific synthetic steps and conditions, with individual molecules linked by covalent bonds (intrinsic molecule design, Fig. 1a). Despite the progress of these approaches, the harsh reaction conditions conflict with the environmental friendliness and sustainability of organic materials. Moreover, organic electrode materials often have a three-dimensional crystalline structure. Without careful design of efficient internal ion transport channels, they may impede full capacity utilization and limit the active material’s mass loading23. Inspired by charge-transfer complexes, which enhance the intrinsic conductivity of materials by generating carriers through electron transfer between two individual molecules (extrinsic intermolecular design, Fig. 1b), we envision: achieving a extrinsic noncovalent structure via co-crystallization of amino-rich small molecules with quinone molecules, where hydrogen bonds mediate electron transfer between them to enhance electrical conductivity (Elementary structure). Additionally, the abundant amino groups can serve as channels for proton transport, effectively accelerating ion transport in bulk crystal materials (Secondary structure). This extrinsic intermolecular design strategy relies solely on weak intermolecular interactions between two individual molecules, which enables its realization through simple mechanical methods—a process that is operationally straightforward, environmentally friendly, and highly consistent with the principles of sustainable development.

Herein, we propose a universal thiourea molecular coupling strategy via simple mechanical mixing of thiourea (TU) with various quinone molecules. Taking phenanthrenequinone (PQ) as an example, in the as-prepared PQ-TU co-crystal, electron-rich TU molecules enhance the electron density on the PQ moiety, driving it into a more electron-rich state and thereby improving its intrinsic electronic conductivity. Moreover, amino groups in TU molecules become protonated in aqueous electrolytes, forming continuous proton-transport channels within the electrode material—an effect that significantly accelerates ion diffusion kinetics. This synergistic design boosts the electrochemical performance of PQ-TU compared to pristine PQ and enables stable operation of high-mass-loading PQ-TU electrodes in Zn | |PQ-TU pouch cells. Importantly, this extrinsic intermolecular design strategy can be applied to various quinone molecules, demonstrating enhanced electrochemical performance at a low carbon additive ratio and increased specific energy of the cathodes.

Results

Similar to commercial inorganic electrode materials (e.g., LiFePO4 and LiCoO2), the design of practical organic electrode materials needs to improve the transport of ions within the bulk material to ensure the stable operation of thick electrodes. Meanwhile, their intrinsic electrical conductivity should also be enhanced to reduce the proportion of conductive carbon additives, thereby improving the specific energy of electrodes. We take the typical organic carbonyl compound PQ as a representative example for a detailed analysis.

Thiourea molecular coupling strategy

The PQ-TU co-crystal was produced through the simple mechanical ball milling of PQ and TU, supplemented with a small quantity of acetonitrile. This mechanochemical synthesis method ensures both a high yield (100%) and the capability for large-scale production (Fig. 2a, see Methods for more details). The crystal structure of the PQ-TU was analyzed using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD), revealing distinct diffraction patterns that were completely different from those of its precursor components, PQ and TU (Fig. 2b). This compelling evidence suggests that the as-synthesized PQ-TU can be classified as a well-defined crystalline phase rather than a mere physical mixture. Further investigations confirmed that the diffraction pattern precisely matched the simulated pattern obtained from the Cambridge crystallographic data center (CCDC 1409744), with no traces of impurity phases detected. The corresponding schematic diagram of the crystal structure is displayed in Fig. 2c. Thanks to the strong hydrogen‑bonding interaction between PQ and TU, an elementary structure is formed. In the resulting PQ-TU cocrystal, PQ molecules stack along the c axis via π–π interactions, connect to TU molecules through intermolecular N–H⋯O hydrogen bonds, and are further reinforced by intramolecular N–H⋯S hydrogen bonds, giving rise to an orthorhombic lattice (space group Pbcn, secondary structure). Adjacent 1:1 PQ-TU pairs further interlink into a 2D network via C–H⋯O/S interactions, visualized clearly in the Hirshfeld surface fingerprint plot analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Compared to the simple van der Waals interactions present in PQ crystals (Supplementary Fig. 2), the abundant interactions within PQ-TU co-crystals effectively enhance the stability of their structure. These weak interactions are directly evidenced in Fourier transform infrared spectra (FTIR, Fig. 2d). While pristine PQ shows a featureless baseline in the 3000–3500 cm−1 region, PQ-TU exhibits distinct splitting into four characteristic peaks at 3378, 3282, 3182, and 3115 cm−1. These are assignable to: asymmetric/symmetric stretching of intramolecular N–H⋯S hydrogen bonds (3378/3282 cm−1), asymmetric/symmetric stretching of intermolecular N−H⋯O hydrogen bonds (3182/3115 cm−1)6. As displayed in Supplementary Fig. 3, compared with pure TU, these bands show a pronounced blue shift, indicating significantly strengthened hydrogen bonding after coupling, which in turn enhances structural stability. Notably, this hydrogen-bonding interaction pattern is fundamentally different from charge-transfer complexes that rely on π–π stacking (see Supplementary Note 1 for more details). Additionally, the irregular particle morphology of prepared PQ-TU was revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Supplementary Fig. 4) with a larger surface area of 17.73 m3 g–1 compared to PQ (Supplementary Fig. 5). This may facilitate electrolyte penetration and shorter ion diffusion paths. Notably, after combining with TU, the color of PQ changed significantly from yellow to orange (Supplementary Fig. 7), which means that electron transfer occurred between PQ and TU, resulting in a transition of the original PQ electronic energy level, thus affecting the absorption of visible light. To probe the electronic state changes upon TU binding, solid-state ultraviolet visible diffuse reflectance (UV–vis DRS) spectroscopy was employed, revealing significantly enhanced electron delocalization in PQ upon TU coupling—evidenced by the pronounced red-shift and broadening of its characteristic absorption band (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectrum has been used to assess the free electron density on both PQ and PQ-TU. As disclosed in Fig. 2f, both PQ-TU and PQ display two distinct signals in their EPR spectra, with g values of 1.9696 and 2.0043, indicating the presence of spin-orbit coupling effects and the existence of multiple free radicals with unpaired electrons. The EPR intensity of PQ-TU is notably higher than that of PQ, indicating the transfer of electrons from TU to PQ and thus enhancing the local electron density around the carbonyl groups in PQ-TU. The specific charge transfer amount between TU and PQ can be identified as 0.16 e− by Mulliken charge analysis (the inset in Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 8). To further elucidate the impact of charge transfer interactions between PQ and TU, the band structures and density of states (DOS) of PQ-TU and PQ are presented in Fig. 2g, h. PQ and PQ-TU demonstrate typical p-type semiconductor characteristics with their valence band maximum near the Fermi level (Ef). After being combined with TU, the conduction band of PQ-TU is significantly reduced, and the bandgap decreases from 1.44 to 0.82 eV.

We found that the electronic conductivity of PQ-TU is 3.6 × 10–7 S cm–1, which is about 10 times higher than that of PQ (4.0 × 10–8 S cm–1), as displayed in Fig. 2i. This indicates that the charge transfer interaction between PQ and TU has effectively enhanced the delocalization of electrons within the material, thereby improving the intrinsic conductivity. Notably, the electronic conductivity of TU is only 2.1 × 10–12 S cm–1, making it an insulator; this indirectly demonstrates that the hydrogen-bond-mediated electron transfer in PQ-TU is highly robust. Significantly, PQ-TU exhibits relatively high electronic conductivity among reported organic electrode materials, comparable to that of charge-transfer complexes, organic radical polymers, organosulfur compounds, and non-conjugated polymers. Its conductivity is higher than that of LiFePO4 and is comparable to commercial LiMn2O424, as summarized in Supplementary Fig. 9.

Electrochemical performances

Next, the electrochemical performance of PQ and PQ-TU electrodes at different conductive additive ratios was investigated. For clarity, the electrode containing 10 wt% conductive carbon is, for example, abbreviated as PQ‑TU‑10%. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) and galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) were used to evaluate the redox kinetics of PQ-10% and PQ-TU-10% electrodes. As scan rates increased, the PQ-TU-10% electrode showed smaller peak separation and peak current shifts, indicating better electrochemical reversibility. Its oxidation/reduction peaks maintained a linear relationship with scan rates (b-values 0.79–0.95, Supplementary Fig. 10), reflecting surface-controlled charge storage kinetics. In contrast, the PQ-10% electrode displayed bulk diffusion-dominated behavior (Supplementary Fig. 11), likely due to insufficient internal ion transport channels. Further GITT test was used to understand the diffusion coefficient of ions within the PQ-TU-10% and PQ-10% electrodes, as displayed in Supplementary Fig. 12. The PQ-TU-10% electrode was discharged at a current density of 100 mA g−1 with a 1-min interval, and then a 60-s relaxation period was provided to allow the voltage to reach equilibrium (Fig. 3d). The long-term cycling performance of the PQ-TU-10% electrode was investigated at a current density of 0.5 A g–1, as displayed in Fig. 3g. After a slow capacity increase in the initial cycles, a specific capacity of 120.7 mA h g–1 can remain after 500 cycles. Furthermore, a high-capacity retention of 80.5% after 6000 cycles was achieved with steady Coulombic efficiency (CE) close to 99.8%, demonstrating enhanced long-term operation durability. Noteworthy, the sloping feature at the end of the initial charge–discharge curves may be attributed to the activation process of Zn2⁺ insertion/extraction (Supplementary Fig. 15). In comparison, the PQ-10% electrode exhibits significant capacity decay, retaining only 75.4 mA h g–1 after 2000 cycles (Supplementary Fig. 16). Moreover, at a high current density of 5.0 A g⁻1 the PQ-TU-10% electrode still delivered a specific capacity of 94.3 mA h g⁻1 and retained 60.1% of its capacity after more than 9000 cycles, substantially outperforming the PQ-10% electrode, as exhibited in Supplementary Fig. 17. Additionally, we also evaluated the electrochemical performance of the PQ-TU-10% electrode under high mass loadings. As shown in Fig. 3h and Supplementary Fig. 18, the Zn | |PQ-TU-10% pouch cell can deliver a capacity of 32.5 mA h (74.4% utilization) at a high mass loading of up to 20 mg cm–2 and retains 83.8% of its initial capacity even after 600 cycles. It is worth noting that although TU is soluble in water, the PQ-TU co-crystal is only sparingly soluble owing to the stronger hydrogen-bonding interactions between TU and PQ (Supplementary Fig. 19), which may improve the utilization of active materials at high mass loading26. Subsequently, in-situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to monitor real-time impedance changes during the charge-discharge process of the PQ-TU-10% electrode, as shown in Fig. 3i. The results demonstrate that as ion intercalation and deintercalation (at ~0.9 V) proceed, the resistance associated with mass transport, represented by the low-frequency region (10–1 to 102 Hz), decreases sharply. This can be ascribed to enhanced proton conduction within the material. Concurrently, the resistance linked to charge transfer, represented by the high-frequency region (102 to 104 Hz), remains at a stable and low level, which can be attributed to the improved electronic and ionic conductivity22 (Fig. 3j).

Proton-dominated redox chemistry

The evolution of PQ-TU-10% during charging and discharging was initially investigated through ex-situ FTIR spectroscopy (Fig. 4a). The FTIR spectra revealed two distinctive peaks at 1672 cm−1 and 1590 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of carbonyl groups (–C = O) in the PQ-TU-10% electrode27. After discharge, a significant reduction in the intensity of the –C = O peak was observed, indicating electron acceptance by the –C = O group and the subsequent formation of –C–O– anions6. Concurrently, cations were inserted to maintain charge balance. Recharging resulted in the FTIR spectrum of the electrode resembling the initial spectrum, providing evidence for the reversible oxidation/reduction of the carbonyl groups. This result can be further confirmed through ex-situ X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). In Fig. 4b, a noticeable shift in the carbonyl peak at 531.5 eV was observed after discharge. Upon recharging, the peak position reverted back to its original position, confirming the reversible conversion from –C = O to –C–O– anions7. Subsequently, the types of cations intercalated in the PQ-TU-10% electrode during the redox process were examined. In the high-resolution spectrum of Zn 2p, clear signals at 1021.5 eV and 1045.5 eV, corresponding to Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2, respectively, can be observed25,28 (Fig. 4c). An in-situ pH measurement was conducted using a pH meter to monitor the pH changes resulting from proton intercalation into the PQ-TU-10% electrode. The measurement was carried out in a custom-made two-electrode reaction setup, enabling real-time monitoring of pH variations throughout the electrochemical processes (Fig. 4d). During discharge, the pH value increased, while during charging, it decreased. This clear trend indicates the reversible nature of proton insertion in PQ-TU-10%. Subsequently, we examined and compared the performance of the PQ-TU-10% electrode in different electrolytes, including 0.5 M H2SO4 (pH = 1.0), 0.5 M H2SO4 + 2 M ZnSO4 (pH ≈ 1.0), 2 M ZnSO4 (pH = 4.2), and 0.5 × 10–5 M H2SO4 (pH = 5.0), as shown in Fig. 4e. It is noticeable that the PQ-TU-10% electrode displays a significant pH dependence. In both electrolytes 0.5 M H2SO4 and 0.5 M H2SO4 + 2 M ZnSO4, the PQ-TU-10% electrode shows nearly identical discharge plateaus and capacities, suggesting that the redox activity of PQ-TU-10% is primarily driven by protons rather than Zn2+ ions18,29,30. As the pH increases (2 M ZnSO4), although the discharge plateau decreases, PQ-TU-10% still maintains a similar capacity. This can be attributed to the continuous hydrolysis of Zn2+ ions in the ZnSO4 electrolyte, providing a constant and abundant supply of protons. At this point, with the localized increase in pH, the hydrolyzed Zn2+ ions react with hydroxide ions to produce zinc hydroxide sulfate, which can be removed during the charging process (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). When the pH continues to increase (0.5 × 10–5 M H2SO4), PQ-TU-10% exhibits a diminished discharge plateau and capacity, indicating the importance of an appropriate acidic aqueous electrolyte. To quantify the relative amounts of proton and zinc storage, inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) was employed. Based on the ICP-OES results (Supplementary Note 2), the molar number of inserted protons is calculated to be approximately 11 times that of Zn2⁺, confirming that proton storage predominates in TABQ during the redox processes. The proton-dominant insertion mechanism in PQ-TU-10% can be attributed to the presence of amino groups on TU, which can undergo protonation in weakly acidic electrolytes. This protonation allows for continuous proton exchange with the electrolyte, creating a proton-active environment that facilitates proton insertion. The protonation of PQ-TU-10% was confirmed through UV–vis spectroscopy analysis. In the deprotonated form of PQ-TU, the amino groups interact with the –C = O group on PQ and the –S = O group on adjacent TU through hydrogen bonding, resulting in a slight transfer of electron density from the lone pair of electrons on the amino groups. However, this interaction is disrupted upon protonation of the amino groups, leading to a noticeable shift in the protonated PQ-TU compared to the deprotonated PQ-TU (Fig. 4f).

The proton conduction mechanism in PQ-TU-10% electrodes is investigated using GITT at different temperatures, as shown in Fig. 4g. The ion diffusion activation energy, obtained by fitting with the Arrhenius equation, is determined to be 140 meV, falling within the range of the Grotthuss-type transport mechanism (<400 meV)8. This suggests that fast proton conduction can occur through a continuous hydrogen bond network specifically present in PQ-TU-10%, which is closely associated with the abundance of amino groups on TU. The continuous hydrogen bond network can be visualized from the crystal structure of PQ-TU, as depicted in Fig. 4h. Within the PQ-TU molecule, TU units form a continuous hydrogen bond network along the c-axis direction, facilitated by interactions between –NH2 and –S = O/–C = O groups. The proton transfer takes place through the formation and rupture of hydrogen bonds between neighboring –C = O and –NH2 groups, driving a characteristic Grotthuss mechanism6,27. This distinctive mechanism plays a key role in the PQ-TU-10% electrode, enabling improved rate performance and high-mass loading by facilitating efficient proton storage.

Thiourea molecule coupling on different quinone molecules

To demonstrate the universality of the thiourea coupling strategy for enhancing conductivity, we selected two representative quinones, 1,2-naphthoquinone (NQ) and pyrene-4,5,9,10-tetraone (PTO), because they also contain adjacent carbonyl groups similar to PQ. As shown in Fig. 5a, b, TU interacts with NQ and PTO via the same H-bond mode through simple ball-milling. This not only forms co-crystals (Supplementary Figs. 22 and 23) to enhance structural stability but also effectively increases the charge density of NQ and PTO through H-bond mediated charge transfer. The crystal structures of NQ-TU and PTO-TU were solved by the charge-flipping method based on Le Bail indexed data, and subsequently refined by Rietveld refinement (Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25), confirming the H-bond interaction of TU and quinone molecules. The detailed crystallographic parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. This extrinsic intermolecular design strategy—thiourea molecular coupling—simply and effectively improves the intrinsic conductivity of materials (3.2 × 10–11 S cm–1 increased to 1.2 × 10–10 S cm–1 for NQ-TU, 3.5 × 10–8 S cm–1 increased to 5.4 × 10–7 S cm–1 for PTO-TU, Supplementary Fig. 26). The FTIR spectra of NQ-TU and PTO-TU clearly exhibited four distinct peaks compared to pristine NQ and PTO, which can be assigned to intramolecular and intermolecular H-bond, respectively (Fig. 5c, e). Notably, slight shifts in their corresponding peak positions were observed due to differences in hydrogen bond strengths. The electron-rich states of NQ-TU and PTO-TU were verified by EPR spectroscopy. As displayed in Fig. 5d, f, TU-coupled NQ-TU and PTO-TU displayed enhanced EPR signal intensity compared to their pristine counterparts. Additionally, charge transfer amounts were calculated (Supplementary Figs. 27 and 28) as 0.13 e− and 0.39 e⁻ for the NQ-TU and PTO-TU, respectively. Comparison of total electrode specific energy

As a core component of the battery, the specific energy of the positive electrode directly determines that of the entire cell. A positive electrode typically comprises active material, conductive carbon, and binder, and the ratio of these three components plays a crucial yet often overlooked role in performance. For example, suppose the active material can fully utilize its capacity with minimal conductive carbon. In that case, a positive electrode prepared with an 8:1:1 mix of active material, conductive carbon, and binder will have about 33 % higher total specific energy than one made at a 6:3:1 ratio (Fig. 6a). This difference arises simply because the 8:1:1 formulation contains 80 wt% active material versus 60 wt% in the 6:3:1 case, so, for the same intrinsic specific capacity and operating voltage, the electrode-level gravimetric energy scales roughly as 0.8/0.6 ≈ 1.33. This indicates that, in addition to synthesizing new high-capacity organic materials to boost specific energy, improving the intrinsic conductivity of the material so that it can achieve full capacity with less conductive carbon is also an effective strategy for enhancing the specific energy of the entire electrode. This universally applicable TU coupling strategy, based on a extrinsic intermolecular design, can effectively enhance the intrinsic electronic conductivity of quinone molecules. As a result, it enables full capacity utilization with only minimal conductive‑carbon addition and thereby significantly increases the total electrode specific energy. As shown in Fig. 6b, relative to some previously reported organic electrodes containing high levels of conductive carbon6,31,32, the prepared thiourea-coupled materials (NQ-TU, PQ-TU, and PTO-TU) exhibit improved intrinsic electronic conductivity. This improvement permits lower conductive‑carbon content while achieving full capacity utilization and leads to substantial increases in total electrode specific energy: 59.5% for NQ-TU, 46.4% for PQ-TU and 36.7% for PTO-TU. The TU-coupling strategy was applied to three structurally related adjacent-dicarbonyl quinones (PQ, NQ, and PTO), and can be readily extended to other quinone derivatives. For example, as displayed in Supplementary Fig. 32, the high-capacity benzoquinone (BQ) can also couple with TU to form BQ-TU, which enhances electronic conductivity and enables high-capacity delivery even with minimal conductive-carbon additive. These results demonstrate the broad generality of the carbonyl–amine hydrogen-bonding motif between quinones and TU, and indicate that TU coupling offers a promising route to improve the performance of organic electrode materials.

Disscusion

In summary, we have demonstrated a universal thiourea molecular coupling strategy for constructing practical organic quinones electrode materials, achieving much high electron and ion transportation as an electrode. Using PQ as an example, we synthesized the PQ-TU co-crystal through a simple mechanochemical ball milling process, combining PQ with the amine-rich small molecule TU. The incorporation of TU increases the electron density on PQ, leading to improved electronic conductivity (3.6 × 10–7 S cm–1), which is higher than that of pristine PQ and is within the range reported for representative inorganic cathode materials, such as LiMn2O4. It can also create continuous proton channels within the PQ-TU structure. This allows the redox processes of the PQ-TU co-crystal to be predominantly driven by proton intercalation/deintercalation, with the charge transport mechanism following a Grotthuss-type mechanism, which greatly enhances the reaction kinetics of the electrode. Benefiting from these features, the Zn | |PQ-TU battery delivers a specific capacity of 187 mA h g⁻1 at 0.1 A g⁻1 with a conductive carbon content of 10 wt%. The cell maintains 91.9 mA h g⁻1 at 5.0 A g⁻1 and shows stable long-term cycling behavior, retaining 80.5% of its capacity after 6000 cycles at 0.5 A g⁻1. Furthermore, even under a high mass loading of up to 20 mg cm–2, the Zn | |PQ-TU pouch cell can still maintain stable cycling for 600 cycles with 83.8% capacity retention. Additionally, the universality of this strategy has also been validated by using NQ and PTO quinone molecules. This universal thiourea molecular coupling strategy presented in this work proposes a promising direction for the design of practical organic electrode materials for aqueous batteries.

Methods

Synthesis of PQ-TU

Specifically, 9, 10-phenanthrenequinone (2.08 g, Aladdin, 99%), and thiourea (0.76 g, Aladdin, 99%) were placed in 50 mL ZrO2 jar with 100 μL acetonitrile (Aladdin, 99.9%) and 20 ZrO2 balls 3 mm in diameter. The mixture was then milled for 2 h in a MITR YXQM-0.4 L Mill operating at 900 rpm. Finally, the obtained orange powder was heated at 60 °C for 12 h to remove the residual solvent. Similarly, NQ-TU (NQ was purchased from Aladdin, >95%), PTO-TU (PTO was purchased from Energy chemical, >97%), and BQ-TU (BQ was purchased from Aladdin, >97%) are both synthesized using the same steps described above.

Material characterizations

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired by a Rigaku D/max 2500/PC with Cu K radiation (40 kV, 15 mA) by scanning in the 2θ range of 5–40°. The possible space groups and unit-cell parameters of NQ-TU and PTO-TU were initially analyzed using the Chekcell program. The diffraction patterns of the two co-crystals were then subjected to Le Bail fitting with TOPAS v5.0 to achieve indexing and profile matching. Based on the indexed reflection information, the Superflip program was employed to perform 50 charge-flipping iterations in order to reduce the R value. For the electron-density cell with the lowest R value, atomic positions were introduced using the EDMA program. Subsequently, the structural model was refined with the TOPAS v5.0 package. A 24th-order Chebyshev polynomial was applied to describe the background, while a pseudo-Voigt function was adopted to model the peak profiles. Preferred orientation was corrected using fourth-order spherical harmonics. The refinement was performed sequentially for the scale factor, lattice parameters, zero-shift, and isotropic displacement parameters, with all variables gradually released until full convergence was achieved. The obtained crystallographic information files were provided in Supplementary Data 1 and 2. Field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) was measured on Gemini 300 with an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and the selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern were carried out on a FEI Tecnai F20. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms were collected on a Micromeritics ASAP 2460 surface area analyzer operating at −196 °C to obtain the pore size of PQ-TU. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectra were recorded by a Bruker EMXplus-6/1. UV‒vis spectrum characterization was performed a Hitachi UH4150 spectrophotometer in a range of 100–800 nm to investigate the protonation changes. Electrical conductivities of all samples were determined by a DC two-probe steady-state current method. Samples (~200 mg) were compressed into pellets (10 mm in diameter) using a PEEK die under 300 MPa with titanium electrodes, and the steady-state currents under applied voltages of 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 V were recorded on a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation. All measurements were performed at 25 °C. The UV–vis test samples were obtained by dispersing an excess amount of PQ-TU in a ZnSO4 and pure H2O solutions to obtain their saturated solutions. For the characterization of PQ-TU at different charge and discharge states, the cells were opened and rinsed with deionized H2O. Ex-situ Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectra were recorded at room temperature using a PerkinElmer spectrometer in the range of 100–4000 cm−1. For ex-situ characterization, the aqueous cells were disassembled in air after being stopped at the designated voltages, and the harvested electrode samples were collected directly without the use of an inert atmosphere. ICP-OES analysis was carried out on ICP-OES 730 (Agilent, America). The oxidation states of the electrodes were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi), with all measurements conducted at room temperature under ambient conditions.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical performances of the Zn | |PQ-TU cells were investigated by assembling a coin-type cell (CR 2032), unless otherwise specified. The PQ-TU electrodes were fabricated by mixing PQ-TU, Ketjen black (KB, CANRD EC600JD), and polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, 60%, CANRD) at mass ratio of 8:1:1, using isopropanol (Aladdin, 99%) as the dispersing solvent, followed by a manual grinding in an agate mortar and pestle at room temperature (25 °C) under ambient conditions. The resulting slurry was uniformly coated onto Ti foil ( > 99.99%, Φ16, 50 μm, kangdeli metal) serving as the current collector. Unless otherwise noted, this compositional ratio and fabrication protocol were consistently applied throughout this work. After coating, the electrodes were dried at 80 °C overnight to remove residual solvent. Similarly, the PQ, NQ, NQ-TU, PTO, PTO-TU electrodes wrer fabricated following the same mass ratio and identical fabrication procedures. The areal mass loading of all positive electrodes was maintained at 2 to 3 mg cm–2.

Electrochemical tests were carried out in 2.0 M aqueous ZnSO4 electrolyte (ZnSO4·7H2O, Aladdin, 99%), which was prepared and stored in a 100 mL glass vial and transferred using a rubber-bulb dropper, with an electrolyte volume of 100 μL added to each coin cell. Zinc foil (>99.99%, Φ16, 50 μm, kangdeli metal) was used as both the negative electrode and the counter electrode. A glass fiber (GF/F, Φ16 mm, Whatman, the thickness is 200 µm, the average pore diameter is 1.6 µm) was used as sepertor to physically isolate the electrodes. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted using a CHI 760E electrochemical workstation within a voltage window of 0.1–1.5 V vs. Zn|Zn2+, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed on the same system. Galvanostatic charge–discharge (GCD) tests were carried out on a LAND CT3002A battery testing system over the same voltage range of 0.1–1.5 V vs. Zn|Zn2+. The Coulombic efficiency (CE) was calculated as the ratio of the discharge capacity to the charge capacity of the preceding charging cycle. The specific energy based on the positive electrode was determined by integrating the discharge curve and subsequently normalized by the mass fraction of the active material (e.g., 0.6 for 60 wt% and 0.8 for 80 wt%).

Pouch cells were assembled in a single-layer configuration by stacking PQ-TU cathodes, separators (GF/F, Φ16 mm, Whatman, the thickness is 200 µm, the average pore diameter is 1.6 µm), and zinc foil (>99.99%, Φ16, 50 μm, kangdeli metal) anodes of identical dimensions (3 × 4 cm2), with Ti mesh (80 mesh, >99.99%, 100 μm, kangdeli metal) serving as the current collector for the cathode, corresponding to a negative-to-positive capacity ratio (N/P) of 7.8. The stacked components were sealed in aluminum-laminated pouches, followed by the injection of 2.0 mL of 2.0 M aqueous ZnSO4 electrolyte. All cell assembly procedures were carried out at room temperature (25 °C), during which pressure was applied using a simple spring-loaded pressing mold, providing a moderate pressure of ~10 MPa. Prior to cycling tests, the assembled pouch cells were rested for 12 h to ensure sufficient electrolyte wetting and interfacial stabilization.

Density functional theory calculations

The theoretical calculations were conducted using the CASTEP module with GGA-PBE function. The TS method for DFT-D correction was used to modify and correct the accuracy of the standard GGA-PBE function for the system. Crystallographic information files (CIF) of PQ and PQ-TU were acquired by Cambridge Structural Database, and their CCDC numbers are 186904 and 1409744, respectively. The Hirshfeld surfaces and crystal voids were obtained by using Crystal Explorer.
