Effects of coenzyme Q10 on growth performance, rumen fermentation, blood metabolites, antioxidant status, and immune responses of pre-weaned Holstein dairy calves
Authors: Mahmoud M. Abdelsattar, Dingkun Fan, Wenzhang Zhou, Naifeng Zhang, Yan Tu, Qiyu Diao, Yanliang Bi, Yu Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study was conducted to investigate the effects of coenzyme Q10 (CoQ) dietary supplementation on growth performance, rumen fermentation, diarrhea frequency, and serum biochemical metabolites of pre-weaned Holstein dairy calves. Forty-five Holstein female calves (37.86 ± 2.38 kg of body weight [BW]; 1 d of age) were randomly assigned into 1 of the 3 treatments: a control group (Q0; without CoQ supplementation) and CoQ supplementation at low dose (Q50; 50 mg/kg dry matter [DM] of CoQ) or high dose (Q100; 100 mg/kg of CoQ) (n = 15). All experimental calves were fed pasteurized whole milk twice daily (06:30 and 17:00) starting from d 2 of age. From d 61 to 70, the calves were gradually weaned following a step-down approach. Calves had free access to clean water and the starter diet, which was introduced from d 7 onward. The study terminated on d 70. The results showed that calves supplemented with both low and high doses of CoQ had significantly higher average daily gain (linear, P = 0.007) and dry matter intake (DMI, linear, P < 0.001) compared with the Q0 group. There was a treatment by age interaction for BW (P < 0.001), where BW at 10 weeks of age was greater at Q50 and Q100 groups than at the Q0 group. Diarrhea frequency from 1 to 5 weeks of age was significantly affected by treatment (P = 0.026), with Q100 calves showing the lowest values. At 10 weeks of age, the Q100 calves showed higher serum total antioxidant capacity (linear, P = 0.012), superoxide dismutase (linear, P = 0.018), glutathione peroxidase (linear, P = 0.012), and β-hydroxybutyrate (linear, P = 0.006) compared with the Q0 group. The serum malondialdehyde was lower in Q100 calves than Q0 calves (linear, P = 0.015). Compared with the Q50 and Q0 groups, serum contents of insulin, growth hormone, and non-esterified fatty acids increased in calves receiving high CoQ dose (linear, P = 0.006, 0.012, 0.006, respectively). Conversely, the Q50 calves showed higher serum glucose compared with the other treatments (quadratic, P = 0.003). The results of the present study suggest that the supplementation of CoQ to milk may promote growth performance, health status, blood metabolites, and hormones, and improve the antioxidant capacity of the Holstein dairy calves. The use of CoQ may be an alternative to feeding antibiotics at 100 mg/kg DM to improve calf health and decrease diarrhea frequency.

Introduction

The transition from the in utero to the extra-uterine environment marks a critical period in the life of newborn calves, characterized by rapid growth and high nutritional demands, accompanied by underdeveloped immune systems (Khan et al., 2016). This vulnerability is further exacerbated by the physiological surge in reactive oxygen species (ROS) following exposure to an oxygen-rich environment at birth (Abuelo et al., 2019). The antioxidant defenses in calves are often underdeveloped to counterbalance the heightened ROS production, leading to oxidative stress—a key contributor to many pathological processes, including diarrhea (Albera and Kankofer, 2011; Carlson and Angel, 2024; Fu et al., 2023; Muller et al., 2007). Diarrhea is a prevalent disease among preweaning calves that leads to high morbidity and mortality rates, and is often indicative of an impaired intestinal mucosal barrier and disturbances of the gut flora, which can significantly impact future growth and production performance (Cho and Yoon, 2014; Heinrichs and Heinrichs, 2011; Liu et al., 2022; Medrano-Galarza et al., 2018).

Many nutritional strategies, including different milk-feeding regimens and feed additive supplementations, have been used to mitigate the effects of diarrhea and improve the health status of preweaning calves (Zhuang et al., 2024). At present, coenzyme Q10 (CoQ) has been widely used in the fields of medicine and human food additives, and it has been recognized in poultry for its role in mitigating oxidative stress and stimulating β-cell proliferation, thereby enhancing immunological productions and antibody responses (Geng et al., 2007; Nemati et al., 2017; Niu et al., 2009). However, there are only very few CoQ studies in ruminants.

Coenzyme Q10, also known as ubiquinone, is a lipid-soluble compound essential for high-energy-demanding organs such as the brain, heart, liver, and kidneys (Crane et al., 1957; Kadian et al., 2022). It plays a vital role in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, facilitating proton translocation and electron transfer, enabling the production of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) via oxidative phosphorylation (Hargreaves, 2003; Littarru and Tiano, 2010). At the plasma membrane, CoQ functions as an intermediate electron carrier, although the entire physiological mechanism of this process remains unclear (Wang and Hekimi, 2016). It is known to inhibit mitochondrial lipid peroxidation, protect the structural integrity of biological membranes, regenerate the antioxidant activity of vitamins and enzymes, and protect cells against oxidative stress (Khan et al., 2022; Song et al., 2017). Additionally, CoQ plays a role in anti-inflammatory and immune system enhancement, affecting energy metabolism, cell signaling, and gene expression in immune cells (Mantle et al., 2021; Tian et al., 2014; Yang et al., 2016).

Although CoQ can be found in organ meats, dairy products, vegetables, fruits, and whole grains (Pravst et al., 2010; Weber et al., 1997), only lower levels can be found, thus it should be supplied by external sources (Goudarzi et al., 2014). This study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary CoQ supplementation on growth performance, diarrhea frequency, rumen fermentation, and serum metabolites, as well as antioxidant, inflammatory, and immune indices in Holstein newborn calves. We hypothesized that CoQ supplementation, due to its antioxidant properties, can improve the health status of suckling calves by enhancing growth performance and blood metabolic antioxidant profiles. This study aimed to provide a theoretical basis and reference for the healthy growth of calves and the role of CoQ in mitigating oxidative stress and enhancing the health and performance of neonatal calves.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The animals were cared for according to the guidelines of the Animal Ethics Committee of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (IFR-CAAS-20220518).

Animals, diets, and housing

The experiment was carried out from June 2022 to September 2022 on a commercial dairy farm located in Cao County, Heze City, Shandong Province, China. In this farm, forty-five healthy newborn female Holstein calves with an average body weight (BW) of 37.86 ± 2.38 kg were separated from their dams immediately after birth and used for this experiment. All experimental calves were placed in a naturally ventilated hutch and kept in individual pens (4.5 m × 1.5 m). The pens were cleaned every morning to ensure the health and hygiene of the calves. Subsequently, the calves were administered 6 L of high-quality maternal colostrum, in two equal portions using bottle teats within the first 6 h postpartum. The calves were randomly assigned to one of three treatment groups and offered different inclusion levels of CoQ (≥99.0% purity; Heilongjiang NHU Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Suihua, Heilongjiang, China). Based on the data from Mazon et al. (2025), where the reported mean average daily gain (ADG) was 0.8 kg/d with a standard deviation of 0.15 kg, the required sample size was calculated to detect a 20% difference in ADG. Using G∗Power 3.1 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany), the sample size was determined using the formula for comparing mean differences between two independent means. With an effect size of 1.07, a minimum of 15 calves would be required in each group to achieve a statistical power (1 − β) of 0.8 and a Type I error (α) of 0.05. Treatments included the control group without CoQ (Q0), a low dose of CoQ (50 mg/kg dry matter [DM], Q50), and a high dose of CoQ (100 mg/kg DM, Q100). Although the optimal CoQ dosage for calves has not been established, maximal responses were observed with a daily supplementation of 100 mg of CoQ/kg diet in Japanese quail (Taha and Al-Tikriti, 2021). Further animal studies support a dose range of 30 to 100 mg/kg for anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular benefits in rats and mice (Choi et al., 2009; Kunitomo et al., 2008; Sohet et al., 2009).

After colostrum feeding, fresh whole milk was collected from the tank (4 °C) before morning feeding and transported to the calf research facility in a milk shuttle at 4 °C to inhibit bacterial growth until pasteurization. The milk was pasteurized at 63 °C for 35 min using a pasteurizer, then cooled to 39 °C in a water bath. The remaining milk was refrigerated at 4 °C until the second feeding, when the required quantity was reheated for feeding. The pasteurized whole normal milk was fed in steel buckets at 39 ± 1.0 °C in 2 meals of equal volumes at 06:30 and 17:00 from d 2 to 7 at 2 L/meal, from d 8 to 14 at 3 L/meal, and from d 15 to 60 at 4 L/meal. From 61 d of age, the pasteurized milk allowance was gradually reduced by 0.5 L per day until complete weaning at 70 d of age. The dietary CoQ was mixed into pasteurized milk and administered only during the morning meal. From d 7 onward, all calves were fed starter feed which was formulated to meet the requirement for calves (NRC, 2001). The starter feed was processed into pellet feed with a diameter of 4 mm and did not contain other additives or antibiotics. During the experiment, the starter feed and clean drinking water were provided ad libitum to calves. The experiment lasted a total of 70 d.

Data collection, sampling, and analysis

The remaining and added amounts of milk and starters for each calf were recorded daily before morning feeding. The dry matter intake (DMI) of the pasteurized whole milk and starter diet was calculated by subtracting the refusals from the feed offered on a DM basis. Representative samples of starter and milk were collected weekly and immediately frozen at −20 °C for further analysis. Milk was analyzed for milk fat, true protein, and other solids using an automated near-infrared milk analyzer (Foss MilkoScan FT3, Foss Electric A/S, Hillerød, Denmark) at the Shanghai DHI testing center, Shanghai, China. The somatic cell count (SCC) was measured using flow cytometry with Fossomatic 7DC (Foss Electric A/S, Hillerød, Denmark). Rumen fermentation

At 60 d of age, eight healthy calves with similar BW were selected from each group. The samples of the rumen contents were collected 3 h after morning feeding using an oro-ruminal probe connected to an adjustable vacuum pump, as described by Paz et al. (2016). The pH of the rumen contents was measured immediately after collection using a Testo 206 pH meter (Testo AG., Titisee-Neustadt, Baden-Württemberg, Germany). A rumen liquid fraction was obtained by filtering the rumen digesta through 4 layers of cheesecloth, and a 10 mL subsample of each strained fluid was collected and stored at −20 °C refrigerator for determination of the volatile fatty acids (VFAs, acetate, propionate, and butyrate), ammonia nitrogen (NH3–N), and microbial protein content. After thawing at 4 °C, the samples were centrifuged at 2500 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of the supernatant from each sample was transferred into a 1.5-mL centrifuge tube preloaded with 0.2 mL of 25% metaphosphoric acid solution containing 2 g/L of 2-ethylbutyric acid as an internal standard. Subsequently, the solution was blended with a vortex mixer, cooled in an ice water bath at 4 °C for 30 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g at 4 °C. Then, 1 mL of supernatant was diverted into a 1.5-mL autosampler vial, and a gas chromatograph (GC-6800; Beijing Beifen Tianpu Instrument Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), as described by Zhuang et al. (2024). The chromatographic column was a quartz glass packed column (6 mm × 2 m). The sample injection volume was set to 1.0 μL. The injection port temperature was maintained at 220 °C, the column temperature was 150 °C, and the flame ionization detector temperature was 280 °C. The carrier gas was high-purity N2 with a flow rate of 30 mL/min. The concentration of NH3–N was analyzed using the colorimetric method (Chaney and Marbach, 1962). Briefly, thawed rumen fluid was centrifuged at 10,000× g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant in sulfuric acid was analyzed using the phenol procedure. Samples were incubated for approximately 20 min at 39 °C, and the absorbance was quantified at 625 nm on a spectrophotometer (UV-120-01, Shimadzu Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The microbial protein component of the rumen fluid was analyzed according to the Coomassie Brilliant Blue method (Makkar et al., 1982). Briefly, thawed rumen fluid was homogenized with a vortex mixer, centrifuged at 408 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, 1.5 mL of supernatant was pipetted into a 2-mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The sediment was alkaline hydrolyzed with 0.5 mL of 0.25N NaOH with vigorous mixing, incubated in 100 °C water bath for 20 min, centrifuged at 25,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. Then, 25 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 75 μL of deionized water and 5 mL of Coomassie brilliant blue G-250 coloration solution at room temperature for 2 min and then quantified spectrophotometrically at 595 nm.

Blood sample collection and analysis

Eight healthy calves with similar BW were selected from each group to collect blood samples from the jugular vein before the morning feeding at 60 d of age. Blood was drawn into 10 mL non-anticoagulant tubes. Then, the serum was separated after centrifuging at 1500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, pipetted into 2 mL cryotubes, and stored at −20 °C until subsequent analysis. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), glucose (GLU), immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM) were analyzed using a KHB-1280 automatic biochemical analyzer (Kehua Biological Engineering Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), as described by Calamari et al. (2016). Contents of serum globulin (GLB) were calculated by subtracting the ALB contents from the TP contents. The following antioxidant indicators were analyzed: superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001-1-2) activity, glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, A005-1-2) activity, catalase (CAT, A007–1-1) activity, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, A015–1-2), and malondialdehyde (MDA, A003–1-2) concentration using relevant reagent kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Research Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China), as described by Zou et al. (2012). Moreover, the contents of β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), growth hormone (GH), and insulin (INS), as well as the serum pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-17A, IL-22, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and the anti-inflammatory cytokines contents such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), IL-4, and IL-10 were analyzed by an automatic microplate reader (STATFAX2100, USA) with corresponding commercial bovine ELISA kits (Jinhaikeyu Biotechnology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). All experimental procedures were performed following the manufacturers’ instructions. The assay sensitivity was 1.0 μmol/L for BHB, 10 μmol/L for NEFA, 0.1 ng/mL for GH, 0.1 mIU/L for INS, 1.0 pg/mL for TNF-α, 0.1 pg/mL for IL-4, 1.0 pg/mL for IL-6, 1.0 pg/mL for IL-8, 0.1 pg/mL for IL-10, 1.0 pg/mL for IL-17A, 1.0 pg/mL for IL-22, 10 pg/mL for IFN-γ, and 1.0 pg/mL for TGF-β.

Statistical analysis

All data were screened for normality and outliers using the UNIVARIATE procedure of SAS (version 9.4, SAS Inst. Inc., Cary, NC, USA). The feed intake and growth performance were analyzed using the MIXED procedure of SAS (version 9.4, SAS Inst. Inc.) with age as repeated measures. The repeated measures model contained fixed effects of treatment, age (days or weeks), and the interaction between treatment and age, and calf as the random effect. The mathematical model employed in this study is articulated as follows:Yijk=μ+Ti+Aj+(TA)ij+Ck+ϵijk,where Yijk is the observation; μ is the overall mean; Ti is the effect of ith level of treatment; Aj is the effect of age; (TA)ij is the interaction between treatment i and age j; Ck is the effect of calf; ϵijk is the random error related to individual observation. Multiple comparisons between means were conducted via Tukey’s test only when the treatment group effect was significant. A polynomial orthogonal contrast was conducted to test the linear and quadratic contrasts of CoQ inclusion levels.

The data of rumen fermentation and serum biochemical composition were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA utilizing the Tukey procedure as the post hoc test using SAS. The statistical analysis model for one-way ANOVA is expressed as:Yij=μ+Ti+ϵij,where Yij is the observation; μ is the overall mean; Ti is the effect of the ith level of treatment; ϵijk is the random error associated with the individual observation. Orthogonal polynomial comparisons were also performed to analyze the linear and quadratic effects of CoQ inclusion levels. Treatment difference with P > 0.05 was considered non-significant, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 0.10 was considered a tendency. The diarrhea frequency was statistically analyzed using the chi-square test, and the results were expressed as percentages. A P-value of less than 0.05 at a 95% confidence level was considered significant. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine the association between the growth performance parameters, rumen fermentation characteristics, and serum parameters using the correlation procedures of IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Pearson correlations were declared significant at P ≤ 0.05, where r = 0 indicates no correlation, r = −1 indicates a perfect negative correlation, and r = +1 indicates a perfect positive correlation. An R-squared (R2) value of 1 signifies that the data points lie exactly on the regression line, while an R2 of 0 indicates that the regression line is horizontal, offering no predictive value.

Growth performance

Growth performance results are presented in Table 4. Overall BW did not differ among Q0, Q50, and Q100 calves (P > 0.05), with mean values of 54.29, 56.25, and 56.53 kg, respectively. The interaction between treatment and age (P < 0.001) showed that the calves during 10 weeks of age had higher BW with the supplementation of CoQ at low or high inclusion levels in comparison with the Q0 group. In addition, the ADG for the Q50 and Q100 groups increased significantly (linear, P = 0.007) compared with the Q0 group, with increases of 16.95% and 22.03%, respectively. Furthermore, the treatment and age interaction was significant for ADG (P = 0.020), where a significantly lower ADG was observed at 8 and 10 weeks of age in the Q0 group compared with the Q100 group. Throughout the experimental period, the DMI of Q50 and Q100 groups increased significantly (linear, P < 0.001) compared with the Q0 group, with respective increases of 19.24% and 25.68%. However, the interaction between the treatment and age for DMI was not significant (P = 0.146). There was a significant increase in BW, ADG, and DMI over age for the calves (P < 0.001). Discussion

Diarrhea in calves, a prevalent health challenge after birth, is linked to oxidative stress, which can trigger immunological and inflammatory responses (Fu et al., 2023). Therefore, CoQ supplementation was utilized for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and properties (Yang et al., 2016), as well as its role in enhancing cellular bioenergetics as a cofactor in respiratory complexes (Kucharská et al., 2021). This study explored the impact of CoQ supplementation on growth performance, rumen fermentation, diarrhea frequency, and blood parameters related to immune response, inflammation, and antioxidant status in dairy calves during the preweaning period. The study aimed to provide insights into the role of CoQ in calves. Moreover, enhancing the antioxidant capacity and reducing diarrhea frequency in neonatal calves were the main purposes for supplementing CoQ into their diets.

In the current study, overall BW did not differ between the treatment groups. In contrast, the interaction between treatment and age showed a significant increase in the final BW of both CoQ treatments. In addition, dietary CoQ supplementation linearly increased the ADG of calves. Similar findings were reported by Wafa and El-Nagar. (2021), who observed improved daily milk yield and BW in dams and their calves at calving following oral administration of CoQ at 1.5 mg/kg BW. Additionally, El-Ela et al. (2017) reported increased BW in Damascene goats supplemented with CoQ at 40 mg/kg BW. Several studies have also indicated a significant improvement in BW with CoQ supplementation in broiler chickens under recommended managerial conditions (Kalantar et al., 2019) and in those subjected to cold-induced ascites (Geng et al., 2007; Nemati et al., 2017) or heat stress with high-energy diets (Gopi et al., 2015). Growth performance responses to CoQ supplementation may be attributable to improved cellular bioenergetics processes due to its pivotal role as an electron carrier in the mitochondrial oxidative respiratory chain (Hargreaves, 2014), thus favoring energy production and supporting the growth and productivity of the organisms (Delkhosh et al., 2021). Studies have shown that in CoQ deficiency, growth retardation is associated with the downregulation of growth-associated genes (e.g., RNA polymerase II, eukaryotic initiation factors) and the upregulation of oxidation reactions (i.e., cytochrome P450, SOD), as quantified by microarray analysis (Fischer et al., 2014). However, the exogenous CoQ supplementation can rescue the expression of these genes, suggesting that CoQ plays a role in promoting the growth process (Fischer et al., 2014). Furthermore, it has been reported that CoQ helps to alter metabolic rate due to its indirect effects on INS, glucagon, and cortisol, as well as the regeneration of vitamin E (Bentinger et al., 2007; Martin et al., 2007). Vitamin E can increase the activity of macrophages and muscle precursor cells, myotubes, and muscle formation in pigs during skeletal muscle regeneration after skeletal muscle injury (Otrocka-Domagała et al., 2015). In addition, Wu et al. (2020) demonstrated that CoQ can prevent orchiectomy-induced bone loss in mice by inhibiting oxidative stress and cellular senescence, thereby promoting osteoblastic bone formation and inhibiting osteoclastic bone resorption.

Our results showed that the DMI was enhanced in both CoQ treatments. Studies have reported that CoQ could increase starter feed intake with or without improving feed efficiency in broiler chicks (Geng et al., 2007; Gopi et al., 2015). As an essential coenzyme in cellular respiration, CoQ promotes the conversion of energy from carbohydrates and fatty acids into ATP (Hargreaves, 2003), thereby providing more energy to calves, increasing their activities, and enhancing digestion and metabolism in the gastrointestinal tract, which ultimately leads to increased feed intake. CoQ has also been reported to improve the activity of digestive enzymes such as protease, amylase, and lipase (El Basuini et al., 2020), likely due to its essential role in the metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins, and lipids through its involvement in the Krebs cycle (Mantle et al., 2023; Shukla and Dubey, 2018). Furthermore, CoQ could improve intestinal absorption by modulating intestinal microflora, enhancing nutritional efficiency (Schmelzer et al., 2008).

Blood indicators are reliable tools for assessing a calf’s health and responses to stressors and external stimuli. According to Carlson and Angel (2024), oxidative stress occurs due to an imbalance between the production and disposal of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Antioxidants play a crucial role in removing ROS and preserving cellular homeostasis. In this study, CoQ supplementation increased serum levels of T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px, while decreasing serum MDA content in calves. These results are consistent with those of Wafa and El-Nagar (2021), who reported a significant increase in SOD and GSH levels in serum of lactating cows treated with CoQ. Similarly, Gabr (2020) reported that CoQ increase serum SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px enzyme activities while reducing serum MDA content in buffalo calves. In addition, El-Ela et al. (2017) reported that CoQ tended to increase serum T-AOC in Damascene goats during late pregnancy and lactation periods. Additionally, supplementation with exogenous antioxidants such as CoQ and vitamin E prevented lipid peroxidation in horses by decreasing blood MDA content (Nemec et al., 2021). Similar antioxidant properties of CoQ have been reported in humans (Lee et al., 2012) and animal models such as mice (Lone et al., 2017). Within cells, CoQ, particularly in its reduced form (ubiquinol), is an effective antioxidant and free radical scavenger that inhibits lipid peroxidation of mitochondrial membranes, protecting phospholipids, LDL, and the mitochondrial protein layer from oxidative damage caused by free radicals (Taha and Al-Tikriti, 2021). Additionally, CoQ plays a role in regenerating vitamin E (Bentinger et al., 2007) and other powerful antioxidants such as α-tocopherol and ascorbate via electron donation (Bentinger et al., 2007). Reduced CoQ also recycles these antioxidants back to their active reduced forms, thereby enhancing the activity of other antioxidant defenses (Bentinger et al., 2007). In addition, CoQ has been shown to improve antioxidant capacity and immunity by upregulating the gene expression of SOD, CAT and GSH-Px in the liver or intestine (El Basuini et al., 2020). Additionally, CoQ has a significant role in the downregulation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase and upregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase in humans with hypercholesterolemia (Bentinger et al., 2010; Tsai et al., 2012).

Several studies have demonstrated that the exogenous administration of CoQ inhibits inflammatory responses by down-regulating oxidative and nitrative stress markers and inflammatory factors, which in turn supports immune function (Schmelzer et al., 2008; Sohet et al., 2009). An increased weight of immune organs was observed in CoQ-treated groups (Kalantar et al., 2019; Nemati et al., 2015). In this study, CoQ did not show significant effects on the serum immunoglobulins and inflammatory cytokines. Similarly, no significant changes in IgG contents were observed in ascites-susceptible broilers fed CoQ (Geng et al., 2007). However, CoQ has been reported to increase the blood IgG content in hepatitis B-vaccinated humans (Barbieri et al., 1999).

An increased fecal score (≥3 points in this study, classified as diarrhea) has a relationship to the oxidative stress, where a reduction in T-AOC, SOD, and CAT levels with an increased lipid peroxidation, MDA, and nitric oxide were observed in diarrheic calves (Fu et al., 2023; Ranjan et al., 2006). The results of this experiment showed that the frequency of diarrhea during the first 5 weeks of life in dairy calves supplemented with CoQ was significantly lower than that of the Q0 group. It has been reported that the CoQ supplementation can protect the intestinal mucosa in Escherichia coli-associated calf diarrhea (Garkhal et al., 2017). This protective effect of CoQ may be attributed to its ability to ameliorate oxidative stress in diarrheic calves by reducing increased levels of MDA, nitric oxide, and glutathione S-transferase (GST), as well as by preventing inflammation (Garkhal et al., 2017). Notably, CoQ could reduce the severity of colitis in mice through the suppression of inflammation by inhibiting phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (p-STAT3) and IL-17, and inducing 5′-adenosine monophosphate (AMP)-activated protein kinase (Lee et al., 2012, 2017). Additionally, CoQ plays an essential role in anti-inflammation by inducing the expression of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2, resulting in the expression of antioxidant and detoxification proteins (Yang et al., 2016). Furthermore, as a lipid-soluble antioxidant and an essential coenzyme involved in mitochondrial energy production, CoQ may strengthen the antioxidant barrier in the intestinal tract of lactating calves and stabilize the microbial community. A well-developed gastrointestinal system is associated with a lower fecal score and diarrhea incidence (Zhang et al., 2024).

The current study showed that the dietary CoQ had no significant effects on the serum BUN contents in calves, indicating normal renal function and health. Similar findings were observed in goats (El-Ela et al., 2017) and rats (Fu et al., 2009). However, decreased contents of serum BUN and creatinine were observed in Awassi rams under oxidative stress (Almoteoty and Abdulnassir, 2022). The improved excretion and disposal of urea may enhance kidney function, which could contribute to the protective effects of CoQ against oxidative stress-related kidney damage. In addition, CoQ had no significant impact on the blood protein profile (TP, ALB, and GLB) in this study, which is consistent with previous findings in rats (Fu et al., 2009) and broilers (Gopi et al., 2016). Conversely, CoQ increased serum TP and ALB contents in lactating cows (Wafa and El-Nagar, 2021), goats (El-Ela et al., 2017), and rats (Ali et al., 2010). These studies suggested that CoQ could enhance protein synthesis through the regulation of anabolic hormones and improved nitrogen absorption.

The contents of ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH remained unchanged throughout the experimental period, regardless of the treatments, indicating the hepatic health of newborn calves. Similarly, CoQ supplementation did not affect AST activity in horses subjected to acute moderate exercise (Nemec et al., 2021). Additionally, dietary CoQ supplementation had no pronounced effect on serum AST and ALT activity in mice (Fu et al., 2009). Conversely, CoQ has been shown to decrease serum AST and ALT activities in lactating cows (Wafa and El-Nagar, 2021), goats (El-Sherbiny et al., 2022), and broilers (Sharideh et al., 2020).

The dietary CoQ has been found to improve mitochondrial function and regulate genes sensitive to ROS, such as adiponectin and PPAR-α (Gholami et al., 2018), enhancing pancreatic β-cell functions and reducing INS resistance (Gholami et al., 2018; Sharideh et al., 2020). This phenomenon was also observed in individuals with INS resistance, where hepatic gluconeogenesis and/or inflammation were lower with the dietary CoQ supplementation, thereby lowering plasma contents of TC and GLU, and reducing abdominal fat (Gholami et al., 2018; Sharideh et al., 2020). However, this study showed significantly higher contents of blood INS in the Q100 group compared with the Q50 and Q0 groups. In addition, a higher serum GLU content was observed in the Q50 group compared with the Q100 and Q0 groups, consistent with findings in lactating cows (Wafa and El-Nagar, 2021) and goats (El-Ela et al., 2017). This effect may be due to the role of CoQ in enhancing cellular bioenergetics (Kucharská et al., 2021). In addition, serum GH concentration increased significantly, peaking in the Q100 group. In this study, both ADG and DMI have a positive correlation with serum hormone levels. In neonatal calves, development of the digestive tract, metabolic processes, endocrine functions, and overall health status are positively influenced by GH, INS, and insulin-like growth factor I (Blum, 2006).

In this study, CoQ had no significant effects on serum TC, HDL, or LDL contents. Similar results were observed in goats (El-Ela et al., 2017) and rats (Fu et al., 2009), where CoQ supplementation did not influence serum TC contents. Conversely, other studies have reported a reduction in serum TC following CoQ supplementation in buffalo calves (Gabr, 2020) and Awassi rams (Almoteoty and Abdulnassir, 2022), which may be attributed to improved mitochondrial respiration, liver function, and decreased oxidative stress (Feher et al., 2007). In addition, a reduction in plasma LDL contents along with an increase in HDL contents was observed with CoQ supplementation (Mabuchi et al., 2007; Mohseni et al., 2014), potentially due to the inhibition of hydroxymethylglutaryl-CoA reductase activity in the liver (Almoteoty and Abdulnassir, 2022; Gopi et al., 2015) and induced gene expression patterns (Schmelzer et al., 2011).

However, despite these reported positive effects of CoQ, a high dose of dietary CoQ increased serum NEFA compared with the Q50 and Q0 groups. Additionally, serum BHB contents were higher in the Q100 group compared with the Q0 group. The Q50 group also experienced numerically increased NEFA and BHB contents compared with the Q0 group, although these differences were not statistically significant. Ahmadi et al. (2023) demonstrated that patients treated with CoQ exhibited increased plasma fatty acids and 3-hydroxybutyrate contents, suggesting enhanced fat oxidation. High serum NEFA and increased ketone body levels are considered indicators of negative energy balance (NEB) in cattle (Li et al., 2016). Other physiological indicators of NEB include decreased blood GLU, INS, and insulin-like growth factor-1 levels, impaired production and immune function, and the development of oxidative stress and inflammation (Li et al., 2016). During NEB, large amounts of ROS are produced in the liver as a result of NEFA oxidation, which raises lipoperoxidative processes and lowers antioxidant status (Li et al., 2016). Some researchers have suggested that excessive doses of CoQ could increase vitamin E availability, leading to metabolic imbalances (El Basuini et al., 2022). Increased vitamin E levels may act as pro-oxidants and promote tissue lipid oxidation (El et al., 2019). Dobbelaar et al. (2010) found that vitamin E supplementation increased serum NEFA and protein-adjusted liver TG in heifers without causing excessive fat mobilization or hepatic steatosis.

Unlike NEB, the Q100 group in this study showed increased DMI, ADG, serum antioxidants (T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px), and hormones (INS and GH), along with reduced diarrhea frequency and serum MDA contents. Additionally, serum liver function, immune, and inflammation indicators were unaffected, and the values for all groups were within the normal range. Additionally, this study found a positive correlation between DMI and both serum BHB and NEFA contents. Previous studies reported that the increased serum BHB contents could be an essential indicator of rumen development in calves (Zhang et al., 2024) and goat kids (Abdelsattar et al., 2022). As anticipated, there was a significant positive correlation between ruminal microbial crude protein and serum BHB content in this study. Intriguingly, ruminal propionate, butyrate, and NH3–N contents in the rumen of calves in the CoQ treatments were numerically greater than those of calves in the Q0 treatment. For ruminal NH3–N, a ruminal concentration of 5.0 to 11.8 mg/dL is vital for maximizing microbial protein synthesis (Reynal and Broderick, 2005; Russell and Strobel, 1987). There was a positive correlation between ruminal NH3–N and serum T-AOC in this study, indicating the positive effects of the ruminal NH3–N content. The rise in butyrate contents might be linked to the higher solid diet intake in early life (Zhuang et al., 2023). Since butyrate is the primary energy source for intestinal epithelial cells (Oliphant and Allen-Vercoe, 2019), its increased level could stimulate intestinal epithelial proliferation and improve nutrient absorption. This could explain the improved ADG in calves supplemented with the CoQ. Moreover, the positive association between rumen butyrate and serum SOD might further explain their beneficial impacts on dairy calves. Butyrate exerts anti-inflammatory effects by enhancing the differentiation of naïve T cells into T regulatory cells and stimulating intestinal epithelial cells and antigen-presenting cells to secrete anti-inflammatory cytokines (Lee and Hase, 2014).

Conclusions

Overall, the study investigated the potential effects of dietary CoQ supplementation on growth performance, diarrhea frequency, immune status, inflammatory responses, and antioxidant capacity in preweaning dairy calves. Dietary CoQ supplementation increased levels of serum antioxidants (T-AOC, SOD, and GSH-Px) and hormones (INS and GH) and reduced serum MDA contents. Consistent with our hypothesis, these findings suggest that feeding CoQ to the newborn Holstein calves can induce significant improvements in feed intake and growth performance, as well as the prevention of calf diarrhea, with the optimal results observed at 100 mg/kg DM. However, the effects of higher doses of CoQ on increased serum NEFA and BHB require further investigation into the underlying mechanisms. Further studies are also needed to explore the impact of dietary CoQ on gut microbiota and the intestinal redox status in newborn calves.
