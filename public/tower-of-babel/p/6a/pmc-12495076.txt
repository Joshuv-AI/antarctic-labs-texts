Relationship between interleukin-6, coronary artery calcium and risk of heart failure: Insights from MESA
Authors: Muhammad Imtiaz Ahmad, Parag A Chevli, Saeid Mirzai, Rishi Rikhi, Neha Pagidipati, Leandro Slipczuk, Moyses Szklo, Michael D Shapiro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the joint association of interleukin-6 (IL-6) and coronary artery calcium (CAC) with the risk of heart failure (HF).Methods and ResultsAmong 6592 participants in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA), individuals were categorized into four groups based on CAC (0 vs >0) and IL-6 (cutoff 1.32 pg/mL via Youden’s index): low CAC/low IL-6 (reference), low CAC/high IL-6, high CAC/low IL-6, and high CAC/high IL-6. Multivariable Cox models were used to assess the risk of total heart failure (HF), HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), and HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Compared to the reference group, the hazard ratios (HRs) for total HF were 1.61 (95 % CI, 1.12–2.31) for high IL-6, 1.56 (95 % CI, 1.11–2.20) for high CAC, and 2.00 (95 % CI, 1.43–2.80) when both were elevated (multiplicative interaction p = 0.28). For HFrEF, the risk was significantly higher only when both markers were elevated: HR 2.34 (95 % CI, 1.44–3.81), compared to isolated CAC (1.11; 95 % CI, 0.65–1.87) or IL-6 (1.07; 95 % CI, 0.59–1.95) (multiplicative and additive interaction p = 0.05 and 0.008, respectively). Conversely, for HFpEF, the combined elevation of IL-6 and CAC was associated with a lower risk than either marker alone: HRs 2.13, 2.31, and 2.21, respectively (antagonistic multiplicative interaction, p = 0.02).ConclusionsCombined elevation of IL-6 and CAC was associated with increased HFrEF risk, with significant additive and multiplicative interactions. For HFpEF, combined elevation conferred less risk than either alone. Inflammation modifies the CAC—HFrEF relationship and merits further study.Keywords: Coronary artery calcium, Inflammation, Heart failure, MESA

Introduction

A complex bidirectional relationship intricately links systemic inflammation and heart failure (HF). Inflammation contributes to the etiology of heart failure (HF) and arises due to the hemodynamic stress associated with this condition [1]. Consequently, inflammatory cytokines, such as interleukin-6 (IL-6), are associated with an increased risk of HF[2,3]. Subclinical or clinical coronary atherosclerosis, a vital risk factor for the onset and development of HF, is itself a consequence of chronic inflammation [4]. For example, IL-6 levels were associated with the prevalence and progression of coronary artery calcium (CAC) in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis (MESA) [5]. Higher levels of IL-6 also predicted future atherosclerotic cardiovascular (ASCVD) events among participants with established coronary artery disease (CAD) [6]. Even in participants with or at high risk of atherosclerotic disease who were on statin therapy, inflammation appeared to be a potent driver of residual ASCVD risk [4]. However, it is still unclear how proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 influence the relationship between subclinical cardiovascular disease, as measured by CAC, and the risk of developing HF in individuals who do not have clinical ASCVD at baseline. Since inflammation plays a critical role in both the development and progression of atherosclerosis and HF, examining the interrelationship between CAC (a marker of subclinical atherosclerosis) and IL-6 (a marker of inflammation) in predicting HF risk can elucidate if inflammation modifies/amplifies the risk of CAC-associated HF risk, thus identifying a high-risk cohort.

Methods

Study population

The MESA study design has been previously described [7]. Participants aged 45 to 84 years were recruited between July 2000 and September 2002 at six field centers: Baltimore, Maryland; Chicago, Illinois; Forsyth County, North Carolina; Los Angeles, California; New York, New York; and St. Paul, Minnesota. All participants were free of clinical ASCVD, including HF, at baseline. All participants provided informed consent, and the institutional review boards at each participating institution approved the study protocol. For this analysis, participants were excluded if they had missing baseline CAC, IL-6, or HF follow-up data.

Baseline characteristics

Baseline covariates were recorded at the initial MESA examination from 2000 to 2002. Demographic data (age, sex, race/ethnicity) were obtained using standardized questionnaires. Participants were considered smokers if they reported smoking at least 100 cigarettes in their lifetime and then further classified into current or former smokers at baseline by whether they “smoked cigarettes during the last 30 days.” Twenty cigarettes were defined as a pack. Pack years were calculated by multiplying the daily intensity in packs by the duration in years. Medical insurance was categorized as having no health insurance, government insurance only (including Medicare, Medicaid, or military coverage), or private health insurance (with or without government insurance). Diabetes mellitus was defined as a self-reported history of diabetes or fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dl or the use of medications for diabetes. The use of antihypertensive drugs, lipid-lowering medications, and antidiabetic medications was determined after reviewing the medication containers brought to the clinic for verification. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure were measured using an automated sphygmomanometer. Each participant had three measurements at 2-minute intervals, and the mean of the second and third readings was used. Body mass index was reported as the weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters. The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula[8]. Blood samples obtained from all participants were stored in EDTA-anticoagulant tubes at −70 °C (University of Vermont and University of Minnesota). Total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides were measured from fasting samples. The Friedewald equation calculates LDL-C (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol) when triglycerides are less than 400 mg/dL [9].

Interleukin-6

At the initial MESA visit, IL-6 levels were measured from blood samples using an ultrasensitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Quantikine HS Human IL-6 Immunoassay; R&D Systems) and quantified in picograms per milliliter (pg/mL). The analytical coefficient of variation was 6.3 % (detection level 0.04 pg/mL) [10].

Coronary artery calcium

CAC score measurement was performed using an electron-beam CT scanner at three of the six MESA field centers (Chicago, Los Angeles, and New York), and the remaining three field centers (Baltimore, Forsyth County, and St. Paul) used a multidetector CT scanner. Each participant underwent two phantom-adjusted scans. CAC score was quantified as Agatston units (AU), and the average of the two CAC measurements was reported.

Heart failure events

MESA participants or their next of kin were contacted by trained staff every 9–12 months following MESA enrollment to inquire about any hospitalizations [7]. If any hospitalization occurred, medical records for those hospitalizations were reviewed for hospitalized HF events. Using standard criteria, the review and diagnosis of hospitalized HF events were adjudicated by the panel of the physician review committee. Probable and definite hospitalized HF events were included in the current study. Probable heart failure (HF) was ascertained by the physician’s previous diagnosis of HF or treatment for HF. Definite HF required confirmed hospitalization with heart failure and at least one additional objective criterion, such as pulmonary edema or congestion by chest radiography, dilated ventricle, reduced left ventricular function by echocardiography or ventriculography, or evidence of left ventricular diastolic dysfunction. Participants were followed up from the baseline visit until newly diagnosed HF, death, dropout, or until December 31, 2019. HF events were classified by ejection fraction (EF) and documented in the medical records at the time of hospitalization. We used a cut-off of EF 40 % to classify HF as HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) (n = 161) and, if EF ≥50 %, as HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) (n = 155). Only a few patients had a mid-range ejection fraction (HFmrEF) (n = 40); therefore, only HFpEF and HFrEF were considered for HF subtype analyses. The unclassified events due to missing EF (n = 70) or those in the HFmrEF category were censored in the analyses examining CAC/IL-6 groups with HF subtypes.

Statistical analysis

IL-6 levels were evaluated as a categorical variable based on a cutoff from Youden’s index. Briefly, Youden’s index is a summary measure of a Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve used to assess the effectiveness of a biomarker and help determine an optimal cutoff point by maximizing the difference between sensitivity and (1-specificity) [11]. The cut point of IL-6 for HF outcome was determined to be 1.32 pg/mL. CAC was also evaluated as a categorical variable, with a cutoff point defined as the presence or absence of any CAC (CAC = 0 vs. CAC > 0). The association between IL-6 and HF met the linearity assumption; however, the association between CAC and HF did not. Therefore, this interrelationship between IL-6 and CAC and HF risk was presented using categorical values of each risk factor described above and log-transformed CAC and IL-6. Briefly, to assess linearity, we compared a model that included IL-6 or CAC as a linear term to a model incorporating a spline term using the likelihood ratio test (LRT) and the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Further details of this method are described in the figure legends of the restricted cubic spline curves for IL-6 and CAC (Supplement Figures 1 and 2).

Our study sample was categorized into the following four groups: participants with CAC = 0 and IL-6 levels below the cutoff (1.32 pg/mL) (reference group), those with CAC = 0 and IL-6 levels at or above the cutoff (1.32 pg/mL), those with CAC > 0 and IL-6 levels below the cutoff (1.32 pg/mL), and finally, participants with CAC > 0 and IL-6 levels at or above the cutoff (1.32 pg/mL). Continuous variables were reported as mean and standard deviation, median (interquartile range (IQR)) for skewed variables, and n ( %) for categorical variables across these four groups. Differences in baseline characteristics across these four groups were compared for statistical significance using the Chi-square test for categorical variables, analysis of variance for normally distributed variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for non-normally distributed continuous variables.

The difference in time to HF events between the CAC and IL-6 groups was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier plot and log-rank test. The cumulative incidence of events was calculated among these groups during the follow-up period. Multivariable Cox proportional hazard models were used to examine the relationship between these four groups and the risk of heart failure (HF). The proportional hazards assumption of the models was evaluated using plots of Schoenfeld residuals and formal testing, which involved a chi-square test calculated as the sum of the Schoenfeld residuals. No significant violations of the assumption were found.

Multivariable Cox proportional Models were adjusted as follows.

Model 1: unadjusted

Model 2 adjusted for demographics (age, sex, and race) and education.

Model 3: model 2 plus insurance status, BMI, diabetes mellitus, pack-year smoking, SBP, DBP, antihypertensive agents, total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, statin use, and eGFR

Model 4: Model 3 plus intercurrent CVD events.

If available, the following covariates were updated at each examination (examinations 2 to 5): pack-years of smoking, HDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, statin use, BMI, use of antihypertensive medication, diabetes, eGFR, and health insurance. The other covariates were used from the baseline measurement.

The likelihood ratio test was used to evaluate the multiplicative interaction between CAC and IL-6 for HF risk in the fully adjusted model. We also tested the additive interaction between CAC and IL-6, measuring additivity by the relative excess risk due to interaction (RERI), the proportion of disease attributable to interaction (AP), and the synergy index (S) [12].

Separate analyses were conducted to examine the association between CAC and IL-6 groups according to HF subtype (HFpEF and HFrEF), while adjusting for the covariates above.

Additionally, we used CAC as a continuous measure, as log(CAC+1), and examined the association of log(CAC+1) with HF outcomes stratified by the IL-6 cutoff using Cox proportional hazard models. Similarly, we log-transformed IL-6 and assessed the association of log(IL-6) with HF outcomes, stratified by the presence and absence of CAC.

Several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the primary analyses. In MESA, N-terminal pro-Brain Natriuretic Peptide (NT-proBNP) data were available for ∼5500 participants at baseline examination. We performed Cox proportional hazard analysis, adjusting for time-updated NT-proBNP (examinations 1 and 3), high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT) (examinations 1 and 3), and baseline C-reactive protein (CRP) in a model with covariates from model 4, as described above.

We used Fine-Gray models to handle the competing risks of mortality. We presented the association of CAC and IL-6 groups with total HF with non-cardiovascular death as competing risks.

Additionally, Fine-Gray models were used to examine the association between CAC and IL-6 groups and HF subtypes, accounting for the competing risk of other types of HF (HFpEF or HFrEF, where relevant) and non-cardiovascular death.

Lastly, model discrimination was evaluated using Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) by comparing base models with and without the addition of log IL-6 or log CAC. The base model included covariates used in Model 4 as above and, where appropriate, also incorporated the complementary biomarker (i.e., CAC was included when testing log IL-6, and log IL-6 was included when testing CAC). Differences in discrimination were quantified as ΔC-index with 95 % confidence intervals, and statistical significance was assessed using likelihood ratio tests. Improvement in risk classification was evaluated using the continuous net reclassification index (NRI), reported overall and separately for events and non-events. Confidence intervals for NRI estimates were obtained by bootstrapping with 1000 replicates.

Data analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) and RStudio (software version 4.3.0). To account for multiple hypothesis testing, we set a Benjamin-Hochberg (BH) adjusted p-value less than the false discovery rate (FDR)) of 0.05 for the primary analysis and 0.10 for interactions [13].

The hazard ratio (HR) and 95 % confidence interval (CI) for HF are displayed in Table 2. Compared to the reference group (low CAC and high IL-6), the hazard ratio (HR) of the models adjusted for covariates including intercurrent CVD events for HF was 1.61 (95 % confidence interval (CI), 1.12–2.31) for low CAC, high IL-6; 1.56 (95 % CI, 1.11–2.20) for high CAC and low IL-6; and 2.00 (95 % CI, 1.43–2.80) for participants with high CAC and high IL-6. A non-significant multiplicative interaction between CAC and IL-6 was observed (p = 0.28) for incident heart failure. There was also a non-significant additive interaction between the CAC and IL-6 groups for total HF (additive p-interaction = 0.59). The joint observed HR of CAC and IL-6 was far below the expected HR at a multiplicative scale (1.61 × 1.56 = 2.56) and an additive scale (1.61 + 1.56 - 1 = 2.17).

Table 3 presents the HRs for HF with HFrEF and HFpEF. Compared to the reference group (low CAC and low IL-6), those with low CAC and high IL-6 and high CAC and low IL-6 demonstrated no statistically significant association with HFrEF [HR 1.07 (95 % CI, 0.59–1.95) and HR 1.11 (95 % CI, 0.65–1.87), respectively]. On the other hand, individuals with high CAC and high IL-6 exhibited a significantly higher risk of HFrEF (HR 2.34, 95 % CI 1.44–3.81; multiplicative and additive interactions between CAC and IL-6, p = 0.05 and p = 0.008, respectively). Similar associations of CAC/IL-6 groups with HFrEF were observed when HFmrEF and HF events, as well as those with missing EF, were excluded. The joint observed HR was higher than the expected value at both a multiplicative scale (1.07 × 1.11 = 1.18) and an additive scale (1.07 + 1.11 - 1 = 1.18). Therefore, there were significant multiplicative and additive interactions.

Accounting for non-cardiovascular death as a competing risk (Supplementary Table 3), the magnitude of the estimates remained significant for overall heart failure (HF) in each CAC and IL-6 category. Still, it remained significant overall, except for the HFpEF risk in the highest-risk category (HR, 1.91 (0.97–3.80) Supplementary Table 4).

When log IL-6 was added to a base model already including CAC, the changes in discrimination were small and non-significant. Still, reclassification improved for total HF (NRI = 0.14) and HFpEF (NRI = 0.18), with the effect in HFpEF primarily attributable to improved event reclassification. In contrast, IL-6 provided only a modest and non-significant incremental value in HFrEF (Supplementary Table 5).

When added to a base model containing IL-6, log CAC yielded only modest and non-significant changes in discrimination (ΔC-index <0.01, all p > 0.15), but significantly improved risk reclassification, with overall NRI values ranging from 0.29 to 0.34 across HF subtypes. These gains were driven by improved reclassification of both events and non-events, as shown in Supplementary Table 6.

Discussion

In this analysis of a large, multiethnic population free of clinical ASCVD at baseline, we investigated the joint association of subclinical atherosclerosis, as measured by CAC, and systemic inflammation, indicated by IL-6, with the risk of developing heart failure. Although the combined presence of elevated CAC and IL-6 was associated with a higher risk of overall heart HF than the presence of either alone, the combined risk was less than the sum or product of their separate risks, resulting in non-significant interaction at both the multiplicative and additive scales [14]. This was primarily driven by an antagonistic interaction between CAC and IL-6 to HFpEF risk, with a stronger association of IL-6 and CAC with HFpEF in isolation than in combination. For HFrEF as an outcome, neither IL-6 nor the presence of CAC, in isolation, had a significant association with HFrEF. However, when both CAC and IL-6 were elevated, there was a significantly higher risk of HFrEF, with a significant positive multiplicative and additive interaction. Moreover, the extent of CAC had a stronger association with HFrEF among individuals with elevated IL-6 levels than among those without them. These findings were robust in several sensitivity analyses. Although we expected the joint effect of systemic inflammation and CAC to carry a higher risk of HFpEF, the findings were contrary to our expectations, likely due to the strong association between inflammation and HFpEF. This is likely due to the “outside-in” (from the periphery to the heart) mechanism of HFpEF, which implicates systemic inflammation and metabolic distress as primary drivers of HFpEF, triggering an immune response that leads to systemic endothelial dysfunction and ultimately impacts the heart [15]. In contrast, inflammation and subclinical atherosclerosis significantly amplified the risk of HFrEF beyond what would be expected from their individual contributions, suggesting a potential role of inflammation in exacerbating subclinical atherosclerosis and ischemia, thereby enhancing the risk of HFrEF.

An association of IL-6 with incident HF, especially HFpEF, is also consistent with the prior observation that cardiometabolic-related HFpEF, the most prevalent phenotype of HFpEF, is pathologically driven by systemic inflammation [16]. Approximately 70 % of individuals with obesity-related HFpEF had a heightened prevalence of inflammation. Participants were likely to be younger and female and had greater adiposity, as indicated by a higher BMI [17]. Our study demonstrated a higher prevalence of inflammation across similar demographics and risk factors. Several mechanisms have been proposed to underlie the association between inflammation and the pathogenesis of HF, especially HFpEF. Proinflammatory cytokines, which cause endothelial dysfunction, contribute to cardiac stiffness by impairing the cyclic guanosine monophosphate/protein-kinase G pathway [18]. Furthermore, endothelial inflammation leads to the overexpression of adhesion molecules, which attract inflammatory cells, resulting in myofibroblast proliferation, interstitial collagen deposition, and adverse cardiac remodeling [19]. Inflammatory cytokines also exert deleterious effects beyond the heart. These include reducing skeletal muscle oxygen extraction during exercise, worsening anemia and sarcopenia, promoting renal sodium retention, and increasing pulmonary pressure during exercise due to pulmonary vasoconstriction. These all contribute to worsening HF symptoms and exercise tolerance [18]. Indeed, higher IL-6 levels predicted a higher risk of HF and were also associated with poorer exercise tolerance among those with HFpEF [20,21].

Although increased IL-6 levels have been associated with a higher risk of developing HF in the general population, the threshold at which IL-6 significantly predicted HF or HFpEF was generally higher than reported in this analysis, ranging from 2.2 to 3.2 pg/mL [22,23]. In addition, the prognostic value of IL-6 in HFrEF remained controversial [22]. Moreover, although changes in model discrimination with the addition of CAC or IL-6 were small and not statistically significant, both biomarkers improved risk reclassification. CAC consistently enhanced risk stratification across HF subtypes, especially HFrEF. By contrast, IL-6 provided incremental value primarily for HFpEF and not for HFrEF. Together, these results highlight that CAC and IL-6 capture distinct, complementary risk pathways —atherosclerosis and inflammation —that may be leveraged to refine prognostic models in HF.

We observed an antagonistic multiplicative interaction between CAC and IL-6 for HFpEF. Antagonistic interaction implies that each risk factor is associated with an outcome in the absence of other risk factors but not in the presence of each other [24]. This pattern is biologically plausible because IL‑6 and CAC index partially substitutable pathways: IL‑6 reflects systemic inflammation, endothelial and microvascular dysfunction, and myocardial stiffening, whereas CAC captures macrovascular atherosclerotic burden. When either pathway is already dominant, the other adds proportionally less risk—consistent with (i) competing‑risk or pathway‑substitution, (ii) ceiling/saturation effects, and (iii) syndrome heterogeneity of inflammation‑/obesity‑driven and ischemia‑driven HFpEF phenotypes in which divergent trajectories can dilute the apparent joint effect, producing a sub-multiplicative pattern [25]. Analogous antagonistic multiplicative effects have been documented elsewhere: high‑sensitivity cardiac troponin T and low ankle‑brachial index showed a significant negative multiplicative interaction for incident CVD events in community cohorts of MESA and Cardiovascular Health Study (CHS); co-occurring chronic kidney disease and established cardiovascular disease increased mortality but without multiplicative synergy, implying sub‑multiplicativity via overlapping inflammatory/vascular pathways and competing causes of death [24,26]. In genetic epidemiology, pack‑years of smoking and polygenic risk score (PRS) for lung cancer in the Framingham Heart Study demonstrated a sub‑multiplicative interaction, where heavy smoking attenuated the relative contribution of PRS [27].

Coronary ischemia is the leading cause of HF. In addition to overt coronary heart disease (CHD), which causes adverse cardiac remodeling through the replacement of necrotic tissue by fibrosis and activation of the neurohormonal cascade, chronic ischemia/hypoperfusion and endothelial dysfunction caused by ongoing coronary atherosclerosis may result in dysfunctional hibernating myocardium and myocardial fibrosis, respectively, ultimately progressing to clinical HF [[28], [29], [30]]. Therefore, in the MESA and Rotterdam Study, CAC and its progression were associated with an increased risk of HF independent of CHD [29,31]. Subclinical atherosclerosis, demonstrated by the CAC score, is also associated with regional wall motion abnormalities and lower EF without known coronary events [32]. We observed a significant multiplicative and additive interaction between CAC and inflammation for HFrEF risk, with a higher risk of HFrEF in the presence of both risk factors. Genetic evidence suggests a causal relationship between IL-6 and the development of coronary heart disease (CHD) [33]. Increasing tertiles of IL-6 were associated with higher coronary atherosclerotic burden and obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD), and this association persisted in participants with obstructive and non-obstructive CAD [34]. Additionally, IL-6 is associated with a higher prevalence and progression of CAC [35]. Therefore, persistent inflammation may potentially amplify the underlying ischemia/hypoperfusion of the myocardium, as conferred by the presence of CAC, thereby increasing the risk of HFrEF.

Despite the overwhelming evidence of inflammation's role in the pathogenesis and progression of HF, specific therapies are lacking. Trials targeting upstream inflammation (IL-1 and IL-6) signal potential benefits. Canakinumab, an IL-1β blocker, significantly reduced HF hospitalization and a composite of mortality and HF hospitalization compared to placebo (with a greater risk reduction in those with larger reductions in IL-6 and CRP [36,37]. Currently, participants with elevated high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) levels and EF>40 % are being recruited for the How Ziltivekimab Works Compared to Placebo in People With Heart Failure and Inflammation (HERMES) trial for the safety and efficacy of Ziltivekimab [38]. Additionally, sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor analogs (GLP-1) have been shown to reduce the inflammatory markers; however, the efficacy of Semaglutide vs. placebo in HFpEF-related symptoms was similar regardless of levels of inflammation, suggesting that inflammation reduction may not be the dominant pathway through which it exerts beneficial effects [17].

Our study has several limitations. First, we evaluated the risk of HF based on a single measure of IL-6 and CAC (IL-6 was measured only at baseline visit), therefore, we could not assess the impact of longitudinal changes in these predictors on the risk of HF. Moreover, causal inference cannot be inferred, given the observational design of this study. Second, although we adjusted for known confounders and time-updated confounders to investigate the association of CAC and IL-6 with HF risk, unmeasured confounding remains a limitation in our analysis. Third, there were 70 unclassified cases of HF (neither HFpEF nor HFrEF), which may have influenced our results. Fifth, we defined HF subtypes based on the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) cutoff in the MESA study. However, we did not have additional echocardiographic parameters to classify HF subtypes (such as imaging quality and reader experience) or the ability to address the effect of interobserver variability, thus incurring a risk of potential misclassification of HF subtypes. Sixth, the definition of probable HF, based solely on physician diagnosis and treatment of HF, has the potential for misclassification of HF. This approach may have led to overdiagnosis (multifactorial nature of dyspnea, related to lung disease, etc., or edema related to venous insufficiency) or underdiagnosis (presentation of HF with mild or atypical symptoms). Seventh, the analyses of HF subtypes were exploratory and thus should be interpreted with caution. As such, these results should be best considered hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive; further studies with large prospective cohorts are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusions

Combined elevated Inflammation and CAC were associated with a higher risk of HFrEF, with significant additive and multiplicative interactions. There was antagonistic interaction on a multiplicative scale for HFpEF. Inflammation modifies the risk of CAC-associated HFrEF and warrants further investigation.
