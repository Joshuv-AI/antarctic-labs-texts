Introducing Skeletally-Mature Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Technique Using Reinforcement (SATURN) With Iliotibial Band Autograft
Authors: Emre Anil Özbek, Armin Runer, Sahil Dadoo, Jonathan Dalton, Michael McClincy, Yi-Meng Yen, Benton Heyworth
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are increasingly common in the adolescent age group. Recent literature has endorsed combining ACL reconstruction with lateral extra-articular procedures to minimize residual rotatory knee instability and reduce the risk of reinjury in this age group. This technique describes a single-bundle combined anatomical ACL reconstruction and lateral extra-articular reinforcement performed with a single iliotibial band autograft. Also this technique allows for the ACL reconstruction and lateral extra-articular stabilization to be performed through a single surgical incision while obtaining autograft tissue without disruption of the extensor or hamstring mechanisms.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) rupture is the most common knee injury in physically active adolescents.1 Although delayed or nonoperative treatments have historically been endorsed in this age group, surgical management has increased in recent years because of evidence of increased secondary injuries (meniscal and cartilage) with delayed surgical stabilization.1,2 Currently, 67% of ACL injuries are treated surgically in the adolescent age group, with a 26-fold increase in ACL reconstruction (ACL-R) in this cohort over the last 2 decades.1,3 Because of the significant functional demands after ACL-R in adolescents, failures rates in this age group are significantly higher than in adults, with rates of 19% to 32% reported in the literature.4,5

Graft choice for adolescent ACL-R has a direct impact on the success of the surgery, with allograft ACL-R failing 3.8 times more often than autografts.6 Hamstring tendon (HT), bone-patellar tendon-bone (BTB), and quadriceps tendon (QT) are the most commonly used autografts in this age group; however, all are associated with disadvantages. HT autograft demonstrates the highest risk for graft failure,7,8 BTB autograft can lead to donor site morbidities such as anterior knee pain and patella fracture, and QT autograft has been shown to decrease knee extensor strength.9,10 Interestingly, the use of iliotibial band (ITB) autograft for ACL-R has resulted in comparable functional outcomes and failure rates to BTB autograft, while achieving faster postoperative knee extensor mechanism recovery than BTB, QT and HT autograft.11,12 This article presents a single-bundle anatomic ACL reconstruction combining lateral extra-articular plasty with ITB autograft (Video 1).

Surgical Technique

Graft Harvesting

An oblique 5 cm lateral skin incision is created from just posterior to the lateral epicondyle. The ITB’s anterior and posterior borders are identified, and the central 80% is planned for harvest, leaving approximately 2 to 3 mm of tissue behind at the anterior and posterior borders (Fig 1). A scalpel is used to initially create the anterior and posterior borders of the ITB graft, and these splits are carried proximally into the thigh with the use of meniscectomies while taking care to avoid convergence of the anterior and posterior graft margins (Fig 2). A graft length of 15 cm from the lateral epicondyle is ideal to ensure adequate tissue for reconstruction. The ITB graft tissue is cut proximally with a curved meniscotome when possible, but a secondary proximal incision can be made to facilitate proximal graft release if meniscotome release is difficult. Distally, the ITB graft tissue is separated past the lateral epicondyle to the level of the lateral joint line, leaving the ITB insertion and joint capsules intact (Fig 3). The ITB graft is then sutured to be tubularized at a length of 2 to 3 cm from its proximal end, and graft thickness is measured (Fig 4) with a typical diameter of 7.5 to 9 mm (Fig 5).

A Cobb elevator is used to blunt dissection between the iliotibial band and subcutaneous fat in the right knee.

Femoral Tunnel Preparation

The standard arthroscopic anterolateral and anteromedial portals of the knee are created, and diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to evaluate the cartilage tissues on the joint surfaces, medial and lateral menisci, posterior cruciate ligament, and the ACL. Appropriate repairs can be made before proceeding to the ACL-R.

To begin the ACL-R, the lateral femoral epicondyle and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) are then identified in the ITB harvest incision. The extra-articular femoral tunnel entry point is identified and marked 5 mm posterior to the lateral epicondyle in line with the anatomic footprint of the anterolateral ligament, which has been previously described (Fig 6).13 While viewing through the medial portal, a retrograde femoral aiming guide (Acufex Trunav; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) is inserted via the lateral portal and placed at the anatomic ACL femoral footprint at the lateral femoral notch. Once the intra-articular footprint is identified and the aiming guide is in place, the extra-articular drill sleeve is placed through the drill guide at the predetermined extra-articular footprint location.14 A guidewire is passed through the guide across the lateral femur to connect the extra-articular (anterolateral ligament) and intra-articular (ACL) femoral footprints, and its position confirmed at both locations. Before tunnel drilling, care is taken to ensure avoidance of injury to the LCL complex at the lateral epicondyle. The femoral tunnel is then created in a retrograde manner using an acorn-style reamer sized to the graft diameter (Fig 7). A looped suture is shuttled across the femoral tunnel to enable future graft passage.

The lateral femoral epicondyle (black arrow) and lateral collateral ligament (white arrow) are then identified in the iliotibial band harvest incision. The extra-articular femoral tunnel entry point is identified and marked 5 mm posterior to the lateral epicondyle in line with the anatomic footprint of the anterolateral ligament (yellow arrow).

A rasp is used to create a smooth lateral entrance of the femoral tunnel.

Tibial Tunnel Preparation

After femoral tunnel preparation. The tibial tunnel is prepared. Viewing from the lateral portal, an aiming arm is introduced through the medial portal, centered at the tibial ACL footprint. The drilling sleeve is provisionally positioned to demarcate the skin to create an incision for tunnel creation and graft fixation. Once the incision is made, the drilling sleeve is reinserted to the tibial cortex and tunnel length measurements are made. A 2.4 mm pin is then introduced across the guide and into the joint, and its anatomic position is confirmed. After anatomic pin placement, an appropriately sized tibial tunnel is drilled using a rigid reamer. The passing stitch through the femoral tunnel is retrieved through the tibial tunnel.

Graft Passage and Fixation

The graft, still attached at Gerdy’s tubercle, is passed through the femoral tunnel (extra-articular to intra-articular), across the joint, and through the tibial tunnel (intra-articular to extra-articular). This effectively routes the ITB graft to span both the anterolateral ligament and ACL anatomy. In this technique the ITB graft is kept superficial to the LCL in line with previous ITB ACL-R techniques from our group and others.15,16

Lateral extra-articular tenodesis fixation is performed with the knee freely flexed over the side of the surgical bed, typically flexing between 70° to 80°. A bioabsorbable screw (1 mm thicker than the graft and 25 mm in length) (Biosure; Smith & Nephew) is inserted at the extra-articular aperture while applying maximal (∼80 N) tension to the graft. Afterward, the lateral extra-articular tenodesis is fixated through the femoral condyle, attention is turned to ACL fixation. The knee is then brought into a position of extension or slight flexion with a reverse-Lachman stress placed the proximal tibia. The ACL graft is then maximally (∼80 N) tensioned and fixated with insertion of a bioabsorbable screw (1 mm thicker than the graft and 25 mm in length) (Biosure; Smith & Nephew).17, 18, 19

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Patients are restricted to minimal weightbearing during the first 2 weeks after surgery, with transition to full weightbearing over weeks 3 to 6. A range-of-motion–limiting brace is used to limit motion outside of rehabilitation to 0° to 90° of flexion. To prevent stiffness, active and passive knee range of motion exercises may be performed as tolerated in the immediate postoperative period. Rehabilitation progression follows previously described ACL-R rehabilitation protocols, with return-to-sports evaluation occurring between 9 and 12 months given adequate neuromuscular control.

Discussion

In the last 15 years, ACL injuries in the adolescent age group have increased by 143%, with a current incidence of 23.3 per 100,000 people.1 The greatest increase is reported in older adolescents aged 15 to 19 years of age.3 The rate of ACL rerupture is reported to be between 7.5% to 25% in the adolescent age group, and revision surgery is reported to have a higher risk of complications when compared to adults.3,7 Soft tissue grafts, allografts, and thin graft thickness are reported as independent risk factors for ACL rerupture.6,7,20 In a recent cohort study including pediatric and adolescent patients, the highest failure rate was reported after use of hamstring autografts, whereas graft thickness less than 8 mm was reported as a risk factor for ACL rerupture in another study.7,20 Although a higher failure rate has been reported with the use of allografts during ACL reconstruction compared to autografts, allografts are also used to augment thin hamstring autografts.6,21 However, a higher rate of ACL rerupture has been reported in this patient group compared to patients using only autograft.21 After tubularization of the ITB autograft using our technique, a graft thickness of 7.5 to 9 mm can be obtained. The senior author did not require any allograft augmentation during the use of ITB, which can achieve more predictable graft thickness values than hamstring autograft.

The BTB autograft is another preferred graft, especially in the adolescent age group with closed physes. Although the BTB autograft has been associated with postoperative anterior knee pain and cosmetic complaints in many studies, satisfactory postoperative success rates have been reported with this autograft, especially in the adolescent age group who expect to return to sports.7,22 However, because of injury recurrence rates in this age group, addition of lateral extra-articular procedures to minimize reinjury have been increasingly supported, especially in patients younger than 20 years of age and those who plan to return to professional sports.4,23 Current techniques require additional surgical incisions and autograft/allograft tissue, and there are complications associated with this additional surgery.24 In the surgical technique we present in this article, no additional incision or graft material is required for the lateral extra-articular reinforcement done in conjunction with ACL-R (Table 1).

In conclusion, this technical note describes a surgical technique for ACL-R using an ITB autograft combined with lateral extra-articular reinforcement via a single incision and graft. The use of the ITB autograft also eliminates disruption of both the hamstring and extensor mechanisms while providing a graft of reliable thickness and length. We believe this technique may be an appropriate technique for adolescent patients with ACL injury to improve rotational stability and minimize the risks of reinjury.
