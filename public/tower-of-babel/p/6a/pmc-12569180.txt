Moderated mediation of technostress, TPACK, and growth mindset on competency in interdisciplinary teaching among STEM lecturers
Authors: Wenhao Su, Hidayah Mohd Fadzil, Rose Amnah Abd Rauf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs technology becomes deeply integrated into university STEM education, technostress is reshaping interdisciplinary teaching and challenging STEM lecturers’ competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT). This study surveyed 405 Chinese STEM lecturers and applied partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) to examine a moderated mediation model. The results showed that technostress had a significant negative effect on CIT (β = -0.373, t = 12.347, p < 0.001) and on technological pedagogical content knowledge (TPACK) (β = -0.271, t = 5.180, p < 0.001). TPACK positively predicted CIT (β = 0.290, t = 5.380, p < 0.001) and partially mediated the technostress-CIT link (β = -0.078, t = 3.540, p < 0.001). Growth mindset (GM) significantly moderated both the technostress-CIT relationship (β = 0.152, t = 6.560, p < 0.001) and the technostress-TPACK relationship (β = 0.223, t = 5.288, p < 0.001), with a stronger buffering effect on TPACK, suggesting that GM plays a greater role in preserving knowledge structures than in directly supporting CIT. These findings validate a novel moderated mediation framework integrating technostress, TPACK, and GM to explain CIT. Introduction

In the 21 st century, teachers are expected to integrate Information and Communication Technology (ICT) effectively into lesson planning, instructional design, and assessment practices1. While technology has improved teaching efficiency, the increasing complexity of digital tools often generates substantial pressure, particularly for teachers lacking adequate technological proficiency2. This has led to the emergence of technology-related stress, commonly known as technostress, which refers to a psychological response caused by individuals’ inability to cope with the demands of using technology in professional settings3.

Technostress is particularly pronounced in Science-Technology-Engineering-Mathematic (STEM) education compared to other disciplines4. STEM teachers often rely on advanced digital tools such as programming platforms, virtual laboratories, and data processing systems to support their teaching and research5. As interdisciplinary teaching reforms progress, these demands have intensified, requiring teachers not only to demonstrate technological proficiency but also to integrate knowledge across multiple domains6. This dual requirement has significantly increased the cognitive and operational burden placed on STEM teachers in both technology use and instructional practice.

In the context of Chinese higher education, this issue is further intensified by national strategic initiatives, such as the “New Engineering” and “Emerging Science” programs, which strongly emphasize interdisciplinary innovation7. Many universities have incorporated interdisciplinary thinking and integrative ability as core learning outcomes in their STEM talent development frameworks8,9. Achieving these goals depends not only on curriculum reform but also, and more critically, on the competency of university lecturers to deliver interdisciplinary teaching10. However, STEM lecturers are often trained within highly specialized academic systems and may lack the experience or confidence to teach across disciplinary boundaries11,12. These challenges are compounded by technostress, which further limits their willingness and competency to engage in interdisciplinary innovation. To effectively address these challenges and support the implementation of interdisciplinary teaching reforms, these lecturers must be able to integrate and apply knowledge from multiple disciplines in their teaching design and implement. This ability is considered a necessary condition for lecturers to effectively conduct instructional activities in contemporary STEM education. In relevant studies, this core ability is defined as competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT)13,14.

The development of CIT relies on teachers’ ability to construct connections across disciplines, with the Technological Pedagogical Content Knowledge (TPACK) framework providing structured knowledge support for this process. Previous studies have shown that the TPACK framework, by emphasizing the integration of technology, subject content, and pedagogy, offers a clear knowledge system to support the development of CIT15,16. In addition, research has indicated that technostress negatively affects teachers’ TPACK proficiency and instructional practices17,18. Evidently, there are direct or indirect logical connections among technostress, TPACK, and CIT. However, existing research has primarily focused on the theoretical discussion of TPACK itself, with limited analysis of its functional mechanisms within specific interdisciplinary teaching contexts. For example, questions such as how teachers balance the demands of technological integration in multi-disciplinary instructional tasks, and how the negative impact of technostress on TPACK can be alleviated through targeted interventions to enhance CIT, remain insufficiently examined.

In this regard, Growth Mindset (GM), which refers to the belief that abilities can be developed through sustained effort and learning, offers a potential psychological mechanism19. Teachers with a strong GM are more likely to overcome challenges such as technostress and remain actively engaged in pedagogical innovation20,21. Although GM has been widely studied in general educational settings22, particularly among K-12 teachers23, its role in supporting interdisciplinary teaching among STEM lecturers in higher education, where disciplinary specialization is stronger and research expectations are emphasized, requires further investigation.

Building on the above analysis, this study focuses on university STEM lecturers in China and aims to address the identified gaps by constructing and empirically testing a model that illustrates the interplay among technostress, TPACK, and GM in shaping their CIT. The study seeks to provide both theoretical insights and practical implications for strengthening CIT among these lecturers within technology-intensive educational contexts.

RQ 1: How does technostress negatively influence STEM lecturers’ CIT?

RQ 2: How does technostress affect STEM lecturers’ TPACK?

RQ 3: How does TPACK influence STEM lecturers’ CIT?

RQ 4: What mediating role does TPACK play in the relationship between technostress and CIT?

RQ 5: What is the moderating role of GM in the relationship between technostress and CIT, as well as between technostress and TPACK?

Hypotheses development and theoretical framework

Theoretical inference

This study draws upon three theoretical models, including the TPACK framework24, the Iceberg Competency25, and Implicit Theories19, to construct a comprehensive framework for understanding how STEM university lecturers develop competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT) under the influence of technostress.

The TPACK framework offers a structural lens for analyzing these lecturers’ technological, pedagogical, and content knowledge integration26. It serves as a proxy for observable instructional knowledge and skills relevant to interdisciplinary contexts. Technostress arises when lecturers perceive misalignments or inadequacies across TPACK domains, especially when required to adopt novel digital tools for interdisciplinary tasks27. As such, the TPACK framework helps explain why lecturers may be more or less susceptible to technostress when navigating interdisciplinary teaching settings. While dedicated stress theories such as the Transactional Stress Theory could further unpack the psychological mechanisms of technostress, this study prioritizes the TPACK framework given its central role in describing lecturers’ instructional competencies. Therefore, technostress is interpreted here in relation to perceived TPACK misalignments, acknowledging the framework’s limited explanatory scope for stress-specific processes.

The Iceberg Competency Model is introduced in this study to provide a conceptual framework for understanding the layered structure of teaching competency. This model distinguishes between visible components, such as knowledge and skills that are readily observable and trainable, and invisible components, such as personality traits, motivations, and values, which are more stable and implicit14. In the context of interdisciplinary teaching, this model underscores that effective performance is shaped by both surface-level instructional proficiency and deeper psychological foundations. However, it is important to note that the present study focuses on the observable and measurable dimensions of CIT14. This model is therefore applied not as a direct operational basis for measurement, but as a conceptual scaffold to interpret the broader conceptual structure of teaching competencies. Its use helps situate the study within a well-established understanding of how professional capabilities can be structured, while the actual measurement remains confined to the visible layer of the model.

The Implicit Theories framework, particularly the notion of Growth Mindset (GM), contributes to understanding how individual beliefs about the malleability of abilities affect one’s capacity to cope with challenges like technostress28. Teachers with a strong GM are more likely to respond to technostress constructively, leveraging effort and adaptive strategies to enhance their TPACK and overall teaching efficacy28. GM, therefore, acts as a psychological buffer that links the underlying cognitive dispositions emphasized in the Iceberg model with the observable instructional competencies represented by TPACK.

Together, these three models provide complementary insights: TPACK captures measurable instructional capacity, the Iceberg model highlights the layered nature of competence, and Implicit Theories explain how individual beliefs modulate the development process. This integrated framework (Fig. 1) supports a nuanced investigation into the pathways from technostress to interdisciplinary teaching practices.

Conceptual Framework.

Technostress and competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT)

This section addresses Research Question 1. In the field of education, teachers’ technostress has emerged alongside the advancement of educational informatization, encompassing psychological, instrumental, and educational attributes11. Studies have demonstrated that technostress has a significant negative impact on teachers’ work performance and engagement29. However, despite these findings, both research and practice have largely focused on the positive impact of technology integration, while paying insufficient attention to how negative factors such as technostress may hinder the development of specific instructional capacities, particularly competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT).

Technostress is typically assessed using the instrument developed by Tarafdar30, which adopts a general technology-centered perspective and is detached from the educational setting. Building on this foundation, Çoklar31 developed a teacher-specific technostress scale, which has since been adopted in various educational studies. For example, a study conducted in China found that pre-service mathematics teachers experiencing moderate-to-high levels of technostress demonstrated notable deficiencies in teaching design and implementation¹¹.

Further literature reveals that the use and integration of educational technologies are considered core dimensions of CIT14. Existing measurement frameworks commonly assess teachers’ ability to apply digital tools in interdisciplinary contexts, and multiple studies affirm that such competency positively influences CIT2,32. In actual interdisciplinary teaching practice, teachers are often expected to synthesize knowledge across disciplines while leveraging technological platforms to support collaborative learning and content delivery. These dual demands significantly increase cognitive load and operational pressure, often resulting in elevated technostress12. Despite these insights, most research linking technostress and teaching competency has focused on general education teachers. Little empirical attention has been given to university STEM lecturers, who operate under more intensive interdisciplinary and technological expectations. This gap highlights the need for targeted investigation into how technostress may influence their CIT in higher education settings.

Although direct empirical evidence linking technostress to CIT remains scarce, indirect insights from the literature indicate a possible negative association. First, technostress may suppress teaching innovation, thus limiting instructors’ ability to creatively design interdisciplinary learning experiences33. Second, it can hinder the effective integration of digital tools and learning resources, thereby lowering the quality and coherence of interdisciplinary course implementation34,35. Third, technostress may impair the accuracy and responsiveness of assessment practices in interdisciplinary contexts35,36. These aspects, which are integral components of CIT, are all susceptible to disruption by technostress. Based on this reasoning, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H1: Technostress has a significant negative impact on CIT.

TPACK as potential mediating factor

This section addresses Research Questions 2, 3, and 4. The level of teachers’ TPACK significantly determines their ability to adapt to digital environments and implement digital teaching, reflecting their competency in integrating technology, pedagogy, and content knowledge6. This integration enables teachers to address challenges such as technostress brought about by shifts in digital landscapes18,27. Furthermore, TPACK represents the capacity to synthesize these three domains of knowledge in ways that foster teaching innovation and ensure the delivery of high-quality interdisciplinary instruction37.

A growing body of research has identified a reciprocal relationship between technostress and TPACK. Some studies have found that technostress negatively affects the development of TPACK by increasing teachers’ cognitive load and emotional exhaustion38. It may also reduce teachers’ willingness to adopt educational technologies, thereby impeding the development of technological knowledge within the TPACK framework39. Notably, one study from a different perspective revealed a positive correlation between moderate levels of technostress, job satisfaction, and TPACK40. In contrast, other studies suggest the reverse direction of influence. For instance, some research indicates that the TPACK framework can shape teachers’ levels of technostress and their attitudes toward educational technologies41. A qualitative study also suggested that stronger TPACK capacities may help mitigate technostress42.

TPACK, as a core theoretical framework, provides essential theoretical support and practical guidance for the integration of teachers’ knowledge structures in interdisciplinary teaching. It supports not only the development of teachers’ instructional capacity but also enhances their interdisciplinary literacy and curriculum design skills16. In STEM education, applying the TPACK framework encourages teachers to integrate content from multiple disciplines in technologically supported environments43. Studies have shown that teachers with higher levels of TPACK are better prepared to address the challenges of interdisciplinary teaching, effectively employing digital tools and pedagogical strategies to promote student engagement and deep learning44. The pedagogical domain of TPACK, in particular, supports the design of interdisciplinary instruction through approaches such as Problem-Based Learning (PBL) and Project-Based Learning (PBL)45. However, how TPACK contributes to the development of STEM lecturers’ competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT) has received limited empirical attention, despite the conceptual value of this framework in interdisciplinary settings.

These interrelationships suggest the possibility that TPACK may act as a mediating factor between technostress and CIT. According to Mackinnon46, mediation is likely when three interlinking relationships are present among the predictor, mediator, and outcome variables. This theoretical premise supports the development of the following hypotheses:

H2: Technostress has a significant negative impact on STEM lecturers’ TPACK.

H3: TPACK has a significant positive influence on STEM lecturers’ CIT.

H4: Technostress may influence CIT partially through the mediating role of TPACK.

The moderating role of growth mindset (GM)

This section addresses Research Question 5. Growth mindset (GM), as a psychological resource, has been shown to exert buffering effects under stress and is widely recognized as a simple, cost-effective intervention for enhancing both student and teacher outcomes28. Within educational contexts, GM is associated with teachers’ capacity to cope with technological changes and instructional challenges20. Technostress, which often leads to psychological fatigue and cognitive overload, can undermine teachers’ instructional innovation and teaching performance. However, psychological resources such as GM have been found to alleviate these negative effects47. For example, empirical studies suggest that GM reduces the adverse influence of technostress on teachers’ work outcomes. When teachers possess a high level of GM, the impact of technostress on their instructional behavior tends to diminish or even become negligible48.

In addition, GM contributes to teachers’ self-efficacy and emotion regulation, supporting sustained psychological resilience in stressful environments. These psychological mechanisms also facilitate the internalization of technological and pedagogical knowledge, thereby supporting the development of TPACK47,49. By maintaining cognitive flexibility and emotional stability, GM may help buffer the adverse effects of technostress on the development of TPACK, particularly within complex digital teaching environments common in STEM education. Moreover, GM may strengthen the positive association between TPACK and competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT), by encouraging STEM lecturers to apply integrated knowledge more confidently and adaptively in complex interdisciplinary settings. In this regard, GM is expected to moderate the relationship between technostress and each of the two outcome variables. Based on these insights, the following hypothesis is proposed:

H5: GM moderates the effects of technostress on both TPACK and CIT.

Methodology

This study employed a cross-sectional quantitative survey design to systematically examine the causal relationships among technostress, TPACK, competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT), and growth mindset (GM) among STEM lecturers. Partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) was chosen for hypothesis testing, given its suitability for complex path models and latent variable analysis.

Participants

The participants in this study were full-time university teaching staff engaged in STEM disciplines at seven first-tier public universities in Yunnan Province, China. Inclusion criteria required participants to have at least one year of continuous teaching experience in a STEM subject, while part-time instructors, visiting scholars, and administrative staff were excluded to ensure the sample represented stable teaching faculty. Although they held different professional titles (e.g., teaching assistant, lecturer, associate professor, and professor), they are collectively referred to as “lecturers” in this study, in line with common usage in Chinese higher education contexts.

These seven universities were selected because they collectively concentrate the province’s primary STEM higher education resources and talent cultivation programs, making them broadly representative of STEM lecturer profiles in underdeveloped regions of China. This region is among the most underdeveloped provinces in China, where lecturers often lack access to high-quality, cutting-edge training in educational information technology50. As a result, these lecturers are more susceptible to experiencing technostress when faced with complex interdisciplinary teaching tasks, making this region representative for such research.

According to the China Educational Statistics Yearbook, there are 10,564 STEM lecturers across these seven universities51. Using Calculate.net, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 371 participants. With an additional 15% buffer to account for potential non-responses, 427 questionnaires were distributed. Over a four-week period, from mid-January to mid-February 2025, participants voluntarily and anonymously completed the survey. In total, 405 valid responses were received, yielding a response rate of 94.85%. Ethical approval was granted by the University of Malaya Research Ethics Committee (No. UM.TNC2/UMREC_4035). The purpose of the study was explained to the lecturers and informed consent was obtained. All procedures involving participants were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

The first section collects demographic information, including gender, age, teaching experience, educational background, and professional title. The second section utilizes a scale developed by Çoklar31 to evaluate technostress across five dimensions: learning and teaching process oriented, profession oriented, technical issue oriented, personal oriented, and social oriented. This instrument evaluates four dimensions, including technological pedagogical science knowledge, technological pedagogical mathematics knowledge, technological pedagogical engineering knowledge, and integrative STEM knowledge. The fourth section employs a scale developed by Chinese scholar Liang and Li14 to evaluate STEM lectures’ CIT. This scale evaluates three dimensions, namely interdisciplinary teaching design, interdisciplinary teaching implementation, and interdisciplinary teaching evaluation, capturing the multifaceted nature of interdisciplinary teaching practices. The final section employs a scale developed by Sigmundsson and Haga53 to evaluate lecturers’ GM. This scale stands out for its superior cultural adaptability and broad applicability, demonstrating excellent feasibility across multiple age groups.

Data analysis followed a two-stage procedure, first validating the measurement model and then testing the structural model. Structural model assessment

Hypothesis evaluation revealed that Technostress negatively and significantly influenced CIT (β=−0.373, p < 0.001), thus supporting H1. Similarly, Technostress demonstrated a negative and significant relationship with TPACK (β=−0.271, p < 0.001), providing support for H2. Additionally, TPACK positively influenced CIT (β = 0.290, p < 0.001), thereby supporting H3. The 95% confidence intervals for all three paths did not include 0, further confirming their statistical significance (Fig. 2; Table 4). The significant negative paths from technostress to both TPACK and CIT highlight the urgency of addressing technostress in STEM lecturers’ interdisciplinary contexts, suggesting a need for targeted support strategies in training programs. Meanwhile, the positive contribution of TPACK to CIT underscores the importance of strengthening these lecturers’ integrated knowledge to enhance instructional effectiveness.

Mediation analysis

To examine whether TPACK mediates the relationship between Technostress and CIT, this study conducted a mediation analysis using SmartPLS 4.0 with bootstrapping (5,000 resamples). Results demonstrated that Technostress had significant negative direct (β=−0.373, p < 0.001) and indirect effects (β=−0.078, p < 0.001) on CIT through TPACK, leading to a significant negative total effect of −0.451 (p < 0.001). Given that both direct and indirect effects were significant and directionally consistent, this mediation is classified as complementary mediation. Therefore, H4 is supported (Table 6). The partial mediation of TPACK suggests that reducing technostress alone may be insufficient; strengthening these lecturers’ integrated knowledge structures is equally vital for enhancing their CIT.

Examination of moderation effects

This study employed PLS-SEM with bootstrapping to examine the moderated mediation model. However, instead of the automatically generated slope plot, Python was used for greater customization, precision, and clarity in visualizing the moderation effect.

The results indicate that the interaction effect of GM significantly predicts CIT (β = 0.152, p < 0.001), demonstrating that GM moderates the relationship between Technostress and CIT (Table 7). Furthermore, the interaction between GM and Technostress significantly predicts TPACK (β = 0.223, p < 0.001), indicating that GM also moderates the relationship between Technostress and TPACK. These findings confirm H5 and establish that GM moderates the mediating role of TPACK in the relationship between Technostress and CIT. The moderating effect of GM suggests that psychological resources may buffer the negative impacts of technostress, providing valuable insights for designing empowerment-oriented interventions for STEM lecturers. The stronger moderating effect on the technostress-TPACK path suggests that GM is particularly effective in preserving integrated knowledge frameworks compared to directly sustaining CIT.

Discussion, conclusion and implication

Discussion

This study offers empirical evidence for the significant negative predictive effect of technostress on competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT), thereby extending technostress research beyond its previously established associations with teaching innovation and performance22,28. Consistent with methodological trends in the field, it applies PLS-SEM as the primary analytical approach26,54. While earlier studies have mainly focused on how technostress impairs teaching in routine or subject-specific contexts, few have examined its effects in interdisciplinary settings2,50. This study contributes to this line of inquiry by examining university STEM lecturers, a group that has received relatively less attention in technostress research, despite their exposure to more complex instructional demands in interdisciplinary contexts compared to K-12 teachers. Compared to K-12 teachers, whose technostress levels are generally low to moderate, STEM lecturers tend to experience a more intensified and multifaceted form of technostress in interdisciplinary teaching settings.

Furthermore, this study confirms a significant negative effect of technostress on TPACK, consistent with the findings of Wang39, which indicate that high levels of technostress can reduce teachers’ willingness to integrate technology and thus hinder the development of their TPACK. Previous studies focusing on pre-service teachers and Chinese primary school generalist teachers have also observed similar trends3,4. However, some research has suggested that, under conditions of adequate organizational support or sufficient resources, moderate levels of technostress might be positively associated with teachers’ job satisfaction and TPACK development5,40. In contrast, the present study among university STEM lecturers found a more pronounced inhibitory effect of technostress. This difference suggests that the potential motivational effect of technostress is not universal but rather depends on teachers’ background and instructional context. While prior research has primarily highlighted the positive contribution of the technological knowledge dimension within the TPACK framework12, this study further reveals the vulnerability of the integrative nature of TPACK under sustained technostress. This finding suggests that if the dimensions within the TPACK framework lack effective integration, even strengths in a single dimension may fail to independently support teaching under conditions of technostress, thereby weakening lecturers’ overall instructional adaptability.

Additionally, this study provides empirical evidence for the positive predictive effect of TPACK on CIT, which is consistent with previous findings on the positive role of TPACK in supporting teachers’general teaching competency37,55,56. However, prior studies have mostly focused on enhancing the teaching competencies of teachers in single-subject contexts, lacking attention to the competency structures required for interdisciplinary teaching15,29. Secondly, previous research has often examined the function of TPACK in low technological complexity or slower technology-update environments11,18, whereas this study focuses on the rapidly evolving, high-technology STEM education context. Finally, earlier studies mainly relied on teachers’ self-reports to infer the link between TPACK and teaching competency27,38, whereas this study complements these findings through structural equation modeling. By confirming this expanded function, the present study contributes to extending the applicability of the TPACK framework within STEM higher education settings where interdisciplinary teaching is gaining increasing importance.

It is noteworthy that this study confirms the partial mediating role of TPACK in the relationship between technostress and CIT, proposing a new theoretical pathway: that STEM lecturers’ CIT under technostress is not merely shaped by external technological environments, but is further influenced and reconstructed through their internal capacity for knowledge integration (TPACK). This perspective challenges the traditional view of TPACK as a relatively static competency structure, which has typically been studied in supportive organizational contexts, by emphasizing its potential adaptive adjustments under stress, thereby affecting the development of CIT6,23,43.Through this cross-theoretical integration, the study not only provides new insights for explaining CIT, but also offers empirical support for the applicability of the TPACK framework and the Iceberg Competency Model in STEM higher education interdisciplinary contexts.

One key finding of this study is that growth mindset (GM) significantly moderates the relationships between technostress and both CIT and TPACK, which is consistent with Dweck’s implicit theory that GM can enhance teachers’ self-efficacy and coping strategies in acquiring knowledge and skills19. While earlier studies mostly highlighted GM’s positive role in general work engagement or emotional regulation, they paid less attention to its potential in supporting STEM lecturers’ integrated knowledge structures under stress57,58. This study demonstrates that GM can help maintain the coherence of TPACK in challenging interdisciplinary teaching environments, extending GM’s theoretical significance from individual attitudes to professional knowledge integration. Moreover, the results showed that GM’s buffering effect on the technostress-TPACK relationship was relatively stronger than on CIT, underlining its specific contribution to preserving knowledge structures. Finally, these results may offer preliminary insights for designing STEM lecturers’ training frameworks in digital educational contexts, although further applied research is needed to transform these theoretical advances into concrete interventions.

Conclusion

This study systematically examines the complex relationships among technostress, TPACK, CIT and GM among STEM lecturers, empirically validating a moderated mediation model. The findings reveal that: (1) Technostress has a significant negative predictive effect on CIT, indicating that high levels of technostress can undermine lecturers’ instructional performance in interdisciplinary teaching. (2) Technostress also exerts a significant negative impact on TPACK, suggesting that it impedes lecturers’ ability to integrate technology, pedagogy, and subject knowledge effectively. (3) TPACK positively predicts CIT, confirming its critical role in facilitating interdisciplinary teaching. (4) TPACK mediates the relationship between technostress and CIT, demonstrating that technostress indirectly weakens CIT by reducing lecturers’ TPACK. (5) GM moderates the direct effects of technostress on both CIT and TPACK. The results indicate that its buffering effect is relatively stronger on the technostress-TPACK relationship than on the technostress-CIT relationship, highlighting GM’s particular role in supporting knowledge integration under stress.

Implication

The theoretical contribution of this study lies in its integration of technostress, TPACK, growth mindset (GM), and competency in interdisciplinary teaching (CIT) into a unified analytical framework, systematically revealing the mechanisms and interactive effects among these variables. By introducing both mediating and moderating mechanisms, this study expands and enriches the applicability of TPACK theory, the Iceberg Competency Model, and Implicit theories within interdisciplinary STEM higher education contexts. This framework provides a novel theoretical perspective for exploring diverse developmental pathways to enhance STEM lecturers’ CIT in technology-driven educational environments.

From a practical perspective, this study offers actionable references for developing STEM lecturer training policies and digital professional development initiatives. First, the findings indicate that technostress significantly undermines CIT, suggesting that universities should establish systematic technology adaptation support systems, such as embedded technical helpdesks, real-time online coaching, and hands-on digital tool training based on authentic teaching cases, to reduce technological uncertainty5,34. Second, the mediating role of TPACK highlights the need to strengthen contextualized TPACK capacity-building in lecturer training policies, including interdisciplinary teaching laboratories23, cross-domain instructional design projects16, and collaborative workshops26, to help STEM lecturers integrate multidisciplinary knowledge and translate it into practical instructional strategies. Third, the moderating role of GM emphasizes the importance of psychological empowerment interventions. Universities may incorporate growth mindset courses, reflective teaching salons48, and peer coaching mechanisms53 into faculty development frameworks to stimulate STEM lecturers’ positive attitudes and continuous adaptability to technological change. Finally, administrators could combine learning outcome certifications (such as micro-credentials)20, regular teaching showcases23, and peer review mechanisms21 to further motivate these lecturers’ sustained participation in CIT enhancement, thereby promoting a continuous improvement cycle in lecturer training policies.

Limitations of the study

Although this study offers new insights at both theoretical and practical levels, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the interpretation of causal relationships and the dynamic evolution of the variables. Second, the sample was drawn from STEM lecturers in a less-developed province in southwestern China, which may limit the external validity and generalizability of the findings. Third, the study relied solely on self-reported questionnaires without triangulating data sources, which may weaken methodological robustness. In addition, the study assumed linear relationships among the variables; however, nonlinear or threshold effects may exist in actual educational contexts.

Directions for future research

To address these limitations, future research could adopt a longitudinal design to explore the dynamic processes of GM, TPACK, and CIT under technostress over time. It is also recommended to extend the sample to include more regions and cultural backgrounds to verify the generalizability of the findings. Combining qualitative or mixed-methods approaches could further deepen the understanding of how STEM lecturers adapt when facing stress in interdisciplinary teaching and technology integration. In addition, future studies could design intervention experiments, such as GM development programs or contextualized TPACK training, to evaluate their practical effects and explore scalable lecturer training models, thereby better supporting the development of STEM education in digital contexts.
