Undetectable Mycobacterium leprae complex in European Pallas's squirrels
Authors: S Souguel, AMRA Ndiaye, G Grine, H Makoudi, J-F Maillard, S Bedjaoui, Y Laidoudi, B Davoust, M Drancourt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEurasian red squirrels were acknowledged as a reservoir for secular leprosy in the British Isles, after detection of Mycobacterium leprae in specimens dating back to the 11th century. In order to question the situation of Pallas's squirrels (Callosciurus erythraeus) recently introduced to the European continent, we investigated a series of 522 samples (241 liver, 241 spleen, and 40 faecal samples) collected from 281 deceased C. erythraeus specimens by means of controlled quantitative PCR targeting the M. leprae specific RLEP repetitive element, the M. lepromatosis RLPM repetitive element, and the most closely related Mycobacterium uberis ESX-1 locus gene. Successful amplification of the internal control certified the absence of PCR inhibition in any samples, which all tested negative for M. leprae complex DNA. Because tissues potentially involved in localized infection were not studied in the absence of clinical signs and the study was geographically restricted, these results cannot be extrapolated beyond dismissed systemic leprosy in tested animals. Nevertheless, the congruency of present data with equally negative ones reported in various continental squirrel populations suggests that, in Europe zoonotic leprosy is restricted to British Islands' red squirrel population.Keywords: Callosciurus erythraeus, Leprosy, Mycobacterium leprae, Mycobacterium lepromatosis, Mycobacterium uberis, Pallas's squirrel, Ticks, Ixodes ricinus

Introduction

Leprosy is a potentially chronic infectious disease primarily caused by Mycobacterium leprae (M. leprae), while Mycobacterium lepromatosis (M. lepromatosis) has been more recently described within the M. leprae complex [1], [2]. Once thought to be strictly human-associated pathogens, these organisms have since been detected in a range of animal hosts, highlighting a broader ecological and epidemiological context for leprosy. Armadillos (Dasypus novemcinctus) in the Americas have been implicated in zoonotic transmission to humans, expanding the spectrum of mammals infected by leprosy pathogens [3]. More recently, a most closely related species, Mycobacterium uberis, has also been described in livestock, where it causes nodular thelitis and tuberculoid scrotitis in continental Europe bovine and caprine individuals and herds [4], [5], [6], [7], [8].

Intriguingly, M. leprae was further detected in Eurasian red squirrels (Sciurus vulgaris) living in the British Isles and exhibiting clinical and pathological signs mimicking those seen in human cases [9], [10]. These findings, supported by the recovery of an almost complete M. leprae genome sequence from a red squirrel specimen dating back to the 11th century [11], suggest that red squirrel populations in the British Isles may have played a role in the persistence and transmission of leprosy [12].

In contrast, studies conducted across continental Europe have consistently failed to detect M. leprae complex mycobacteria in squirrel populations. For instance, a large-scale molecular survey conducted in France, Germany, United Kingdom and Switzerland did not detect M. leprae DNA in several species, including the European red squirrel and the introduced species Pallas's squirrel (Callosciurus erythraeus), Eastern grey squirrel (Sciurus carolinensis) and Siberian chipmunk (Tamias sibiricus), although potential PCR inhibitors were not thoroughly documented [13], [14]. These findings support the current view that red squirrels in the British Isles represent the only confirmed rodent reservoir of M. leprae in Europe.

Pallas's squirrel (C. erythraeus), native to Southeast Asia, is a recently introduced invasive species in Europe with viable populations in France and Italy and eradicated from Belgium and the Netherlands [15], [16]. It was first reported in the 1960s at Cap d'Antibes (Alpes-Maritimes, France) as a result of the animal trade [15]. Despite its expanding distribution and its presence in peri-urban environments near human populations, this species has only been investigated once for the presence of M. leprae [13]. Given its ecological interactions with other wildlife and humans, assessing whether Pallas's squirrels may harbor M. leprae and M. lepromatosis is important for evaluating potential zoonotic risks and refining our understanding of leprosy epidemiology in Europe [15].

This study therefore aimed to investigate the presence of M. leprae complex in Pallas's squirrels in France using sensitive molecular detection techniques while controlling for PCR inhibition, with a focus on tissues most likely to harbor the pathogen [3], [16], [17], [18], [19].

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

Specimens were obtained from 241 carcasses of invasive squirrels euthanized by lethal shooting, carried out by agents of the French Office for Biodiversity (Office Français de la Biodiversité, OFB) and licensed hunters, as part of annual population control operations conducted in the Bouches-du-Rhône and Alpes-Maritimes departments; in addition to 40 feces materials. These operations are authorized by prefectural orders and implemented in accordance with French and European regulations, including: Regulation (EU) No. 1143/2014 on the prevention and management of the introduction and spread of invasive alien species; Law No. 2016–1087 of 8 August 2016 on the restoration of biodiversity, nature and landscapes (notably Article 149 amending the Environmental Code); the French Environmental Code (Articles L.411–5 to L.411–10 and L.427–6); and the Ministerial Order of 14 February 2018 on invasive alien animal species in metropolitan France.

Study area and sample collection

This study involved the post-mortem examination of 522 biological samples collected from Pallas's squirrel (Callosciurus erythraeus) from the regions of Istres (n = 281) and Antibes (n = 241) in southern France (Fig. 1), comprising 241 liver samples, 241 spleen samples, and 40 faecal samples. All specimens were collected in between August 2021 and February 2025 and were subsequently transported to the Institut Hospitalo-Universitaire Méditerranée Infection (IHU-MI) in Marseille, France, for molecular analyses. We autopsied and removed bodies of invasive squirrels euthanized by lethal shooting carried out by agents of the French Office for Biodiversity (OFB) and authorized hunters as part of annual population control operations in Bouches-du-Rhône and Alpes-Maritimes, according to authorization as above.

Molecular detection of the M. leprae complex

Each sample was spiked with a qPCR synthetic internal control sequence TIIS (Témoin d'Inhibition de Synthèse Synthétique; Table 1) controlling for both extraction efficiency and PCR inhibition. Total DNA was then extracted from above-mentioned tissues using the EZ1 Advanced XL automated platform following the manufacturer's protocol (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Extracted DNA concentrations were measured using the Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with the dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) assays were used to independently detect each one of the three M. leprae complex species. The RLEP (repetitive element) sequence present in 37 copies within the M. leprae genome was targeted as a proxy for M. leprae for its validated sensitivity and specificity [20]. For M. lepromatosis, the detection targeted the Repetitive Element Lepromatosis (RLPM) [21]. For M. uberis, the qPCR assay targeted a 231-bp intergenic region located within the ESX-1 locus of the M. uberis genome. This region was selected after BLAST analysis confirming the absence of significant sequence homology with any other publicly available sequences, ensuring assay specificity (primers and probes are detailed in Table 1) [6]. Each qPCR reaction was performed in a 20-μL volume consisting of 5 μL of DNA template and 15 μL of Roche LightCycler® 480 Probes Master Mix, containing species-specific primers and hydrolysis probes.

The analytical sensitivity of the molecular assays used in this study has been previously evaluated. For M. leprae, the RLEP-based qPCR assay is highly sensitive due to the multicopy nature of the RLEP target, with a reported limit of detection (LOD) of approximately 30 bacilli per specimen (≈0.76 bacilli per reaction) [21]. For M. lepromatosis, the species-specific RLPM qPCR assay developed by Sharma et al. (2020) showed an LOD of approximately 122 bacilli per specimen (≈3.05 bacilli per reaction). In contrast, no formally established LOD has been reported to date for M. uberis-specific molecular assays, likely reflecting the rarity of this organism and the limited availability of well-characterized reference material for analytical validation.

To minimize contamination risk, sample preparation, DNA extraction, qPCR mix preparation, and post-amplification analyses were performed in physically separated areas using dedicated equipment and aerosol-resistant filter tips. Negative extraction controls and no-template controls were included in each run. Reactions were conducted under the following cycling conditions: a two-minute initial denaturation at 95 °C followed by 45 amplification cycles at 95 °C for 15 s and 58 °C for 60 s. Each run incorporated a negative extraction control (ultrapure HPLC-grade water) and a no-template control. Positive controls were included when available, using validated DNA samples specific for each mycobacterial species. Amplification data were analysed using the LightCycler® 480 software, and results were considered interpretable only when the internal control was successfully amplified and negative controls showed no amplification. Samples were considered positive when amplification curves crossed the threshold within the defined cycle range and with expected melting profiles, when applicable.

Statistical analysis

In the case of zero observed events, we estimated the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval using the exact binomial (Clopper–Pearson) method.

Results and discussion

This investigation of the M. leprae complex in 522 samples collected from 281 Pallas's squirrels in southern France failed to detect any trace of M. leprae, M. lepromatosis, or M. uberis in any of these animals. This negative result was validated by the successful amplification of the internal TIIS qPCR control in all tested samples, with cycle threshold (Ct) values ranging from 21 to 25, indicating efficient DNA extraction and absence of detectable PCR inhibition. Negative extraction and PCR controls also consistently behaved as expected, ruling out contamination or assay failure. Given that none of the 281 tested squirrels were positive for M. leprae, M. lepromatosis or M. uberis, the upper 95% confidence bound for detectable infection prevalence yielded a one-sided upper confidence limit of approximately 1.07% in the studied population.

These observations should not be interpreted as definite evidence for the absence of leprosy across all European squirrel populations, as the present survey was geographically restricted and specifically addressed systemic rather than localized infection in the absence of visible clinical signs. Here also, 40 faecal samples were found to be negative after M. leprae bacilli were identified in one human and five leprosy chimpanzees faecal sample [24], [25] and liver and spleen were selected as materials of choice after these profound organs have been shown to harbor detectable bacillary loads during naturally and experimentally disseminated leprosy in immunocompromized mice and armadillos [3], [17], [22], [26]; consistent with M. leprae being occasionally demonstrated in the blood of infected individuals, supporting the concept of transient bacteraemia [22], [23]. In squirrels also, liver and spleen have been considered informative tissues for surveillance and diagnostic purposes [27]. These organs however are not considered optimal for detecting early or localized infection, which preferentially involves skin, nasal tissues and peripheral nerves. Therefore, low-prevalence, focal, or low-burden infections cannot be completely excluded and future investigations should integrate these tissue types whenever clinically and logistically feasible. Furthermore, negative molecular findings should be interpreted as absence of detection under the analytical conditions tested rather than evidence of absolute absence of infection, especially considering possible heterogeneous tissue distribution and bacterial loads below the detection threshold [21].

Nevertheless, the negative results validated here concur with equivalent findings reported in studies investigating localized and systemic leprosy pathogens in Pallas's squirrel as well as Sciurus vulgaris and Tamias sibiricus populations in continental Europe [13], [14], and with negative findings reported in Eastern grey squirrel populations from the British Isles (Fig. 2). Indeed, at present, available pieces of evidence suggest that naturally documented squirrel-associated leprosy remains restricted to red squirrel populations in the British Isles [9], [10], [11], [12], [28], [29], [30]. Whether this pattern reflects true biological specificity or differences in ecological exposure, environmental persistence, historical host-pathogen interactions or surveillance intensity remains unresolved. The recovery of ancient M. leprae genomes from British red squirrels further suggests long-term ecological continuity rather than isolated spillover events [11]. Also, experimental evidence demonstrated that M. leprae may survive in tick-derived cells and remain transmissible under controlled conditions, supporting the biological plausibility of arthropod-associated exposure pathways [32]. However, no evidence currently supports ectoparasite-mediated transmission in European squirrels. Nevertheless, because ectoparasite communities differ between British Isles and continental squirrel populations (Table 2), these organisms remain relevant ecological indicators that may be integrated into future surveillance programs.

While Pallas'squirells may not be a source for zoonotic leprosy, nevertheless their contacts with environment make them interesting as sentinel animals for leprosy: Environmental investigations demonstrated the presence of M. leprae DNA in soil notably in Brownsea Island ecosystems where red squirrels were found infected, suggesting that environmental persistence of M. leprae may contribute to exposure dynamics independently of direct host-to-host transmission [31].

Current environmental changes make previous observations of importance: studies observations already suggested environmental associations between acid-fast bacilli tentatively attributed to the M. leprae complex in northern Europe pit ecosystems [38]. Under climate change and ecological disturbance scenarios, environmental conditions potentially influencing persistence, exposure opportunities and wildlife contacts may evolve over time. Therefore, surveillance should not rely exclusively on passive detection of clinical cases but integrate regular field monitoring of wildlife populations such as squirrel populations together with environmental sampling of soil, water and ecological matrices. Such integrated One Health approaches may help clarify whether the contrasted epidemiological situation observed between the British Isles and continental Europe reflects differences in reservoir distribution, environmental persistence or exposure dynamics.
