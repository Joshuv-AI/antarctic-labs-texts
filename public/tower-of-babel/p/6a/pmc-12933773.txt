Development and evaluation of a mobile health (mHealth) application for smoking cessation among adolescents in Indonesia: a mixed methods randomised controlled trial study protocol
Authors: Radian Ilmaskal, Yayi Suryo Prabandari, Vicka Oktaria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAdolescent smoking poses a critical public health challenge in Indonesia, where approximately 7.4% of adolescents are current smokers. This issue is compounded by the tobacco industry’s aggressive marketing strategies, particularly targeting youth. Mobile health (mHealth) interventions offer promising alternatives for smoking cessation because of their accessibility and adaptability. However, while mHealth applications have shown potential in promoting smoking cessation, their efficacy remains inconsistent, particularly among adolescent populations in low- and middle-income countries. This study aims to develop an innovative mHealth intervention model designed explicitly for adolescent users.Methods and analysisThis study used a mixed-methods, sequential, exploratory approach, including a randomised controlled trial. The research will be conducted in three phases: (1) a qualitative study to inform the app’s design; (2) a development phase using the Rapid Application Development model; and (3) a single-blind, two-arm randomised controlled trial to evaluate the app’s efficacy. Participants will be randomised into either the mHealth intervention group or a paper-based control group using block randomisation to ensure balanced allocation and minimise bias. The protocol is compliant with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines for transparency and reporting. The study population will consist of adolescent smokers aged 13–15 years who reside in Padang City. A total of 110 participants will be recruited, with 55 adolescents in the intervention group and 55 in the control group. The primary outcome is smoking abstinence at 1-, 3- and 6-month follow-ups, which will be assessed through a self-report questionnaire. Secondary outcomes include changes in self-efficacy, motivation, social support, nicotine dependence and user engagement with the intervention.Ethics and disseminationThe study complies with protocols, the Helsinki Declaration, the principles of Good Health Research Practice and regulatory requirements. The protocol was approved by the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Medicine, Public Health and Nursing, Gadjah Mada University (Ref No: KE-FK-0864-EC-2025). Informed consent will be obtained from both adolescents and their parents/guardians, ensuring confidentiality and voluntary participation. The results of this study will be published in peer-reviewed journals.Trial registration numberNCT07198828.

Introduction

Adolescent smoking represents a critical public health challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Indonesia.13 Globally, approximately 6.5% of adolescents aged 13–15 years use tobacco, with Southeast Asia contributing substantially to this burden.246 Indonesia’s situation is particularly concerning: smoking prevalence among adolescents escalated from 7.2% in 2013 to 9.1% in 2018,7 8 with the 2019 Global Youth Tobacco Survey reporting rates as high as 19.2%.9 Although recent estimates suggest a decline to 7.4% in 2023,10 adolescent smoking remains a persistent public health priority. Indonesia’s status compounds this challenge as the only Asian nation yet to ratify the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control.11

The health and social consequences of adolescent smoking are well-documented and severe. Early tobacco initiation increases risks of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases and substantially raises the likelihood of continued smoking into adulthood.12 13 Moreover, adolescent smoking is associated with mental health complications, including depression, anxiety and suicidal ideation.1418 Tobacco-related mortality claims over 8 million lives annually worldwide,19 20 with an estimated 225 700 deaths in Indonesia alone attributable to smoking.21 22

Despite implementing various tobacco control measures, including increased tobacco taxation and free cessation consultation services through Quitline.ina and smoke-free zone regulations—Indonesia has struggled to achieve sustained reductions in adolescent smoking rates.2329 This persistent challenge necessitates innovative, accessible interventions tailored to adolescent populations.

Mobile health (mHealth) applications present a promising avenue for smoking cessation interventions among adolescents.3033 With Indonesia ranking as the fourth-largest smartphone market globally34 35 and widespread smartphone ownership among adolescents, mHealth platforms offer unprecedented reach and engagement potential. However, evidence regarding mHealth effectiveness for smoking cessation yields inconsistent results, particularly in LMIC contexts where accessibility, cultural relevance and sustained engagement may differ from high-income settings.323638

Current literature reveals several critical gaps. First, while mHealth interventions demonstrate short-term efficacy in promoting smoking cessation, their long-term effectiveness—especially among adolescent populations—remains inadequately evaluated.31 39 Second, existing interventions often fail to adequately address adolescent-specific developmental needs, including peer influence dynamics, motivational factors and preferred communication styles. Third, there is a notable absence of mHealth applications specifically designed for Indonesian adolescents that integrate behavioural change theories, social support mechanisms and interactive features tailored to local contexts.

This study addresses these gaps by developing and evaluating an innovative, theory-driven mHealth intervention designed explicitly for Indonesian adolescent smokers. The intervention integrates two complementary theoretical frameworks: the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT2)40 and the Transtheoretical Model (TTM).41 UTAUT2 guides the development of an engaging, user-friendly application that accounts for peer influence, ease of use and intrinsic motivation—factors critical for adolescent technology adoption. Specifically, UTAUT2 constructs inform key application features, including social support forums and peer-sharing functions (social influence), intuitive navigation and simple interface design (effort expectancy), gamification elements and achievement rewards (hedonic motivation) and personalised motivational messages (habit and behavioural intention). TTM provides a framework for delivering stage-matched content and support, adapting intervention components to each adolescent’s position in the smoking cessation process—from precontemplation through maintenance stages. For example, adolescents in the pre-contemplation stage receive consciousness-raising content about smoking harms, while those in the preparation stage access action-oriented tools such as quit date planners and craving management strategies. This dual-theory approach ensures both technological acceptability and behavioural effectiveness, ultimately promoting sustained behaviour change and improved cessation outcomes among Indonesian adolescent smokers.

Study goal and hypothesis

Study goal

This study has three primary objectives. First, to explore adolescent smokers’ perspectives, needs and preferences regarding mHealth-based smoking cessation interventions through qualitative inquiry. Second, to develop a theory-driven mHealth application integrating behavioural change principles specifically tailored for Indonesian adolescent smokers. Third, to evaluate the effectiveness of the developed mHealth intervention compared with conventional paper-based materials in promoting smoking cessation among adolescents aged 13–15 years in Padang City, Indonesia.

Primary hypothesis

We hypothesise that adolescents receiving the mHealth intervention will demonstrate significantly higher smoking abstinence rates at 1-, 3- and 6-month follow-ups compared with those receiving paper-based cessation materials.

These hypotheses are grounded in the theoretical premise that technology-mediated, stage-matched interventions that provide continuous support and interactive features will yield superior cessation outcomes compared with static, paper-based approaches among digitally native adolescent populations.

Methods and analysis

This study will employ a sequential exploratory mixed-methods design conducted in three phases.4749 Phase 1 will involve a qualitative inquiry to inform application design. Phase 2 will encompass the development of an mHealth application using the Rapid Application Development (RAD) model. Phase 3 will comprise a single-blind, two-arm randomised controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate the effectiveness of the intervention. This mixed-methods approach will enable a comprehensive understanding of the research context, resulting in evidence-based, contextually appropriate intervention development and rigorous evaluation of effectiveness.

Phase 1: qualitative study

Study design

Phase 1 will employ a qualitative phenomenological approach to explore adolescent smokers’ lived experiences, perceptions and needs regarding smoking cessation interventions. This phase will aim to identify essential mHealth application features, content preferences, potential barriers to engagement and facilitators of sustained use. The study design will adhere to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines.50

Sampling strategy

Purposive sampling will be used to recruit information-rich participants who represent diverse perspectives. Sample size will be determined by data saturation, anticipated at approximately 20–30 participants across all informant categories. Recruitment will continue until no new themes emerge from the data.

Data collection methods

Data will be collected through (a) in-depth interviews with adolescent smokers, parents, teachers and healthcare workers and (b) focus group discussions (FGDs) with adolescent smokers and peer informants. All interviews and FGDs will be guided by semi-structured interview protocols developed based on literature review and theoretical frameworks (UTAUT2 and TTM). Sessions will be audio-recorded with participant consent, conducted in Indonesian and facilitated by trained researchers. Each session will last approximately 60–90 min.

In-depth interviews will enable exploration of sensitive, personal experiences with smoking initiation, addiction and cessation attempts that adolescents may be reluctant to share in group settings due to social desirability bias or peer judgement. FGDs will complement individual interviews by capturing peer interaction dynamics, social norms surrounding smoking and group perspectives on intervention features that resonate with adolescent culture. However, participants will not participate in both data collection methods; each adolescent will be assigned to either an individual interview or a focus group based on their comfort level and the specific information needs.

Confidentiality and sensitive information handling

Given the sensitive nature of adolescent smoking behaviours, we recognise that some participants may be smoking without parental knowledge. To address this ethical consideration while maintaining research integrity, we have implemented the following procedures: (1) Adolescent participants will be informed during the consent process that their smoking status will remain confidential and will not be disclosed to parents, teachers or other parties without their explicit permission. (2) Parental consent will be sought for adolescent participation in research about ‘health behaviors and smoking cessation support’, without requiring parents to confirm their child’s smoking status. (3) Interviews with parents will focus on their general perceptions of adolescent smoking and cessation support rather than their specific child’s behaviours. (4) When parents are recruited as key informants, they will be selected based on their acknowledgement of having an adolescent child who smokes, ensuring informed participation. (5) All data will be stored with unique identification codes and names will not appear on any research materials accessible to multiple research team members.

Data analysis

Audio recordings will be transcribed verbatim in Indonesian and analysed using thematic analysis following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework. The analysis process will include (a) data familiarisation through repeated reading of transcripts, (b) initial code generation, (c) theme identification and development, (d) theme review and refinement, (e) theme definition and naming and (f) report production.

NVivo software (V.12 or later) will facilitate coding and thematic analysis. To ensure rigour, two researchers will independently code a subset of transcripts, with discrepancies resolved through discussion and consensus.

Trustworthiness and rigour

Study trustworthiness will be established through (a) credibility: member checking, peer debriefing and prolonged engagement; (b) transferability: thick description of context, participants and findings; (c) dependability: audit trail of analytical decisions and methodological documentation; and (d) confirmability: reflexivity practices and triangulation across multiple informant types.

Phase 2: application development

Development approach

The mHealth application to be developed is built on the results of the qualitative data analysis in Phase 1. The mHealth development flow will be carried out using the RAD model, a software development process that emphasises rapid development.51 RAD was chosen for its ability to deliver high-quality software quickly and efficiently. Its iterative, user-centred and cost-effective nature makes it an attractive choice for projects that require rapid development and adaptability. This model is instrumental in dynamic environments, where speed and user satisfaction are paramount.52 Figure 1 illustrates the RAD development cycle.

Rapid Application Development (RAD) model. Iterative RAD cycle for mHealth application development comprising four stages: (1) Requirements Planning—defining user needs from Phase 1 findings; (2) User Design—creating interface mockups with continuous user feedback; (3) Construction—coding and integrating intervention components; and (4) Cutover—testing, refinement and deployment. The circular arrangement emphasises rapid prototyping and continuous refinement based on adolescent feedback.

This phase will include expert validation of the application design and a pilot testing phase to assess usability, content relevance and potential barriers to engagement.

Phase 3: randomised controlled trial

Study design

Phase 3 will comprise a single-blind, two-arm, parallel-group RCT comparing the mHealth intervention to paper-based cessation materials. The protocol adheres to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines for transparent protocol reporting.53

Study setting and population

The trial will be conducted in Padang City, Indonesia, recruiting adolescent smokers from junior high schools and community settings. Padang City, the capital of West Sumatra Province, will provide an appropriate urban setting with adequate infrastructure for the delivery of mHealth interventions and diverse socioeconomic representation. The city’s established network of schools and community health centres will facilitate participant recruitment and follow-up assessments.

Eligibility criteria

Adolescents will be eligible for inclusion if they are aged 13 to 15 years, report smoking at least one cigarette in the past 7 days, own a personal smartphone with internet access and reside in Padang City throughout the study period. Participants must be willing to commit to study participation for the 6-month duration, with the understanding that withdrawal is permitted at any time without penalty or loss of benefits, express a desire to quit smoking and be able to provide informed assent with accompanying parental or guardian consent. Adolescents will be excluded if they are currently participating in other smoking cessation programmes or using cessation applications, plan to relocate outside Padang City during the study period, have severe psychiatric or medical conditions requiring intensive treatment or are unable to use smartphone applications due to literacy or technical limitations.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated using OpenEpi V.3 (www.openepi.com),54 an open-source epidemiological calculator, with a two-tailed significance level of 0.05 and statistical power of 80%. Based on a recent systematic review and meta-analysis of mobile phone-based smoking cessation interventions among young people by Zhou et al,31 which reported a risk ratio of 1.64 (95% CI 1.23 to 2.18) for continuous abstinence at 3-month follow-up for SMS text messaging interventions compared with inactive controls, a minimum of 92 participants will be required. To account for an expected 20% dropout rate, the total sample size will be 110 participants, with 55 adolescents per group.

Recruitment strategy

Participants will be recruited through multiple channels to ensure diverse representation and adequate enrolment. School-based recruitment will be conducted through teacher announcements and information sessions during school assemblies. Flyers will be distributed in adolescent-frequented locations, including youth centres, sports facilities and community gathering spaces. Social media advertisements will be placed on platforms popular among Indonesian adolescents to reach potential participants in their preferred digital environments. Additionally, community health centres will be engaged to refer eligible adolescents who present for health services. All promotional materials will clearly state the basic eligibility criteria and provide the research team’s contact information for interested participants.

Adolescents who respond to advertisements or express interest will undergo initial pre-screening via telephone or an online form to assess preliminary eligibility. Candidates who pass the initial screening will be invited to undergo a comprehensive in-person screening and will be provided with detailed research information sheets and written informed consent forms. Comprehensive participant consent forms and parental/guardian consent forms are provided in online supplemental file 1.

Randomisation and allocation concealment

Participants meeting all eligibility criteria will be randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group using block randomisation with a fixed block size of four. This method will ensure balanced allocation throughout the recruitment period, minimising the risk of selection bias and temporal confounding. The randomisation sequence will be computer-generated using the online randomiser tool (randomizer.org) by an independent statistician who will have no direct contact with participants or involvement in recruitment procedures. To maintain allocation concealment, the randomisation sequence will be secured in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. These envelopes will be retained by an independent third party not involved in participant recruitment or outcome assessment. Only after a participant has completed the consent process and baseline assessment will the envelope be opened to reveal group assignment, ensuring that allocation decisions cannot influence recruitment or baseline measurement procedures.

Blinding

Given the visibility of mHealth interventions relative to paper-based materials, complete participant blinding will not be feasible in this trial. However, rigorous blinding procedures will be implemented for outcome assessors and data analysts to minimise detection and analysis bias. Data collectors conducting follow-up assessments will remain blind to participants’ group assignments throughout the study. To achieve this, participants will be instructed not to disclose their intervention type during assessment visits, and assessments will be conducted in neutral settings without intervention materials present. Statistical analysts will receive fully coded datasets with group assignments masked by neutral labels until all primary and secondary analyses are completed. Recruitment staff will not have access to the randomisation sequence, preventing any conscious or unconscious influence on participant selection. This single-blind design will minimise detection and analysis bias while acknowledging the inherent limitations of behavioural intervention trials where participant awareness of intervention type is unavoidable.

Intervention group: mHealth application

Participants randomised to the intervention group will receive comprehensive access to the smoking cessation mobile application. On randomisation, participants will receive installation guidance and personalised login credentials for the application. While the application is designed to be intuitive and user-friendly without requiring formal training, each participant will be offered an optional 30 min orientation session, delivered either individually or in small groups. This brief session will not provide technical training on basic app navigation—which adolescents should be able to accomplish independently—but instead will: (1) explain the study protocol and assessment schedule, (2) highlight key evidence-based features that research suggests improve cessation outcomes (eg, craving management tools, social support forums), (3) demonstrate how to personalise notification settings to optimise engagement without being intrusive and (4) establish rapport with research staff and provide contact information for technical support.

The application will include both mandatory and optional components to balance intervention fidelity with user autonomy. Core mandatory features will consist of educational modules on the health effects of smoking and the benefits of smoking cessation, as well as personalised motivational content delivered via push notifications and in-app messages. Optional features that participants may engage with according to their preferences include peer support forums for sharing experiences and offering encouragement, progress-tracking dashboards visualising cessation milestones, craving management tools offering real-time coping strategies and gamification elements such as achievements, rewards and challenges designed to enhance engagement and motivation.

The active intervention period will span 12 weeks, during which participants will be encouraged to engage regularly with the application. However, participants will retain access to the application through the 6-month follow-up period to support long-term cessation maintenance. Technical support will be available via a dedicated hotline to address any troubleshooting needs or technical difficulties that may arise during application use. Intervention adherence will be continuously monitored through automated usage logs embedded in the application, capturing detailed metrics such as login frequency, session duration, specific feature engagement patterns and educational content completion rates. These usage data will provide valuable insights into engagement patterns and will inform secondary analyses examining the relationship between app usage intensity and cessation outcomes.

Control group: paper-based materials

Participants randomised to the control group will receive comprehensive paper-based smoking cessation materials designed to provide content equivalent to the mHealth application while maintaining a purely non-digital intervention format. The primary intervention component will be a detailed cessation booklet containing motivational content emphasising the benefits of quitting smoking, educational information on the health effects of tobacco and the physiological process of nicotine addiction, practical guidance for identifying personal smoking triggers and developing individualised coping strategies, evidence-based relapse prevention techniques and inspirational success stories from former adolescent smokers to provide peer modelling and encouragement.

To ensure methodological consistency and minimise potential bias from prior exposure to assessment instruments, data collection for both intervention and control groups will be conducted by trained research assistants at scheduled follow-up time points using standardised procedures. The control group booklet will contain only intervention content, without embedded data collection forms, ensuring that the assessment methodology remains identical across study arms and that participants cannot complete assessments before designated time points. This approach preserves the integrity of longitudinal outcome measurement while maintaining equivalent participant burden across groups. The intervention period will span 12 weeks, matching the active intervention duration for the mHealth group, and participants will be instructed to retain their materials through the 6-month follow-up period for continued reference and support. This control-group design provides a rigorous comparison by ensuring that both groups receive evidence-based cessation content, thereby isolating the incremental effects of mobile technology delivery, interactive features, real-time support mechanisms and personalised notifications inherent to the mHealth platform.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome will be smoking abstinence, operationalised as self-reported 7-day point prevalence abstinence assessed at 1, 3 and 6 months post-baseline. Abstinence will be defined as no cigarette smoking in the 7 days preceding each assessment point. This dichotomous outcome will provide a precise, clinically meaningful measure of short-term and medium-term cessation success. While biochemical verification through cotinine testing or exhaled carbon monoxide measurement would strengthen the validity of the outcomes, logistical and budgetary constraints preclude their use in this trial, which represents an acknowledged limitation that will be addressed in the discussion section.

Secondary outcomes will provide a comprehensive assessment of behavioural, psychological and engagement factors hypothesised to mediate or moderate intervention effects. Self-efficacy for smoking cessation will be measured using the 12-item Smoking SEQ-12, which assesses participants’ confidence in their ability to resist smoking across diverse high-risk situations, including social pressure, negative emotional states and habitual routines. Motivation to quit smoking will be assessed using the Richmond test, a brief 4-item scale that evaluates both motivation strength and perceived readiness to attempt cessation. Perceived social support for cessation efforts will be measured using the MSPSS. This 12-item instrument separately assesses perceived support from three key sources: family members, friends and significant others. Nicotine dependence severity will be evaluated using the FTND, a widely validated 6-item scale measuring addiction intensity through assessment of smoking patterns, morning smoking urgency and difficulty refraining in restricted situations.

User engagement will be assessed through multiple complementary approaches. For the intervention group, objective engagement metrics will be captured through automated application usage logs recording login frequency, session duration, specific feature utilisation patterns and content completion rates. For both groups, subjective engagement and satisfaction will be evaluated using the MAUQ for the intervention group, with parallel items adapted for booklet usability assessment in the control group. Additionally, change in smoking frequency will be tracked as a continuous secondary outcome, calculated as the difference in self-reported cigarettes smoked per day from baseline to each follow-up assessment. All measurement instruments will be administered in validated Indonesian-language versions where available. Instruments not previously validated in Indonesian will undergo rigorous forward-backward translation procedures conducted by bilingual health professionals, followed by cognitive interviewing with adolescent representatives to ensure comprehension and cultural appropriateness.

Data collection procedures

Data will be collected at five predetermined time points throughout the study period. At baseline (T0), immediately following consent and before randomisation, comprehensive sociodemographic characteristics will be documented, including age, gender, educational level, family socioeconomic status, parental smoking status and household composition. A detailed smoking history will be recorded, encompassing age of initiation, years of smoking, current smoking frequency, previous quit attempts and perceived triggers. All primary and secondary outcome measures will be administered at baseline to establish pre-intervention levels and enable within-subject change analyses.

Follow-up assessments will be conducted at 1 month (T1), 2 months (T2), 3 months (T3) and 6 months (T4) post-baseline. The 1-month assessment will capture all primary and secondary outcomes along with intervention satisfaction ratings and participant-reported barriers encountered during the initial intervention period. The 2-month evaluation will focus on primary and secondary outcome measurement to track intermediate progress. The 3-month assessment, coinciding with the conclusion of the active intervention period, will again include all outcome measures, a comprehensive evaluation of intervention satisfaction and qualitative feedback on intervention utility and areas for improvement. The final 6-month follow-up will replicate the complete baseline assessment battery to enable comprehensive evaluation of sustained intervention effects, long-term abstinence maintenance and relapse patterns among those who initially achieved cessation.

Follow-up assessments will be conducted using a hierarchical approach, prioritising data quality while accommodating participant preferences and logistical constraints. The preferred method will be in-person interviews conducted by trained research assistants at convenient locations, including community health centres, schools or other mutually agreed neutral settings. For participants unable to attend in-person appointments due to scheduling conflicts or transportation barriers, telephone interviews will be offered as an alternative, following standardised scripts to maintain assessment consistency. As a final option for participants unreachable through in-person or telephone methods, self-administered online questionnaires will be provided, with subsequent data collector verification through brief follow-up contact to confirm response authenticity and address any ambiguities. This flexible multi-modal approach will maximise follow-up completion rates while maintaining data integrity.

Retention strategies

Multiple evidence-based retention strategies will be implemented to minimise participant attrition throughout the 6-month study period. Both intervention and control group participants will receive monthly reminder messages via SMS or WhatsApp, scheduled 1 week before each assessment appointment, to reinforce the importance of study participation and confirm availability. Small incentives will be provided on completion of each follow-up assessment, consisting of mobile phone credit vouchers or shopping vouchers valued at approximately 50 000 Indonesian Rupiah (approximately US$3–4), an amount sufficient to acknowledge participation without creating coercive influence. Follow-up appointments will be scheduled with maximum flexibility, offering evening and weekend time slots and multiple convenient locations to accommodate adolescents’ school schedules and family obligations.

Multiple contact methods will be obtained at baseline, including personal mobile phone numbers, alternative family contact numbers, email addresses and social media handles where appropriate, ensuring multiple avenues for reaching participants if primary contact methods fail. Throughout the consent process and at each subsequent contact, research staff will emphasise the study’s importance in advancing adolescent smoking cessation interventions. It will highlight each participant’s valuable contribution to improving future health programmes for Indonesian youth. Participants will be informed that their continued participation, even if they have resumed smoking, provides crucial data for understanding real-world intervention effectiveness and relapse patterns, potentially reducing shame-based dropout among those who experience cessation difficulties.

Data management

All collected data will be managed in accordance with stringent confidentiality and security protocols. On enrolment, each participant will be assigned a unique identification number that will be used on all study documents and electronic records, with identifying information stored separately in locked files accessible only to designated research staff. Data will be entered into a secure, password-protected electronic database with role-based access restrictions ensuring that only authorised personnel can view or modify records. The database server will be housed within the university’s secure research computing environment, with daily automated backups stored in geographically separate locations to prevent data loss.

Consent forms containing participant names and signatures will be stored separately from study data in locked file cabinets within a secure research office, with access restricted to the principal investigator and designated research coordinators. All electronic files will be encrypted using industry-standard protocols, and any data transfers will occur only through secure channels. Paper-based data collection forms will be transported in sealed envelopes and stored in locked cabinets immediately on return from field sites. Data quality will be ensured through multiple verification procedures, including double-entry verification of a random 10% sample of all entered data, with discrepancies identified and resolved by reference to source documents. Regular data audits will be conducted monthly throughout the data collection period to identify and correct entry errors, missing data patterns and inconsistencies requiring clarification with participants or field staff.

All study data will be retained for a minimum of 5 years following publication of primary study results, in accordance with institutional research data retention policies and Indonesian research ethics requirements. At the conclusion of the retention period, paper documents will be securely shredded, and electronic files will be permanently deleted using data destruction software that prevents file recovery. Throughout the study period and retention period, data access will be logged and monitored to maintain an audit trail of all individuals accessing participant information.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be conducted according to the intention-to-treat principle, in which all randomised participants will be analysed according to their assigned group, regardless of intervention adherence, protocol deviations or withdrawal from the intervention. This approach preserves the benefits of randomisation and provides conservative estimates of intervention effectiveness under real-world conditions. A supplementary per-protocol analysis will be conducted as a sensitivity analysis, including only participants who completed the full intervention and attended all scheduled follow-up assessments, to examine intervention efficacy under ideal circumstances.

Descriptive statistics will be calculated to characterise the study sample and assess whether randomisation successfully balanced baseline characteristics across groups. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages will be presented; for continuous variables, means with SD for normally distributed variables or medians with IQRs for skewed distributions will be used. Between-group comparisons of baseline characteristics will be conducted to assess randomisation balance, though statistical significance testing of baseline differences will be interpreted cautiously, given that any differences are attributable to chance rather than confounding.

The primary outcome of smoking abstinence at 1, 3 and 6 months will be analysed using multiple complementary approaches to provide a comprehensive evaluation of intervention effects. χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests (when cell counts are small) will compare abstinence rates between groups at each time point. Logistic regression models will be fitted to estimate ORs for abstinence in the intervention group relative to the control group, with models adjusted for baseline covariates, including age, baseline smoking frequency and baseline nicotine dependence score, to improve precision and account for potential imbalance. To account for the repeated-measures structure of the data and the within-subject correlation across time points, generalised estimating equations 55 with an exchangeable correlation structure will be employed, allowing simultaneous modelling of abstinence across all time points while properly accounting for the non-independence of repeated observations from the same individuals.

Survival analysis techniques will be used to examine the time to smoking relapse among participants who initially achieve abstinence. Kaplan-Meier survival curves will be constructed for each group, with visual comparison of relapse-free survival distributions over the 6-month follow-up period. The log-rank test will assess whether survival curves differ significantly between groups. Cox proportional hazards regression models will estimate HRs for relapse, quantifying the relative risk of relapse in the intervention group compared with the control group while adjusting for relevant covariates. The proportional hazards assumption will be tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and time-varying coefficient models will be fitted if violations are detected.

Secondary outcomes measured on continuous scales, including self-efficacy, motivation to quit, social support and nicotine dependence scores, will be analysed using repeated measures approaches that appropriately handle correlated longitudinal data. Between-group comparisons at specific time points will be conducted using independent-samples t-tests for normally distributed outcomes or Mann-Whitney U tests for non-normally distributed outcomes. Mixed-effects linear models56 (also known as linear mixed models or hierarchical linear models) will be fitted to assess trajectories of change over time, allowing estimation of both between-group differences in overall levels and differences in rates of change over time through group-by-time interaction terms. These models will include random intercepts to account for baseline heterogeneity across participants and will be fitted using restricted maximum likelihood estimation. Effect sizes will be calculated using Cohen’s d for all significant between-group differences to enable interpretation of clinical significance beyond statistical significance.

Missing data will be addressed using multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption. Specifically, multiple imputation by chained equations will be used to generate 20 imputed datasets,57 with missing values imputed based on observed baseline characteristics, available follow-up data and auxiliary variables predictive of missingness and outcome values. Imputation models will be carefully specified to include all variables related to the outcome and missingness mechanism. Analyses will be performed separately on each imputed dataset, with results combined using Rubin’s rules to obtain pooled parameter estimates and appropriately adjusted standard errors that reflect both within-imputation and between-imputation variability. Sensitivity analyses will explore alternative missing-data assumptions, including complete-case analysis and pattern-mixture models that allow for differential outcome distributions between observed and missing cases, to assess the robustness of the primary findings to these assumptions.

Exploratory subgroup analyses will examine whether intervention effects vary across clinically relevant participant characteristics. Separate analyses will be conducted within subgroups defined by baseline nicotine dependence level (low dependence defined as FTND score 0–3 vs moderate-to-high dependence defined as FTND score 4 or higher), gender (male vs female), baseline smartphone usage patterns (low vs high daily usage) and baseline motivation to quit (low vs high Richmond test scores). Subgroup analyses will be conducted by fitting statistical models including interaction terms between group assignment and the subgroup variable, with significant interactions indicating differential intervention effects across subgroup levels. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses and increased risk of Type I error from multiple comparisons, subgroup findings will be interpreted cautiously and primarily used to generate hypotheses for future research rather than draw definitive conclusions.

All statistical analyses will be performed using Stata V.17 or later or R statistical software V.4.0 or later. Statistical significance will be defined using a two-tailed alpha level of 0.05. For multiple comparisons involving secondary outcomes, no formal adjustment will be made given their exploratory nature, though the potential for inflated Type I error will be acknowledged in result interpretation. All analysis scripts will be documented and retained to ensure reproducibility and transparency.

Study timeline

The study is planned to commence on 1 March 2026, with primary completion anticipated by 31 May 2026, and final study completion by 31 August 2026. At the time of manuscript submission, recruitment had not yet begun; the study was in the pre-recruitment phase. Participant enrolment for Phase 3 (RCT) is scheduled to start in March 2026, following completion of Phase 1 (qualitative study) and Phase 2 (application development). The overall study will be conducted over 24 months with clearly defined phases. Months 1 through 4 will be dedicated to Phase 1 qualitative data collection and analysis, during which in-depth interviews and FGDs will be conducted with all stakeholder groups, and thematic analysis will be completed to inform application design. Months 5 through 12 will encompass Phase 2 application development activities, including requirements planning, user interface design, application construction, expert validation, usability testing with adolescent representatives and iterative refinement based on feedback. Months 13 through 18 will focus on Phase 3 participant recruitment and baseline data collection, with active outreach through schools and community settings to enrol the target sample of 110 adolescents. To facilitate this recruitment timeline, we have established formal partnerships with 15 junior high schools and 8 community health centres in Padang City before study commencement, with school administrators and healthcare providers trained to identify and refer eligible adolescents. We will monitor weekly recruitment rates and implement adaptive strategies if enrolment falls below expected targets of approximately 18–20 participants per month. If recruitment targets are not met within the initial 6-month period, we will implement enhanced strategies including: (1) extending recruitment to additional schools and community settings, (2) increasing social media advertisement frequency and reach, (3) offering referral incentives where adolescent participants can nominate eligible peers and (4) conducting additional information sessions at youth centres and sports facilities. Months 18 through 24 will comprise the active intervention delivery period and initial follow-up assessments at 1, 2 and 3 months post-baseline, with continuous monitoring of intervention fidelity and participant retention. Finally, months 25 through 26 will include the final 6-month follow-up assessment, comprehensive statistical analysis of all outcomes, interpretation of findings in light of existing literature and preparation of manuscripts for peer-reviewed publication. Ethics and dissemination

Protocol amendment

Following initial ethics approval on 28 May 2025, a protocol amendment was submitted and approved on 24 December 2025 to implement methodological improvements to data collection procedures. The changes between the original and amended protocols are summarised below.

Original data collection protocol (May 2025): The original protocol specified that the intervention group would use the mHealth application with mandatory education and motivation features, with monthly monitoring to track progress and minimise dropout. At the end of each month, participants would be asked to complete questionnaires regarding smoking status, satisfaction with the application (intervention group) and challenges faced in smoking cessation efforts through embedded forms within the application. For the control group, participants would receive a smoking cessation module containing content equivalent to the application, with baseline data and pre-test forms on the first page and post-test forms on the final page, to be completed at week 12. Participant recruitment was initially scheduled to begin in January 2026.

Amended data collection protocol (December 2025): The amended protocol standardises data collection procedures across both study arms to ensure methodological consistency and minimise potential bias. Data collection for both the intervention and control groups will now be conducted by trained research assistants at scheduled follow-up time points using standardised procedures, rather than via embedded forms. The control group booklet will contain only intervention content, without embedded data collection forms, ensuring that the assessment methodology remains identical across study arms and that participants cannot complete assessments before designated time points. This approach preserves the integrity of longitudinal outcome measurement while maintaining equivalent participant burden across groups. Additionally, participant recruitment has been rescheduled to begin in March 2026 to allow adequate time for ethics amendment processing, application refinement and research team training on standardised data collection procedures.

Ethics approval

This study will be conducted in full compliance with the protocol, the Declaration of Helsinki, the principles of Good Health Research Practice and all applicable Indonesian regulatory requirements for research involving human subjects, particularly adolescent populations. The complete study protocol, including all three phases (qualitative inquiry, application development and RCT), was reviewed and approved by the Medical and Health Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Public Health and Nursing, Universitas Gadjah Mada in collaboration with Dr. Sardjito General Hospital on 28 May 2025 (Reference Number: KE-FK-0864-EC-2025). This ethics committee is registered with the Indonesian National Ethics Committee and operates in accordance with international standards for research ethics review. An ethics amendment (Reference Number: KE-FK-0864-EC-2025) was approved on 24 December 2025 to incorporate modifications to the data collection procedures. The amendment addressed three key revisions: (1) clarification of control group intervention description from ‘paper-based smoking cessation module’ to ‘paper-based cessation materials’ for terminological consistency; (2) standardisation of data collection procedures for the intervention group, specifying that data collection will be conducted by trained research assistants at scheduled follow-up time points using standardised procedures, rather than through embedded questionnaires within the application; and (3) comprehensive revision of control group procedures to ensure methodological consistency, including removal of embedded data collection forms from the booklet and specification that data collection for both intervention and control groups will be conducted by trained research assistants at scheduled follow-up time points using standardised procedures. These modifications ensure identical assessment methodology across study arms and prevent participants from completing assessments before designated time points, thereby preserving the integrity of longitudinal outcome measurement. The study has also been prospectively registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (Registration Number: NCT07198828) before participant enrolment, ensuring transparency and accountability in trial conduct and reporting. Any substantial modifications to the study protocol, including changes to eligibility criteria, outcome measures, intervention components, sample size or data collection procedures, will be submitted to the ethics committee for review and approval before implementation. All approved amendments will be documented in the trial registry and reported in subsequent publications. Annual progress reports will be submitted to the ethics committee throughout the study duration, detailing recruitment progress, participant retention, adverse events and any protocol deviations or unanticipated problems. In accordance with BMJ Open’s data sharing policy, de-identified individual participant data underlying the results reported in this article will be made available 3 months after article publication and for 5 years thereafter.
