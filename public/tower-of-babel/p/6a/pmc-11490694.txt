The effect of cold chain disruption on the microbiological profile of chilled chicken meat
Authors: Lenka Necidová, Alena Zouharová, Danka Haruštiaková, Šárka Bursová, Klára Bartáková, Jozef Golian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the influence of inadequate transport conditions on the microbiological quality of chilled chicken meat packaged in plain and modified atmosphere packaging (MAP). The experiments simulated the temperature increase during sample transport to 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, and 25°C with exposure times of 1, 2, 3, and 4 h. Aerobic plate count (APC), psychrotrophic microorganisms count (PMC), β-D-glucuronidase-positive Escherichia coli, and Salmonella spp. were evaluated immediately after the exposure to the elevated temperature (0 h), 3 h, and 24 h after the return to the temperature of ≤4°C. The upper acceptable limits for APC and PMC were set for each combination of investigated chicken meat and packaging type, taking also the initial bacterial condition into account. Chilled chicken breast samples in plain packaging exceeded the APC limits in 16 cases and PMC limits in 20 cases when exposed to temperatures of >4°C, while only 2 MAP samples exceeded APC limits and 8 samples PMC limits, respectively. In chicken legs, 8 samples in plain packaging exceeded the APC limits and 15 the PMC limits, while 12 samples in MAP exceeded the APC limits and 19 the PMC limits. In 402 samples (31.9%) in which the presence of E. coli was detected, its amount ranged from 1.70 to 3.65 log CFU.g−1. It was more commonly detected in chicken legs (255 of 630; 40.5%) than chicken breasts (147 of 630; 23.3%) but was not related to exposure temperature, exposure time, or time until examination. The presence of Salmonella spp. was not detected in any of the samples. Data acquired in the presented study will be used in the development of software helping the national supervisory authorities in the Czech Republic to evaluate whether inadequate transport of samples to analytical laboratories could have affected the microbiological profile of the sample.Key words: plain packaging, modified atmosphere, aerobic plate count, psychrotrophic microorganism

INTRODUCTION

Poultry meat is, in view of its physicochemical properties, susceptible to microbial spoilage, which depends on the bacterial contamination of the carcasses, hygienic procedures at manipulation, and the storage period and temperature. In healthy living animals, the muscle is virtually sterile, and contamination of the meat occurs mainly during slaughter. Minimizing bacterial contamination of poultry meat is, therefore, the goal during slaughter and meat processing (Soro et al., 2020). According to the so-called Hurdle Effect Theory, the conditions of food storage, i.e., external factors (temperature, relative air humidity, composition of the packaging atmosphere, duration of storage), combined with the internal factors (food composition, water activity, pH, redox potential and texture) are the principal factors influencing shelf life and safety (Leistner, 2000).

Storage temperature is, therefore, one of the most important factors influencing the growth and multiplication of microorganisms in chicken meat. According to the current legislation, fresh poultry meat must be chilled to a temperature not exceeding 4°C (European Union No 853/2004). This temperature must be maintained before transport as well as during it (European Union No 853/2004). Temperature has a great influence on the kinetics of microbial growth and chemical deterioration, especially when it fluctuates during transport, retail, and at home (Tuncer and Sireli, 2008; da Silva et al., 2016).

Although the scientific literature is inconsistent about the spoilage bacteria in poultry, Pseudomonas spp., Photobacterium spp., Brochothrix spp., the Enterobacteriaceae family, and lactic acid bacteria are most commonly reported to be associated with poultry spoilage. The predominance of individual genera is mainly influenced by the packaging method and storage temperature (Casaburi et al., 2015; Cauchie et al., 2020; Zouharová et al., 2023). Herbert et al. (2015) reported that as the spoilage is caused by a wide range of microorganisms, no specific microorganism could be identified as the dominant originator for the deteriorative changes. Rouger et al. (2018) stated that Brochothrix thermosphacta, along with the Pseudomonas and Carnobacterium genera, were the dominant microbiota in chicken legs packed in modified packaging. Hansen et al. (2023) used the genera Pseudomonas, Carnobacterium, Hafnia, Serratia, Brochothrix, and Shewanella in their spoilage experiments performed on modified atmosphere-packed chilled chicken breasts.

In accordance with the European legislation (European Union No 2073/2005), the level of hygiene in poultry slaughterhouses as well as the safety and microbiological quality of poultry meat are assessed through the absence of Salmonella in 25 g of mixed neck skin sample and through the Campylobacter spp. counts from poultry carcasses samples taken after chilling. The absence of Salmonella Typhimurium and Enteritidis in 25 g samples is further monitored in the market during the shelf life. However, the total amount of mesophilic and psychrotrophic microorganisms (Yimenu et al., 2019; Demirhan and Candogan, 2017) as well as the counts of E. coli and Enterobacteriaceae, which can be used as indicators of fecal contamination at slaughterhouses (CSN 569609/2008; Hauge et al., 2023b), are also indicative of the microbiological quality of poultry meat.

Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) and various types of active packaging are often applied in the packaging of poultry. Foods the shelf-life of which has been extended by the use of packaging gases must be visibly designated as 'packed in a protective atmosphere'. Only gases approved under the European Union No. 1333/2008 may be used for this purpose. For chilled poultry packed in MAP, the protective atmosphere usually consists of approximately 70% O2, 20% CO2, and the remainder is composed of other gases, with nitrogen being the most widely used (Zouharová et al., 2023). Herbert et al. (2015) report MAP for poultry fillets in Germany to typically consist of 70% O2 and 30% CO2.

The microbial quality of meat and meat products in the supply chain is of utmost importance and regular checks by the authorities both at the manufacturers and in the retail market are necessary. However, transportation (during which the cold chain may be in some cases violated for a brief time period) and storage of samples are often subjects of challange by the suppliers whose samples are evaluated as microbiologically unsatisfactory. For this reason, we have performed a series of experiments (e.g., Bartáková et al., 2023) aiming at assessing the dynamics of bacterial count changes in various food products, which were subsequently utilized for developing software that can determine whether (and to what degree) a sample which underwent a cold chain interruption during transport is still suitable for microbiological analysis and can yield valid results.

The present study assessed how elevated transport temperature, the duration of temperature violation, and the time till sample examination after resuming the cold storage can affect the microbiological profile of chicken meat (in our study, the term chicken will be used to describe chicken legs and chicken breasts) in plain and modified atmosphere packaging.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Samples of Chilled Chicken Meat

Chilled poultry meat (chicken legs and breasts) was used in the presented study. The fresh poultry meat was purchased in the retail and transported to the laboratory at temperatures not exceeding 4°C. Immediately upon receipt at the laboratory, the individual meat samples intended for plain packaging (approx. 150 g of chicken breast without skin or 250 g of chicken leg skin on, respectively) were individually aseptically placed in plastic bowls made of polypropylene and covered with polyethylene stretch film. Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) samples, i.e., chicken breast without skin or skin-on chicken legs were acquired and used for the experiment in the original producer-supplied packaging with the weight of approx. 500 g (smallest MAP packaging available in retail, typically 3-4 chicken breasts or 2 thighs in a tray covered with cling film). The MAP was oxygen-rich (75% O2 25% CO2) as verified for each batch using the CheckMate3 instrument (O.K. Servis BioPro, s.r.o., Czech Republic). All samples for both packaging types originated from the same producer. This practice simulated the way in which the supervisory authorities sample retail chicken meat. Before the start of the experiment, all prepared samples were stored at a temperature of ≤4°C overnight. Throughout the whole experiment, the ambient temperature as well as that of the cores of dedicated meat samples stored together with the analyzed samples, was monitored. The ambient temperature is the key parameter in interpretation of our results, as this value is entered by the users into the software developed based on the dataset acquired in this study.

Simulation of the Cold Chain Disruption

Before cold chain disruption, packages from each packaging type (plain or MAP) and type of chicken meat (legs or breasts) meat were randomly selected (21 for each combination) and used for the determination of the initial microbiological parameters (n = 84). Subsequently, a simulation of the violation (increase) of the external temperature during the transport of the samples was carried out using the following temperatures: 8, 11, 14, 17, 20, and 25°C. The duration of the samples’ exposure to the elevated temperature was 1, 2, 3, and 4 h. Twelve packages were used for each combination of temperature, packaging, and chicken meat (leg, breast) types. Immediately after the respective tested period, the samples were returned to the temperature of 4°C, where they were left until sample collection. Analytical samples were collected immediately after exposure to the elevated temperature (0 h, samples labeled E0), after 3 h (E3), and 24 h (E24) after returning to the low (storage) temperature, respectively. From each package, 3 parallel samples were taken for the analyses. Chicken meat samples stored at ≤4°C (temperature required by legislation) without interruption were used as control samples for all types of packaging (Figure 1).

Microbiological and Chemical Parameters

The aerobic plate count (ČSN EN ISO 4833-1/2014; aerobic incubation on Plate Count Agar (HiMedia, India) at 30°C for 72 h), the psychrotrophic microorganisms count (ČSN ISO 17410/2020; aerobic incubation on Plate Count Agar (HiMedia, India) at 6.5°C for 10 d), count of β-D-glucuronidase-positive Escherichia coli (ČSN ISO 16649-2/2003; aerobic incubation in Tryptone Bile X-Glucuronide medium (Bio-Rad) at 44°C for 18–24 h) and the presence of Salmonella spp. (ČSN EN ISO 6579-1/2020; parallel aerobic incubation (1) in XLD (Modified) medium (HiMedia, India) at 37°C for 24 h and (2) on Brilliant Green Agar, modified (Oxoid, UK) at 37°C for 24 h) were evaluated. APC, PMC, and E. coli were determined for each collected analytical sample. The presence of Salmonella spp. was evaluated before the start of the increased storage temperature simulation and then in all samples taken 24 h after the exposure to the increased temperature (V24 samples). Basic processing of the collected samples was carried out in accordance with ČSN EN ISO 6887-1/2018 using sterile saline enriched with peptone (0.85% NaCl + 0.1% peptone, pH 7) as a dilution medium. Immediately after taking the aliquot of the sample for the microbiological examination, pH value and water activity (aw) were determined from another aliquot of the same sample. A microprocessor pH meter 211 (Hanna Instruments) was used to determine the pH value. The pH was measured in each individual sample using an injection electrode at a temperature of 25 ± 1°C. Water activity was determined using a LabMaster aw-meter (Novasina AG, Switzerland) at a temperature of 25 ± 1°C. Considering the time needed to measure 1 sample, the water activity was always measured for a pooled sample characterizing an individual package of 3 individual chicken legs or breasts. In all, 1,044 samples were examined for aerobic plate count (APC), psychrotrophic microorganisms count (PMC), and E. coli, and pH. Water activity was analyzed in 348 samples. Salmonella spp. were analyzed from 408 samples (in all samples at the beginning and 24 h after the end of the exposure to the elevated temperature).

Statistical Analysis

The obtained values of CFU.g−1 counts were logarithmically transformed using the decimal logarithm (log CFU.g−1) and expressed using the mean and standard error of the mean (Supplementary Tables S1–S8). For each type of chicken meat (leg, breast), t-test was used to compare the initial values of aerobic plate count (APC), psychrotrophic microorganisms count (PMC), pH, and water activity (aw) among the 2 types of packaging (plain, MAP). For further statistical analysis, the APC (and PMC) values were always adjusted to represent the ratio of the measured value and the average initial value for the given temperature (Supplementary Tables S9–S16). A general linear model (GLM) – ANCOVA was used to evaluate the effect of temperature, cold chain interruption length, sample examination time, and type of packaging on adjusted APC and PMC. Sample examination time and type of packaging were included as categorical factors; the temperature and cold chain interruption length served as continuous predictors. All performed tests were evaluated at a significance level of α = 0.05. The Statistica software, version 14.0, was used for data processing.

RESULTS

Aerobic Plate Count

The initial total amount of microorganisms expressed as aerobic plate count (APC) recorded before the cold chain interruption in the chilled chicken breast was 5.76 ± 0.18 log CFU.g−1 in plain packaging and 4.50 ± 0.10 log CFU.g−1 in modified atmosphere packaging (MAP), respectively (Table 1). These values were statistically significantly different (t-test: t = 6.013, df = 40, P < 0.001). After cold chain interruption, APC was affected by the duration of the cold chain interruption (ANCOVA: P = 0.004), by the time from the cold chain interruption until examination (P = 0. 004), and by packaging type (P < 0.001). The influence of the temperature the sample was exposed to was not significant (P = 0.331; Table 2). The longer cold chain interruption and later time of examination were associated with higher APC. APCs were generally higher in plain packaging samples than in MAP samples (Table 1, Supplementary Tables S1, S2, S9, S10).

Initial APC before the cold chain interruption differed significantly between samples in plain packaging (5.08 ± 0.14 log CFU.g−1) and MAP packaging (3.93 ± 0.08 log CFU.g−1) also in chilled chicken legs (t-test: t = 7.141, df = 40, P < 0.001). In chicken leg samples, APC limits were exceeded more often in samples with later examination time (8x for samples in plain packaging), and in samples exposed at temperatures of >4°C (12x for samples in MAP packaging; Table 3, Supplementary Tables S3, S4).

Psychrotrophic Microorganisms Count

Initial psychrotrophic microorganisms counts (PMC) differed significantly between the samples in the plain and MAP packaging, both in chicken breasts (plain: 5.81 ± 0.20 log CFU.g−1, MAP: 4.28 ± 0.10 log CFU.g−1, t-test: t = 6.790, df = 40, P < 0.001) and in chicken legs (plain: 5.23 ± 0.15 log CFU.g−1, MAP: 3.68 ± 0.12 log CFU.g−1, t-test: t = 8.27, df = 40, P < 0.001; Table 1). After the cold chain interruption, the PMC in chicken breast samples were significantly affected in the same way as the APC by the duration of the cold chain interruption (ANCOVA: P = 0.001), time until examination (P < 0.001), and packaging type (P < 0.001). The effect of temperature was not significant (P = 0.501; Table 2). Higher PMCs were found in samples with longer exposure and later examination time; in terms of packaging, higher PMCs were found for plain packaging (Table 1, Supplementary Tables S5, S6, S13, S14). Samples of chicken breast exceeded the limits frequently (20x plain packaging, 8x MAP), especially at longer exposures at temperatures of >4°C and later examination times (Table 4, Supplementary Tables S5, S6).

Escherichia coli and Salmonella spp.

Most samples (858 of 1260 total) contained less than 1.70 log CFU.g−1 of E. coli (i.e., values below the limit of detection of the used plate method). In 402 samples (31.9 %) in which the presence of E. coli was detected, its amount ranged from 1.70 to 3.65 log CFU.g−1. It was more common in chicken legs (255 of 630; 40.5 %) than chicken breasts (147 of 630; 23.3 %) but was not related to the exposure temperature, exposure time, or time until examination. The presence of Salmonella spp. was not detected in any of the samples (n = 480).

Water Activity and pH

The initial pH recorded before the cold chain interruption in chicken breast was 5.90 ± 0.04 in plain packaging and 5.83 ± 0.03 in MAP packaging, respectively. These values did not differ significantly (t-test: t = 1.332, df = 40, P = 0.191). After cold chain interruption, pH in chicken breast samples was 5.98 ± 0.01, and 5.87 ± 0.01 in plain and MAP packaging, respectively (Table 1).

The initial pH of chicken legs samples was 6.43 ± 0.03 (plain packaging) vs. 6.34 ± 0.02 (MAP packaging), which was statistically significantly different (t-test: t = 2.329, df = 40, P = 0.025). After exposure at temperatures of >4°C, pH was 6.52 ± 0.01 and 6.39 ± 0.01 for samples in plain and MAP packaging, respectively (Table 1).

The water activity found in chicken breasts before the cold chain interruption was 0.9853 ± 0.0009 (plain packaging) vs 0.9818 ± 0.0023 (MAP packaging); after the cold chain interruption, the values were 0.9835 ± 0.0003 (plain packaging) and 0.9769 ± 0.0002 (MAP packaging). In chicken legs, water activity was 0.9837 ± 0.0003 (plain packaging) and 0.9800 ± 0.0012 (MAP packaging) before the cold chain interruption, and 0.9826 ± 0.0005 (plain packaging) and 0.9784 ± 0.0002 (MAP packaging) after cold chain interruption (Table 1).

DISCUSSION

The initial contamination of poultry meat during the slaughter process is crucial for its microbiological quality. APC is an important criterion for assessing the microbial quality of poultry meat. Hauge et al. (2023a) evaluated the microorganism counts and the possibilities to reduce them in broiler carcasses in Norway. The mean APC of untreated carcasses was 4.9 log CFU/mL (determined by the rinse method), with cold water cooling showing no significant reduction of APC. These numbers correspond with the baseline APC of chilled chicken meat exposure in our study. Similarly, mean APCs of 4.70 and 4.93 log CFU/g in broiler carcasses after chilling were reported by Hauge et al. (2023b) in 2 examined slaughterhouses (determined using the Petrifilm method). Svobodová et al. (2012) determined APC in broiler carcasses after chilling (rinse method) at a slaughterhouse in the Czech Republic to be 3.7 log ± 0.6 CFU/mL (ranging from 2.3 log to 5.2 log).

In broiler carcasses, APC is greatly influenced by the method of chilling; most processing plants in Europe (80 %) prefer chilling by air to cooling by water (Belucco et al., 2016). The growth and proliferation of bacteria in chicken meat is significantly influenced by the packaging method. In their model experiments, Hansen et al. (2023) stored chicken breast fillets packed in MAP at 4°C for 11 d. All bacterial cocktails grew relatively well in chicken meat packed with 100% N2, while a gas mixture of 60% CO2/40% N2 resulted in growth inhibition of all isolates compared to 100% N2. The modified atmosphere for chicken meat in our study consisted of 75% O2 and 25% CO2. A similar gas composition (70% O2 and 30% CO2) has been previously reported for chicken fillets in Germany (Herbert et al., 2015).

The results of our study confirm the effect of temperature increase and cold chain violation time on APC and PMC, consistently with the conclusions of other authors. Tuncer and Sireli (2008) evaluated the effect of storage temperature (0, 4, 7°C), cooling method (air and water), and packaging (nonpacked chicken, 2 types of synthetic packaging) on APC in broiler carcasses. Their study showed significant differences in APC as a function of (mainly) the storage temperature. The authors recommended the sale of prepackaged chicken meat, air cooling, and a storage temperature of 0°C. Yimenu et al. (2019) evaluated the shelf life of chicken breasts at storage temperatures of 0, 5, 10, and 15°C for 7-15 d. Their results confirmed the storage temperature of chicken meat as a factor of critical importance in ensuring the microbiological quality of chilled chicken meat and APC as a key criterion for evaluating the microbiological quality determining the freshness and shelf-life of chicken meat. The mathematical shelf-life model developed in that study showed an even decrease of shelf-life with increasing temperature. It can be used to estimate the remaining shelf-life of packaged chicken meat under any temperature condition in the range from 0 to 15°C.

Remarkable reduction in the shelf life of fresh pork and poultry when short temperature upshifts (from 4°C to 7 or 15°C) occurred at the beginning of the storage was described by Bruckner et al. (2012), especially if the temperature abuses took place at the beginning (in the first 60 h) of storage. Reduction of shelf live of up to 2 d were observed for both meat types even when the time with an abusive temperature was <5 % of the total storage time.

Bruckner et al. (2013) developed a common predictive shelf life model applicable to fresh pork as well as poultry meat. Based on the growth of Pseudomonas sp., the model was developed by combining the Gompertz model as primary and the Arrhenius model as secondary model. Ghollasi-Mood et al. (2016) showed that the Gompertz model satisfactorily predicted the growth of Pseudomonas spp. (selected as the specific spoilage organism in the air‐packaged chicken meat stored under different temperature conditions). Our results are consistent with those by Herbert et al. (2015) who also tested MAP (70% O2 and 30% CO2) prepacked chicken breasts at different temperatures (2, 4, 10, and 15°C) and confirmed that the microbial growth and meat spoilage increases with elevating storage temperature.

In our study, E. coli was more frequently detected in chicken legs (40.5%) than in chicken breasts (23.3%). This is not unexpected considering that legs, unlike breasts, contain skin, a significant source of E. coli. Lee et al. (2023) reported the overall prevalence of E. coli in samples of retail chicken meat in South California (67.86%, 38/56). Similarly, Teles et al. (2021) proved the presence of E. coli in 47.36 % of chicken meat samples from local supermarkets in Brazil.

Salmonella spp. were not detected in any of the samples analyzed in our study. Svobodová et al. (2012) detected Salmonella spp in a slaughterhouse in the Czech Republic only in 6 of the total of 160 broiler carcasses (3.75%). Bailey et al. (2020) reported a 5% prevalence of Salmonella spp. in water-chilled and 8% prevalence in air-chilled broiler carcass rinses, respectively, in 2 slaughterhouses during conventional and organic processing in the United States. Sampling performed to evaluate the compliance with process hygiene criteria in carcasses at the slaughterhouses in the context of European Union No 2073/2005 for Member States found 11.8% of chicken samples collected by the competent authorities for chicken (EFSA, 2023) to be Salmonella-positive. In our study, we detected no Salmonella-positive sample; this indicates that the supply chain from which we obtained our samples adheres well to the hygienic regulations (note that we cannot generalize this finding to the entire retail market as for maintaining maximum homogeneity of the samples, we procurred all from the same supplier).

As every study, the presented also comes with certain limitations. One possible limitation likes in the fact that we have manually re-packed the plain packaging samples in our laboratory, which might have theoretically led to contamination. However, we have worked in sterile environment taking appropriate precautions; moreover, all samples packed on the same day always originated from the same batch from the same manufacturer and, therefore, the microbial quality of the entire batch was likely similar, limiting the risk of possible cross-contamination. Also, Salmonella species were not analyzed in every sample but only at the beginning and end of the evaluation period; however, as no Salmonella was detected in any of the samples even at the end of the evaluation period, it is unlikely that we would have captured any Salmonella contamination in the mid-period samples.

CONCLUSIONS

In this study, we have determined the microbiological profile (aerobic plate count, psychrotrophic microorganisms count, E. coli) of chicken meat (breasts and legs) exposed to various temperatures for different periods of time at various time points after the end of the simulated cold chain interruption. In addition, we analyzed the presence of Salmonella in these samples; however, none of the samples was positive for Salmonella spp. The results of the presented study will serve as one of the data sources for the development of software helping the national supervisory authorities in the Czech Republic to evaluate whether temperature violations during the transport of samples to analytical laboratories, the time of temperature violation, and the time it takes for the sample to be examined can affect the microbiological profile of the sample. This software (https://webstudio.shinyapps.io/transportvzorku/) may reduce the number of samples rejected by the laboratories due to improper transport. The results of the study can also be used as a defense against challenges aiming to cast doubts on the results of analyses of samples taken during routine inspections. Finally, the results can be also valuable for producers of poultry.
