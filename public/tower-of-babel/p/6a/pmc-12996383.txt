Molecular resilience of neurons to repetitive mechanical compression
Authors: Allegra Coppini, Valentina Cappello, Syeda Rubaiya Nasrin, Alessandro Falconieri, Oz Mualem, Gadiel Saper, Orit Shefi, Henry Hess, Akira Kakugo, Vittoria Raffa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cells and organs constantly experience mechanical forces. Neurons, in particular, are exposed to such stimuli during development, aging, disease, and normal activities like movement and homeostasis. Recent studies highlight the key role of microtubules (MTs) in mechanotransduction, adjusting cytoskeletal dynamics in response to mechanical cues. While the effects of acute forces on MTs are known, the impact of repetitive mechanical stimuli over time remains unclear. In this study, we applied repetitive mechanical motion to neurons from the dorsal root ganglia and analyzed responses at varying strain levels. A 10% strain caused MT and organelle damage, leading to cell death. In contrast, a 2.5% strain did not harm cells and instead stabilized MTs. A 5% strain caused damage to the MT structure and leads to MT destabilization, but neurons activate a molecular response to counteract and recover from this damage, suggesting the involvement of the Ras pathway in response to injury. These findings suggest that neurons can adapt to repetitive mechanical stress, maintaining homeostasis when strain is below a certain threshold. Our results improve understanding of how mechanical forces influence neuronal structure and function, and how cells respond to injury by initiating protective pathways.

Introduction

Neurons are exposed to mechanical stimuli throughout their lifetime, not only in pathological conditions or during neurodevelopment but also in daily activities1–6. For example, nerves are continuously exposed to cyclic mechanical stimuli during movements of the trunk, head, and limbs. During flexion–extension motions, grasping, or spinal movements, they experience combinations of tensile, shear, and compressive forces that lead to nerve gliding, stretching, or compression7. These stimuli arise from interactions between neurons and their external environment5. To allow proper function while withstanding mechanical stresses, neurons convert mechanical stimuli into biological responses through the process of mechanotransduction8.

In this study, we focus on repetitive compression, a type of stress that neurons experience throughout their lifetime. Some studies have investigated the impact of cyclic compression, although most of these studies have focused on non-neural cells such as cardiomyocytes9, epithelial cells10, cancer cells11, etc. In these cases, it has been observed that repetitive compression induces adaptive responses such as cell reorientation and cell shape deformation through MT and actin cytoskeleton remodeling10–14.

Regarding neurons, most studies focus on cyclic stretching, which promotes neurite alignment perpendicular to the direction of strain, likely involving the reorganization of MTs, microtubule-associated proteins (MAPs), and stress fibers15–17.

Most studies investigating the effects of repetitive mechanical stimuli have predominantly focused on stretching forces or non-neuronal cells. However, compression and the unique features of neurons, such as their polarization into the axonal and dendritic compartments and their related functions, have been poorly considered. Furthermore, the molecular pathways that sustain neuronal resilience to repetitive compression remain largely unexplored. Given that mechanical forces can inflict damage and that the peripheral nervous system (PNS) experiences repetitive, high-magnitude compressive movements, it is remarkable that its neurons remain functional throughout life without succumbing to mechanical fatigue18. This highlights the importance of studying the effects of cyclic compression on neurons, especially at the MT cytoskeleton level. In fact, Kabir and colleagues observed that under mechanical compression, MTs can undergo buckling and breakage19,20. Repetitive compression on MTs has revealed their susceptibility to mechanical fatigue. Nasrin and colleagues showed that repeated compression can induce MT breakage at a rate that increased by 5000-fold when the strain rose from 12.5% to 20%21. This also raises the question of whether neurons possess specific protective mechanisms to tolerate and adapt to MT damage arising from repetitive mechanical compression. Recent literature has proposed some potential mechanisms of mechano-protection against MT damage. These include the self-healing properties of MTs22 and the protective acetylation of α-tubulin at lysine 40 (αK40), which reduces the accumulation of mechanical damage in MTs23–25. In addition, several papers have identified MAPs (such as CLIP-170 and CLASP) as being involved in the response to mechanical damage9,10,12,26,27. However, the molecular pathways that sustain neuronal resilience in living neurons under cyclic compression remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we aimed to address this gap by investigating how cyclic strain applied longitudinally to axons affects neuronal structure and function, as well as the capacity of neurons to recover from mechanical damage and the molecular mechanisms potentially involved. Using dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons, we applied 20 cycles of compression at varying strain levels. Our results revealed magnitude-dependent effects on neuronal survival. We found a threshold at which axonal damage occurs, disrupting MT stability and organization. Remarkably, neurons can recover from these alterations when the strain does not exceed a critical value. We detected transcriptional changes potentially linked to the resolution of the stress response. Among these changes, the Ras signaling pathway—a key regulator of cellular growth and survival28,29 implicated in cytoskeletal dynamics, mechanotransduction, neuroprotection, and response to injuries28–32—emerged as a possible regulator of resilience, playing a role in orchestrating recovery after mechanical damage due to cyclic compression.

Results

Force induces MT stabilization and reorganization of the MT network in the soma

Cyclic compression was applied using the previously developed micro-stretcher device21 customized for cell culturing on a stretchable substrate (Fig. 1A). The distance in the stretcher can be controlled through a micrometric screw. The portable cartridge system for the stretcher allowed the maintenance of the length of the polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrate in its desired configuration (e.g., resting position or stretched position) during manipulation. The experimental setup combines an 800 μm-thick PDMS stretchable substrate, mounted on the micro-stretcher with a silicon chip (XONA) on top33,34 for cell culture compartmentalization (Fig. 1B). The setup of the silicon chip-PDMS substrate assembly serves as a microfluidic chamber for cell culture where DRG neurons are seeded in the cellular compartment (CC). At the same time, the axons can cross the microchannels (MCs) to reach the axonal compartment (AC) (Fig. 1C). In this configuration, neuronal soma remain confined to the CC (Fig. 1C, Hoechst staining). For the cyclic compression experiment, the silicon chip-PDMS assembly was mounted onto the cartridge in a pre-stretched configuration and placed within the instrument (Fig. 1D). DRG neurons were seeded in the CC on pre-stretched PDMS (DIV0, Fig. 1E) and the cartridge was placed in the incubator for cell culture. At DIV1, cells were subjected to compression cycles (DIV1, Fig. 1E). During each compression cycle, the PDMS alternated from stretched-to-rest position and neurons alternated from rest-to-compression position. In this work the number of cycles n was constant (n = 20) and the strain s was 2.5% or 5.0% or 10.0% (Fig. 1F).

Specifically, we selected different compressive strains to identify a tolerance range. A maximum strain of 10% compression was chosen, as the literature indicates this as a critical threshold capable of triggering cellular responses and, in some cases, cell death, depending on the application frequency and cell type10. Then, the lower values were selected using a two-fold reduction factor (i.e., 5% and 2.5%). Compression was applied at a rate r of 0.42%/s, with a resting time (tr) from compression-to-resting of 5 s (Fig. 1F). Hereafter, we will refer to this group as COMPR group (Fig. 1B3). SHAM groups refer to cells seeded in CC on pre-stretched PDMS placed into the instrument but not subjected to cyclic compression (Fig. 1B2). CTRL group consists of cells seeded in CC on non-stretched PDMS, which were neither placed in the instrument nor subjected to cyclic compression (Fig. 1B1).

PDMS itself did not affect cell morphology, as the adhesion of DRG neurons cultured in microfluidic chambers attached to PDMS was similar to the adhesion of DRG neurons cultured in chambers attached to standard glass coverslip (Supplementary Fig. 1). Additionally, we investigated whether the process of culturing cells in the chamber mounted onto the cartridge in a pre-stretched configuration and placing it within the instrument for time t would impair cell viability. We found that the percentage of dead cells versus live cells was not statistically different between the CTRL and SHAM groups (supplementary Fig. 2, 3.40 ± 0.79% and 4.26 ± 0.94% in CTRL and SHAM groups, respectively, p = 0.52). Based on these results, subsequent experiments mainly focused on comparing the COMPR and SHAM groups.

10% cyclic compression induces axonal buckling, degeneration, and cell death

The application of 10% cyclic compression affected the axonal morphology of DRG neurons 6 h post-compression. In detail, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed progressive degeneration. In the SHAM group, the soma (Fig. 2A) and processes (Fig. 2C1) appeared organized and intact. Conversely, in the COMPR group, features of degeneration were evident, including chromatin condensation (Fig. 2B1, stars) and nuclear envelope degradation (Fig. 2B2, arrows). Furthermore, within the axons, the cytoplasm exhibited increased electron density compared to the control, with organelles and MTs no longer visible (Fig. 2C2). We quantified the frequency of degeneration observed in somata and axons. In a population of 20 cells, we observed damage in 5% of cells in the SHAM group and in 85% of cells in the COMPR group (Fig. 2D1, p < 0.0001). In a population of 50 axons, we observed damage in 2% of axons in the SHAM group and in 74% of axons in the COMPR group (Fig. 2D2, p < 0.0001).

Viability assessed 6 hours post-stimulation showed an increase in cell death, with the percentage of dead versus viable cells measured at 5.42 ± 0.45% in the SHAM group and 12.43 ± 1.58% in the COMPR group (p = 0.013 and Supplementary Fig. 3A), and a percentage of apoptotic cells measured at 3.51 ± 1.12% in the SHAM group and 17.66 ± 8.70% in the COMPR group (p < 0.0001 and Supplementary Fig. 3B), suggesting the onset of degenerative processes. In good agreement with previous studies10, we found that cyclic compression at a strain of 10% induces degenerative processes in DRG neurons, ultimately leading to cell death. This rapid progression likely prevents the activation of potential repair mechanisms.

For a quantitative assessment, we measured buckling as the ratio between L/L₀, where L is the length of an axon fragment with a distance between the terminal points of L₀ = 100 µm (Fig. 3A4, scheme). Consistent with qualitative observations, no significant differences in the L/L₀ ratio were observed between SHAM and COMPR (Fig. 3A4, p = 1). However, significant differences were found between CTRL and COMPR (Fig. 3A4, p < 0.0001) as well as between CTRL and SHAM (Fig. 3A4, p = 0.0001). These findings indicate that axonal buckling occurs due to the transition from the pre-stretched to the resting position of the PDMS when the chamber is released from the cartridge. Apart from this transition, the repeated cycles of compression did not exacerbate the buckling effect. However, the application of 2.5% cyclic compression induced some alterations in axonal morphology. Specifically, the analysis of axon length revealed a 20% reduction in axonal length in the COMPR group compared to SHAM (273.6 ± 10.8 µm in SHAM and 220.4 ± 8.0 µm in COMPR, p = 0.0005, Fig. 3A5).

Cyclic compression at a strain of 2.5% is not sufficient to impair neuron viability but does induce axon retraction. A recent study highlighted the possibility that cyclic compressive strains may promote MT stabilization to counteract this stressor10. Since α-tubulin acetylation is associated with stable MTs, whereas tyrosination correlates with dynamic instability, the ratio between α-tubulin acetylation and tyrosination levels is used as a marker of MT stability35–38.

To investigate this, we performed immunostaining for these two post-translational modifications of α-tubulin. By analyzing axon bundles in MCs, we found a higher signal for α-tubulin acetylation (Ac TUBA) in the COMPR group (red, Fig. 3B2) compared to the SHAM group, where α-tubulin tyrosination (Tyr TUBA) was more prevalent (green, Fig. 3B1). We then analyzed the ratio between α-tubulin acetylation and tyrosination levels in the MCs, where axons form bundles. We found that cyclic compression at 2.5% strain resulted in a significant increase in the COMPR group compared to the SHAM group (2.58 ± 0.10 and 2.90 ± 0.10 in SHAM and COMPR, respectively, p = 0.005, Fig. 3B4). The same trend was observed in isolated axons within the AC, where a significant increase in the COMPR group compared to SHAM was detected (2.59 ± 0.14 and 3.18 ± 0.18 in SHAM and COMPR, respectively, p = 0.02, Fig. 3B5). No differences were observed in MTs within neuronal somata (2.24 ± 0.13 and 1.98 ± 0.07 in SHAM and COMPR, respectively, Fig. 3B3, p = 0.17).

Ultrastructural characterization revealed no significant changes in axonal ultrastructure. No signs of degeneration were observed when comparing SHAM (Fig. 3C1) and COMPR (Fig. 3C2), nor were there significant alterations in MT density (9.48 ± 0.29 MTs/µm and 9.21 ± 0.31 MTs/µm in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.53, Fig. 3C3), inter-MT distance (88.7 ± 3.7 and 95.4 ± 5.3 nm in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.73, Fig. 3C4), or the presence of MT twists (Fig. 3C5, p = 0.60).

These findings suggest that DRGs are not significantly affected by 2.5% cyclic compression. While axonal shortening is observed, neurons respond by inducing post-translational modifications of tubulin that promote increased MT stability. This response may promote a recovery of physiological levels, as no differences between SHAM and COMPR groups can be detected anymore 24 h post-compression in axon length (supplementary Fig. 4B) or MT post-translational modifications (Supplementary Fig. 4C). This mechanism is a well-documented cellular protective response to mechanical stimuli and is particularly prevalent in cells frequently subjected to mechanical stress23,24.

As expected, 5% cyclic compression induced a reduction in axon length, with a mean length of 258.4 ± 11.1 μm in the SHAM group and 223.3 ± 10.1 μm in the COMPR group (Fig. 4A5, p = 0.022). However, unlike the 2.5% strain condition, 5% cyclic compression had an opposing effect on MT post-translational modifications in axons. Figure 4B shows representative images demonstrating an increase in the α-tubulin tyrosination signal in the COMPR group (Fig. 4B1) compared to the SHAM group (Fig. 4B2). Quantitative analysis of axon bundles in the MCs revealed a reduction in the acetylated-to-tyrosinated tubulin ratio in the COMPR group (2.011 ± 0.077) compared to the SHAM group (2.45 ± 0.11) (Fig. 4B3, p = 0.001). A similar trend was observed in isolated axons growing in the AC, with values of 2.50 ± 0.15 in the SHAM group and 2.08 ± 0.10 in the COMPR group (p = 0.028, Fig. 4B4). However, no effects on MT stabilization were detected in the soma (2.26 ± 0.09 and 2.34 ± 0.19 in SHAM and COMPR, respectively, Fig. 4B5, p = 0.87).

In addition to the reduction in MT stability, TEM analysis revealed disorganization of axonal MTs. At 30 min post-compression, the COMPR group showed a decrease in MT density (9.56 ± 0.29 MTs per µm in SHAM vs. 8.64 ± 0.31 MTs per µm in COMPR, p = 0.033, Fig. 4C8) and an increase in MT inter-distance (88.69 ± 3.85 nm in SHAM vs. 104.20 ± 5.12 nm in COMPR, p = 0.017, Fig. 4C9). Moreover, compared to the SHAM group (Fig. 4C1), the COMPR group exhibited a higher frequency of overlapping MTs, which lost their parallel alignment with the axonal axis. The extent of MT disorganization varied within the COMPR samples: some axons maintained the standard organization in parallel bundles (Fig. 4C2), while others displayed more pronounced alterations, including overlapping MTs (Fig. 4C3, 4, arrows) and, in rare cases, complete axonal twisting (Fig. 4C5). This phenomenon was quantified by counting axons with overlapping MTs (twists), revealing a significant increase in twisted structures in the COMPR group compared to the SHAM group (p = 0.0022, Fig. 4C10). Additionally, some axons exhibited breakage, a condition not observed in the SHAM group (Fig. 4C6, 7).

Collectively, these findings indicate that 20 cycles of 5% compression significantly impact axons, leading to axonal shortening, MT destabilization through post-translational modifications, and disruptions in MT organization. However, no cell death was observed 6 h after stimulation, with the percentage of dead cells relative to live cells at 4.76 ± 0.33% and 5.43 ± 0.63% in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively (p = 0.39, Supplementary Fig. 5). The absence of cell death raises the question of whether neurons can activate molecular pathways to recover and counteract the ultrastructural damage caused by 5% cyclic strain.

Neurons recover from the structural damage induced by 5% cyclic compression

To confirm that the application of cyclic compression at 5% strain does not induce permanent damage leading to cell death, a live/dead assay was performed at 24 h after stimulation, corroborating the absence of significant differences in cell viability between the two conditions (the percentage of dead cells relative to the alive ones was 4.32 ± 0.06% and 4.51 ± 0.36% in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.63, supplementary Fig. 6). Additionally, the fraction of cells positive for activated Caspase 3 (aCasp3) was also quantified 24 h post-stimulation, showing no differences (2.18 ± 0.20 and 2.29 ± 0.17% in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.93, Supplementary Fig. 7). These results confirm that neurons are resilient to 5% cyclic strain. Indeed, we evaluated whether ultrastructural damage was also recovered after 24 h. Axon shortening was not anymore detectable 24 h post-stimulation (598.7 ± 22.79 µm and 553.1 ± 23.52 µm in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.17, Fig. 5A3), suggesting a recovery mechanism. Consistently, the signal for α-tubulin acetylation and tyrosination was similar between the two conditions (Fig. 5A1, 2). Specifically, the acetylated-to-tyrosinated tubulin ratio returned to the control levels (Fig. 5A1, 2) 24 hours after compression in both axon bundles (2.46 ± 0.11 and 2.47 ± 0.12 in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.90, Fig. 5A4) and isolated axons (2.62 ± 0.18 and 2.63 ± 0.16 in the SHAM and COMPR groups, respectively, p = 0.70, Fig. 5A5). Similarly, ultrastructural alterations appeared to be resolved. An analysis of axons revealed that MTs in the COMPR group (Fig. 5B4–6) had regained a parallel orientation relative to the axonal axis, similar to those observed in the SHAM group (Fig. 5B1–3). Ras pathway is involved in the response to 5% cyclic strain

We sought to understand the mechanisms neurons employ to recover from or exhibit resilience to damage caused by cyclic compression. Given the limited investigation of this topic in the literature and the absence of molecular data, we performed transcriptomic profiling of neurons to identify potential pathways involved in the stress response.

Having observed damage in the short term and recovery in the subsequent 24 h, we investigated two time-points (6 h, 24 h post-compression). RNA was extracted from the SHAM group after 6 h as an internal control. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the transcription profiles of SHAM and COMPR (6 h post-stimulation) were very similar but different from the COMPR (24 h post-stimulation) group (Supplementary Fig. 8), suggesting that the activation of the stress-induced response occurs after several hours. This gene list was used to perform a Gene Ontology Enrichment Analysis (GOEA) to identify enriched pathways. The analysis identified pathways related to synapses (GO:0045202), process development (GO:0031175), adenylate cyclase-modulating GPCR signaling (GO:0007188), G-protein-coupled peptide receptor activity (GO:0008528), and Ras signal transduction (GO:0007265), with Ras signaling showing the highest fold enrichment (Fig. 6A). GEFs regulate the transition of G-proteins from an inactive to an active state by facilitating the exchange of GDP for GTP39. Specifically, we found for the mentioned GEFs an increase in their expression at 24 h post-stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 10). We found that the application of a cyclic compression suddenly caused a 34% reduction in its active form Ras-GTP (p = 0.043, Fig. 6C1). To gather evidence that this mechanism is specific for Ras proteins, we analyzed Rho, a protein that belongs to the same superfamily and is involved in cytoskeletal remodeling41, mechanotransduction42, modulation of axon growth and growth cone dynamics43–45 but which is not a target of the GEFs we found dysregulated by GOEA (Fig. 6B). Consistent with our hypothesis, the levels of Rho-GTP were similar in the COMPR versus SHAM groups at both 30 min after stimulation (p = 0.54, Fig. 6C3) and 24 h post-stimulation (p = 0.096, Fig. 6C4).

Indeed, the compression induced axon shortening (Fig. 3A5) and a reduction in the active form of Ras (Fig. 6C1), indicating a temporary impairment of its functions supporting axon growth. Ras can modulate axon growth by multiple effectors. Since we observed an impairment of MT stability (Fig. 4B3, 4), we focused our attention on GSK3 - an enzyme inhibited by the Ras-GTP/PI3K/Akt cascade (Fig. 6D1)−which negatively regulates MT stability by phosphorylating MAPs29,46–48. Because the Ras/PI3K/Akt pathway inhibits GSK3β activity through phosphorylation at Ser9, we performed immunostaining for pGSK3βSer9 in the COMPR versus SHAM condition (Fig. 6D2). Fluorescence intensity levels of pGSK3βSer9 (normalized to total GSK3β) were analyzed at the axonal level 30 min after stimulation, revealing a reduction in protein phosphorylation in the stimulated group (COMPR vs SHAM, p = 0.024; Fig. 6D3). This finding is consistent with our experimental hypothesis that Ras inactivation leads to an increase in the active form of GSK3β and, consequently, to MT instability (Fig. 6D1). However, within 24 h, the cells may modulate the Ras pathway through regulatory GEFs (Fig. 6A, B, Supplementary Fig. 10), to rescue its activity (Fig. 6C2). These results suggest that reactivation of Ras pathway may play a critical and specific role in recovery damages from compression.

Discussion

Neurons in our body are constantly subjected to mechanical stimuli during nervous system development, daily movements, and pathological conditions2–4,49–51. This raises an important question: how do neurons, which experience mechanical stress daily, continue to function throughout our lives? Are there repair mechanisms to restore compromised axonal structures and functions? One can expect that high compressive forces, like those observed in traumatic events, cannot be tolerated by neurons52–54. Similarly, one can expect that low compressive forces, such as those generated by cell-to-cell and cell-to-matrix interactions, should be well tolerated and may even be beneficial for neuronal development55,56. It is also likely that an intermediate range of compressive forces exists—especially repetitive forces that neurons experience during daily movements and aging—which may cause damage, yet neurons are resilient enough to withstand them.

To explore this topic, we used neurons isolated from dorsal root ganglia. Located within the spinal foramina, DRG neurons transmit proprioceptive, thermal, and nociceptive signals to the central nervous system from both within the body and the external environment57. DRG neurons are exposed to relatively high mechanical forces, and their axons undergo chronic compression under physiological, pathological, or debilitating conditions. To simulate repetitive compression, we cultured isolated neurons on a PDMS stretchable substrate. Compression was applied over 20 cycles at a rate of 0.42% per second, with strain ranging from a minimum of 2.5% to a maximum of 10%. Our findings reveal that the maximum strain caused detrimental morphological changes in neurons, initiating degenerative processes. These processes include destruction of axonal MT cytoskeleton, changes in organelle morphology, fragmentation of nuclear envelopes and other membranes, chromatin condensation, ultimately leading to cell death (Fig. 2). Compared to other cell types (e.g., epithelial cells) stimulated with repeated cycle compression at the same strain10, neurons may be more vulnerable to this stimulation.

Conversely, the minimum strain did not impair cell viability (supplementary Fig. 4A) but induced a reduction in axon length (Fig. 3A5). Previous studies have shown that mechanical stimuli can induce growth cone collapse and neurite retraction52,58. However, axon retraction can also precede subsequent axonal regrowth. As observed by Abraham et colleagues repeated stretching cycles initially induce process retraction, which is then followed by regrowth. The analysis of the axon ultrastructure revealed no changes in MT network, in terms of MT density (Fig. 3C3), MT-MT inter-distance (Fig. 3C4), and absence of damage such as break or twists (Fig. 3C5). Interestingly, we found an increase of MT stability in axons (Fig. 3B4, B5), which is generally associated with axon growth35,36,59. Indeed, this low intensity repetitive compression impacts neurons by reducing axon length, but neurons still initiate an immediate positive response by increasing MT stability. This stabilization may serve as a protective mechanism to promote axonal repair and regrowth following compression, as physiological values are restored 24 h post-stimulation (Supplementary Fig. 4B, C).

As the next step, we focused our investigation on the intermediate strain (5%) to assess whether this level caused neuronal damage and if recovery was possible. This strain had a significant impact, leading to a reduction in axon length (Fig. 4A5). Ultrastructural analysis of the axon revealed alterations in MT architecture (Fig. 4C), including twisted MTs (Fig. 4C10), suggesting possible cytoskeletal torsion and axon bending (Fig. 4C3, 4, 5). In some cases, axonal breaks were observed (Fig. 4C6, 7), along with a decrease in MT density (Fig. 4C8) and an increase in MT-MT inter-distance (Fig. 4C9). These findings indicate that repeated compression can disrupt MT organization. This is not surprising, as previous studies on MTs grown in vitro have already shown that compressive forces may cause MT breakage and buckling19,20. Additionally, reduced MT density and loss of parallel alignment are characteristic features of compromised axons, observed during aging60, mechanical stress61, and in retraction bulbs. These changes coincide with retraction bulbs and post-translational modifications indicative of decreased stability62. Indeed, we also observed a decrease in MT stability in isolated axons (Fig. 4B4) and within axon bundles (Fig. 4B3). During axon formation in hippocampal neurons, a causal relationship has been identified between Ras pathway inactivation and the activation of GSK-3β63—which leads to microtubule destabilization37. Consistent with this model29, we observed that stimulation-induced damage leads to Ras inactivation (i.e., decrease of Ras-GTP, Fig. 6C1), an increase of active GSK-3β (i.e., decrease of the phosphorylated form, Fig. 6D3), a reduction in the ratio between acetylated (stable) MT versus tyrosinated (dynamic) MTs (Fig. 4B3, 4), and axon retraction (Fig. 4A5). The axon retraction observed experimentally suggest a loss of cell-matrix adhesion. Additionally, p120 is involved in the integrin signaling, and the dissociation of p120 GAP from focal adhesion kinase has been reported to lead to its association with Ras, resulting in Ras inactivation64–66. However, at this strain value, no changes in cell survival were observed post-compression (Supplementary Figs. 5 and 6), and axons recovered their length within 24 h (Fig. 5A3), raising the possibility that neurons may have strategies to recover from this damage. Additionally, alterations in MT ultrastructure (density, spacing, and twisting) (Fig. 5B7–9) and MT stability (Fig. 5A4, 5) were also restored to control levels within 24 h, reinforcing the idea that neurons can reorganize their MTs, regain stability, and recover from the effects of 5% strain within 24 h after compression.

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying this recovery, we profiled the transcriptome and performed a DE analysis between a shorter (6 h) and a longer (24 h) time point after compression, followed by a gene GOEA. We found that the GO term with the highest enrichment score was Ras protein signal transduction (GO:0007265) (Fig. 6A). This analysis indicates that mechanical insult upregulates the expression of GEFs acting on Ras (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Fig. 10), leading to the restoration of physiological levels of active Ras at 24 h post-stimulation (Fig. 6C2).

In conclusion, neurons respond differently to repeated mechanical stimuli depending on the intensity of the applied compression (Fig. 7). At high strain, neurons experience irreparable structural damage leading to cell death, highlighting their inability to activate effective repair mechanisms due to severe structural failures. However, under lower-intensity stimuli, neurons exhibit greater resilience. Specifically, low strain does not cause significant damage, as neurons promptly activate protective mechanisms—such as post-translational tubulin modifications—to stabilize MT structure. At intermediate strain, although MTs are initially compromised, neurons appear capable of restoring MT density and organization over time by promoting the reactivation of the Ras pathway. Methods

Animals

All mice were used in accordance with protocols approved by the Italian Ministry of Public Health and the local Ethical Committee of the University of Pisa (39E1C.N.5Q7), in strict compliance with European Directive 2010/63/EU. C57BL/6J mice were housed in a controlled environment (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 5% humidity) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and had ad libitum access to food and water.

Cell cultures

Post-natal (P3) mouse pups of both sexes were sacrificed by decapitation, and 30 DRGs were collected from each mouse in DPBS (Gibco, #14190-094) supplemented with 6.5 mg/ml D-glucose (Sigma, #G7021). DRG neuron dissociation was performed as described by ref. 36. Briefly, the cells were digested in DPBS containing 1.8 mg/ml D-glucose, 3 mg/ml Dispase II (Sigma, #D4693), and 0.3 mg/ml Collagenase from Clostridium histolyticum (Sigma, #C7657). Subsequently, the cells were incubated in DPBS with 0.52 mg/ml trypsin inhibitor from Glycine max (Sigma, #T9003) and 0.08 mg/ml Deoxyribonuclease I from bovine pancreas (Sigma, #DN25).

Mechanical dissociation was carried out using a fire-polished glass Pasteur pipette, and the cell suspension was passed through a 40 µm cell strainer (Corning, #352340). DRGs were then pelleted and resuspended in culture medium consisting of Neurobasal-A (Gibco, #12349015), 2% B27 (Gibco, #17504-044), 1% GlutaMAX (Gibco, #35050-038), and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, #15140-122), supplemented with 100 ng/ml NGF (Sigma, #N5415) and 2.5 µM AraC (Sigma, #C1768). The cells were seeded into XONA chambers (200,000 cells per chamber).

After 2 h, a chemical gradient of NGF (100 ng/ml in the axonal compartment and 50 ng/ml in the soma compartment) was established to promote axonal growth within the axonal compartment.

Device for application of cycles of mechanical compression

The device for applying mechanical stress consists of 2 µm screws that allow movement of the clamps holding the elastic substrate. Once the clamp position is set, the cartridges can be unscrewed while maintaining the designated distance between the clamps. The micro-stretcher was mechanically calibrated by placing two fiducial marks on the PDMS substrate and measuring their displacement during controlled stretching. The measured strain values (9.78 ± 0.53%, 5.82 ± 0.29%, and 2.31 ± 1.03% for nominal 10%, 5.0%, and 2.5% inputs, respectively, from three replicates) confirmed linear and reproducible deformation behavior.

First, 800 µm-thick PDMS pieces (Limitless Shielding, #PDMS800), measuring 23 × 29 mm2, were sterilized with 70% ethanol (EtOH), followed by 30 min of UV light exposure. Each piece was fixed horizontally in the clamps of the cartridge positioned at the resting state (L0). The PDMS was then elongated to the desired percentage (pre-stretched position), and an XONA microfluidic chamber (XONA, #RD150) was attached to it. Each cartridge was subsequently removed from the device and stored in a Petri dish.

PDMS coating was performed within the XONA chamber using 10 µg/ml Laminin (Sigma, #L2020) and 0.5 mg/ml PLL (Sigma, #P4707). The coating was incubated overnight (o/n) at 37 °C.

Cyclic compression protocol

Three experimental groups were considered: the control group (CTRL), where cells were grown on PDMS; the sham group (SHAM), where cells were grown on pre-stretched PDMS loaded into the cartridge but not subjected to compression cycles; and the compressed group (COMPR), where cells were grown on pre-stretched PDMS loaded into the cartridge and subjected to 20 compression cycles at DIV1.

At DIV1, XONA chambers were detached, and each cartridge designated for compression was secured into the device. Twenty cycles of 2.5%, 5%, or 10% compression were applied at a rate of 0.42%/s. During each cycle, the PDMS transitioned from the pre-stretched position to the resting position, compressing the cells, and then returned to the pre-stretched position, where the cells were no longer compressed.

The strain of compression was calculated using the following formula:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\mathrm{Strain}}}( \% )=\Delta L/L0$$\end{document}Strain(%)=ΔL/L0

In this equation, L₀ represents the initial length of the PDMS in its resting position before pre-stretching, which was consistent across all applied percentages of strain, and ΔL is the change in length applied during pre-stretching.

Protein extraction and ELISA assay

ELISA assays were conducted using the RhoA G-LISA Activation Assay Kit (Cytoskeleton, #BK124), the Total RhoA ELISA Assay Kit (Cytoskeleton, #BK150), and the Ras G-LISA Activation Assay Kit (Cytoskeleton, #BK131). Proteins were extracted at 30 min and 24 h post-compression. For each group, lysates from three XONA chambers were pooled, quantified, frozen, and used for each assay. Before performing the assays, protein concentrations for each replicate were equalized to 0.3 mg/ml for the Ras activation assay and 0.4 mg/ml for the Rho activation assay. Protein extraction, quantification, and ELISA assays were performed according to the respective kit instructions.

RNA extraction and sequencing

The experiment was conducted on the sham group, the compressed group at 6 h post-compression, and the compressed group at 24 h post-compression. RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Kit (Qiagen, #74034) from eight replicates, resulting in 24 RNA extracts. Eighteen samples that passed the quality check (QC) were selected for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (RNA integrity number, RIN > 9). QC and RNA-seq were performed by Macrogen Europe (Amsterdam). Sequencing was conducted using the NovaSeqX platform.

The RNA library was prepared with poly(A) selection and paired-end (PE) sequencing, with a read length of 150 bp and 15 Gb of data generated per sample. The reference genome and mouse annotation used were Ensembl GRCm39. BAM data, gene counting, and DE analysis were performed on the Galaxy platform (https://usegalaxy.eu/) using the BWA-MEM, featureCounts, and DESeq2 tools, respectively. FastQ files and gene counts are available in the GEO repository (GSE286272).

GO and GOEA were performed using the PANTHER Overrepresentation Test (DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.7942786). The analyzed list corresponded to Mus musculus (all genes in the database), using the Fisher test with FDR correction for GO biological processes.

Immunostaining

Before fixation, the PDMS sheets attached to the cartridges were returned to their resting state. Cells were fixed with 2% PFA and 4% sucrose, permeabilized for 10 min with 0.5% Triton X-100 in DPBS, blocked for 1 h in blocking solution (0.3% Triton X-100 in DPBS with 5% serum), and incubated o/n at 4 °C in antibody solution (0.2% Triton X-100 in DPBS with 3% serum) containing the primary antibodies diluted as follows: TUBBIII (Sigma-Aldrich, #T8578, 1:500), acetylated α-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, #T7451, 1:400), tyrosinated α-tubulin (Abcam, #ab6160, 1:400), α-tubulin (Cell Signaling, #9661, 1:400), aCasp3 (Cell Signaling, #9661), phospho-GSK3β Ser9 (Invitrogen #MA514873) and GSK3β (Cell Signalling, #9832).

The cells were then incubated for 1 h with secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A11029, #A21244, #A11006, #R6393, #A11011, 1:500) and Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, #H3570, 1:500).

LIVE/DEAD assay

The LIVE/DEAD™ Viability/Cytotoxicity Assay (Invitrogen, #L32250) was performed 6 h after the compression cycles. Culture medium containing 0.25 µM Sytox and 2 µM Calcein was incubated with the cells for 30 min at room temperature (RT). After two washes with DPBS, the cells were observed under a confocal microscope.

Fluorescent imaging

Fluorescent images were captured using a laser scanning confocal microscope (Nikon A1 Eclipse Ti or Nikon Confocal Microscope AX). For LIVE/DEAD analysis, images were acquired at 10× magnification. For morphological and apoptosis analysis, 20× magnification was used, while 60× magnification was employed for microtubule stability analysis. Images were acquired at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels, utilizing a 405 nm laser (425–475 nm emission filter), a 640 nm laser (666–738 nm emission filter), a 488 nm laser (500–550 nm emission filter), or a 561 nm laser (570–620 nm emission filter). Images (and associated acquisition parameters) can be downloaded in the original format from the ZENODO repository (10.5281/zenodo.17700876).

Sample preparation for TEM

For the ultrastructure analysis, DRGs were treated as monolayer, as described in refs. 35,67. Briefly, cells were fixed in 1.5% Glutaraldehyde in Sodium Cacodylate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) for 1 h at RT, rinsed, and post-fixed in a solution of reduced osmium tetroxide (1% OsO4 and 1% K3Fe (CN)6) in Sodium Cacodylate buffer. After further rinses, cells were stained for 1 h with a homemade staining solution68 diluted 1:10 (v/v) in 20% EtOH. Subsequently, cells were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series and embedded in epoxy resin (Epoxy embedding medium kit, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), which was then baked for 48 h at 60 °C. After resin polymerization, the PDMS was peeled off, the embedded neurons were mounted on supports, and sectioned using a UC ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a 35° diamond knife (Diatome Ltd, Switzerland). Sections were cut perpendicularly to the direction of compression, and 90 nm sections were collected on 300-mesh copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences, #G300Cu). The samples were observed using a Zeiss Libra 120 Plus TEM, operating at 120 kV and equipped with a column omega filter and a 16-bit CCD camera (2kx2k, bottom-mounted; Zeiss).

Image analysis

The “ImageJ” software was used to analyze the images. The LIVE/DEAD assay was employed to assess cell survival. The percentage ratio of the integrated density of Sytox fluorescence (dead cells) to Calcein fluorescence (live cells) was used as an indicator of the ratio between dead and live cells.

Apoptosis was evaluated as the ratio between the number of cells positive for both aCasp3 and Hoechst staining to the total number of nuclei stained by Hoechst. For each image, a Z-stack projection was generated before counting the nuclei using the “Analyze Particles” function in Fiji software, while aCasp3-positive cells were manually counted using the “Cell Counter” function.

The NeuronJ plugin69 was used to evaluate axon length and buckling. Axons (stained for -tubulin) were traced from their tip to their exit point from the channels to measure their length. Buckling was estimated for each axon over a 100 µm distance between two points (see scheme in Fig. 3A4), calculated as the ratio of the axon’s linear length to the 100 µm distance.

TEM analysis

Ultrastructural analysis was conducted on axons aligned with the direction of compression. Axonal degeneration was defined by the presence of at least two of the following characteristics: mitochondrial alterations, absence of microtubules, and electron-dense cytoplasm with no defined ultrastructures. For soma degeneration, the presence of at least two of the following characteristics was used as criteria: mitochondrial alterations, altered chromatin and nuclear envelope, and electron-dense cytoplasm with no defined ultrastructures. To measure MT density within the axons, the axon diameter d was firstly measured as the distance between the distal point of the axonal membrane in the longitudinal cross-section of the axon. Then, the number n of microtubules intersecting the diameter was counted, and their density was calculated as n/d.

Microtubule twisting was recorded if MTs were found overlapping within a ROI of 700 nm along the axon length (longitudinal section). The region was chosen to be free of organelles and with clearly visible MTs. The MT inter-distance was calculated as the distance between the axes of two adjacent microtubules.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data were analyzed and plotted using GraphPad 9.5.1. Results are presented as scatter plot (mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM)) or data distribution (violin plot). The D’Agostino & Pearson normality test, Shapiro-Wilk normality test, and Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality test were employed to assess data distribution normality. For data following a normal distribution, unpaired t-tests were used, whereas for non-normally distributed data, the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test was performed. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. In the axon buckling analysis, outliers have been removed.

Sample size was calculated using G*Power (α = 0.05, power = 0.8), resulting in n = 120 axons for axon length (effect size: 0.35), n = 90 axons for axon buckling (effect size: 0.4), n = 65 axons for MT stability (effect size: 0.5), and n = 50 axons for MT ultrastructural analysis (effect size: 0.55). These axons were derived from at least four biological replicates for all measurements, except for TEM analysis, where the biological replicates were two. The sample size for RNAseq n = 6 was calculated by RNASeqpower (Bioconductor) (depth = 10, cv = 0.3, effect = 2, α = 0.05, power = 0.8).
