Exploration of prognosis and immune infiltration characteristics in glioblastoma multiforme based on lipid metabolism related genes
Authors: Peng-Cheng Li, Hua-Xuan Chen, De-Bo Yun, Qian-Yi Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesLipid metabolism reprogramming was a key adaptive mechanism for rapid proliferation of tumor cells. It affected tumor progression by altering the expression of lipid metabolism related genes (LMRGs). The aim of this study was to identify novel LMRGs-related prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets for glioblastoma multiforme (GBM).MethodsThe clinical and transcriptomic data of GBM patients were extracted from the TCGA database. the LMRGs were obtained from the MSigDB database, and screened for prognostic related genes. The immune cell infiltration status was evaluated using ESTIMATE, QUANTISEQ, CIBERSORT, MCPcounter algorithm and ssGSEA. The relevant signaling pathways were screened by the GO, KEGG, GSEA and GSVA analyses. The hub genes with prognostic value were screened through LASSO-Cox regression analysis. The distribution of the hub genes in GBM cells was determined by the TISCH2 database. Use DepMap database to demonstrate functional dependence of hub genes. Finally, validation was performed using the GEO dataset.ResultsWe divided GBM patients into two clusters, with cluster 2 (C2) had a worse prognosis than cluster 1 (C1) ( P = 0.0269 ), and C2 had higher abundance of M2 macrophages, NK cells and dendritic cells (DCs). 40 prognostic LMRGs were mainly enriched in lipid metabolism pathways such as steroid and phospholipid through GO/KEGG analysis. GSVA showed significant enrichment of phospholipid and steroid metabolism in C1. Four hub genes, INPP5F (hazard ratio > 1), PTEN, MTMR2 and IDI1, were identified through LASSO-Cox regression. Single-cell sequencing data showed a high proportion of oligodendrocytes in INPP5F-positive cells, and DepMap validation showed peak gene effects of INPP5F and IDI1 < 0, MTMR2 and PTEN>0.ConclusionWe have identified four prognostic LMRGs: INPP5F ( risk gene ), PTEN, MTMR2 and IDI1, which provide new insights into the mechanisms of GBM progression, as well as new prognostic biomarkers and immunotherapy targets.

Introduction

Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) originates from the glial cells in the central nervous system. It is the most prevalent intracranial malignancy and is characterized by high aggressiveness and recurrence rate. Despite significant advances in treatment modalities, the prognosis of GBM remains poor, with a median survival duration of approximately 15 months [1, 2]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify new prognostic biomarkers for GBM in order to devise suitable personalized treatment plans and improve clinical outcomes [3].

Lipid metabolism reprogramming is a key adaptive mechanism for tumor cells to sustain their rapid proliferative rates, and has been recognized as one of the hallmarks of cancer [4, 5]. It involves changes in the expression of lipid metabolism-related genes (LMRGs) that regulate the synthesis, breakdown, and transport of lipids, and plays a crucial role in tumor progression and the remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) [6–8]. Recent studies have also revealed the prognostic potential of LMRGs in multiple tumors, including breast cancer [9], ovarian carcinomas [10], lung adenocarcinoma [11], pancreatic cancer [12], hepatocellular carcinoma [13], renal cell carcinoma [14], and so on. Thus, lipid metabolism pathways can be explored as promising targets for novel cancer therapies [15]. However, the correlation between expression levels of LMRGs, and the immune landscape and prognosis of GBM is unclear.

In this study, we first employed various bioinformatics methods to evaluate the impact of LMRGs on the immune infiltration characteristics and survival of GBM patients. Our findings provide new insights into the potential molecular mechanisms underlying GBM progression, along with novel prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Data retrieval

Clinical information and RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) data of GBM patients were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). LMRGs were obtained from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB, http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb) [16, 17]. After excluding missing data and duplicated values, the RNA-Seq data were converted to the log2(x + 1) format. This study complied with the published guidelines of TCGA, and ethical approval and informed consent of the patients were waived.

Identification of molecular subgroups and immune analyses

The R package “survival” was used to integrate survival time, survival status, and gene expression data, and the prognostic significance of each gene was evaluated using Cox regression. The genes related to GBM prognosis were obtained by setting the logrank p value less than 0.05. Cluster analysis was performed using “ConsensusClusterPlus”, which was a class discovery tool with confidence assessment and project tracking capabilities [18]. Meanwhile, Using agglomerative PAM clustering with a 1-pearson correlation distances and resampling 80% of the samples for 10 repetitions. The optimal number of clusters was determined based on the offline area of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve and the average consistency within the clustering group. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the prognostic genes identified by univariate Cox regression to verify the clustering results.

The StromalScore, ImmuneScore and ESTIMATEScore of each patient were calculated using the R package “ESTIMATE” (Estimation of Stromal and Immune cells in Malignant Tumour tissues using Expression data). The degree of immune cell infltration was estaimated using the QUANTISEQ (QUANTItative Immune cell SEquence), CIBERSORT (Cell-type Identification By Estimating Relative Subsets Of RNA Transcripts) and MCPcounter (Microenvironment Cell Populations counter) algorithms, and calculated and visualized with “IOBR” R package. Furthermore, the infiltration levels of 14 immune cell types were quantified by single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) using the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) R package, and the difference in immune infiltration between the two clusters was calculated.

Functional enrichment analysis and PPI network analysis

The R packages “ggplot2” and “clusterProfiler” were used for Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses. GSEA was performed using “clusterProfiler” and GSEA software version 4.3.3 with h.all-v7.4.symbols.gmt and c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.symbols.gmt as the reference gene sets [19]. The significantly enriched pathways were identified on the basis of normalized enrichment score (|NES| >1), nominal (NOM) p value < 0.05, and false discovery rate (FDR) adjusted q-value < 0.25. GSVA was performed to analyze the differences in signaling pathways between two clusters using the “GSVA” R package. In addition, the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) database ( https://cn.string-db.org/) was used to predict the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network and identify possible relationships (combined score >0.95). The PPI network was analyzed and visualized using the Cytoscape software version 3.7.2 ( degree cutoff = 2, node score cutoff = 0.2, k-core = 2 and max. depth = 100 ).

LASSO-Cox regression analysis

The LMRGs-related genes with prognostic relevance were screened by LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) Cox regression analysis using the R package glmnet. To avoid over-fitting, the coefficients for LASSO modeling were determined by 10-fold cross-validation, with the minimum lambda defined as the optimal value. The patients were divided into the high-risk and low-risk groups, and their prognoses were compared by Cox regression analysis. A predictive nomogram for GBM was established using clinical data and the prognostic genes, and a calibration curve was plotted to compare the predicted and actual probabilities.

Validation of hub genes

The differential expression of these hub genes between the GBM tissues and normal tissues was confirmed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis (GEPIA) database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/), and the correlation between the hub genes and immune infiltration was determined using the Tumor Immune Estimation Resource (TIMER, https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/) database. The expression of hub genes in different GBM cell types were evaluated from the Tumor Immune Single-cell Hub 2 (TISCH2) database ( http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/), which provided pre-processed and annotated single-cell RNA-seq datasets with detailed tumor microenvironment classifications. The GSE131928 and GSE148842 datasets were used to analyze the cellular distribution of hub genes. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots and heatmaps were generated to visualize hub gene expression across different annotated cell types.

GBM were divided into four molecular subtypes: Proneural, Neural, Classical and Mesenchymal by using by abnormalities in PDGFRA, IDH1, EGFR and NF1 [20]. Verify the correlation between these hub genes and GBM subtypes. Subsequently, the screened prognostic hub genes were compared with GBM established biomarkers ( IDH mutation, MGMT methylation and EGFR amplification ) to validate the accuracy and potential advantages of these hub genes in predicting patient survival.

DepMap database validation hub genes

The hub genes were identified through the Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) database ( https://depmap.org/portal/ ). This database used CRISPR-Cas9 technology to systematically knock out each gene in 930 cancer cell lines [21]. It is possible to accurately identify the importance of one gene in a large number of different cancer cell lines and cancer types [22]. By integrating transcriptome data from 99 GBM patients in DepMap database with CRISPER-Cas9 screening on 67 GBM cell lines, and visualized with “ggplot2”R package. we used a strategy based on the fitness score of cell lines to determine the gene effect values of hub genes. Thus, the impact of core genes on tumor cell growth can be more reliably evaluated, enhancing the stability of prognostic analysis.

Validation of hub genes in GSE13041 (GPL8300) and GSE13041 (GPL96) dataset

We used mRNAs profiles from the Gene Expression Omnibus ( GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/ ) Database as an external validation set. Based on the median risk score, 49 GBM patients in the GSE13041 ( GPL8300 ) dataset and 191 GBM patients in the GSE13041 ( GPL96 ) dataset were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups. A nomogram was established based on clinical information and prognosis LMRGs, and a calibration curve was drawn to evaluate the predictive efficiency of the nomogram.

Statistical analyses

Discontinuous data have been presented as number/percentages and continuous data as mean ± standard deviation (SD). And normality tests were performed on gene expression data from three datasets: TCGA - GBM, GSE13041 ( GPL8300 ) and GSE13042 ( GPL96 ). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare two groups. The correlation between two groups was calculated by Spearman’s correlation analysis. The Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank test were used to estimate survival. A Cox proportional hazards model was used for univariate and multivariate analyses. Multiple groups were compared using Analysis of Variance (ANOVA). All statistical analyses were conducted by R software (version 4.3.2). P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of LMRG-based clusters

The flowchart of this study is shown in Fig. 1. We identified 3280 prognostic genes through univariable Cox analysis and obtained 757 LMRGs from the GSEA database. As shown in the Venn diagram in Fig. 2A, there were 40 overlapping genes from the two sets. Using the consensus clustering approach, we divided the GBM patients in TCGA dataset into two clusters based on the expression profiles of the 40 prognostic LMRGs (Table 1). The optimal clustering stability was identified when K = 2, and 100 patients were grouped into cluster 1 (C1) and 72 patients into cluster 2 (C2) (Fig. 2B-F). In the PCA, the data of the 172 GBM patients were distinct, and the difference between the two clusters was relatively large (Fig. 2G). The expression levels of the prognostic LMRGs showed differences between C1 and C2 (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, the overall survival rate of C1 patients was higher than that of C2 patients (P = 0.026; Fig. 2I). Taken together, the LMRGs classified the GBM patients into two clusters with different prognoses.

LMRGs-based clusters in GBM have distinct immune characteristics

According to the ESTIMATE algorithm, C2 patients had significantly higher Immune Score (P = 0.02) compared to the C1 patients, whereas the ESTIMATESCore (P = 0.17) and StromalScore (P = 0.98) were similar in both clusters (Fig. 3A). In addition, the QUANTISEQ algorithm indicated significantly higher abundance of NK cells (P < 0.01), M2 macrophages (P <0.001) and DCs (P<0.01) in C2 compared to C1, and a higher abundance of Tregs in C1 (P< 0.0001). However, no significant difference was observed in the abundance of B cells (P = 0.40, Fig. 3B). The CIBERSORT algorithm indicated that compared to C1, the abundance of plasma cells and macrophages M1 was significantly higher in C2 (P < 0.01, Fig. 3C). The MCPcounter algorithm showed that compared to C1, the abundance of monocytic lineages (P = 0.02) in C2 was significantly higher, while the abundance of cytotoxic lymphocytes (P < 0.01) and fibroblasts (P = 0.02) was significantly lower (Fig. 3D). Finally, the results of ssGSEA revealed significant enrichment of the activated DCs (aDCs), CD56bright cells, Th17 cells, and Tregs in C2 relative to C1 (Fig. 3E). Taken together, the molecular subtypes of GBM stratified on the basis of LMRGs have distinct immune landscapes.

Functional enrichment and PPI network analyses

To further explore the pathways potentially regulated by LMRGs, we identified and functionally annotated the 40 prognostic LMRGs. GO analysis revealed that the LMRGs were enriched in biological processes like steroid metabolism, phospholipid metabolism, glycerolipid metabolism, and regulation of lipid metabolic process, along with various molecular functions and cellular components (Fig. 4A-C). Furthermore, KEGG analysis showed significant enrichment of signaling pathways associated with the lipid metabolism, including the sphingolipid signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, cholesterol metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (Fig. 4D). The differentially expressed pathways between the two clusters were then identified through GSEA and GSVA. The results of GSEA showed that β-alanine metabolism, tyrosine metabolism, WNT signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway and HEDGEHOG signaling pathway were significantly enriched in C1 (Fig. 4E), while allograft rejection and primary immunodeficiency signaling pathways were enriched in C2 (Fig. 4F). As shown in the GSVA heatmap in Fig. 4G, phospholipid metabolism, organophosphate metabolism, lipid metabolism and steroid metabolic process were significantly enriched in C1, while small molecule biosynthesis and organic acid metabolism were enriched in C2. Furthermore, the PPI network of the LMRGs revealed that 8 hub genes may affect the progression of GBM by regulating lipid metabolism (Fig. 4H).

LMRGs are potential prognostic markers for GBM

The 40 prognostic LMRGs were next evaluated by LASSO-Cox regression analysis, and 10 genes were filtered with optimal λ value of 0.09 (Fig. 5A and B). The GBM patients were classified into the high-risk and low-risk groups based on the expression levels of the candidate genes. As shown in Fig. 5C, patients in the low-risk group overexpressed PTEN, MTMR2, IDI1 and IDI2, while INPP5F, AKR1B1 and FHL2 were overexpressed in the high-risk group. Furthermore, patients in the low-risk group had a better overall survival rate than those in the high-risk group (Fig. 5D). Through multivariate Cox analysis, we identified four genes that were significantly related to prognosis, including INPP5F, PTEN, MTMR2 and IDI1 of which INPP5F was the risk gene with hazard ratio exceeding 1 (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, all four genes were confirmed to be independent prognostic factors through univariate Cox regression analyses (Fig. 5F). We also observed a significant correlation between age and survival rate (Fig. 5G and H). A prognostic nomogram was constructed using clinical features and the expression levels of prognostic genes (Fig. 5I). As indicated in the calibration curve in Fig. 5J and the C-index curve in 5 K, the nomogram predicted the 1- and 2-year prognosis of GBM patients with good accuracy and reliability.

Identification of hub genes associated with prognostic LMRGs

Using the GEPIA website, we confirmed that the expression levels of these hub genes were significantly different between normal tissues and GBM tissues (Fig. 6A-D). Furthermore, INPP5F (Fig. 6E) and IDI1 (Fig. 6F) were positively associated with CD8⁺T cells and neutrophils, and PTEN (Fig. 6G) and MTMR2 (Fig. 6H) were associated with increased proportion of neutrophils and DCs.

We also explored the immune landscape of GBM at the single-cell level using the GSE131928 (Fig. 7A) and GSE148842 (Fig. 7B) datasets. The INPP5F and MTMR2 positive cells were mainly associated with oligodendrocytes(Fig. 7C and E), and PTEN was widely expressed in the monocytes/macrophages (Fig. 7D). In addition, the CD8⁺T cells comprised a significant proportion of the IDI1 positive cells (Fig. 7F).

Among the four molecular subtypes of GBM (Fig. 8A-C), IDI1 was highly expressed in the Neural subtype and the difference is statistically significant (p < 0.01). INPP5F and PTEN were highly expressed in the Neural subtype, while MTMR2 was highly expressed in the Classical subtype. Therefore, it can be concluded that the four prognostic LMRGs were more correlated with the Neural subtype. In comparison with the established biomarkers of IDH mutation (IDH1), MGMT promoter methylation (MGMT) and EGFR amplification (EGFR) in GBM, it was found that the AUC value of INPP5F was greater than 0.5 (Fig. 8D), indicating that it has good accuracy in predicting the prognosis of GBM patients. We verified the four hub genes in the DepMap database at the genomic level and transcriptomic level. The ridge plot showed that their gene effect values were mainly concentrated between − 0.5 and 0.5. it was found that the gene effect peaks of INPP5F and IDI1 were less than 0, while the gene effect peaks of MTMR2 and PTEN were greater than 0 (Fig. 8E).

Discussion

The role of lipid metabolism in tumor development and progression has been recognized in recent years. The aberrant expression of LMRGs affects the proliferation and survival of tumor cells, as well as the infiltration and function of immune cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME). For example, the oncogene c-SRC reprograms fatty acid synthesis in glioma cells by regulating LMRGs such as ACSS2 and ACLY, thereby promoting tumor progression [23]. In our study, we identified two LMRGs-related subtypes of GBM that differed significantly in terms of gene expression and prognosis, indicating that LMRGs influence the biological behavior of GBM.

Tumor initiation and development are closely related to changes in the surrounding matrix. In particular, the infiltrating immune cells play a crucial role in tumor growth and immune evasion, and may predict the prognosis of cancer patients [24–26]. The ESTIMATE algorithm is routinely used to quantify immune infiltration in tumor samples and infer the ratio of immune cells to stromal cells [27]. The ESTIMATE scores were significantly lower in C1 compared to C2, indicating less immune cell infiltration and lower immunosuppression in the former. This suggests that high immune infiltration is likely associated with poor prognosis in GBM, which is consistent with previous reports [28]. Furthermore, the C1 patients had fewer immunosuppressive cells, such as B cells, M2 macrophages, and Tregs, and a greater abundance of anti-tumor immune cells such as CD8⁺ T cells, M1 macrophages, NK cells, Th17 cells, etc. Therefore, the TME in the C1 patients appeared to be more conducive to mounting an anti-tumor immune response and preventing immune evasion, eventually leading to favorable prognosis. On the other hand, although NK cells and DCs were more abundant in the TME of C2 patients, their anti-tumor functions could be affected by the immunosuppressive M2 macrophages. Studies show that the immunosuppressive factors secreted by M2 macrophages, such as IL-10 and TGF-β, can inhibit the activity of NK cells and DCs through the STAT3 pathway [29, 30]. In addition, M2 macrophages induce PD-L1 upregulation on tumor cells, which can directly inhibit T cells and NK cells through the PD-1/PD-L1 axis [31]. Furthermore, M2 macrophages, NK cells, and DCs regulate tumor progression and therapeutic response through bidirectional and dynamic interactions [32]. Therefore, we surmise that the abundance of M2 macrophages in the TME of C2 patients may be a key determinant of their poor prognosis. A deeper understanding of the immune environment of GBM can help in the development of optimal immunotherapy strategies.

Lipid metabolism significantly affects the proliferation, invasion and treatment resistance of tumor cells by regulating energy supply, membrane structure remodeling, and activation of signaling pathways [33]. The prognostic LMRGs identified in this study were significantly enriched in pathways regulating steroid metabolism, phospholipid metabolism and glycerolipid metabolism, which provide cells with energy and raw materials required for biosynthesis. For example, the metabolism of glycerophospholipids generates phospholipids for the synthesis of cell membranes, which is crucial for the rapidly proliferating tumor cells. The products of lipid metabolism, such as fatty acids, may promote cell proliferation by activating cyclin dependent kinases (CDKs) [34]. For instance, aberrant lipid metabolism in glioma cells is associated with enhanced tumor growth and invasion [35]. In addition, changes in steroid metabolism may activate tyrosine kinases, promote downstream PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathways, and accelerate tumor cell proliferation [36, 37]. Abnormal cholesterol metabolism can promote tumor proliferation by activating the Hedgehog and mTORC1 signaling pathways, and enhance metastasis through epithelial mesenchymal transition (EMT) [38, 39]. Therefore, a greater understanding of the mechanisms of abnormal lipid metabolism in GBM can help identify key enzymes and potential therapeutic targets.

In the LASSO-Cox regression analysis, INPP5F was identified as the only prognostic biomarker associated with lipid metabolism and HR value >1. INPP5F encodes inositol polyphosphate 5-phosphatase, which maintains the stability and fluidity of cell membranes, and influences cell signaling by regulating phosphatidylinositol metabolism [40]. The abnormal expression of INPP5F is closely related to various pathological conditions. For example, INPP5F dysregulation in hepatocellular carcinoma cells activates the ASPH-mediated oncogenic Notch signaling pathway, leading to poor prognosis [41]. In addition, high expression of INPP5F is a risk factor for chronic lymphocytic leukemia [42]. In this study, low expression or absence of INPP5F was associated with poor prognosis in glioma patients, which is consistent with the findings of Kim et al. [43].

We speculate that it can be explained from two aspects: PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway regulation and mitochondrial function & mitophagy. Specifically, INPP5F hydrolyzes PIP3 (phosphatidylinositol-3,4,5-trisphosphate), the critical lipid second messenger generated by PI3K. Loss or reduced expression of INPP5F leads to accumulation of PIP3. Elevated PIP3 hyperactivates the oncogenic PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling axis, promoting glioma cell survival, proliferation, migration, invasion, and resistance to therapy. This positions INPP5F as a tumor suppressor counteracting PI3K signaling, akin to PTEN but with distinct regulation and potentially different substrate preferences [43–45]. After that, INPP5F localized to mitochondria. Its phosphatase domain negatively regulates PINK1-Parkin mediated mitophagy (the selective autophagic clearance of damaged mitochondria). Dysregulated mitophagy contributes to cancer progression. INPP5F loss could lead to increased mitophagy under stress, potentially aiding tumor cell adaptation, metabolic reprogramming, or survival in the harsh tumor microenvironment [46, 47].

While technically challenging, INPP5F expression holds promise as a valuable prognostic biomarker and a potential predictive biomarker for PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway inhibitors. Its independent prognostic value, potentially additive to markers like IDH mutation or MGMT methylation status, warrants validation in prospective cohorts. Assessment (IHC, mRNA profiling) could help stratify patient risk in clinical practice. Meanwhile, GBM with low INPP5F exhibit hyperactive PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling. These tumors might be inherently more sensitive to inhibitors targeting this pathway (e.g., AKT inhibitors like Capivasertib, mTORC1/2 inhibitors, PI3K inhibitors). INPP5F status could guide patient selection for clinical trials involving these agents. Exploiting the synthetic lethality concept to develop novel, targeted therapies specifically for INPP5F-deficient GBM, offering hope for more effective treatment of this devastating disease. Furthermore, INPP5F expression was positively correlated with the infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, which were key effector cells in anti-tumor immune responses [48]. Therefore, low expression of INPP5F in GBM tissues may lead to reduced infiltration of CD8⁺ T cells, resulting in worse prognosis. Further research was crucial to fully elucidate its mechanisms and translate this knowledge into clinical utility.

Our study has some limitations that ought to be considered. First, the data for this study were retrieved from public databases such as TCGA, and its retrospective nature adds an inherent bias. Second, the limited sample size may affect the accuracy and reliability of the results, and more research samples were needed to confirm our findings. In addition, we also included “treated tumors” during sample selection, which may have affected our results and led us to miss critical molecular markers. Finally, our conclusion is based solely on bioinformatics analysis and has not been confirmed through experimental and clinical studies. Therefore, further research is needed to validate the prognostic value of LMRGs in GBM.

Conclusion

We identified two LMRGs-based subtypes of GBM with different prognoses and immune status, and identified four prognostic LMRGs, including INPP5F, PTEN, MTMR2 and IDI1, of which INPP5F was the risk gene. The nomogram consisting of clinical features and the key LMRGs accurately predicted the 1-year and 2-year prognosis of GBM patients. This study provides novel insights into the mechanisms of GBM prognosis based on LMGRs, and identifies potential prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets.
