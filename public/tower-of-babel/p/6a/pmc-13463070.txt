Single cell spatial transcriptomics track the evolutionary hierarchy and microenvironment remodeling during breast carcinoma invasion
Authors: Di Wang, Qichen Dai, Jingwen Guo, Dan Li, Changyuan Guo, Peiqing Ma, Feng Wen, Xiangyu Tong, Changhao Gong, Han Cheng, Meng Li, Ranjiaxi Wang, Jianlin Liu, Yingying Feng, Fengpu Fan, Xiaoqian Shi, Qian Zhang, Xingmei Shu, Yu Sun, Xunan Shen, Ziqing Deng, Jiaqi Liu, Fei Ma, Yipeng Wang, Lin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The progression from ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) to invasive breast carcinoma (IBC) critically determines patient outcomes, yet its mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Integrating single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptomics, and genomics across 28 patients with synchronous DCIS and IBC, we delineate the spatial-molecular hierarchy of this transition. Invasion is primarily driven by clonal expansion of pre-existing DCIS subclones, emphasizing transcriptional reprogramming and tumor microenvironment (TME) remodeling over acquisition of additional driver alterations. IBC cells exhibit pronounced epithelial-mesenchymal transition and metabolic reprogramming. We uncover dynamic TME remodeling at the invasive front, identifying key ligand–receptor interactions (e.g., PPIA-BSG, MDK-LRP1, CXCL12-CXCR4) facilitating basement membrane disruption, angiogenesis and immunosuppression. Deconvolution of basement membrane breach reveals four molecularly defined stages (NMFT1–NMFT4) with progressively worsening patient survival. This study establishes a unified spatial-molecular atlas of DCIS-IBC progression, highlighting clonal expansion, transcriptional plasticity and TME remodeling as key drivers of invasion.

Introduction

Breast cancer represents the foremost cause of mortality and health concerns among women. Based on the extent of tumor cell invasion, breast cancer is broadly categorized into ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive breast carcinoma (IBC), each associated with distinct clinical outcomes1–3. DCIS, a non-invasive precursor to breast cancer, is typically confined within the ductal-lobular structures of the breast, separated from adjacent stromal tissue by an intact layer of basal-myoepithelial cells and a basement membrane1,4. The widespread adoption of mammographic screenings has led to an increased detection rate of non-invasive ductal carcinoma5. While a majority of individuals with DCIS achieve favorable outcomes with surgery and adjunctive therapies, a significant proportion (20–50%) progress to invasive disease6. This transition from a localized to an invasive tumor marks a critical turning point in breast cancer progression, drastically reducing patient survival7,8. However, the underlying evolutionary mechanism driving this progression and the precise dynamics of microenvironmental remodeling during invasion remain to be elucidated.

Two main models have been proposed to explain the evolutionary relationship between DCIS and IBC. The parallel evolution model posits that DCIS and IBC arise independently, with no shared genomic variation9,10. In contrast, the bottleneck evolution model proposes a direct genomic lineage relationship between DCIS and IBC, with both sharing consistent genomic alterations. This model suggests that multiple subclones evolve within DCIS, and those acquiring invasion-related genomic variations breach the basement membrane to form invasive carcinoma10–12. However, due to the inherent heterogeneity of breast cancer, traditional studies using bulk tissue or micro-dissected samples have struggled to precisely delineate the cellular lineage relationships and dynamic microenvironment remodeling processes between DCIS and IBC.

Single-cell sequencing methods have partially overcome the challenge of tumor heterogeneity, but they lack spatial information13–15. While previous studies employing spatially resolved single-cell genome sequencing identified multiclonal invasion between DCIS and IBC, they were limited by the number of cells analyzed and could not provide a comprehensive spatial picture of the complex relationship16. More recently, base-specific DCIS sequencing has enabled the capture of point mutations in different DCIS subclones, but it precludes single-cell genotyping17. To fully comprehend the dynamics of tumor invasion, we need to delineate the expression profiles of different subclones and their interactions with the surrounding microenvironment during the evolutionary process.

In this study, we used single-cell resolution spatial transcriptome technology Stereo-seq18 in conjunction with single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), microdissection-based whole-exome sequencing (WES), and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to dissect the spatial evolutionary relationship between DCIS and IBC, their clonal dynamics, and the associated microenvironment remodeling. We further integrate histopathological analysis to characterize the morphological features of different subclones and their relationship to invasion. This study provides insights into the invasive evolution of breast cancer and its clinicopathological characteristics, laying the foundation for the development of diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for improved breast cancer management.

Results

Spatial landscape of DCIS and IBC at single-cell resolution

In order to investigate the evolutionary relationship between DCIS and IBC in the spatial dimension, as well as the remodeling of their microenvironment, we performed scRNA-seq and single cell resolution spatial transcriptome sequencing (Stereo-seq) onfresh, pathologically validated breast cancer specimens with synchronous DCIS and IBC. 32 samples from 28 patients were subjected to scRNA-seq, with nine also undergoing Stereo-seq (Fig. 1a, Suppl. Data 2). The scRNA-seq data from these 32 samples yielded a total of 341,559 single cells, with an average of 10,673 cells per sample. And 12 distinct cell types were identified based on the expression of their marker genes (Fig. 1b, c). We successfully distinguished between malignant and normal epithelial cells, including the identification of three well-known non-malignant epithelial cell types, namely luminal hormone-responsive (LumHR) cells (PGR, ESR1), luminal secretory (LumSec) cells (KRT15,KRT23), and basal-myoepithelial (Basal) cells (KRT5, KRT14), which were consistent with previous reports13,19 (Fig. 1b, 1c, Suppl. Fig. 1a, 1b). Significant inter-patient heterogeneity in cell type composition was observed across different individuals (Suppl. We performed Stereo-seq on nine samples, initially conducting H&E staining on adjacent sections to delineate tissue regions. Two independent pathologists outlined seven regions, including active hyperplasia, atypical hyperplasia, DCIS, IBC, immune cell aggregation, stromal, and normal tissue (Fig. 1d, Suppl. Fig. 2a). Following ssDNA staining and spatial transcriptome sequencing, we integrated ssDNA images with spatial gene expression patterns to enable single-cell segmentation and analysis of spatial groups (see Methods). A total of 1,767,217 single cells in spatial groups were obtained, with an average of 196,357 cells per sample.

Based on spatial mapping of cell types identified through scRNA-seq, we annotated clusters of spatial single cells (Fig. 1e, f, Suppl. Fig. 2a). Notably, the spatial mapping of all three types of normal epithelial cells identified by scRNA-seq aligned with their known histological locations. Importantly, we could distinguish between DCIS and IBC tumor cells based on their spatial location, highlighting the power of high-resolution spatial transcriptomics. Additionally, the cell composition of spatial transcriptome data more accurately reflected the real cellular composition of tumor tissues20,21. Compared to scRNA-seq data, spatial data showed an increased content of tumor cells, consistent with previous research findings13,22 (Suppl. Fig. 1d, 2b). The results revealed a significant individual heterogeneity in the composition of cell types within each sample, particularly in the ratio of DCIS to invasive carcinoma. Furthermore, distinct cellular phenotypes exhibited markedly characteristic expression of marker genes within the spatial datasets, underscoring the precision of cell type identification. (Suppl. Fig. 2c).

While H&E staining provides valuable histopathological information (Fig. 1d, Suppl. Fig. 2a), emerging researches indicate that it may not fully capture the genetic and transcriptomic variations within a tumor16,17,23. To delineate distinct spatial functional regions and effectively differentiate various ecosystem hubs within breast cancer tissue, particularly in the regions of DCIS and IBC, we conducted a spatial domain analysis of the spatial transcriptomic data using SPACEL based on cell types, cell spatial position, and gene expression profile24. A total of 10 distinct spatial domains (three categories) were identified and annotated based on the spatial position and composition of cell types (Fig. 1f–i, Suppl. Fig. 3a–c). (1) Tumor cell-enriched regions: The DCIS region (CISR) and IBC region (IBCR) were primarily composed DCIS and IBC tumor cells, respectively. Intriguingly, the CISR and IBCR exhibited comparable cellular compositions within the same sample. Notably, samples P15, P16, P22, and P41 exhibited increased immune cell infiltration in both CISR and IBCR compared to other samples (Fig. 1i, Suppl. Fig. 3d). (2) Tumor-adjacent regions: The CISR around region (CISAR), directly surrounding the CISR, showed an increased prevalence of basal-myoepithelial cells and fibroblasts. The IBCR was sequentially surrounded by two regions, the IBCR around region 1 (IBCAR1) and IBCR around region 2 (IBCAR2), characterized by a gradual reduction in invasive tumor cells and a corresponding increase in fibroblasts, macrophages, T cells, and B cells (Fig. 1h, Suppl. Fig. 3b, c), insinuating a potential correlation between these cellular populations and the invasive progression of carcinoma. (3) Other microenvironment regions: We identified regions enriched with immune cells (immune cell enriched region, ICR), mesenchymal cells (mesenchymal cell enriched region, MESR), and a mixture of both (the mixture of mesenchymal and immune cells region, MIMR). An additional region predominantly composed of non-malignant epithelial cells was designated as NMR (non-malignant region). Lastly, regions lacking a defined spatial distribution and cellular composition were classified as undefined regions (UNDR). (Fig. 1h, i, Suppl. Fig. 3b, c).

Compared to traditional pathological partitioning, spatial transcriptomic segmentation enabled a more precise characterization of functional zones within the tumor microenvironment, revealing a higher degree of spatial heterogeneity. Through the examination of spatial correlations between distinct regions, we identified CISAR enveloping the CISR, IBCAR1 and IBCAR2 encircling the IBCR successively. In addition, the ICR was found higher proximity to the CISAR, MMR, and MIMR (Fig. 1j, Suppl. Fig. 3a, c), implying that stromal cells and those within DISAR may engage in more robust interactive responses with immune cells.

Tracing genetic variation in DCIS and IBC tumor cells

To assess the genetic divergences within DCIS and IBC carcinomas cells, we firstly performed inferCNV25–27 analysis on the epithelial cells derived from Stereo-seq and scRNA-seq, which was succeeded by a clustering examination of the tumor cells according to their CNV occurrences to delineate distinct tumor subclones (Fig. 2a–e, Suppl. Fig. 4, Suppl. Data 3). Notably, the DCIS and IBC tumor cells cannot be distinguished solely via gene expression or CNV evaluations. Spatial mapping revealed that most subclones identified though scRNA-seq were present in both DCIS and IBC regions (Fig. 2a, b, Suppl. Fig. 4), insinuating that tumor cells within DCIS and IBC carcinoma zones possess analogous gene expression profiles and genetic variances. To further reconstruct the spatial clonal distribution and the evolutionary trajectory of carcinomas cells, we additionally undertook microdissection of DCIS and IBC regions from 9 Stereo-seq specimens, subjecting all to WES and WGS independently. WGS-based CNV analysis confirmed the results obtained from scRNA-seq and Stereo-seq data (Fig. 2f, Suppl. Fig. 5a, b), validating the precision and reproducibility of Stereo-seq for studying tumor spatial evolution at single-cell resolution. These findings elucidate that DCIS and IBC tumor cells shared CNV incidents, denoting a common progenitor in their early stages, which is consistent with previous studies12,16,17. Certain shared CNV incidents, discernible in all tumor cells of a specimen, likely emerged early during tumorigenesis and may be pivotal to cancer development, such as chr6q deletion in P16, P35, chr8q amplification in P13, P36, P41, chr16q deletion in P13, P22, P35, P36, P41, chr16p amplification in P22, chr22 deletion in P15, chr19q amplification and chr20q amplification in P29, chr14p amplification, chr14q deletion, chr15q amplification, chr17q amplification in P36 (Fig. 2f, Suppl. Fig. 6a–f).

Additionally, Analysis of WES data revealed shared driver gene mutations in DCIS and IBC, including TP53, MYCL, and PIK3CA. Clonal evolution analysis, based on mutation frequencies across different genes, revealed a dominant clone shared by DCIS and IBC, further supporting a common origin (Fig. 2g), which was consistent with previous studies12,16,17. We did not observe a consistent trend of increasing tumor mutation burden (TMB) or clonal heterogeneity from DCIS to IBC (Fig. 2h, i). Furthermore, the clonal composition profiles of DCIS and IBC within the same sample were comparable (Fig. 2j). Interestingly, clonal evolution examinations revealed that the timing of clonal divergence and differentiation varied across specimens, resulting in diverse and complex patterns of tumor clonal evolution (Suppl. Fig. 6a–f).

Clonal lineage tracing reveals diverse evolutionary trajectories

To elucidate the clonal evolution patterns during breast cancer progression, we performed a spatial clone evolution analysis of abnormal epithelial cells by describing the spatial distribution and lineage relationships of distinct clones. We constructed phylogenetic trees at the single-cell resolution based on CNV events.

Although hyperplasia lesions often occur near breast tumors, their precise relationships to malignant transformation and their potential to progress to cancer remains uncertain28,29. In sample P41, we found that epithelial cells within the active hyperplasia zone primarily belonged to subclone A, which lacked pronounced CNV events (Fig. 3a, b). Morphologically, however, this subclone shared a ductal lobular unit with a DCIS-associated subclone (subclone B) and was situated near a significant aggregation of immune cells (Fig. 3c). These observations suggest that tumor-related morphological changes may precede detectable genetic alterations and could elicit a strong inflammatory response. In sample P13, we identified a subclone within the atypical hyperplasia area (subclone A, Figs. 2c, 3d). While no evolutionary linkage was evident between the CNV events in subclone A and adjacent neoplastic subclones, we did observe significant CNV events such as the chr16p gain which have been reported as one most common alteration in breast cancer and atypical hyperplasis30,31. This finding suggests that subclone A might possess a propensity for tumorigenic development. The lack of shared CNVs could be due to the inherent heterogeneity of cancer, highlighting the possibility of multiple evolutionary paths leading to malignancy within a single tumor. We observed diverse clonal evolutionary patterns across our tumor regions. Four samples (P13, P28, P35, and P41) exhibited a branching evolutionary pattern, characterized by subclones sharing a subset of CNVs while also harboring unique alterations. For example, in sample P13, we identified four genetically linked subclones (subclone B-D) in tumor regions. Subclone B, likely an early tumor subclone, harbored loss of chr8p, chr16q, and chr17p, and gain of chr8q. This subclone likely progressed, giving rise to subclone C via chr1q gain, which further developed into subclones D and E through additional chromosomal gains of chr16p and chr3q, respectively (Figs. 2c, 3e). These primary CNV events were confirmed by microdissection-based WGS analysis (Suppl. Fig. 5b). Notably, microdissection-based WES analysis unveiled similar evolution pattern, comprising an ancestral clone with mutations in canonical driver genes, such as PIK3CA and MAP2K4, alongside three derivative subclones (Fig. 3f, g). This indicates potential convergence in tumoral evolution at CNV and SNV strata. Similarly, sample P35 displayed branched evolution with subclones sharing gains of chr1q, chr8q, chr16, chr18, chr21q, and loss of chr6q, but each subclone (A, B, and C) also displayed unique CNV events (Fig. 3h, i). Although no shared ancestral clone was identified based on CNV analysis, a predominant clone with a MYCL mutation was identified through WES (Fig. 3j, k). Sample P28 showed a comparable pattern with three clones sharing some CNVs (chr7 gain, chr8p loss, and chr8q gain), but each also possessing distinct alterations (Fig. 3l, m). The primary clone, inclusive of a TP53 mutation, was also identified through microdissection-based WES (Fig. 3n, o).

In contrast to the branched patterns, sample P22 displayed a linear evolutionary trajectory. Two subclones were identified, both sharing CNVs of chr16p gain and chr16q loss, with a clear ancestral relationship that subclone A subsequently gave rise to its descendent subclone (subclone B) after acquiring an additional chr8q gain (Suppl. Fig. 4e, f, Suppl. Fig. 6g, h). Finally, within four specimens (4/9, P15, P16, P29, P36), cancer cells from a singular tissue section were found to possess identical CNV alterations, suggesting clonal homogeneity (Suppl. Fig. 7a–d). This clonal presence has been corroborated by scRNA-seq data from the respective samples (Suppl. Fig. 7e–i).

Furthermore, we observed significant alterations in the molecular profiles of tumor cells throughout their evolutionary trajectories. Notably, the expression of ESR1, PGR, and ERBB2 genes were significantly modulated during tumor progression. ERBB2, particularly, demonstrated a pattern of increased expression throughout the evolutionary trajectory. For example, in sample P13, a clear upward trend was evident from subclones B and C to subclone D (Fig. 3p). Similarly, in sample P41, tumor subclones were characterized by markedly increased ERBB2 expression and concurrent loss of ESR1 and PGR expression (Fig. 3q). These observed ERBB2 expression patterns were consistent with their CNVs (Fig. 3r). Additionally, compared to non-neoplastic subclones, tumor cells consistently exhibited elevated MKI67 expression, signifying their enhanced proliferative capacity (Fig. 3p, q).

Notably, the same subclone proliferates from the DCIS to adjacent areas, giving rise to invasive neoplasms (Fig. 4a, b, 4d, e, 4g, h, 4j, k). We observed significant intra-tumoral clonal heterogeneity within DCIS lesions. Notably, individual DCIS regions often harbored distinct subclones, implying that clonal evolution, driven by CNV events, occurs throughout DCIS development. This pattern was specifically discernible in specimens P13, P28, and P35 (3/9 samples). For example, we observed that a majority of DCIS regions in P13 contained only one subclone, while a single DCIS region (DCISm) exhibited the concurrent presence of subclones B, C, D, and E (Fig. 4a, n). Similarly, P28 and P35 also displayed multiple subclones coexisting within individual DCIS regions (Fig. 4d, 4o, and Fig. 4g, p). Interestingly, DCIS regions harboring multiple subclones frequently exhibited a compromised basement membrane and increased immune cells infiltration in the adjacent microenvironment (Fig. 4n–p), suggesting that clonal evolution could be instrumental in fostering DCIS invasion and inciting local inflammatory responses. While prior researches had highlighted the genetic diversity of DCIS10,16,17, these findings provide insights into the complexity of DCIS heterogeneity at the single-cell level, surpassing observations from traditional macroscopic analyses.

Our spatial clonal analysis revealed that the evolutionary relationship between DCIS and IBC often follows a pattern of clonal expansion32–34. Specifically, we observed that DCIS subclones could invade adjacent tissues and progress to IBC without necessarily acquiring additional genetic mutations or forming entirely distinct subclones. This was exemplified in P13, where clonal evolution within the DCIS lesion led to the invasion of adjacent tissues by individual clones (Fig. 4a, b), aligning with the multiclonal invasion model proposed in other study16. A similar phenomenon of clonal expansion from DCIS to IBC was also seen in sample P28 (Fig. 4d, e). Furthermore, microdissection-based WES data revealed the absence of a specific dominant clone within IBC, corroborating the clonal expansion model from DCIS to IBC (Fig. 3g, k, o). Interestingly, subclones with a higher number of CNVs, such as subclone E in P13 and subclone C in P28, often constituted a substantial proportion of the IBC (Figs. 2c, 3h, 4d, e). This suggests that subclones at more advanced evolutionary stages might possess enhanced invasive potential.

However, we observed cases where the relationship between DCIS and IBC was more complex. In sample P35, all subclones shared some CNVs, including gains of chr1q, chr8, chr16, chr17, and chr21, and loss of chr6q. Subclones A and B were predominantly detected in DCIS regions, whereas subclone C was chiefly present in the IBC regions and one DCIS region (DCIS4, Fig.4g, h), indicating a transition from DCIS to IBC for this specific subclone. Interestingly, subclone C presented less CNVs compared to A and B, as lacking losses in chr11p and chr13 (Fig. 3l). Similarly, in sample P41, a subclone (subclone C), displaying distinct CNV events (lacking chr14q gain and chr17q loss but exhibiting chr19 loss) despite sharing chr1q and chr8 gains with other DCIS dominant subclones, was predominantly found in the IBC region and surrounding DCIS areas (DCIS4-7, Fig. 4j, k). Notably, in P22, a linear progression from subclone A to subclone B was observed, with both subclones expansion from DCIS to IBC, culminating in subclone B as the dominant entity in the IBC domain (Suppl. Fig. 8a–c). Throughout this evolutionary trajectory, neoplastic cells acquired chr8q gain (Suppl. Fig. 6g), an aberration also identified in P13, P28, P35, P36, and P41, and previously correlated with cancer progression35,36. Collectively, these findings implied that invasive subclones do not necessarily arise through linear evolution from the dominant DCIS subclones, and the development of IBC does not necessarily mark the endpoint of a DCIS subclone’s evolution.

During clonal evolution, genetic variation resulted in the alteration of transcriptional profile. Interestingly, the tissue architecture and morphology alteration between different invasive subclone was also observed. For instance, in specimen P13, significant morphological disparities were evident among invasive subclones. Subclone B exhibited a tubular configuration, suggesting a higher degree of differentiation (Fig. 4q), while subclone D progeny displayed an aggregated morphology, reflective of lower differentiated (Fig. 4r). These morphological distinctions were mirrored by their transcriptomic profiles as subclone B upregulated genes associated with E2F targets and mTORC1 signaling, whereas subclone D overexpressed genes involved in development and Wnt signaling (Fig. 4s, Suppl. Fig.8d). Notably, 96 out of 132 genes upregulated in subclone D were located on chromosomes 1q and 16p, consistent with previously identified chr1q and chr16p gains in this subclone (Suppl. Fig. 8e). The evaluation of breast cancer progression typically relies on histomorphological analysis37–40. However, these findings underscore that neoplastic progression is a multifaceted process requiring the integration of both genetic and histopathological insights for a comprehensive understanding.

Functional and compositional distinctions between DCIS and IBC

Despite the lack of clear genetic distinctions between DCIS and IBC cells, their distinct spatial arrangements and functional attributes suggest different mechanisms underlying their behavior. To elucidate these functional differences, we utilized non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) for feature extraction on gene expression profiles. Notably, DCIS cells exhibited significant enrichment for oxidative phosphorylation-related metabolic processes, akin to those found in atypical hyperplasia zones. In contrast, IBC cells were characterized by prominent epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) signature (Fig. 5a, b), consistent with previous studies41,42. These findings suggest that oxidative phosphorylation might be the primary energy source during early breast cancer development, while a metabolic shift towards glycolysis accompanies tumor invasion and progression. To further investigate the mechanisms underlying tumor invasion, we compared gene expression profiles between matched DCIS and IBC subclones. IBC cells exhibited significant upregulation of genes implicated in extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, including SPARC, FN1, TRAF2, ARAF, SERPINF1, as well as cell cycle-related genes such as TOP2A (Fig. 5c, Suppl. Fig. 8f). Notably, ARAF, a serine/threonine-specific protein kinase of the Raf kinase family, has been linked to tumor growth and drug resistance43,44, while TRAF2, a member of the TNF receptor-associated factor protein family, has been reported involved in various cancer-relevant cellular process45,46. We further confirmed the pro-proliferative and pro-invasive roles of ARAF and TRAF2 genes in breast cancer using MCF7 and T47D cell lines (Fig. 5d–g, Suppl. Fig. 9).

One subclass exhibited upregulation of genes predominantly involved in ECM remodeling and cell migration, such as MMP, SERPINE1, and RRAS (e.g., P13, P15, P16). In contrast, the other group displayed increased expression of genes associated with cell cycle and DNA replication (e.g., P35, P22) (Fig. 5h, i). One group, termed “Tumor cell loose pattern”, was characterized by a lower density of tumor cells, with non-tumor cells constituting over 50% of the tumor mass. The other group, “Tumor cell Dense pattern” exhibited densely packed tumor cells, comprising over 90% of the tumor mass (Fig. 5j). These patterns were further corroborated by H&E staining, which revealed distinct histo-morphological features. The loose pattern exhibited well-differentiated structures, including tubule formation by tumor cells, increased fibrosis, and immune cell infiltration. In contrast, the dense pattern displayed lower differentiation, with aggregated tumor cells and reduced stromal and immune cell infiltration (Fig. 5k).

TME remodeling during Breast Cancer invasion

Transcriptional alterations of tumor cells play a critical role in breast cancer progression, even in the early stages. We hypothesized that tumor cells within the DCIS region adjacent to the basement membrane (CISAR) might play a crucial role in driving invasion. Comparison of the tumor cell expression profiles between CISR and CISAR revealed that CISAR tumor cells exhibited significantly elevated expression of genes linked to key processes involved in tumor invasion and malignancy, including matrix remodeling, cell motility, angiogenesis, Wnt signaling modulation, and proteolysis (e.g., LAMA4, INHBA; Fig. 6a–c). Furthermore, we observed a uniform distribution of subclones across spatial domains with no significant amplification of genomic variations in CISAR tumor cells compared to CISR ones. This finding underscores the importance of transcriptional changes, independent of large-scale genomic alterations, in driving phenotypic changes associated with tumor invasion.

To understand the microenvironment changes accompanying tumor invasion, we assessed the cellular composition at the juncture of DCIS, utilizing the peripheral DCIS cancer cells as a demarcation (Fig. 6d). Comparative studies indicate that the external milieu of DCIS is abundantly populated with fibroblasts, plasma cells, macrophages, and T lymphocytes (Fig. 6e). Cell-cell interaction analysis revealed that fibroblasts, pericytes, and epithelial cells are the primary sources of CXCL signaling pathway activation (Suppl. Fig. 11a), suggesting their potential role in immune cell recruitment. Notably, minimal CXCL signaling activity was observed in basal-myoepithelial cells (Basals). Basals, however, showed significant interactions with fibroblasts, pericytes, plasma cells, and T lymphocytes (Fig. 6f), predominantly emitting signals through the APP, THBS, and LAMININ pathways, while receiving signals through the Collagen, FN1, and SPP1 pathways (Suppl. Fig. 11a).

Our spatial analysis revealed minimal infiltration of T cells, B cells, and plasma cells within the CISR and IBCR, both characterized by a high density of tumor cells (Fig. 1h, Suppl. Fig. 3b). However, the CISAR displayed a pronounced presence of plasma cells, consistent with the observed potent interaction between plasma cells and Basals, suggesting a potential role for plasma cells in basement membrane disruption during DCIS progression. Interestingly, we observed greater T cell infiltration in the IBCAR; however, these T cells displayed reduced co-stimulatory and cytotoxic potential when located near or within the tumor cell-enriched regions (Fig. 6g, h).

To further investigate immune cell infiltration patterns, we analyzed the cellular composition within 30 μm, 50 μm, and 100 μm radii of invasive cancer cells. Our findings demonstrated that specimens P41, P15, P16, and P22 exhibited pronounced immune cell infiltration, particularly of T cells and plasma cells, in stark contrast to specimens P13, P35, P29, P28, and P36 (Fig. 6i). Interestingly, samples with low immune infiltration displayed increased interactions between cancer cells and CAFs but reduced interactions with immune cells (Fig. 6j). Samples with high immune infiltration showed enhanced signaling through pathways like CCL, CXCL, CD45, and MHCII, crucial for immune cell recruitment, exemplified by receptor-ligand interactions such as CXCL12-CXCR4 and CCL2-ACKR2 (Fig. 6k, Suppl. Fig. 11b, c). Conversely, samples with low immune infiltration exhibited more frequent signaling through pathways like BMP and EGF (Fig. 6k, Suppl. Fig. 11b).

Four molecularly defined stages during the basement disruption

Basement membrane disruption is a prerequisite for tumor escape. We identified 4 regions with recently emerged partial basement membrane disruption in 3 samples (P13, P28, P35) (Fig. 7a) and compared them with non-disrupted regions in the 3 samples. At the early stage of basement membrane disruption, immune cells such as T cells and B cells infiltrated across the basement membrane, with a significant increase in quantity, while the number of normal epithelial cells decreased (Fig. 7b). We conducted a comparative analysis of cell interactions before and after basement membrane disruption (Fig. 7c) and found that internal DCIS cells stimulates internal fibroblasts through PPIA-BSG, inducing the production and release of extracellular matrix metalloproteinases, which promote basement membrane degradation. IBC cells interact with pericytes through MDK-LRP1 to stabilize nascent blood vessels and promote tumor escape. Meanwhile, IBC cells also recruit macrophages and T cells through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis. Subsequently, IBC cells bind to the LRP1 receptor on macrophages using MDK as a ligand, inducing macrophage polarization. These interactions delineated a sophisticated, temporally organized sequence of microenvironmental reprogramming. To systematically deconvolute this process, we applied non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to the spatial transcriptomic profiles, consolidating the resulting components into a robust, four-stage molecular model of BM disruption (NMFT1-NMFT4) (see Methods). These 4 categories can be regarded as the four main stages (NMFT1-NMFT4) of basement membrane disruption.

This model initiates with NMFT1, characterized by microenvironmental perturbation and innate immune activation, where resident macrophages sense BM compromise to trigger inflammatory and interferon-gamma responses, recruiting adaptive immune cells while fibroblasts initiate early matrix loosening. The process transitions to NMFT2, defined by neoplastic metabolic adaptation and proliferative escalation, wherein tumor cells demonstrate enhanced proliferation through sustained hormone signaling and undergo metabolic reprogramming toward glycolysis and fatty acid metabolism to support expansion. The critical breach occurs during NMFT3 through coordinated stromal destabilization and an angiogenic switch, featuring tumor cell-mediated matrix degradation via EMT and collagen metabolic processes, coupled with pericyte-driven vasculogenesis and KRAS-mediated motility enhancement. The program culminates in NMFT4, marked by effector humoral immunity and microenvironmental fixation, where plasma cell-dominated immunoglobulin production and immune effector secretion establish an irreversible state lacking pro-repair pathways (Fig. 7d–g). Critically, this molecular staging system demonstrated significant prognostic power, with progression through NMFT stages showing a strong, progressive association with worsened overall survival in the TCGA-BRCA cohort (P < 0.0001), establishing it as an independent prognostic framework (Fig. 7h, i).

Discussion

This investigation conducts a comprehensive examination of the evolutionary dynamics and spatial microenvironment remodeling between DCIS and IBC using high-resolution spatial transcriptomics. We identified the common progenitor for DCIS and IBC, with clonal evolution initiating within DCIS during breast cancer progresses. Furthermore, nearly all invasive carcinoma cases in our cohort arose from the adjacent DCIS without the emergence of additional subclones, a pattern termed “clonal expansion”. This suggests that clone expansion of existing DCIS subclones, rather than de novo subclone formation or acquisition of additional CNVs, can drive the transition to invasive carcinoma.

Prior studies using genomic sequencing have proposed a multiclonal invasion model for the progression from DCIS to IBC, with identical clones present in both lesions16,47, aligning with our insights. Nonetheless, our research integrates single-cell resolution spatial transcriptomics with microdissection-based WES and WGS to elucidate the evolutionary interplay between DCIS and IBC. Initially, we can chart the clonal evolution trajectories across distinct spatial domains at a whole-slice, single-cell level. Our CNV-based clonal analysis through spatial transcriptomics was consistent with scRNA-seq data, and spatial cluster annotations aligned with histopathological assessments of adjacent H&E-stained sections, supporting the validity of our findings.

We were able to track clonal evolution across spatial domains, identifying both single-clone and multi-clone within individual DCIS lesion. Notably, in specimen P13, the earliest DCIS subclone (subclone B) already possessed the capacity for invasion. Moreover, microdissection-based WES and WGS also confirmed a shared clonal origin for DCIS and IBC, not only at the CNV but also at the SNV level. The absence of identified specific driver genomic events in IBC suggests that tumor cells may not necessarily acquire invasive potential solely through genetic alterations. Instead, epi-genetic alterations or other regulatory mechanisms leading to altered expression could play a crucial role in driving tumor invasion48–50. Such as ARAF and TRAF2 identified and validated in this study maybe play one important role in breast cancer invasion, but more functional and animal tumor model experiments should be conducted in future researches. Furthermore, our results displayed active interaction between basal-myoepithelial cells and surrounding microenvironment cells, which indicated the potential function of microenvironment cells in the prolonged degradation of the DCIS basement membrane51,52, a hypothesis requiring further experimental validation.

Our study went beyond simply monitoring genomic alterations in tumor cells. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of the dynamic changes in tissue pathology, gene expression, and the tumor microenvironment during breast cancer progression. Our observations indicate that genomic alterations, especially CNV events, during clonal evolution drive shifts in gene expression patterns, accompanied by distinct histomorphological changes in IBC. Furthermore, comparing IBC to their DCIS counterparts, we discerned two distinct invasive carcinoma phenotypes - termed loose and dense – previously characterized in pancreatic cancer53. We categorized IBCs in our cohort into these two phenotypes and validated their prognostic significance using TCGA datasets based on tumor cell-intrinsic gene expression features. This analysis confirmed the distinct gene expression signatures and prognostic outcomes of loose and dense IBC subtypes, emphasizing the close connection between molecular and histopathological features and the biological behavior of tumor cells. However, future studies with larger sample sizes, integrating spatial transcriptomics with digital pathology and artificial intelligence analytics54–56 are needed to definitively elucidate the roles of these phenotypes and their prospective clinical implications.

Additionally, we identified that our specimens could be classified into two distinct groups based on immune cell infiltration surrounding the IBC cells: high immune infiltration and low immune infiltration. Cell-cell interaction analysis revealed that signaling pathways like CCL, CXCL, CD45, and MHCII were significantly more active in the high-infiltration group, consistent with previous researches linking elevated chemotactic factor expression with augmented immune infiltration57–59. Further studies should further dissect the microenvironmental variances between these two infiltration states in larger cohorts to unearth regulatory mechanisms governing tumor immune infiltration.

However, our research faces certain constraints due to technical and sample limitations, which necessitate future optimization. Firstly, while striving for single-cell resolution, spatial transcriptomics currently offers lower gene capture efficiency compared to scRNA-seq, limiting the precise annotation of every cell within the spatial context. Additionally, limitations in isolating distinct subclonal regions within IBC for microdissection-based WES or WGS prevented a perfect correlation between microdissection findings and spatial transcriptomic results. Thirdly, considering that the CNVs inferred solely from transcriptome data using InferCNV may contain a large number of false positive segments, we improved the CNV segment calling method by incorporating WGS data. However, this method relies on stable clustering results generated by InferCNV across samples. Finally, inconsistencies between different omics data types in some samples might reflect intrinsic tumor heterogeneity, as samples for each data type were collected from slightly different regions. Future technological advancements enabling simultaneous multi-omics sequencing from same tumor regions will facilitate a more comprehensive and unbiased analysis. On the other hand, whole spatial detection within one tumor or a more substantial cohort in the future studies could provide more profound understanding on tumor evolution and microenvironment remodeling during breast cancer invasion.

In conclusion, our investigation unveils insights into the phylogenetic evolutionary relationship between DCIS and IBC, underscoring that invasion can occur through clonal expansion without requiring genetic alterations. This highlights the importance of studying factors contributing to basement membrane disintegration and exploring the TME for a complete understanding of breast cancer invasion. Furthermore, we identified two distinct IBC subtypes with different gene expression profiles, morphological features, and prognoses. Two different immune cell infiltration patterns in IBC were also identified, as well as their different cell interaction within tumor microenvironment. This highlights the necessity for personalized and precision-based diagnostic and therapeutic approaches that consider both morphology and gene expression, in addition to the traditional molecular subtyping, for improved breast cancer management.

Methods

Compliance with ethical regulations

This study was approved by the ethics Committee of Cancer Hospital Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (No. 22/249-3451) and the institutional review board of BGI ethics committee (No. BGI-IRB22153). All patients provided written informed consent for the collection of clinical information. No personally identifiable information is released.

Specimens and clinical data

All samples were from patients with unilateral breast cancer who underwent surgery at the Cancer Hospital of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (CHCAMS) on 2022. All participants included in this study were female breast cancer patients/tissue donors. Participant sex was self-reported during clinical enrollment and confirmed using clinical registration information in hospital electronic medical records. Gender identity was not collected in this study. Because all participants were female, sex- or gender-stratified analyses were not performed. All participants have signed written informed consent authorizing publication of relevant clinical information. No identifiable individual information is presented. This cohort included thirty-one breast tumor samples, with twenty classified as luminal A subtype, two as luminal B subtype, five as HER2-positive subtype, and four as triple-negative subtype. Adjacent sections were stained with hematoxylin-eosin (HE) for each sample. A pathologist identified and marked various regions, which included the in-situ cancer region, invasive cancer region, atypical hyperplasia epithelial tissue, normal epithelial tissue, and immune cell clusters. Among the collected samples, single-cell sequencing was performed on all samples, while Stereo-seq sequencing was performed on 9 samples (Fig. 1a). Patients provided informed consent for this work.

Preparation of single-cell suspension

Breast tissue margin area samples obtained from patients were immersed in a solution consisting of 90% Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) for transportation to the laboratory. The tissues were dissected into smaller pieces and transferred into MACS C tubes (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany). Tumor dissociation was conducted using a kit and a gentle MACS Dissociator, involving the process of milling, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 30 minutes on a shaker. This is followed by additional milling, another 30 minutes of incubation at 37 °C, and concluded with a final milling step. The resulting suspension underwent filtration through a 70 mm filter in 2% FBS and a final centrifugation step at 300 × g for 7 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in cell resuspension buffer to achieve a cell concentration of 1000 viable cells/μL.

scRNA-seq library construction and sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) libraries were generated utilizing the DNBelab C4 system, following established protocols1. Barcoded mRNA capture beads, droplet generation oil, and the single-cell suspension were loaded into their respective reservoirs on the chip to initiate droplet generation. Following collection, the droplets were gently broken, and their contents were retrieved. The bead pellet was resuspended in 100 μL of reverse transcription (RT) mix and underwent thermal cycling. Subsequently, the bead pellet was resuspended in 200 μL of exonuclease mix and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) master mix was added to the bead pellet and subjected to thermal cycling. The resulting amplified cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads. Fragmentation of the cDNA was achieved using dsDNA Fragmentase. Indexed sequencing libraries were constructed using the C4 scRNA-seq kit, which involved steps such as size selection, end repair, A-tailing, adapter ligation, and post-ligation purification. Subsequent procedures included sample index PCR and size selection. Barcode sequencing libraries were quantified using Qubit and sequenced on the DIPSEQ T1 sequencer. The sequencing process employed a paired-end read structure.

Tissue sectioning, fixation, staining, and imaging

Tumor tissue, margin areas, paratumor tissue, and lymph node samples were collected for tissue sectioning, fixation, staining, and imaging. Necrotic areas and regions around major vessels were excluded. The tumor tissue, embedded in optical cutting tissue (OCT), was sliced into cryosections with a thickness of 10 μm using a Leika CM1950 cryogenic slicer. The tissue sections were then affixed to the surface of the Stereo-seq chip and underwent methanol fixation by incubating at 37 °C for 3 min and at −20 °C for 30 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were stained with a nucleic acid dye (Thermo Fisher, Q10212) for the visualization of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA), and imaging was performed using a PA53 FS6 Motic microscope on the chips.

Stereo-seq library construction and sequencing

The Stereo-seq libraries were prepared using the STOmics Gene Expression kit S1 (BGI, 1000028493) following the standard protocol18. The sequencing process was carried out on the DNBSEQ T1 sequencer platform (China National GeneBank), MGISEQ-2000, or DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing platform (BGI-research, Beijing) in paired-end mode. Each sequencing read consisted of a 50 bp Read 1 and a 100 bp Read 2.

scRNA-seq data preprocessing

After obtaining the FASTQ file of each sample, STAR-v2.7.1a60 was used to align them to the GRCh38 genome reference (Ensembl 98). PISA v0.7-2461 was utilized for adjusting the Mapping Quality (MAPQ) score, filtering alignments, annotating genes and correcting the unique molecular identifiers (UMI). The output of this pipeline was an UMI count matrix for each sample, which was converted into a Seurat object by the Seurat v.4.3.0 package62 in R (v4.2.0). Quality filters included the following criteria to remove low-quality barcodes: insufficient UMIs (< 1000); cells expressing insufficient number of genes (< 500); cells expressing excessive number of genes (> 8000); mitochondrial gene expression represents a disproportionately high proportion of total transcript counts (> 10%); high percentage of potential doublets (>0.05%); potential presence of dead cells. Finally, we merged the datasets of all samples and 341,599 single cells remained.

Seurat function “NormalizeData” was applied to log normalize the UMI count matrix. Additionally, function “ScaleData” was used to scale and center the data. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the top 2000 hypervariable genes in order to decrease the dimensionality of the scRNA-Seq dataset. For clustering the cells, we utilized the Louvian algorithm through the functions “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” offered by Seurat. During the clustering process, the first 30 PCA principal components were considered.

scRNA-seq data cell type annotation

We used the RunUMAP function from Seurat to perform dimensionality reduction and visualization on single-cell data. Based on the positions of each cluster on the UMAP plot and the expression of marker genes for each cell type on the UMAP plot, we annotated the cell types of single cells. For annotated epithelial cells, we used B cells, T cells, macrophages, and endothelial cells as reference and used R package inferCNV v1.12.0 to select cells without CNV events as normal epithelial cells, while the rest were tumor cells. Furthermore, for normal epithelial cells, we used Seurat RunPCA for dimensionality reduction, and Seurat FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions to classify subpopulations. For non-epithelial cells, we used NMF algorithm in R package RcppML v0.5.4, together with R package harmony v0.1.0, to remove the batch effect of B cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, macrophages, pericytes, plasmocytes, and T cells.

Stereo-seq data preprocessing

The alignment of the FASTQ file to the reference genome (GRCh38) was performed using STAR2. Coordinate identities (CIDs) were determined by aligning them with the coordinates of the in situ captured chip. To account for sequencing and PCR errors, one base mismatch was tolerated. Subsequently, the mapped reads with MAPQ score larger than 10 were counted and annotated to their respective genes. UMIs with identical CID and gene locus were merged. Lastly, the gathered data was utilized to create a matrix of expression profiles that included CID information. For each CID sequence, its corresponding spatial location information was contained in another image file named cell-mask image. Detailed information about this entire process can be accessed at https://github.com/STOmics/SAW63

The cells were segmented using nucleic acid staining of the same section, in which the stained image was projected onto a Stereo-seq chip. The nucleic acid staining images from each field of view were automatically stitched together and then carefully adjusted manually, considering the overlap and chip grid lines. Afterward, the expression matrix of the chip was visualized as a grayscale image, which was determined by the UMI count of each spot. The grid lines of the chip in the grayscale image were superimposed onto the stained image, and manual registration was conducted. Finally, the registered stained image was exported as registered cell-mask image. The aforementioned operations were completed in StereoCell64 software.

Then, the Stereopy algorithms were employed to conduct tissue segmentation and cell segmentation on the registered image using the “SingleStrandDNATissueCut” and “cell_seg_v3” functions, respectively. Automated processing may conduct cell segmentation on the background, requiring tissue segmentation filtering to reduce background influence. Furthermore, the “cell_correct” function was utilized to perform cell correction using the Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) method when integrating tissue image and expression matrix. As a result, an expression matrix at spatial single-cell resolution was generated, containing cell label, gene expression, and centroid position information in the form of Anndata.

Cell type annotation and region partitioning

Scanpy (v1.10.0)65 package in Python (v3.9.13) was imported for data manipulation. Firstly, genes expressed in less then 10 cells were removed by “scanpy.pp.filter_genes “ function. The “scanpy.pp.normalize_total” function and “scanpy.pp.log1p” function were used to normalize and log-transform the expression matrix. To obtain cell annotations, we employed the Spoint module of the SPACEL package24 to compute deconvolution scores for each cell in Stereo-seq data based on the cell subtype labels from the scRNA-seq data. For each cell, the cell type label with the highest score was assigned. Next, the tumor cells were classified into two categories: in-situ cancer cells and invasive cancer cells. This classification was achieved by manually selecting the corresponding cancer regions on the cell-mask image based on the annotated HE image. We obtained the indexes of the cells within the specific regions and adjusted their labels accordingly.

To identify unique spatial regions across all samples, the Splane module of the SPACEL package was used. The parameters for region partitioning were configured as the following: n_cluster = 10, n_neighbors = 8, k = 2, and gnn_dropout = 0.8. A total of 10 regions were designated to capture the primary information contained in the spatial image. Based on the location and cellular composition of each region, they were classified as two regions related to in-situ cancer, three regions related to invasive cancer, three regions related to immune and stroma, and one region representing normal epithelial tissue.

Cell aggregation

To improve mRNA capture efficiency at individual DNA nanoball (DNB) points at a subcellular resolution of 500 nm, we combined the gene counts of adjacent cells within the same region and cell type, encompassing a group of 30 neighboring cells. This process involved using the Python scikit-learn package66 for Bisecting K-Means clustering to ensure most clusters contained approximately 30 cells. The gene counts in each cluster were combined and normalized, resulting in an expression dataset with decreased resolution but enhanced mRNA capture efficiency. The combined cells were subsequently used for further analyses including clonal evolution analysis, differential gene expression analysis, functional enrichment analysis and cell communication analysis.

WES&WGS data preprocessing

SOAPnuke67 v2.1.0 and FastQC v0.12.1 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) were used to perform quality control on FASTQ. The quality-controlled FASTQ files were then aligned to reference genome (GRCh38) using bwa v0.7.17-r1188 (bio-bwa.sourceforge.net). We use samtools v1.11 (http://www.htslib.org/) to convert sam files to bam format and sort them. Duplicate sequences were marked using the MarkDuplicates function from GATK v4.1.8.1 (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us) and the resulting bam was indexed using picard v2.23.8. Base quality score recalibration was performed using GATK’s BaseRecalibrator and ApplyBQSR functions. Additionally, an index was generated for the BQSR-processed bam file using samtools. In the case of WES data, SNVs were identified using GATK’s FilterMutectCalls functionality. The resulting VCF file was further annotated using ANNOVAR (version 2019Oct24, annovar.openbioinformatics.org). For WGS data, CNVs were detected using CNVkit (version 0.9.10, github.com/etal/cnvkit) and GISTIC2 (github.com/broadinstitute/gistic2).

SNV evolution Inference based on genomic data

Tumor purity was inferred from WES data by R package sequenza68 v3.0.0. Low-confidence SNVs in WES vcf were filtered out(MUTECT2 filter = ‘PASS’). Remained SNVs, together with copy number information derived from WGS data and tumor purity calculated before were put into Python software Pyclone v1.50.269. This step generated a file named cluster.tsv recording the identified tumor subclones. Evolution trees were finally constructed using subclones in cluster.tsv with high CCF (mean > 0.1).

CNV evolution Inference based on transcriptomic data

For transcriptomic data obtained from stereo-seq and scRNA-seq, we initially used normal epithelial cells or immune cells as reference to call CNV of tumor cells in each sample using inferCNV (github.com/broadinstitute/inferCNV). For stereo-seq, we used merged cell to call CNV.

Considering the potential false positive CNV events caused by calling CNVs based on transcriptome data using InferCNV, we designed a CNV filtering pipeline to solve it. Firstly, using Python package cv2 v4.10.0 (pypi.org/project/opencv-python) and the CNV heatmap generated by InferCNV, we marked large-scale gain/loss regions and identified the genes and cells corresponding to each region. Secondly, among the CNVs detected by the three omics technologies (SC, ST, and WGS), we screened out the CNVs that are found by at least 2 technologies as the final CNVs contained in the sample. Thirdly, we used the filtered CNVs to re-filter the InferCNV results in SC and ST, which are then used for the construction of subclonal evolutionary trees.

Subsequently, we used the CNV information obtained as described above to cluster tumor cells into subclones and build clone trees. Considering there were small CNV differences among most cells and there was no golden standard guiding the resolution of clustering as it was an unsupervised clustering problem, we performed clustering and built clone tree mainly based on the following two principles. The top one was our research objective. As we aimed to clarify whether there were genomic changes between DCIS and IBC, we struggled to identify one or more CNV events that can effectively separate DCIS from IBC as much as possible by adjusting the clustering resolution and methods. If such CNVs could be found, it would indicate that there are genomic-level differences between DCIS and IBC. Otherwise, it would suggest that there can be no genetic alterations between the DCIS and IBC. The second one was the fundamental assumption that CNV would not be lost once they occur. It was expected that the CNV in child subclones would include all the CNV present in their parent subclone. To be specific, we firstly used R package phylogram v2.1.0 (github.com/ropensci/phylogram) to read the inferCNV clustering tree file (infercnv.observations_dendrogram.txt) and classified tumor cells into 10 clusters. By considering the CNV distribution in heatmap and cell positions in spatial plot, we merged clusters with no obvious difference in both aspects (adjacent in cluster tree; no chromosome arm-level CNV difference; both DCIS or IBC in spatially adjacent regions). For each of the rest clusters, if it was pure DCIS or IBC, we kept it. If there were DCIS and IBC in one cluster, we used the dis.matrix function (method = ‘euclidean’) in R package wordspace v0.2.8 (wordspace.r-forge.r-project.org) to calculate CNV score similarity matrix and reclustered it with the hclust function (method = ‘ward.D’) from R package stats v4.2.0. Each observable CNV segment on the CNV score heatmap would be considered as a feature for reclustering. If there was a segment that can separate DCIS from IBC, the clustering result would be retained. Therefore, we constructed a subclonal evolutionary tree for each sample.

With regards to the same subclone detected by both stereo-seq and scRNA-seq, we assigned them the same name. Furthermore, we examined spatial cell locations of each subclone and calculated the DCIS/IBC proportion of each subclone. For stereo-seq data, we generated a spatial map based on the coordinates. For scRNA-seq data, single cells were mapped to spatial coordinates by using FindTransferAnchors and TransferData functions from the seurat package or the SPACEL algorithm. The radius of each subclone node was determined by log2(cell_number)/10, and the length of the line connecting subclones by log2(number of CNV of child nodes - number of CNV of parent nodes) x 0.3 + 2.4.

Driver genes, TMB and ITH statistics in WES

To filter out driver genes in vcf files, we used Cosmic_CancerGeneCensus_v98_GRCh38.tsv (https://cancer.sanger.ac.uk/cosmic/download/cosmic) and moved out fusion genes. TMB was calculated by dividing total number of filtered mutations (DP > = 10, case_var_counts > = 3, control_var_counts < 3, filter = ‘PASS’) by total length of exome. hg38_Twist_ILMN_Exome_2.0_Plus_Panel_annotated.BED was used to specify exomic regions. ITH was calculated by dividing number of subclonal mutations by total number of clonal and subclonal mutations.

DCIS and IBC CNV comparisons in WGS datasets

CNVs documented in GISTIC2 result *.seg file were extracted and cutted into 10 kb segments70. Mean GISTIC score were calculated among each segment intervals. For intervals whose lengths were less than 10 kb, mean GISTIC scores were calculated using the actual lengths. “stat_cor” function in R package ggpubr71 was used to compute correlation coefficients and p-values.

External and internal regions around basement membrane

Given the encapsulation of DCIS by basement membrane, our initial step involves defining DCIS’s boundary. We commenced by reducing the spatial image’s scale by a factor of 20, subsequently generating a modified spatial image of clusters with/without DCIS label by employing the gen_cluster_image function from the digitization.contour module of Python package spateo72. Contours of DCIS were drawn by using getStructuringElement, morphologyEx, findContours functions in Python package cv2 v4.10.0 and morphology.remove_small_objects function in Python package skimage v0.21.0 (scikit-image.org/). Subsequently, we applied the circle, findContours, drawContours, pointPolygonTest, and floodFill functions from package cv2 to outline the regions inside and outside DCIS’s boundary with a radius of 250μm. In the comparative analysis of TME before and after basement membrane breach, regions with an intact basement membrane and an extrabasement membrane IBC proportion exceeding 15% were excluded, as TME alterations in these areas might result from infiltration of IBC originating from other regions.

Comparison between intact and broken basement membrane regions and NMFT phase division

To compare the differences in cell types and their functions between regions with intact and broken basement membranes, we performed NMF on the spatial transcriptomic expression profiles of cells in these regions. Specifically, we firstly used the Python package cnmf v1.7.0 to perform NMF decomposition (harmony_vars = [‘sample’], n_top_rna_genes = 5000, librarysize_targetsum = 1e6) on cells from the intact and broken basement membrane regions extracted in the previous step, resulting in 13 NMF components. For the H matrix containing cell information, we split it into two H matrices based on whether the cells belonged to the intact or broken basement membrane regions. We then merged the cell-NMF matrix into a cell type-NMF matrix using the mean value, and subtracted these two matrices to obtain a broken-intact cell type-NMF matrix. For the W matrix containing gene information, we selected the top 5% of genes with the highest NMF values or the top 100 genes as the top genes for each NMF component. Subsequently, for each NMF component, we multiplied the values of the top genes in W matrix by the sum of the values in the broken-intact cell type-NMF matrix and the intact H matrix to obtain the expression profiles of each cell type under the 13 NMF states. Meanwhile, we used Metascape to perform functional enrichment on each NMF component based on the top genes, and grouped the 13 NMF states into 4 biological phases (NMFT1-NMFT4) of basement membrane damage according to their biological functions. We used the R package BayesPrism to deconvolve the TCGA BRCA transcriptomic expression profiles, obtaining the expression profile of each cell type in each sample. We calculated the similarity between each sample’s expression profile and the 13 NMF state expression profiles using cosine similarity to determine which NMF state each sample was closer to, and then classified the sample into its corresponding NMFT phase.

Identification of gene module

Due to the nature of subcellular resolution, the spatial expression profile matrix was convoluted to N × N sized expression profile bin matrix (referred to as binN, where N = 50). The preprocessing of the bin50 data followed the same procedure as the preprocessing of scRNA-seq data. RcppML package was used to perform non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) on the bin50 matrix for each sample. The optimal number of principal components was determined through the implementation of the “crossValidate” function. A gene matrix was generated, in which genes exhibited varying contribution scores across different components. The gene matrices of all samples were merged, and genes with zero accumulated weight in all components were filtered out. Components with a correlation coefficient greater than 0.8 across all samples were merged into a single component. The remaining components were subjected to Pearson correlation calculation and clustered using the “NbClust” function from the NbClust package73 in R. Finally, a total of 32 gene modules were obtained, and the top 100 genes with the highest weights within each gene module were retained. Gene modules were annotated by their enriched pathways calculated by the “compareCluster” function in the clusterProfiler package74. For every data matrix of Stereo-seq sample, the cell weight of each gene module was calculated using the “predict” function in the RcppML package. Cell weights were log-normalized and visualized on cell bin spatial plot.

Differential gene expression analysis and functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression analysis for Stereo-seq data was conducted by Wilcoxon rank-sum test method when analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups. The criteria for identifying DEGs were as follows: the p-value was less than 0.05, the false discovery rate (FDR) was less than 0.05, the absolute value of the log2 fold change was greater than 0.4, and the mean gene expression within the group was greater than or equal to 0.2. To determine the biological function of selected DEGs within each cluster, functional enrichment analysis was performed using the Metascape75 website with standard setting.

Cell neighboring analysis

The neighbors of a reference cell were determined as the cells located within a designated radius region where the reference cell as centroid. The radius was quantified as image coordinate length. We selected the following radius for the purpose of searching cell neighbors: 30px (15 μm), 50px (25 μm), 100px (50 μm), and 200px (100 μm). The cell type percentage was calculated by dividing the number of present cells of a specific type by the total number of cells in the region.

The average connectivity of cells from one region defined by SPACEL to another region was quantified by Squidpy v1.2.376 package in python. Spatial neighbors of each cell centroid within a 30px radius were determined by the “spatial_neighbors” function. Afterwards, the “nhood_enrichment” function was employed to compute the number of neighboring cells between regions. The average connectivity matrix was obtained by dividing the neighboring number by the total number of cells in each region.

Cell communication analysis

Cell communication was defined as the interaction of known ligands expressed in sender cells and receptors expressed in receiver cells, which was analyzed by the CellChat v1.6.177 package in R. We incorporated receptor-ligand pairs of CellChat’s public database with receptor-ligand pairs data from NicheNetR78. When comparing the cell communication results of two different regions or two different categories, the two interaction object were initially combined using the “mergeCellChat” function. Differential expression analysis of ligands and receptors was performed using the “identifyOverExpressedGenes” function, with a threshold of 0.2 for the log-transformed fold change.

In the last section, python package commot v0.0379 was used to compute spatial cell communications in intact and broken regions. Cell interactions involving fewer than 5 cells were filtered out. Wilcox test was used to examine whether there were significant differences of COMMOT scores between the intact and broken basement mambrane groups. Only interaction pairs showing significant differences were retained.

Cell proliferation

Cell proliferation was examined using the xCELLigence Real-Time Cell Analyser (RTCA)-Multiple Plates (MP) system (Acea Biosciences/Roche Applied Science), which can measure the growth status of cells in real time. MCF-7 (3 × 103 cells/well) and T47D (1 × 104 cells/well) cells were plated in 96-well electronic microplates (E-Plate 96, Roche Applied Science). Then, the E-Plate 96 was placed in the RTCA-MP device and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Every 15 min, the cell index was detected automatically according to the change of electrical impedance.

Western blotting and antibodies

Total proteins extracted from breast cancer cells were resolved by SDS-PAGE (10%) and transferred to PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and the secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: ARAF (Abclonal, Cat# A8687, 1:1000), TRAF2 (Abclonal, Cat# A19129, 1:50000), β-Actin (CST, Cat# 3700S, 1:1000), and GAPDH (Proteintech, Cat# 60004-1-Ig, 1:10000).

Small interfering RNA transfection and lentivirus infection

Cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting ARAF (RiboBio #stB0004885A: GGGATGGCATGAGTGTCTA; #stB0004885C: CGCTGACCATGCACAATTT), TRAF2 (RiboBio #stB0003864A: ACGAGGGCATATATGAAGA; #stB0003864B: CCAGTCAACGACATGAACA) or nonsense control siRNA (siNC) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transwell assay

In vitro cell migration and invasion assay was conducted by using transwell chambers (Corning, New York, USA). Approximately 1 × 105 cells were suspended in 200 μl of serum-free medium and seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was filled with 700 μl medium with 20% fetal bovine serum. After 16-20 h of incubation at 37 °C, invaded cells were fixed in methanol and stained with 2% crystal violet, and the cells on the upper surface of the filter were removed by cotton swabs. Cells in five visual fields of each well were counted and photographed under a microscope. Independent experiments were carried out in triplicate. Invasion assays were performed similarly to the migration assay, but 100 μL 2% Matrigel (Corning, New York, USA) was added to the upper chamber in advance. The number of cells were counted in each field by Image J software.

Transwell migration/invasion assays

Migration and invasion assays were performed as described18. The ability of breast cancer cells to migrate and invade was detected by Transwell chambers (Corning, New York, New York, USA). For the migration experiment, 100 μL serum-free medium was used to resuspend 1 × 105 transfected cells in the top chamber. Then, we filled the lower chamber with 600 μL medium containing 30% FBS. Fifteen to 24 h later, we fixed the upper chambers with methanol for 5 min and then stained them with 2% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) solution at room temperature for 8 min. We rinsed the chambers with water, carefully wiped off the medial membrane with cotton, and dried them. The chamber membrane was cut off and fixed on the slide. We used a phase-contrast microscope to observe images and photographs. Nine fields were randomly selected from each chamber to count cells using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA), and the results were counted by GraphPad Prism 7 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Invasion assays were performed similarly to the migration assay, but 100 μL 2% Matrigel was added to the upper chamber in advance.

Transwell HUVECs migration assay

A transwell system was used to estimate the migration ability of HUVECs affected by ESCC cells with or without TMTC3 in vitro. ESCC cells transfected with siRNA of TMTC3 or negative control and condition media were collected. The condition media were added into the lower chamber of transwell, and HUVECs (5 × 104) were seeded on upper chamber. After incubation for 3 h at 37 °C, the HUVECs in lower chamber were fixed and stained with crystal violet. The number of cells were counted in each field by Image J software.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed on tissue sections using the CONFIRM anti-Topoisomerase IIα rabbit monoclonal primary antibody (clone JS5B4; prediluted/ready-to-use, approximately 0.3 μg/mL; VENTANA/Roche Diagnostics, Tucson, AZ, USA; cat. no. 790-4371; lot no. N02828) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Two experienced pathologists who were blinded to the clinical data independently scored each tissue sample based on the percentage of tumor cells stained and staining intensity, range from 0 to 3 (0% = 0, 1%–9% = 1, 10–29% = 2, 30%–100% = 3).

Dense/loose pattern classification in TCGA cohort and Survival analysis

For each sample in TCGA breast cancer RNA-seq dataset80, we calculated dense and loose signature scores based on dense and loose signature genes (listed in Suppl. Data 4) using the gsva function from R package GSVA (github.com/rcastelo/GSVA). If the dense score of a sample was much higher than its loose score (> = 0.05), the sample was classified into the dense group. On the contrary, if the dense and loose scores were similar (< 0.05), the sample was not included in the following analysis.

We extracted grade information manually from copies of patient pathology reports provided by TCGA-Reports81. Following the Nottingham histological grade system, well-differentiated tumors or tumors with a total score of 3,4,5 in aspects of tumor tubule or gland formation, nuclear pleomorphism, and mitotic counts were classified as Grade I, moderately differentiated tumors or tumors with a total score of 6,7 were classified as Grade II, poorly differentiated tumors or tumors with a total score of 8,9 were classified as Grade III. Function molecular subtyping in R package Genefu82 v2.30.0 was used to identify breast cancer molecular subtypes.

Survdiff and survfit function from R package survival (github.com/therneau/survival) were used to perform Cox regression and calculate the survival difference between groups. Stage, grade, age, molecular subtype, and dense/loose patterns were included in univariate Cox regression model. Function cox.zph was used to test the proportional hazards assumption and function coxph was used to fit the regression models. As grade was insignificantly correlated with overall survival, stage, age, molecular subtype, and dense/loose patterns were included into multivariate Cox regression model. Samples missing stage information were excluded from the dataset. All four predictors were time-invariant and the final Cox proportional hazard model was built. Dense/loose patterns adjusted survival curve was plotted by ggadjustedcurves function from R package survminer (github.com/kassambara/survminer). Forest plot for the model was plotted by ggforest from survminer.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was conducted using the stat_compare_means function of the ggpubr package, which involved the implementation of Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Student’s t-tests. A P value of <0.05 was used as the criterion for statistical significance. Statistical tests used in this study are two-sided except for special statement. Graph generation was performed in R (version 4.2.0) and Python (version 3.9.13).
