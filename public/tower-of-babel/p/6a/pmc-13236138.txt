Trends in atrial fibrillation/atrial flutter and cirrhosis-related mortality in the United States from 1999 to 2020: a CDC WONDER analysis
Authors: Asad Ali Ahmed Cheema, Muhammad Touseef, Maryam Asad, FNU Zeeshan, Ramesha Tahir, Bushra Areej, Mustafa Saad, Warda Nasir, Shayan Roshan, Fatima Hayat, Katee Kumari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Atrial fibrillation/atrial flutter (AF/AFL) and cirrhosis have emerged as significant contributors to mortality in the United States (U.S.). This retrospective study investigates the trends associated with the coexistence of AF/AFL and mortality related to cirrhosis in adults between 1999 and 2020.Methods:To study mortality trends related to AF/AFL and cirrhosis, the CDC WONDER (Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiological Research) database was used. Mortality rates were examined using both crude mortality rates (CMR) and age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMR). To evaluate mortality trends, Joinpoint regression analysis was applied, allowing us to determine annual percentage change (APC), average annual percentage change (AAPC), and statistical significance (P < 0.05).Results:Mortality due to AF/AFL and Cirrhosis from 1999 to 2020 reached a total of 32 461 deaths, with the AAMR increasing from 4.43/1 000 000 deaths in 1999 to 18.48/1 000 000 deaths in 2020. This trend demonstrates a significant increase in mortality over the study period, with the largest proportion of deaths occurring in a hospital facility-inpatient (48.95%). The AAMR was highest among males (12.33), older adults aged 85 and above (CMR: 45.77), Hispanic populations (9.28), residents in the West (10.95), and those in non-metropolitan regions (9.4). Notable state-level disparities were identified, with Vermont (14.28), West Virginia (12.94), and Oregon (12.91) reporting the highest AAMRs. Although increases varied over time, the most rapid growth occurred between 2015 and 2020.Conclusions:AF/AFL and cirrhosis-related mortality surged nationwide, disproportionately affecting vulnerable groups. Escalating trends demand urgent, equity-focused strategies in prevention, diagnosis, and healthcare delivery to mitigate this critical and worsening public health threat.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation/atrial flutter (AF/AFL) is the most frequent type of sustained cardiac arrhythmia worldwide, with an estimated 59.7 million prevalent cases and 8.39 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost in 2019[1]. Cirrhosis is also a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, accounting for around 1 million deaths globally in 2010[2]. Both conditions significantly burden the health care system, particularly in the United States (U.S.), with the growing prevalence of AF/AFL[3], and the expected rise in cases of liver cirrhosis (LC)[4]. Cirrhosis accounted for 460 760 deaths in the U.S. from 1999 to 2016[5]. Furthermore, an estimated 5.6 million U.S. citizens are projected to develop AF/AFL by 2050[6].

AF/AFL and cirrhosis have a complex pathophysiological relationship. Although this relation is not entirely understood, it may involve metabolic inflammation, with liver-derived inflammatory factors potentially contributing to or even triggering AF/AFL[7]. AF/AFL occurs more frequently in patients with LC and is recognized as a predictor of increased morbidity and mortality in cirrhotic patients[8,9]. Moreover, liver disease is an independent risk factor for new-onset AF/AFL, particularly in cases of metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), MASLD with increased alcohol intake (MetALD)[10], and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)[11,12].

Beyond shared risk factors, the co-occurrence of AF/AFL and cirrhosis represents a distinct, high-risk clinical phenotype with competing hazards of ischemic stroke and major bleeding. Stroke prevention is particularly challenging because guideline-directed anticoagulation for AF/AFL must be individualized in the setting of portal hypertension, varices, thrombocytopenia, and altered hemostasis, where bleeding risk may be high while thromboembolic risk remains clinically meaningful[13–15]. Emerging data suggest that anticoagulation, including direct oral anticoagulants in selected patients, may be feasible and may reduce thromboembolic risk, but decisions remain complex and practice patterns vary across severity strata[16,17]. Therefore, defining the population-level epidemiology and long-term mortality trends of AF/AFL with cirrhosis is clinically important to guide risk-stratified strategies and integrated care pathways for this growing, vulnerable group.

Although previous studies using the CDC WONDER (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research) database have examined mortality trends in patients with AF/AFL, no study has specifically investigated how AF/AFL trends have changed over time in patients with cirrhosis. Given the high-stakes management dilemmas at this intersection, particularly stroke prevention and bleeding risk mitigation, understanding long-term mortality patterns in patients with coexisting AF/AFL and cirrhosis is essential to inform future risk-stratification and multidisciplinary care strategies.

This study primarily aims to analyze trends in AF/AFL and cirrhosis-related mortality among U.S. adults aged 35 years and older from 1999 to 2020, utilizing the CDC WONDER database, and has been conducted according to the Transparency in the Reporting of Artificial Intelligence (TITAN) 2025 Guidelines, to establish ethical, clear, and reliable documentation of all areas involving artificial intelligence, throughout the whole process of research and publication[18]. In this study, AF/AFL was defined using ICD-10 category I48 recorded in the multiple-cause fields on death certificates; therefore, our endpoint reflects combined AF/AFL rather than atrial fibrillation alone. Secondary objectives include exploring geographical and demographic patterns such as age, sex, and race/ethnicity, and identifying opportunities for early detection, preventive measures, and targeted interventions to enhance patient outcomes.

Methodology

Study setting and population

In this retrospective cohort study, data were extracted from the death certificates of adults aged 35 years or above using the CDC WONDER website. This open-access database is publicly available. The registry includes death certificates, each with an underlying cause and up to 20 contributing causes, categorized by the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems-10th Revision (ICD-10) Codes. The Multiple Cause of Death request form was used to collect data for the years 1999 to 2020. We used the CDC WONDER Multiple Cause of Death (MCD) dataset (1999–2020). A death was included if AF/AFL (ICD-10 category I48) and the prespecified cirrhosis-related ICD-10 code set were listed in any cause-of-death field, including the underlying cause or any of the up to 20 multiple/contributing cause fields on the death certificate. To investigate mortality trends related to AF/AFL and cirrhosis, specific ICD-10 codes were used. For disease classification, AF/AFL was identified using ICD-10 category I48, while cirrhosis was captured using set of codes encompassing alcoholic fibrosis and sclerosis of liver (K70.2), alcoholic cirrhosis of liver (K70.3), toxic liver disease with fibrosis and cirrhosis of liver (K71.7), chronic hepatic failure (K72.1), hepatic failure, unspecified (K72.9), hepatic fibrosis (K74.0), hepatic sclerosis (K74.1), hepatic fibrosis with hepatic sclerosis (K74.2), other and unspecified cirrhosis of liver (K74.6), portal hypertension (K76.6), hepatorenal syndrome (K76.7), esophageal varices (I85), and gastric varices (I86.4)[19–22]. This composite approach (diagnosis codes plus key decompensation/complication codes) was used to improve capture of cirrhosis involvement in multiple-cause mortality records, where cirrhosis may be represented by downstream manifestations such as portal hypertension or variceal disease; similar cirrhosis/complication code groupings have been evaluated and used in prior administrative and mortality research. Ethical implications

Approval from the local Institutional Review Board was not applicable, as it used a de-identified, publicly available dataset on deceased patients issued by the U.S. government. To improve the rigor of this observational study, adherence to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) Guidelines was ensured[25].

Data abstraction

Our data included deaths abstracted at the national level, stratified by year, sex, census region, race, state, urbanization, and location of death. Sex categories included males and females. The racial categories were Hispanic/Latino, Non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, NH American Indian/Alaska Native, and NH Asian/Pacific Islander. Places of death were divided into home, hospice, nursing home/long-term care facility, and medical facilities (outpatient, emergency room, inpatient, death on arrival, or status unknown). The U.S. Census Bureau categorizes the geographical regions as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West[26]. According to the National Center for Health Statistics Urban-Rural Classification Scheme (2013), areas were designated as urban or rural, with metropolitan areas classified as urban and non-metropolitan areas as rural[27].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed to assess the mortality trends in patients with AF/AFL with concurrent Cirrhosis. Crude mortality rate (CMR) and Age-Adjusted Mortality Rate (AAMR) per 1 000 000, with their 95% Confidence Interval (CI) were reported. The crude death rate was obtained by dividing the number of deaths by the total population each year. AAMRs were calculated by the direct method, which standardizes age-specific death rates to the 2000 U.S. standard population to account for differences in age distribution. Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.4.0.0, National Cancer Institute) was employed to compute the Annual Percent Change (APC) in AAMRs, with 95% CI[28]. This method uses the log regression model to determine the lines of best fit for increasing or decreasing mortality trends. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Overall mortality and annual trends

Across the study period, a total of 32 461 AF/AFL and cirrhosis-related deaths were observed among adults aged 35 years and older, and the AAMR was 8.56 (95% CI: 8.46–8.65) per one million population (Supplemental Digital Content Table 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113). The AAMR was 4.43 in 1999 and increased to 18.48 in 2020. From 1999 to 2007, there was a gradual increase in mortality (APC: 2.66%; 95% CI: 1.07–4.28; P = 0.002). From 2007 to 2015, there was a marked increase in mortality (APC: 7.43%; 95% CI: 5.88–8.99; P < 0.001), followed by the most substantial rise in mortality from 2015 to 2020 (APC: 13.47%; 95% CI: 11.69–15.28; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 3 and 4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113). Place of death patterns

Among 32 461 deaths related to AF/AFL and coexisting cirrhosis, 48.95% deaths occurred in the medical facility-inpatient. This was followed by 23.7% of deaths at home, 15.0% in nursing homes or long-term care, 6.6% in a hospice facility, and others (Supplemental Digital Content Table 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113).

Mortality trends stratified by gender disparities

From 1999 to 2020, men consistently exhibited higher AAMR related to AF/AFL and cirrhosis than women. Men had an overall AAMR of 12.33 (95% CI: 12.16–12.51), while women had an AAMR of 5.61 (95% CI: 5.51–5.71). In men, the AAMR slightly increased from 6.1 in 1999 to 8.38 in 2008 (APC: 2.77%; 95% CI: 1.0–4.5; P = 0.003) was observed, followed by a marked rise to 13.65 in 2015 (APC: 7.66%; 95% CI: 5.2–10.1; P = 0.001), and subsequently a substantial increase to 26.26 in 2020 (APC: 13.50%; 95% CI: 11.2–15.7; P < 0.001). In contrast, among women, AAMR non-significantly rose from 3.19 in 1999 to 3.37 in 2006 (APC: 1.99%; 95% CI, − 0.3–4.3; P = 0.08), followed by a notable increase to 6.41 in 2015 (APC: 6.96%; 95% CI: 5.3–8.5; P < 0.001), and a sharp escalation to 12.41 in 2020 (APC: 13.10%; 95% CI: 10.8–15.4; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 3 and 4, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113).

Mortality trends stratified by age groups

CMR increased with advancing age groups. In age-stratified analyses, we report age-specific crude mortality rates (CMR), as age adjustment is intended for comparisons across populations with differing age structures rather than within age-defined strata. From 1999 to 2020, the CMR for individuals aged 45–54 years was 1.59 (APC: 7.94%; 95% CI: 6.9–8.9; P < 0.001). In those aged 55–64 years, the CMR was 6.89 (APC: 9.40; 95% CI: 8.1–10.6; P < 0.001). For the 65–74 years age group, the CMR was 18.67 (APC: 7.50; 95% CI: 6.6–8.3; P < 0.001), while among those aged 75–84 years, the CMR was 35.16 (APC: 6.11; 95% CI: 5.0–7.2; P < 0.001). In the oldest group (85+ years), the CMR was 45.77 (APC: 5.56; 95% CI: 4.3–8.8; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2, Supplemental Digital Content Table 5, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113). Trends for the 35–44 age group were not reported because the CDC WONDER database provided unreliable estimates. Mortality trends stratified by racial and ethnic disparities

When stratified by race, disparities were observed, with the highest AAMR among Hispanic or Latino individuals (AAMR: 9.28; 95% CI: 8.9–9.6), followed by NH White (AAMR: 8.86; 95% CI: 8.7–8.9), and NH Black or African American individuals (AAMR: 5.83; 95% CI: 5.5–6.0). In all races, there was an increase in mortality by APC from 1999 to 2020. Among Hispanic or Latino individuals, the AAMR significantly increased over time from 3.64 in 1999 to 18.72 in 2020 (APC: 8.92; 95% CI: 7.7–10.0; P < 0.001). In NH White adults, there was a slight but significant increase in AAMR from 1999 (4.57) to 2007 (5.76) (APC: 2.77; 95% CI: 1.1–4.4; P = 0.002), followed by a notable increase to 10.16 in 2015 (APC: 7.53; 95% CI: 5.9–9.1; P < 0.001), and a sharp increase to 19.61 in 2020 (APC: 13.63; 95% CI: 11.7–15.5; P < 0.001). In NH Black or African American individuals, the AAMR shows a non-significant increase, 3.21 in 1999 to 3.36 in 2009 (APC: 1.80; 95% CI: −1.3 to 5.0; P = 0.24), followed by a significant rise to 12.86 in 2020 (APC: 10.99; 95% CI: 9.2–12.7; P < 0.001). NH American Indian or Alaska Native and NH Asian or Pacific Islander populations were omitted from analysis because the CDC WONDER data for these groups were deemed unreliable (Fig. 3, Supplemental Digital Content Tables 1and 6, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/B113). Mortality trends stratified by regional disparities

Across different geographical regions, the highest AAMR were observed in the West (10) per one million population, followed by the South (8.77), the Midwest (7.65), and the Northeast (7.58). A statistically significant upward trend in mortality was observed in the West from 1999 to 2012 (APC: 5.42%; 95% CI: 4.3–6.4; P < 0.001) and 2012 to 2020 (APC: 11.99%; 95% CI: 10.7–13.2; P < 0.001); in the South from 2007 to 2015 (APC: 9.47%; 95% CI: 7.1–11.8; P < 0.001) and 2015 to 2020 (APC: 13.9%; 95% CI: 11.4–16.4; P < 0.001); in the Midwest from 1999 to 2007 (APC: 3.65%; 95% CI: 1.6–5.6; P < 0.001), 2007 to 2016 (APC: 5.74%; 95% CI: 4.1–7.3; P < 0.001), and 2016 to 2020 (APC: 16.88%; 95% CI: 13.5–20.3; P < 0.001); and in the Northeast from 2005 to 2018 (APC: 5.38%; 95% CI: 4.1–6.6; P < 0.001) and 2018 to 2020 (APC: 20.8%; 95% CI: 5.3–38.5; P = 0.01). Mortality trends stratified by metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas

When analyzed based on urbanization, the AAMR were higher in non-metropolitan regions (9.4) compared to metropolitan ones (8.37). Discussion

According to our review of the literature, this study provides novel insight into mortality trends among cirrhotic patients with AF/AFL stratified by demographic factors and regions in the U.S. from 1999 to 2020, using the CDC WONDER database. Over the study period, a total of 32 461 deaths were reported. Initially, there was a steady increase in mortality rates from 1999 to 2015, followed by a surge from 2015 to 2020. Both genders demonstrated a significant upward trend in mortality over time; however, men consistently showed higher AAMRs than females. The highest mortality rates were noted among the oldest age group (85+ years). Hispanic or Latino individuals had the highest AAMR, with NH White individuals exhibiting the highest APC from 2015 to 2020. There were notable regional disparities in mortality rates, with the Western and Southern regions experiencing the highest rates. States such as Vermont, West Virginia, Oregon, Rhode Island, and Texas were identified among those in the top 90th percentile, having an AAMR nearly three times that of states in the lowest 10th percentile. Notably, non-metropolitan areas experienced the greatest rise in mortality after 2016, with an APC of 18.47. Most fatalities were recorded in inpatient medical facilities.

Importantly, the temporal rise observed in this analysis likely reflects both a true increase in underlying disease burden and changes in detection and documentation over time. Over the past two decades, advances in cardiac rhythm monitoring and broader clinical use of longer-duration ambulatory monitoring and implantable loop recorders have improved identification of AF/AFL, which may increase the likelihood that AF/AFL is recognized and recorded as a contributing cause of death certificates[29–31]. In addition, evolving clinical awareness and coding and certification practices for chronic liver disease may have increased recognition and reporting of cirrhosis-related conditions in mortality records across the study period[32]. Accordingly, the observed trends should be interpreted as a composite of increasing comorbidity burden and improved diagnostic and coding sensitivity, rather than definitive evidence of a single causal driver.

Our findings represent more than a fourfold increase in AAMR from 1999 to 2020, with a sharp escalation after 2015, exceeding trends observed in either cirrhosis or AF/AFL alone. These findings align with a National Inpatient Sample (NIS) based study that reported higher AF-related inpatient mortality in cirrhotic patients.[8] Similarly, prior evidence using the MIMICIII database, on critically ill patients with cirrhosis, demonstrated AF/AFL as an independent predictor of inpatient and all-cause mortality[33]. National data have shown steady increases in AF/AFL-related cardiovascular mortality[34]. Similarly, cirrhosis-related deaths, particularly those attributed to NAFLD and alcohol-associated liver disease, have significantly increased over the past two decades[35]. A sharp increase in mortality in 2020 may have coincided with and been influenced by the widespread disruptions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, which disrupted healthcare utilization and treatment priorities globally, leading to increases in various disease-specific mortalities[36]. By quantifying these long-term national trends, this study highlighted the need to integrate multidisciplinary care and reduce preventable mortality.

Gender based stratification revealed that mortality increased significantly over the study period in both genders. However, men consistently exhibited higher mortality rates than women, aligning with the findings from a cohort study on AF/AFL showing that male sex is associated with a significantly greater risk[35]. This gender disparity is also observed in a CDC WONDER-based study of cirrhosis-related mortality, which reports similar findings in terms of male predominance[36]. This may be because men have a higher overall incidence and prevalence of AF/AFL compared to women, as evidenced by a study conducted from 1980 to 2000[37]. Another potential cause of the male-to-female disparity can be attributed to the heavy prevalence of alcohol consumption among males, which further increases the risk of liver cirrhosis[38]. Another factor can be the protective effect of estrogen in pre-menopausal women against liver fibrosis[39]. Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA) is a condition linked to the development of Cardiovascular Diseases (CVDs), including AF/AFL[40]. Studies have shown a higher prevalence of OSA in males compared to females, which further predisposes men to develop AF/AFL[41]. Addressing these gender-specific disparities is important for developing treatment strategies that consider underlying pathophysiological differences in each gender instead of applying uniform standards.

Across age groups, as individuals get older, their risk of mortality tends to rise, with the most significant increase observed in those aged 85 years and above. However, the greatest relative increase occurred among patients aged 55–64 years with an APC of 12.93. Similarly, a study assessing mortality rates in cirrhotic patients with heart failure showed that AAMRs were the highest and most rapidly changing in patients aged 75 years and older[24]. Such trends may be linked to the growing prevalence of obesity and diabetes with advancing age, highlighting the cardiometabolic vulnerability in these age groups[42]. According to a cohort study on 50-year trends of AF/AFL, the risk of developing AF/AFL doubled with each decade after the age of 60 years[3]. Furthermore, older patients are more likely to be frail, leading to increased hospitalization, hepatic decompensation, and poor clinical outcomes[43]. This emphasizes the need for close monitoring and regular screening of older cirrhotic patients with AF/AFL.

Significant racial differences revealed that Hispanic or Latino patients had the highest mortality due to AF/AFL and cirrhosis, followed by NH Whites and NH Black or African Americans, respectively. The greatest APC change observed in NH Whites was 13.63 from 2015 to 2020. For decades, race and ethnic disparities have remained a deeply rooted issue in the U.S. In our study, NH Black or African Americans had the lowest AAMR when compared to NH Whites and Hispanics, primarily because of the lower prevalence of AF/AFL in African Americans, as demonstrated in different studies[6,44]. Additionally, a Cohort study determined that Hispanics were more likely to present with cirrhosis at a much younger age compared to NH Whites and NH African Americans, who presented relatively at older ages[45]. In contrast to our study, a national cohort study showed the highest mortality among NH Blacks in cirrhotic patients suffering from heart failure[46]. Moreover, another cohort study analyzing mortality trends in patients suffering from AF/AFL alone showed the highest death rate among NH Whites[34]. Hispanic populations face multiple barriers to healthcare access, including lower rates of health insurance coverage, higher levels of poverty among recent immigrants, especially after COVID-19, and language barriers[47,48]. Knowledge of these racial differences in disease prevalence and outcomes is essential for addressing equity and prioritizing the most vulnerable groups.

Moreover, significant trends in mortality were observed across states and census regions. In the U.S., the highest AAMR were in the West and South, underscoring widespread regional inequities. Almost similar findings were also observed in a study by Iftikhar et al, which reported higher mortality rates in the South and West[36]. A study analyzing the prevalence of metabolic syndrome (MetS) among U.S. adults found that the Western region had the highest prevalence of MetS. In contrast, the Southern region showed a high prevalence of MetS among older adults[49]. MetS is a known risk factor for the development of MASLD, which can progress to cirrhosis[50]. The rise in mortality can be attributed to limited access to specialized cardiac services and broader healthcare barriers faced by rural and underserved populations[51]. Vermont reported the highest atrial fibrillation (AF/AFL)- and cirrhosis-related mortality in our analysis. In line with this, a national study on AF/AFL-related heart failure mortality also identified Vermont as one of the states with the highest mortality rates[34]. Similarly, a study on liver disease-related mortality ranked West Virginia first for age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR), which is consistent with our findings, where it ranked second[52]. Moreover, a meta-analysis demonstrated that the prevalence of AF/AFL is significantly higher among patients with cirrhosis[53]. This elevated prevalence may partly explain the higher mortality observed in Vermont, where the coexistence of AF/AFL and cirrhosis likely contributes to the overall disease burden. Collectively, these findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions in states with disproportionately high mortality and limited healthcare resources.

Similarly, the non-metropolitan areas had a higher AAMR compared to metropolitan areas. These urban-rural disparities are consistent with previous studies[48,54]. A critical consideration is the marked shortage of physicians practicing in rural areas. Just 9% of physicians in the U.S. work in rural regions, although 60 million people live in these areas. This situation makes it more challenging for residents to access prompt healthcare services[51]. These disparities can be linked to the high costs of healthcare utilization in patients with AF/AFL, with a mean per capita medical spending of $10 355 higher than for patients without AF/AFL[55], limiting access to timely, affordable care. The persistent urban-rural gap highlights the urgent need to direct healthcare resources toward rural areas.

Lastly, our study demonstrates that the highest percentage of deaths occurred in inpatient medical facilities (48.95%) versus deaths at home (23.73%). A CDC analysis on cirrhosis by Iftikhar et al reported similar findings, with approximately 49% inpatient fatalities[36]. Similarly, another national study on stroke-related mortality due to AF/AFL by Naveed et al demonstrated similar findings[56]. According to a NIS study, AF/AFL is prevalent in approximately 14.18% of all cirrhosis patients in the intensive care unit (ICU), with AF/AFL causing significantly more mortality in cirrhotic patients than in non-AF/AFL cirrhotic patients[8]. This correlates with the highest number of deaths seen in the medical facility inpatient areas.

As inferred by the study, AF/AFL independently predicts worse outcomes in cirrhosis, including higher inpatient and all-cause mortality[8,33]. Concurrent occurrence of AF/AFL in cirrhotic patients is not a rarity; rather, it serves as a driver of excess mortality and requires strategies tailored beyond the standard algorithms. Furthermore, both conditions also increase the risk of developing ischemic stroke and bleeding complications[57]. Recent evidence indicates that Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) offer protection against stroke in patients with AF/AFL and cirrhosis, and have a better safety profile than warfarin[58]. Left atrial appendage occlusion may provide a potential alternative in the future, but its effects still require further demonstration in cirrhotic patients[59].

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, relying on ICD codes and death certificates introduces the risk of misclassification[60]. Additionally, CDC WONDER does not provide patient-level clinical detail (e.g., cirrhosis severity by Child-Pugh class or MELD score, AF/AFL subtype and burden, anticoagulation use, rhythm-control strategies, or procedural interventions), which precludes assessment of how disease severity and treatment patterns may have influenced the observed mortality trends. In addition, secular changes in diagnostic intensity (e.g., broader use of extended ambulatory monitoring) and evolving coding and certification practices for AF/AFL and chronic liver disease may have contributed to temporal increases in ascertainment across the study period. Our cirrhosis definition also incorporated portal hypertension and variceal codes to improve capture of cirrhosis involvement in multiple-cause mortality records; however, this strategy may identify cirrhosis-related complications alongside code-confirmed cirrhosis and could modestly affect absolute case counts. Importantly, the primary inferences of this study focus on temporal and subgroup patterns, which are less sensitive to small shifts in case ascertainment than single-year counts. Second, the CDC WONDER database lacks details on comorbidities, treatment types, socioeconomic status, and laboratory results, limiting our ability to adjust for confounders. Third, individuals of American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, or Pacific Islander descent, and those in the 35–44 age group were excluded due to the limited reliability of the data. CDC WONDER does not provide AAMRs; instead, it gives crude rates when stratified by age group or place of death. Fourth, various factors that can influence mortality, such as socioeconomic factors, other comorbidities, and the accessibility of healthcare facilities, were not considered. The dataset also omits critical information about patients’ comorbidities, risk factors, laboratory results, and genetic testing. Furthermore, a significant proportion of deaths occurred at home or in unspecified locations, raising uncertainty about the accuracy of atrial fibrillation and cirrhosis diagnoses. Finally, as this study is a retrospective analysis, the sample size was predetermined by the CDC WONDER mortality data, precluding the need for sample size calculation or justification.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our analysis from CDC WONDER data from 1999-2020 showed a significant rising trend in AF/AFL and cirrhosis-related mortality in the U.S., with the marked escalation over the last decade. This notable rise likely reflects a combination of increasing cardiometabolic and liver disease burden (including NAFLD and alcohol-associated liver disease), ongoing anticoagulation and care-delivery challenges in this high-risk population, and contemporaneous improvements in detection, clinical awareness, and mortality coding over time. Several variations were found in AAMRs, with the highest values found among Hispanics and men, with a pronounced rise beyond the age of 64 years, which may be influenced by factors such as increased risk of hypertension, obesity, alcohol use, viral hepatitis, and diabetes. Furthermore, our study indicates the highest mortality in the western region, particularly in Vermont and non-metropolitan areas, which may point towards regional disparities in healthcare detection and management. This study expands on existing literature by studying large-scale temporal trends associated with AF/AFL and cirrhosis-related mortality, while also highlighting the demographic and geographic variables. The present analysis delineates a convergent, rapidly rising disease burden, emphasizing the necessity of integrated management, early screening, and equitable access to advanced healthcare and personalized anticoagulation strategies in patients with coexisting AF/AFL and cirrhosis.
