Major injuries and associated factors in traffic accidents among motorcycle food delivery riders during the COVID-19 pandemic in Thailand
Authors: Siriaran Kwangsukstith, Vithawat Surawattanasakul, Chollada Mahakkanukrauh, Jinjuta Panumasvivat, Wachiranun Sirikul, Amornphat Kitro, Penprapa Siviroj
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMotorcycle food delivery riders (MFDRs) are at a higher risk of traffic accidents compared to regular motorcyclists. Their safety is a significant issue in many developing countries, particularly in Thailand, which has the highest rate of motorcyclist fatalities globally. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of traffic accidents and explore the association between risky riding behaviors, concerns for working conditions, and accidents among MFDRs. This cross-sectional study was conducted in Chiang Mai, Thailand. Data were collected from a total of 709 MFDRs through an online platform. Collected data included working conditions, risky riding behaviors, concerns about their work conditions, and experience with traffic accidents. In the past six months, the incidence of traffic accidents among MFDRs was 18.9 %., with 2.4 % experiencing hospitalized major injuries, 11.4 % having non-hospitalized major injuries, and 5.1 % having minor injuries. A total of 84.3 % of MFDRs exhibited at least one risky riding behavior, while 5.8 % engaged in ten or more such behaviors. Multivariable logistic regression analysis with adjustments for sex, age, and body mass index indicates that red-light running (adjusted odds ratio, aOR 2.78, 95 % CI 1.07 to 7.21) and being concerned for customer behaviors (aOR 2.66, 95 % CI 1.26 to 5.62) were significantly associated with major injuries. Additionally, factors such as sleeping less than 6 h per day (aOR 1.68, 95 % CI 1.10 to 2.56), riding the wrong way (aOR 2.49, 95 % CI 1.12 to 5.52), and having concern for customer behaviors (aOR 1.91, 95 % CI 1.03 to 3.55) were associated with total number of accidents. Red-light running and concerns for customer behavior in MFDRs are associated with a higher risk of traffic accidents with major injuries. Effective strategies and interventions should focus on strengthening the enforcement of traffic risk behaviors and encouraging food delivery companies to establish regulations relating to the workers’ concerns.Keywords: Food delivery, Motorcycle riders, Traffic injuries, Hospitalization, Wrong way driving, Red-light running, Risky riding behaviors

Introduction

Road traffic accidents are a leading cause of death globally [[1], [2], [3]]. The number of fatalities annually increased from 1.25 million in 2013 to a peak of 1.35 million in 2016 [1,2]. Notably, based on 2019 data on the age distribution of all-cause mortality, road traffic injury remains the leading cause of death for children and young people aged 5–29 years [2,3]. In low- and middle-income countries, the convergence of rapid economic growth often coincides with heightened use of motorcycles and a subsequent surge in traffic accidents, resulting in a rise in fatalities [[1], [2], [3]]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [3], traffic accidents remain a leading cause of death globally, with approximately 1.19 million fatalities reported in 2021; corresponding to a rate of 15 road traffic deaths per 100,000 population. Nine out of every ten of these deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries, while people in low-income countries continue to face the highest risk of death per population [3]. Furthermore, the WHO reported in 2021 that the South-East Asia (SEA) Region experienced the highest number of fatalities, accounting for 330,222 deaths, or 28 % of the global burden, with a fatality rate of 16 per 100,000 population [3].

In Thailand, the number of road traffic deaths reached 21,745 in 2016 and 16,957 in 2021, with corresponding rates of 32.7 and 25.4 deaths per 100,000 population, respectively, averaging between 46 and 60 fatalities per day [2,3]. Of these deaths, 74.4 % in 2016 and 51.0 % in 2021, were reported to be riders of motorized two and three-wheeled vehicles, predominantly motorcycles [2,3]. In 2023, Thailand had a population of 71,801,279 [4]. Estimated road traffic deaths in 2022 and 2023 amounted to 15,012 and 14,098 respectively [5]. Notably, 89.60 % and 90.53 % of these fatalities in 2022 and 2023 were motorcyclists, with individuals aged 15–35 years comprising 45.84 % and 43.16 % of road traffic deaths [5]. Furthermore, data from the Ministry of Public Health Thailand reveals in 2021, the majority of motorcycle accidents resulted in minor injuries, accounting for hospitalization at 31.43 % and 1.82 % of fatalities [6].

The gig economy has revolutionized the food delivery industry by providing both supply and demand solutions. It empowers workers with flexible job opportunities and has changed consumer behavior, making it easier for them to access a variety of restaurants and diverse meal options delivered directly to their homes [7]. The COVID-19 pandemic reshaped consumer behavior, with infection control measures, such as working from home and staying home whenever possible [8]. This led to a surge in demand for food delivery services, particularly in Thailand [[9], [10], [11], [12]]. In 2022, the demand for these services in Thailand hit its highest point, achieving a net worth of 79 billion baht and a growth rate of 4.5 percent compared to the year before [11]. However, this heightened demand also exacerbated existing challenges, such as precarious working conditions and lack of labor protections. It is crucial to recognize the unique position of Thai Motorcycle Food Delivery Riders (MFDRs) within the informal labor sector [13]. Despite their integral role in facilitating food delivery services, MFDRs often lack formal labor sector protections, leaving them vulnerable to exploitation and unsafe working conditions. This exclusion from formal protections further compounds the risks faced by MFDRs, particularly in the context of road traffic accidents, where they disproportionately experience fatalities and disabilities. Consequently, MFDRs are ineligible for benefits such as minimum daily wages, insurance coverage, access to protective equipment, sick pay, or health insurance. The wages of MFDRs vary based on several factors, including order volume, working hours, platform-specific minimum wages per order, delivery distance, and commission per order.

It is crucial to recognize that motorcycle accidents lead to more severe injuries, which often require extended medical treatment and greater resource allocation compared to accidents involving other forms of transportation [14]. Additionally, mental disorders appeared to be more prevalent among motorcyclists than individuals using other modes of transport. There was a significant association between attention deficit/hyperactivity disorder and an increased frequency of traffic accidents [15]. Furthermore, a previous study in Korea found a significant four-year (2014–2018), a two-fold increase in injuries caused by delivery motorcycles due to the growing food delivery industry [16].

Previous studies conducted in Vietnam and China have identified associations between traffic accidents and the working conditions of delivery drivers. These conditions encompass factors such as heavy workload, high daily order volumes, route planning, time pressure, stress, and fatigue [17,18]. While these studies provide valuable perspectives on challenges faced by delivery drivers, it is important to recognize that the specific context of Thailand may present unique nuances that require further investigation. Interestingly, there is a relationship between work-related time pressures, physical and mental exhaustion, and an increased rate of traffic accidents. Additionally, during the COVID-19 pandemic, delivery riders faced heightened financial pressures and experienced substantial income losses. Consequently, those with insufficient incomes are more likely to work under tight time constraints and in unsafe conditions. This, in turn, leads to risky behaviors such as disregarding traffic regulations, including not using turn signals, encroaching into car lanes, exceeding speed limits, and running red lights [17,19].

Despite existing studies identifying an association between work-related factors and an elevated incidence of road traffic injuries among delivery riders, there was a noticeable gap concerning this phenomenon in Thailand. Given the rapid expansion of Thailand’s food delivery industry and the high fatality rates attributed to motorcycle traffic accidents, there is a need for research to address this gap. Additionally, the exacerbation of financial strains on delivery riders during the COVID-19 pandemic and its impact on their working conditions and risky riding behaviors remain underexplored in the Thai context. Consequently, this study endeavors to shed light on the specific challenges faced by MFDRs in Thailand, with the overarching aim of contributing to the existing literature on road traffic accidents in the gig economy. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of road traffic accidents among MFDRs in Chiang Mai, Thailand, and explore the association between risky riding behaviors, concerns for working conditions, and road traffic accidents among MFDRs.

Material and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Chiang Mai, Thailand, from November 2021 to February 2022. We collected data from actively employed MFDRs through an anonymous online survey. To ensure respondent anonymity, the survey included confidentiality statements and refrained from collecting personally identifiable information. Participants received a unique link, and authorized personnel adhered to strict data handling procedures. The study specifically recruited individuals who had been working as full-time food delivery riders for a minimum of six months at the time of the study. This criterion was implemented to ensure that participants possessed the necessary experience, stability, and familiarity with food delivery work. During the survey period, three distinct food delivery platforms–“GrabFood,” “LINE MAN,” and “FoodPanda”–were operating in Chiang Mai Province. To maximize the response rate and ensure the relevance of the findings, a diverse range of recruitment methods were employed. These methods included distributing flyers, posting posters, and utilizing online platforms such as Facebook groups and LINE OpenChat for announcements. By employing diverse recruitment channels, the study aimed to reach a wider and more representative sample of MFDRs, thereby enhancing the validity of the study. Prior to completing the questionnaire, participants were provided with an informed consent form and assured of anonymity. If participants read the inform consent and agree to participate in the project, they can press the consent button and can complete the questionnaire immediately (consent by action). Importantly, participants were not obliged to participate in the survey. A total of 1028 participants accessed the online platform, with 709 participants (68.9 %) providing complete responses to all the survey questions. Our recruitment strategy involved utilizing multiple communication channels every two weeks, demonstrating a proactive approach. Combining regular communication with clear explanations of the study's significance, aimed to minimize biases and maximize participant commitment in this study.

Data collection

This self-reported survey, conducted within the framework of a larger project, comprised four main sections and included a total of 38 questions. The internal consistency of the questionnaire on “concerns for working conditions” and “risky riding behaviors” was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Scores above 0.7 are considered acceptable, resulting values for these questionnaires were 0.85 and 0.92, respectively, indicating a high level of reliability for both measures. The four parts of the questionnaire are as follows:

Part 1 focused on gathering socio-demographic characteristics and health profiles to explore their relationships with the occurrence of accidents and risky behaviors among motorcycle riders. Weight and height provide insights into the physical characteristics of participants, which can influence their ability to ride motorcycles safely. Furthermore, smoking and alcohol consumption, known to impair cognitive and motor functions, were assessed due to their potential influence on accident risk. Socio-demographic variables included age (in years), sex (male/female), and level of education (primary/secondary/undergraduate/graduate or higher), while health profiles encompassed weight (in kilogram), and height (in centimeters) measurements. Questions were also posed regarding sleeping duration, asking, “How many hours per day do you sleep on average?”. Participants were queried about their smoking and alcohol consumption, with responses limited to “yes” or “no”.

Part 2 involved an assessment of working conditions over the past six months which included examining factors such as daily working hours, shift times, and years of work experience. These factors are crucial for assessing working conditions because they directly influence the physical and mental states of motorcycle riders, consequently affecting their behavior and susceptibility to accidents. This section comprised six questions, as follows: (1) How many hours per day do you work on average?, (2) How many days per week do you work on average?, (3) What hours do you mostly work? (day or night shifts), (4) How long have you been driving a motorcycle to deliver food or transport passengers?, (5) What is your current average daily income?, and (6) How many orders do you receive on average per hour? Additionally, we gathered information regarding adherence to routine motorcycle maintenance and possession of accident insurance, with the response as either yes/no.

Part 3 focused on assessing the frequency of risky riding behaviors and concerns for working conditions expressed by riders. A set of 13 questions was designed to evaluate risky riding behaviors, while five questions aimed to gauge rider concerns. Examples of these questions included ‘riding against the lane direction or the wrong way on a one-way street’ (designated as wrong-way riding) and ‘facing difficulties in locating restaurants, navigating routes, or identifying delivery areas despite using GPS’ (termed wayfinding). Participants were required to rate their engagement in these behaviors or concerns using a scale ranging from 1 to 5. On this scale, 1: Never, 2: Seldom, 3: Sometimes; 4: Usually, and 5: Always, reflecting their experiences over the past six months. A higher score on the Likert scale indicates a greater frequency of risky behaviors or concern for working conditions. Participants who reported responses of “always”, “usually”, or “sometimes” were categorized as engaged in risky behavior or expressing concern, while those reporting “seldom” or “never” were classified as not engaging in such behavior or concern.

Part 4 focused on gathering self-reported data regarding hospitalization rates and Emergency Room (ER) visits stemming from the most severe accidents experienced in the past six months. Participants were asked whether they had been involved in accidents while performing food delivery. The criteria used to classify accidents into major and minor injuries were based on the severity of the injury and the required medical treatment. Responses related to ER visits, hospitalization, or self-care/mild injuries were typically collected through self-reporting by the participants involved in the accident. Riders who reported hospitalization or ER visits were classified as having experienced major injuries, while those reporting self-care/mild injury were categorized as having experienced minor injuries. Participants who had not experienced any accidents were classified as having experienced no accidents.

Statistical analysis

The survey data collected from the online-based platform were analyzed using STATA software version 16.0 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables were reported as frequencies with percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate for the data distribution. To compare the characteristics of MFDRs who experienced major injuries with those who sustained minor injuries or had no accidents, as well as to assess differences between those who experienced any accidents (both major and minor) and those without accidents, either the chi-squared test or one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was utilized, depending on the variable type. Additionally, a multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with both major injuries and total accidents among MFDRs. The selection of potential associated factors was informed by previous research and insights gained from a pilot study [20].

Associations were expressed as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI). All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at 0.05 to determine statistical significance. The study's findings were reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [21].

Results

Characteristics and working conditions

The majority of MFDRs were male (68.7 %), and had a mean age of 32.87 years, with a standard deviation [SD] of 8.60 years. The majority of participants had completed secondary school (45.8 %), and 66.1 % reported being single. Nearly half of them were classified as obese (47.4 %), while 25.8 % and 47.5 % reported they smoked and drank. On average, the riders reported sleeping for 7.11 h per night (SD ± 1.22). In terms of work experience, the riders reported a median of two years, with an interquartile range [IQR] of two years. The median number of receiving orders per hour was three [IQR 1.0] (data not shown). The average daily working hours were 9.14 h (SD ± 2.81). The participants reported an average daily income of $15.13 (SD ± 6.05) as of February 2023. The majority of them (77.2 %) worked longer than the 8-h daily limit and exceeded the 48-h weekly allowance (70.9 %). Furthermore, the majority (80.7 %) worked throughout the day, performed routine vehicle maintenance (91.1 %), and had accident insurance (61.6 %). We utilized the Chi-square test to assess any significant associations between two categorical variables: the different types of accidents and their associated factors. Additionally, we employed One-way ANOVA to compare the means of continuous variables across three groups representing different types of accidents. It must be noted that there were significant differences between MFDRs who had been engaged in traffic accidents and those who had no accident among these variables—age, daily income, sleep duration, orders per hour, work hours, work shift, accident insurance, and engaging routine vehicle maintenance (Table 1).

Prevalence of traffic accidents among MFDRs

Fig. 1 shows the prevalence of traffic accidents among MFDRs. Out of the 709 MFDRs, 134 (18.9 %) reported having a traffic accident while working. The average number of accidents was 2.44 times (SD ± 3.19) (data not shown). Among these accidents, the prevalence of major injuries with hospitalization was 2.4 %, while the prevalence of non-hospitalized major injuries and minor injuries were 11.4 % and 5.1 %, respectively.

Prevalence of traffic accidents among MFDRs classified by the severity of injuries.

Risky riding behaviors and concerns among MFDRs

Out of the total 709 MFDRs, 598 (84.3 %) reported engaging in at least one risky riding behavior listed in the questionnaire. Among those, 254 (35.8 %) engaged in only one risky behavior, while 41 (5.8 %) reported engaging in ten or more risky behaviors (data not shown). The most common risky behavior (response answers ranged from “always” to “sometimes”), reported by 559 MFDRs (78.8 %) was using a mobile phone (hand-held) while riding, followed by using only one hand to ride (29.1 %) and speeding over the limit (more than 90 km/h) (20.3 %). On the other hand, not wearing a helmet (9.3 %) was the least prevalent risky riding behavior, followed by running a red light (9.6 %) and cutting other vehicles off (9.7 %) (Fig. 2A). In terms of mobile phone usage while riding, approximately 69.4 % of motor food delivery riders (MFDRs) reported using their phones for navigation. Other common purposes included accepting new work and competing for assignments (52.0 %), making or receiving personal calls unrelated to work (41.6 %), chatting with customers and/or restaurants (37.9 %), and calling customers and/or restaurants (37.1 %), respectively (data not shown).

Frequency of (A) risky behaviors and (B) concerns for working conditions reported by the MFDRs.

In terms of concerns, 361 MFDRs (50.9 %) stated that they were concerned about all five conditions (expressed with “always” to “sometimes” as response answers), while 34 riders (4.8 %) reported they had no concerns at all (data not shown). The most frequent concern reported was about the ‘road conditions’ (90.4 %), followed by the ‘weather conditions’ (79.1 %), the ‘vehicle conditions’ (71.9 %), the ‘wayfinding’ (77.7 %), and the ‘customer behaviors’ (71.9 %) (Fig. 2B).

Factors associated with major injuries and total accidents among MFDRs

The results of our exploratory model, employed multivariate linear regression to analyze the association between sleep duration, work-related factors, risky riding behaviors, and concerns for working conditions. This analysis was conducted with adjustments made for confounders such as sex, age, and body mass index. We provide a summary of the key findings revealed by regression analysis in Table 2. Additionally, Fig. 3 serves as a visual aid, summarizing the relationships observed. To ensure the stability and interpretability of our analysis, we excluded certain variables that exhibited multicollinearity. Specifically, ‘Using a mobile phone while riding’ and ‘Concerns for road conditions’ were omitted from the multivariable model for two reasons. Firstly, within the context of food delivery platforms, most riders need to use their phones while riding–for tasks such as wayfinding and communicating with customers–made it challenging to isolate the independent impact of this variable on accident occurrences. Secondly, riders predominantly operate in urban areas, where traffic conditions tend to be homogenous, so the variability of responses to ‘Concerns for road conditions’ was limited. Furthermore, we removed the variable ‘Concerns for vehicle conditions’ from our analysis due to redundancy with another variable already included in the model, namely ‘Vehicle maintenance’.

Our findings for Model A, which examined the factors associated with total accidents (both major and minor injuries) compared to no accidents among MFDRs, indicated several findings. Firstly, riders who slept less than 6 h per day had a 1.68 times higher aOR of being involved in total accidents, with a 95 % CI ranging from 1.10 to 2.56, and a p-value of 0.016. Secondly, riding the wrong way was associated with a higher risk, with an aOR of 2.49 (95 % CI 1.12 to 5.52, p = 0.025). Thirdly, riders who expressed heightened concern for customer behaviors had a 1.91 times higher risk of total accidents (aOR 1.91, 95 % CI 1.03 to 3.55, p = 0.039). Lastly, red-light running also increased the risk of being involved in total accidents, with higher odds observed that the riders who ran a red light increased the risk of being involved in total accidents. (aOR 2.42, 95 % CI 1.01 to 5.84; p = 0.049). These findings highlight the significance of sleep duration, adherence to traffic regulations, concerns for customer behaviors, and running a red light in predicting the likelihood of rider involvement in accidents.

Model B examined the association between factors and major injuries with and without hospitalization compared to minor injuries and no accidents, and demonstrated several findings. Firstly, riders who ran a red light had a substantially higher risk of major injuries compared to those who did not run a red light, with an aOR of 2.78 (95 % CI: 1.07 to 7.21; p = 0.036). Secondly, riders who expressed a high level of concern over client behaviours were also had an increased risk of major injuries than those who did not. (aOR 2.66, 95 % CI: 1.26 to 5.62; p = 0.011). These findings demonstrate the importance of traffic compliance and concerns for customer behaviors in reducing the risk of major injuries among MFDRs.

In our analysis, we thoroughly investigated several variables for their potential association with accidents. These variables included various work-related factors, additionally risky riding behaviors, as well as concerns about weather conditions and wayfinding. Despite our comprehensive investigation, these factors did not show a significant association in our study. While these variables were explored, their lack of significant association emphasizes the complexity of factors contributing to accidents among riders. Both significant and non-significant variables are crucial for developing holistic strategies aimed at accident prevention within this context. While our study provides valuable insights into the factors associated with accidents among MDFRs, the study’s limitations should be acknowledged. The data relied on self-reporting, which may introduce recall bias and difficulties in completely controlling confounding variables.

Discussion

The main objective of our study was to investigate the prevalence of road traffic accidents among MFDRs in Thailand. We conducted an analysis focusing on both major injuries and minor injuries, as well as associated factors, aiming to identify potential associations with the frequency of road traffic accidents within this demographic. Our analysis encompassed a wide range of variables, including work-related factors, risky riding behaviors, and concern for working conditions. Through this comprehensive analysis, we aimed to gain a deeper understanding of the challenges experienced by MFDRs on Thai roads. In this discussion, we summarize our main finding–the prevalence of road traffic accidents among MFDRs and explore the factors identified as significant contributors to these accidents.

The prevalence of road traffic accidents among MFDRs in our study was 18.9 %, reporting involvement in at least one accident over the past six months. Among these incidents, 13.4 % resulted in major injuries, and 2.4 % required hospitalization. A comparison with previous studies reveals both similarities and differences in injury rates, indicating a higher incidence compared to our findings. Our study reveals that the rate of road traffic accidents among MFDRs is lower than those reported differs from those reported in similar studies. Compared to China’s self-reported road traffic accidents in 2019, which ranged from 21.4 % to 31.0 % over the past 12 months [18,22], our study reported lower rates. However, our study’s rate of major injuries or hospitalization was higher (2.4 % and 13.4 %, respectively) than that reported in a Chinese study (11.3 %) [18] and lower than in Greece (25.3 %) [23]. These differences may be due to these previous studies being conducted before the COVID-19 pandemic, covering a longer period. Moreover, a Malaysian study found a 41.0 % traffic crash prevalence among delivery drivers, covering two years of delivery driver experience [24]. This disparity influences the significance of different time frames and geographical contexts when assessing accident rates among delivery drivers. In contrast, our study examines accident rates during the COVID-19 pandemic, focusing on the past six months. Despite a reduction in road traffic accidents, deaths, and injuries, the pandemic has led to an increase in the severity of injury and death [25]. This temporal and contextual difference could potentially contribute to variations in reported rates among MFDRs due to unique challenges or changes in traffic patterns. It is crucial to recognize that our study may have underestimated the rate of major injuries. We did not include those riders who died and perhaps those who suffered serious injuries or mental trauma who declined questionnaire participation. This limitation underscores the need for further research, particularly in the form the hospital-based studies, to more accurately assess the extent of injuries among MFDRs by directly accessing hospital records and emergency departments, researchers can obtain a more comprehensive understanding of the injuries.

The analysis of red-light running incidents among MFDRs unveils significant insights into the factors contributing to road traffic accidents. In our study, 34.6 % of MFDRs self-reported experiencing red-light running, which underscores the complex interplay of individual, environmental, and systemic factors influencing road safety. Comparative data from previous studies, consistent with a study in Bangkok, 38 % of observed “red-light running” instances involved delivery drivers in 2022, while “red-light running” accounted for only 2 % of all road traffic accidents, the majority of these incidents resulted in severe injuries or fatalities [26]. Our study is still relatively low when compared to self-reported "red-light running" rates among commercial motorcycle riders in Malaysia [27], reported a rate of 16 %, Vietnam [19], and Greece [23] indicate higher prevalence, ranging from 20 % to 30 %. Moreover, food delivery riders in Greece mentioned issues including limited work experience and hourly wages, which can lead to instances of red-light running. The decision-making process behind this behavior could be influenced by the time constraints associated with ensuring prompt food delivery [23,28]. International literature suggests that the enforcement of red-light cameras is highly effective in reducing violations related to red-light running [29], with a study indicating a 29 % decrease in injury crashes [30]. In Thailand, ineffective law enforcement significantly contributes to the persistent issue of red-light running, influenced by various factors including low fines for violations and inadequate enforcement of traffic laws.The challenges of road safety differ from one region to another, influenced by a variety of factors including traffic regulations, enforcement methods, and cultural norms. Common practices among delivery drivers of speeding through intersections when faced with yellow lights to deliver food quickly, which raises the risk of accidents. Employers can promote safe driving practices by providing training, emphasizing compliance with traffic laws, and utilizing technology such as route optimization software to minimize intersection delays and enhance safety.

Customer behaviors significantly influence accident rates in the food delivery industry. Customers use applications to rate the performance of MFDRs, enabling managers to manage and monitor their workforce. If a customer reports misconduct or delays, the company can take action, such as suspension or banning. The pressure to meet customer expectations, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic, can create psychological stress for delivery riders, potentially influencing their on-road behavior and increasing accident risk. The gig economy has highlighted various stressors faced by workers, particularly delivery riders. They may feel pressured to complete orders quickly to meet delivery time expectations set by customers. This time pressure can lead to risky behaviors such as speeding, running red lights, or taking shortcuts, increasing the likelihood of accidents [31,32]. Fear of receiving negative reviews or reports of mistakes can impair their decision-making abilities while on the road [32,33]. The COVID-19 pandemic has added additional stressors, including exposure to the virus, navigating safety regulations, and managing increased workloads due to increased demand for delivery services [17]. The food delivery industry can address psychological stressors by providing mental health resources, stress management training, and clear communication channels to address customer expectations and performance pressures, enhancing overall customer satisfaction.

On the other hand, riders who maintain high ratings can increase earnings through incentive reward programs [34]. However, in Thailand and Malaysia, many MFDRs faced stress and anxiety due to customers' high expectations for food delivery times, especially during the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, some riders violated traffic laws to increase earnings and avoid customer complaints, increasing their involvement in traffic accidents [24,35]. Considering the significant impact of customer ratings on accident rates, the company should revise its customer rating system to account for real-time factors such as weather conditions, accidents, and peak traffic hours. Implementing a "rating back" system, along with transitioning from a payment model linked to order quantity to a monthly salary structure, could be beneficial. Additionally, riders could rate customers based on their behavior, and those who receive high ratings could be eligible for discounts.

Another two factors, namely ‘sleeping less than 6 h’ and “wrong-way riding” are significantly associated with road traffic accidents of MFDRs. Our findings align with previous studies, that explored the association between prolonged working hours, insufficient sleep, and traffic accidents [17,18]. Moreover, it is essential to recognize that rider fatigue significantly affects work performance and riding safety. This relationship involves various factors such as health status, task duration, sleep deprivation, stress, task demands, and circadian rhythm disruptions [17,18,36]. The most frequently underreported and common traffic-related accident is wrong-way riding, and perceptions of behavioral control can affect these behaviors [37,38]. This is consistent with a previous study conducted in Thailand, which found that up to 83.7 % of motorcycle riders had engaged in wrong-way riding [39]. In our findings, at least 60 % of MFDRs reported experiencing wrong-way riding in the past six months, which is consistent with findings from MEDRs in Greece (75 %) [23]. Sleep deprivation can lead to cognitive impairment, affecting attention, decision-making, and reaction time, and can also impair motor skills and coordination, affecting a rider's ability to control their motorcycle effectively [40]. Riders experiencing inadequate sleep may have a reduced ability to process information, assess risks, and make appropriate judgments while on the road [41]. Fatigue resulting from insufficient sleep can lead to decreased alertness, slower reaction times, and impaired judgment have been associated with a higher likelihood of accidents [41]. This behavior is often driven by the fact that Thailand’s road infrastructure is mainly designed for four-wheel vehicles, prompting motorcyclists to engage in wrong-way riding to minimize conflicting movements, reduce travel distances, and spacing between U-turn openings.

Moreover, the food delivery industry has a concerning safety culture, with 85 % of MFDRs engaging in risky riding behaviors, similar to countries such as Korea [16], China [18], Vietnam [19], and Greece [23]. This study revealed that the most prevalent risky behavior in MFDRs (78.8 %) is ‘Using a mobile phone while riding’, which is similar to previous studies on motorcycle taxis in Vietnam, where rates ranged from 52 % to 91.6 % [19,42]. Smartphones are increasingly used by MFDRs for job alerts, work-related activities, navigation, and communication with customers and restaurants. However, this use can distract drivers and reduce their ability to react, as it can take up to two persons to accept a position [43]. This study also found that 29.1 % of mobile phone users reported ‘Using only one hand to ride’, a significantly lower prevalence than a previous study in Greece, which reported a high of 70 % [23]. Thailand prohibits using handheld devices while riding a motorcycle, with fines of 30.0 USD (as of February 2023). However, riders can use hands-free devices. The number of food delivery riders in Thailand using handheld or hands-free devices is unknown. Nevertheless, mobile phone use creates distractions and hazards, leading to accidents and injuries, regardless of how they are held [44,45]. Interestingly, among MFDRs, not wearing a helmet was determined to be the most common risky behavior (9.3 %). This percentage is significantly lower than the rates that were reported in previous studies conducted in Thailand between 2007 and 2010 (ranging from 40 % to 55 %) [[46], [47], [48]]. To address road safety challenges for MFDRs, it is crucial to provide comprehensive training programs on safe riding practices, traffic laws, and defensive driving techniques. Additionally, implementing stricter enforcement of traffic laws and safety regulations is essential. Leveraging technology solutions such as GPS tracking, telematics, and real-time monitoring systems can further aid food delivery companies in identifying and addressing risky riding behaviors proactively.

The significant reduction in non-compliance with helmet usage can be attributed to the food delivery company's inclusion of 'wearing a helmet' as a mandatory requirement in their rules and regulations for MFDRs. Furthermore, the company enforces penalties on riders who are reported for not wearing helmets, with the most severe consequence being a permanent suspension from their services. These actions illustrate the collaborative efforts made by both the Thai government and food delivery companies to minimize the number of MFDRs who neglect helmet usage. Regular monitoring and evaluation of helmet compliance rates, coupled with feedback from riders, can identify areas for improvement and refine strategies to enhance compliance over time. Implementing a multifaceted approach that combines education on helmet risks and protective benefits, positive reinforcement alongside penalties, access to quality helmets, and a supportive work environment can contribute to greater helmet compliance among MFDRs, promoting sustained helmet compliance.

To our knowledge, this study is the first investigation in Thailand, shedding light on road safety challenges faced by MFDRs and offering valuable insights to enhance rider safety in the food delivery industry. Through a comprehensive analysis, this study examined various factors contributing to road traffic accidents among MFDRs, including sleep duration, risky riding behaviors, and concern for working conditions. Our findings provide valuable insights for stakeholders in the food delivery industry, including companies, policymakers, and regulatory authorities, to develop effective interventions and policies promoting rider safety.

However, it is essential to acknowledge several limitations in this study. Firstly, the cross-sectional design of this study limits our ability to establish causality and assess changes in road safety behaviors over time. Secondly, our findings may not be generalizable from Chiang Mai and other regions in Thailand, which may have underreported severe accidents, fatalities, and mental health impacts from accidents, as well as different traffic conditions, cultural norms, and regulatory environments. This selection bias, specifically survival bias, could have led to an underestimation of the number of riders who experienced severe injuries and fatalities. Thirdly, relying on self-reported data for certain variables, such as red-light running incidents, may introduce bias and inaccuracies. Lastly, recall bias may have influenced our findings due to the reliance on self-reported questionnaires. Nevertheless, future studies addressing these limitations, such as employing more rigorous data collection methods and longitudinal analyses, are needed for advancing knowledge in this area and informing evidence-based interventions.

Conclusions

The prevalence of road traffic accidents among MFDRs in Thailand over the past six months was 18.9 %, lower than neighboring countries such as Vietnam [19] and China [18], which reported higher rates before the COVID-19 pandemic. Notably, Malaysia reported higher rates conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [24]. Factors contributing to these accidents include red-light running, wrong-way riding, concerns about customer behaviors, and inadequate sleep. The pressure to meet delivery deadlines customer expectations for timely service, and irregular work schedules exacerbate these risks, leading to risky riding behaviors. Additionally, inadequate sleep and fatigue impair cognitive function, reaction times, and impaired judgment on roads. The high-risk environment of the gig economy heightens the likelihood of accidents.

Governments can improve road safety by enforcing traffic laws, introducing a 'rating back system', and transitioning from a per-order payment structure to a monthly wage system. A comprehensive healthcare system is essential for gig economy workers, including insurance, regular health check-ups, mental health support, occupational health programs, and health promotion initiatives. The delivery industry should prioritize safe driving practices, traffic law compliance, route optimization, and customer rating systems. Challenges such as lack of awareness, geographical barriers, and transportation issues may arise. Targeted outreach and education campaigns can help raise awareness and expand healthcare access, while telemedicine services can reduce physical travel, enhancing healthcare accessibility for gig economy workers.

Further research should focus on the perceptions of risky riding practices among MFDRs and the influence of organizational culture on safety attitudes and behaviors. It also investigates psychological factors such as stress, fatigue, and coping mechanisms during high-pressure situations. These findings offer insights to improve safe driving practices, using technology and enhancing customer rating systems. However, further research is needed to understand variations across different regions and countries. The study's focus on a specific geographic region may limit its generalizability to other regions with different traffic conditions, cultural norms, and regulatory environments, requiring caution when extrapolating findings to broader contexts. Hospital-based data and longitudinal analyses on road traffic injuries among MFDRs are also necessary to advance knowledge and guide evidence-based interventions.
