Standardized protocols for reflexing to a multigene panel for patients with non–small cell lung cancer: prevalence and perceived barriers to comprehensive, pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex testing
Authors: Lynnette Savaloja Pineault, Nikki Martin, Melissa A Kelly, Robyn L Temple-Smolkin, Alexandra S Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the use of standardized protocols for reflexing to broad, multigene panels in non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and identify barriers that limit timely pathologist‑ordered biomarker reflex testing.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was administered to a national sample of health care professionals involved in NSCLC biomarker testing.ResultsOf 111 respondents in the dataset, 77.5% reported using an institutional standardized protocol for comprehensive biomarker testing (CBT), and 88.4% of these protocols included reflexing to a multigene panel. Most respondents with reflex protocols indicated that 80% or more of newly diagnosed patients with NSCLC received CBT; however, fewer respondents reported that results were available before the oncology visit to select first‑line treatment. Pathologists were the primary ordering providers in institutions with standardized protocols. Respondents agreed that pathologist‑ordered reflex biomarker testing improves cancer care and supported Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) recognition of pathologists as ordering physicians for broad biomarker panels. Despite protocol adoption, respondents cited persistent barriers—including reimbursement, insurance authorization delays, and the Medicare 14-day rule.ConclusionsLocally standardized protocols incorporating reflex multigene panel testing have been reported and are associated with high rates of CBT. Institutional and operational barriers delay the availability of results and limit the impact of these protocols on first‑line treatment decisions. Clarifying CMS guidance on pathologists as valid ordering physicians for broad biomarker panels, strengthening coordination with multidisciplinary teams, addressing workflow constraints, and educating the laboratories and payers on pathologists having ordering authority may enhance the effectiveness and adoption of reflex biomarker testing models.

INTRODUCTION

Despite numerous advances in treatment over the past decade, lung cancer remains the most common cause of cancer-related deaths in both men and women. The most recent estimates indicate that 226,650 new cases of lung cancer were diagnosed in the United States in 2025, and non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 83% to 85% of all lung cancer cases.1,2 Most patients are diagnosed with lung cancer after symptoms develop, when the disease is already at an advanced stage. Across early-stage, advanced, and metastatic disease, treatment decisions depend on biomarker testing to identify actionable genomic and molecular alterations.

National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend biomarker testing with broad molecular panels for patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC.3 However, a growing body of evidence shows that many eligible patients do not receive guideline-recommended biomarker testing. In 1 study, only 45% of these patients received recommended testing. In the same study, 90% of patients with metastatic NSCLC underwent testing for at least 1 biomarker, and only 46% received any of the 5 recommended biomarker tests (EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, and PD-L1). The coordination required to ensure that the correct biomarker tests are ordered for the right patient at the right time is complex and relies on communication among various members of the patient’s care team. Stakeholders include oncology, pathology, laboratory, surgical, and interventional pulmonology/radiology teams, as well as nurse practitioners and navigators.

Compounding these coordination challenges, the landscape for lung cancer detection is also changing. Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening is increasing the detection of early-stage lung cancer cases, and hospital screening programs are identifying growing numbers of new patients at earlier, potentially curable stages.4,5 Together, these trends expand the population entering the diagnostic and treatment pathway, increasing the number of patients who require biomarker testing. Cases diagnosed with LDCT often rely on small biopsy or cytology specimens, placing greater demands on effective tissue utilization and underscoring the need for efficient biomarker testing workflows that can accommodate these specimen constraints across disease stages.

Pathology practices across the United States have increasingly spearheaded efforts to implement pathologist- or laboratory-initiated reflex cancer biomarker testing, under established, preapproved standard operating procedures (protocols), upon diagnosis of a specific cancer type, rather than waiting for a separate, formal order from a treating physician.6‐9 These initiatives rely on close collaboration with oncology teams to determine standardized protocols for test ordering, biomarker inclusion, vendor or laboratory selection, and reporting requirements. Given the rapidly evolving landscape of biomarker testing, these protocols require ongoing review to ensure that they remain current and are being used appropriately. In some settings, laboratories have dedicated laboratory personnel responsible for overseeing and coordinating these complex services.10,11

Even when institutionally standardized biomarker ordering protocols are implemented, laboratories may face challenges in ensuring comprehensive patient testing. In many US clinical settings, molecular testing may be delayed or not performed during the diagnostic process, which can contribute to treatment delays or treatment inappropriateness.12 As a result, comprehensive biomarker test results are frequently not available at the time of initial oncology decision-making, limiting their ability to inform first-line treatment selection. These challenges can occur in various ways, both internally and externally, such as insurance-related authorization delays, for comprehensive analysis, communication gaps between treating clinicians and laboratories, and limited tissue availability.13 The latter is of particular concern in screen-detected cases, where small biopsy or cytology specimens place additional constraints on comprehensive analysis.

METHODS

A cross-sectional, exploratory survey was developed by a working group comprising representatives from the LUNGevity Foundation, the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP), and the American Society for Clinical Pathology (ASCP). The survey was deployed via email to the LUNGevity network of health care professionals and to the membership of AMP and ASCP. Invitations were directed to a nationwide sample of pathologists, oncologists, nurses, laboratory administrators, and other health care providers who had a role in ordering, performing, or using the results of molecular biomarker tests to provide patient care. The survey was open from January 21, 2025, to March 11, 2025, and elicited information such as whether the respondents’ institution used a standardized protocol for comprehensive biomarker testing, the timing of when the results of such testing were available, whether pathologists were the ordering provider, and what barriers were perceived to the latter. For the purposes of this survey, “comprehensive biomarker testing” was defined as the use of extended multigene panels to inform the selection of evidence-based, guideline-concordant therapy based on the 2024 NCCN recommendations for biomarkers performed on NSCLC, including biomarkers such as ALK, BRAF, EGFR, ERBB2 (HER2), KRAS, MET exon14, NTRK, RET, ROS1, and PD-L1.

The survey also included questions on the perceived impact and benefits of pathologist-ordered biomarker testing under the US Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) “treating physician” provision, as well as the clinical implications of complying with the Medicare Date of Service Policy (commonly known as the 14-day rule) and its impact on comprehensive biomarker testing and treatment selection. This policy requires billing for laboratory tests collected during an inpatient stay to be bundled with the patient’s hospital bill, unless the test is ordered 14 days after the patient’s discharge. While exceptions to the original rule were made in 2018 for certain molecular tests collected in the outpatient setting, this rule is often cited as a barrier for timely biomarker testing and will be examined in detail in a future study.

Biomarker testing protocols

As shown in Figure 1, most respondents (n = 86, 77.5%) reported that their institution used a standardized protocol for ordering comprehensive biomarker testing for patients diagnosed with NSCLC, compared with 18.9% (n = 21) who reported that their site did not use a standardized protocol. Furthermore, 88.4% of the respondents with a standardized protocol indicated that it included reflexing to a multigene panel (n = 76, 68.5% of total respondents), compared with 8 respondents whose standardized protocol did not include multigene testing (9.3%, 7.2% of total respondents; Figure 2). Two additional respondents also indicated that the survey question was not applicable to them (eg, representing reference laboratories).

Biomarker test-ordering practices

To examine ordering practices for biomarker testing, the survey asked a subset of respondents whose standardized protocol included reflexing to a multigene panel whether the testing was ordered by a pathologist, an oncologist, or another provider. More than two-thirds of these respondents reported that testing was ordered by a pathologist (n = 28, 68.3%; Figure 4), which was more than 5 times the second most frequent ordering scenario for comprehensive biomarker testing, via a standing order established by an oncologist (n = 5, 12.2%). Only 1 respondent (n = 1, 2.4%) reported that testing was ordered by an oncologist or other provider after the first patient visit. Respondents also described how ordering protocols are established in their institutions, including ordering by an oncologist or pulmonary provider “when the final pathology report is released,” an established protocol where testing is “pathologist-initiated based on reflex policy that is endorsed by oncologists and approved by Med Exec committee,” and via a “request to laboratory chief for approval to send.”

The survey also asked respondents whose institution used a standardized protocol, including reflexing to a multigene panel with most results available before the initial appointment for treatment selection, whether any of the 7 factors shown in Figure 8 had posed barriers during implementation. A minority of these respondents reported no barriers (n = 12, 29.3%). Reimbursement-related challenges were the most frequently cited obstacles. Over half of the respondents identified “reimbursement for broad multigene panels for early-stage NSCLC patients” as a barrier (n = 21, 51.2%), making it the most selected barrier. It was also one of the top barriers identified among sites that had not yet implemented a standardized protocol but were considering one (n = 5, 20.0%). Reimbursement for advanced-stage NSCLC biomarker testing was similarly reported across both groups: 5 respondents considering a standardized protocol (20.0%) and 10 respondents with a standardized protocol, with most test results available before the first-line treatment appointment (24.4%).

Operational and administrative barriers were also common. “Prior authorization” was cited by 16 respondents (39.0%), and the “CMS 14-day rule for inpatients needing biomarker testing” was reported by 14 respondents (34.1%). Among sites still considering implementation, the 14-day rule represents the third most frequently selected barrier (n = 4, 16.0%). A small number of respondents described additional barriers related to workflows and institutional factors, such as “organization unsure how the reflexive testing will be done and current staffing shortage,” “LIS/IT system and staffing,” and a lack of clarity around whether a “multi-gene panel has already been ordered for patients coming to our institution for second opinion/referral consultation.”

DISCUSSION

The survey results provide insights into the current landscape of biomarker testing for NSCLC in the United States, with a particular focus on the adoption and implementation of institutionally standardized, pathologist-ordered reflex protocols. Although more than three-fourths of the survey respondents reported using standardized protocols for comprehensive biomarker testing (n = 86, 77.5%), variability exists in practice patterns, the timing of test results, and the roles of ordering providers. For example, the percentage of respondents who reported having a standardized biomarker testing protocol at their institution (77.5%) dropped to 68.5% of the total respondents when asked if the protocols included reflexing to multigene panels. There was a subsequent decline in the percentage of respondents stating that results were available prior to the initial medical oncology visit when first-line treatment would be determined (47.7%). This gap demonstrates that despite widespread adoption of standardized protocols, biomarker results are often not available at the time of treatment decision-making, limiting their clinical impact.

Pathologist-ordered reflex testing supports more timely result delivery

Among respondents whose institutions used a standardized protocol that includes multigene panels for at least 80% of newly diagnosed patients, with the results being available before the first-line treatment selection visit, most indicated that the pathologist serves as the ordering provider (57.6%), compared with those who reported that the testing is ordered by an oncologist or other clinician (42.2%). Pathologists are uniquely positioned to initiate reflex biomarker testing due to their proximity to the specimen; they can select the most appropriate tumor block, assess tumor cellularity, and optimize the use of limited tissue, recommending alternative testing options, as appropriate.

Although these data do not allow causal inference, this finding is consistent with reports that suggest pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex testing is the optimal workflow for NSCLC.14‐16 Academic centers, which benefit from additional infrastructure and funding, were most likely to use pathologist-ordered reflex workflows. However, smaller or resource-constrained communities and independent sites have also demonstrated success using alternative reflex mechanisms, underscoring that there is no single model for implementation. Each institution must determine, through its multidisciplinary team (MDT), the approach that best fits its operational context.

Perceived barriers differ by setting

Although not all institutions report experiencing obstacles, efforts to standardize biomarker testing workflows may face barriers at multiple stages in the ordering pathway, including both pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex tests and the adoption of standardized multigene testing protocols. When pathologists serve as the ordering providers, most sites report no significant barriers to biomarker test ordering. However, a minority of respondents cited payer denials for pathologist-ordered tests and a lack of MDT consensus as the most frequently cited obstacles (Figure 8). While 29.3% of respondents reported no barriers to implementing reflex multigene protocols, the remainder identified a range of challenges (Figure 8). The most common included reimbursement for broad multigene panels in early-stage patients, prior authorization, and the Medicare 14-day rule. Among sites that implemented a standardized reflex biomarker testing protocol, reimbursement for early-stage broad panels was the predominant concern. In contrast, at sites considering adoption, reimbursement for both advanced- and early-stage panels was cited at comparable levels (approximately 20% of respondents). Prior authorization requirements and the 14-day rule emerged as more prevalent barriers for sites currently employing a broad reflex multigene workflow than for those not yet using one.

Reimbursement issues delay adoption of standardized cancer biomarker protocols

Nearly 40% of participants from institutions without protocols for reflex testing to a broad multigene panel that would yield results by the first oncology appointment reported that they were considering a standardized protocol (Figure 3), whereas 34.4% indicated that they were not. For those considering the adoption of standardized protocols that reflex to a broad multigene panel, most barriers to implementation were due to reimbursement considerations across the biomarker testing pathway. Challenges included managed care restrictions and prior authorization requirements, insurance denials for broad multigene panels, and confusion regarding the 14-day billing rule, which were consistent with the types of barriers reported in the literature on cancer biomarker testing.12,13 The data also reveal that even in settings with established protocols, barriers remain that can impede timely and comprehensive testing. Notably, reimbursement challenges—particularly for broad multigene panels in early-stage NSCLC—were among the most frequently cited obstacles, echoing concerns raised in prior studies.17,18 These findings support the need for additional clarity and consistency in biomarker testing coverage, particularly given that comprehensive, multigene panels are strongly supported by NCCN and other national guidelines, emphasizing the critical gap between guideline recommendations and real-world reimbursement policy in NSCLC.

Regulatory guidance needed to support pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex testing

The study findings indicate broad support for pathology-ordered cancer biomarker testing (91.9%), reflecting a shared view that pathology-driven workflows can improve the timeliness and consistency of molecular results. At the same time, respondents emphasized the importance of safeguards, with two-thirds (66.7%) stating that these should be supported by guidelines developed through MDT consensus. While nearly all respondents also agreed that pathologists should be recognized as the ordering physician for cancer biomarker testing (93.7%), responses were split as to whether this should be allowed in all cases (45.9%) or only within established guidelines (47.7%). Most participants cited the need for regulatory guidance to facilitate the widespread adoption of this practice. Nearly three-fourths of respondents strongly agreed that CMS clarification of ordering clinician language in support of pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex testing would benefit Medicare patients.

Adapting optimized biomarker testing workflows to local environments

Although our study did not evaluate institutional variability directly, these findings must be interpreted within the broader context of heterogeneous workflows and specific Medicare administrative contractor (MAC) regulatory requirements. Institutions vary widely in their clinical workflows, staffing models, and payer environments; therefore, no single reflex testing model is universally applicable. Each site must determine whether a pathologist-ordered reflex testing, standing order, or alternative workflow best fits its operational capacity and regulatory context. Regional policies can influence these decisions. For example, sites under MolDx may rely on the May 2024 CMS clarification affirming that pathologists can serve as the “ordering provider” in certain contexts.19 In contrast, institutions overseen by other MACs may need to navigate local coverage determinations (LCDs) that affect pathologist-ordered reflex testing. Developing an optimized workflow that is tailored to local resources and payer expectations supports more equitable and consistent access to biomarker testing across diverse care settings.

Emerging evidence and guideline alignment

The results of this survey contribute to the growing body of support for pathologist-ordered biomarker reflex testing. While falling short of recommending reflex testing ordered by pathologists, the commentary included in the 2018 clinical practice guidelines update released by the College of American Pathologists/International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer/Association for Molecular Pathology and endorsed by the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO) acknowledges that “reflex-testing initiated by the pathologist is reasonable” when part of an institutional program established through collaboration between oncology and pathology teams.20 However, they provide recommendations around clinically relevant and actionable time frames, such as recommending that laboratories make test results available for oncology team review within 10 working days of receiving the specimen in the testing laboratory.21 Ongoing studies and data collection will be necessary to evaluate and support the incorporation of these workflows into regulatory accreditation guidelines.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted with caution due to several limitations. Because the survey was distributed through professional organization membership networks, the number of individuals who received the survey could not be determined, and an overall response rate could not be calculated. While the study sought to capture a broad, national sample of health care providers involved in molecular biomarker testing, the resulting cohort was relatively modest in size (n = 115), limiting the generalizability of the results and potentially underestimating the diversity of institutional practices across the United States. Respondents from networks with more established biomarker testing programs may overestimate the prevalence of standardized biomarker testing protocols more broadly (77.5%).

Although most respondents were pathologists or molecular pathologists (n = 69 total, 62.2%), this represents a limited sample for drawing conclusions specific to pathologist practice. The concentration of pathologist respondents may also introduce professional bias. For example, the near-unanimous support for CMS recognition of pathologists as ordering providers (93.7%) should be interpreted in the context of this limitation. Additionally, while pathologist-initiated reflex testing has the potential to streamline and standardize biomarker ordering, the risk of overtesting, along with associated costs and harms, in cases where comprehensive biomarker results may not ultimately inform treatment decisions, warrants consideration. Implementation within a multidisciplinary policy framework, as supported by most study respondents (91.9%), may serve as an important safeguard against these potential risks.

Consistent with the study’s aim to examine the perspectives on standardized protocols for broad, multigene biomarker testing, the survey relied on self-reported practices rather than on audited institutional data. Although audited data would have supported more direct, objective conclusions about test-ordering patterns, obtaining such information was not logistically feasible. As a result, responses may reflect perceived rather than actual workflow performance, particularly regarding turnaround times, ordering authority, and the proportion of patients receiving comprehensive testing.

Variability in how respondents interpret terms such as “standardized protocol,” “comprehensive testing,” or “pathologist-ordered” may also affect response consistency. While “comprehensive testing” was explicitly defined in the context of the survey question to refer to the 2024 NCCN recommendations (“Methods,” p. 4), the question did not contain an explicit definition for “standardized protocol.” The definition of this term was assumed based on the NCCN guidelines.

In addition, the survey focused on a subset of potential barriers to pathologist-ordered reflex testing and standardized reflex-testing protocols (Figure 8) and therefore may not have captured the broader operational, financial, or regulatory challenges experienced across institutions. The survey also did not assess patient-level factors (eg, comorbidities, tissue adequacy, and social determinants of health) or institutional constraints (eg, staffing, Laboratory Information System [LIS] capabilities, and local coverage determinations) that may influence testing pathways. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of the study captures practices and perceptions at a single point in time during a period of rapid evolution in biomarker testing, reimbursement policy, and multidisciplinary workflow models. As such, the findings may not reflect emerging changes in institutional processes or regulatory guidance.

Despite these limitations, the study provides timely insights into how institutions are approaching reflex biomarker testing for NSCLC and highlights persistent operational and structural barriers that warrant further investigation.

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

This study provides insights into the current landscape of biomarker testing for NSCLC in the United States, with a particular focus on the adoption and implementation of standardized, pathologist-ordered reflex biomarker testing protocols for NSCLC. Although most survey respondents reported using standardized protocols for comprehensive biomarker testing (n = 86, 77.5%; Figure 1), variability exists in practice patterns, timing of test results, and the roles of ordering providers. These findings highlight several opportunities to strengthen biomarker testing workflows and support the broader adoption of standardized reflex protocols. Future research examining institutional models in greater depth, including comparative evaluations of pathologist-ordered reflex, standing order, and hybrid workflows across diverse practice settings, could help identify the operational features that most reliably support timely and comprehensive testing.

Further work is needed to clarify the regulatory environment surrounding the ordering authority. Respondents overwhelmingly supported CMS recognition of pathologists as ordering providers under MDT-approved protocols for biomarker testing, underscoring the need for national guidance that reduces ambiguity and variation across Medicare Administrative Contractors. Studies that evaluate the impact of such clarification—on turnaround times, payer denials, and treatment selection—would provide valuable evidence for policymakers and professional societies.

In addition, reimbursement challenges emerged as a central barrier, particularly for broad multigene panels in early-stage disease. Future studies should explore payer policies, prior authorization processes, and laboratory benefit manager practices to identify opportunities for streamlining and standardization. Economic analyses may also help quantify the downstream value of timely reflex biomarker testing, including reductions in treatment delays and avoidance of redundant or sequential testing.

Finally, as reflex biomarker testing becomes more common, ongoing data collection will be essential to inform efforts toward standardized reflex biomarker testing protocols. Professional societies may benefit from real-world evidence describing the safety, feasibility, and clinical impact of pathologist-ordered biomarker testing across various institutional contexts. Such evidence could support the integration of optimized reflex biomarker testing workflows into national recommendations and help ensure more equitable access to comprehensive biomarker testing for patients with NSCLC.

The adoption of institutional standardized protocols that reflex biomarker testing to a broad multigene panel for patients with NSCLC represents a promising approach to improving the timeliness and completeness of biomarker testing. By combining evidence-based workflows with clarified regulatory and payor guidance and site-specific adaptation, health care institutions can advance toward more equitable and effective patient care, while generating the evidence base needed to support broader guideline adoption.
