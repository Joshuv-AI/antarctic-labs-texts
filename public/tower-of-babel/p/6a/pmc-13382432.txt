PeaDetect dataset: Curated audio recordings of Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus) vocalizations for bioacoustic monitoring
Authors: Rukshani Puvanendran, Asanka P Sayakkara, Sampath S Seneviratne, Titus Jayarathna, Roshan G Ragel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis data article describes PeaDetect, the first curated audio dataset specifically designed for detecting Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus) vocalizations. The dataset contains 2,950 five-second audio clips (4.1 hours total duration), evenly balanced between peafowl presence (1,475 clips) and absence (1,475 clips) classes. Recordings were sourced from two public repositories: Xeno-Canto (332 original peafowl recordings from India and Sri Lanka, 2003-2024, other bird species vocalizations) and Freesound (environmental sounds and insects from Sri Lankan habitats). All audio was standardized to 44.1 kHz, 16-bit, stereo WAV format and segmented into 5-second clips. Each clip was manually validated by an ornithologist, with inter-annotator agreement assessed on 20% of the dataset (Cohen's kappa = 0.905). To prevent data leakage, source-aware 5-fold cross-validation splits are provided, ensuring all clips from the same original recording remain within the same fold. The dataset includes comprehensive metadata with geographic (87.1% India, 11.9% Sri Lanka, 1.0% other regions), temporal (2003-2024, all seasons), and acoustic specifications. This dataset supports research in bioacoustic monitoring, human-wildlife conflict mitigation, and machine learning for conservation.Keywords: Bioacoustics, Human-peafowl conflict, Vocalization analysis, Machine learning, Dataset, Conservation technology, Soundscape ecology

Background

Human-wildlife conflict in agricultural regions of South Asia poses significant challenges to farming communities, particularly in Sri Lanka and India [1,2]. Iconic species include elephants, tigers, monkeys and peafowls. Among them, the Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus) frequently raids crops [3,4]. The Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus; ITIS TSN: 176113) presents a compelling intersection of ecological, cultural, and socioeconomic challenges in South Asian agricultural landscapes [5]. As a legally protected and culturally revered species, it cannot be harmed even when it causes substantial crop losses through raiding behaviour [5,6]. Traditional mitigation methods such as scarecrows, auditory deterrents, and community-based monitoring have shown limited effectiveness due to their reactive nature and lack of scalability [6]. Technology-driven monitoring approaches offer a promising direction for more sustainable and proactive mitigation strategies. Bioacoustic monitoring offers a non-invasive and scalable solution by enabling species detection through vocalization analysis, allowing continuous surveillance in agricultural landscapes [7,8]. However, the development of automated detection systems has been constrained by the absence of a dedicated, well-annotated dataset for peafowl vocalizations. Existing bioacoustic datasets focus on multi-species classification or general environmental sounds, and are not tailored for species-specific detection in human-wildlife conflict contexts. To address this gap, we present PeaDetect, the first curated audio dataset specifically designed for detecting Indian peafowl vocalizations to support the development of intelligent monitoring systems and data-driven mitigation strategies.

Data Description

The PeaDetect dataset consists of 2,950 five-second audio clips in WAV format, totalling 4.1 hours of recordings. The dataset is organized into three main directories as shown in tree structure below.

Full_Dataset

The Full_Dataset/ directory contains a total of 2,950 audio clips, consisting of presence and absence classes. The presence class includes 1,475 audio clips containing vocalizations of the Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus). The absence class includes 1,475 audio clips containing no peafowl vocalizations, comprising other bird species calls and environmental noises. Each audio file is stored in WAV format with a sampling rate of 44.1 kHz, 16-bit depth, and stereo channels. All clips have a fixed duration of 5 seconds.

Ecological metadata language

To ensure full compliance with FAIR (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) data principles and professional ecological data standards, dataset metadata was documented using Ecological Metadata Language (EML), an ISO-compliant XML-based standard widely adopted in ecological and biodiversity informatics. EML was selected over generic metadata formats because it natively supports structured fields for taxonomic classification, geographic bounding coordinates, temporal coverage, methodological provenance, and data licensing. The EML record for PeaDetect encodes the species taxonomy (Pavo cristatus; ITIS TSN: 176113) [9], recording geography (India and Sri Lanka), temporal span (2003–2024), preprocessing pipeline, annotation protocol, and licensing terms.

Dataset statistics

The PeaDetect dataset contains 4.1 hours of 2,950 five-second WAV (44.1 kHz, 16-bit, stereo) audio samples. The dataset is perfectly balanced, with 1,475 presence clips (50%) containing peafowl vocalizations and 1,475 absence clips (50%) containing no peafowl sounds. Within the absence class, 1,092 clips (37% of the total dataset) are from Xeno-Canto (XC), featuring vocalizations of other bird species that coexist with peafowl in South Asian habitats [[9], [10]]. The remaining 383 absence clips (13% of the total dataset) are from Freesound (FS), comprising environmental and other animal sounds [11]. Figure 1 presents comprehensive statistics for the dataset. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Data collection

Xeno-canto repository

Audio recordings of Indian peafowl (Pavo cristatus) were collected from the Xeno-Canto online repository (www.xeno-canto.org) [10]. The repository was searched using the scientific name Pavo cristatus (ITIS TSN: 176113). The search yielded 332 original recordings spanning from 2003 to 2024. Only recordings with quality ratings A through E (as assigned by Xeno-Canto contributors) were included in the initial collection. Each recording was downloaded along with available metadata, including: Catalog number/Source ID, Recordist name, Recording date, Recording time, Country /location, Elevation, Recording type (call, song, alarm call), Quality rating (A through E).

It is important to note that the term "peacock'', which is widely used colloquially across South Asia, including Sri Lanka and India, where this study is primarily focussed, refers strictly to the adult male of the species in formal ornithological usage; the female is termed a “peahen”, and the correct collective noun is “peafowl”. Conducting searches using “peacock” alone would therefore have systematically excluded female (peahen) and juvenile vocalizations, introducing sex-based sampling bias into the presence class. Therefore, all searches were accordingly conducted using the collective term “peafowl” together with the binomial Pavo cristatus. This ensures that the presence class captures the vocal repertoire of the species irrespective of sex or age. Throughout this paper, “peafowl” is used as the species appropriate collective noun encompassing both sexes. The taxonomic hierarchy of the target species (Indian Peafowl) is shown in Table 4.

Freesound repository

Negative samples (audio containing no peafowl vocalizations) especially environmental noises and animal sounds were collected from the Freesound online repository (www.freesound.org) [11]. Each file was downloaded using its unique sound ID along with available metadata. The recordings of the bird species that share similar acoustic patterns in Sri Lankan context are collected from Xeno Canto.

The negative class (absence) was deliberately constructed to maximize acoustic diversity and ecological realism, comprising 19 distinct categories drawn from both Xeno-Canto (12 avian species) and Freesound (7 environmental/animal categories) as shown in Table 5. Avian negative samples were selected on the basis of ecological co-occurrence and acoustic similarity to peafowl: species such as Junglefowl, Guineafowl, Koel, Coucal, and Spurfowl share overlapping frequency ranges and habitats with Pavo cristatus in South Asian agricultural landscapes, making them the most likely sources of false positives in real-world deployment. Non avian Freesound samples, including domestic animals (cat, pig), primates (monkey), amphibians (frog), insects, and anthropogenic noise (vehicle engine, horn), capture the full acoustic profile of farming environments where the detection system would operate. This deliberate multi-category construction ensures that trained classifiers learn peafowl specific acoustic features rather than simply discriminating bird calls from silence, substantially strengthening the eco logical validity and deployment robustness of models trained on PeaDetect.

Inter-annotator agreement

Inter-annotator agreement was assessed on the 590-clip validation subset. Two independent annotators labeled these clips following the same protocol. Agreement was quantified using Cohen's Kappa coefficient. The obtained k = 0.905 indicates ``almost perfect agreement'' [13].

Bias mitigation and data leakage prevention

Bioacoustic datasets are often subject to several potential biases [[7], [8]]. To address these biases, the PeaDetect dataset incorporates recordings from diverse sources, applies uniform preprocessing procedures, and maintains a carefully balanced distribution between classes.

Source-aware fold creation

To prevent data leakage, a source-aware 5-fold cross-validation split was created [14]. Within each fold, class balance was verified and maintained. This ensures that all clips from the same original recording remain in the same fold, preventing acoustically similar clips from appearing in both training and testing sets. Limitations

Most of the recordings (87.1%) come from India, with another 11.9% from Sri Lanka. The sounds might be different at different locations enough to affect performance. The dataset has plenty of the familiar ``may-awe'' calls that peafowl is known for. But other vocalizations such as alarm calls, courtship calls, and juvenile calls are underrepresented.
