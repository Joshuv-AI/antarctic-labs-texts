Outpatient Care Fragmentation and Patient Satisfaction With Care
Authors: Isabella Ratto, Neelam Ahmed, Mengyuan Chen, Nasim Ferdows, Gary J Young, Brady Post
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThe aim of this study is to examine the association between outpatient care fragmentation and self‐reported patient satisfaction for Medicare beneficiaries.Study Setting and DesignWe measured outpatient fragmentation, that is, how dispersed patients’ care was, at the physician and practice levels with a reverse Herfindahl–Hirschman concentration index (HHI). We used three domains of satisfaction with care: access, communication, and coordination and quality. We tested the relationship between fragmentation and patient satisfaction using ordinary least squares regressions, adjusting for age, income, race/ethnicity, clinical severity, and care utilization.Data Sources and Analytic SampleWe conducted a retrospective analysis using secondary data from 2020 to 2022. We used the Medicare Current Beneficiary Survey and Medicare claims, both collected annually by CMS.Principal FindingsOutpatient fragmentation was common in our sample (n = 2294), with an average reverse HHI of 0.39 at the provider level and 0.22 at the practice level. Rural beneficiaries experienced more fragmentation at the practice level than their urban (reverse HHI 0.37 vs. 0.19, p < 0.001) or suburban (0.37 vs. 0.28, p < 0.001) counterparts. Provider‐level fragmentation, although higher (reverse HHI 0.44 rural vs. 0.39 suburban vs. 0.39 urban), was not statistically different between populations. Non‐urban beneficiaries reported lower satisfaction with accessibility (9.82 suburban vs. 10.05 urban and 9.65 rural vs. 10.05 urban, p‐values 0.004 and 0.003) and coordination and quality (13.33 suburban vs. 13.64 urban and 13.37 rural vs. 13.64 urban, p‐values 0.002 and 0.115). Communication scores were similar between populations. Our regression models did not establish a statistically significant relationship between outpatient fragmentation and any of our satisfaction with care scales.ConclusionsRurality was strongly linked to greater care fragmentation, while fragmentation was not linked to differences in patient satisfaction. Health systems should note that reduced fragmentation may improve health outcomes, but these may not immediately translate to improved patient experiences.Keywords: care fragmentation, medicare, outpatient care delivery, patient satisfaction

Introduction

Patients today receive care from a greater number of healthcare providers than in previous decades. A comparative study observed that, on average, the Medicare beneficiary population saw 47% more primary care providers (increasing from 0.96 to 1.41) and 44% more medical specialists (increasing from 1.77 to 2.54) in 2019 than in 2000 [1]. This rise in fragmentation, by which individuals obtain care from multiple providers or medical practices, is understood to stem from the increasing specialization of medicine and from insurance structures that tie coverage to providers’ in‐network status [2].

Given this increase, care fragmentation is of high interest to researchers. Some studies have assessed the relationship between fragmentation and clinical outcomes, often finding that fragmentation negatively affects care quality [3, 4]. Specifically, fragmented care has been associated with increased morbidity, treatment duplication, and unnecessary imaging [5]. However, little research has evaluated patients’ subjective experiences, for example, their reported experience with the care they receive. Patients who see several different providers may report more frustration with communication or coordination, leading to lower satisfaction with care. Significant barriers to inter‐provider communication exist when physicians from different practices share a patient [6]. Alternatively, having multiple providers may increase satisfaction with care due to additional availability, access, or greater information. The net effect of fragmentation on different components of the patient experience remains unclear, even while this consideration is growing in relevance, and it is increasingly factored into reimbursement by payment systems [7].

Our analysis extends existing work by evaluating patient satisfaction with care as an outcome, using a nationally representative sample of Medicare beneficiaries. We link administrative Medicare claims data with survey data and apply a multi‐dimensional model to analyze the effect of outpatient fragmentation on patient satisfaction with three domains of care: accessibility, communication, and coordination and quality. We also introduce a regional comparison by assigning beneficiaries to geographic groups, allowing us to understand whether fragmentation and its relationship with patient satisfaction differs between urban and rural populations. We hypothesize that greater outpatient fragmentation is associated with lower patient satisfaction, especially in rural areas. Our results apply to policy conversations that address the increasingly fragmented modern healthcare system and those related to rural health.

Methods

Data and Sample

We used the 2020–2022 Medicare Current Beneficiary Surveys (MCBS). The MCBS is a nationally representative, longitudinal survey administered in person three times per year by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, with over 10,000 respondents annually. We linked MCBS survey data to Medicare Part A inpatient and Part B outpatient claims data, which provide detailed information on patient characteristics, clinical profile, and care utilization (more below). We selected these datasets because they offer comprehensive information about our key variables: The MCBS captures patient satisfaction, and the claims include variables necessary for our calculation of fragmentation, such as location of care and provider seen for each visit.

Our overall sample included distinct Medicare beneficiaries (i.e., beneficiaries with responses in multiple years were not included twice) who completed the 2020, 2021, or 2022 MCBS; for those with responses in multiple years, the latest year was used. Inclusion criteria required beneficiaries to be aged 65 or older, reside in one of the 50 US states or the District of Columbia, be continuously enrolled in fee‐for‐service Medicare (Parts A and B) for the full calendar year of observation, have at least one outpatient, non‐emergency department claim during the survey year, and provide responses to all MCBS questions used in our patient satisfaction scales. We excluded individuals enrolled in Medicare Advantage due to the unavailability of complete claims data. More details about our sample are in Appendix 1. Our study was deemed exempt by our organization's institutional review board.

Key Variables

Our primary independent variable was outpatient care fragmentation. We calculated fragmentation using a reverse Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI). HHI is a common measure of concentration versus fragmentation, where 0 traditionally represents maximum dispersion, and 1 represents maximum concentration. To make our results more intuitive (i.e., so that higher values correspond to higher fragmentation), we follow the precedent in the literature. Beneficiaries who received outpatient care from one provider have HHIs of 0, and those with care split across many providers have values closer to 1 [8, 9]. We used HHI instead of alternative measures of fragmentation (i.e., Bice–Boxerman's Continuity of Care Index or Breslau's Usual Provider of Care measure) because it accounts for both the number of providers seen by an individual and the concentration of their visits with each provider [10, 11]. For example, a patient with five visits to one physician and one visit to another has a reverse HHI of 0.28, indicating relatively continuous care. Comparatively, a patient with three visits to one physician and one visit each with three other providers has a reverse HHI of 0.63, reflecting their more fragmented care across the same total number of visits.

We calculated two reverse HHIs for each beneficiary based on national provider identification (NPI) numbers: one at the provider level (i.e., calculating HHI based on how many different clinicians provided care) and one at the practice level (i.e., calculating HHI based on how many different healthcare organizations provided care). This practice‐level measure does not necessarily capture how many distinct care facilities a patient visited. It reflects the degree of continuity in the employing institution of their providers. An individual referred to a specialist within the same care organization as their primary care physician would be reported as two visits to a single practice. These HHI measures helped us to characterize multiple forms of fragmentation and allowed for the possibility that the effects on satisfaction with care will differ by fragmentation type. The literature supports this distinction; several researchers have calculated outpatient care continuity at the practice level [11, 12]. More details about our HHI measure are included in Appendix 2.

For our dependent variables, we adopted three scales of patient satisfaction from the MCBS based on existing literature: accessibility, communication, and care coordination and quality [13]. Each scale was constructed from a set of MCBS questions with Likert responses from 1 to 4, where higher numbers indicated better experiences. To capture overall satisfaction with care for each patient, we summed the Likert responses included in the scales.

The four survey questions comprising the care coordination and quality scale, along with the three questions comprising the accessibility scale, came from Bernal and colleagues’ analysis of MCBS data [14]. The four questions comprising the communication scale replicated the “How Well Providers Communicate with Patients” measure from the Consumer Assessment of Healthcare Providers and Systems Clinician and Group Survey 3.0 [15]. The survey questions are shown in Appendix 3.

Although these scales are not designed specifically to target patients’ experiences with fragmented care, the questions they pose are still likely to be sensitive to such experiences. Consider, for example, the communication scale. When care is fragmented, patients often become responsible for reporting their medical history to each of the various providers from whom they seek care. The time and effort this task entails can negatively affect the therapeutic relationship as providers have less time for empathetic, patient‐centered communication, which previous research indicates to be of much value to patients [16, 17]. Accordingly, fragmented care is likely to have some impact on how satisfied patients report they are with interpersonal communication as measured in the scale we used for the study.

To test the validity of these scales, we conducted confirmatory factor analysis on the survey questions that had been grouped together in the previously noted studies. As indicated by the root mean squared error of approximation (0.044), comparative fit index (0.980), Tucker–Lewis index (0.973), and standardized root mean squared residual (0.029), our mapping of survey items to latent variables (i.e., scales) was appropriate. We further calculated Cronbach's alpha for each scale to assess internal consistency. Taking these additional steps gave us confidence not only that the survey items were appropriately grouped together to measure the latent constructs of interest but also that the items within each scale were measuring unique dimension of patients’ satisfaction and doing so consistently (see Appendix 3).

We adjusted for key demographic and clinical covariates. These included beneficiary age, race/ethnicity, sex, and household income. As the literature established a positive relationship between fragmentation and care utilization, we used individuals’ aggregate emergency department and inpatient claims during their survey year as control variables [12, 18]. Researchers have also found a connection between fragmentation and clinical complexity. Patients with relatively high comorbidity burdens, multiple clinical conditions, often consume more care, making them susceptible to decreased continuity and, to some degree, its adverse effects [12, 19]. However, some studies have challenged this view noting that the most clinically complex patients often require a multidisciplinary care team. Therefore, fragmentation's negative impact on quality is possibly more harmful to the population with an intermediate disease burden [4, 20].

To assess clinical complexity, we calculated the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), a clinical complexity score that is based on weights assigned to 17 chronic diseases, for each member of our sample and included this measure as a covariate [21]. CCI scores are generally associated with an increased risk of mortality and hospitalization. Scores of 0–1 are considered mild comorbidity, 2–4 moderate, and 5 or greater severe [22]. Diseases of interest were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, 10th version. Beneficiaries in the top 1% of care users, based on number of outpatient claims, and the top 1% for the CCI score were excluded from our analysis to mitigate outlier influence and in recognition of the established relationship between care consumption and fragmentation [9].

Further, because geographic differences in physician availability or referral tendencies can affect fragmentation, we assigned beneficiaries to geographic population groups (urban, suburban, or rural) based on Rural–Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code classifications created by the USDA Economic Research Service. This classification system assigns numerical values to census tracts that range from 1, highly metropolitan, to 10, highly rural [8, 23]. We rely on RUCA codes rather than other geographic indicators, as it is likely that commuting patterns and census tracts are influential in dictating where beneficiaries travel to seek care.

Our three geographic groups, referred to as urban, suburban, and rural throughout this paper, reflect differences in beneficiaries’ adjacency to metropolitan areas rather than just the size of the county in which they live. We defined urban using metropolitan RUCA codes (1–3), suburban with metropolitan adjacent codes (4, 5, 7, 8), and rural with metropolitan non‐adjacent codes (6, 9, 10). The RUCA codes assigned to each population group and their definitions are in Appendix 4. As previous studies of fragmentation have not considered patient location in this way, we have based these groupings on those used in the more general literature of geographic comparisons (see Approach #3 taken by Whitney Zahnd and co‐authors) [24]. No uniform system of classifying RUCA codes exists, nor is there a necessarily preferred method [25]. Given our focus on fragmentation in this work, adjacency‐based geographic groupings provide unique advantages. Medical providers are concentrated in the urban United States [26]. It follows that beneficiaries with easier access to these areas will have an enhanced ability to remain within one health system than those living in non‐adjacent geographies.

Analysis

Descriptive statistics included frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and means with standard deviations for continuous variables. Unpooled t‐tests were used to compare differences across urban, suburban, and rural respondents. We used linear ordinary least squares regression models to evaluate the relationship between outpatient fragmentation (reverse HHI) and each of our three patient satisfaction scales: accessibility, communication, and coordination and quality. Results

Unweighted Analysis

Of the 27,521 Medicare beneficiaries that participated in the 2021 or the 2022 MCBS, 2294 were included in our sample. Many respondents were excluded because either they were not continuously enrolled in fee‐for‐service Medicare during the study period or did not answer all the MCBS survey questions used in our scales. Across the full sample, the average patient age was 77.7 (7.3) years, and 54% were female. By race/ethnicity, 90% of respondents were White, 5% Black, 1% Asian, and 2% Hispanic. Geographically, 30% of the sample resided in a rural area, and the average household income was $83,283 (97,835). Full descriptions of beneficiary characteristics are presented in Appendix 5.

Our sample was demographically similar across geographic groups. Patients in urban areas had significantly higher incomes than those in both suburban and rural areas (p < 0.001). In contrast, rural (8.23 vs. 6.01, p < 0.001) and suburban (7.67 vs. 6.01, p < 0.001) groups had more outpatient visits than urban groups, reflecting greater ambulatory care utilization. Rural patients also saw more distinct outpatient providers than urban patients (3.35 vs. 2.80, p = 0.007). The difference in the number of providers seen by the suburban and urban groups was not statistically significant. Further, inpatient care utilization did not differ significantly across the three groups.

Average fragmentation values for our sample (0.39 provider level and 0.22 practice level) indicated that most beneficiaries’ visits were moderately concentrated with a subset of the providers they saw. A 0.39 reverse‐HHI value, for example, might correspond to a patient who received most of their care from one physician (three visits) but saw a specialist once. We observed that fragmentation was higher at the provider level than at the practice level for all groups. In other words, patients were more likely to see multiple distinct providers within the same practice than to receive care across multiple practices. The difference between these measures was larger for urban beneficiaries (reverse HHI 0.39 at the provider level vs. 0.19 at the practice level) than for suburban (0.39 vs. 0.28) or rural (0.44 vs. 0.37) beneficiaries. This suggests that urban beneficiaries more often remained within a single medical practice even when seeking care from distinct providers when compared to non‐urban beneficiaries. Although both fragmentation measures were highest among rural beneficiaries, only practice‐level reverse‐HHI was significantly different, compared to the other groups (p < 0.001 vs. urban and vs. suburban). Satisfaction with care for all members of our sample was high with an average score of 38.86 out of 44 possible or 88.3% satisfied. Of the three scales that we constructed, the highest‐scoring scale was communication (15.35 out of 16 possible or 95.94% satisfied). Scores for accessibility (9.97 out of 12 or 83.08%) and coordination and quality (13.55 out of 16 or 84.69%) were lower. Overall satisfaction with care was significantly higher for urban beneficiaries, compared to their suburban (39.04 vs. 38.46, p = 0.004) and rural (39.04 vs. 38.34, p = 0.033) counterparts. A similar pattern was observed for accessibility, where urban beneficiaries had significantly higher scores than those in suburban (10.05 vs. 9.82, p = 0.004) and rural areas (10.05 vs. 9.65, p = 0.003). There were no significant differences in satisfaction with communication between the three geographic groups, with average scores of 15.36 for urban, 15.32 for suburban, and 15.32 for rural beneficiaries. Coordination and quality scores differed significantly only between the urban and suburban groups (13.64 vs. 13.33, p‐value = 0.002); differences between urban and rural groups were not statistically significant. Ordinary Least Squares Regression Models

Next, we estimated our regression models for our three satisfaction scales. Provider‐ and practice‐level fragmentation were not significantly associated with satisfaction scores in our sample or with any of the individual variables included in our scales. While the associations varied across the three dimensions of satisfaction domains and geographic groups, only one was statistically significant. Overall, outpatient care fragmentation did not show a consistent statistically significant relationship with satisfaction scores.

The result for highly fragmented care (reverse‐HHI = 1) was more negative at the practice level, where satisfaction scores were associated with a decrease of 0.202 points (95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.849 to 0.445) on average. At the provider level, fragmentation was associated with a smaller decrease of 0.127 points (95% CI: −0.662 to 0.408). Overall satisfaction was not associated with provider‐level fragmentation for urban, suburban, or rural patients.

Higher levels of provider‐level fragmentation had a limited statistical association with our three satisfaction with care scales. Fragmentation was generally estimated to be positively associated with patients’ accessibility scores across geographies and fragmentation types. This statistical association was strongest for rural beneficiaries. The relationship between fragmentation and our other satisfaction scores was less consistent among population groups. Our models predicted fragmentation to negatively affect communication for our urban and suburban populations but to have a positive effect for the rural population. The coordination and quality scale coefficients were the most inconsistent. Results for the individual variables included in our satisfaction scales were also highly inconsistent between fragmentation type and population groups. Discussion

In this study, we used survey data and medical claims for Medicare beneficiaries to evaluate outpatient fragmentation and patient satisfaction. Overall, we found relatively high rates of patient satisfaction, particularly among urban patients. Further, we found a moderately low level of fragmentation at the provider and practice levels, indicating that most patients had some degree of concentration across their outpatient visits. Further, we found significantly higher fragmentation among rural patients. However, neither practice‐level nor provider‐level fragmentation was significantly associated with patient‐reported satisfaction with care. Together, these findings suggest that geography is likely to play a more important role than the fragmentation of care in determining patient satisfaction.

Our findings add to the existing literature. Fragmentation was lower at the practice level than the provider level for the entire sample and for each of our geographic groups. This aligns with previous studies, which observed that patients’ visits, though often spread across several individual providers, tend to cluster within practices though they are often with several individual providers [11]. Moreover, while all geographic groups experienced similar rates of fragmentation at the provider level, at the practice level, fragmentation was significantly higher for rural beneficiaries than for the urban group. Previous studies have not compared fragmentation across geographic groups. This represents an important contribution offered by our work. These findings align with what is known about care composition in rural areas where beneficiaries have less choice in providers and may struggle to remain within one health system, especially when seeking specialty care [27].

Overall satisfaction with care was highest for urban beneficiaries. Additionally, suburban and rural patients reported significantly lower accessibility and coordination and quality scores than the urban group. These results only partially align with previous studies that have found higher satisfaction among rural populations. Specifically, a study comparing rural and non‐rural hospitals found that rural hospitals significantly outperform their urban counterparts in areas including provider communication, staff responsiveness, and cleanliness [28]. Our results may reflect differences in how communication is experienced versus how care is coordinated across fragmented systems. While our lower coordination and quality scores for rural and suburban beneficiaries deviate from earlier findings, they may capture structural limitations in provider availability or system‐level integration. At the same time, lower accessibility scores among rural respondents are consistent with prior work documenting limited inpatient provider availability and geographic barriers to hospital care [29].

Our OLS models did not identify a statistically significant relationship between care fragmentation and any dimension of patient satisfaction. Although prior studies have reported significant negative associations between fragmentation and satisfaction, these studies generally relied on binary measures of fragmentation [3]. We extend these results by using a continuous measure of care fragmentation, constructing satisfaction scales from Medicare survey data, and analyzing a nationally representative sample. Our findings indicate that the relationship between care continuity and patient satisfaction is more nuanced. While the results for communication and coordination and quality varied at both the provider and practice levels of fragmentation and by geographic group, fragmentation was generally positively associated with care accessibility.

These findings raise important questions about patient surveys designed to capture perceptions of care. While care continuity is linked to measurable improvements in outcomes, patients may not always directly associate fragmentation with decreased satisfaction. This disconnect suggests that satisfaction measures may reflect individual or logistical aspects of care and that these measures have a more subtle relationship with structural quality issues. Further research should consider how satisfaction measures interact with other dimensions of healthcare performance.

Given prior evidence linking decreased care continuity to poorer care quality, our finding of higher fragmentation in rural areas highlights an important area for policy attention. Efforts to improve continuity in rural settings may help reduce longstanding disparities in care quality between rural and urban populations. Policies that expand provider availability and incentivize long‐term practice in rural communities may reduce fragmentation by increasing patients’ ability to maintain relationships with a consistent set of providers. Additionally, efforts to maintain in‐network status of providers may prove beneficial. Policy approaches such as rural physician incentive programs or improved telehealth infrastructure could be particularly effective. Initiatives to promote structured partnership models between healthcare networks, which have increasingly emerged in rural areas to address regional challenges in care delivery, are another intervention worth considering [30]. Rural healthcare consolidation is potentially beneficial in addressing fragmentation by preventing hospital closures and creating access to large provider networks though such consolidation requires additional study given the lack of consensus regarding effects on costs and patient outcomes [31]. These findings may inform ongoing federal and state efforts aimed at strengthening care delivery and improving patient satisfaction.

Our study had several limitations. First, we focused exclusively on patients in the traditional Medicare program. As such, our results may not reflect the experiences of patients with Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, or commercial health coverage. However, with over 30 million Americans currently enrolled in traditional Medicare, the implications of our findings are still broadly relevant. Further, the phrasing of the MCBS question may limit the connection between beneficiaries’ satisfaction responses and their whole care experience, as they are inclined to think about their most recent outpatient visit. Additionally, while we adjusted for many covariates known to influence patient satisfaction, unobserved confounders may remain, such as the length of time between patients’ visits [32] or provider characteristics like teaching status [33]. Accordingly, further research is warranted to investigate the causal mechanisms by which hospital‐physician integration might affect patients’ satisfaction with care.

In conclusion, research focused on patient‐reported perceptions of care offers the opportunity to assess quality relative to structural and process features of healthcare delivery in the United States.
