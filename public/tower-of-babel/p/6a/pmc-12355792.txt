Association between estimated glomerular filtration rate and memory function mediated by brain Gray matter volumes: a cross-sectional study using the ADNI database
Authors: Xiaoyu Wang, Jiafeng Wang, Qianqian Xie, Yan Wang, Chunyan Li, Lin Song, Yifeng Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEstimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is associated with gray matter volumes (GMVs) in memory-related regions, but its relationship with memory function and the mediating role of GMVs remain unclear. We aimed to investigate whether eGFR levels are associated with memory function and whether GMVs mediate this association.MethodsThis study utilized data from the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI). Participants were categorized into eGFR quartiles. Memory function was assessed using the ADNI composite memory score (ADNI_MEM). GMVs were normalized to total intracranial volume. Multivariate linear regression models examined associations between eGFR, GMVs, and memory function. Structural equation modeling (SEM) assessed the mediating role of GMVs.ResultsA total of 1311 older adults were included. Higher eGFR was associated with better memory function (adjusted β = 0.33, 95% CI: 0.14–0.50), particularly in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients (adjusted β = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.23–0.67). Higher eGFR was also associated with larger GMVs in key memory-related regions, including the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, and middle temporal gyrus (all P < 0.001). GMVs significantly mediated the relationship between eGFR and memory function (all mediation P < 0.05).ConclusionsHigher eGFR levels are positively associated with memory performance, particularly in individuals with MCI, and this association is mediated by GMVs. These findings suggest that renal function may be a critical factor in memory decline risk assessment.

Introduction

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), due to its ease of measurement, is the most direct and widely utilized clinical indicator of kidney function [1]. Several studies have reported that a rapid decline in eGFR was associated with global cognitive decline and the occurrence of vascular dementia [2–4]. Recently, it was reported that lower level of eGFR significantly increased the likelihood for mild and advanced cognitive dysfunction [5]. Thus, declining renal function and reduced eGFR may play significant roles in cognitive impairment [6].

Studies indicated that memory decline often represented one of the earliest signs of cognitive decline [7]. Brain gray matter volume (GMV) atrophy has been shown to play a critical role in the progression of memory impairment [8, 9]. For instance, researchers have suggested the association between hippocampal atrophy, particularly in the left hippocampus, with poorer memory performance [10]. Furthermore, the entorhinal cortex was thought to be integral to memory retrieval and the formation of associative memories [11, 12], while the fusiform gyrus has been implicated in facial recognition and semantic memory processing [13, 14]. Additionally, the middle temporal gyrus was associated with verbal short-term memory performance and could predict the memory for emotional events [15, 16]Meanwhile, other studies have indicated that a potential association between lower eGFR level and the aforementioned brain GMV atrophy [17, 18]. However, there was a lack of study to explore whether eGFR levels associated with memory function, and whether brain GMVs played a crucial role in this association.

Therefore, based on the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database, we aimed to conduct a large-scale investigation to explore whether eGFR levels associated with memory function, and whether brain GMV mediated this association.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

ADNI was launched in 2004 and has enrolled a diverse population of participants, including individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), Alzheimer’s disease (AD), and healthy elderly controls [19]. It involved collaboration among multiple research centers across North America, including prestigious institutions such as the Mayo Clinic, University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA), University of Pennsylvania, and many others. These centers contributed to a rich dataset that supports the investigation of various aspects of AD, including clinical, neuroimaging, and biomarker assessments [20].

To date, ADNI has completed four primary phases: ADNI-1, ADNI-GO, ADNI-2, and ADNI-3 [20]. Due to the absence of serum creatinine measurements in ADNI-3, it was not possible to obtain eGFR values; therefore, we included only participants from the first three phases in our study. We excluded individuals with missing data for age, sex, race, and serum creatinine, which would prevent the calculation of eGFR. Additionally, participants with missing neuropsychological test results or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) data were also excluded in our study.

Data collection

Baseline demographic data included age, sex, race, years of education, Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score, composite scores for memory (ADNI-MEM), medical history, such as smoking history and alcohol intake history, creatinine levels, baseline brain GMVs, including hippocampus, entorhinal, fusiform, and middle temporal gyrus. Specifically, ADNI-MEM was derived primarily from original scores on the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test (RAVLT), Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale-Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-Cog), Logical Memory, and the MMSE [21]. These scores were analyzed using recoded variables in Mplus software [21]. Detailed methodology and Mplus code can be accessed at the study’s data repository (https://ida.loni.usc.edu/). We used ADNI_MEM, defined as memory scores, to assess memory function. Besides, blood samples collected from ADNI participants were stored at −80 °C in ultra-low temperature freezers to ensure the preservation of sample integrity for future biomarker analyses [22]. The Serum creatinine assessment and APOE genotyping were conducted at the Alzheimer’s Disease Biomarker Laboratory at the University of Pennsylvania [22, 23].

The eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation, defined as follows: [24]

Female: 144 × (Scr/0.7)–1.209 × 0.993age, Male: 141 × (Scr/0.9)–1.209 × 0.993age.

The individuals were categorized into eGFR-based quartile groups (eGFR < 55.54, 55.54–66.32, 66.32–77.57, and > 77.57 ml/min/1.73 m2, respectively).

Neuroimaging data

As reported in previous studies, all participants underwent conventional MRI scanning using either a 1.5-T or 3.0-T magnetic resonance scanner (Siemens Magnetom Prisma, GE Signa Excite, or Philips Intera) [25]. To ensure data comparability, the ADNI researchers standardized imaging protocols across all participating centers. This meticulous standardization process ensured that, despite variations in scanning equipment, the quality and consistency of the imaging data remained high, thus meeting the rigorous standards necessary for robust scientific research. The imaging data were analyzed by the University of California, San Francisco ADNI study team using the FreeSurfer version 5.1 software suite for image interpretation and processing [26–28]. Comprehensive information regarding the acquisition and processing of MRI data within the ADNI framework was available on the official ADNI website (https://ida.loni.usc.edu/). Regional brain volumes in our study were normalized by dividing each regional volume by the total intracranial volume (ICV) to adjust for interindividual differences in head size [17].

Statistical analyses

Individual characteristics were reported as percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means with standard deviations (SDs) for continuous variables. Comparisons across quartiles were conducted using the Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or the Kruskal–Wallis H test, depending on the data distribution. Standardized hippocampal and entorhinal volumes were scaled by a factor of 1000, while standardized fusiform and middle temporal volumes were scaled by a factor of 100 to enhance the clarity of result presentation.

Univariate and multivariate analyses with linear regression were used to investigate the association between the eGFR quartiles and memory scores, as well as the association between eGFR as a continuous variable (per SD increase) and memory scores. We additionally employed univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses to examine the association between eGFR and brain GMVs, as well as the association between GMVs and memory scores. We conducted two models in our regression analyses: Model1 was adjusted for age and sex, while model2 was adjusted for age, sex, years of education, medical history of smoking and alcohol intake, APOEε4 allele, history of hypertension, diabetes, and depression. The results were presented as β-coefficient and 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and we applied the Benjamini-Hochberg False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction to control for type I error.

To evaluate the mediating effect of GMVs in the association between eGFR and memory scores, we conducted structural equation modeling (SEM), with the bootstrapping of 5000 times. We adjusted age, sex, years of education, medical history of smoking and alcohol intake, APOEε4 allele in this SEM.

Finaly, in the subgroup analysis, we divided the individuals into subgroups based on cognitive status. Multivariate linear regression analyses were used to explore the association between eGFR levels and memory function in the various subgroups.

All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P value < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.0).

Results

Baseline characteristics

After excluding individuals with missing baseline data on age, sex, race, and MRI measures, a total of 1,311 older adults were included in our study (Fig. 1). The number of participants with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² was 467 (35.62%). Individuals were stratified into quartiles based on eGFR levels, and a comparison of baseline characteristics across these quartiles was presented in Table 1. The mean age of the participants was 73.30 years. Participants in the lowest eGFR quartile were older (P < 0.001), and they had lower MMSE scores (P < 0.001), smaller brain GMVs (P < 0.001), and a higher proportion of AD incidence (P = 0.011). Additionally, the highest eGFR quartile had a significantly lower proportion of white races (P = 0.008). No statistically significant differences were observed between quartiles for other baseline characteristics, such as smoking and alcohol history, years of education, and the presence of the APOEε4 allele.

Association between eGFR levels and memory function

The association between eGFR levels and memory function was shown in Table 2. In unadjusted model, the β-coefficient and 95%CI of the highest eGFR quartile for memory function was 0.51 (0.33–0.70). After adjusting for age, sex, education, and other potential confounders, compared to the lowest quartile of eGFR, the highest quartile was significantly associated with memory function (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.33; 95%CI, 0.14–0.50, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001). Similarly, when treating eGFR as a continuous variable, one SD increase in eGFR was associated with a 0.11-point improvement in memory scores (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.12; 95%CI, 0.05–0.18, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001).

Association between eGFR levels and brain GMVs

As Table 3 shown, after adjusting for confounding factors, compared to the lowest quartile of eGFR, the highest quartile was significantly associated with normalized hippocampus volume (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.30; 95%CI, 0.18–0.41, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001), normalized entorhinal volume (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.15; 95%CI, 0.07–0.24, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001), normalized fusiform volume (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.07; 95%CI, 0.03–0.10, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001), and normalized middle temporal volume (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.07; 95%CI, 0.03–0.09, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001), respectively. Furthermore, significant associations were observed between each 1-SD increase in eGFR and corresponding increases in normalized hippocampal volume, normalized entorhinal volume, normalized fusiform gyrus volume, and normalized middle temporal gyrus volume (all P for FDR-corrected < 0.001; Table 3).

Association between brain GMVs and memory function

The association between brain GMVs and memory function was shown in Table 4. After adjusting for confounding factors, the adjusted β-coefficients and 95%CIs of the normalized hippocampal volume, normalized entorhinal volume, and normalized fusiform gyrus volume for memory function were 0.77 (0.70–0.85), 1.07 (0.95–1.18), and 2.53 (2.18–2.88). Likewise, increased volume of the middle temporal gyrus was associated with the improved memory scores (adjusted β-coefficient, 2.99; 95%CI, 2.69–3.30, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001; Table 4).

Mediation of brain GMVs in the association of eGFR levels with memory function

Considering the interconnected associations between eGFR levels, GMVs, and memory function, we performed a mediation analysis, adjusting for potential confounding factors, to evaluate the mediating roles of specific GMVs. The results were displayed in Fig. 2. We found that 69.67%, 44.17%, 41.50%, and 49.33% of the total association of eGFR levels with memory function were attributed to hippocampal, entorhinal, fusiform, and middle temporal gyrus volume, respectively (all P values for mediation effects < 0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Given that cognitive status may affect memory scores, individuals were categorized into three subgroups based on their cognitive status, to investigate the association between eGFR levels and memory function across different cognitive status. As Fig. 3 shown, among patients with MCI, those in the highest quartile of eGFR showed a significant association with memory scores (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.45; 95%CI, 0.23–0.67, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001). For each SD increase in eGFR, memory scores increased by 0.16 (adjusted β-coefficient, 0.16; 95%CI, 0.08–0.24, P for FDR-corrected < 0.001). However, this association was not statistically significant among healthy controls or patients with AD.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the association between eGFR and memory function based on the data from the ADNI database. Our findings demonstrated the positive association between higher eGFR levels and better memory performance, particularly in individuals with MCI. Furthermore, this association appeared to be mediated by brain GMVs.

In this study, we used the CKD-EPI equation to calculate eGFR. Previous studies comparing the two commonly used formulas for estimating GFR have shown that the modification of diet in renal disease (MDRD) equation tend to underestimate measured GFR, particularly in individuals with eGFR > 60 mL/min/1.73 m² [29, 30], which may lead to overdiagnosis of CKD [31]. In contrast, the CKD-EPI equation incorporated the same four variables as the MDRD formula but provided more accurate estimates, especially in individuals with preserved renal function [32, 33]. Given that the ADNI cohort represented a general older adult population rather than a kidney disease-specific sample, the use of the CKD-EPI equation likely yielded estimates that were closer to actual GFR values, thereby enhancing the accuracy of our analysis. Future investigations in populations with history of kidney disease could help elucidate how differences between MDRD and CKD-EPI equations may influence the observed associations with cognitive function.

Previous studies have identified the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex as brain regions strongly associated with memory formation [10–12], while the fusiform gyrus and the middle temporal gyrus were implicated in facial recognition and semantic memory processing [13–16]. According to previous research and the variable composition of the ADNI database, we selected aforementioned brain regions which were critically associated with memory scores for our analyses. We found that higher levels of eGFR, whether considered as a categorical variable based on quartiles or as a continuous variable, were statistically significantly associated with the volumes of these brain structures (all P values < 0.05). A study, which explored the association between brain structure and renal function, found the significant association between eGFR and cortical volume (β-coefficient, 1.53e-04; SE, 6.72e-05, P = 0.023) [34]. Furthermore, eGFR was significantly associated with subcortical GMVs only in male individuals (P = 0.001) [34]. Patients with chronic kidney disease exhibited notably reduced total brain GMV (P = 0.040) [35]. There was also a strong association between eGFR < 60 ml/min/1.73 m² and brain atrophy (OR, 5.93;95% CI, 1.82–19.27) [18]. The above results could be interpreted as follows: the brain, being a high-energy consuming organ, relied on a stable and efficient blood supply to maintain its normal physiological functions [36, 37]. Declining renal function may impact systemic hemodynamics, resulting in reduced cerebral blood flow, particularly to the brain gray matter, due to its high neuronal density [38, 39]. Inadequate blood supply may impair neuronal function, potentially leading to the atrophy of GMV [40, 41]. Among patients with impaired renal function, the accumulation of inflammatory mediators may impair the integrity of the blood brain barrier (BBB), facilitating the entry of these factors into the brain and subsequently inducing localized neuroinflammation [42–45]. This process was likely to contribute to neurodegenerative changes and accelerated brain gray matter atrophy. However, similar studies based on the ADNI database have reported that eGFR was associated with the volumes of aforementioned brain structures only in the unadjusted model, while this association disappeared after accounting for confounding factors [17]. The discrepancy in results may stem from the more stringent exclusion criteria applied in our study, which removed more individuals based on baseline demographic variables such as age, sex, and race, as well as those with missing MRI data for the brain GMVs. This approach likely contributed to more rigorous analyses compared to prior research.

It was found that impaired renal function, as well as the severity of renal dysfunction, was significantly associated with deficits in learning and memory, language, and complex attention (all P values < 0.001) [46]. Studies also indicated that lower levels of eGFR was associated with an increased risk of cognitive impairment (OR, 1.42; 95% CI, 1.02–1.98, P = 0.037) [47]. Additionally, eGFR levels were found to be associated with performance on the word list memory task (β-coefficient, 0.53, P = 0.005), word list recall (β-coefficient, 0.86, P < 0.001), and word list recognition scores (β-coefficient, 0.43, P = 0.030) [47]. This further corroborated the association between eGFR levels and cognitive function, particularly memory performance. In contrast to the prior studies which relied on a single memory scale, our study used the composite of memory scores derived from multiple scales, including the MMSE, ADAS-Cog, Logistic Memory and RAVLT, based on data from the ADNI database. It provided a more nuanced and holistic assessment of memory function. Our findings revealed that individuals in the highest quartile of eGFR exhibited a positive association with memory function compared to those in the lowest quartile (β-coefficient, 0.32; 95% CI, 0.14–0.50, P < 0.001). This may be attributed to the elevated inflammatory levels observed in individuals with reduced eGFR [48, 49]. In such cases, the accumulation of metabolic waste products in the bloodstream, such as creatinine and urea, in conjunction with inflammatory cytokines, could compromise the BBB [50]. This disruption, in turn, negatively impacted synaptic connectivity in neurons, ultimately leading to memory impairment [50–52].

In the subgroup analysis of cognitive status, we found that the association between eGFR and memory function was observed exclusively in the MCI subgroup. This phenomenon may be explained by the hypothesis that individuals with normal cognitive function possess greater cognitive reserve or compensatory mechanisms, which may mitigate the adverse effects of renal dysfunction-induced BBB disruption on cognitive performance [53]. Among AD patients, compensatory mechanisms may already be exhausted, and cognitive decline may have reached a critical threshold. As a result, further reductions in eGFR and the accompanying inflammatory changes might exert minimal additional impact on memory function [54]. In contrast, individuals with MCI were undergoing a gradual decline in cognitive reserve that had not yet been fully depleted [55]. Thus, reductions in eGFR and associated inflammatory changes may have a more pronounced impact on their memory function compared to patients with AD.

Due to strong collinearity between GMVs in different brain regions, which makes it unsuitable for inclusion in parallel mediation models, we decomposed the analyses into multiple simple mediation models for separate evaluation. Our findings revealed that, apart from the mediation model involving the entorhinal cortex volume, where a weak direct association between eGFR levels and memory function was observed, no significant direct effects were found in the mediation models for other GMVs. This suggested that brain gray matter structures play a crucial mediating role in the association between eGFR levels and memory function. These results implied that, in clinical practice, patients with lower eGFR levels should undergo early neuroimaging evaluations to closely monitor changes in GMVs, especially among MCI patients. Such monitoring would allow clinicians to assess the risk of cognitive decline based on both renal function and changes in brain structure, ultimately facilitating more targeted interventions and treatment strategies.

Our study had certain strengths. First, by utilizing the ADNI database, it benefited from a larger sample size compared to previous studies, making the findings more representative of the elderly population. Second, the study investigated the association between eGFR levels and memory performance, revealing a positive association between eGFR levels and memory function, with brain gray matter structure mediating this association. These findings may allow clinicians to assess the risk of cognitive decline based on both renal function and changes in brain structure, ultimately facilitating more targeted interventions and treatment strategies. Finally, the results of this study may provide valuable insights for future research exploring the mechanisms underlying the kidney-brain axis.

Our study also had several limitations. First, this study utilized only baseline data from the ADNI database for a cross-sectional analysis, which precluded the establishment of causal association. Given this design, the temporal ordering among renal function, brain volume, and cognitive outcomes remained speculative and required confirmation through longitudinal or interventional studies. Second, although we hypothesized that reduced renal function may affect brain structure via impaired cerebral perfusion, direct perfusion measures such as arterial spin labeling (ASL)-derived cerebral blood flow (CBF) were available only for a small subsample (n = 198), limiting the generalizability and statistical power of the mediation analysis. Therefore, the potential mediating role of cerebral perfusion remained inconclusive and warranted confirmation in larger and more representative cohorts with complete perfusion data. Third, as inflammatory biomarkers were not available for most participants in our study, the potential role of inflammation markers in the association between renal function and memory remained to be fully elucidated. Future studies incorporating comprehensive inflammatory markers were needed to clarify the contribution of systemic inflammation to this pathway. Finally, we selected four representative GMVs most likely associated with memory function for investigation. Further studies should examine the contribution of other brain structures to the association between renal function and cognitive performance.

Conclusion

In summary, we investigated the association between eGFR and memory function based on the data from the ADNI database. Our findings demonstrated the positive association between higher eGFR levels and better memory performance, particularly in individuals with MCI. Furthermore, this association appeared to be mediated by brain GMVs. These findings may allow clinicians to assess the risk of memory decline based on both renal function and changes in brain structure, ultimately facilitating more targeted interventions and treatment strategies.
