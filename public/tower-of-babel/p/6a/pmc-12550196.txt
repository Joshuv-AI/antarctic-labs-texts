Ultrasonic-assisted Haematococcus pluvialis protein-pectin complex significantly enhances DHA stability and bioavailability for 3D food printing☆
Authors: Bin Li, Xu Zhong, Xinlei Zhang, Yinxu Zhang, Xinghua Gao, Xiaomei Liu, Wentao Su, Yuxiao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Developing a highly stable and bioavailable docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) delivery system is critical for functional food applications especially in 3D food printing. This study introduces an ultrasonic-assisted Haematococcus pluvialis protein-pectin (HP/P) complex as a novel natural Pickering emulsifier to enhance DHA stability and bioavailability. HP/P complexes were prepared via 25 kHz ultrasonic treatment for 0–40 min. The HP/P-20 sample treated for 20 min showed optimal performance: +15 mV Zeta potential 660.9 nm particle size and 84.0° contact angle. Compared with the non-ultrasonic group HP/P-20 significantly improved DHA oxidative stability and bioavailability. 3D printing experiments confirmed that HP/P-stabilized emulsions exhibited suitable viscosity printability structural integrity and self-supporting properties validating their feasibility in customized food formulations. This study provides a novel strategy for DHA encapsulation using ultrasonic-engineered protein-polysaccharide complexes offering a sustainable plant-based alternative for functional food applications.

Introduction

Polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have been extensively studied and proven to offer various health benefits (Hirabara et al., 2013). However, mammals are unable to synthesize ω-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids on their own and must obtain these essential fatty acids through diet (Santos et al., 2021), with natural products such as algal oil being their primary dietary sources. In these years, more attention has been paid to DHA algae oil, because of its lower contamination, higher yield, higher content of DHA, and taller proportion of DHA/eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) (> 3) (Arterburn et al., 2007; Demirbas & Demirbas, 2011; Djordjevic et al., 2004; Doughman et al., 2007). Nevertheless, the application of algal oil in functional foods still faces challenges, such as poor water solubility, low oxidative stability, and insufficient bioavailability. Emulsified forms of DHA have been reported to improve release and absorption by facilitating micelle formation, a crucial factor for lipid bioavailability in the gastrointestinal tract (Ma et al., 2020; Sun et al., 2022).

Emulsion systems play a crucial role in the delivery of functional food ingredients due to their excellent emulsification, protection, and sustained-release capabilities. Research has shown that emulsions could effectively stabilize highly hydrophobic and easily oxidized functional substances such as DHA/EPA (Tang et al., 2024), curcumin (Lu et al., 2024), and carotene (Dai et al., 2024). The selection of emulsifiers is of great importance to the performance of emulsions. With the increasing demand from consumers for natural and health-conscious products, plant proteins with biocompatibility and ecological acceptability have attracted much attention (A'yun et al., 2020; Lin et al., 2024). Microalgae have become a research hotspot because they do not occupy arable land, have strong environmental adaptability, and are rich in natural nutrients (Fu et al., 2021). Haematococcus pluvialis (H. pluvialis), a unicellular microalga in the phylum Chlorophyta, is an excellent natural source of astaxanthin, but the residue after astaxanthin extraction from it is often discarded. Although some studies have explored the applications of H. pluvialis residue in biological fermentation, biofuels, and animal feed (Nishshanka et al., 2022), its application in the food field is still limited. Research has shown that high - concentration broken algal cell wall residues can effectively form Pickering emulsion systems by adsorbing oil droplets to the water interface (Mnif & Ghribi, 2015). Proteins are often used to prepare Pickering emulsions because of their excellent emulsifying properties, interfacial adsorption ability, and conformational adjustability (Schwenzfeier et al., 2013). For example, the emulsifying effect of spirulina protein increased with the improvement of extract purity (Böcker et al., 2021), and the emulsifying ability of chlorella protein is comparable to that of commercial emulsifiers (Ursu et al., 2014). Our previous research found that the protein extracted from the decolorized residue of H. pluvialis by ultrasonic extraction and salt precipitation possessed excellent emulsifying properties and could reduce the loss of astaxanthin and DHA in algal oil during the dumpling-boiling process (Liu, Cheng, et al., 2023). Aqueous protein extracted from Haematococcus Pluvialis residues (APHRs) through ammonium sulfate precipitation composed of 23.6 % protein and 64.9 % carbohydrate was proved be a good stabilizer for Pickering emulsion, due to its excellent emulsifying characteristics related to the secondary structure of protein and the existence of polysaccharides (Yin et al., 2023). However, the application of emulsion systems in functional food carriers, functional substance delivery, and fat simulators is limited because of their lack of a fixed form and irreversible interfacial adsorption (Wang et al., 2022).

With the growth of personalized dietary needs, customized food has become a new trend in the food industry. The application of 3D printing technology has promoted the development of modern customized food (Li et al., 2023). The key to this technology lies in the construction of 3D printing inks with shear-thinning, viscoelastic, and thixotropic properties. Single proteins usually cannot meet these requirements, and printable inks need to be prepared through composite modification (Tan et al., 2022). The 3D objects printed by the FPHRs-Gelatin complexes emulsion gels had superior print resolution and shape fidelity (Wang et al., 2023). Research has shown that non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic forces are the main molecular forces for stabilizing protein gel networks (Zhu et al., 2021). Pectin and plant proteins can form soluble or insoluble complexes by adjusting their concentrations and ratios, thereby enhancing the strength of protein gels (Du et al., 2022). For example, adding 0.5 % pectin to pea protein gel could significantly improve its rheological and textural properties (Zhang et al., 2022). However, the direct mixing of pectin and plant proteins can only enhance the gel strength without producing a significant cross-linking effect. Therefore, scholars have begun to explore improving the functional properties of pectin through structural modification, but they have paid insufficient attention to improving its cross-linking interaction with plant proteins (Burana-osot et al., 2010). Ultrasonic technology has been widely used in the modification, degradation, and transfer of macromolecules due to its cavitation effect (Aguiló-Aguayo et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2017). The self-supporting ability and viscoelasticity required for 3D printing inks were difficult to achieve by simply mixing pectin and plant proteins (Zhang et al., 2023). Ultrasonic treatment could effectively change the structure of protein-polysaccharide conjugates, endowing them with more desirable functional properties (Qu et al., 2018). Therefore, the combination of pectin and ultrasonic technology is expected to enhance the gel strength of plant protein inks and provide a theoretical basis for the development of printable fat-substitute emulsion gels.

This study systematically investigated the effects of the H. pluvialis protein-pectin complex (HPPPC) on the stability and bioavailability of DHA using ultrasonic-assisted technology (Scheme 1). It focused on examining the regulatory effects of ultrasonic treatment time on the physicochemical properties of the complex and analyzing the influence mechanisms of pH value and pectin concentration on the emulsion system. Through rheological analysis, including steady shear tests and thixotropic recovery experiments, the rheological properties and viscoelastic behavior of the composite emulsion were comprehensively characterized, and its application potential as a 3D-printed food material and fat substitute was evaluated.Scheme 1Schematic of the preparation process of HEGs used to improve the stability of DHA and their three-dimensional printing and stability studies.Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Materials

The main materials used in this study include substances such as DHA algal oil, H. pluvialis extract, pectin (65 % citrus-derived), absolute ethanol, DPPH, ABTS, n-hexane, potassium bromide, sodium chloride, and ammonium sulfate. Among them, the H. pluvialis extract was provided by Kunming Baiou Microalgae Technology Co., Ltd. Pectin (65 % citrus-derived) was provided by Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Absolute ethanol was provided by Tianjin Fuyu Fine Chemical Industry Co., Ltd. DPPH, ABTS, and n-hexane were provided by Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd.; and potassium bromide, sodium chloride, and ammonium sulfate were provided by Tianjin Damao Reagent Factory.

Extraction of H. pluvialis protein

The method employed for extracting protein from H. pluvialis was adapted from that described by (Muñoz et al., 2015), with minor modifications. Initially, 100 g of H. pluvialis powder was mixed into 2.0 L of a 2 % NaCl solution (m/v) and stirred until the powder was uniformly dispersed. Subsequently, the solution underwent ultrasonic treatment in a 46 °C water bath for 2 h to disrupt the cell walls, thereby enhancing protein extraction efficiency. Following ultrasonic treatment, the dispersion was centrifuged at 8800 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected. Ammonium sulfate was then added to the supernatant at a concentration of 0.662 g/mL to precipitate the proteins. The mixture was centrifuged again at 12,000 rpm for 8 min at 4 °C to collect the precipitated proteins, which were then dissolved in 250 mL of PBS to yield the H. pluvialis protein solution. This solution was further concentrated through vacuum rotary evaporation at 45 °C. The protein extraction yield was determined to be 23.6 %, the extracted protein was freeze-dried into powder and made into a 3 % protein solution.

Preparation of H. pluvialis protein-pectin complex

To prepare the protein-pectin complex, 3 g of pectin was first dissolved in deionized water to a total volume of 100 mL, and the mixture was heated with stirring in a magnetic-stirring water bath at 65 °C for 30 min. Afterward, stirring continued on a conventional magnetic stirrer for 2 h until no visible pectin particles remained, resulting in a 3 % pectin solution. Thirty milliliters of the H. pluvialis protein solution was then mixed with an equal volume of the pectin solution. The mixture was adjusted to a 1:1 volume ratio, and the pH was adjusted accordingly. The mixture was stirred for 5 h to form the H. pluvialis protein-pectin complex. This complex was sonicated at 25 kHz for durations of 0, 10, 20, 30, and 40 min, resulting in samples designated as HP/P-0, HP/P-10, HP/P-20, HP/P-30, and HP/P-40, respectively.

Turbidity measurement

The turbidity of the HP/Ps complexes was assessed by adjusting the pH of the solutions to 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11. Absorbance readings at these pH levels were recorded using a UV–Vis (UV-5200, Shimadzu, Japan) spectrophotometer at 600 nm. To ensure clear visibility of turbidity variations, all complex concentrations were diluted 100-fold before measurement. The turbidity of the complexes at the five pH values was determined.

Fluorescence spectroscopy measurement

The effects of ultrasonic treatment on the tertiary structure of proteins within the HP/Ps solutions at pH 3 were investigated using endogenous fluorescence spectroscopy. Fluorescence spectra were collected using a fluorescence spectrometer (F-2700, HITACHI, Japan). Following the modified procedure of Luo et al. (2019), the endogenous fluorescence of each variant, HP/P-0 through HP/P-40, was measured. The fluorescence spectrometer settings were as follows: excitation wavelength at 280.0 nm, emission range from 300.0 to 440.0 nm, slit width at 5.0 nm, scanning speed of 200.0 nm/min, and measurement voltage at 500.0 mV.

HP/Ps potential measurement

For zeta potential analysis, samples were diluted 100-fold and placed into a cuvette for measurement with a Zeta potential analyzer. The parameters set for the test included a measurement temperature of 25 °C and an equilibrium time of 90 s. Each sample was measured three times to calculate the average value.

Preparation of H. pluvialis protein-pectin Pickering emulsion gels (HEGs)

The preparation of oil-in-water Pickering emulsion gels was executed using a straightforward one-step homogenization process utilizing high-speed shearing technology. Specifically, the HP/Ps aqueous solution and DHA algal oil were measured and combined in a volumetric ratio of 3:7. This mixture was then processed in a homogenizer at 12,000 rpm for 2.5 min to produce HEGs. Similarly, the preparation of water-in-oil Pickering emulsified gels was conducted using an identical method, where the HP/Ps aqueous solution and DHA algal oil were measured, mixed at a volumetric ratio of 3:7, and subsequently homogenized at 12,000 rpm for 2.5 min to obtain HEGs. The variants of the emulsions, designated as HP/P-0, HP/P-10, HP/P-20, HP/P-30, and HP/P-40, were correspondingly named HEG0, HEG1, HEG2, HEG3, and HEG4.

Contact angle and particle size measurement

The contact angle of HP/Ps subjected to different durations of ultrasonic treatment was measured using a DSA 25 Drop Shape Analyzer from Krüss, Germany. Following a previously established method, HP/Ps were pressed into thin slices using a tablet press, thoroughly saturated with DHA algal oil, then removed and dried. Subsequently, 5 μL of deionized water was applied to the thin slices, and the droplet shape was captured using a high-speed camera (Abd El-Aty et al., 2022). The particle size of the H. pluvialis protein-pectin emulsion gel was determined using a Nano Particle Size and Zeta Potential Analyzer (Zeta sizer Nano ZS90). Each sample was measured in triplicate at a controlled room temperature of 25 °C.

Observation of HEGs by laser confocal microscopy

The observation method was slightly adapted from that described by He et al. (2022). The H. pluvialis protein-pectin complex was prepared as outlined in section 2.7, with the addition of an adequate amount of FITC. This mixture was stirred overnight at room temperature to ensure complete integration of the stain within the complex. Similarly, Nile Red was incorporated into the DHA algal oil for staining purposes. The emulsion gel was then prepared according to the protocol described in section 2.7. To further study the microstructure of the HEGs, samples were observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (DM IL LED Fluo, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). All procedures were performed in a darkened environment. All procedures were performed in a darkened environment.

Stability measurement of HEGs

Light stability

A volume of 8 mL of the prepared HEGs was placed in a glass sample bottle and exposed to room temperature lighting for a duration of 15 days. Photographic records were taken on days 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15 to monitor color changes and the separation of water and oil, thereby assessing their light stability.

Centrifugal stability

A volume of 6 mL of the prepared HEGs was transferred to a centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Post-centrifugation, a 1 mL syringe was employed to extract the water separated from the lower layer of the emulsion, and its volume was measured. This data facilitated the calculation of the emulsion's water-holding capacity, expressed as follows formula (1).(1)Water holding capacity%=1−V1V0where V1 represents the volume of water separated from the lower layer of the emulsion, and V0 denotes the original volume of the emulsion transferred to the centrifuge tube.

Thermal stability

In this experiment, 5 mL of the prepared HEG samples was introduced into a glass sample bottle. The samples were then subjected to a heating protocol in a water bath maintained at 90 °C for durations of 30 min and 60 min. Following the heating process, the samples were allowed to cool to ambient temperature. Subsequent observations of both macroscopic and microscopic alterations were conducted to assess the thermal stability of the emulsions.

Freeze-thaw stability

To evaluate the freeze-thaw stability, 4 mL of freshly prepared HEG samples were placed in glass sample bottles and subjected to freezing at −20 °C for 4 h, followed by thawing at room temperature (approximately 25 °C) for 5 h. This procedure constituted a single freeze-thaw cycle. Post-thaw, the samples were examined for changes in both macroscopic and microscopic structures to determine the stability of the emulsions under freeze-thaw conditions.

Water distribution and rheological properties

HEG samples, subjected to varying durations of ultrasonic treatment, were analyzed for water distribution and state using a low-field nuclear magnetic resonance analyzer. This sophisticated equipment facilitated precise measurements within the samples. The rheological properties of the HEGs were investigated utilizing methodologies established in prior studies. The assessments were performed using a Discovery HR-2 rheometer configured to frequency sweep mode (ranging from 0.1 to 10 Hz with a stress setting of 1 Pa). This setup enabled the examination of the rheological behavior of Pickering emulsion gels. The apparent viscosity of these gels was determined through a three-interval thixotropic analysis, where shear rates varied from 0.1 s−1 to 100 s−1, and strain levels were set at high (100 %) and low (0.1 %). Measurements were consistently taken at 25 °C with a 1 mm gap. Both the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") were graphically represented as functions of frequency, delineating the dynamic spectra within the defined linear viscoelastic range.

3D printing

The adoption of 3D printing technology is progressively increasing in the food industry, primarily due to its capacity to precisely control the movement of the printing nozzle relative to the platform. In this study, a 3D printer from Fochif Mechatronics Technology, China, was employed to investigate the properties of latex-based gels in 3D printing scenarios. Initially, the gel was loaded into the ink cartridge of the printer. The printing process was conducted at 25 °C and atmospheric pressure, where the emulsified gel was extruded through a 1 mm nozzle at a rate of 1 mm3 s−1. The fidelity of the printed 3D structures was documented through photographic records.

Determination of antioxidant activity in HEGs

DPPH radical scavenging assay

A 0.2 mmol/L solution of DPPH in ethanol was prepared by diluting 10 μL of a 200 μmol/L DPPH ethanol solution into 0.98 mL of absolute ethanol, followed by thorough mixing. Approximately 0.1 g of Pickering emulsion gel was weighed and combined with 2.5 mL of absolute ethanol, vortexed for 2 min, and subsequently centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The resultant supernatant was collected. For the assays, the supernatant was mixed in a 1:1 ratio with the 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution for the sample group (A1), with deionized water for the blank group (A0), and deionized water was mixed with the 0.2 mmol/L DPPH solution for the control group (A2). Triplicate samples of each mixture were transferred to a microplate reader. After a 30-min reaction period in the dark, the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. The DPPH radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following formula (2).(2)DPPH freeradical clearance%=1−A1−A2A0×100

ABTS radical scavenging assay

An ABTS radical stock solution was prepared and stored in the dark at 4 °C for 12 to 16 h. This stock solution was then diluted with absolute ethanol at a volume ratio of 1:40. The preparation was deemed complete when the absorbance of this diluted solution, measured at room temperature at 734 nm, reached 0.7 ± 0.02. Similarly, 0.1 g of Pickering emulsion gel was weighed, added to 4 mL of absolute ethanol, vortexed for 2 min, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min to collect the supernatant. For analysis, 20 μL of this supernatant (A1) and 150 μL of the ABTS ethanol solution were mixed, and the absorbance was measured at 734 nm after a 30-min reaction period in the dark. Absolute ethanol served as the blank (A0). The ABTS radical scavenging activity was determined using the following formula (3).(3)ABTS freeradical clearance%=1−A0A1×100

Hydroxyl radical

To assess hydroxyl radical scavenging activity, 100 μL each of 5 mM salicylic acid, 5 mM FeSO₄, and 8.8 mM H₂O₂ solutions was mixed with 100 μL of the test sample, and deionized water was added to make the total volume up to 1.5 mL (A1). For control measurements, deionized water replaced the test sample (A0) and H₂O₂ (A2). The mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, after which the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. The hydroxyl radical scavenging activity was computed with the following formula (4).(4)Hydroxyl radical clearance%=1−A1−A2A0×100

Reducing power

The sample was combined with K₄[Fe(CN)₆] and subsequently engaged in a colorimetric reaction with FeCl₃ to assess its reducing power. In detail, 1 mL of the sample was mixed with 2.5 mL of PBS (pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of 1 % K₄[Fe(CN)₆], followed by incubation at 50 °C for 20 min. Subsequent to incubation, 2.5 mL of 10 % C₂HCl₃O₂ was added, thoroughly stirred, and then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 10 min. Following centrifugation, 2.5 mL of the supernatant was combined with an equivalent volume of water and 0.5 mL of FeCl₃. After a 10-min rest period, the absorbance was measured at 700 nm, indicating the reducing power of the sample.

In vitro simulated digestion

Drawing on the findings of existing research (Zhang et al., 2022), the protocol for in vitro gastrointestinal digestion was refined. This experiment was structured into three phases: salivary digestion, gastric digestion, and intestinal digestion, all conducted at 37 °C. Digestive fluids were preheated to 37 °C for 2 h. During the salivary digestion phase, equal volumes of HEG0, HEG2, and DHA algal oil were diluted, introduced to artificial simulated saliva, adjusted to pH 6.8, and agitated on a shaker for 10 min. For gastric digestion, the sample mixture was combined with an equal volume of artificial simulated gastric fluid, adjusted to pH 2, and incubated in a vessel for 110 min. During the intestinal digestion phase, simulated intestinal fluid was mixed with the gastric effluent in equal volumes, and the pH was continuously adjusted to 7 using 0.02 M NaOH. The volume of NaOH utilized over 120 min was recorded, and the percentage of free fatty acids (FFA) released during this phase was calculated based on the NaOH consumption. Throughout the digestion process, the pH was maintained at 7.0 by titrating with 0.01 mol/L NaOH every 5 min. The percentage of FFA released during intestinal digestion was determined using the following formula (5).(5)FFA%=VNaOHt×MNaOH×Mlipid2×Wlipid×100%where V NaOH(t) is the volume of NaOH consumed at time t (mL), M NaOH is the molarity of NaOH used for titration, M lipid is the molecular weight of DHA algal oil (228.5 g/mol), and W lipid is the mass of DHA algal oil present in the digestive system (g).

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

The endogenous fluorescence spectrum of proteins can provide insights into their tertiary structure. This structure, which emerges from further coiling and folding of the secondary structure, is predominantly stabilized by intermolecular hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions. In this study, the structural and polarity characteristics of HP/Ps were examined using fluorescence spectroscopy. Previous research (Miriani et al., 2012) has established that tryptophan and tyrosine residues within proteins emit fluorescence under specific conditions and are highly sensitive to environmental changes. In our experiments, HP/Ps was prepared utilizing an ultrasonic-assisted method. The findings are depicted in Fig. 1. B. When excited at a wavelength of 280 nm, HP/Ps exhibited a maximum emission peak near 350 nm. It was observed that with an increase in ultrasonic treatment duration, the fluorescence intensity of HP/P-20 was the highest, whereas that of HP/P-40, which underwent the longest duration of ultrasonic treatment, was the lowest. This decrease in fluorescence intensity may be attributable to the dissociation of some fluorescent groups from the H. pluvialis proteins due to excessive ultrasonic treatment.

Zeta potential analysis

Zeta potential is an essential parameter for characterizing the electrostatic interactions between particles, serving as an indicator of the stability of colloidal systems (Huang et al., 2021). Under specific conditions, an increase in the repulsive potential energy among particles correlates with a higher Zeta potential, suggesting enhanced stability and emulsifying capacity of the complex. The Zeta potential can also provide information about the charge properties and stability of the particle surface. As illustrated in Fig. 1. C, there was no significant change in the Zeta potential of HP/Ps with ultrasonic treatment durations of 0 and 10 min. However, when the treatment duration reached 20 min, there was an increase in the Zeta potential of HP/Ps, indicating improved stability and emulsifying capability of the complex in the emulsion gel system. When the ultrasound time reached 30 min, the potential of HP/P-30 decreased. And when the ultrasound time reached 40 min, the potential of HP/P-40 was slightly close to that of HP/P-20.

Particle size distribution analysis

Extant research (Lang et al., 2024) suggests that particle size can function as an indicator of the emulsifying efficacy of an emulsifier. Notably, a smaller particle size correlates with enhanced emulsifying capability. Furthermore, the particle size distribution is intimately associated with the stability of an emulsion. A narrower particle size distribution generally results in improved emulsion properties (Lu et al., 2018). Fig. 1. D illustrates the particle size distributions of HP/Ps subjected to varying durations of ultrasonic treatment. At an ultrasonic treatment duration of 0 min, the particle size distribution of HP/P-0 was markedly less uniform compared to those of other HP/P samples modified with ultrasonic assistance, exhibiting a maximum particle size of 768.5 nm. This observation underscores that ultrasonic-assisted treatment considerably refines the uniformity of particle size distribution, thereby enhancing the emulsifying performance and stability of HP/Ps. When the treatment duration was extended to 10 min, the particle size distribution of HP/P-10 was relatively uniform; however, its maximum particle size significantly exceeded those observed under other ultrasonic treatment durations, peaking at 1406 nm. This suggested that the emulsifying performance and stability of HP/P-10 were moderate at this interval. At an ultrasonic duration of 20 min, HP/P-20 not only demonstrated a tight particle size distribution but also featured a relatively smaller maximum particle size of 660.9 nm, constituting 20.85 % of the sample. Consequently, HP/P-20 exhibited superior stability and emulsifying performance at this specific duration. For durations of 30 and 40 min, the particle size distributions of HP/P-30 and HP/P-40 remained tight, with both registering maximum particle sizes of 768.5 μm. However, compared to HP/P-20, their emulsifying performance and stability were inferior. This analysis clearly indicates that the performance of HP/P-20 at an ultrasonic duration of 20 min surpasses that of other HP/P samples.

Contact angle (θ) analysis

The gelation properties of emulsions are significantly influenced by the wettability of the complex particles involved. A contact angle (θ) approaching 90° denotes favorable wettability, which facilitates the effective coalescence of aqueous and oil phases at the interface. The wettability of HP/Ps was assessed by measuring the contact angle (θ). Fig. 1. E depicts the characteristic water droplet shapes on the surface of HP/Ps in a DHA algal oil bath at various ultrasonic treatment durations. At 0 min of ultrasonic treatment, the contact angle was minimal at 63.7°. As the duration of ultrasonic treatment increased up to 20 min, the contact angle progressively rose, yet remained below 90°. Upon reaching 20 min of treatment, the contact angle peaked at 84.0°. Beyond this point, the contact angle of HP/Ps gradually decreased as the duration of ultrasonic treatment extended.

Influence of ultrasonic time on the stability of HEGs

Light stability

The light stability of HEGs is evaluated and presented in Fig. 3. A. With prolonged exposure to light, the color of HEGs transitions from orange to light yellow, accompanied by the separation of oil and water phases. The initial orange hue of HEGs was attributed to the presence of astaxanthin, a pigment extracted from H. pluvialis, which naturally exhibited a color spectrum ranging from orange to dark red. Under extended illumination, astaxanthin underwent decomposition, leading to oxidation and degradation products (Kulikov et al., 2020), which resulted in the observed color fade of the emulsion gel over time. Additionally, Fig. 3. A indicates that no oil phase separation occurred in HEG2 after 20 min of US, whereas emulsion gels processed under other ultrasonic durations exhibited some degree of oil phase separation. Notably, the quantity of water separated at the bottom of HEG2 was less than that observed in other HEG samples, suggesting that HEG2 possessed superior light stability compared to those processed for shorter or longer ultrasonic durations.Fig. 3(A) HEGs light stability from 1 to 15 days. Centrifugal stability

The separation of water from HEG following centrifugation reveals that HEGs formulated from HP/Ps without ultrasonic-assisted treatment exhibit instability under conditions of centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, with a significant phase separation observed. Conversely, HEGs prepared from HP/Ps with ultrasonic-assisted treatment demonstrate enhanced stability under similar conditions. Thus, HEGs derived from HP/Ps exhibit superior centrifugal stability compared to those prepared solely with the HP emulsifier, indicating a more effective encapsulation capability. Fig. 3. B–C illustrates that HEGs subjected to varying durations of ultrasonic treatment exhibit different extents of water separation post-centrifugation, signifying a decrease in their emulsification quality. The extent of water separation can be utilized to determine the water-holding capacity of these HEGs. An analysis of this parameter allowed for a more precise evaluation of the centrifugal stability across different pectin concentrations in HEGs, as detailed in Fig. 3. D. The figure reveals that the HEG2 treated with 20 min of ultrasonic exposure achieved the highest water-holding capacity at 81.41 %. When the ultrasonic treatment duration was increased to 30 min, the water-holding capacity slightly decreased to 80.78 %. The water-holding capacities for treatment durations of 0, 10, and 40 min showed a progressive improvement, although they remained lower than those observed at 20 and 30 min. Notably, HEG0, which did not undergo ultrasonic-assisted treatment, exhibits the lowest water-holding capacity at 74.61 %. A comprehensive analysis thus indicated that the centrifugal stability of HEG2 with a 20-min ultrasonic treatment duration was superior to that of HEGs treated for different durations.

Thermal stability

HEGs were subjected to heating at 90 °C for durations of 30 and 60 min, respectively. Figure A illustrates the macroscopic alterations in HEGs post-heating for both time intervals, while Figure B depicts the corresponding microscopic changes. As observed in Fig. 4. A, subsequent to heating, HEGs exhibited phase separation involving both water and oil. This separation suggested the thermal instability of the emulsion during the heating process. Freeze-thaw stability

Subjecting samples to freezing at −20 °C for 4 h followed by thawing at room temperature (25 °C) for 5 h constitutes a single freeze-thaw cycle. Figures present the macroscopic and microscopic images of HEGs after undergoing six such cycles. As depicted in Fig. 5. A, following a single cycle, all HEG samples except HEG2—which was subjected to 20 min of ultrasonic treatment—exhibited significant separation of both the water and oil phases. The extent of this phase separation was notably pronounced. As the number of freeze-thaw cycles increased, the color intensity of the HEGs progressively diminished. Concurrently, the oil phase separation augmented, leading to a higher dissolution of astaxanthin in the upper oil layer, which in turn darkened its color. The water phase exhibited a corresponding increase with the accumulation of cycles, stabilizing only when no further separation occurred. Notably, when the ultrasonic treatment duration was fixed at 20 min, the separation of both the water and oil phases was minimal. Therefore, the examination of both macroscopic and microscopic structures robustly confirmed that HEG2, with a 20-min ultrasonic treatment, manifests the optimal freeze-thaw stability among the tested samples.

Influence of ultrasonic treatment time on the water distribution of HEGs

The state and distribution of water within HEGs can be effectively analyzed using low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR). This technique is particularly advantageous as it ensures that the structural integrity of the samples remains unaltered, thereby providing a reliable method of analysis. The water distribution in HEGs, as determined by LF-NMR, is illustrated in Fig. 6. A. The abscissa of the figure denoted the transverse relaxation time (T2) for HEGs, and the ordinate indicated the corresponding amplitude. From the data presented in the figure, it was evident that three distinct peaks manifest across different relaxation time ranges. These peaks correspond to various states of water within the gums. Specifically, the peak within the 0.1–10 ms range represents the content of bound water (T21), the peak in the 10–200 ms range corresponds to the content of immobilized water (T22), and the peak in the 200–2000 ms range indicates the content of free water (T23) (Song et al., 2021). When the ultrasonic treatment was applied for 0 min, the amplitude of the T23 peak was observed to be the highest, which suggested that the content of free water was predominant at this point. Concurrently, the amplitude of the T22 peak was also relatively high, denoting a significant presence of immobilized water, while the T21 peak was the smallest, indicating that the content of bound water was at its minimum. These observations support the conclusion that ultrasonic-assisted modification of HP/Ps could significantly optimize the performance of HEGs. The possible mechanisms for this enhancement included the optimization of electrostatic repulsion and steric hindrance effects among pectin molecules (Liu et al., 2024), alterations in the structural characteristics of H. pluvialis proteins that improved their emulsifying properties (O'sullivan et al., 2016), and the cavitation effect of ultrasound which not only reduced the particle size but also leaded to a more uniform distribution of particle sizes (Zhang et al., 2020). Upon increasing the ultrasonic treatment time to 10 min, the amplitude of the T23 peak decreased, indicating a reduction in the content of free water. In contrast, the amplitudes of both the T22 and T21 peaks increased, suggesting higher contents of immobilized and bound water, respectively. This phenomenon could be attributed to the interaction of most of the free water with other components, leading to its transformation into either bound or immobilized water. Extending the ultrasonic treatment time further to 20 min resulted in a decrease in the amplitudes of both the T23 and T22 peaks, while the amplitude of the T21 peak reached its highest level. This shift indicated that the contents of both free and immobilized water were less at this stage, while the content of bound water was significantly increased. This suggested that the water molecules in H. pluvialis protein and pectin were now more extensively bound with other components due to increased cross-linking at this stage of ultrasonic treatment. Consequently, HEG2 exhibited enhanced stability, and water was less likely to separate from the mixture. As the ultrasonic treatment time was increased to 30 and 40 min, the amplitudes of the T23 and T21 peaks were observed to be intermediate compared to those at other treatment durations, indicating that the contents of free and bound water were relatively evenly distributed, while the amplitude of the T22 peak was lower, suggesting a decrease in the content of immobilized water. To summarize, the analysis of water distribution in HEG2 demonstrated that an ultrasonic treatment time of 20 min yielded a more favorable configuration than other durations.Fig. 6(A) Moisture distribution of HEGs. Rheological properties of emulsion gels

Influence of different ultrasonic treatment times on the viscosity of HEGs

Fig. 6. B illustrates the relationship between the viscosity of the emulsion and the shear rate under varied ultrasonic treatment durations. This graph effectively demonstrates the initial viscosity of HEGs stabilized by HP/Ps at different ultrasonic treatment times, and how it varies as the shear rate increases from 0 to 100 s−1. Notably, under identical shear conditions, HEG2 treated for 20 min exhibited the highest viscosity. This observation was attributed to the fact that high shear rates not only disrupt the entanglement and flocculation of macromolecules, which presented spatial hindrance such as dense and loose structural distributions, but also altered the morphology of oil droplets, consequently reducing the resistance and viscosity within the fluid. Significant variances in viscosity among different HEG samples suggested that the interactions between H. pluvialis protein and pectin significantly influenced the viscosity of HEGs. The viscosity of the Pickering emulsion initially increased and then decreased with prolonged ultrasonic treatment, achieving optimal results at 20 min for HEG2.

Influence of different ultrasonic treatment times on G' and G" of Pickering emulsions

Fig. 6. C–D display the variations in the elastic modulus (G') and viscous modulus (G") of the emulsion across different frequencies and ultrasonic treatment times. Within a specified frequency range, G' consistently surpassed G", indicating that the emulsion gel stabilized by HP/Ps possessed a robust interconnected network structure, wherein elasticity predominantly governed the formation of the gel. Both G' and G" rose with increasing shear rates at a constant ultrasonic treatment duration. Moreover, as the ultrasonic treatment time extended, both G' and G" initially increased and then decrease. The peak values of G' and G" observed at an ultrasonic treatment duration of 20 min could be elucidated by the closer proximity and densification of the emulsion droplets over time, leading to enhanced modulus and resistance to deformation.

3D printing

Ultrasound treatment during the preparation of emulsion gels influences not only their stability but also their mechanical properties. This study highlighted variations in the morphological stability and line clarity of 3D printed structures as a consequence of different ultrasound treatment durations. As depicted in Fig. 6. E, the HEG0 sample, which did not undergo ultrasound treatment, struggled to maintain shape and exhibits an unstable structure. Conversely, the HEG2 sample, subjected to 20 min of ultrasound treatment, maintained uniform filaments and structural integrity, with closely stacked layers and enhanced overall support capability. However, increasing the ultrasound treatment time to 40 min resulted in blurred inter-layer lines and a phenomenon of uneven distribution. It can be concluded that sonication is an effective approach to regulating the 3D printing performance of emulsion gels; however, its effect is time-dependent. Specifically, moderate sonication treatment (20 min) can optimize the stability of the printed structure and enhance its clarity, whereas insufficient or excessive treatment will result in structural defects.

Evaluation of the DPPH radical scavenging activity of HEGs subjected to different ultrasonic treatment durations

In the context of antioxidant properties, food emulsions composed of polysaccharides and proteins exhibit notable functionalities. Proteins, through their ability to chelate transition metals and scavenge free radicals, can inhibit interactions with lipids within emulsion droplets, thereby mitigating lipid oxidation. Pectin, although capable of forming a molecular fibrous network at the water/oil interface or within the aqueous phase, possesses a relatively loose structure that may not sufficiently form a closed protective layer around oil bodies. Consequently, its antioxidant efficacy is potentially inferior to that of emulsions prepared using multiple emulsifiers. To assess the antioxidant capacity of HEG samples, their ability to scavenge DPPH radicals was measured. The results, illustrated in Figs. 7. A-B, indicated that distinct differences in scavenging activity among the samples. Initially, at 0 min, the DPPH radical scavenging rate for HEG0 was approximately 40 %, whereas for HEG1, it was slightly lower, around 30 %. HEG2 demonstrated a superior scavenging rate of approximately 65 %, the highest among the five groups analyzed. This enhanced activity correlates with the smallest particle size observed in the microscopic structural analysis of HEG2, aligning with findings from prior studies (McClements & Decker, 2000). In contrast, samples HEG3 and HEG4, which underwent prolonged US, exhibited a significant reduction in scavenging activity, approaching 35 %. This reduction might be attributed to the modifications induced in the HP/Ps structure by ultrasonic-assisted technology, which subsequently affected the formation of a network structure at the oil-water interface. Antioxidant activity of HEGs

The ABTS radical scavenging assay operates on the principle whereby the blue-green ABTS radicals interact with antioxidants, leading to a measurable decolorization. This change in absorbance at a specific wavelength serves as a metric for evaluating the ABTS radical scavenging capacity of the samples. As depicted in Fig. 7. C–D, it was observed that HEG2 exhibited the highest ABTS radical scavenging rate, demonstrating the most effective antioxidant activity. Nonetheless, it was noteworthy that the ABTS radical scavenging rates of the other samples were consistently no less than 80 %. This suggested that the emulsion gel, stabilized by HP/Ps and subjected to a 20-min ultrasonic treatment, exhibited enhanced antioxidant activity.

Further assessment of the antioxidant capacity of the emulsion gel was conducted through the hydroxyl radical scavenging assay. Hydroxyl radicals, which were highly reactive oxygen species with potent oxidation capabilities, were effectively scavenged by the samples. The results, displayed in Fig. 7. E, indicated that HEG2 achieved the highest hydroxyl radical scavenging rate, approximately 82 %. During the initial 30 min of US, there was an increase in the hydroxyl radical scavenging rate corresponding to the increase in ultrasonic treatment time, while a notable decrease in this rate was observed in HEG4.

The determination of reducing power is also commonly associated with antioxidant properties. As detailed in Fig. 7. F, HEG2 demonstrated the highest reducing power among the samples. HEG0 and HEG1 exhibited comparable levels of reducing power, whereas HEG3 and HEG4 displayed slightly higher values than both HEG0 and HEG1. The process of US facilitates intermolecular interactions between proteins and pectin, thereby enhancing the stability of oil-in-water droplets. However, excessive US might lead to alterations in protein structure, which resulted in changes in reducing power, a decrease in the loading capacity for DHA algal oil, leakage phenomena, and a consequent reduction in antioxidant properties.

In light of these antioxidant-related experiments, we selected the emulsion HEG0, prepared without any ultrasonic treatment, and the emulsion HEG2, which underwent a 20-min ultrasonic treatment, for the subsequent in vitro simulated digestion experiment. Typically, the hydrolysis of a triglyceride molecule results in the release of two molecules of FFA and one molecule of monoglyceride. To ensure the reliability of the experiment and eliminate interference from other substances, a control group consisting solely of DHA algal oil was established, as shown in Fig. 7. G. Without ultrasonic treatment, the FFA release rate from HEG0 was observed to be higher than that of the control group. Following ultrasonic treatment, the FFA release rate from HEG2 increased significantly and exceeded that of HEG0. During the 80-min digestion period, the FFA release rates for all three groups initially surged but eventually plateaued, indicating a stabilization towards the experiment's conclusion. The kinetic analysis revealed that larger complex droplets, formed without US, were prone to gravitational separation, which impeded the adsorption of lipase onto the oil droplets. Conversely, ultrasonic treatment reduced the size of these droplets, thus increasing the surface area exposed to lipase and enhancing the overall degree of digestion. The experimental results demonstrated that the FFA release rate of HEG2 was the highest among the three groups. The higher the release efficiency for the digestion of functional lipids, the more greater the accessibility of FFAs were for absorption, consequently enhancing the bioavailability of DHA and nutrients (Ji & Luo, 2023; Ye et al., 2018).

Conclusions

This research investigated the impact of ultrasonic treatment duration on the stability, microstructure, 3D printing performance, and antioxidant activity of HEG. Initially, HEG0 produced from HP/Ps, without the application of ultrasonic treatment, exhibited heterogeneous droplet sizes and a disorganized distribution. This resulted in suboptimal 3D printing outcomes characterized by challenging shaping processes and diminished antioxidant activity. As the duration of ultrasonic treatment increased, notable enhancements were observed in the stability of HEG. There was a progressive homogenization of droplet sizes and an incremental increase in viscosity. However, extending the ultrasonic treatment beyond 20 min did not yield significant further improvements, and a decline in antioxidant activity was observed. In terms of 3D printing, despite the ambiguity in the printed lines, the material retained its capacity for shaping. Furthermore, an in vitro simulated digestion experiment demonstrated that ultrasonic treatment could enhance the bioavailability of HEG. The findings of this study affirmed that the ultrasonic-assisted formulation of H. pluvialis protein-pectin complexes significantly augmented both the stability and bioavailability of DHA, which were crucial for 3D food printing applications. The employment of Pickering emulsions in the design of 3D printing materials introduced novel methodologies for utilizing high-solid viscous food slurries in the production of personalized 3D printed food items.
