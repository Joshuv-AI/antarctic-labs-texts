Strategies employed by Iranian nurses for managing workplace bullying: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Saeedeh Piri, Rostam Jalali, Alireza Khatony
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to explore the strategies employed by nurses to manage workplace bullying.DesignQualitative descriptive study.SettingThe study was conducted in a hospital located in western Iran between 21 March and 22 September 2023.ParticipantsA purposive sample of 14 nurses was selected.MethodsData collection was conducted through semistructured in-depth interviews. The collected data were analysed using a conventional content analysis approach. Lincoln and Guba’s (2004) criteria were employed to ensure data validity. The data management process was facilitated using MAXQDA software (2020 version).ResultsNurses’ experiences of workplace bullying were categorised into two themes: ‘constructive strategies’ and ‘non-constructive strategies’. These themes were further divided into categories: ‘seeking support’, ‘individual empowerment’, ‘avoidance’, ‘confrontation’ and ‘no reaction’. The subcategories encompassed specific strategies employed by nurses to manage workplace bullying.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrate that nurses use both constructive and non-constructive strategies to manage workplace bullying. Understanding nurses’ strategies for managing workplace bullying can assist managers in developing comprehensive intervention programmes to address these issues. It is crucial to provide nurses with training on using constructive strategies and avoiding non-constructive ones. Creating a healthy and supportive work environment and addressing the psychological and social aspects of bullying phenomena also play a vital role in reducing and managing bullying behaviours.Keywords: Nurses, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Health, Intensive Care Units

Introduction

Today, workplace bullying in the nursing profession is recognised as a significant and complex challenge.1 2 Bullying refers to repeated and persistent behaviours3 in which the victim is subjected over time to verbal attacks, inappropriate work orders, physical threats and psychological pressure.4 These behaviours occur repeatedly, at least weekly, for a duration of 6 months or longer.5 6 The sources of such bullying behaviours in the workplace may include physicians, supervisors, colleagues, patients and their companions.7

The prevalence of workplace bullying varies across different countries.8 A systematic review conducted in 2018 on 79 articles reported that 81% of nurses experienced bullying in their workplace.9 In the USA, the prevalence among nurses has been reported at 31%,10 while in Saudi Arabia, 54.8% of nurses have reported experiencing bullying.11 In Iran, a 2021 study with a sample size of 184 nurses in Zanjan indicated that approximately 75.5% had experienced bullying during their career.12

The negative consequences of workplace bullying can be described at three levels.7 13 At the individual level, physical and psychological disorders, job burnout and decreased job satisfaction are observed.7 14 At the organisational level, reduced productivity, organisational distrust and intentions to leave the job have been reported.15 16 Regarding patient care, decreased quality of nursing care and increased medical errors are among the most significant impacts.2

Nurses who are victims of workplace bullying interpret and respond to this experience in various ways.17 In Portugal, nurses suffering from workplace bullying employ a combination of formal and informal strategies to manage bullying.17 Among American nurses, direct responses such as assertive communication and organisational departure are predominant,18 whereas in Turkey, indirect strategies such as silence and unit transfer are more common.19

Although numerous studies have addressed workplace bullying, research gaps remain.20 Most previous studies have predominantly adopted quantitative approaches, offering limited deep understanding of nurses’ lived experiences. Moreover, the primary focus has been on Western cultures, limiting our understanding of this phenomenon within the Iranian cultural context. While some studies have reported nurses’ experiences with bullying and its consequences, research focusing specifically on practical and effective coping strategies remains scarce. Therefore, considering these research gaps, this study aimed to elucidate the strategies employed by nurses to manage workplace bullying. The qualitative approach of this study facilitates a deeper understanding of how nurses confront bullying in their work environments and the coping strategies they use. The findings of this research, as one of the few comprehensive studies on coping strategies for bullying in Iranian nursing settings, provide an important framework for managing this phenomenon. Furthermore, these findings can inform the design of culturally appropriate educational programmes, the development of relevant organisational policies and serve as a foundation for future research. With this purpose in mind, the study was designed and conducted to explore the strategies employed by Iranian nurses in managing workplace bullying. The primary research question addressed in this study was: what strategies do nurses use to manage bullying behaviours in the workplace?

Methods

Study design

This study employed a conventional qualitative content analysis approach. As one of the types of qualitative content analysis, the conventional approach is rooted in the naturalistic paradigm and is typically used when existing theories or prior research on a phenomenon are limited or insufficient.21 In this approach, codes, categories and subcategories are derived inductively and directly from the data, rather than being imposed by pre-existing theoretical frameworks or coding schemes.

Epistemologically, the study is grounded in a constructivist perspective, which assumes that social realities are constructed and interpreted through human experiences. The researcher, through deep immersion in the data and by minimising personal biases, seeks to uncover the meanings of the phenomenon from the participants’ perspectives.22 In this context, objectivity is not achieved by distancing from the data, but rather through a transparent, systematic coding process and confirmability of the findings.23

Participants and sampling

This study was part of a larger qualitative project conducted between 21 March and 22 September 2023, in the internal medicine, surgical and intensive care wards of a teaching hospital in a city in western Iran.

14 nurses were selected using purposive sampling. Initially, the head nurses prepared a list of eligible nurses working in the target wards. Inclusion criteria included having a bachelor’s degree or higher in nursing, at least 1 year of clinical experience, a meaningful personal experience of being subjected to workplace bullying and a willingness to participate in the study.

The first author contacted the potential participants by phone, explained the study objectives and assessed their eligibility. If the criteria were met and the participant agreed, a face-to-face interview was scheduled. The first author, who has a professional background in nursing, was not employed at the study site during data collection but used her clinical expertise to identify participants with rich and relevant experiences. This professional familiarity also helped in crafting exploratory, contextually grounded interview questions. Maximum variation sampling was employed to ensure diversity in terms of age, work experience and educational background.

In qualitative research, sample size is commonly guided by the principle of data saturation—defined as the point at which no new information, codes or themes emerge from the data.24 In this study, after conducting 12 in-depth semistructured interviews, repeated patterns and redundancy in responses indicated that data saturation had likely been achieved. To ensure the adequacy and completeness of data, two additional interviews were conducted. As no new codes or themes emerged during these final interviews, data saturation was confirmed, resulting in a total of 14 interviews. All interviews were conducted in single sessions, and no participants withdrew from the study.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semistructured interviews guided by a structured interview guide (online supplemental file 1). The questions were developed based on a review of reputable literature on workplace bullying in healthcare settings.20 25 The initial draft of the interview guide was prepared by the first author and reviewed by three Iranian nurses to ensure cultural appropriateness. Based on their feedback, three culturally inappropriate questions were removed and replaced with three new items focusing on patient-initiated bullying.

The final version of the interview guide was developed after conducting three pilot interviews with eligible nurses who were not part of the main study sample. These pilot interviews helped refine the wording, relevance and flow of the questions.

At the beginning of each interview, the objectives of the study were explained to the participants, and informed consent was obtained. All interviews were conducted in a quiet room within the participants’ workplace by the first author, a female nurse with 12 years of clinical experience and a PhD candidate in nursing, who has prior experience conducting qualitative research. Each interview lasted approximately 30–45 min.

Interviews generally began with the open-ended question: ‘Please describe an experience when you encountered bullying in the workplace and explain how you managed that situation’. Follow-up questions were asked depending on the participants’ responses. These included inquiries such as which strategy they found most helpful, what specific and practical actions were effective at the moment of confrontation, whether their coping strategies had changed over time and whether they had ever used a strategy that proved ineffective or made the situation worse.

During the interviews, various strategies were employed to build trust with participants and address potential communication challenges. At the outset, the interviewer shared her professional background in nursing and clearly explained the objectives of the study to establish a safe and non-judgmental environment. The time and location of each interview were chosen by the participants based on their preferences.

In cases where participants provided incomplete or inconsistent information, reflective techniques were applied (eg, ‘If I understood you correctly, you felt … at that moment?’) to encourage clarification. Additionally, exploratory prompts such as ‘Please elaborate’, ‘How?’ and ‘Why?’ were used to elicit deeper and more detailed responses.

When participants had difficulty expressing their experiences or displayed strong emotional reactions, the researcher used brief pauses, topic shifts or short breaks to maintain the flow of conversation and ensure a sense of psychological safety. These techniques were practised and refined during the pilot interviews to strengthen the interviewer’s ability to obtain rich and meaningful data.

All interviews were conducted in Farsi, the native language of the participants. Following transcription and prior to the content analysis, the interview texts were translated from Farsi to English. To improve linguistic quality, the translations were initially refined using artificial intelligence-based language tools and then reviewed by an independent supervisor (academic advisor) to ensure both conceptual and linguistic accuracy. To further enhance the validity of the translation and minimise semantic distortion, a participant feedback process was implemented. In this step, selected translated excerpts were back-translated into Farsi and shared with participants to confirm that the intended meanings and content of the interviews had been accurately conveyed.

Reflexivity was considered a crucial component for ensuring transparency and methodological rigour throughout the research process. Before conducting each interview, the first author—who is a registered nurse with over 12 years of clinical and academic experience and has been trained in qualitative research—engaged in self-reflection to identify and bracket personal assumptions, values and potential biases. Reflexive journaling and field notes were maintained throughout the study to document thoughts, emotional responses and subjective interpretations. This process supported reflexive bracketing, enabling the researcher to minimise personal influence and approach the data with openness.

These records were revisited regularly to maintain awareness of the researcher’s positioning and ensure that findings emerged inductively from participants’ narratives rather than from prior beliefs or professional experiences. Additionally, the researchers discussed their positions and interpretations in team meetings to critically examine each other’s perspectives.

Data analysis

Data were analysed using the conventional content analysis approach described by Graneheim and Lundman.23 The analytical process began concurrently with the first interview and continued until data saturation was reached. Each interview was transcribed verbatim immediately after completion, and the transcripts were read several times by the research team to obtain a holistic understanding of the content.

In the next step, meaning units were identified and condensed. Two researchers independently performed the initial coding. Any discrepancies in coding were discussed and resolved in consultation with the third author. To enhance the credibility and confirmability of the findings, the extracted codes were returned to three participants for validation (member checking). Two participants fully endorsed the codes, while one provided corrective feedback, which was incorporated into the final analysis.

Additionally, triangulation was employed through peer debriefing: an external qualitative expert not involved in the initial coding reviewed a random sample of transcripts and corresponding codes to ensure interpretative consistency and accuracy.

Subsequently, validated codes that were conceptually similar were grouped into subcategories, and their inter-relationships were examined. These subcategories were then clustered into broader and more abstract categories, from which overarching themes emerged. The analytical process was iterative and non-linear, involving continuous movement between the raw data, codes, subcategories and themes to construct a coherent and data-driven thematic structure.

Across all coding decisions, particular emphasis was placed on participants’ own evaluative language: whether they deemed a given strategy effective/helpful or ineffective/counterproductive. Based on these explicit judgements, codes were assigned to one of two inductively derived categories: constructive strategies and non-constructive strategies. These category labels were not imposed by the researchers but rather emerged from participants’ descriptions and were applied only after reaching team consensus through discussion.

To ensure methodological rigour, several strategies were employed throughout the analysis. An audit trail was maintained to document all analytical decisions, coding definitions and memos. Reflexive bracketing was practised, with the first author regularly engaging in self-reflection through journaling and memo writing to identify and suspend personal biases and assumptions during the analytical process. These reflections were discussed in regular team meetings, where the interpretations were critically examined and refined through collaborative dialogue.

Data management was facilitated using MAXQDA software (2020 version). Initial coding and category development were conducted by the first author and were then reviewed and refined in collaboration with the second and third authors, both of whom have over 15 years of experience in qualitative research and content analysis.

Trustworthiness

The trustworthiness of the findings was evaluated using Lincoln and Guba’s (2004) criteria, including credibility, dependability, confirmability and transferability.26

To enhance credibility, the researchers engaged in prolonged interaction with participants, allocated sufficient time for data collection and analysis and conducted member checking. In the member-checking process, interview transcripts and extracted codes were returned to three participants, and they were asked to provide feedback on the researchers’ interpretations of their statements. Participants were invited to clarify any misinterpretations or discrepancies. All feedback provided by the participants was carefully considered and incorporated into the final stages of data analysis.

To enhance dependability, all steps and procedures of the study were thoroughly documented to ensure auditability. Additionally, collaborative coding was employed: two researchers independently coded a subset of the data, then discussed any discrepancies to reach consensus. A progressively developed codebook was refined throughout the analysis process during regular team meetings, ensuring consistency with emerging themes.

Confirmability was supported through external review: both the interview data and the analytical interpretations were examined by the second and third authors, who had expertise in qualitative research and the study’s subject matter. Their input contributed to the credibility of the coding and overall analytical rigour.

To ensure transferability, the study provided rich descriptions of the thematic categories, research setting, participants’ demographic characteristics, data collection and analysis procedures and direct participant quotations.

The study adhered to the COREQ (Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research) checklist.27

Ethical considerations

Prior to data collection, each participant attended a short briefing session in which the aims and procedures of the study were clearly explained. Participants were informed that their involvement was entirely voluntary and that they had the right to withdraw at any point without any consequences. All questions raised by participants were addressed.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants both verbally and in writing. Explicit permission was also sought and obtained for audio-recording the interviews. Participants were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their data. To protect their identities, each participant was assigned a unique code, and no identifying information was included in the transcripts or final report.

The audio files were encrypted and securely stored on a password-protected personal computer. These files were not shared with any individuals or institutions outside the research team. The data will be retained for 5 years following publication, after which they will be permanently deleted using secure data destruction software.

No financial or non-financial incentives were offered to participants for their involvement in the study. Participation was entirely unpaid and based on informed, voluntary agreement.

Patient and public involvement

Participants (nurses) were not involved in the design, recruitment or conduct of the study. However, their perspectives were central to the research objectives, and their input directly informed the thematic analysis and interpretation of the findings. Participants did not contribute to the writing or editing of this manuscript, and results will be disseminated through academic publication rather than direct participant feedback.

Results

The participants included 14 nurses (8 women and 6 men) with a mean age of 36.5 years (range: 25–47 years) and an average of 12.8 years of professional experience (range: 2–25 years). Regarding educational background, nine nurses (64%) held a bachelor’s degree, while five nurses (36%) held a master’s degree. Half of the participants (n=7, 50%) were working in intensive care units, while the remainder were employed in medical wards (n=4, 29%) and surgical wards (n=3, 21%).

The initial codes comprised 293 instances, which were reduced to 205 after removing duplicate codes and merging similar codes. After qualitative content analysis of the interview data, 2 themes, 5 categories and 11 subcategories were extracted. The themes were ‘constructive strategies’ and ‘non-constructive strategies’. The categories included ‘seeking support’, ‘individual empowerment’, ‘avoidance’, ‘confrontation’ and ‘no reaction’ (figure 1).

Constructive strategies

One of the main insights extracted from the data was the constructive strategies for managing workplace bullying, which were expressed through two categories: ‘seeking support’ and ‘individual empowerment’.

Seeking support

One of the actions frequently mentioned by most participants to manage bullying behaviours in the workplace was seeking resources, information and necessary support to improve skills and handle bullying behaviours. This action was categorised into three subcategories: ‘consulting with experienced colleagues’, ‘reporting to higher authorities’ and ‘psychological support’.

Consulting with experienced colleagues

One of the supportive strategies in managing workplace bullying is consulting with colleagues. This strategy was more commonly used by less experienced nurses. Such an approach can contribute to the development of a culture of open and supportive communication in the workplace, creating a healthy and constructive environment for discussing, examining and sharing issues and challenges experienced by nurses facing bullying.

I try to manage bullying behaviours at work by drawing on the experiences of more seasoned colleagues, and sometimes I ask them for advice. (Participant 8, male, 3 years of experience)

Colleagues with more work experience or strong communication skills can be extremely helpful to less experienced nurses in managing bullying behaviours. (Participant 3, male, 2 years of experience)

One of the newly hired colleagues was overweight, and some staff were mocking her, which really upset her. I called her aside and talked to her about it, offering a few strategies to deal with the mockery. After some time, she thanked me and said that with my advice, a large part of her problem had been resolved. (Participant 5, female, 18 years of experience)

Reporting to higher authorities

The interviewees shared experiences indicating that, in managing bullying behaviours, they often sought support from higher-level managers. This strategy appeared to be significantly influenced by gender, with male participants more likely than their female counterparts to report such incidents to senior authorities.

Our head nurse was a bully, and all of the staff had complaints about his behaviour. We held a meeting with the unit staff to discuss the issue, and with everyone’s agreement, we reported his behaviour to the management along with supporting documentation. A few days later, he was removed from his position as head nurse. (Participant 13, male, 15 years of experience)

We were in the middle of treating a critically ill patient when a physician who had just joined the hospital entered the unit and asked for the file of the patient in bed 5 to do a consultation. I told him we were dealing with several critical patients and asked him to wait a moment, but he started yelling in a disrespectful tone and threw the patient’s file at me before storming out. We followed up on the matter with the hospital administration, and the physician was reprimanded. (Participant 6, male, 12 years of experience)

Psychological support

Psychological support emerged as another strategy for managing bullying behaviours, and it was emphasised more by female and experienced participants. In a few cases, male participants also reported using this strategy.

One of the managers constantly had expectations beyond my job description, which caused me both physical and psychological distress. His behaviour became so unbearable that I had no choice but to seek help from a psychologist. (Participant 12, female, 24 years of experience)

We were really distressed by the heavy workload and being asked to do tasks beyond our job duties. When we brought it up with the head nurse, she suggested that we listen to calming music in the ward whenever we felt overwhelmed. (Participant 4, female, 25 years of experience)

Individual empowerment

Individual empowerment of nurses refers to enhancing their capabilities and personal skills to enable them to perform at their best when confronted with various bullying situations. This category encompasses two subcategories: ‘strengthening emotional management skills’ and ‘enhancing communication skills’ in dealing with bullying behaviours.

Strengthening emotional management skills

Emotion management skills—including the regulation of one’s emotional states, behaviours and reactions—were identified by participants as one of the key strategies for coping with bullying. These skills help nurses maintain a sense of control and empowerment in high-pressure situations, make better decisions and stay aware of and effectively manage their emotions. The use of this strategy was more evident among female participants and those with a master’s degree.

In my opinion, it’s very important to always see ourselves as strong and resilient individuals in the face of bullying behaviours—not as people who easily give in. With high self-confidence and by developing our personality, we can stand up for our rights and professional values. (Participant 2, female, 17 years of experience)

I try to stay calm even when I feel upset or angry because of others’ behaviours. When I leave the hospital, I do my best to let it all go and forget about it. (Participant 4, female, 25 years of experience)

When I had just started working, I made a few unintentional mistakes. Once, I entered several medications incorrectly into the system, and the shift supervisor yelled at me. At that moment, I swallowed my anger and told myself, ‘You can do this!’ That helped boost my confidence, improve my focus, and reduce my mistakes. (Participant 11, male, 9 years of experience)

Enhancing communication skills

Possessing communication skills plays a crucial role in managing bullying behaviours, a point repeatedly emphasised by the participants. Female participants tended to highlight respectful communication and being a good listener, while male participants focused more on acquiring communication skills through workshops and responding to unreasonable demands.

When faced with bullying behaviour, we should respond to the bully calmly and respectfully, rather than reacting quickly and emotionally. (Participant 5, female, 18 years of experience)

I’m very interested in attending workshops that teach communication skills and how to deal with bullying! When someone has the necessary communication skills, they can stand up to unreasonable demands from managers or the powerful physician system without being disrespectful, or make the person who is bullying them realize their mistake. (Participant 14, male, 19 years of experience)

A patient’s companion was extremely upset about bruising on the patient’s hand and started yelling: ‘I want to speak to the nurse!’ When I realized what was happening, I approached, introduced myself, and listened to his concerns. After I explained that the bruising was a side effect of the medication and not the nurse’s fault, he calmed down. (Participant 9, female, 23 years of experience)

Non-constructive strategies

Another theme explored in the study was the presence of non-productive strategies for managing bullying, which were categorised into three groups: ‘avoidance’, ‘confrontation’ and ‘no reaction’.

Avoidance

One of the non-constructive strategies for managing bullying behaviours identified in this study was avoidance, which was frequently reported by many participants. The specific form of avoidance varied depending on the source of the bullying—whether it originated from colleagues or from patients and their companions. In situations involving colleagues, where participants had some degree of control over interpersonal dynamics, they adopted interactive avoidance, such as deliberately limiting communication or interaction. In contrast, when the bullying came from patients or their companions—encounters that could not easily be avoided or refused—participants resorted to passive avoidance, such as physically leaving the situation, typically as a last-resort measure. This category was therefore divided into two subcategories: interactive avoidance and passive avoidance.

Interactive avoidance

Avoiding interaction with bullies was identified as an avoidance-based strategy for managing workplace bullying, particularly when dealing with colleagues. In this approach, individuals minimise their interactions with the bully and limit communication to only what is necessary. This strategy was more commonly emphasised by male participants, although a few female participants also reported avoiding social interactions with bullies at work.

I always try not to get too involved with physicians. Instead, I keep my interactions with them to a minimum. In many cases, they act in a very domineering way. This strategy doesn’t really solve the problem in the long run, but it helps me calm down a bit. (Participant 13, male, 15 years of experience)

My head nurse would always humiliate me in front of colleagues. So during shifts, I tried to avoid confronting her directly and kept myself busy with patients and paperwork. Her behaviour wasn’t something I could change, but at least this approach helped me get through the day without feeling completely drained. (Participant 8, male, 3 years of experience)

When I worked the same shift as one of the senior nurses, I was always forced to do her work too. I asked my supervisor not to schedule us together, but I realized it was useless because she would just find other ways to bully me—like constantly speaking badly about me behind my back. (Participant 6, female, 12 years of experience)

Passive avoidance

Passive avoidance, defined as deliberately not entering or withdrawing from a specific situation, was frequently employed by participants when facing bullying from patients and their companions, and in some instances, from colleagues. This strategy was more commonly adopted by nurses with less work experience.

A patient’s companion insisted on visiting during non-visiting hours. Despite my repeated explanations about the ward rules, he refused to comply and started shouting, swearing, and insulting me. He even pushed me and tore my uniform. At that moment, I tried not to engage and left the situation—I walked out of the ward! Because the system always defends the rights of patients and their companions. But afterward, I felt extremely upset. I kept thinking I should have stayed and defended myself. (Participant 7, female, 6 years of experience)

Sometimes, when colleagues talked about unethical issues, I would quickly leave the area under the pretext of giving medication to a patient. (Participant 12, female, 24 years of experience)

Confrontation

Confronting the bully was another strategy mentioned by some participants for managing bullying. This confrontation took place in both direct and indirect forms.

Direct confrontation

The majority of participants referred to using direct confrontation when dealing with the bully. A range of reactive responses was employed during these encounters, influenced by factors such as gender and work experience. Male participants tended to resort to physical altercations and direct verbal insults when confronting bullying. In contrast, female participants were more likely to use direct yet respectful verbal warnings. According to the participants, this strategy was typically employed when bullying behaviours escalated to a point where the individual could no longer tolerate the situation.

Once, I told one of the senior staff members who would always assign her tasks to me that she needed to take care of her own responsibilities. I said, ‘I know what my job description entails, and I’m not supposed to do both my own work and yours. (Participant 9, female, 23 years of experience)

When someone insults or harasses me, I try to reciprocate in kind. (Participant 8, male, 3 years of experience)

When I face bullying from colleagues or supervisors, I try to keep my composure and avoid physical altercations. However, when the bullying comes from patients or their companions, it often escalates into verbal or even physical conflicts. Perhaps it’s because we are in a position of power over them. Once, when a patient’s companion verbally insulted me and my colleague, I lost control and ended up in a physical confrontation. Other colleagues had to intervene to separate us. (Participant 3, male, 2 years of experience)

Indirect confrontation

A smaller number of participants adopted an indirect confrontation strategy when dealing with bullying behaviours. In this approach, individuals redefined their relationships with the bully—while maintaining physical presence—and employed subtle behaviours such as deliberate silence, ignoring or interacting in a cold and formal manner.

Once, I was deeply hurt by my colleague’s mocking remarks, but I pretended not to care and just smiled. (Participant 1, female, 29 years old, 7 years of experience)

I sometimes tried to ignore the insults and disrespectful behaviour of the bully, just to deprive them of the satisfaction they might get from upsetting or provoking me. (Participant 6, male, 12 years of experience)

One time, a physician asked me to move the echocardiography machine for him. I looked at him with surprise, as it wasn’t part of my job duties and I hadn’t expected such a request. He eventually had to ask the service staff to move the machine instead. (Participant 10, female, 12 years of experience)

When I realized that my colleague was repeatedly leaving the shift under various pretexts and assigning her patients to me, effectively shifting her responsibilities onto me, I changed the nature of our relationship. I began to interact with her in a very formal and distant manner so that she would no longer make such requests and become more accountable for her duties. (Participant 11, male, 9 years of experience)

No reaction

With the proliferation of bullying incidents and the lack of effective coping strategies, nurses are driven towards non-reactivity. This category was further delineated into two subcategories: ‘passive acceptance’ and ‘patience and tolerance’.

Passive acceptance

Some experienced nurses reported that, over time, they had become accustomed to various forms of bullying and had come to accept its different dimensions. This experience helped them manage such situations more effectively and control their stress when facing bullying incidents.

Bullying is woven into the very fabric of the nursing profession—there’s nothing we can do about it. We have to accept it as part of our job. (Participant 5, female, 18 years of experience)

When I encounter bullying, I consider it just another aspect of my job. I remind myself that these problems are not unique to me—other colleagues may also be dealing with similar issues. (Participant 14, male, 19 years of experience)

Patience and tolerance

Patience and tolerance were identified as another strategy employed in response to bullying. In the present study, many less-experienced participants repeatedly referred to using this approach as a means of managing bullying behaviours.

Once, someone whose patient had just passed away insulted me. I didn’t react at all. I tried to understand their emotional state and chose not to respond. (Participant 7, female, 6 years of experience)

During my first years on the job, I was treated very unfairly and made to work excessively. I tolerated it a lot. I believed I shouldn’t speak up against those who were bullying me because it might put my job at risk. (Participant 8, male, 3 years of experience)

Discussion

This qualitative study aimed to elucidate the strategies employed by Iranian nurses in managing workplace bullying. In the current study, the approach of nurses in dealing with bullying behaviours in the workplace was explained in terms of constructive and non-constructive strategies. Workplace bullying behaviours lead to various reactions among nurses in coping with and managing these behaviours.28 Evidence suggests that bullying victims initially respond to these behaviours using constructive coping strategies, but after realising the inability to resolve the issue, they resort to non-constructive and destructive strategies.29 Individuals who engage in constructive and positive countermeasures experience positive emotions and behaviours, leading to improved job satisfaction and a greater inclination to continue working.30 On the other hand, individuals who engage in negative and non-constructive actions experience negative thoughts and escape behaviours, resulting in a decrease in their quality of life and capabilities.31

One constructive strategy used by nurses to cope with and manage workplace bullying is seeking support. They identified three important strategies in this regard: consulting with experienced colleagues, reporting to higher authorities and receiving psychological support. Evidence demonstrates that social support is an effective strategy in dealing with bullying.17 32 Workplace support refers to the assistance individuals receive from their supervisors and colleagues.33 Receiving adequate support from experienced colleagues can prevent the development of psychological issues in employees and enhance feelings of calmness, security, boosted morale, acceptance, belonging and comfort.33 Other studies have also highlighted strategies for managing bullying behaviours, such as seeking support and consulting with colleagues in the initial stages, collective confrontation with bullies, filing formal concerns with organisational authorities25 and psychotherapy,34 which can help reduce the tensions caused by bullying. Unfortunately, some nurses refrain from reporting instances of bullying, not due to a lack of awareness of policies, but because of power imbalances between bullying victims and perpetrators.35 36 The results of a study in Turkey showed that 65.3% of bullies held senior management positions.37 This indicates that power differentials, even in environments with antibullying policies, can hinder the reporting of bullying incidents. Therefore, to effectively manage destructive bullying behaviours in the workplace, the provision of psychological counselling, peer support and the establishment and improvement of reporting systems for bullying incidents are necessary.

Another constructive strategy mentioned by all participants in controlling workplace bullying behaviours is individual empowerment through strengthening communication skills and emotional management skills. Evidence shows that nurses lack the necessary competency and skills to manage bullying behaviours.38 There is a direct and meaningful relationship between nurses’ knowledge and skills and the strategies employed to manage workplace bullying behaviours.39 In this regard, the results of a qualitative study in the USA (2019) indicate that bullying victims who possess the necessary skills to control their emotions and maintain self-confidence in performing their tasks can deter bullies from continuing their destructive behaviour.40 The findings of another study in Australia (2009) suggest that healthcare providers can manage their emotions when facing bullies through actions such as walking, exercising, practising anger control, humour and deep breathing.41 Individuals who manage their emotions are capable of understanding others’ emotions and effectively communicating with them, which can lead to better coping with workplace bullying.42 Evidence suggests that conducting training courses for nurses on communication and conflict management to address bullying behaviours can be a useful strategy, reducing verbal abuse and bullying rates from 90% to 76%.34 43 Therefore, organisational managers need to incorporate the strengthening of communication skills and emotional management in nurses through training and empowerment programmes to effectively manage destructive bullying behaviours in the workplace.

In dealing with destructive bullying behaviours in the workplace, some participants used non-constructive strategies of interactive avoidance and passive avoidance. These strategies involve avoiding interaction with unwanted situations and bullying, and instead of problem-solving or confronting the situation, the individual withdraws from it.44 Although these approaches may temporarily reduce tension, overall, they do not contribute to problem resolution and may even exacerbate the situation.5 Consistent with the current study, the results of a study in Iran (2021) showed that nursing students use avoidance methods such as escape and passivity to cope with bullying.45 The findings of a qualitative study in Turkey (2015) also indicated that participants in bullying situations used avoidance strategies such as distancing themselves from the bully and leaving the bullying situation.25 The use of non-constructive strategies to cope with bullying behaviours in the workplace can have negative physical and psychological consequences for nurses, such as mental health deterioration, decreased quality of life and increased work fatigue.46 Given these implications, it is necessary to familiarise nurses with constructive and non-constructive strategies for managing bullying behaviours in the workplace through training programmes. These programmes help nurses improve their problem-solving skills and have constructive and effective responses when dealing with bullies.

Direct or indirect confrontation with bullies was another non-constructive strategy used by some participants to manage bullying in the workplace. In direct confrontation, individuals can demand the cessation of the bully’s inappropriate behaviours by decisively confronting and speaking with the bully.47 A recent cross-sectional study conducted in Australia (2021) revealed that 49% of participants indicated their ability to challenge bullies’ inappropriate behaviour respectfully.36 However, in cases of severe violence, physical engagement with the bully may occur.48 Some individuals might feel insecure when facing bullies or exhibit less assertiveness in situations where the bully holds more power, leading them towards indirect, dreamlike approaches.25 49 In the context of indirect confrontation strategies, individuals attempt to alleviate their emotional distress by employing emotional responses such as crying, anxiety and fear, withdrawal from social interactions, silence or ignoring the bully. Reporting the incident to the police can also serve as an indirect method for managing workplace bullying.2549,51 Although these strategies can be effective in certain cases, long-term use should be approached with caution due to potential negative effects on the physical and psychological well-being of victims, as well as their job performance.52 A study conducted in the USA (2013) revealed that direct confrontation with the bully before the escalation of their behaviour was the most common strategy employed to halt workplace bullying.47 However, a study conducted in Portugal (2023) showed that approximately 13% of nurses felt unable to directly address or take direct action against bullies.17 Therefore, nurses need to receive training on how to respond appropriately to the inappropriate behaviours of bullies in a firm and non-confrontational manner. Training programmes should encompass effective communication, conflict management, stress management and anger control.

Some participants employed the non-constructive strategy of not reacting to manage bullying behaviours.

Silence or self-silencing is a functional mechanism for dealing with bullying that occurs due to energy constraints to resist the repulsive force and change the current situation.53 In this regard, the results of a qualitative study (2012) that examined the experiences of 99 American nurses about the strategies used in dealing with bullying showed that not responding to bullying was one of their used strategies.54 Also, in an exploratory study with a qualitative and quantitative approach in Brazil (2019), the results showed that in therapeutic health centres, employees were willing to accept problems and suggested to victims of bullying to use the acceptance strategy by downplaying and ignoring wrong behaviours.55 In another study (2020), experienced Australian nurses used emotion-focused strategies such as ‘accompanying fate’ and ‘waiting to see what happens’ under the title of accepting bullying behaviours.36 Many victims of bullying, due to fear of losing their job and creating engagement, try to tolerate bullying by using the acceptance strategy, which leads to job dissatisfaction and a tendency to leave the profession.56 To maintain the physical and mental health of nurses, and proper management of bullying behaviours in the workplace, it is necessary for nurses to receive the necessary training and pay special attention to the negative risks of using the silence and acceptance strategy.

Although qualitative research is inherently limited in terms of generalisability, the transferability of these findings to other settings with similar organisational structures can be considered. Specifically, in work environments characterised by hierarchical structures, collectivist cultures and high-stress conditions where interpersonal conflicts are common, these findings may be relevant. From a practical standpoint, the results highlight the necessity of establishing confidential reporting systems, developing clear organisational policies to address workplace bullying and fostering a culture that supports reporting such behaviours. Regarding education, designing training programmes to empower nurses in communication skills, emotional management and resilience is recommended. Future research could expand this area by employing mixed-method approaches to explore workplace bullying in different settings and among various occupational groups, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of contributing factors and coping strategies.

Limitations

In this study, a conventional qualitative content analysis approach was employed, where analysis was conducted inductively and without an initial reliance on a specific theoretical framework. This approach allows for the emergence of concepts grounded in the data and facilitates a deeper understanding of participants’ experiences. However, it may present theoretical limitations, as the absence of a predefined conceptual framework can complicate the comparison of findings with existing theories.

From a methodological perspective, several limitations should be acknowledged, including the interpretive nature of the analysis, the potential influence of researchers’ assumptions and the inability to achieve statistical generalisability. Additionally, selecting participants from a single geographical region may limit the transferability of the findings to other contexts. Efforts were made to enhance credibility through strategies such as member checking, coding by multiple researchers and thorough documentation of the analytical process.

Data were collected through interviews, which may have influenced participants’ responses and introduced potential bias. However, the researcher attempted to mitigate this by reassuring participants about the confidentiality of their identities and responses.

Another limitation was the lack of a systematic analysis regarding the impact of demographic variables on nurses’ coping strategies against bullying. Although such data were collected, the descriptive nature of the study did not permit in-depth exploration of group differences. Therefore, this limitation provides a foundation for future research focusing specifically on how demographic factors influence coping strategies in workplace bullying.

Conclusion

The findings of this qualitative study, grounded in an inductive content analysis approach, revealed that Iranian nurses employ a diverse range of strategies when confronted with workplace bullying. These strategies were broadly classified into two main categories: constructive and non-constructive. Constructive strategies involved consulting experienced colleagues, reporting bullying to superiors, seeking psychological counselling, enhancing emotional regulation and improving communication skills. Conversely, some participants resorted to non-constructive strategies such as passive acceptance, avoidance or direct and indirect confrontation.

These results underscore the complex and multifaceted nature of bullying within nursing environments, shaped by individual, organisational and cultural factors. While some strategies align with global patterns identified in previous studies,53 54 others distinctly reflect the unique cultural context of the participants.

Effectively addressing workplace bullying requires a comprehensive, multilevel approach. At the individual level, training in communication and emotion management is essential. At the team level, fostering collaboration and mutual support plays a crucial role. At the organisational level, establishing clear policies, confidential reporting systems and antibullying interventions is vital to create a safer work environment.

Future research should explore these strategies across larger and more diverse samples and within theoretical frameworks to deepen understanding. Additionally, evaluating the effectiveness of educational interventions on nurses’ coping mechanisms can provide practical insights. Integrating conflict management skills into nursing curricula is also recommended to empower nurses to better handle workplace challenges.
