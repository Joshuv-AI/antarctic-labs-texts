Paradigm shift: from pulmonary vasodilation to cardiopulmonary dual-track therapy—a comprehensive review of pathophysiology and advances in pulmonary hypertension-associated right heart failure
Authors: Rongtao Chen, Chang Liu, Jiange Han, Jinrui Dong, Wenqian Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH)-induced right heart failure (RHF) remains a major determinant of poor patient outcomes, driven by multifaceted pathophysiological mechanisms including impaired right ventricle-pulmonary artery coupling, metabolic reprogramming, mitochondrial dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and gut–lung axis disruption. Traditional therapies focusing solely on pulmonary vasodilation provide symptomatic relief but often fail to halt progressive right heart deterioration. Recent advances underscore a paradigm shift toward cardiopulmonary dual-track strategies that simultaneously reduce right ventricular afterload and directly protect or enhance right heart function. Novel agents such as Sotatercept—an activin signaling inhibitor—significantly reverse vascular remodeling and improve hemodynamics. Metabolic modulators (e.g., metformin), mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants (e.g., Elamipretide), and immunomodulators (e.g., tocilizumab) show promise in correcting bioenergetic deficits and inflammatory activation. Interventional approaches like percutaneous pulmonary artery denervation (PADN) and atrial septostomy serve as effective bridging options, while percutaneous right ventricular assist devices (pRVADs) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) offer critical circulatory support in end-stage disease. Emerging therapies—including gene editing, cell-based treatments, and exosome technology—hold potential for targeted, personalized intervention. Despite these innovations, challenges persist such as variable treatment responses, long-term safety concerns, and translational inefficiencies. Future efforts should prioritize multi-omics-guided precision medicine, multidisciplinary care integration, and novel technologies like CRISPR/Cas9 to advance disease-modifying therapies and improve quality of life.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Clinical burden and prognosis

Right heart failure (RHF) is a major cause of disability and death in patients with pulmonary hypertension (PH), with its core pathophysiology involving ventricular remodeling, metabolic abnormalities, and neuroendocrine activation triggered by increased right ventricular afterload [1]. Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), a subtype of PH characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, often progresses to RHF and even death [2]. Epidemiological data show that RHF is the most common reason for hospitalization in PH patients, accounting for approximately 56% of all hospitalizations. Among patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), about 68% have secondary PH, and these individuals face a poorer prognosis with an in-hospital mortality rate of 14%. If inotropic support or ICU admission is required, mortality can rise to 46–48% [3–5].

Multiple studies have established key predictors of RHF prognosis. Among hemodynamic parameters, a right ventricle-pulmonary artery coupling (RV-PA coupling) ratio Ees/Ea < 0.68 is an independent predictor of mid- to long-term all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 0.2, P = 0.038), indicating right ventricular decompensation [6, 7]. Other independent predictors include pulmonary arterial compliance < 2.3 mL/mmHg, left ventricular ejection fraction < 30%, and right ventricular end-diastolic volume > 171 mL [8]. Clinically, WHO functional class (FC) is the strongest predictor of long-term survival: the REVEAL registry showed that FC III patients who improved to FC I/II within one year had a 3-year survival rate of 84%, compared to only 66% or lower in those who did not improve [9]. Hyponatremia (≤ 136 mEq·L⁻1), low systolic blood pressure (< 100 mmHg), and comorbid connective tissue disease (CTD) are all independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality in RHF patients (OR 4.29–4.92) and significantly affect survival at 3, 6, and 12 months after discharge [2].

Globally, although PAH is considered a rare disease, its burden is substantial. There are approximately 192,000 cases worldwide, with an age-standardized prevalence rate of 2.28 per 100,000. The prevalence is higher in women than in men (2.75 vs. 1.78 per 100,000) [10, 11]. Annual deaths from PAH total around 22,000, with an age-standardized mortality rate of 0.27 per 100,000 [12]. Although the age-standardized years of life lost (YLL) rate has decreased by 38.2% since 1990, the disease burden of PAH remains comparable to that of chronic myeloid leukemia or multiple sclerosis and is particularly prominent in older adults (75–79 years) and women (accounting for 62% of cases) [11].

Clinical assessment of RHF is inherently complex due to its diverse causes and frequent overlap with left heart failure or other critical conditions, posing challenges for early recognition and accurate diagnosis [1]. Common causes include pulmonary hypertension, right ventricular myocardial infarction, valvular disease, cardiomyopathy, pulmonary embolism, and chronic lung disease [13]. The core pathological mechanism involves the high sensitivity of the right ventricle to acute increases in afterload or sudden impairment of myocardial function, leading to right ventricular dilation, leftward shift of the interventricular septum, and impaired left ventricular filling. This reduces cardiac output and coronary perfusion, exacerbating right ventricular ischemia and functional decline in a vicious cycle [14]. Common clinical manifestations of RHF include dyspnea, fatigue, jugular venous distension, hepatomegaly, peripheral edema, and hypotension [15]. Elevated BNP/NT-proBNP levels are often observed, while cardiac biomarkers, liver function tests, and lactate levels help assess myocardial injury, congestion, and perfusion status [15, 16].

Imaging evaluation centers on echocardiography. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) allows real-time assessment of right ventricular size and function, septal motion, inferior vena cava diameter and collapsibility, and estimation of pulmonary artery pressure [17]. Hand-held ultrasound devices (HHUD) show good agreement with conventional ultrasound in critical care settings, demonstrating strong clinical utility [18]. Cardiac magnetic resonance (cMRI) is the gold standard for evaluating right ventricular structure and function, and derived parameters such as the stroke volume-to-end-systolic volume ratio (SV/ESV) also have prognostic value [19].

In hemodynamic assessment, right ventricle-arterial coupling is a key concept reflecting the match between right ventricular function and afterload [19]. Although the gold standard is the invasive measurement Ees/Ea, non-invasive surrogates such as the TAPSE/sPAP ratio are commonly used in clinical practice. A reduced TAPSE/sPAP ratio indicates impaired coupling and predicts poor outcomes [20]. Studies suggest that exercise hemodynamics can reveal occult right heart dysfunction not apparent at rest, particularly in patients with HFpEF-related pulmonary hypertension. Preload mechanisms play a significant role during exercise, manifested as a sharp rise in right atrial pressure and an increased RAP/CO slope [21].

The necessity of a shift in treatment paradigm

Current standard treatment for PAH primarily targets pulmonary vasodilation, using drugs such as endothelin receptor antagonists, phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors, and agents acting on the prostacyclin pathway [22, 23]. Although the use of multi-drug combination strategies has increased 5-year survival from 34% in 1991 to around 60% in 2015, a significant proportion of patients still show inadequate response or disease progression. This highlights the limitations of conventional therapies in delaying or reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling [24].Recent advances in understanding the pathophysiology of right heart failure (RHF) suggest that right ventricular dysfunction is not solely caused by increased afterload. It also involves multiple mechanisms such as metabolic remodeling, inflammatory activation, and mitochondrial dysfunction [25, 26]. Therefore, treatment strategies that only focus on reducing afterload are insufficient. There is an urgent need to adopt a dual-track approach that simultaneously lowers afterload and directly improves right heart function.

This shift in treatment paradigm is strongly supported by evidence from multiple research fields [24, 25]. For example, the Phase III STELLAR trial demonstrated that the activin signaling inhibitor sotatercept not only reduced pulmonary vascular resistance but also significantly improved right ventricular function. Treated patients showed a notable reduction in the right ventricular workload index, and cardiac function continued to improve during a median follow-up of two years [27].Meanwhile, interventions targeting right ventricular metabolic remodeling show great potential. Preclinical studies indicate that metformin can enhance myocardial glucose utilization and reduce lipid accumulation by activating AMPK signaling [28]. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants such as Elamipretide (ELAM) have shown potential for improving ventricular volume and function in heart failure patients [29]. In anti-inflammatory therapy, the IL-6 receptor antagonist tocilizumab is currently under investigation in a Phase II clinical trial (TRANSFORM-UK), which aims to evaluate its effects on pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular function [30].In the area of device-based support, percutaneous right ventricular assist devices (such as Impella RP® and ProtekDuo®) and veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) provide temporary circulatory support for hemodynamically unstable end-stage patients. These can serve as a bridge to transplantation or recovery [31–33]. Additionally, non-pharmacological interventions such as remote pulmonary artery pressure monitoring (CardioMEMS) and pulmonary artery denervation offer new directions for personalized treatment [34].

In summary, the treatment strategy for PAH-induced RHF should shift from the traditional "lung-centered" approach to a "cardiopulmonary dual-track" model. This means that while reducing pulmonary vascular resistance, active interventions should also target the metabolic, inflammatory, and fibrotic processes in the right ventricle. Such a comprehensive strategy may more effectively delay disease progression and improve patient outcomes. Future research should focus on exploring novel therapies that directly target right heart function and promote the integration of multi-mechanism drugs with personalized device support [35].

Right ventricular response to increased afterload: from adaptation to decompensation

Traditional hemodynamic perspective

Under the traditional hemodynamic perspective, the right ventricle (RV) initially compensates for pressure overload through two main mechanisms. The first is myocardial hypertrophy, where the ventricular wall thickens to enhance contractility and normalize wall stress according to Laplace’s law [36]. The second is homeometric autoregulation (also known as the Anrep effect), in which the heart increases contractile efficiency and calcium sensitivity without changing its initial length, allowing it to respond to a sudden rise in afterload [19, 37].

A central concept in evaluating right ventricular function and its adaptation to afterload is right ventricle-pulmonary artery coupling (RV-PA coupling) [38]. The gold standard for assessing this coupling is the invasive measurement of the RV pressure–volume loop using cardiac catheterization, which calculates the ratio of ventricular elastance (Ees) to arterial elastance (Ea) [19, 39]. A ratio close to 1 indicates good coupling and optimal ventricular efficiency [39, 40]. In clinical practice, non-invasive alternatives are more commonly used. Among these, the ratio of tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion to pulmonary artery systolic pressure (TAPSE/PASP) has been widely validated as a practical and reliable marker. A reduced ratio (typically < 0.36 mm/mmHg) suggests impaired coupling and is significantly associated with poor patient outcomes [40, 41].

Recent advances in echocardiography have enabled the use of three-dimensional echocardiography-derived parameters such as the stroke volume to end-systolic volume ratio (SV/ESV), which shows better agreement with invasive measurements. This is especially useful in patients where PASP cannot be accurately estimated or in post-surgical cases [7, 42, 43]. Additionally, newer metrics like the ratio of right ventricular forward stroke volume to end-systolic volume (RV forward SV/ESV) have demonstrated superior predictive value in patients with secondary tricuspid regurgitation compared to traditional indicators [42].Despite ongoing progress in assessment methods, the central role of RV-PA coupling remains unchanged. It continues to be a key concept for understanding the adaptation and eventual decompensation of right ventricular function, as well as an essential tool for risk stratification and treatment decision-making [39].

Modern pathophysiological core advances

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a progressive disease characterized by pulmonary vascular remodeling, persistently elevated pulmonary arterial pressure, and right ventricular failure [44]. Its pathogenesis involves complex interactions across multiple systems and pathways (Table 1), including genetic factors (e.g., BMPR2 mutations) [63], epigenetic dysregulation (DNA damage, abnormal miRNA expression) [59, 64], metabolic reprogramming (enhanced glycolysis, impaired fatty acid oxidation) [48], ion homeostasis disruption, and activation of inflammatory and immune responses [44, 51]. Right ventricular function is a key determinant of outcomes in PAH patients. Sustained pressure overload triggers the transition from compensatory hypertrophy to decompensated heart failure [65]. This process involves multiple mechanisms, including metabolic disturbances (e.g., Warburg effect, impaired mitochondrial energy production) [66], ischemia due to capillary rarefaction, oxidative stress, neurohormonal dysregulation, and abnormal adrenergic signaling [49, 67]. These pathological changes collectively contribute to maladaptive remodeling and impaired contractile function of the right ventricle.

Current treatment strategies for PAH primarily target pulmonary vascular resistance using prostanoids, endothelin receptor antagonists, and phosphodiesterase inhibitors. However, there remains a lack of specific therapies that directly improve right heart function [26, 68]. Recent research highlights the importance of multi-mechanism interventions, such as PDK inhibitors (e.g., dichloroacetate) to correct metabolic abnormalities [69], modulation of adrenergic signaling (e.g., carvedilol) [67], and suppression of endoplasmic reticulum stress [61]. These approaches reflect a shift from a "lung-centered" to a "cardiopulmonary-integrated" treatment paradigm.Future efforts should focus on a deeper understanding of molecular mechanisms, combined with multimodal imaging and risk stratification, to advance precision therapies aimed at protecting the right ventricle and modulating multiple pathways [70].

Metabolic remodeling

Pulmonary arterial vascular cells in PAH—such as smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts—exhibit metabolic features similar to cancer cells, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect (enhanced aerobic glycolysis) [45]. These cells shift from a normal contractile phenotype to a rapidly proliferating, apoptosis-resistant state, while their energy metabolism switches from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis [46, 47]. This metabolic reprogramming drives pulmonary vascular remodeling by supplying biosynthetic precursors, making glycolysis inhibition (e.g., using 2-deoxyglucose) a promising new treatment approach [45, 71].

Metabolic disturbances are especially pronounced in cases with BMPR2 gene mutations, a major genetic cause of PAH (Fig. 1) [72]. Loss of BMPR2 function inhibits BMP/SMAD1/5/8 signaling while abnormally activating the TGF-β/SMAD2/3 pathway. This imbalance enhances glycolysis through multiple mechanisms, including the miR-124/PTBP1/PKM2 axis, GSK-3β/β-catenin signaling, PPARγ suppression, and HIF2α upregulation, collectively accelerating disease progression [44, 63, 72]. BMPR2 mutation also profoundly affects right ventricular metabolic homeostasis: cardiomyocytes show impaired mitochondrial respiration, reduced glucose responsiveness, and insulin resistance [72]. Mechanistically, the protein MFGE8 promotes fatty acid uptake via the PI3K/mTOR pathway (increasing ACC phosphorylation and CD36 membrane translocation), leading to lipid accumulation and lipotoxicity. This provides experimental support for the lipotoxicity hypothesis of right heart failure [63].Fig. 1Signaling imbalance in pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) pathogenesis and mechanism of sotatercept intervention

Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) plays a dual role in PAH. In human PAH patients, genes related to FAO (such as FABP1, FABP4, and CPT1) are upregulated in small pulmonary arteries. In animal models, FAO inhibitors (e.g., oxfenicine targeting CPT1) can reverse vascular remodeling and reduce right ventricular systolic pressure [73, 74]. However, activating the PPARγ pathway has shown protective effects in the SuHx rat model. The PPARγ agonist pioglitazone upregulates genes like CPT1b and FABP4, corrects miRNA network imbalances (e.g., miR-197 and miR-146b), and improves lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function, thereby preventing right ventricular failure [75]. These contradictory findings suggest that FAO modulation requires precise targeting.

One study used PET imaging combined with cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) to analyze 17 PAH patients, revealing a critical link between right ventricular metabolic reprogramming and functional decline [76]. As mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) increased, right ventricular glucose uptake (FDG SUVPVE) rose significantly (r = 0.68), and the FDG/FTHA ratio also increased (r = 0.60), indicating enhanced glycolysis alongside abnormal fatty acid metabolism. These metabolic disturbances correlated negatively with right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) (FDG: r = −0.56; FTHA: r = −0.62), confirming that metabolic imbalance drives right ventricular decompensation [68, 76].

Metabolic abnormalities also extend to epigenetic regulation. Lactate produced from glycolysis induces histone and non-histone "lactylation" modifications (e.g., H3K18la), which regulate inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and cell phenotype switching (such as promoting M2 macrophage polarization and smooth muscle cell proliferation). This exacerbates vascular remodeling and right heart failure [71]. Metabolic dysregulation in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells is equally critical, characterized by the Warburg effect, disrupted FAO, TCA cycle imbalance, and increased ROS. These are mediated through mechanisms such as BMPR2 loss, eNOS uncoupling, and impaired mitophagy, leading to endothelial dysfunction and mesenchymal transition [66, 74]. Targeting the glycolysis-lactate axis and mitochondrial metabolic pathways has emerged as a potential therapeutic strategy.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a central role in the pathogenesis of PAH, involving multiple abnormalities such as metabolic reprogramming, dynamic imbalance, and oxidative stress (Fig. 2) [50]. In PAH patients’ pulmonary vascular cells (endothelial and smooth muscle cells) and right ventricular cardiomyocytes, mitochondria show significant functional disturbances. These include enhanced aerobic glycolysis (Warburg effect), disrupted tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, dysregulated fatty acid oxidation (FAO), upregulated glutaminolysis, and impaired electron transport chain (ETC) function [44, 48, 49]. These changes are driven by stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor HIF-1α, imbalance in the PDK/PDH axis, and excessive mitochondrial fission mediated by dynamin-related protein Drp1, ultimately promoting abnormal cell proliferation, apoptosis resistance, and vascular remodeling [46, 48, 77].Fig. 2Metabolic reprogramming in right ventricular failure

Dynamical imbalance is one of the core pathological features. Right ventricular ischemia activates Drp1 translocation to mitochondria, leading to excessive mitochondrial fission, fragmentation, loss of membrane potential, structural swelling, and ultimately impaired diastolic function [78]. Preclinical studies have confirmed that the Drp1 inhibitor Mdivi-1 and the Drp1-Fis1 interaction inhibitor P110 can effectively improve mitochondrial ultrastructure and cardiac function [79, 80]. Metabolic reprogramming is also complex: chronic inhibition of mitochondrial complex III induces a shift to glycolysis, causing accumulation of glucose and intermediates like glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate in lung tissue, with glucose levels strongly correlating with right ventricular systolic pressure (r = 0.77) [81]. Mutations in the NFU1 gene impair mitochondrial respiration by reducing lipoic acid (LA) bioavailability, and LA supplementation can reverse angiogenic defects such as reduced pulmonary capillary density [82]. Integrated multi-omics analysis further supports the central role of ETC dysfunction (GSEA NES = −1.851), showing decreased citrate, elevated 3-phenyllactic acid and ADP levels, and abnormal expression of 14 mitochondria-related genes (e.g., MT-ATP6/8) in lung tissue [83].

Notably, oxidative stress and disrupted calcium homeostasis exacerbate disease progression. Mitochondrial dysfunction increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) and nitrosative stress. Females are more susceptible to protein nitration due to estrogen-enhanced nitric oxide production, leading to endothelial injury [81, 84]. The HMGB1 protein activates the TLR4-ERK axis, phosphorylating Drp1 (Ser616), inducing mitochondrial fission and stimulating autophagy. This subsequently degrades BMPR2 and downregulates Id1 expression, ultimately driving proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells [85].

Targeted mitochondrial therapies show promise based on these mechanisms. Inhibiting Drp1 (e.g., with Mdivi-1 or Drpitor1a) or enhancing fusion (e.g., adenovirus-mediated Mfn2 overexpression) can restore mitochondrial network integrity [77, 86]. Metabolic interventions include the PDK inhibitor dichloroacetate (DCA) to restore oxidative phosphorylation, the FAO inhibitor trimetazidine to improve right heart function, and the glutaminase inhibitor CB-839 to block abnormal catabolism [87]. Emerging therapies such as mitochondrial transplantation, antioxidants targeting nitrosative stress, and LA supplementation have been shown to reverse pathological phenotypes in animal models [88]. However, the use of mitochondrial membrane potential as a prognostic biomarker remains limited by disease duration and treatment interference. Future studies should focus on exploring the mitochondrial-metabolic-oxidative stress network to advance personalized treatment [89].

Inflammation and immune response

Inflammation is not merely a secondary phenomenon in PAH, but a central driver of disease progression [51]. The core mechanism involves a complex positive feedback loop (Fig. 3): various immune cells—including macrophages, T/B lymphocytes, neutrophils, dendritic cells, and mast cells—are recruited by cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6), chemokines (e.g., CCL2), and inflammatory mediators (e.g., LTB4, HMGB1). These cells infiltrate the perivascular areas and form tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) [51–53]. Activated immune cells interact with abnormally phenotyped vascular wall cells (endothelial, smooth muscle, and fibroblast cells), continuously releasing inflammatory factors that together promote pulmonary vasoconstriction, medial hypertrophy, intimal hyperplasia, and adventitial fibrosis, ultimately leading to irreversible vascular remodeling [53, 90].

Notably, specific immune cell subsets exacerbate pathology through distinct mechanisms [91]. M1 macrophages upregulate matrix metalloproteinases MMP-1 and MMP-10 via MAPK/PI3K/AKT/STAT3 pathways under hypoxia, with STAT1 specifically regulating MMP-10 and promoting pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration [92]. T lymphocytes show a Th17/Treg imbalance: increased Th17 cells enhance inflammation, while impaired Treg function weakens immune suppression [93]. Bidirectional Mendelian randomization studies confirm that CD4 + Treg cells promote PAH progression via the IL-6/gp130 signaling pathway [94]. B cell activation leads to circulating autoantibodies (e.g., anti-endothelial antibodies) that directly induce endothelial apoptosis. A specific B cell phenotype (CD20–CD38–AC) has been causally linked to PAH risk [91, 93]. Additionally, expansion of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (CD11b +), neutrophil elastase-mediated extracellular matrix degradation, and enhanced inflammatory cytokine expression via BMPR2 mutation-activated NF-κB further amplify immune injury [91, 94].

Critically, inflammation and pulmonary arterial stiffening form a bidirectional vicious cycle [90]. Inflammatory infiltration induces endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT), extracellular matrix remodeling (collagen deposition, elastin degradation), and smooth muscle calcification (driven by RUNX2) via NF-κB/STAT3 pathways, collectively leading to vascular stiffening [90, 93]. Stiffened vessels, in turn, increase shear stress and further activate endothelial inflammatory responses, creating a self-reinforcing pathological loop. The BRD4/miR-204 axis has been shown to regulate both inflammatory factors (IL-6/MCP-1) and calcification genes [90, 91].

Targeted immunomodulatory strategies show promise. Preclinical studies confirm that inhibiting the IL-6 receptor (tocilizumab), depleting B cells (rituximab), blocking IL-1 (anakinra), or neutralizing neutrophil elastase can improve experimental PAH [95, 96]. Some clinical trials have shown that these approaches alleviate symptoms or improve hemodynamics, though heterogeneity in efficacy and mechanistic complexity have limited widespread use [96]. Future efforts should integrate multi-omics and precise immune profiling to explore combinations of immunomodulators with conventional vasodilators or metabolic modulators, aiming to overcome current therapeutic limitations.

Gut-lung axis

A bidirectional exists between gut microbiota dysbiosis and PAH through the "gut-lung axis," which collectively drives disease progression (Fig. 4). Both PAH patients and animal models (e.g., induced by MCT, hypoxia, or Sugen5416) show significant gut microbiota disruption. This is characterized by an increase in Firmicutes and Proteobacteria, and a decrease in Bacteroidetes [97]. Additionally, there is a reduction in short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria with anti-inflammatory effects (such as Akkermansia and Butyrivibrio), while pro-inflammatory genera (e.g., Bacteroides, Streptococcus) become more abundant [97, 98].

This microbial imbalance compromises intestinal barrier integrity and increases permeability, allowing lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and microbial metabolites to enter the bloodstream [98]. Among these, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO) exacerbates pulmonary vascular inflammation and remodeling by activating the HMGB1/TLR4/NF-κB pathway [99]. Reduced levels of SCFAs—especially butyrate—weaken their anti-remodeling effects, which are normally mediated through HDAC6 inhibition and activation of G protein-coupled receptors FFAR2/3 [54]. Increased serotonin (5-HT) activates the RhoA/ROCK pathway, leading to vasoconstriction [98].

Altered microbial metabolism also leads to decreased production of secondary bile acids (e.g., lithocholic acid), impairing the function of bile acid receptors TGR5 and FXR, and reducing endothelial protection (such as eNOS upregulation and ET-1 inhibition) [54]. Clinical metagenomic studies further reveal that gut microbiota diversity (Shannon index) is negatively correlated with pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) in PAH patients [100]. Enrichment of Bacteroidetes is accompanied by increased TMA synthesis genes, leading to elevated plasma TMAO in high-risk patients [55]. Genes involved in butyrate and valerate synthesis (e.g., phosphate butyryltransferase) and the abundance of corresponding bacteria are reduced, resulting in lower circulating SCFAs. Reduced copy numbers of bile acid hydrolase genes further disrupt bile acid metabolism [101]. These changes exacerbate PAH through three main pathways: TMAO activates macrophages and promotes smooth muscle proliferation; SCFA deficiency reduces regulatory T cells; and impaired bile acid signaling worsens endothelial function [54].

Bidirectional Mendelian randomization studies provide genetic evidence supporting a causal role of the gut-lung axis. Certain genera such as Alistipes and Victivallis increase PAH risk, while others like Coprobacter and Erysipelotrichaceae UCG003 appear protective [56]. PAH itself can also alter microbiota composition—for example, increasing genera such as Butyricicoccus. Although PAH patients share about 30% of core microbiota with cohabiting family members, patient-specific enrichment of Bacteroidetes and significant reduction in anti-inflammatory metabolites (e.g., butyrate, lithocholic acid) may serve as disease-specific markers [54, 56].

Interventions targeting the gut microbiota show therapeutic potential. Probiotics (e.g., Lactobacillus), prebiotics (e.g., inulin), and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) can restore SCFA levels, inhibit TMAO/5-HT and TLR4-mediated inflammation, and improve pulmonary vascular remodeling and right heart function in animal models (e.g., SU5416/hypoxia rats) [99]. Antibiotic treatment and intermittent fasting have also shown benefits in preclinical studies [102]. However, further research is needed to identify PAH-specific pathogenic bacteria and their metabolic pathways, and to verify whether microbial changes are primary abnormalities in treatment-naïve patients, in order to advance targeted microbial therapies into clinical practice [100].

Hypoxia

Hypoxia plays a key role in the pathology of PAH, particularly in driving the transition from compensatory hypertrophy to decompensated failure of the right ventricle [65]. In PAH patients, increased pulmonary vascular resistance raises the afterload on the right ventricle, triggering initial compensatory hypertrophy. However, restricted coronary blood flow, imbalance in oxygen supply and demand, and reduced microvascular density together lead to chronic myocardial hypoxia [65, 67]. Hypoxia activates the HIF-1α pathway to promote angiogenesis in response to myocardial needs (Fig. 5), but severe hypoxia triggers p53-mediated suppression of HIF-1α, which impairs vascular formation [103]. This process induces metabolic remodeling (enhanced glycolysis, suppressed fatty acid oxidation) and energy depletion, ultimately contributing to right ventricular decompensation [65]. It is worth noting that effective treatments for right ventricular failure remain limited, and anti-hypoxia strategies such as hemoglobin oxygen affinity modulators (e.g., ITPP) may offer a new therapeutic direction [65, 104].

HIF-1α signaling pathway in normoxia vs. Hypoxia

The HIF signaling pathway plays a central role in the hypoxic response. HIF-1α promotes pulmonary vascular remodeling by activating CD36-mediated fatty acid uptake and Parkin/PINK1-dependent mitophagy, driving proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells and myocardial metabolic reprogramming. The discovery of its upstream regulator, PI3K p85α, offers a new intervention target [103]. Meanwhile, HIF-2α is highly expressed in pericytes, leading to enhanced contractility, impaired endothelial-pericyte interaction, and pericyte transition toward smooth muscle-like cells, contributing to distal vascular muscularization. Genetic studies show that specifically inhibiting pericyte HIF-2α significantly improves these pathological changes [57, 58].

Epigenetic regulation and hypoxia work together to worsen disease progression. HIF-2α activation upregulates the long non-coding RNA KMT2E-AS1 and its neighboring gene KMT2E, forming a positive feedback loop: KMT2E-AS1 stabilizes KMT2E protein, increasing H3K4me3 histone modification, which in turn activates HIF-2α itself and other pro-proliferative genes. This drives enhanced glycolysis and abnormal proliferation in pulmonary arterial endothelial cells [57]. A single nucleotide variant (SNV rs73184087, G allele) within the KMT2E gene intron strengthens HIF-2α binding and is associated with increased PAH risk [57].

Environmental factors significantly interact with hypoxia. Amphetamine or methamphetamine abuse activates protein phosphatase PP2A, which inhibits Akt signaling and enhances Sirtuin1-mediated HIF-1α deacetylation and degradation. This disrupts adaptive metabolic reprogramming under hypoxia (e.g., suppresses PDK1 expression and cytochrome c oxidase switching) [105]. These processes lead to mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS accumulation, and caspase-3 activation, synergistically worsening DNA damage and vascular remodeling in pulmonary endothelial cells under hypoxic conditions. This helps explain the molecular mechanisms through which substance abuse promotes PAH development [105].

Other mechanisms

The pathogenesis of PAH involves multiple emerging pathological pathways that collectively drive vascular remodeling and right heart dysfunction. Ferroptosis has been shown to promote lipid peroxidation, complement activation, and inflammatory macrophage infiltration, leading to metabolic dysregulation and immune activation in pulmonary vessels. Its inhibitor Ferrostatin-1 significantly alleviated pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular dysfunction in animal models [106]. Human genetic studies further support that variations in ferroptosis-related genes (e.g., ACSL4 overexpression) are associated with PAH severity [62].

DNA damage represents another core mechanism: chronic inflammation and oxidative stress induce single-/double-strand breaks and oxidative base modifications in pulmonary vascular cells. Defects in DNA damage response (DDR)—such as PARP1 overexpression and downregulation of BRCA1/TopBP1—lead to genomic instability and apoptosis resistance. Mitochondrial DNA, lacking histone protection, is more vulnerable to damage. Genotoxic agents (e.g., alkylating drugs) further exacerbate DNA injury, creating a vicious cycle with metabolic reprogramming, impaired BMPR2 signaling, and sex-specific differences (e.g., greater nitrosative stress susceptibility in females) [59, 61].

Endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndoMT) has been directly demonstrated in occlusive vascular lesions in human PAH. Confocal microscopy revealed α-SMA + mesenchymal-like cells originating from endothelium, co-expressing CD31/VE-cadherin and α-SMA/vimentin, with loss of junctional proteins and upregulation of Twist-1. This process is associated with defective BMPR2 signaling (as confirmed in BMPR2Δ140Ex14 rat models) and TGF-β activation. Rapamycin partially reversed the pathology by inhibiting Twist-1 [60]. Non-coding RNA regulation also plays a key role: long non-coding RNAs (e.g., H19, TYKRIL) regulate vascular cell proliferation and metabolic reprogramming [107]. Reduced circulating miR-29a-3p promotes cardiac exosomal secretion of THBS2, driving the transformation of cardiac fibroblasts into myofibroblasts—an axis with diagnostic and prognostic value [108].

Furthermore, imbalance in the plasminogen system drives PAH progression [109]. PAI-1 deficiency leads to sustained activation of urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA), stimulating the Akt/mTOR and TGF-β/Smad3 pathways and promoting abnormal smooth muscle cell proliferation. PAI-1 knockout mice spontaneously develop PAH, while uPA inhibitors effectively attenuate disease progression in animal models [109].

Although these mechanisms operate independently, they collectively form a complex pathological network in PAH. Targeted interventions such as PARP inhibitors, ferroptosis inhibitors, and EndoMT modulators (e.g., rapamycin) show therapeutic potential. However, challenges including poor species conservation and pathway crosstalk must be addressed to improve clinical translation [60, 106, 110].

Therapeutic advances I: targeting pulmonary vasculopathy to reduce right ventricular afterload

New targeted drugs

Sotatercept

Sotatercept is a novel activin signaling inhibitor(Table 2). As a fusion protein, it targets and modulates the TGF-β superfamily signaling pathway to rebalance pro-proliferative and anti-proliferative signals, thereby directly reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling in PAH [111, 112]. Its mechanism primarily involves antagonizing ligands such as activin and GDFs, inhibiting SMAD2/3 signaling, while promoting BMPRII–SMAD1/5/8 pathway activity. Multiple clinical studies have consistently shown that adding Sotatercept to background therapy significantly improves exercise capacity, hemodynamics, and disease severity in PAH patients [123, 124]. Key Phase II PULSAR and Phase III STELLAR trials demonstrated that after 24 weeks of treatment, patients experienced an average increase of about 40 m in the 6-min walk distance, significant reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance, improved WHO functional class, and an 84% reduction in clinical worsening risk [125, 126]. Notably, in the STELLAR study, the Sotatercept group also showed significant benefits in multiple secondary endpoints including NT-proBNP levels and French risk score [126]. Recent trials in high-risk populations also indicated that the drug significantly reduces a composite endpoint risk of all-cause death, lung transplantation, or PAH-related hospitalization [127].

Mid-term analyses from long-term extension studies (such as the PULSAR open-label extension and SOTERIA trial) show that the benefits of Sotatercept are sustained. With a median follow-up of over two years, it continues to demonstrate a favorable safety and tolerability profile [128, 129]. Common adverse events include epistaxis, telangiectasia, increased hemoglobin, and thrombocytopenia, most of which are mild to moderate. The incidence of serious adverse events was similar to that in the placebo group [124, 130].

Sotatercept received FDA approval in 2024 for the treatment of PAH patients in WHO functional class II–III, making it the first disease-modifying therapy specifically targeting pulmonary vascular remodeling [125]. It not only significantly improves patients’ exercise capacity and hemodynamics but may also extend survival and reduce the need for hospitalization, representing a major breakthrough in PAH treatment [123, 130]. Future research should further clarify its applicability in treatment-naïve patients, those with comorbidities, and non-group 1 pulmonary hypertension, as well as optimize long-term treatment strategies and its role in personalized medicine.

Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors (TKIs)

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) offer a new direction for treating PAH by targeting signaling pathways such as platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) [113]. Imatinib, a representative TKI(Table 2), inhibits the activity of tyrosine kinases including PDGFR and c-KIT, thereby reducing pulmonary vascular remodeling [115]. Multiple clinical studies have shown that adding imatinib to background therapy significantly improves patients’ exercise capacity (with an average increase of 43 m in the 6-min walk distance) and hemodynamics (such as a reduction in pulmonary vascular resistance by approximately 397 dyn·s·cm⁻5 and a decrease in mean pulmonary arterial pressure by about 7 mmHg) [131]. However, it did not show significant advantages in reducing mortality or clinical adverse events, and its long-term efficacy and safety still require further validation [113].

It is important to note that systemic side effects and potential risks of imatinib have limited its clinical use [114]. Recent research has explored optimization strategies, including inhaled administration (such as the dry powder formulation AV-101) to reduce systemic exposure and minimize side effects, as well as using genotyping (e.g., PDGFRB gene polymorphisms) to guide personalized dosing [131, 132]. Additionally, combination therapy shows promise. For example, the mTOR inhibitor rapamycin combined with low-dose imatinib synergistically inhibits pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation and significantly reduces right ventricular hypertrophy and vascular remodeling in animal models [133].

New TKI drugs are also under development. The inhaled inhibitor seralutinib multi-targets PDGFRα/β, CSF1R, and c-KIT. It not only has greater potency than imatinib but also upregulates BMPR2 expression and has now entered clinical studies [115]. The highly selective PDGFR inhibitor WQ-C-401 specifically inhibits the downstream ERK1/2 signaling pathway and has shown beneficial anti-remodeling effects in a monocrotaline-induced PAH rat model [134].

Although TKIs have a clear mechanism targeting pulmonary vascular remodeling and show therapeutic potential, their impact on right ventricular function requires attention. Some studies suggest that imatinib may worsen pressure overload-induced right ventricular dysfunction by activating the JNK/Runx2 pathway, indicating that combining it with a JNK inhibitor could potentially improve its safety profile [135]. Overall, TKIs provide an important therapeutic direction for PAH, but more research is needed to optimize dosing strategies, identify the right patient populations, and explore combination therapies [136].

Interventional device therapies for afterload reduction

Percutaneous Pulmonary Artery Denervation (PADN)

Pulmonary artery denervation (PADN) is an innovative catheter-based treatment that uses radiofrequency ablation to precisely disrupt sympathetic nerve fibers and baroreceptors in the main pulmonary artery and its bifurcation. This procedure aims to block the pathological contraction and remodeling of pulmonary vessels driven by excessive sympathetic activation [137].The rationale for PADN lies in the fact that patients with PAH often have heightened sympathetic nerve activity. This activates α-adrenergic receptors and affects the local renin-angiotensin system, worsening pulmonary vasoconstriction, smooth muscle proliferation, and endothelial dysfunction [138].

Multiple high-quality clinical studies support the effectiveness of PADN. The first randomized, sham-controlled, multicenter trial (PADN-CFDA trial) included 128 PAH patients who were not on targeted drugs. Results showed that, compared to the sham procedure combined with a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor (PDE-5i), PADN plus PDE-5i significantly improved patients’ 6-min walk distance (between-group difference 33.8 m, 95% CI: 16.7–50.9, p < 0.001). It also markedly reduced pulmonary vascular resistance (adjusted difference −1.4 WU), mean pulmonary arterial pressure, and NT-proBNP levels [139, 140]. Long-term follow-up (PADN-5 trial) further confirmed that the PADN group had significantly fewer clinical worsening events—including death, rehospitalization, or transplantation—within 3 years compared to the control group (37.5% vs. 62.0%), with a large reduction in rehospitalization risk [141].

It is worth noting that the benefits of PADN vary by risk level [142]. Subgroup analysis based on REVEAL risk scores showed that intermediate- and high-risk patients benefited most. These patients saw greater improvements in 6-min walk distance, pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR reduced by –6.1 ± 0.6 WU), and NT-proBNP levels compared to low-risk patients. In the low-risk group, the added benefit of PADN was relatively limited. Furthermore, a network meta-analysis (including 65 RCTs) indicated that PADN outperformed several conventional monotherapies (such as macitentan and bosentan) in improving exercise capacity, ranking as the most effective option [142, 143].

PADN is now considered a potential treatment, especially for PAH patients who respond poorly to or cannot tolerate medications. It also offers a new intervention strategy in the era of combination therapy [137, 144]. However, several challenges remain. Most current evidence comes from patients not previously on targeted background therapy, so its added value for those already on standard combination drugs is still unclear. Long-term safety data—such as risks of vascular injury at the ablation site, aneurysm formation, or nerve regrowth—still require validation through larger studies. Finally, the technique lacks standardization in terms of optimal ablation sites, energy delivery, and procedural endpoints [138, 145].

Future research should focus on using multimodal imaging and neural navigation to improve ablation targeting. More randomized trials are needed in patients already on medication. Studies exploring the synergistic effects of PADN with existing drugs (e.g., sotatercept, prostacyclin analogs) will also be important. This will help generate high-quality evidence needed to establish PADN’s role in PAH treatment guidelines [138].

Atrial Septostomy (AS)

Atrial septostomy (AS) is an interventional palliative procedure used for patients with end-stage PAH. It creates an artificial opening in the atrial septum to establish a right-to-left shunt, thereby reducing right ventricular afterload, increasing left heart preload and cardiac output, and improving systemic oxygen delivery [146, 147]. First introduced in the 1960 s, AS remains an important bridging therapy for patients with drug-refractory PAH or those awaiting lung transplantation [146].

Studies have shown that AS can significantly improve hemodynamics and clinical outcomes. After the procedure, patients showed an average 21% increase in cardiac index and a 16% reduction in right atrial pressure. Improvements were also observed in 6-min walk distance (6MWD) and WHO functional class [148, 149]. A retrospective study of 12 patients with idiopathic PAH reported significant immediate reductions in mean pulmonary arterial pressure (mPAP) and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR). The survival rate was 100% at one year and 83.3% at three years [149]. Cardiac MRI follow-up confirmed that AS promotes reverse cardiac remodeling, seen as increased left ventricular volume, decreased right heart chamber size, and improved cardiac output [150].Despite its benefits, AS carries some perioperative risks, with a mortality rate around 7.1%, primarily due to severe hypoxemia [149].

In recent years, techniques for AS have continued to evolve. These include graded balloon dilation, implantation of atrial flow regulators (AFR), and modified approaches combined with radiofrequency ablation. These innovations allow more precise control of shunt size and reduce the risk of spontaneous closure [148]. Currently, AS is mainly used as a bridge to lung transplantation, particularly in high-risk patients with refractory right heart failure despite maximal medical therapy [147]. Future developments may involve creating adjustable shunt devices, exploring early intervention strategies, and combining AS with life support systems such as ECMO to further improve patient outcomes [147].

Therapeutic advances II: direct targeting and support for the right ventricle

Metabolic modulation therapy

Metformin

Metformin, an activator of the AMPK signaling pathway, demonstrates multiple therapeutic potentials in the treatment of PAH [151]. Its mechanisms include activating AMPK, regulating lipid metabolism, inhibiting cell proliferation, and improving right ventricular function [28, 116]. A single-center, open-label Phase II clinical trial showed that idiopathic or heritable PAH patients treated with metformin for 8 weeks experienced good safety profiles. The right ventricular area change fraction significantly improved (from 23 to 26%, p = 0.02), and most patients showed a clear reduction in right ventricular triglyceride content (from 3.2% to 1.6%, p = 0.015). Exploratory metabolomic analysis indicated significant regulation of lipid metabolic pathways [28].

Mechanistic studies suggest that metformin inhibits xCT/Slc7a11 expression, thereby blocking its suppression of AMPKα and subsequently inhibiting mTOR signaling activation—leading to reduced pulmonary vascular remodeling [152]. It also suppresses aromatase activity, lowering estrogen levels in the lungs and circulation, while downregulating CYP1B1 and AhR expression to inhibit pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation. This mechanism may be particularly relevant for treating PAH in women [116]. Additionally, metformin’s modulation of the leptin signaling pathway may be specific to BMPR2 mutations, supporting its use in precision medicine [153].

To enhance efficacy and reduce systemic side effects, novel drug delivery systems such as pulmonary artery-targeted metformin nanocapsules have been developed [151]. This formulation allows lung-specific drug accumulation. In PAH rat models, it significantly improved hemodynamics and ventricular remodeling while reducing the required dose by approximately 80%, without causing liver or kidney injury or lactic acidosis [151]. Studies also indicate that combining metformin with the peripheral CB1 receptor antagonist JD5037 produces synergistic improvements in hemodynamics, fibrosis, and inflammation in rat models, outperforming monotherapy [154].

Through its multi-target effects on metabolism, cell proliferation, and right heart function, metformin holds broad application prospects in PAH treatment. Future research should focus on determining optimal dosing strategies, suitable patient populations, and long-term efficacy to facilitate its clinical translation [28].

Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants

Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants are designed to specifically accumulate in mitochondria and neutralize excess reactive oxygen species, thereby reducing oxidative damage. They represent a promising strategy for treating PAH [79].One such agent, the mitochondria-targeted peptide Elamipretide (ELAM), stabilizes cardiolipin, improves electron transport chain function, and reduces ROS production. Preclinical studies have shown that it can improve cardiac function [117]. A randomized controlled trial in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction demonstrated that a single intravenous infusion of Elamipretide was safe and well-tolerated. The highest dose group showed a significant reduction in ventricular volume, suggesting potential benefits for heart function [117]. In aging models, eight weeks of Elamipretide treatment slowed the progression of frailty and improved cardiac contractility and skeletal muscle endurance. However, transcriptomic and epigenetic analyses did not show significant reversal of biological age, indicating that functional improvements may occur independently of changes in molecular aging markers [155].

Besides Elamipretide, other mitochondria-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ, SkQ1, and SS-31 have also drawn attention [84]. SS-31 (an analog of elamipretide) scavenges mitochondrial ROS, reduces endoplasmic reticulum stress, and improves pulmonary vascular resistance and mean pulmonary arterial pressure [79]. In various animal models of PAH, these compounds have demonstrated improved hemodynamics, reduced right ventricular hypertrophy, and suppressed vascular remodeling [84].

Nevertheless, most research on mitochondria-targeted antioxidants remains at the preclinical stage. Their potential for clinical application still requires further validation through high-quality randomized controlled trials [84]. Future studies should focus on evaluating long-term safety, developing tissue-specific delivery strategies, and exploring combination therapies with other targeted agents (such as those modulating kinetics or metabolism) to facilitate translation into clinical use [78].

Anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic strategies

Anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic strategies are increasingly important in the treatment of PAH. These approaches focus on targeting immune-inflammatory responses and inhibiting pathological vascular and right ventricular remodeling [95, 156].Strong evidence shows that PAH patients have significant infiltration of inflammatory cells—such as T/B lymphocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells, and mast cells—and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines/chemokines including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α, MCP-1, and fractalkine. These mediators activate signaling pathways like JAK-STAT, NF-κB, and TLR, which together promote pulmonary vascular proliferation, endothelial dysfunction, and right ventricular hypertrophy and fibrosis [29, 96, 110].

Biologics that target specific inflammatory pathways have shown promise in preclinical and early clinical studies [157]. The IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra reduced high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP) and improved heart failure symptoms in pilot studies [119–121]. The IL-6 receptor antagonist tocilizumab is currently under evaluation in a Phase II trial (TRANSFORM-UK) for its effects on pulmonary vascular resistance and right ventricular function [118]. Other interventions targeting innate immune components—such as the complement system and neutrophil elastase—as well as inflammasomes are also being explored. Pirfenidone not only inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation and reduces IL-1β and IL-18 production, but also suppresses ERK1/2 phosphorylation and promotes FoxO1 nuclear translocation. In experimental models, it has been shown to improve hemodynamics and reduce pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular fibrosis [158, 159].

In the area of anti-fibrotic and multi-target therapy, the tyrosine kinase inhibitor nintedanib blocks endothelial-mesenchymal transition (EndMT) and smooth muscle cell proliferation by inhibiting PDGFR and FGFR signaling. However, its clinical benefits remain controversial, as some studies suggest it may worsen hemodynamics, requiring careful risk evaluation [160, 161]. Sotatercept, an activin receptor fusion protein that rebalances BMP/SMAD signaling, has now been approved for PAH treatment. It represents a new strategy that targets immunometabolic mechanisms to reverse vascular remodeling [122]. Other agents targeting the TGF-β superfamily, epigenetic regulators (e.g., the BET inhibitor apabetalone), and extracellular matrix metabolism are also under investigation [95].

Although these strategies are mechanistically novel and some drugs have shown significant effects, most are still in preclinical or early clinical stages. Their translation faces several challenges, including variable treatment responses, potential side effects, and a lack of highly effective inhibitors specifically targeting right ventricular inflammatory and fibrotic pathways [110]. Future research should focus on identifying biomarkers to better select patients who may benefit, optimizing combination therapies, and developing new immunomodulators that target the pulmonary vascular and right heart microenvironment. These efforts may lead to more effective and safer treatment options for PAH patients [96].

Mechanical Circulatory Support (MCS)

Percutaneous right ventricular assist devices (pRVADs)

Percutaneous right ventricular assist devices (pRVADs) provide important mechanical circulatory support for patients with acute right ventricular failure or cardiogenic shock that does not respond to drug therapy [162]. Among these, the Impella RP® is the first pRVAD specifically approved by the US FDA for right heart support. After being implanted via the femoral vein, it can provide blood flow up to 4.4 L/min, effectively reducing the load on the right ventricle and buying time for cardiac recovery [163, 164].However, its use has certain limitations. It is not suitable for patients with thrombi in the vena cava, right heart, or pulmonary artery, or those who already have mechanical valves [163]. Registry studies show that although the Impella RP improves hemodynamics, device-related complications are relatively common (approximately 46.7%), and in-hospital mortality remains high (also around 46.7%) [164, 165]. Research also suggests that compared to vasoactive drugs alone—such as norepinephrine combined with milrinone—the Impella RP performs better in maintaining right ventricular workload, but may be less effective than medication strategies in improving cerebral venous oxygen saturation [164].

Another widely used pRVAD is the ProtekDuo® dual-lumen cannula. It can be connected to a centrifugal pump (e.g., TandemHeart) to provide right ventricular support after percutaneous implantation. It can be configured for right ventricular support only, biventricular support, or used together with an oxygenator [31, 166]. A key advantage of this system is that it requires only a single puncture site and is relatively easy to operate. The average support duration ranges from 10 to 58 days.Multiple studies indicate that patients using ProtekDuo have a weaning rate between 24 and 91%, with a 30-day survival rate ranging from 60% to 85.2%. The device shows a relatively low complication rate and good clinical feasibility, making it particularly suitable for patients who develop right ventricular failure after left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation [167, 168].

In summary, pRVADs offer crucial hemodynamic support for critically ill patients with right heart failure. The Impella RP and ProtekDuo each have distinct technical features and suitable clinical scenarios (Table 3). However, their application still faces challenges such as relatively high complication risks and uncertain long-term survival benefits [166]. Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO)

Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (ECMO) serves as a critical life-support measure for patients with PAH, especially those experiencing acute right heart failure or refractory hypoxemia [169]. Depending on the support goals and circuit configuration, ECMO is primarily divided into veno-venous (VV-ECMO) and veno-arterial (VA-ECMO) modes [170].VV-ECMO is suitable for PAH patients with severe hypoxemia but relatively preserved cardiac function. Its advantages include relatively low anticoagulation requirements and reduced risks of thrombosis and bleeding. It can serve as an effective bridge to lung recovery, particularly in patients with complications such as diffuse alveolar hemorrhage or those ineligible for transplantation [170].

VA-ECMO is mainly used for patients with severe cardiogenic shock or biventricular failure, and is often employed as a bridge to lung transplantation. International expert consensus recommends VA-ECMO as a key bridging strategy prior to transplant in PAH patients [171]. In cases of severe right ventricular dysfunction, awake ECMO cannulation may be considered [171]. Continuous ECMO support during and after surgery, combined with invasive hemodynamic monitoring and ultrasound evaluation, helps stabilize circulation, promote right heart recovery, and allow time for cardiac remodeling post-transplant [33]. Some studies indicate that prolonged central VA-ECMO support (median 4 days) along with delayed chest closure after lung transplantation, although associated with bleeding risks (e.g., around 45% required evacuation of hemothorax), significantly reduces postoperative circulatory failure and leads to acceptable short-term survival [172].

In the context of acute right heart failure due to PAH decompensation, ECMO and right ventricular assist devices together form the final support options for refractory cases. Although ECMO has significantly improved survival in high-risk patients, complications such as bleeding, thrombosis, and infection remain common, and overall outcomes are still suboptimal [173]. Currently, there are no unified guidelines for ECMO use in PAH, and decisions must be individualized [171]. Future efforts should focus on optimizing patient selection criteria, refining anticoagulation and support strategies, and exploring combined use with other circulatory assist devices to further improve outcomes in critically ill PAH patients.

Future directions

Emerging frontier therapies

Gene therapy

Gene therapy represents a cutting-edge approach for treating PAH, with the goal of reversing pulmonary vascular remodeling and right ventricular dysfunction at the molecular level by correcting the expression or function of disease-causing genes [174] (Fig. 5). Its core mechanisms involve restoring critical signaling pathways such as BMPR2-SMAD [175], regulating ion and metabolic homeostasis—for instance through SERCA2a overexpression to improve calcium handling [176]—and suppressing abnormal proliferation and inflammatory responses, such as by reducing ceramide levels or interfering with senescence-associated pathways [177] (fig. 6).

Extensive research has focused on several genetic targets [178]. BMPR2 mutations or downregulation are strongly associated with heritable and idiopathic forms of PAH [178]. Studies have demonstrated that delivering the BMPR2 gene via adenoviral or adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors can reactivate SMAD signaling, inhibit smooth muscle proliferation, and significantly reduce pulmonary arterial pressure, right ventricular hypertrophy, and vascular remodeling in animal models of chronic hypoxia or monocrotaline-induced PAH [178, 179]. Similarly, reduced expression of SERCA2a, which plays a key role in calcium regulation and vascular tone, is commonly observed in PAH patients [180]. AAV1-mediated delivery of SERCA2a—administered via tracheal nebulization in MCT rats—has been shown to lower pulmonary artery pressure, reverse vascular remodeling, and improve right heart function, mediated through inhibition of NFAT/STAT3 signaling and enhanced eNOS activity [180, 181]. Another promising target is acid ceramidase (AC); gene therapy aimed at reducing lung ceramide levels has been shown to attenuate inflammation and cellular senescence, leading to improved hemodynamics and reduced right ventricular hypertrophy in preclinical models [177].

The success of gene therapy heavily depends on the choice of delivery vector and strategy. Recombinant AAV vectors—particularly serotypes such as AAV1, A5, and AAV9—are commonly used due to their low immunogenicity, sustained expression, and high tropism for lung tissue [180, 182]. They can be delivered via intravenous injection or inhalation for localized transduction [180]. Adenoviral vectors offer high transduction efficiency but are associated with stronger immune responses, limiting their use primarily to preclinical studies [180]. Lentiviral vectors are often employed in ex vivo settings, such as engineering endothelial progenitor cells to overexpress BMPR2 before transplantation [180]. Non-viral systems—including lipid nanoparticles, polymer-based carriers, and exosomes—offer improved safety profiles and can deliver siRNA targeting genes such as miR-145 or TRPC1, as well as plasmid DNA, though their transduction efficiency remains a challenge [174]. Emerging strategies also involve CRISPR-Cas9-based gene editing and epigenetic modulators, for example using SIN3a to reduce BMPR2 promoter methylation or engineering exosomes to deliver regulatory miRNAs [183].

Despite promising preclinical results, clinical translation of gene therapy faces several obstacles, including immune reactions, pre-existing neutralizing antibodies, limited target specificity, and unresolved long-term safety concerns [182, 184]. Early-phase clinical trials such as SERCA-LVAD—which evaluated AAV1/SERCA2a in heart failure patients—confirmed feasibility but did not demonstrate significant functional benefits [176]. Gene therapy for PAH remains in its early stages, with most studies confined to animal models [185]. Future efforts should prioritize the development of lung-specific promoters such as Tie2, optimizing nano-delivery systems for improved efficiency, exploring multi-target gene regulation, and advancing individualized gene editing strategies [186, 187]. In summary, gene therapy offers a promising path toward modifying the course of PAH rather than merely managing symptoms, but its broad clinical application will require further vector optimization and validation through large-scale trials.

Cell therapy

Cell therapy is an emerging treatment strategy for PAH that primarily involves stem cell transplantation or exosome-based cell-free approaches to target and improve pulmonary vascular remodeling and right heart function [188]. Its core mechanisms include multi-pathway interventions: repairing damaged endothelial function, such as by upregulating eNOS expression and nitric oxide (NO) production to improve vasodilation [189]; inhibiting abnormal proliferation of pulmonary arterial smooth muscle cells (PASMCs), for instance through regulation of the STAT3-BMPR2 signaling pathway [189]; modulating metabolic reprogramming to correct mitochondrial dysfunction and the Warburg effect; and exerting anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects.

Commonly used cell types include mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) [190]. MSCs can be obtained from bone marrow, adipose tissue, or umbilical cord blood, with umbilical cord-derived MSCs showing particularly strong potential in improving right ventricular function and reducing inflammation [188]. EPCs directly contribute to vascular endothelial repair; clinical trials have shown that EPC transplantation can increase the six-minute walk distance by 48.2 m in patients with idiopathic PAH and significantly improve hemodynamic parameters [189]. Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) provide a new platform for disease modeling and personalized therapy. They can be differentiated into functional endothelial cells for studying mechanisms and exploring treatments for heritable PAH (e.g., BMPR2 mutations) [191].

Exosomes, which are key mediators of the stem cell paracrine effects, have increasingly attracted considerable interest in recent years. [192]. They carry bioactive molecules such as miRNAs (e.g., miR-196b, miR-204), mRNAs, and proteins, which can inhibit PASMC proliferation, promote endothelial repair, and significantly reduce pulmonary arterial pressure and right ventricular hypertrophy in animal models [189, 193]. For example, iPSC-derived exosomes (iPSC-Exo) can target HIF-1α and Runx2, downregulating proteins such as cyclinD1, PCNA, and MMP-9, thereby reversing vascular remodeling [194]. Engineered exosomes display targeted peptides (e.g., CARSKNKDC) or functional proteins like Apelin-13 [195]. This capability enables highly precise delivery to diseased lung tissues and significantly improves therapeutic efficacy [195].

Although preclinical results are promising, the clinical translation of cell therapy still faces multiple challenges. These include heterogeneity in cell sources, low survival rate after transplantation, potential tumorigenic risks, and difficulties in large-scale standardized production of exosomes [188, 189, 191]. Additionally, EPCs derived from hypertensive patients often show functional impairment, highlighting the need for careful cell source selection [189]. Future research should prioritize developing efficient delivery strategies and optimizing engineering approaches, such as the use of targeting peptides and smart carriers. Larger clinical trials also need to be promoted to confirm long-term safety and efficacy [188]. This will help pave the way for the broader clinical application of cell therapy in PAH management. Personalized medicine

Personalized medicine is increasingly becoming an important direction in the treatment of PAH. It aims to develop precise treatment strategies based on a patient’s genetic background, molecular phenotype, and clinical characteristics, moving beyond the limitations of the traditional one-size-fits-all approach [26]. The pathogenesis of PAH is complex, involving multiple pathological processes such as BMPR2 gene mutations, immune and inflammatory abnormalities, metabolic reprogramming, and epigenetic changes. These mechanisms offer potential targets for personalized interventions [87, 95, 156]. For example, patients with dysregulated BMPR2 signaling may benefit from novel drugs like sotatercept, which rebalances pro- and anti-proliferative signals to reverse vascular remodeling [196]. Those with significant inflammatory activation may respond better to IL-6 or IL-1 pathway inhibitors [156].

The implementation of personalized medicine currently relies on tools such as multi-parameter risk stratification, biomarker testing, and genotyping. By integrating clinical indicators, imaging, hemodynamic parameters, and circulating molecular markers (such as NT-proBNP, inflammatory factors, and metabolites), patients can be risk-stratified and their treatment response dynamically evaluated [87, 197]. At the same time, the development of targeted therapies for specific mutations (e.g., BMPR2, ACVRL1) or epigenetic profiles offers more tailored treatment options for different patient subgroups [95].

The future of personalized medicine will require further integration of multi-omics data to develop prognostic models and identify novel biomarkers for improved accuracy. In addition, the use of non-invasive monitoring technologies such as CardioMEMS may enable real-time assessment of treatment response and dose adjustment [95]. Although most personalized strategies are still in the research stage and face challenges in clinical translation, cost-effectiveness, and standardization, they represent a major shift from symptom management to disease modification—with the ultimate goal of improving long-term outcomes and quality of life for PAH patients [87, 95].

Combination therapy and multidisciplinary management

The future of PAH treatment is shifting toward a more personalized approach, combining multi-target therapies and multidisciplinary management. With growing understanding of PAH mechanisms and the development of new drugs, patients can expect more precise and adaptable treatments at different stages of the disease. For instance, a patient might receive Sotatercept to rebalance BMPR2 signaling and reverse pulmonary vascular remodeling [198], along with metformin to enhance right heart energy metabolism and reduce myocardial fibrosis [28]. During episodes of acute failure, temporary support from a percutaneous right ventricular assist device (pRVAD) could help stabilize blood circulation and allow time for medications to work [172].

Central to this precision medicine model is the ongoing involvement of a multidisciplinary team (MDT), which includes heart failure specialists, interventional pulmonologists, respiratory physicians, critical care experts, cardiac surgeons, rehab therapists, and specialized nurses [199]. The MDT holds regular consultations to design treatment plans, monitor progress using imaging, hemodynamics, and biomarkers, and adjust strategies as needed—ensuring continuous and comprehensive patient care [200].

Although this integrated strategy shows great promise, it still faces challenges such as potential drug interactions, determining the best timing and sequence for treatments, high costs, and the need for smooth team collaboration [201]. Moving forward, more real-world studies are needed to refine effective treatment combinations. The use of artificial intelligence in clinical decision-support systems could help standardize and spread this approach—ultimately improving long-term outcomes and quality of life for people with PAH.

Conclusion

The pathophysiology of right heart failure (RHF) caused by pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is highly complex. It involves a dynamic process of abnormal remodeling and dysfunction affecting both the pulmonary vessels and the right ventricle across multiple levels and pathways [156]. At the molecular and cellular level, this process includes genetic mutations (such as BMPR2) [202], epigenetic dysregulation (DNA methylation/miRNA) [59], metabolic reprogramming (enhanced glycolysis, mitochondrial dysfunction) [88], chronic inflammation and immune infiltration (e.g., IL-6, macrophage activation) [91], oxidative stress, and ion imbalance [62]. These mechanisms together lead to pulmonary vasoconstriction, vascular wall thickening, in situ thrombosis, and loss of microvessels, eventually causing the right ventricle to progress from compensatory hypertrophy to dysfunctional dilation and fibrosis [203].

Current PAH treatment has gradually shifted from traditional vasodilation (targeting endothelin, NO, and prostacyclin pathways) toward a multi-modal strategy aimed at modifying the disease [204]. Emerging drugs such as Sotatercept (which remodels blood vessels by regulating BMP/TGF-β signaling) [205], tyrosine kinase inhibitors (e.g., imatinib, seralutinib) [133], metabolic modulators (e.g., metformin, DCA) [116], mitochondrial-targeted antioxidants (e.g., elamipretide), and anti-inflammatory agents (tocilizumab, anakinra) [120, 206]have shown promise in preclinical and clinical studies for improving hemodynamics and slowing right heart decline. Additionally, innovative approaches like gene therapy (e.g., AAV-delivered SERCA2a or BMPR2) [175, 176], cell-based therapy (MSCs, EPCs), and exosome-mediated treatments [188] offer new ways to precisely target right heart dysfunction. For support in advanced stages, percutaneous right ventricular assist devices (pRVADs, e.g., Impella RP®, ProtekDuo®) and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) serve as vital life-support and bridge-to-transplant options [162].

However, treating PAH-RHF still faces multiple challenges. Many novel therapies are still in early research stages, with unclear long-term safety, variable effectiveness, and optimal usage strategies [118]. There is also a lack of highly selective drugs targeting right heart-specific mechanisms [204]. Translating molecular discoveries into clinical benefits remains inefficient, with significant individual variation. Mechanical support devices still carry risks of complications and uncertain long-term survival benefits [170].

Future progress will require efforts in several key areas: deeper research into the molecular mechanisms and heterogeneity of RHF, especially the interplay between metabolism, immunity, and fibrosis, as well as organelle communication and epigenetic regulation [207]; careful translation and rigorous validation of new treatments from bench to bedside, using better disease models and improved trial design [174]; developing non-invasive biomarkers and multi-omics tools for precise subtyping and dynamic risk stratification [150]; integrating drugs, interventions, devices, and rehabilitation through multidisciplinary teams (MDTs) to build a holistic management model focusing on both heart and lung [201]; and actively exploring combination therapies and next-generation technologies such as CRISPR/Cas9-based gene editing [49].

In summary, the management of PAH-induced RHF has entered a new phase defined by targeted mechanisms, precision intervention, and comprehensive care. Only through cross-disciplinary collaboration, deeper integration of basic and clinical research, and thoughtful adoption of innovative strategies can we truly alter the course of this severe disease—moving from symptom management toward disease modification and ultimately improved long-term outcomes and quality of life.
