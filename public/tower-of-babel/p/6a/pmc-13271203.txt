Advances in multiplex precision genome editing in eukaryotic and prokaryotic systems
Authors: Devin A Golla, Chunxiao Sun, Logan Haugh, Naomi Straub, Xue Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiplex genome editing (MGE) enables coordinated modification of multiple genomic loci and is foundational for engineering complex biological traits. Traditional CRISPR-Cas nuclease-based strategies rely on DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), which limit precision and pose scaling challenges for incorporating simultaneous edits across different loci. Recent advances in genome editing technologies that operate without generating DSBs have expanded the accuracy and feasibility of multiplexed genomic manipulation. This review focuses on emerging strategies for precise MGE, including base editing, prime editing, and related genome rewriting platforms. We highlight key engineering principles that impact the success of scalable multiplexing, including the choice of editing platform, edit size, and guide RNA architecture, and discuss applications across mammalian, plant, fungal, and bacterial systems. Together, these technologies establish MGE as a versatile framework for precise multigene control in biotechnology and agriculture.

Introduction

The ability to precisely manipulate genomic information has revolutionized modern biology, enabling major advances in biomedical research, biotechnology, and agriculture [1–3]. Over the past decade, programmable nucleases, most notably CRISPR-Cas systems (clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats [CRISPR], alongside CRISPR-associated [Cas] proteins), have transformed genome editing from a technically demanding, low-throughput endeavor into a versatile and widely accessible platform technology [4–6]. CRISPR technologies thus far have enabled systematic interrogation of gene function, engineering of cellular phenotypes, and programmable control of complex biological systems [7,8]. A central frontier in this field is multiplex genome editing (MGE), which aims to introduce simultaneous modifications at multiple genomic loci within a single cell. MGE is emerging as an essential toolkit for dissecting polygenic traits, reconstructing multigene regulatory networks, engineering intricate metabolic pathways, and generating sophisticated disease models [5,9–11]. Through coordinated genetic perturbations, multiplexed editing approaches can achieve more nuanced, combinatorial regulation over complex biological systems compared to single-locus approaches, and can more closely approximate how traits and/or biochemical pathways are tuned by naturally occurring gene regulatory networks [12,13]. The conceptual foundation for MGE emerged early in the development of genome engineering, following the discovery of the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 (SpCas9) nuclease, when researchers demonstrated that multiple guide RNAs in the same cell could direct a single effector protein to distinct genomic targets [5]. However, broader applications of SpCas9-based multiplex editing have been limited by several intrinsic constraints, including strict protospacer adjacent motif (PAM) requirements, reliance on DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs), and dependence on endogenous DNA repair pathways that are often inefficient or error-prone [14–16]. These limitations pose substantial challenges for applications that demand high precision, predictable outcomes, or extensive combinatorial editing across many loci [17–19].

To address these challenges and expand the functional scope of MGE, a diverse suite of next-generation genome editing technologies has emerged beyond SpCas9 [17,20]. These approaches pair engineered programmable targeted nucleases or nickases with a range of biochemical effectors to improve editing precision, broaden targeting flexibility, and, critically, enable multiplexed genetic modifications without introducing DSBs. Major innovations supporting this development include base editing (BE), which facilitates single-base conversion via localized nucleotide deamination [21,22], and prime editing (PE), which utilizes reverse transcription to incorporate edits from a provided guide RNA (gRNA) template into a defined genomic site [23]. Together, these platforms support programmable nucleotide substitutions as well as small insertions and deletions with minimal genomic disruption. In parallel, integrase-linked systems and retron-based platforms can facilitate scarless DNA integration, large-scale genome rewriting, and continuous in vivo diversification (Figure 1a) [24,25]. Collectively, these advances have transformed MGE from a direct extension of single-locus editing into a modular and versatile toolkit for precise installation of defined genomic edits across a broad range of organisms (Table 1).

In this review, we summarize recent progress in precise MGE technologies that operate independently of DNA DSBs, with emphasis on molecular mechanisms, multiplexing strategies, guide RNA architectures, and emerging applications across eukaryotic and prokaryotic systems (Figure 1b).

Multiplex base editing

Multiplex base editing (MBE) enables the simultaneous introduction of precise nucleotide substitutions, most commonly C-to-T or A-to-G transitions, across multiple genomic loci without inducing DSBs, making it particularly attractive for applications requiring predictable editing outcomes and reduced genotoxic stress. By co-delivering a single base editor with multiple gRNAs, MBE has been successfully implemented across a wide range of biological systems, including mammalian cells, plants, fungi, and bacteria.

In mammalian systems, many high-priority areas of therapeutic development, such as engineering allogeneic chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells, benefit from the high precision and efficiency offered by MBE. In a recent study, the BE4 system [26], delivered as a ribonucleoprotein in complex with multiple co-delivered gRNAs, was shown to precisely introduce stop codons at multiple loci that simultaneously knock out the PDCD1, TRAC, and B2M genes in T cells equipped with a CD19 CAR. Such a strategy substantially reduced deleterious DSB-induced translocations associated with traditional Cas9-mediated editing strategies, demonstrating the potential of BEs to improve the safety profile of widely deployable cell therapies [27]. MBE has also been applied as a tool for the selection and enrichment of successfully edited cells in therapeutic contexts. Upon verifying that adenine base editors (ABE) can edit a splice site in the CD33 gene to introduce exon skipping and eliminate a surface-exposed epitope recognized by CD33-targeted therapies, researchers demonstrated simultaneous editing of CD33 and HBG in human hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) through coelectroporation of single gRNAs (sgRNAs) targeting each locus. Treatment with the CD33-targeted agent gemtuzumab ozogamicin resulted in selective resistance of multiplex edited cells, leading to enrichment of edited HSPCs both in vitro and in vivo in xenograft mouse models [28]. Beyond this, MBE has also enabled more complex synthetic biology strategies to address questions of cell selection and conditional survival. Chen et al. used cytosine base editors (CBE) to convert ‘TAG’ stop codons into ‘TAA’ codons across essential genes and isoforms, while concurrently removing cellular components that link the ‘TAG’ codon to transcription termination [29]. This strategy, known as ‘recoding’, enables viral resistance and biocontainment by coupling cell survival to the availability of synthetic amino acids [30]. Moreover, MBE has accelerated the generation of mammalian disease models, drastically shortening their development timeline. For example, triplex CBE- or ABE-mediated editing was achieved in the blastomeres of cynomolgus monkey embryos, bypassing the need for a five-to-six-year breeding cycle to obtain animal models carrying multiple single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) via genetic crossing [31].

Co-delivery of multiple gRNAs with a single BE enables multiplex editing in many cases, but in in vivo applications where delivery cargo size is constrained or where uniform editing outcomes across cell populations are required, recent strategies have focused on compact gRNA array architectures that are processed intracellularly into functional gRNAs after delivery. One approach exploits the intrinsic ability of the nuclease Cas12a to process multiple gRNAs from a single transcript; such Cas12a-derived BEs have been shown to simultaneously edit up to 15 target sites in HEK293 and HeLa cells [32]. In contrast, Cas9-based BEs lack endogenous guide RNA processing capabilities, necessitating alternative strategies for multiplexing. Yuan and Gao developed the drive-and-process (‘DAP’) array, in which individual gRNAs are interspaced by transfer RNA (tRNA) motifs and expressed as a single transcript containing multiple tRNA-gRNA units [33]. These tRNA motifs are efficiently cleaved by conserved endogenous tRNA processing enzymes RNase P and RNase Z, releasing functional guide RNAs within human cells and enabling simultaneous MBE or MPE while minimizing the need for additional promoter sequences (Figure 2a). Related polycistronic tRNA-gRNA expression systems [34] have recently been shown to achieve efficient multiplex BE in other mammalian systems, including porcine fetal fibroblasts [35].

Importantly, the utility of MBE extends well beyond mammalian cell contexts, as it has become a powerful approach for plant genome engineering, synthetic biology, and metabolic pathway optimization. In plants, advances in BE engineering, including the development of improved TadA deaminase variants, have enabled highly efficient multiplex ABE, facilitating the simultaneous modification of multiple agronomically important loci [36]. Cas12a-based BEs further expand this capability by leveraging the intrinsic RNase activity of Cas12a to process guide RNA arrays, enabling efficient MBE in crops such as rice using compact, single-transcript guide architectures [37]. In microbial and fungal systems, MBE has emerged as an effective tool for synthetic biology and metabolic engineering. In Bacillus subtilis, MBE of ribosome-binding sites has been used to quantitatively tune gene expression across entire metabolic pathways, redirect carbon flux, and substantially increase lycopene production without disrupting coding sequences [38]. Similarly, in filamentous fungi, MBE has facilitated combinatorial perturbation of regulatory elements, supporting systematic activation of cryptic biosynthetic gene clusters and accelerating natural product discovery [39]. By targeting multiple epigenetic or transcriptional regulators simultaneously, these approaches reveal complex regulatory interactions that are difficult to resolve using single-locus perturbations.

Additional layers of multiplex control have been achieved through the use of orthogonal RNA scaffolds and other aptamer-protein interaction modules, enabling the simultaneous deployment of distinct BE activities or the integration of BE with other genome perturbation modalities. Recent studies have demonstrated the use of orthogonal RNA aptamer and coat protein pairs to distinguish gRNAs assigned to either ABE or CBE, allowing simultaneous operation of both systems within the same cell [40]. Other approaches have combined aptamer-fused and aptamer-free gRNAs to enable MBE alongside Cas9-mediated transgene insertion [41]. Temporal and spatial control has also been introduced by expressing Cas12-based BE under heat-inducible promoters, enabling localized or time-controlled MBE in response to ultrasound stimulation [42]. Scaffold-level orthogonality provides an additional dimension for multiplex genome control. Systems such as SWISS, which combine Cas9 nickases with engineered orthogonal CRISPR RNA scaffolds, illustrate how guide RNA architecture alone can expand multiplexing capacity in plant systems without reliance on DSBs [43]. Collectively, these advances illustrate how MBE has evolved from a simple co-delivery of single-locus base editing machinery into a flexible and modular platform for coordinated and tunable genome manipulation. Through continued integration of protein engineering, RNA architecture, and gene expression control, MBE is steadily expanding the design space for precise genome engineering across diverse biological systems.

Multiplex prime editing and related systems

Prime editing provides additional flexibility to incorporate small, desired edits that are not readily accessible with base editors, positioning it as a powerful tool for interrogating the function of specific genetic variants. Recent works have shown that cells engineered to stably express PE machinery, when combined with large pooled libraries of prime editing guide RNAs (pegRNAs), can be used for high-throughput screening of variant effects [44,45]. These efforts have been further enabled by self-targeting PE ‘sensor’ libraries, in which an engineered pegRNA (epegRNA) is linked to a ‘surrogate’ targeted sequence embedded within the same construct [46]. In this configuration, the relative abundance of a given pegRNA-sensor can serve as a quantitative readout of the functional impact of the encoded edit, enabling systematic comparison across large collections of single- or multi-nucleotide variants (Figure 2b). Reported libraries include designs that introduce premature stop codons or splicing alterations across the genome [44], as well as focused libraries targeting cancer-related genes TP53 [46], SMARCB1, or MLH1 [45]. Extension of these screening strategies to additional genes and to genomic regions with poorly characterized functions is expected to yield new insights into the molecular mechanisms that govern gene regulation and disease.

Beyond screening applications, multiplex prime editing (MPE) has been increasingly adopted for precision genome engineering in crops. In rice, modular pegRNA assembly strategies have enabled simultaneous prime editing of several agronomically important genes, while surrogate PE systems have simplified pegRNA design and improved editing efficiency across several loci [47,48]. Efficient MPE has also been demonstrated in hexaploid wheat, highlighting the feasibility of DSB-independent multiplex precision editing even in highly complex polyploid genomes [49]. These advances show the promise of MPE as a transformative tool for crop improvement, where coordinated modification of multiple traits is often required to achieve economically important outcomes like increased yield or disease resistance.

Similar to MBE, many practical and therapeutic applications of MPE necessitate that edited cells acquire uniform combinations of edits in a single perturbation, rather than through random delivery of individual gRNAs from a pool. To address this, recent studies have used high-capacity delivery systems such as baculovirus-derived vectors to package PE machinery together with multiple pegRNAs under defined promoters, achieving efficient MPE in human cell lines [50]. However, baculovirus-mediated gene delivery still faces challenges, including incomplete characterization of in vivo performance, motivating the development of MPE systems compatible with adeno-associated virus or lentiviral vectors. Such cargo-size-limited systems rely on compact gRNA array architectures, such as Csy4-based arrays or tRNA-based DAP arrays, in order to achieve MPE using multiple pegRNAs while remaining within viral packaging limits [33,51]. Related strategies use array motifs such as Csy4, tRNA, or hammerhead ribozyme to express a pegRNA with its associated nicking guide RNA from a single promoter, followed by intracellular processing to generate functional RNA components after delivery [52].

In parallel to the engineering of multi-gRNA constructs for MPE, advancements in other aspects of the PE machinery continue to increase the efficiency of MPE and expand its functional capabilities. Han et al. utilized EBNA1/oriP motifs derived from the Epstein-Barr virus to prolong intracellular expression of PE within the cell and to increase local DNA methylation at targeted sites, thereby suppressing DNA mismatch repair pathways. Together, these effects enhanced PE efficiency and facilitated 3-loci MPE using a Csy4-based gRNA array [53]. Yuan et al. incorporated MS2 stem loops into truncated sgRNAs to recruit transcriptional activators from the synergistic activation mediator (SAM) system to Cas9-based PE. This strategy enabled simultaneous PE and targeted gene activation using a single tRNA-based gRNA array [54]. Looking forward, further expansion of MPE systems, including the use of longer gRNA arrays or additional auxiliary genome-perturbing effectors, will require coordinated engineering of both PE proteins and pegRNA architectures to ensure robust performance across diverse cellular contexts.

Mechanistically distinct but conceptually related to PE, retron-mediated MGE provides an alternative route to DSB-independent precision editing. By coupling retron-mediated single-stranded DNA donor integration with CRISPR-based targeting, this approach enables simultaneous introduction of multiple edits, including substitutions, insertions, and deletions, and supports continuous in vivo diversification. As a result, retron-mediated systems offer a promising platform for rapid evolutionary engineering in bacterial systems [55]. Although current implementations remain limited in eukaryotic contexts, retron-based platforms highlight an expanded design space for multiplex precision editing beyond canonical Cas9-based PE approaches.

Multiplex genome rewriting beyond CRISPR-based systems

Beyond nucleotide-level precision editing enabled by base and prime editors, an expanding class of genome engineering technologies enables multiplex ‘genome rewriting’ through programmable DNA recombination and integration, without reliance on CRISPR-based systems. These enzymatic systems operate at a higher level of genomic organization by catalyzing large targeted insertions, deletions, inversions, or cassette exchanges (which have remained a challenge for Cas9-based editors). This functionality is achieved by exploiting the intrinsic directionality and specificity of naturally occurring recombinases and integrases. Importantly, their orthogonality and modularity make these systems particularly well-suited for scalable multiplex genome manipulation across diverse organisms.

Site-specific recombinases such as Cre, Flp, and φC31 have long been used as tools for conditional genome manipulation [56]. Early applications, however, were limited by a lack of orthogonal target sites and by cross-reactivity between recombination events. Advances in directed evolution and rational protein engineering have substantially expanded this toolkit. In a foundational study, multiple recombinases with distinct target specificities were engineered and validated in animal genomes, demonstrating the feasibility of parallel and independent recombination reactions within a single genome [57]. This work established the conceptual basis for multiplex recombinase-mediated genome engineering by showing that orthogonality can be designed at the level of enzyme and target site pairs. Subsequent efforts have further improved recombinase specificity and performance. For example, systematic functional characterization of Bxb1 recombinase variants revealed tunable activity and enhanced selectivity toward engineered attachment sites in mammalian cells. Following this, use of orthogonal Bxb1 site pairs enabled predictable multiplex insertion or rearrangement of genomic payloads, extending recombinase-mediated genome rewriting beyond simple binary genetic switches [58]. More recently, engineered variants of LoxP recombinase with reduced cross-reactivity have enabled highly multiplexable recombination in both prokaryotic and eukaryotic hosts [59]. These systems maintain high recombination efficiency while supporting multiple independent recombination events within a single genome. Collectively, recombinase-based genome rewriting platforms provide a powerful alternative to CRISPR-based multiplex editing, particularly for applications that require large-scale genomic rearrangements or repeated cycles of genome rewriting.

Recombinase systems have also been adapted for programmable control of genome architecture rather than static sequence modification. A recently developed eukaryotic multiplexed site-specific inversion system enables simultaneous and reversible inversion of multiple genomic segments, providing a dynamic means of rewiring regulatory and metabolic networks. Applied to metabolic engineering, this approach allows combinatorial exploration of pathway configurations by toggling gene orientation and regulatory context without introducing permanent DNA damage [60]. This capacity for reversible and multiplex structural genome editing highlights a unique strength of recombinase-based systems that is difficult to replicate using base or prime editing alone.

Bridging CRISPR-mediated targeting with recombinase chemistry, RNA-guided integrases represent a distinct class of enzymes capable of multiplex genome rewriting. CRISPR-associated transposase and integrase systems couple RNA-guided DNA recognition with site-specific integration of large DNA cargos, enabling efficient MGE without DSB formation [61]. Demonstrations of these systems in bacteria have shown simultaneous and high-efficiency insertion of multiple genetic elements at defined genomic locations (Figure 2c), establishing a programmable route to large-scale genome rewriting that is orthogonal to CRISPR-Cas9-mediated editing. Although these systems are currently best characterized in prokaryotic contexts, RNA-guided integrases offer a compelling blueprint for future eukaryotic genome engineering platforms. Their ability to integrate sizable DNA fragments in a multiplexed and directionally controlled manner complements the fine-scale precision of base and prime editing, and defines a distinct tier of MGE that enables structural and architectural control over genomes rather than nucleotide substitution alone. Compared with BE or PE, these systems are less suited for subtle sequence changes, but excel at predictable, orthogonal, and extensible manipulation of large genomic elements. As engineering strategies continue to improve the specificity, orthogonality, and inducibility of these effectors, genome rewriting technologies are likely to play an increasingly central role in applications such as synthetic chromosome design, metabolic pathway optimization, and long-term cellular memory encoding.

Conclusions, challenges, and future prospects

As a result of these important developments over the past decade, precise MGE technologies have now been established as reliable and flexible approaches for coordinated multi-gene modification across diverse biological systems. Proof-of-concept studies in mammals, plants, fungi, and bacteria have collectively demonstrated that MGE can achieve predictable editing outcomes while overcoming many of the limitations associated with traditional CRISPR-based strategies. Despite this progress, substantial challenges continue to limit broader adoption across organisms, tissues, and application areas. Even when MGE machinery performs efficiently at well-characterized loci, extension to new genomic sites requires systematic design and empirical evaluation of optimized gRNAs from large combinatorial design spaces. Each guide must be validated individually before integration into multiplex configurations. Continued development of high-throughput guide RNA optimization methods, together with improved computational tools to minimize guide interference and competition, will be critical for enabling wider application of MGE in therapeutic and agricultural contexts.

In parallel, efficient delivery of MGE machinery to desired cells and tissues remains a major barrier to broad adoption, particularly in plant systems, which often contain complex polyploid genomes as well as cellular structures that impede genetic transformation. Advancements in CRISPR delivery technologies across plants and other organisms have been reviewed elsewhere [62–65], detailing emerging approaches that expand species compatibility, improve cell viability following delivery, enhance safety profiles, and address cargo size constraints, all of which may be useful in future MGE development. As emerging delivery strategies continue to be validated and refined, MGE is expected to enable an expanding range of applications across diverse tissues and species.

The conceptual scope of what cellular behavior MGE can be used to perturb is also undergoing a notable shift. Whereas early efforts focused primarily on combinatorial gene disruption or correction of pathogenic variants, recent advances in base editing and prime editing have highlighted the potential of MGE to achieve multiplexed and quantitative gene expression modulation. Targeting regulatory elements, untranslated regions, epigenetic regulators, and translational control sequences enables graded and predictable tuning of gene expression, metabolic flux, and regulatory network behavior. Such strategies are particularly effective for metabolic engineering, natural product discovery, and crop improvement, where subtle and coordinated perturbations across multiple loci often outperform binary genetic interventions that rely on simple activation or inactivation [39,66,67].

Finally, MGE now encompasses an expanding and increasingly diverse repertoire of programmable genomic effectors. While extensively engineered platforms such as Cas9 continue to provide a foundation for genome manipulation, newer effectors and non-CRISPR systems, particularly the recombinase- and integrase-based technologies, are broadening the scope of multiplex editing through increased orthogonality, reduced PAM requirements, and support for larger or more complex genomic modifications. Continued diversification and optimization of these effectors will offer greater flexibility in the scale and content of introduceable edits and in their effects on gene regulation, enabling more precise genome engineering strategies and yielding more sophisticated models of genetic function.

Together, these converging advances position MGE as a central technology for interrogating and manipulating biological complexity. Continued progress across protein engineering, genome biology, delivery science, and computational biodesign is rapidly expanding the capabilities of precise MGE. As these fields increasingly intersect, MGE is expected to drive substantial innovation in biomedical research, agriculture, and biotechnology in the coming years.
