The Subspine Sweep Technique for Hip Anterior Inferior Iliac Spine Extra-Articular Impingement
Authors: Megan E Flynn, Isabella A Wallace, Etan P Sugarman, Ady H Kahana-Rojkind, Benjamin G Domb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtra-articular impingement has been reported alongside femoroacetabular impingement syndrome as a source of hip symptomatology, arising specifically from the anterior inferior iliac spine—also known as the “subspine.” Advancements in arthroscopic surgical techniques have enabled surgeons to address these extra-articular impingement sources in a minimally invasive manner. Previous evidence has shown improvement in patient-reported outcomes, a low risk of postoperative complications, and a decreased need for revision surgery. We present an arthroscopic surgical technique for achieving intra- and extra-articular subspine decompression that is reproducible. We hope this will lead to improved clinical results in patients with subspine impingement who undergo arthroscopic subspine decompression.

Femoroacetabular impingement (FAI) syndrome is classically described as pathologic contact between the femoral neck’s cam deformity and the acetabular-sided pincer deformity and is a common cause of nonarthritic hip pain.1 Extra-articular impingement has also been reported as a source of symptoms, specifically arising from the anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS)—also known as the “subspine”—as well as the greater trochanter, lesser trochanter, and ischium.2,3

Advancements in arthroscopic surgical techniques have enabled surgeons to address these extra-articular impingement sources in a minimally invasive manner, with 2 meta-analyses reporting improvement in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) and a low risk of postoperative complications or need for revision surgery.1,2 In fact, in a propensity-matched cohort study, competitive athletes with FAI syndrome and subspine impingement who underwent primary hip arthroscopy and subspine decompression showed favorable outcomes with high return-to-sport rates at minimum 2-year follow-up.4

We present an arthroscopic surgical technique for achieving optimal intracapsular and extracapsular decompression of a large AIIS (i.e., subspine) while maintaining the attachment of the rectus femoris using fluoroscopic guidance to confirm accuracy and ultimately improve postoperative patient outcomes. Surgical Technique

Patient Preparation, Positioning, Portal Placement, and Capsulotomy

Video 1 explains the intracapsular and extracapsular subspine decompression in detail.

General anesthesia and full muscle relaxation of the operative extremity are essential for manipulation and accurate subspine resection. The patient is placed in the supine position on a postless traction table (Supine Hip Positioning System; Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) with a traction pad for assistance. The bed is manipulated to approximately 10° to 15° of Trendelenburg. Once the patient is secured, manual traction is applied to the nonoperative leg, followed by the operative leg. With a 70° arthroscope (Arthrex, Naples, FL), the hip joint is accessed via the 12-o’clock portal,5 mid-anterior portal, and distal anterolateral accessory portal, in addition to the posterolateral portal if needed for intra-articular labral work.6,7 An interportal capsulotomy is performed between the mid-anterior and 12-o’clock portals.

Diagnostic Arthroscopy and Central-Compartment Management

Although beyond the scope of this technique article, the presence of other abnormalities commonly encountered with FAI—including labral tears, loose bodies, ligamentum teres tears, and acetabular cartilage damage—should be systematically identified, classified, and treated with grading schemes and treatment algorithms described previously.8,9

Intracapsular Subspine Decompression

Using a bird’s-eye view to evaluate the acetabulum from the 12-o’clock portal, the surgeon places a traction stitch in the proximal capsule. Then, while performing the acetabuloplasty, the surgeon elevates the capsule using a 5.5-mm round burr (Dyonics; Smith & Nephew), taking care to avoid disrupting the capsule, using the safety edge of the burr against the capsule itself (Fig 1).

Arthroscopic bird’s-eye view of intracapsular resection: left hip, viewing from 12-o’clock portal, with burr in mid-anterior portal.

Extracapsular Subspine Decompression

After central-compartment work, the capsular traction stitch is removed. The surgeon maneuvers above the capsule and then carries out dissection using a 4.5-mm curved shaver (Dyonics) superficial to the capsule, working between the capsule and indirect head of the rectus femoris. Care is taken to preserve the muscle fibers themselves and establish a plane between the layers. A soft spot can then be identified between the indirect and direct heads of the rectus femoris. This can be further confirmed using fluoroscopy (Fig 2).

Fluoroscopic image of extracapsular localization of subspine in left hip. The image is obtained with the C-arm coming from the contralateral side, perpendicular to the patient, with a 30° tilt to attain a false-profile view.

A Coblation wand (Ambient; Smith & Nephew) is used to skeletonize the extra-articular portion of the AIIS and join the extracapsular dissection to the intracapsular decompression (Fig 3). With the use of fluoroscopy for guidance, a burr is used to complete the proximal, extra-articular decompression of the AIIS (Fig 4).

(A) Arthroscopic image of left hip viewing from 12-o’clock portal with wand in mid-anterior portal: extracapsular subspine prior to resection, with wand indicating level of prior intracapsular resection. (B) Arthroscopic image of completed subspine resection and decompression.

Final fluoroscopic image of left hip using 30° tilt to obtain false-profile view, showing completed resection of subspine.

Capsular Management

If joint instability is present, as indicated by preoperative and intraoperative criteria,10,11 capsular plication is performed. If the joint is stable, capsulorrhaphy is performed. The capsule may be left open in select cases.

Discussion

The goal of arthroscopic subspine decompression is to correct the anatomy and reduce extra-articular sources of impingement. There is a paucity of literature describing patient outcomes solely attributed to the management of subspine decompression because it is a procedure typically undertaken in conjunction with treatment of FAI, labral tears, and other intra-articular pathology. A systematic review and meta-analysis performed by Schaver et al.2 included 10 different studies to evaluate the outcomes of patients who had undergone arthroscopic subspine decompression at the same time as intra-articular procedures. A total of 547 patients were identified, including 620 hips. PROs including the modified Harris Hip Score and Hip Outcome Score—both the Activities of Daily Living and Sports-Specific subscores—improved significantly; the risk of postoperative complications was 1.1%, and the risk of revision surgery was 1%. Furthermore, a cohort study of 30 athletes with subspine impingement performed by Jimenez et al.,4 published in 2022, revealed significant improvement in all recorded PROs and a high rate of return to sports (88.5%), with achievement of the minimal clinically important difference in the Hip Outcome Score–Sports-Specific subscale at a high rate (80%) and a low rate of revision surgery (6.7%), as well as no increase in adverse events. These studies indicate that decompression of the AIIS is safe and effective.2,12

The described technique for achieving intra- and extra-articular subspine decompression provides a reproducible framework, minimizing the complications of over- or under-resection. This will likely lead to improved clinical results in patients with subspine impingement who undergo arthroscopic subspine decompression.
