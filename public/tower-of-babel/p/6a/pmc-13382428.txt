Availability and public reimbursement of early breast cancer care across European expert centres: a PORTRAIT from an EUSOMA initiative
Authors: Eduard-Alexandru Bonci, Rosa Di Micco, Lorenza Marotti, Marisa Miraldo, Isabel T Rubio, Giuseppe Curigliano, Maria-Joao Cardoso, the PORTRAIT Study Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCross-country disparities in access to guideline-recommended early breast cancer care persist across Europe. PORTRAIT is a clinician-reported, cross-sectional access study mapping availability and state reimbursement of key services across the early breast cancer pathway.MethodsA 49-item survey covering diagnostics, pathology and genomics, systemic therapy, surgery, radiotherapy, and supportive care was emailed from June to September 2024 to multidisciplinary teams at one expert breast centre in 42 European countries. Respondents provided a consensus perspective on service availability and reimbursement. Descriptive proportions with full, partial, or no access were calculated.ResultsThirty-nine countries responded (93%). Core diagnostics were widely available, but gaps persisted: MRI-guided biopsy was absent in 29% of countries and vacuum-assisted biopsy was fully reimbursed in 66%. Genomic assays were unavailable in 14% and not fully reimbursed in 35%. Public funding gaps were reported for PARP inhibitors (33%) and CDK4/6 inhibitors (26%). Immediate implant-based reconstruction and biological meshes lacked reimbursement in 21% and 33%, respectively. Despite broad availability of hypofractionated radiotherapy, 38% of countries still used per-fraction reimbursement. Psycho-oncology was limited in 35%, fertility preservation in 46%, and patient-reported outcome measures absent in 43%.ConclusionBasic services are nearly universal, but gaps persist in advanced imaging, molecular testing, targeted therapies, radiotherapy reimbursement, reconstructive/oncoplastic surgery, and survivorship care. PORTRAIT identifies practical targets for policy audit, reimbursement alignment, and resource stewardship. Findings reflect clinician perspectives from expert centres, potentially representing best specialist care rather than average national care, and are not population-representative estimates or direct measures of patient outcomes.Keywords: Breast cancer, Patient pathway, Accessibility, Disparities, Healthcare equity

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer among women worldwide, with more than 2·3 million new cases recorded annually. Over recent decades, advances in screening, systemic therapies, and multidisciplinary care have led to substantial improvements in overall survival with a decline in breast cancer mortality by over 40% in many high-income countries (HICs) [1,2]. However, these gains have not been shared equally, and significant outcome variations persist across regions. Persistent survival gaps both between and within regions indicate that biology alone cannot explain outcome variation; structural features of health systems play a decisive role.

Europe exemplifies this paradox of scientific success but still unacceptable inequities - five year relative survival now exceeds 90% in Northern Europe but remains almost 10% lower in parts of Central and Eastern Europe [[3], [4], [5]]. Cross-country differences in breast-cancer outcomes contravenes the European Union (EU) commitment to equitable access to high-quality healthcare under Article 168 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the EU [6]. Insufficient healthcare funding in some settings, unequal implementation of screening programs, and variability in the availability of modern therapeutics all contribute to these inequalities [7].

Healthcare is funded differently across the European continent – from centralised national health services to mixed public/private insurance models – and higher expenditure does not automatically guarantee better results, mainly due to variations in clinical practice, resource use, fragmented care provision and variations in access to diagnostics and care [[8], [9], [10]]. In some countries, reimbursement policies and budget constraints delay adopting guideline-recommended treatments, forcing patients to either forego state-of-the-art care or incur out-of-pocket costs [[11], [12], [13]]. Such structural disparities mean that quality of breast cancer care and associated health outcomes are shaped not only by tumour biology and disease stage, but also broader determinants of health including health systems organizational aspects, patient's socio-economic background and place of residence and by other intra-national inequities across regions.

To move from description to remedy, robust cross-country comparisons are indispensable. Mapping where care pathways diverge illuminates modifiable system failures and informs European level initiatives aimed at levelling up standards. Existing work, such as the European Society for Medical Oncology's (ESMO) global drug access survey [14], underscores the value of such exercises but has concentrated largely on pharmaceuticals. A comprehensive appraisal of the full breast cancer continuum - diagnostics, surgery, radiotherapy (RT), systemic therapy and supportive care - remains lacking [15].

The PORTRAIT study (euroPean differences in accessibility and cOst of the early bReasT canceR pAtIent paThway) responds to this evidence gap. While this study focuses on structural components of care such as availability and reimbursement, equity in healthcare is a multidimensional concept. Future analyses could be strengthened by adopting established frameworks such as PROGRESS-Plus [16], the Whitehead typology [17], or the WHO UHC cube [18], which account for social determinants, access, utilisation, financial protection, and outcomes. PORTRAIT provides a partial but critical view of this broader landscape. The intended contribution of PORTRAIT is therefore pragmatic rather than causal. It does not seek to explain survival differences, measure patient-level utilisation, or replace formal health-system audits. Instead, it addresses an earlier step in the equity pathway: whether key components of guideline-concordant early breast cancer care are reported by clinicians as available and publicly reimbursed across countries. This structural access layer is directly relevant to policy because unavailable or unreimbursed services cannot be implemented equitably, even when they are supported by clinical guidelines.

Initiated by the European Society of Breast Cancer Specialists (EUSOMA), PORTRAIT is compiling clinician reported data from 39 European countries on two structural pillars: the reported availability of early breast cancer care services and the extent to which these services are financed by public systems. This paper presents a cross-sectional clinician-perspective analysis of these structural dimensions across Europe's diverse public healthcare systems. Its aim is to identify access and reimbursement gaps that may inform policy audit, quality improvement, and future benchmarking under Europe's Beating Cancer Plan [19], the European Cancer Inequalities Registry [20], the EU Health Technology Assessment framework [21], the European Commission Initiative on Breast Cancer (ECIBC) [22], and other European quality-assurance initiatives [11,[23], [24], [25], [26]].

Methods

Questionnaire development

EUSOMA experts developed a comprehensive 49-item survey to collect information across six key domains of early breast cancer management: diagnosis, pathology and molecular testing, medical oncology, surgery, RT, and supportive care. Each item included 9 standardised questions and a free-text field for additional comments. Respondents for each key domain were also asked to provide basic personal, demographic, and professional details.

The survey addressed the availability of services in public and private hospitals, insurance coverage, public reimbursement practices, and the estimated medium cost to the care setting of selected interventions and therapies. This first version did not collect patient-level utilisation, waiting times, geographic proximity, travel burden, co-payment levels, out-of-pocket expenditure, or outcome data. It also did not capture several pathway-quality metrics relevant to breast-conserving treatment, including margin-assessment processes, margin-positivity or re-excision rates, and specimen mammography/radiography, nor did it assess peripheral support systems for patients receiving systemic therapy.

Respondents were asked to report availability within both public and private sectors. “Available” was defined as presence of the service within the healthcare system, regardless of whether it was accessible through public institutions or only via private providers. In our survey, we distinguish between services accessible in public settings (with or without reimbursement) and those only accessible privately. This paper provides the first analysis of the survey, focusing on public healthcare systems - the primary drivers of cross-country inequities. The interaction with private practice is more intricate and therefore falls outside the scope of the present study.

The complete list of survey items and standardised questions can be found in Appendix 1. Operational definitions were standardised across items. “Available” indicated that the service was reported to exist within the country, irrespective of whether it was delivered through public or private providers. “Available in the public healthcare system” indicated that eligible patients could access the service through public-sector pathways; “partially available” indicated restriction to selected centres, regions, indications, or capacity-limited pathways. Reimbursement was classified as full, partial, none, or unknown. “Full state reimbursement” indicated that the service was reported to be publicly covered for eligible indications without routine mandatory patient payment; “partial reimbursement” indicated co-payments, reimbursement caps, restricted indications, dependence on supplementary insurance, or other incomplete public coverage. Where respondents could not verify an item after clarification, the response was coded as “Unknown”.

Study design and participants

This study employed a cross-sectional, clinician-reported, expert-centre survey design. A purposive sampling strategy was used to obtain a multidisciplinary clinician perspective from breast cancer centres recognised for leadership in delivering or coordinating multidisciplinary breast cancer care. The study was designed to map reported structural access and reimbursement, not to generate population-representative estimates of all breast cancer providers within each country. The primary sampling frame was the EUSOMA Breast Centres Network [27] which brings together institutions certified against evidence-based quality standards across the breast cancer care continuum. In countries without an EUSOMA centre or where additional perspectives were sought, a snowball sampling approach was used. National and regional experts affiliated with professional societies (e.g., oncology, radiology, surgery) and clinical networks were asked to nominate prominent breast cancer centres or clinical teams actively involved in delivering or coordinating breast cancer care at a national level.

Centres were selected based on the following inclusion criteria: i) provision of multidisciplinary breast cancer care, with established practice in at least four of the six core domains (radiology, pathology, surgery, medical oncology, radiation oncology, supportive care); ii) recognised leadership role within national or regional breast cancer service delivery or guideline implementation; iii) capacity to convene a multidisciplinary team able to provide expert insight into clinical practice, pathways of care, and - where possible - system-level factors such as reimbursement and access barriers. Centres that did not meet these criteria or that primarily provided single-discipline services were excluded.

Each centre was asked to convene a multidisciplinary team representing the six core domains of breast cancer care: radiology, pathology, surgery, medical oncology, radiation oncology, and supportive care/specialist nursing. Teams were instructed to respond collaboratively and to provide a consensus view whenever possible. Respondent verification was operational rather than registry-based: respondents were identified through the EUSOMA network or professional clinical networks, were affiliated with the nominated centre, and provided specialty, professional role, years of clinical practice, institution type, and dedicated breast cancer practice status. Domain responses were reviewed for consistency with the respondent's clinical field and with the centre's nominated role. Formal credential verification against national professional registers, national registries, or reimbursement databases was not performed.

A total of 39 European countries were included (Fig. 1) in the final sample. Each country contributed a single consolidated response, completed by a multidisciplinary team from one expert breast cancer centre. This approach prioritised informed clinician perspectives from leading institutions, but it does not constitute a population-representative sample of all breast cancer providers within each country. In some settings, the selected centre may represent the best available specialist care rather than average national care, and the design cannot capture within-country variation across regions, rural/peripheral facilities, or non-specialist hospitals.

The survey included questions addressing both clinical service availability and system-level factors such as reimbursement status. While clinicians were best placed to report on practice-level availability and barriers encountered during care delivery, we recognise that some questions - particularly regarding health system financing and reimbursement pathways - extend beyond everyday clinical practice. Respondents were asked to answer based on their institutional experience and, where relevant, to consult administrative colleagues or rely on their leadership roles in national cancer networks. However, the data reflect clinician-reported perceptions of access and reimbursement, rather than formal health system policy data. This practical perspective is essential to understanding how structural barriers affect care delivery at the point of care, but does not replace system-wide analyses of financing and policy frameworks.

Data collection

Data collection was conducted by EUSOMA, through a web-based survey platform (SurveyMonkey), from June through September 2024. Following submission, the data were anonymised and compiled into a central database.

Given the nature of this survey, formal ethical approval was not required. Prior to participation, all respondents were informed about the purpose of the survey and explicitly provided their consent before initiating their responses. All data collection and management were conducted in strict accordance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).

Data validation and quality assurance

Upon completion, responses were reviewed by two researchers (E.-A.B and R.D.M.). The process began with automated checks for missing answers, out-of-range values, and logical inconsistencies. The researchers then conducted a manual review using a standardized checklist to confirm completeness and internal coherence. In cases of ambiguous entries (for example, instances where innovative drugs were unavailable in HICs), respondents were contacted for clarification. If clarification could not be obtained, the item was coded as “Unknown”. No data were altered without electronic written confirmation from the original respondent. This process was critical to maintaining the integrity of the dataset.

These procedures were intended to improve completeness and internal coherence of clinician-reported responses. They should not be interpreted as independent validation against national registries, policy databases, reimbursement catalogues, or health-technology assessment decisions.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise survey responses, including service availability and state reimbursement. Availability and reimbursement scores were calculated as described in Fig. 2 legend and summarised by country and domain. No inferential or causal analyses were undertaken in this first report because the study used one expert-centre response per country and was designed as an initial structural access map. Exploratory secondary analyses linking PORTRAIT domain scores to external country-level indicators, such as income group, Human Development Index, health expenditure, oncology workforce, or radiotherapy infrastructure, are planned for future work. Heatmaps were created in RStudio (version 2024.12.1) using R standard visualisation tools.

Diagnostic

Thirty-eight countries completed the diagnostic section of the survey, which consisted of eleven items.

Digital mammography is available in all countries but not fully state-reimbursed in 9 (23·7%), while breast tomosynthesis is limited or unavailable in 9 (23·7%) countries. Contrast-enhanced mammography is absent in 6 countries (15·8%) and not fully reimbursed by the state in 12 (31·6%). Core biopsy is accessible in all surveyed countries but faces partial state reimbursement in 10 (26·3%). Vacuum-assisted core biopsy (VAB) is not available in 1 country, is limited in the public healthcare of 10 (26·3%), and lacks full state reimbursement in 12 (31·6%). Although MRI is available in all 38 countries - albeit limited in 5 (13·2%) countries’ public sector and partially state-reimbursed in 13 (34·2%) - MRI-guided biopsy is unavailable in 11 (28·9%) and further restricted in the public healthcare system of 7 (18·4%) countries. Tumoural clipping or marking techniques are not performed in 3 (7·9%) countries. CT scans are universally accessible; however, bone scans are unavailable in the public sector of 1 country, and PET/CT scans are limited or unavailable in 5 (13·2%) national public healthcare systems and not fully state-reimbursed in 10 (26·3%).

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 1 to Table 11.

Pathology and molecular testing

Thirty-seven countries completed the pathology and molecular testing section of the survey, which consisted of three items.

Immunohistochemistry is universally accessible, albeit not fully state-reimbursed in 12 (32·4%) countries. Genomic testing is unavailable in 5 (13·5%) countries and not fully state-reimbursed 13 (35·1%). Likewise, genetic testing is inaccessible in 2 (5·4%) countries and lacks full state reimbursement in 9 (24·3%).

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 12 to Table 14.

Medical oncology

Thirty-nine countries completed the medical oncology section of the survey, which consisted of nine items.

Chemotherapy dose-dense regimens are unavailable in 1 country, further restricted in another, and not fully state-reimbursed in 4 (10·3%). Granulocyte colony-stimulating factor is universally available but has limited availability in 2 (5·1%) countries. Access to essential oncology medications also varies across countries: trastuzumab is unavailable or limited in 3 (7·7%), pertuzumab is unapproved in 1 and has limited availability in 7 (17·9%) countries, and pembrolizumab is either unavailable or unapproved in 5 (12·8%) and limited in the public sector of another 10 (25·6%). Similarly, CDK 4/6 inhibitors are not approved or unavailable in 5 (12·8%) countries and face limited availability in another 5 (12·8%) national public healthcare systems. In contrast, PARP inhibitors face limitations or unavailability in 13 (33·3%) countries, including 6 (15·4%) where they are unapproved. Bisphosphonates are unavailable or not approved in 3 (7·7%) countries.

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 15 to Table 23.

Surgery

Thirty-six countries completed the surgical section of the survey, which consisted of seventeen items.

Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) and modified radical mastectomy are available in all countries. Radioisotope sentinel lymph node biopsy is unavailable in 2 (5·5%) countries and has limited access in 4 (11·1%). Alternative tracers are completely unavailable in 9 (25%) countries, are restricted in another 9 (25%), and lack full state reimbursement in 9 (25%). Targeted axillary dissection is not offered in the public healthcare systems of 9 (25%) countries.

Oncoplastic breast conservative mammoplasties with contralateral symmetrisation is partially or fully restricted in 6 (16·7%) countries, and even where available, state reimbursement is limited or absent in 10 (27·8%). BCS using chest wall perforator flaps is unavailable in 3 (8·3%) countries and further limited or not provided in the public sector of another 7 (19·4%). Among countries where these procedures are available, state reimbursement for chest wall perforator flaps is limited or absent in 6 (16·7%). Skin-sparing mastectomy with immediate implant-based reconstruction is either unavailable or has limited availability in the public healthcare systems of 5 (13·9%) countries, and lacks full state reimbursement in 8 (22·2%). Skin-sparing mastectomy with pedicled flap-based reconstruction is not available in 2 (5·5%) countries, unavailable or limited in 8 (22·2%) national public healthcare systems, and not fully reimbursed by the state in 6 (16·7%). Skin-sparing mastectomy with free flap-based reconstruction is unavailable in 4 (11·1%) countries, has limited or no availability in the public sector of another 10 (27·8%), and does not receive full state reimbursement in 5 (13·9%).

Tissue expanders are unavailable or limited in 6 (16·7%) countries, and breast implants do not receive full state reimbursement in 10 (27·8%). Additionally, biological and synthetic meshes are unavailable in 8 (22·2%) countries, have limited access in the public sector of another 5 (13·9%), and are not fully covered by the state in 13 (36·1%). Risk-reducing mastectomy, although universally available, is partially or fully restricted in 4 (11·1%) countries and does not have complete state reimbursement in 6 (16·7%). Similarly, contralateral prophylactic mastectomy is available everywhere but only partially in 3 (8·3%) countries, and without state reimbursement in 5 (13·9%). Finally, symmetrisation surgery on the contralateral breast is either unavailable or limited in the public sector of 6 (16·7%) countries and lacks full state reimbursement in 11 (30·5%).

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 24 to Table 40.

Radiation oncology

Thirty-seven countries completed the RT section of the survey, which consisted of four items.

External beam RT is universally available, although reimbursement varies; 14 (37·8%) countries rely on a per-fraction payment (fee-for-service) model. Moderate hypofractionation is offered in 30 (81·1%) countries, while ultra-hypofractionation is available in 23 (62·2%). Intensity-modulated RT (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc RT (VMAT) is unavailable or limited in 3 (8·1%) countries. Partial breast external beam RT (PBI) is absent in 11 (29·7%) countries, limited in one country's public sector, and not fully state-reimbursed in 3 of the 26 countries where it is available. Intraoperative RT remains inaccessible in 23 (62·2%) countries.

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 41 to Table 44.

Supportive care

Thirty-seven countries completed the supportive care section of the survey, which consisted of five items.

Physiotherapy is universally available but limited in 6 (16·2%) countries, and not fully state-reimbursed in 15 (40·5%). Psycho-oncology services are unavailable or limited in 13 (35·1%) countries. Fertility preservation is universally available but inaccessible in 7 (18·9%) countries, limited in 2 (5·4%), and lacks full state coverage in 17 (45·9%). Genetic counselling is unavailable in 4 (10·8%) countries, not provided in the public sector of one, and not fully state-reimbursed in 9 (24·3%). Additionally, patient-reported outcome measures are unavailable in 16 (43·2%) countries, limited in 3 (8·1%) national public healthcare systems, and fully state-reimbursed in only 10 (27%).

All items, corresponding questions and answers are detailed in Appendix 2 – Table 45 to Table 49.

Discussion

PORTRAIT should be interpreted as a clinician-reported structural access audit of early breast cancer care across Europe. Its main contribution is not methodological complexity or causal inference, but the breadth of the care pathway assessed: diagnostics, pathology and molecular testing, systemic therapy, surgery, radiotherapy, and supportive care. The findings identify where clinicians report that guideline-relevant services are unavailable, capacity-limited, or incompletely reimbursed in public systems. This information is useful for policy audit and prioritisation, while recognising that the study does not measure patient-level utilisation, waiting times, affordability, or outcomes.

The results of the questionnaire concerning the public sector illustrate that basic diagnostic tools such as digital mammography, core biopsy, and CT scans are universally available across Europe, indicating a baseline level of diagnostic capability. However, advanced diagnostic techniques such as PET/CT, MRI, MRI-guided biopsy and VAB exhibit marked limitations in public healthcare systems. Although the need for MRI-guided procedures is relatively limited [28], limited access to VAB increases the likelihood of unnecessary surgical interventions, inappropriate management of suspicious breast lesions, and higher overall healthcare expenditures [29]. Our survey indicates that MRI is accessible in 93% and MRI-guided biopsy in 67% of HIC settings, but in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these figures drop to 73% and 18%, respectively. These findings emphasize significant challenges in achieving accurate staging and effective surgical treatment planning, particularly after neoadjuvant therapies, where MRI is considered the gold-standard imaging modality for assessing tumour response and guiding surgery [30,31]. However, the equity objective is not indiscriminate expansion of technology, but reliable access for patients who meet evidence-based indications, alongside stewardship to avoid low-value use.

Pathology and molecular testing services-similarly exhibit uneven distribution. Although immunohistochemistry is broadly accessible, genomic and genetic testing, essential for precision medicine approaches, remain restricted in several countries. Limited access or partial reimbursement for genomic tests, such as Oncotype DX or MammaPrint, can lead to more patients receiving unnecessary chemotherapy, incurring avoidable toxicity and costs [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Additionally, patients awareness is crucial even in HICs with unrestricted access to genomic tests. A recent study by Cardone et al. [36] revealed that 57·8% of the breast cancer patients surveyed were unaware about genomic testing. Key disparities in the availability of innovative and targeted treatments could further contribute to suboptimal treatment. Pertuzumab, pembrolizumab, CDK 4/6 inhibitors, and PARP inhibitors were notably limited in several countries, especially within the public sector. Such limitations potentially deprive patients of level I evidence-based drugs, resulting in lower invasive disease-free survival [37,38] and overall survival [39,40]. From a policy perspective, ensuring broad access to genomic tests and targeted treatments can improve cancer care value – delivering better outcomes, reduce unnecessary treatments, and improve quality of life (QoL) for patients while making more efficient use of healthcare resources. Moreover, resolving the partial reimbursement issues that lead to higher out-of-pocket costs and financial toxicity, particularly for low socioeconomic status patients, is essential for equitable healthcare in Europe.

Regarding surgical settings, although standard procedures are widely available, access to modern oncoplastic surgery and breast reconstruction remains limited. Consequently, not all patients are offered the same range of surgical options when intervention is indicated. Breast reconstruction with implants is the most commonly performed reconstructive [[41], [42], [43]] procedure, and mastectomy followed by reconstruction significantly improves patients' QoL. However, the limited availability and state reimbursement of tissue expanders and breast implants remain a significant indicator of inequity in access to care. As oncoplastic breast surgery often serves as a viable alternative to mastectomy [44], the lack of availability and/or full reimbursement for advanced oncoplastic procedures may lead to a higher number of mastectomies being performed. Furthermore, restricted access to contralateral breast surgery for risk reduction or symmetrisation may negatively impact psychological well-being and aesthetic outcomes, potentially affecting patients' QoL.

RT has undergone significant advancements in the last decade, particularly with the promotion of modern de-escalation regimens [45]. Unfortunately, these developments in RT research have not been reflected in corresponding changes to reimbursement models. The current survey revealed that at least one-third of the countries are still relying on per-fraction payment (fee-for-service) model. Adopting moderately hypofractionated RT could significantly reduce both social and economic burdens. However, the slow adoption of these regimens may be influenced by the financial losses healthcare providers face under a fee-for-service reimbursement model. Thus, per-fraction reimbursement may not be ideal for RT and should be modified [46]. Furthermore, technologies such as IMRT and VMAT are not universally available, thereby limiting the range of treatment options for the individual patient. PBI and intraoperative radiotherapy should be interpreted within local evidence appraisal and guideline-defined indications, rather than as universal requirements. The key equity issue is whether patients have access to appropriate radiotherapy options, including de-escalated regimens and modern planning techniques when indicated, and whether payment systems support rather than obstruct high-value care.

Supportive care services, crucial for holistic patient management, also revealed stark differences. According to PORTRAIT, fertility preservation services, a major concern in young patients with breast cancer, are available in 92% of HICs public healthcare systems but only 36% of LMICs, leaving these patients with reduced fertility due to gonadotoxic treatment with resulting psychosocial distress. Even HICs such as France are facing challenges in enhancing access to fertility preservation services for individuals diagnosed with breast cancer [47,48]. Additionally, the current study revealed that at least one-third of European nations are still facing a shortage or complete lack of publicly accessible psycho-oncology services. A study by Travado et al. [49], published in 2017, under the European Partnership for Action Against Cancer revealed that while 78% of surveyed European countries included psychosocial oncology care in their national cancer control plans, only 37% had specific budgets for it. The lack of consistent access to these services compromises overall patient care quality, potentially leading to increased physical morbidity, psychological distress and financial toxicity.

Data utility for European stakeholders

The PORTRAIT findings are relevant to European stakeholders because they identify structural access gaps that may limit implementation of guideline-concordant care. They should be viewed as complementary to, rather than a substitute for, existing European quality initiatives [11,19,20,[23], [24], [25], [26]]. In particular, the ECIBC provides evidence-based guidance for screening and diagnosis, a European quality assurance scheme for breast cancer services, and quality-related requirements and indicators [22]. PORTRAIT adds a clinician-reported access and reimbursement perspective that may help identify where such quality frameworks are difficult to implement in practice.

The European goal of ensuring access to National Comprehensive Cancer Centres [19] is also relevant, but it should not be interpreted as meaning that one centre per country is sufficient to deliver equitable care. A National Comprehensive Cancer Centre can function as a hub for expertise, quality management, research, education, and referral pathways, but equitable access depends on its integration with regional breast units, peripheral services, screening programmes, pathology/radiology networks, radiotherapy infrastructure, and reimbursement systems. PORTRAIT did not assess the organisation of these networks in each country; therefore, its single-centre-per-country design should be interpreted as an expert-centre perspective, not as a country-level organisational audit.

These findings nevertheless identify practical policy targets: aligning reimbursement with evidence-based indications; reducing payment models that disincentivise de-escalation; strengthening access to molecular testing, modern diagnostics, radiotherapy, oncoplastic/reconstructive surgery, and supportive care; and integrating access indicators into European benchmarking only when they are aligned with established quality-management and guideline frameworks.

However, breast cancer care equity spans beyond the availability of services or their reimbursement status. In health systems research, equity is more broadly defined as the absence of unfair, avoidable differences in healthcare access, utilisation, financial protection, and outcomes across social groups and geographies. While PORTRAIT provides new evidence on the availability and coverage of services, it does not capture other critical dimensions of equity – such as waiting times, patients’ out-of-pocket costs, geographic distribution of facilities within countries, or fragmentation in care pathways. These factors can be significant: for example, even if a therapy is available, long waiting lists or prohibitive co-payments may still prevent timely treatment for some patients. Future work should therefore situate our findings within established equity frameworks to paint a more comprehensive picture of breast cancer care disparities.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it provides a cross-sectional snapshot of clinician-reported access and reimbursement in 2024. Availability, reimbursement rules, and clinical practice may have changed since data collection.

Second, the data were reported by clinicians and multidisciplinary teams rather than extracted from national registries, reimbursement catalogues, health-technology assessment documents, or policy databases. Although responses were reviewed for missing values, internal consistency, and ambiguous entries, and respondents were contacted when clarification was required, no independent external validation was performed. The findings should therefore be interpreted as practice-based clinician perspectives on structural access, not as formal national policy statements.

Third, each country contributed one consolidated response from one expert breast cancer centre. This design enabled broad European coverage and multidisciplinary input but limits national representativeness. Participating centres are likely to be better resourced and more specialised than many peripheral, rural, smaller, or non-specialist providers. Consequently, PORTRAIT may describe the best available specialist care in some countries rather than average national care, and may underrepresent within-country regional disparities.

Fourth, the survey focused on service availability and state reimbursement. It did not collect several equity and quality metrics that are important for breast cancer care, including waiting times from diagnosis to treatment, geographic distance or travel burden, patient-level co-payments and out-of-pocket expenditure, financial toxicity, utilisation rates, treatment completion, clinical outcomes, or patient-reported experience. It also did not assess postoperative margin-assessment processes, specimen mammography/radiography, margin-positivity or re-excision rates, or peripheral support systems for patients receiving systemic therapy.

Fifth, the analysis was descriptive. It did not model associations between healthcare infrastructure, national financing, workforce capacity, or country-level socioeconomic indicators and the observed access gaps. Such analyses would require linkage of validated country-level PORTRAIT scores to external indicators and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than causal.

Despite these limitations, PORTRAIT provides a broad clinician-reported map of structural access and state reimbursement across the early breast cancer pathway. These data can complement formal policy audits, patient-reported studies, registry analyses, and European quality-assurance initiatives by identifying where clinicians perceive practical barriers to implementing guideline-concordant care.

Future directions

Future PORTRAIT phases should broaden both the metrics and the stakeholder base. Additional data collection should include waiting times, geographic access, rural-urban differences, patient-level out-of-pocket expenditure, financial toxicity, treatment utilisation, treatment completion, patient-reported outcomes and experiences, and peripheral support systems for systemic therapy. Surgical quality metrics should include margin-assessment pathways, specimen mammography/radiography where relevant, margin-positivity, and re-excision rates. Triangulation with patient organizations, health-system administrators, payers, national registries, and policy databases will be necessary to move beyond clinician-reported structural access.

Secondary analyses could also examine whether availability and reimbursement scores are associated with country-level indicators such as income group, Human Development Index, health expenditure, public/private financing mix, oncology workforce, radiotherapy infrastructure, or health-technology assessment timelines. Such analyses would be hypothesis-generating and should avoid causal interpretation given the ecological design and small number of country-level observations.

The ultimate goal is to translate evidence into concrete policy action and advocacy by turning PORTRAIT into a recurring Breast-Cancer Equity Index. However, the proposed Breast Cancer Equity Index should be considered a future methodological objective rather than an immediately implementable instrument. A credible index would require pre-specified indicators, transparent weighting, governance, validation against independent data sources, sensitivity analyses, and alignment with ECIBC quality assurance [22], EUSOMA quality standards, and guideline interpretation. Its purpose should be to support quality improvement and policy prioritisation, not to create an unvalidated league table of countries.

Conclusion

PORTRAIT identifies a European patchwork of clinician-reported access and public reimbursement across the early breast cancer pathway. Core diagnostic and surgical services are widely available, but important gaps remain in advanced diagnostics, MRI-guided biopsy, PET/CT access, multigene assay reimbursement, targeted therapies, oncoplastic and reconstructive procedures, modern radiotherapy delivery, and supportive care.

The immediate value of PORTRAIT is as a structural access map for policy audit and quality improvement. Its findings do not provide population-representative national estimates or patient-level outcome data, but they identify where clinicians report practical barriers to implementing guideline-concordant care. Closing these gaps will require reimbursement systems that support evidence-based use, de-escalation where appropriate, and equitable access to high-value services.

Future benchmarking should be aligned with established European quality-management frameworks, including ECIBC and EUSOMA initiatives, and should incorporate patient-level affordability, waiting times, geographic access, utilisation, and outcomes. The proposed Breast Cancer Equity Index may be a useful future tool only if developed with transparent methods, external validation, and clear integration into European breast cancer quality assurance and guideline implementation.
