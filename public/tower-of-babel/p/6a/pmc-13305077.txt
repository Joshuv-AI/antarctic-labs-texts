Exploring Patient Perspectives on Upper Tract Urothelial Cancer: A Qualitative Interview Study
Authors: Dina Filipenko, Michael Acquadro, Ola Ghatnekar, Carl Haakon Samuelsen, Ingolf Griebsch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatient interview data exploring the experiences of the most relevant symptoms and impacts of upper tract urothelial cancer (UTUC) remain scarce. This study aimed to develop a conceptual disease model (CDM) of UTUC grounded in patients’ own experiences of UTUC and to capture disease-specific symptoms and impacts experienced before, during, and after cancer treatment.MethodsA targeted literature review (TLR) informed the qualitative interview guide development. Qualitative concept elicitation interviews were then performed with patients who had UTUC to understand their experiences of the disease and its treatment, focusing on symptoms and impacts. Interview transcripts were thematically analyzed using ATLAS.ti©. The findings from both the TLR and qualitative interviews were synthesized to develop a CDM.ResultsWe interviewed 15 adults with UTUC (mean age, 56 years), including nine with low-grade and six with high-grade UTUC. Patients reported 22 symptoms: 11 pre-treatment and 21 during treatment and/or post-treatment. The most frequently reported pre-treatment symptoms were blood in urine (60%), fatigue/tiredness (53%), and pain (47%). During or post-treatment, the most frequently reported symptoms were fatigue/tiredness (93%), pain (47%), and weight loss (40%). Patients with low- and high-grade UTUC reported comparable numbers of distinct symptoms, both pre- and post-treatment. UTUC and its treatment impacted patients’ emotional well-being (100%), activities of daily life (73%), relationships (73%), social functioning (60%), sleep (60%), and physical functioning (27%), and treatment burden was explicitly mentioned by 13%. The CDM of UTUC was developed, capturing the symptoms and impacts experienced by patients with low- or high-grade UTUC.ConclusionsThis qualitative study identified a broad range of symptoms and impacts of UTUC and its treatment, highlighting the unmet patient needs associated with current UTUC management. Our findings provide a qualitative foundation to inform endpoint and clinical outcome assessment selections in clinical research and to guide patient-centered care strategies for UTUC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40487-026-00423-0.Keywords: Patient experience data, Qualitative interviews, Symptoms, Conceptual disease model, Emotional well-being, UTUC, Disease burden, Health-related quality of life

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Health-related quality of life in patients with upper tract urothelial cancer (UTUC) can be negatively impacted by the disease and its treatment.Patients’ experiences of UTUC, including unmet needs, symptoms, and impacts of the disease and its treatments, are poorly characterized.This qualitative interview study aimed to develop a conceptual disease model of UTUC grounded in patients’ perspectives and to identify key disease-specific symptoms and impacts experienced before, during, and post treatment.What was learned from the study?Fatigue/tiredness, pain, and blood in urine were the most frequently reported and the most bothersome symptoms for patients, and almost twice as many symptoms were reported during or post-UTUC treatment compared to pre-treatment, suggesting a significant burden associated with the current management of the disease.These findings provide a qualitative foundation to inform endpoint and clinical outcome assessment selection in clinical research and to guide patient-centered approaches to UTUC management.

Introduction

Upper tract urothelial cancer (UTUC) is a rare cancer that occurs in the urothelial lining of the renal pelvis or ureters and accounts for 5–10% of all urothelial carcinomas [1]. Its annual incidence in Western countries is approximately two cases per 100,000 people [2] and increases with advancing age [3].

The most common symptoms of UTUC include hematuria and flank pain, with the latter usually caused by a clot or a tumor-related obstruction [1, 2, 4]. More advanced disease is often associated with symptoms such as weight loss, fever, night sweats, anorexia, and coughing up blood [2, 4]. Low-grade (LG) UTUC accounts for approximately 26% of all UTUC cases and is less aggressive than high-grade (HG) UTUC [5]. However, LG UTUC carries a high risk of tumor recurrence and subsequent potential for progression to more aggressive HG disease, which has a worse prognosis and requires more aggressive treatment [2, 6]. The choice of treatment for UTUC varies by the location, grade, and stage of the tumor. For HG UTUC, the current standard of care is radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) [2, 7]. For LG UTUC, kidney-sparing approaches are preferred, as they minimize morbidity while achieving comparable survival outcomes to RNU [2, 6, 7].

There is rather limited evidence regarding the health-related quality of life (HRQoL) of patients with UTUC; however, available data suggest a negative impact of the disease on general health, social functioning, and vitality [8]. Patient-reported outcome (PRO) data from clinical trials showed worsened HRQoL in patients with UTUC after radical surgery or adjuvant chemotherapy, although the worsening was generally temporary [9, 10].

Patient experience data are increasingly incorporated into regulatory and health technology assessment reviews to inform decisions and guide medical product development [11–13]. Patients’ perspectives are particularly important when evaluating the overall value proposition of treatments for rare diseases, such as UTUC, where clinical data are scarce [14, 15]. To our knowledge, only one recent qualitative study has investigated patient experiences of UTUC, with an emphasis on LG UTUC treatments [16]. Further research is needed to characterize patients’ experiences and unmet needs across the UTUC care pathway, from diagnosis through post-treatment. Such data can help highlight the outcomes most important to patients and may inform regulatory decision-making, medical product development, and patient-centered treatment strategies.

In the current study, we sought to address this gap by conducting a targeted literature review (TLR) and qualitative patient interviews to explore disease- and treatment-related symptoms and impacts experienced by patients with UTUC. The study also aimed to develop a conceptual disease model (CDM) of UTUC grounded in patients’ perspectives to inform endpoint and clinical outcome assessment selection in clinical research and to guide patient-centered approaches to UTUC treatment.

Methods

Targeted Literature Review

A TLR was conducted to identify disease symptoms and impacts specific to UTUC or its treatments and PRO measures previously used in the UTUC population. MEDLINE and Embase were searched for articles published between January 13, 2018, and January 5, 2023, using the criteria outlined in Table S1. The titles and abstracts of identified articles were screened by two primary reviewers, followed by a senior reviewer for the final inclusion and exclusion decisions for full-text screening and data extraction. Findings from the literature review informed the development of an initial CDM and the interview guide for subsequent patient interviews.

Interview Sample

Patients were adults (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with UTUC residing in the United States (US), selected through convenience sampling. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are summarized in Table S2. Patients were recruited by a research vendor specializing in patient recruitment, using their databases, physician and patient association referrals, and social media. UTUC diagnoses were confirmed through review of copies or photographs of patients’ health or medication records; tumor prognosis (high or low risk) was self-reported.

Ethical Approval

The study protocol was approved by an institutional review board (Salus IRB Board IRB00013544) before initiation of interviews. Prior to their interviews, all study participants provided their informed consent to report and disseminate their anonymized data. Participants were remunerated upon completion of the interviews.

Interview Procedures

After providing online consent, all patients completed a pre-interview questionnaire on sociodemographic characteristics (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity, highest level of education, and employment status) and medical information (e.g., time since diagnosis, UTUC characteristics such as tumor size, location, type, and prognosis, treatments received, and comorbidities). All interviews were conducted from September 2022 to May 2023 by experienced interviewers trained in qualitative data collection and using semi-structured interview guide developed for this study.

One-on-one web-based interviews lasted approximately 60 min. First, information on UTUC diagnosis, its treatment, and history of other malignancies was solicited. The second part of the interview focused on the symptoms that patients had experienced at diagnosis (pre-treatment) and during or post-treatment for UTUC. Patients were asked open-ended questions to describe the severity, duration, frequency, change over time, and potential triggers of their symptoms, as well as the most bothersome symptoms experienced; no rating scales were used. Patients then discussed the impact of UTUC on their HRQoL, including general health, emotional well-being, daily activities, relationships, and social life. Patients were also asked about the fluctuations and causes (i.e., directly attributable to UTUC or to its treatment) of each impact and the most bothersome impact experienced.

Data Analysis and Quality Control

All interviews were audio-recorded with patients’ permission and transcribed by a professional transcription vendor. Before thematic analysis, a quality review of the transcripts was conducted, and all personally identifiable information was removed. De-identified transcripts were uploaded, coded, and analyzed on an ongoing basis using ATLAS.ti© v22 or later (Scientific Software Development GmbH, Berlin), which facilitates systematic organization and interpretation of qualitative (textual) data and supports identification of patterns, themes, and relationships across datasets. An initial coding dictionary was developed based on the themes and concepts that emerged during the first three interviews and then revised as new themes and concepts emerged from subsequent interviews. To ensure consistency in coding, two researchers independently reviewed and coded the first two interview transcripts, and the coding was compared and reconciled. When inter-coder agreement was achieved, the researchers divided the remaining transcripts between them and completed the coding process.

To assess saturation of concepts, interview transcripts were ordered chronologically, and codes derived from successive transcripts were compared. Saturation was considered achieved when no new concepts emerged from subsequent transcripts [17, 18].

CDM Development

The initial CDM of UTUC, which was informed by the findings from the TLR, was updated based on the qualitative interview findings, including symptoms experienced across treatment stages, as well as impacts of UTUC and its treatment. All concepts included in the CDM represented subjective experiences reported by patients rather than outcomes measured by clinicians and were therefore treated as symptoms or impacts.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics from the pre-interview questionnaire.

Results

Targeted Literature Review

The literature search identified 292 articles (Figure S1). Following abstract screening, 12 articles were selected for full-text review, with 10 deemed eligible for data extraction. These included four quantitative studies, one mixed-method study, and five case studies [8, 19–27]. The sample size of the included studies ranged from 1 to 700 patients, and the study population included patients with LG, HG, or metastatic UTUC and those whose tumor grade was unspecified. A total of 23 concepts were identified from eight studies [19–26], including eight UTUC symptoms, 12 treatment-related adverse events, and three concepts reported as both UTUC symptoms and treatment-related adverse events (Table S3). The most frequently reported concepts included flank/loin pain (reported in n = 6 articles), blood in urine (n = 3), fever (n = 2), and sepsis (n = 2). Impacts of UTUC and its treatment on mental health and daily activities were identified from one study [8].

Patient Characteristics

Fifteen US adult patients (mean [standard deviation] age 56 [7] years) participated in the qualitative interviews, including nine with LG UTUC and six with HG UTUC (Table 1).

Six patients (40%) were male, eight (53%) were female, and one (7%) reported their gender as nonbinary or other. Twelve patients (80%) were white, and three (20%) preferred not to disclose their race. Just over half of patients (53%) had a college degree. Approximately one-third each were retired (33%), unemployed (33%), or living with a disability due to UTUC (27%).

The median time since UTUC diagnosis was 0.7 years (range 0–5) for the eight patients who provided information on this. The most commonly received UTUC treatments and procedures were kidney-sparing surgery/treatment (87%), cystoscopy (73%), and chemotherapy (73%). Some patients had undergone endoscopic ablation (53%), RNU (40%), and immunotherapy (27%).

UTUC Symptoms

In total, patients reported 22 symptoms experienced pre-treatment, during treatment, and/or post-treatment of UTUC, with 19 symptoms (86%) being spontaneously mentioned (Table 2). Fatigue/tiredness (n = 14/15, 93%), pain (n = 10/15, 67%), and blood in urine (n = 9/15, 60%) were the most common symptoms considering both time periods. Notably, these symptoms were mostly reported spontaneously (fatigue [n = 12/14, 86%], pain [n = 9/10, 90%], blood in urine [n = 8/9, 89%]), while weight loss (n = 6/15, 40%), malaise (n = 4/15, 27%), and fever (n = 3/15, 20%) were endorsed only when probed, irrespective of the time period considered.

Symptoms by UTUC Treatment Stage

Patients reported experiencing 11 symptoms pre-treatment, with blood in urine (n = 9/15, 60%), fatigue/tiredness (n = 8/15, 53%), and pain (n = 7/15, 47%) being the most common symptoms (Table S4). Blood in urine prompted eight patients (n = 8/9, 88%) to seek medical help prior to diagnosis, with some describing the color of their urine as “red/scary reddish” (n = 3/9, 33%), “pink” (n = 2/9, 22%), or having an “amber” tint (n = 1/9, 11%). Patients described their pre-treatment fatigue/tiredness using terms such as “tired/tiredness” (n = 7/8, 88%), “energy” levels (n = 4/8, 50%), “feeling fatigued” (n = 2/8, 25%), “drained” (n = 1/8, 13%), “exhausted” (n = 1/8, 13%), and “wiped out” (n = 1/8, 13%). Of those who reported pain, all experienced back pain (n = 7/7, 100%), with single reports of pelvic and abdominal pain (n = 1/7 each, 14%). Pain severity varied from mild to very intense, and five patients (n = 5/7, 71%) mentioned experiencing constant pain for several weeks and seeking medical advice for it. The number of symptoms nearly doubled to 21 during treatment or post-treatment, with fatigue/tiredness (n = 14/15, 93%), pain (n = 7/15, 47%), and weight loss (n = 6/15, 40%) being the most frequently reported symptoms (Table S4). While nine symptoms were present pre-treatment and continued after treatment initiation, the onset of 11 symptoms, including brain fog/concentration, bruising, cough, fever, flu-like symptoms, hair loss, nausea, skin coloring, sweats, weak immune system, and weight gain, was only experienced during treatment or post-treatment (Table 2). Illustrative quotations relating to the symptoms experienced only during treatment or post-treatment are presented in Table S5.

Pre-treatment, blood in urine (n = 6/10, 60%), pain (n = 3/10, 30%), urgent urination (n = 1/10, 10%), frequent urination (n = 1/10, 10%), and fatigue/tiredness (n = 1/10, 10%) were identified as the most bothersome symptoms. During treatment or post-treatment, fatigue/tiredness (n = 4/8, 50%), malaise (n = 2/8, 25%), pain (n = 1/8, 13%), decreased appetite (n = 1/8, 13%), nausea (n = 1/8, 13%), and sweats (n = 1/8, 13%) were reported as the most bothersome symptoms.

Symptoms by Tumor Grade

Patients with LG and HG UTUC experienced similar numbers of symptoms: nine versus seven symptoms pre-treatment and 17 versus 16 symptoms during treatment or post-treatment (Table S4). The most frequently reported pre-treatment symptoms were fatigue/tiredness (n = 6/9, 67%), pain (n = 6/9, 67%), and blood in urine (n = 5/9, 56%) among patients with LG UTUC, while those for patients with HG UTUC were blood in urine (n = 6/9, 67%), physical weakness/stamina/strength (n = 2/6, 33%), and fatigue/tiredness (n = 2/6, 33%).

UTUC Impacts

All 15 patients reported emotional impacts (100%), including anxiety/fear (n = 14/15, 93%), stress/feeling overwhelmed (n = 9/15, 60%), irritability/anger (n = 5/15, 33%), feeling sad/down/depressed (n = 4/15, 27%), and affected confidence (n = 1/15, 7%) (Table 4). Eleven patients (73%) reported impacts on different aspects of daily life, including chores/errands (n = 6/15, 40%), other everyday activities (n = 4/15, 27%), sports (n = 3/15, 20%), and work (n = 1/15, 7%). Eleven patients (73%) reported impacts on relationships, including those with family (n = 8/15, 53%), a partner (n = 4/15, 27%), and friends (n = 3/15, 20%). Patients also reported sleep disturbance (n = 9/15, 60%) and negative social impacts (n = 9/15, 60%), such as altered social engagements (n = 7/15, 47%), affected traveling (n = 6/15, 40%), and a need for shielding (n = 2/15, 13%). Four patients (27%) mentioned physical functioning impacts regarding ability to move, sitting too long, leaning, walking, and/or bending, with one participant reporting that they could not operate, eat, or do anything. Two patients (13%) explicitly mentioned treatment burden. Emotional (n = 5/11, 45%) and social (n = 4/11, 36%) impacts were most frequently reported as the most bothersome. Concept Saturation

Of the 22 symptom concepts elicited in the interviews, 18 (82%) were endorsed in the first eight interviews, and the rest (18%) emerged in the remaining seven interviews: brain fog/concentration, flu-like symptoms, weight gain, and memory loss. The concept of memory loss was mentioned during the 15th interview by one patient (aged 67 years), who attributed the symptom to both UTUC-related anxiety and their age. Since the literature review did not identify any memory symptoms associated with the condition, saturation of symptom concepts was considered reached.

Half of the impact concepts (nine out of 18) were reported during the first interview and 94% (17 out of 18) by the fourth interview. The last impact concept, confidence, was mentioned during the 14th interview. Saturation of impact concepts was achieved as no new impact concepts emerged in the last interview.

A CDM of UTUC was developed based on the findings from the TLR and qualitative interviews. Of the 22 symptom concepts elicited from the interviews, 21 were included in the CDM (Fig. 1). Discussion

Data on the HRQoL of individuals with UTUC, particularly their subjective experiences of the condition and treatment throughout the course of the disease, are limited. Available qualitative interview data in this population primarily focused on UTUC treatment in patients with LG disease [16]. Our study extends current knowledge of UTUC, identifying symptoms and impacts experienced by patients with LG and HG UTUC pre- and post-treatment through in-depth qualitative interviews. To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative interview study of patients with UTUC that aims to develop a CDM of this type of cancer.

In the study, 22 symptoms were reported by patients, considering all UTUC treatment phases. Almost twice as many symptoms were experienced during or post-treatment as were experienced pre-treatment (21 vs. 11 symptoms), suggesting treatment burden associated with current care. The most frequent pre-treatment complaints were blood in urine, fatigue/tiredness, and pain, while those during treatment or post-treatment were fatigue/tiredness, pain, and weight loss. When asked, patients most often indicated blood in urine as their most bothersome symptom experienced pre-treatment, while fatigue/tiredness was perceived as the most bothersome complaint during or post-treatment. Disease burden affected multiple domains of patients’ lives, including emotional well-being, social functioning, daily activities, relationships, sleep, physical functioning, and treatment burden. Several new symptoms and impacts that have not been previously reported in the literature were identified in the study. Additionally, of the 23 concepts regarding UTUC symptoms and treatment-related adverse events identified in the literature, only eight symptom concepts were supported by the interview data, highlighting a critical need for more patient-centered research in this area. The CDM of UTUC, developed based on qualitative data and TLR, demonstrates the unmet needs of this population. Notably, many of the reported symptoms and impacts were endorsed by both LG and HG groups. However, given the sample size, caution should be exercised when comparing these patients.

In the qualitative interview study by Song et al., US patients with LG UTUC reported blood in urine as the most common symptom before diagnosis, and patients who underwent RNU noted resolution of this symptom [16]. Similarly, our study showed that blood in urine was the most frequent symptom experienced pre-treatment. Additionally, blood in urine was more frequently endorsed as a symptom pre-treatment than during treatment or post-treatment, which is likely due to its resolution by the treatment. However, with only six patients having undergone RNU, more research is needed to confirm the finding.

Mental health issues are more prevalent in patients with cancer than in the general population [28], although the prevalence varies by type of cancer [29, 30]. A systematic review of studies on patients with bladder cancers found that the prevalence of psychological distress ranged from 4% to 78% [31]. A cross-sectional study of patients aged ≥ 65 years with UTUC showed a negative impact of UTUC on mental health within the first year and 5–10 years after diagnosis [8]. Consistent with these findings, all patients in our study reported impacts of UTUC and its treatment on their emotional well-being, with 93% experiencing anxiety/fear. Anxiety in patients with cancer often arises from their uncertainty about their prognosis, worry about the future, and fear related to cancer treatment [32]. Our findings further emphasize the importance of providing appropriate psychological support for patients with UTUC.

Further research is warranted to confirm our findings, given a few limitations of the current study. The sample size was not sufficient to make robust comparisons between the LG and HG groups or between subgroups by demographic characteristics, although this sample size is typical for qualitative studies [33], and evidence suggests that concept saturation was reached for the overall population. The generalizability of the results to all UTUC cases may also be limited. The study recruitment utilized a convenience sample in the US, which may not be representative of the broader UTUC population. Patients were younger than the target population affected by UTUC [2, 3]. As older patients often experience multiple comorbidities, their experiences of UTUC symptoms and impacts might be different and not be fully captured in our CDM. However, the influence of comorbidities on symptoms and impacts was likely small in this younger patient sample, and as such, the proposed CDM could be reflective of the isolated effects of UTUC. Additionally, treatments can differ substantially between LG and HG UTUC [2, 7]. Although most patients in our study received chemotherapy, some received other treatment modalities, which may have differing impacts on their HRQoL. Future studies are needed to explore these differences across various UTUC treatments. Moreover, the clinical data in this study were self-reported and may be subject to recall or reporting bias. As the average time since diagnosis exceeded 1 year, the journey of these patients, as well as their ability to accurately recall their experiences, might have been different from that of newly diagnosed patients.

Despite these limitations, our findings offer novel insights into patient experience of UTUC. The qualitative concept elicitation interviews allowed for identification of concepts that may be missing or insufficiently covered in existing HRQoL measures for patients with cancer, resulting in a CDM that is consistent with patients’ perceptions of their UTUC experience. Patient interview data are also explicitly recommended by the US Food and Drug Administration to inform the development of clinical outcome assessments and their incorporation into clinical study endpoints [34, 35]. This study provides a qualitative foundation for selecting endpoints and clinical outcome assessments that are patient-focused, comprehensive, and conceptually aligned with patients’ experiences for future clinical research. The study results also equip clinicians with a deeper understanding of the patient’s experience of the disease, thereby supporting more patient-centered communication, assessment, and care planning.

Conclusions

Our study contributed a subjective perspective on patient experiences of the disease and its treatment that was grounded in qualitative interview data with UTUC patients. The findings of this interview study were used to develop a CDM that can inform endpoint selection for clinical trials, as well as patient-centered approaches to disease management.
