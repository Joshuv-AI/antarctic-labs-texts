Melatonin alleviates oxidative stress induced damage in human iPSCs harboring mtDNA 3243A>G mutation via MAPK pathway
Authors: Zhixin Pu, Peiwen Wang, Mengyao Wang, Yaxin Chen, Yating Zhu, Yuan Jiao, Weiwei Zou, Biao Yu, Huiru Cheng, Yajing Liu, Yunxia Cao, Dongmei Ji, Dan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMitochondrial diseases are a group of serious inherited multisystem disorders caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA and still have faced a significant challenge to therapy due to their complicated genotype–phenotype relationships and diverse clinical manifestations. Human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC) offered novel opportunities for cell-based modeling mitochondrial diseases in a patient-specific level. This study aims to explore possibility to potential strategy against mutation-associated oxidative damage through hiPSCs derived from mitochondrial diseases patients.MethodsA human induced pluripotent stem cell line (mt-hiPSCs) from a patient harboring 70.70% heteroplasmic m.3243A>G mutation was established and exposed to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂). The cell viability, apoptosis level and mitochondrial function were measured through CCK-8, western blot, flow cytometry, RT-qPCR, fluorescence staining and compared to wild-type hiPSCs. Thereafter, the participation of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) pathway in the melatonin-mediated protection against H₂O₂-induced oxidative injury was also evaluated.ResultsUnder prolonged low-dose hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) exposure, mt-hiPSCs showed significantly reduced viability, elevated apoptosis (52.13 vs. 25.62% in wild-type hiPSCs, P < 0.001), and exacerbated mitochondrial dysfunction, including superoxide accumulation and membrane potential depolarization. Melatonin pretreatment effectively mitigated H₂O₂-induced damage, restoring cell viability, reducing lactate dehydrogenase release, and suppressing apoptosis by normalizing BAX/BCL2 ratios and CASPASE-3 activation. Moreover, melatonin preserved mitochondrial fusion dynamics (MFN1) and respiratory chain integrity (COX IV), counteracting H₂O₂-induced abnormalities. Mechanistically, mt-hiPSCs displayed hyperactivation of MAPK signaling (p-p38, p-ERK, p-JNK) under oxidative stress, which was attenuated by melatonin. Consistently, administration of the MAPK inhibitor SB203580 further confirmed that melatonin’s protective effects are closely associated with modulation of MAPK pathway activity in mt-hiPSCs exposed to oxidative stress.ConclusionsThese findings highlight the vulnerability of m.3243A>G mutant cells to oxidative stress and demonstrate melatonin’s therapeutic potential in alleviating mitochondrial dysfunction via MAPK pathway modulation. This study provides a patient-derived model for exploring mitochondrial disorders and identifies melatonin as a promising cytoprotective agent against mutation-associated oxidative damage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-025-04666-y.Keywords: mtDNA, Melatonin, Human induced pluripotent stem cells, Apoptosis, Mitochondrial dysfunction, MAPK signaling pathway

Introduction

Mitochondrial disorders, arising from mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear genes encoding mitochondrial proteins, represent a significant challenge to therapy due to their complex genotype–phenotype relationships and multi-systemic clinical manifestations [1, 2]. Among pathogenic mtDNA variants, the m.3243A>G mutation in the MT-TL1 gene is one of the most common causes of mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes (MELAS), with an estimated global prevalence of 7.8–16.3 in 100,000 individuals [3–5]. This mutation disrupts mitochondrial translation machinery, leading to respiratory chain deficiencies, impaired oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and chronic oxidative stress as hallmarks of mitochondrial dysfunction [6–9]. While cybrid models and patient-derived fibroblasts have provided valuable insights into the metabolic perturbations associated with this mutation [10, 11], these models are limited by artificial heteroplasmy manipulation and inability to fully recapitulate tissue-specific pathophysiology under controlled environmental stressors.

Recent advances in induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology offer unparalleled opportunities for modeling mitochondrial diseases in a patient-specific context [12]. Unlike cybrid models, iPSCs preserve the donor’s genetic background including heteroplasmic mtDNA mutations and can be differentiated into disease-relevant cell types [13, 14]. These capabilities enable systematic exploration of mutation-specific cellular responses to environmental challenges within physiologically relevant conditions. In parallel, melatonin, a pleiotropic indoleamine with potent antioxidant and mitochondria-protective properties, has emerged as a promising candidate for mitigating oxidative damage associated with mitochondrial dysfunction [15–17]. Studies in mesenchymal stem cells and neurons have demonstrated melatonin’s ability to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS), stabilize mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), and inhibit apoptosis pathways [18–20]. However, its therapeutic efficacy against mtDNA mutation-induced mitochondrial dysfunction in human iPSC-based systems remains unexplored. Evidence suggests that mtDNA mutations amplify cellular vulnerability under external stressors by disrupting redox homeostasis and mitochondrial dynamics [10]; however, key molecular mechanisms particularly those involving stress-responsive pathways such as MAPK signaling remain poorly defined in patient-derived models. Furthermore, while melatonin (MT) has shown protective effects across various oxidative injury models, its ability to counteract mutation-specific defects through targeted modulation of key pathways requires further investigation using genetically relevant systems.

Here, we established a heteroplasmic m.3243A>G human iPSCs (mt-hiPSCs) derived from peripheral blood mononuclear cells of a patient. This study aimed to determine whether the m.3243A>G mutation exacerbates cellular vulnerability to H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress; characterize the associated mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis; and elucidate melatonin’s therapeutic mechanisms through integrated analyses of MAPK pathway activity, mitochondrial dynamics, and redox homeostasis. Our findings validate hiPSCs as a physiologically relevant platform for studying mitochondrial disorders while identifying melatonin as a potent modulator of mutation-associated oxidative damage that provides mechanistic insights for future targeted intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Generation of mt-hiPSCs and cell culture

All experimental procedures involving human samples were thoroughly reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China; Approval No. PJ20220128). Prior to enrollment in the study, written informed consent was obtained from all donors. Donors received comprehensive information regarding the research purpose, sample usage, privacy protection measures, available counselling resources, and potential risks and benefits. Additionally, they were informed about their right to participate or decline using an opt-out option before signing the consent forms. Whole blood samples were collected from donors, and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated via density gradient centrifugation. The isolated PBMCs were then expanded in culture dishes. Pluripotency factors, including OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, LIN28, c-MYC, KLF4, and SV40LT, were delivered into PBMCs using a non-integrated reprogramming system over a period of 10 days [21]. Following transfection, cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium on Matrigel®-coated plates (Corning, USA, Cat#354277). Subsequently, hiPSC clones formed and were maintained in ncTarget hPSC Medium (Shownin, China, Cat#RP01020) supplemented with blebbistatin (1:4000 dilution; Shownin, China, Cat#RP01008) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The culture medium was replaced daily to ensure optimal growth.

The genotyping of mt-hiPSCs was conducted as follows: Cells were harvested, and genomic DNA was extracted using a DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN, China, Cat#DP304). The extracted DNA was subjected to PCR amplification, and the amplified products were analyzed via Sanger sequencing. The primer sequences used for the amplification were as follows:

m.3243-Forward: AGTAATCCAGGTCGGTTTCTATC

m.3243-Forward: AGTAATCCAGGTCGGTTTCTATC

m.3243-Reverse: GCGTCAGCGAAGGGTTGT

m.3243-Reverse: GCGTCAGCGAAGGGTTGT

The sequencing data were processed and analyzed using the Applied Biosystems™ Minor Variant Finder software to detect genetic variants and the details were shown in Supplementary Table 1. The wild-type human iPSCs (wt-hiPSCs) line, utilized as a control, was generously provided by Nuwacell Biotechnologies Co., Ltd (China).

For experimental treatment, both wt-hiPSCs and mt-hiPSCs were seeded on Matrigel®-coated plates at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/cm2 and cultured at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Upon reaching approximately 60% confluence, cells were treated with hydrogen peroxide (H2O2; Sigma, USA, Cat#88597) at varying concentrations (0, 100, 150, and 200 µM), diluted in ncTarget hPSC Medium for 24 h. For experiments investigating the protective effects of melatonin (Sigma, USA, Cat#M5250), cells were pretreated with different concentrations of melatonin (25 and 50 µM) and/or p38 MAPK inhibitor (SB203580, Selleck, USA, Cat#S1076) (1, 5 and 10 µM) for 12 h upon reaching ~50% confluence prior to H2O2-induced oxidative stress exposure for an additional 24 h.

Animals

Four-week-old NOD-SCID mice were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and housed in the Experimental Animal Center of Anhui Medical University under controlled conditions, with a constant temperature and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. Standard food and water were provided ad libitum throughout the study period to ensure optimal animal welfare. All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with ethical guidelines and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China; Approval No. PJ20220128).

Alkaline phosphatase staining of mt-hiPSCs

The pluripotency of mt-hiPSCs was identified by alkaline phosphatase (AP) staining. Cells were evenly seeded in P60 dishes and maintained under standard culture conditions, with daily medium changes. After 3 days, the cells were washed twice with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS; Gibco, USA, Cat#14190144) and subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma, USA, Cat#P6148) at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the fixed mt-hiPSCs were incubated in freshly prepared AP buffer for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Staining was performed by incubating the cells with BCIP/NBT dye (Invitrogen, USA, Cat#N6547) for 30 min in the dark.

Immunofluorescence

The mt-hiPSCs were seeded on 24-mm diameter round coverslips. After cell density was up to 60%, the mt-hiPSCs were fixed in 4% PFA overnight at 4 ℃. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS), permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in DPBS for 30 min, and subsequently blocked with 1% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA; Sigma, USA, Cat#126615) for 1 h at room temperature. The primary antibodies (SOX2, NANOG, OCT4) were applied to the cells and incubated for 2 h, followed by secondary antibodies in the dark for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were counterstained using DAPI (1:1000; Invitrogen, USA, Cat#62248) for 5 min. Images were visualized by using a confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany, LSM 880). Fluorescence signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (1.52a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). Details of all antibodies used are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Teratoma formation and histological analysis

To evaluate the differentiation potential of mt-hiPSCs, we performed a teratoma formation assay [22–24]. The mt-hiPSCs were suspended at 5 × 106 cells/ml in Matrigel pre-dissolved in DMEM/F12 solution. The 4-week-old SCID mice with comparable body weights were randomly selected and subcutaneously injected into bilateral groins with 400 µl of cell suspension (2 × 106 cells per mouse). The sample size was based on previously published studies [21]. A mouse was the experimental unit (n = 2 mice, 4 injection sites total). Mice were assessed every day for the size of teratomas to prevent the mean diameter exceeding 1.2 cm per teratoma and the cages were lined with enough hay to reduce mice’ discomfort. The cages were placed in the middle of the rack to minimize the effect of bright light. Eight weeks post-injection, all mice developed visible teratomas. To avoid excessive tumor burden and animal distress, the experiment was terminated at this point. No teratomas were excluded in this experiment because there were no oversized tumors or premature death of the mice. The mice were euthanized by exposure to CO2 (100%) until death was confirmed, after which teratomas were surgically excised. No survival surgeries or invasive procedures other than cell injection via syringe were performed on live mice; therefore, no anesthesia was required during this experiment. For histological analysis, the teratomas were washed in DPBS and cut into suitable sized tissue blocks. Firstly, the samples were fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, then dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. These tissues were serially sectioned and cut into sections. After deparaffinization by xylene twice and rehydrated in different concentrations of ethanol solution (90, 80, 70%). The slices were dipped into hematoxylin dye solution for 5 min, followed by eosin dye for 2 min. After dehydration in different concentrations of ethanol solution (70, 80, 90%) and mounting with neutral resin, they were observed by fully automated high-resolution panoramic imaging system (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Mice were assigned, injected and sectioned by different investigators and the operator analyzed with no knowledge of the experiment. The teratomas (n = 4) showed three primary germ layers were included in this study. A detailed description of all protocols can be found in the ethics approval report by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Anhui Medical University (Hefei, China; Approval No. PJ20220128). The experiment has been reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Cell viability assay

Then cells were digested with Solase single-cell digest (shownin, China, Cat#RP01021) in 6-well plates and the cell suspension was mixed with 0.4% Trypan Blue Staining at a 9:1 ratio. After staining for 3 min, cell viability was tested at once by using Countess™ 3 Automated Cell Counter (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). In addition, 10% CCK-8 solution (Dojindo, Japan, Cat#CK04) was added to each well of a 96-well plate containing cells. Plates were incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 for 2 h in the dark. The optical density (OD) values were analyzed at 450 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax 190, Molecular Device, USA). All measurements were conducted within three minutes to ensure data accuracy.

Flow cytometric analysis of cell apoptosis

Apoptosis was assessed using Annexin V-APC/Propidium Iodide (PI) double staining (Liankebio, China, Cat#AT107-100). After melatonin and/or H2O2 treatment as described previously, hiPSCs were digested into single cells using Solase digestion and subsequently collected. After centrifugation, the cells were incubated with 5 µl of Annexin V-APC and 10 µl of PI for 5 min at room temperature. The stained cell samples were analyzed via flow cytometry (BD FACSVerse, USA), and the proportion of Annexin V-positive cells was quantified to determine the apoptosis rate.

TUNEL staining

The hiPSCs were cultured in Matrigel-coated dishes containing glass slides at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/cm2 at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Following a 12-h treatment with melatonin and/or H2O2, the apoptosis rate was evaluated using the TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, Cat#C1088). Cells were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min and subsequently permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 solution for 5 min at room temperature. Fluorescent labeling solution containing TdT enzyme was then applied to the samples, followed by incubation at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI for 10 min. Finally, confocal laser-scanning microscopy (Zeiss, Germany, LSM 880) was employed to capture FITC-labeled TUNEL-positive nuclei images. The fluorescence intensity of apoptotic nuclei was quantified using ImageJ software (version 1.52a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Mitochondrial function analysis

For the detection of mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial superoxide, growing log-phase hiPSCs were planted in Matrigel®-coated 20 mm diameter glass dish at a density of 1.5 × 104 cells/cm2. After treatment, cells were incubated with TMRM (1:1000; Invitrogen, USA, Cat#T669)/MitoSOX™ Red (1:1000; Invitrogen, USA, Cat#M36008) and Hoechst 33342 (1:1000; ThermoFisher Scientific, USA, Cat#62249) for 30 min. The TMRM fluorescence signal (excitation/emission = 550/575 nm) and the MitoSOX™ Red fluorescence signal (excitation/emission = 556/571 nm) were detected by using confocal laser-scanning microscope (Zeiss, Germany, LSM 880). The Hoechst 33342 fluorescence signal was detected at an excitation/emission = 350/460 nm. The fluorescence intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software (1.52a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total cellular RNA was extracted using an RNA extraction kit (Xinbeibio, China, Cat#TQ301) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were treated for 12 h as previously described, collected, and lysed using the provided Lysis Buffer. RNA separation was performed via centrifugation following standard procedures. Reverse transcription to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) was carried out using an RNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Xinbeibio, China, Cat#R202). The primers used for cDNA amplification are listed in Supplementary Table 3. Reverse transcription conditions included incubation at 50 °C for 60 min, followed by denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min. Amplification involved a cycling program consisting of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 15 min, over a total of 17 cycles. Quantitative RT-PCR analysis was conducted using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, China, Cat#711) along with gene-specific primers designed for the target genes. The fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time System, West Berkeley, CA, USA) was employed to quantify mRNA levels under the following protocol: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; followed by cycling steps comprising denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s over a total of 40 cycles. The housekeeping gene ACTIN served as an internal reference standard to normalize transcription levels. Relative gene expression changes were calculated employing the 2−ΔΔCT method. Each experiment was repeated at least three times independently.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates from hiPSCs were prepared following different treatments as described above. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto 10% or 12% SDS–polyacrylamide gels. The separated proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet protein transfer system (Bio-Rad, West Berkeley, CA, USA) for 1.5 h at 4 °C. After blocking with 5% nonfat dry milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibodies listed in Supplementary Table 2. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Ultimately, the immunoreactive signals were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence system (Tianon 5200, China) and the relative intensities of the bands were quantified using ImageJ software (1.52a, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). GAPDH was used as an internal control at a dilution ratio of 1:10,000 to normalize protein expression levels.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Data are presented as mean ± SEM across biological replicates. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. P values were calculated using either One‐way or Two‐way ANOVA analysis and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Generation of human induced pluripotent stem cell line carrying m.3243A>G mutation

To establish a patient-specific cell model, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were collected from a female patient harboring a confirmed 70.70% heteroplasmic m.3243A>G mutation. These PBMCs were transfected with pluripotency factors (OCT4, SOX2, NANOG, LIN28, c-MYC, KLF4, and SV40LT) using an episomal non-integrative reprogramming system [21], leading to the successful induction of iPSC colonies (Fig. 1A, B). For comparative studies and validation purposes in subsequent experiments, we utilized hiPSCs derived from healthy individuals without the m.3243A>G mutation as controls (designated wt-hiPSCs). Genetic analysis confirmed that the established iPSCs possessed approximately 60.70% heteroplasmy for the m.3243A>G mutation at the mitochondrial DNA locus (Fig. 1C), hereafter referred to as mt-hiPSCs. Karyotype analysis revealed that mt-hiPSCs retained a normal diploid karyotype (46, XX), without any abnormalities in chromosome number or structure (Fig. 1D). AP staining further validated the pluripotency of this cell line (Fig. 1E). Immunofluorescence assays demonstrated robust expression of pluripotency transcription factors NANOG, OCT4, and SOX2 in mt-hiPSCs (Fig. 1F), confirming their stem cell properties. Moreover, mt-hiPSCs formed teratomas comprising all three embryonic germ layers in SCID mice (Fig. 1G), providing strong evidence of their pluripotent capacity. Thus, these findings indicated the successful establishment of mt-hiPSCs derived from a patient with mitochondrial disorders associated with the m.3243A>G mutation provides a more efficient and convenient disease model for further study in mitochondrial disease, as well as a strong rationale for preclinical experiments.

Melatonin mitigates oxidative stress-induced cell damage in mt-hiPSCs with m.3243A>G mutation

The mt-hiPSCs, despite harboring 60.70% of m.3243A>G mutation, displayed normal cell morphology similar to wt-hiPSCs (Fig. 2A). And trypan blue assay further confirmed no significant difference in cell viability between mt-hiPSCs and wt-hiPSCs under normal culture conditions (Fig. 2B). Upon exposure to low-dose prolonged H2O2 treatment (100–200 µM for 24 h), the mt-hiPSCs showed a significantly greater reduction in cell viability compared to wt-hiPSCs, characterized by an increase in dead cells and smaller colonies (Fig. 2A). The result was further supported by the CCK-8 assay, which revealed a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability with increasing concentrations of H2O2 (Fig. 2C). Notably, we observed that the viability of mt-hiPSCs was markedly lower than that of wt-hiPSCs, with the most pronounced difference occurring at 150 µM H2O2 exposure. These findings suggested that mtDNA mutation may exacerbate cellular susceptibility to external oxidative insults. Based on this result, subsequent experiments were conducted using this concentration.

Interestingly, melatonin (MT) demonstrated a strong protective effect against H2O2-induced cellular damage in mt-hiPSCs. Cells were pretreated with either 25 µM or 50 µM melatonin for 12 h before being exposed to 150 µM H2O2 under light-protected conditions for an additional 24 h. The results showed that melatonin significantly reduced the number of dead cells in the H2O2 + MT group compared to the H2O2-only group. Additionally, cells treated with melatonin only exhibited normal growth patterns, indicating that melatonin has no effect on cellular growing. Consistent with these observations, CCK-8 analysis showed that higher concentrations of melatonin progressively improved cell survival rates (Fig. 2D, E). Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), a cytosolic enzyme released into the extracellular space upon cell damage, served as another marker for assessing cellular injury [25]. Measurement of LDH levels in the culture medium demonstrated that exposure to H2O2 induced significant cellular damage, particularly in mt-hiPSCs which correlated with higher LDH activity levels compared to wt-hiPSCs. Remarkably, melatonin treatment effectively reversed this increase in LDH activity (Fig. 2F). Collectively, these results strongly confirmed that mtDNA mutations exacerbate susceptibility to oxidative stress-induced damage in mt-hiPSCs, which can be effectively alleviated by melatonin.

Melatonin protected against H2O2-induced apoptosis in mt-hiPSCs

To further investigate oxidative stress-induced apoptosis in mt-hiPSCs, we conducted comparative analysis of wt-hiPSCs and mt-hiPSCs using TUNEL assay. Under normal culture conditions, mt-hiPSCs exhibited significantly higher levels of apoptosis (1.40-fold increase compared to wt-hiPSCs; P < 0.01). Upon H2O2 treatment, this predisposition to apoptosis was exacerbated, with mt-hiPSCs showing 1.24-fold greater apoptotic cell death compared to wt-hiPSCs (P < 0.01) under the same condition. Notably, melatonin supplementation markedly attenuated H2O2-induced apoptosis in mt-hiPSCs, reducing apoptotic levels to normal level (Fig. 3A, B). Flow cytometric analysis using Annexin V/PI staining corroborated these observations. The proportion of apoptotic cells (Annexin V+/PI− and Annexin V+/PI+ populations) in H2O2-treated mt-hiPSCs reached 52.13 ± 6.89%, which was 2.03-fold higher than that observed in equivalently treated wt-hiPSCs (25.62 ± 3.13%; P < 0.001). Remarkably, melatonin supplementation completely normalized basal apoptosis in mt-hiPSCs to levels of wt-hiPSC controls (33.87 ± 2.96% vs.17.77 ± 2.40%; P < 0.05) (Fig. 3C, D).

RT-qPCR revealed that exposure to H2O2 increased the pro-apoptotic BAX/BCL2 mRNA ratio by 1.60 ± 0.15-fold in mt-hiPSCs versus 1.28 ± 0.04-fold in wt-hiPSCs (P < 0.05). Similarly, CASPASE-3 mRNA expression surged by 1.58 ± 0.09-fold in mt-hiPSCs compared to 1.27 ± 0.02-fold in wt-hiPSCs (P < 0.05). Melatonin treatment effectively suppressed these elevations in mt-hiPSCs, restoring BAX/BCL2 and CASPASE-3 expression levels close to those observed in wt-hiPSC controls (Fig. 3E, F). Western blot analysis further confirmed these findings. H2O2-induced accumulation of pro-apoptotic BAX and activation of CASPASE-3 were significantly reduced following melatonin co-treatment (Fig. 3G, S1A-F). These results collectively demonstrate that mitochondrial genetic instability fundamentally increases susceptibility to apoptosis by amplifying cellular responses to oxidative stress. Importantly, melatonin counteracts this vulnerability through coordinated regulation of both upstream apoptotic signaling pathways and downstream effector mechanisms.

Melatonin protected against mitochondrial dysfunction in mt-hiPSCs with the treatment of H2O2

To evaluate the impact of mtDNA mutations on mitochondrial function, we assessed mitochondrial responses to oxidative stress induced by H₂O₂ [26]. Using MitoSOX™ Red dye to stain mitochondrial superoxide in live cells, minimal fluorescence signals were observed in the NC (non-treatment control group) and MT groups under normal conditions. However, exposure to H₂O₂ resulted in a rapid increase in fluorescence intensity, indicating significant accumulation of superoxide within mitochondria. This effect was more pronounced in mt-hiPSCs compared to wt-hiPSCs (Fig. 4A, B). Notably, melatonin treatment significantly alleviated mitochondrial abnormalities, suggesting its potential as a therapeutic agent for mitigating mitochondrial dysfunction caused by mtDNA mutations.

Mitochondrial bioenergetics are closely associated with mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), which is essential for maintaining oxidative phosphorylation and scavenging reactive oxygen species [27]. To assess Δψm dynamics, we performed TMRM staining. Under normal conditions, strong red fluorescence signals were observed in both NC and MT groups, reflecting intact membrane potential. Upon H₂O₂ exposure, however, a marked depolarization of Δψm occurred in both cell types; this depolarization was significantly greater in mt-hiPSCs than in wt-hiPSCs (Fig. 4C, D). Notably, melatonin supplementation effectively restored Δψm integrity following H₂O₂-induced damage. Additionally, morphological changes such as cellular shrinkage and nuclear fragmentation were observed (indicated by white arrows), consistent with apoptotic body formation induced by oxidative stress.

Mitochondrial protein expression profiles were systematically evaluated under oxidative stress conditions. Our findings revealed that hydrogen peroxide exposure significantly upregulated two critical mitochondrial components: MFN1 (mitofusin 1), a key regulator of mitochondrial fusion dynamics, and COX IV (cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4), an essential electron transport chain complex subunit, when compared with untreated controls. These increases were particularly evident in mt-hiPSCs compared with untreated controls or wt-hiPSCs. Notably, melatonin administration effectively normalized the expression levels of these proteins to baseline values observed in physiological conditions. In contrast, the expression pattern of UQCRC2 (ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase core protein 2), another fundamental component of the respiratory chain complex, remained no change across all experimental groups without statistically significant variation (Fig. 4E–K). These results collectively indicated that cells harboring mtDNA mutations are more vulnerable to disruptions in mitochondrial homeostasis under H2O2-induced oxidative stress compared with wt-hiPSCs. Importantly, melatonin was able to mitigate these dysfunctions by preserving Δψm integrity and addressing anomalies within the mitochondrial respiratory chain. Taken together, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of melatonin in protecting against oxidative stress-induced damage in cells with compromised mitochondrial homeostasis due to mtDNA mutations.

Melatonin suppressed oxidative stress induced MAPK hyperactivation in mt-hiPSCs

The MAPK pathway is a well-characterized cellular damage response pathway that plays a crucial role in regulating oxidative stress and chronic inflammation [28]. To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of melatonin against H2O2-induced apoptosis and mitochondrial dysfunction in mt-hiPSCs, we analyzed the expression levels of key proteins involved in the MAPK signaling pathway (Fig. 5A). Our results revealed distinct differences in MAPK activation between wt-hiPSCs and mt-hiPSCs following H2O2 exposure. In wt-hiPSCs, most MAPK-related proteins showed no significant changes after treatment with H2O2, with the exceptions of p-p38 and p-ERK, which were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5B–G). By contrast, mt-hiPSCs exhibited robust activation across nearly all measured components of the MAPK signaling cascade (Fig. 5H–M). Specifically, the expression levels of p-p38, p38, p-ERK, ERK, p-JNK, and JNK were all significantly elevated in mt-hiPSCs upon H2O2 stimulation. This pronounced activation indicates that cells harboring mitochondrial DNA mutations may be more susceptible to oxidative stress-induced dysregulation of the MAPK pathway.

Remarkably, melatonin treatment effectively counteracted these alterations in both wt-hiPSCs and mt-hiPSCs. The upregulated protein levels observed following H2O2 exposure were markedly reduced after melatonin supplementation, restoring them to almost normal levels. This suggests that melatonin exerts its protective effects by suppressing excessive activation of the MAPK pathway during oxidative stress. These findings highlight that cotreatment with melatonin not only mitigates apoptosis but also restores mitochondrial function by downregulating aberrant MAPK signaling under H2O2-induced oxidative stress. Moreover, our data suggest that mt-hiPSCs exhibit heightened sensitivity to oxidative stress due to an exaggerated activation of the MAPK pathway when compared to their wild-type counterparts.

To further investigate the role of the MAPK pathway in mt-hiPSCs under oxidative stress, we employed SB203580, a well-characterized p38 MAPK inhibitor. Administration of SB203580 at an optimal concentration of 5 µM significantly improved cell viability under H₂O₂ treatment, as evidenced by quantitative analyses (Fig. 6A–C). Notably, SB203580 alone did not induce detectable adverse effects in either wt-hiPSCs or mt-hiPSCs. Importantly, SB203580 treatment suppressed H₂O₂-induced hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway and effectively restored phosphorylated p38 (p-p38) levels while ameliorating the reduction in total p38 expression observed in mt-hiPSCs (Fig. 6D–H), further supporting our hypothesis regarding the involvement of p38 MAPK signaling. Thus, these findings demonstrate that melatonin exerts its protective effects against H2O2-induced cellular damage primarily through modulation of MAPK signaling pathways, especially in mt-hiPSCs.

Discussion

Mitochondrial disorders caused by the m.3243A>G mutation in MT-TL1 present significant therapeutic challenges due to their multisystemic manifestations [29, 30]. Our study leverages patient-derived iPSCs harboring this mutation to model oxidative stress vulnerability and demonstrates melatonin’s robust cytoprotective effects, offering novel insights into its therapeutic potential for m.3243A>G-associated pathologies.

We performed a series of experiments related to the biological characteristics of mt-hiPSCs. After confirming mt-hiPSCs carried a 60.70% A>G heterozygous mutation at the mtDNA 3243 locus, as well as the absence of numerical or structural chromosome abnormalities, we used immunofluorescence to detect the expression of relevant pluripotency factors. Since iPSCs have high differentiation potential in vivo, we subcutaneously injected mt-hiPSCs into SCID mice with a low rate of rejection and a high transplantation rate, and H&E staining of the teratomas revealed the presence of three germ layers. These differentiated tissues confirmed the full multi-differentiation potential of the established mt-hiPSC.

The heightened sensitivity of mt-hiPSCs to H₂O₂-induced damage underscores the critical role of mitochondrial integrity in stress resilience. While wt-hiPSCs maintained viability under oxidative challenge, mt-hiPSCs exhibited exacerbated apoptosis (52.13 vs. 25.62%) and mitochondrial dysfunction, mirroring the accelerated tissue degeneration observed in MELAS patients. Melatonin pretreatment normalized apoptosis rates and restored viability, aligning with its established role in modulating mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic pathways. The suppression of BAX/BCL2 imbalance and CASPASE-3 activation highlights melatonin’s ability to stabilize the intrinsic apoptotic machinery, previously reported in neuronal models but newly validated here in patient-specific pluripotent cells. These findings extend prior observations in cybrids and fibroblasts, emphasizing the translational relevance of iPSC-based models for mitochondrial disease research.

Mitochondrial dysfunction in m.3243A>G hiPSCs under oxidative stress manifested as superoxide accumulation, ΔΨm collapse, and respiratory chain perturbations (e.g., elevated COX IV). Melatonin not only scavenged mitochondrial ROS but also preserved ΔΨm and respiratory complex integrity, suggesting a dual role in redox balance and bioenergetic maintenance. Importantly, we identified hyperactivation of the MAPK pathway (p-p38, p-ERK, p-JNK) as a key contributor to mutation-associated vulnerability. While MAPK signaling transiently aids stress adaptation, its sustained activation in mt-hiPSCs likely exacerbates mitochondrial damage through pro-apoptotic signaling and fission–fusion dysregulation. Furthermore, p38 MAPK inhibition demonstrated cytoprotective effects comparable to melatonin, consistent with our hypothesis regarding MAPK pathway involvement. Melatonin’s suppression of phospho-MAPK isoforms reveals a novel mechanistic axis, complementing its direct antioxidant effects. In addition to acting as a direct free-radical scavenger, melatonin may also mediate its effects via its receptors. Here, it remains unclear which mechanism plays the dominant role in protecting cells under oxidative stress. Therefore, determining whether melatonin’s main protective effect relies on its antioxidant properties or receptor-dependent signaling will be important for future research.

The clinical urgency for effective MELAS therapies underscores the significance of our findings. Current management relies on symptomatic relief (e.g., arginine for stroke-like episodes) [31–33], with no treatments addressing mitochondrial dysfunction at its source. Melatonin’s efficacy in rescuing iPSCs with 60.70% heteroplasmy, a threshold associated with severe phenotypes, suggests its potential to mitigate tissue-specific damage in patients. Its ability to cross the blood–brain barrier is particularly advantageous for addressing neurological manifestations, a hallmark of MELAS. Furthermore, melatonin’s safety profile in chronic use (e.g., sleep disorders, neurodegenerative diseases) supports its rapid repurposing [34–37]. Future studies should explore synergies with mitochondrial cofactors (e.g., coenzyme Q10) or NAD+ boosters to enhance respiratory chain recovery, as well as tissue-specific responses using differentiated mt-hiPSC derived neurons or cardiomyocytes [38–41].

It is important to note that the melatonin concentrations (50 μM) used in this study exceed physiological serum levels observed in vivo, which poses a key challenge for translating these findings into clinical practice. Achieving therapeutically effective melatonin concentrations at the target tissues or organs remains an important issue to resolve before clinical application can be realized. For inherited diseases caused by mitochondrial mutations and mainly affecting tissues or organs with high energy demands such as muscle, strategies like transdermal microneedle patches or injectable hydrogels may help deliver effective doses directly to affected areas [42, 43]. For disorders involving internal organs or less accessible tissues, advanced targeted delivery systems such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, or micelles could offer controlled release and enhanced targeting efficiency [44, 45]. These approaches together provide promising directions for optimizing melatonin delivery and improving its translational potential in future therapy.

Although this study focuses on undifferentiated iPSCs as a model system, future investigations should extend these findings by examining clinically relevant cell types derived from mt-hiPSCs (e.g., pancreatic β-cells or skeletal myocytes) [12, 46]. Additionally, the long-term effects of melatonin on pluripotency and genomic stability require evaluation. In vivo validation using animal models carrying the m.3243A>G mutation will strengthen translational relevance. Finally, elucidating melatonin’s impact on mtDNA heteroplasmy dynamics whether it selectively protects healthy mitochondria or promotes mutant mtDNA clearance could refine its therapeutic application.

Conclusions

In summary, our work establishes melatonin as a multi-mechanistic guardian against oxidative damage in m.3243A>G iPSCs, targeting both mitochondrial dysfunction and stress signaling pathways (Fig. 7). By integrating patient-specific modeling with mechanistic pharmacology, this study advances melatonin as a clinically accessible candidate for mitochondrial disorders. Its ability to concurrently stabilize redox homeostasis, suppress apoptosis, and modulate MAPK cascades positions it uniquely among emerging mitochondrial therapeutics, offering hope for halting progression in MELAS and related syndromes.
