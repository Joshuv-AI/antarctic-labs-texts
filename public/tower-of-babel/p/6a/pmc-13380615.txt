Analysis of Gut Microbiota Metabolites in Chronic Kidney Disease–Associated Pruritus Using Network Pharmacology and Molecular Docking
Authors: Lingling Sun, Yining He, Min Jiang, Weiming He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus (CKD-aP) remains a frequent and distressing symptom in patients with renal dysfunction, yet its biological mechanisms are still not well defined. Growing evidence suggests that gut microbiota-derived metabolites may participate in systemic inflammatory regulation, providing a potential entry point for exploring CKD-aP pathogenesis.Objectives: This study aimed to elucidate how gut microbial metabolites interact with host molecular targets involved in CKD-aP, and to identify candidate metabolites with therapeutic relevance through integrative bioinformatics approaches.Materials and Methods: Gut metabolite data were retrieved from the gutMGene database, and CKD-aP–related genes were collected from GeneCards, DisGeNET, and OMIM. Intersection targets were processed through STRING-based protein–protein interaction (PPI) analysis, followed by Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment using the DAVID platform. An integrated microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target (MSMT) network was constructed to visualize microbial–metabolic interactions. Key metabolites were subsequently examined by molecular docking to assess binding affinity with pivotal targets.Results: A total of 44 overlapping genes were identified between microbial metabolites and CKD-aP, among which IL6, AKT1, and PPARG were highlighted as major hub regulators. MSMT network analysis indicated strong associations among Lacticaseibacillus paracasei, tryptophan-related metabolites, and linoleic-acid–derived compounds in modulating CKD-aP. KEGG results emphasized pathways enriched in inflammatory and immune responses, including IL-17, C-type lectin receptor, and Toll-like receptor signaling. Docking simulations supported robust interactions for several metabolites, particularly 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid with PPARG, Genipin with CASP3, and Urolithin A with CYP1A1.Conclusion: Our findings suggest that gut microbial metabolites may alleviate CKD-aP by targeting IL6, AKT1, and PPARG through inflammatory signaling pathways. Notably, 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid from Lacticaseibacillus paracasei demonstrates strong binding affinity with PPARG, highlighting the potential of probiotic-derived metabolites as novel therapeutic strategies for managing pruritus in chronic kidney disease patients.Keywords: CKD-associated pruritus, gut microbiota, metabolites, bioinformatics, molecular docking

1. Background

Chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus is a chronic pruritus related to chronic kidney disease by excluding other diseases, which is one of the high incidence complications of chronic kidney disease ( 1 ). Pruritus symptoms gradually worsen with the progress of chronic kidney disease. DOPPS research report shows that CKD-aP leads to sleep disorders in patients, with the occurrence of mental symptoms such as depression and anxiety, severely affecting patients’ quality of life. Moreover, moderate to severe CKD-aP increases the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events, hospitalization rates, and mortality ( 2 ). The pathogenesis of chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus is still unclear. The exact mechanism of chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus remains unclear. Studies have shown that CKD-aP is caused by factors such as accumulation of uremic toxins, immune dysfunction, microinflammatory states, unbalanced opioid receptor activity, and disorders of calcium and phosphorus metabolism ( 3 ). A healthy, well-balanced gut microbiome plays a vital role in supporting general wellbeing. Research indicates that disruptions in gut bacteria populations often correlate with various persistent skin irritation disorders. For instance, disruptions in the gut microbiome have been closely linked to skin disorders like atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, acne, and alopecia areata ( 4 ). The emerging approach of using probiotics and fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) offers a novel avenue for alleviating or managing these skin issues ( 5 ). Multiple research findings have shown that the balance of the intestinal microbiota is disrupted during the progression of chronic kidney disease from early to late stages. This imbalance is characterized by a decrease in microbial diversity, an increase in the number of pathogenic bacteria, and a decline in the number of beneficial bacteria ( 6 ). Propionibacterium acnes is an anaerobic bacteria that parasitic on the skin that protects against harmful pathogens. Studies have shown that Propionibacterium acnes produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which dissolve calcium and phosphorus compounds and relieve itching symptoms in patients with CKD ( 7 ). Studies of the skin microbiota have shown a decrease in the number of Propionibacterium acnes associated with CKD-aP, and that the severity of pruritus is associated with a decrease in the species of Staphylococcus epidermidis ( 8 ). Uremic Toxins, dietary limitations, medication use, and ongoing inflammation are key contributors to an unbalanced gut microbiome. Such an imbalance in the gut’s microorganisms is linked to increased overall mortality rates among dialysis patients. The gut microbiome contributes significantly to generating high levels of uremic toxins, such as indolyl sulfate, p-cresol sulfate, and trimethylamine N-oxide. These substances can lead to various issues linked to chronic kidney disease, including heart-related complications, disturbances in calcium and phosphorus balance, and skin itching ( 9 , 10 ). Patients suffering from chronic kidney disease often experience an enhanced permeability in their intestinal walls, allowing for inflammatory agents and waste products, known as uremic toxins, to infiltrate the bloodstream. This process sets off a chain reaction of systemic inflammation and the buildup of toxins, which in turn fosters an increase in pro-inflammatory compounds associated with itching and the accumulation of toxins within the skin, ultimately leading to pruritus. Consequently, restoring the gut microbiota’s equilibrium, reinforcing the integrity of the intestinal lining, and mitigating widespread inflammation could ease the complications tied to chronic kidney disease and late-stage renal failure. As of now, there’s a dearth of effective treatments for the pruritus that often accompanies chronic kidney disease. Delving into the potential of manipulating the gut microbiota to alleviate pruritus in kidney disease patients is a promising avenue for further research. In chronic kidney disease patients, a compromised intestinal barrier enables inflammatory agents and uremic toxins to breach the bloodstream, inciting inflammation and the pooling of toxins associated with kidney failure ( 9 , 11 ). This system encourages the production of inflammatory compounds, which trigger the urge to scratch, and simultaneously boosts the buildup of endotoxins in the skin, causing an itchy sensation. Consequently, by restoring the balance of microorganisms, strengthening the integrity of the intestinal barrier, and lessening systemic inflammation, we can potentially ease the complications associated with End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD).

Currently, there are limited effective approaches for alleviating or treating CKD-aP. Therefore, exploring gut microbiota-related targets to improve pruritus in CKD patients represents a promising research direction.

2. Objectives

In our study, we employed network pharmacology approaches to pinpoint critical molecular targets, signaling pathways, metabolites, and gut microbiota involved in modulating CKD-aP, while also examining how these elements interact with one another. Following this, molecular docking techniques were utilized to validate the binding relationships between metabolites and their key targets. The overarching aim was to uncover gut-originated metabolites that could potentially alleviate the itching linked to chronic kidney disease, opening new doors for therapeutic development. The findings aim to set the stage for future experimental research and help shape targeted treatment strategies centered on specific metabolites for managing CKD-aP.

3. Materials and Methods

3.1. Identification and Selection of Gut Metabolites and Their Candidate Targets

Information on gut microbiota–derived metabolites was first collected from the gutMGene v2.0 database ( 12 ). The corresponding molecular structures in SMILES format were obtained from the PubChem repository. To predict the potential protein targets of these metabolites, two separate computational tools—Similarity Ensemble Approach (SEA) and Swiss TargetPrediction—were employed. The predicted target lists from both platforms were then compared, and only the overlapping targets were retained. This intersection was determined using an online Venn diagram tool, ensuring that subsequent analyses focused on consistently predicted metabolite–target associations.

The gutMGene database was selected as the primary source for metabolite data due to its comprehensive coverage of gut microbiota-derived metabolites with experimentally validated microbial sources, which is essential for constructing the microbiota-substrate-metabolite-target (MSMT) network.

Compared to general metabolite databases such as HMDB, gutMGene provides specific microbial origin information and substrate-metabolite relationships that are critical for understanding the gut-kidney axis in CKD-aP pathogenesis. This database has been widely utilized in microbiome-metabolite research and offers curated data on metabolite-microbiota associations that align with our study objectives.

3.2. Core Target Identification in CKD-Associated Pruritus

To pinpoint key targets linked to chronic kidney disease-associated pruritus, we conducted an extensive search across multiple databases—DisGeNET (https://disgenet.com), GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org) and OMIM (https://omim.org/)—using both “chronic kidney disease-related pruritus” and “uremic pruritus” as primary search terms. We selected targets with a correlation score of 10 or above from GeneCards for further research. Core targets were identified by intersecting the target sets from all three databases, which we propose as crucial factors in CKD-aP pathogenesis.

3.3. Protein-Protein Interaction (PPI) Network Construction and Analysis

The STRING database (https://string-db.org/) was employed to generate a PPI network for the core targets, which was subsequently visualized and analyzed using Cytoscape 3.10.3.

3.4. Functional Enrichment Analysis (GO and KEGG)

The intersected target genes were imported into the DAVID platform (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/tools.jsp) to perform Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment. Visualization of the enrichment results was completed using an online bioinformatics plotting tool (http://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). GO categories and KEGG pathways meeting the statistical cutoff of P < 0.05 were considered meaningful and subsequently included in further analyses.

3.5. Microbiota-Substrate-Metabolite-Target (MSMT) Network Analysis

A network diagram showing the interactions between the gut microbiota, substrates, metabolites and targets was assembled and viewed using Cytoscape 3.10.3 software. In this network, each component or target was represented by a node, and connections between them were represented by lines to reveal the mutual relationship.

3.6. Molecular Docking of Metabolites and Targets

Metabolite structures in SDF format were extracted from PubChem and optimized using ChemBio3D to generate energy-minimized 3D models, which were then converted to MOL2 files. AutoDockTools facilitated the addition of hydrogen atoms and conversion to PDBQT format. Target protein structures were retrieved from the RCSB-PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org), standardized via UniProt (https://www.uniprot.org), and refined in PyMOL by removing solvents and ligands. Docking simulations were executed in AutoDock4, with PyMOL visualizing the most stable binding conformations based on energy scores.

3.7. Drug-Likeness and Toxicity Evaluation

The major metabolites identified in this study were further assessed for pharmacokinetic suitability using the SwissADME platform (http://www.swissadme.ch/) and ADMETlab 2.0 (https://admetmesh.scbdd.com/). Evaluation criteria were based on the Lipinski “rule of five,” including limits for molecular weight, lipophilicity (LogP), hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, and topological polar surface area. Metabolites that conformed to these drug-likeness parameters were subsequently subjected to additional analyses.

3.8. Statistical Analysis

Descriptive and enrichment-related statistical analyses were performed using built-in tools from the respective databases and platforms. For GO and KEGG enrichment, significance thresholds were determined using the default algorithms of the DAVID platform, with P < 0.05 considered statistically meaningful. Network topology parameters—including degree, betweenness, and closeness centrality—were calculated in Cytoscape (cytoNCA plugin) to identify hub nodes. Molecular docking scores were generated automatically by AutoDock4 based on binding free energy calculations, and more negative values indicated stronger predicted affinities. All statistical procedures were carried out using standard settings, and no additional manual weighting or adjustment was applied.

4.5. Docking Testing of Metabolites and Targets for Molecular

We used metabolites corresponding to the nine core targets identified from the gutMGene v2.0 database for molecular docking. RELA was excluded from the analysis because there were no matching metabolites. We performed molecular docking analysis on 8core targets (IL6, AKT1,PPARG, EGFR, CASP3, CXCL8, CYP1A1, JUN) screened through the plugin cytoNCA and 21metabolites (3-Indolepropionic acid, Acetate, Butyrate, Propionate, Trimethylamine oxide, Vancomycin, 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid, Indoxyl sulfate, p-Cresol sulfate, Phenylacetylglutamine, Geni-pin, Hydroquinone, Succinate, Baicalein, Baicalin, Indole, Oxindole, 3-Indoleacrylic acid, Skatole, Kynurenic acid, urolithin A). Molecular docking of Vancomycin was not performed because conformer generation was disallowed due to too many atoms. Receptor-ligand interactions with docking binding energies below 0 kcal·mol-1 indicate spontaneous binding. The more negative the binding energy, the more stable this interaction is. As the energy decreases, the possibility of binding increases, thereby forming a more stable conformation and enhancing subsequent reaction potential. When docking binding energies are below-5 kcal·mol-1, a strong binding affinity is usually reflected. The results showed that PPAG with 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid (-5.77), EGFR with Indoxyl sulfate (-5.21), CASP3 with Genipin (-5.05), CXCL8 with Succinate (-5.77), CYP1A1 with 3-Indoleacrylic acid, CYP1A1 with Kynurenic acid (-6.03), CYP1A1 with urolithin A (-5.92) have strong binding potential (Table 2). Indoxyl sulfate, succinate, 3-indoleacrylic acid, and kynurenic acid are uremic toxins or potential uremic toxins, so no further visualization analysis was performed on these compounds. Visualization analysis of molecular docking results (Fig. 5) shown that 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid interacted with Lys367(B) through hydrogen bonds in PPARG, Genipin interacted with Tyr274(A),Tyr37(A), Met-39(A), and His277(A) through hydrogen bonds in CASP3. Urolithin A interacted with Thr310 (A), Asp309 (A), Leu372 (A) and Met374 (A) through hydrogen bonds in CYP1A1. These results suggest that 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid, Genipin, 3-Indoleacrylic acid, Kynurenic acid, and urolithin A may be potential metabolites affecting CKD-aP.

5. Discussion

The imbalance of gut microbiota can trigger immune inflammatory responses in the body. It can also lead to the production and accumulation of various uremic toxins. The development of pruritus associated with chronic kidney disease is believed to be linked to the buildup of these toxins and immune system inflammation. Studies indicate that Type 2 cytokines are the primary regulatory factors involved in chronic pruritus secreted by mast cells, innate lymphoid cells, and Type 2 T lymphocytes, including IL-4, IL-13, IL-17, IL-31, IL-33, and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP) ( 13 ). These cytokines can directly affect primary sensory neurons or change the sensitivity of other pruritus-related mediators. Many studies have confirmed that modulating the microbiota-gut-skin axis can reduce and inhibit cytokines associated with pruritus ( 14 ). Our study used network pharmacology and molecular docking methods to explore possible signaling pathways and intestinal metabolites associated with CKD-aP, with the goal of identifying new treatment paths for the disorder. In the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network, AKT1, IL6 and PPARG were identified as key target genes.

CKD-aP patients present a micro-inflammatory state, and the levels of inflammatory factors are higher than those in patients without CKD-aP. Studies have revealed that inflammatory factors mainly IL6 have a strong correlation with the occurrence of CKD-aP ( 15 ). The accumulation of calcium and phosphorus in the skin is one of the causes of pruritus in uremic patients. Experimental studies have found that the skin injection of Cap in mice can induce the production of pruritus IL-6, and the use of IL-6 inhibitor can improve the pruritus symptoms. IL-6 induces a chronic pruritus and microinflammatory state in the form of IL-6/p-BTK/p-ERK signaling in calcium phosphate-mediated pruritus in hemodialysis patients ( 16 ). AKT1 functions as a key kinase that mediates signaling within the PI3K/AKT pathway, which is critical for regulating cell proliferation and survival ( 17 ). AKT1 is hyperphosphorylated in many cancers and is one of the targets for cancer therapy ( 18 ). Kim I Y et al. studied how Akt1 affects the progression of mice from acute kidney injury (AKI) to CKD. Their research highlighted the involvement of the Akt signaling pathway in the disease progression of both acute kidney injury and chronic kidney disease. In addition, they found that targeting the Akt1 pathway could serve as a promising therapeutic strategy to prevent the transition from AKI to CKD ( 19 ). Dehydroevodiamine can directly act on AKT1 to improve ulcerative colitis, enhance intestinal mucosal barrier function, inhibit apoptosis, and reduce inflammatory response and oxidative stress ( 20 ). PPARG is a gene associated with immune function and is crucial for controlling metabolic processes and reducing inflammation. Agonists that target PPARG can significantly treat many acute and chronic inflammatory conditions while enhancing the body’s ability to fight inflammation. The PPARG pathway affects the balance between the differentiation of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory macrophages, guiding their functional polarization. Studies have found that the downregulation of PPARG gene expression in ulcerative colitis patients can cause mucosal inflammation ( 21 ). The three core targets mentioned above are all significantly involved in immune inflammation and could potentially affect CKD-aP via immune inflammation pathways.

KEGG enrichment analysis of 44 core targets revealed that pathways related to immune inflammation were enriched, including the Toll-like receptor signaling pathway (TLRs), the IL-17 signaling pathway, and the C-type lectin receptor (CLR) signaling pathway. These signaling pathways may be closely involved in the development of CKD-aP.

The signaling pathway of Toll-like receptors (TLRs) plays a crucial role in identifying pathogens and activating the body’s innate immune response. By controlling the activation of antigen presenting cells and key cytokines ( 22 ), TLRs serve as an important bridge between innate immunity and adaptive immunity. TLR signaling can directly affect the behavior of T cells, including their activation, proliferation, differentiation, maturation, and overall function. Specifically, stimulation of TLR4 on CD4 + T cells affect their properties and their ability to trigger inflammation in the gut ( 23 ). Yu Shan Ye etc. revealed Electroacupuncture improved pruritus in morphine-induced pruritus model mice through the TLR2/4-MyD88-NF-κB pathway and down-regulating IL-6 ( 24 ). IL-17 signaling pathway plays an important role in inflammation-related diseases, especially in skin inflammatory diseases. The IL-17 cytokine produced by T cells and other cell types efficiently activates keratinocytes in a feedforward cycle to promote inflammation. In normal human skin, IL - 17 is critical to maintaining the skin barrier and resisting extracellular bacteria and fungi. It achieves this function by prompting epithelial cells to release inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, matrix-degrading enzymes and antibacterial substances ( 25 ).

Chandra et al. demonstrated that the IL-17 pathway enhances gut barrier defense by regulating microbial populations. They also found that the imbalance of the intestinal microbiota caused by the loss of interleukin-17 receptor a (IL-17RA) signaling in intestinal epithelial cells can affect the systemic immune response, which in turn leads to the development of tumors in other parts of the body ( 26 ). Disruption of the IL-17 signaling pathway maintains persistent inflammation in autoimmune and inflammatory disorders such as psoriasis and hidradenitis suppurativa ( 27 ). Inhibitors targeting interleukin-17 have shown safety and effectiveness in treating conditions such as psoriasis, psoriatic arthritis and ankylosing spondylitis (28). Research found that serum IL-17 and blood creatinine show a parallel relationship during the progression of chronic kidney disease ( 29 ). IL17A can cause the production and fibrosis of kidney and vascular inflammation. When on a high-salt diet, the level of Th17 cells that release a large amount of IL-17 increases, further promoting the increased secretion of IL17A in the intestine, which further leads to the progression of chronic kidney disease and the generation of a micro-inflammatory state ( 30 ). C-type lectin receptor (CLR) signaling pathway activates innate immune responses by recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) ( 31 ). This triggers downstream signaling (e.g. Syk/CARD9-NF-κB/NLRP3 inflammasome) lead to pro-inflammatory cytokine production (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α). These cytokines directly stimulate hepatocytes to synthesize CRP, an acute-phase protein regulated primarily by IL-6 via JAK-STAT signaling. Blocking Mincle or LOX-1 attenuates renal inflammation/fibrosis in animal models ( 32 ).

Through analysis of the gutMGene database, we identified 44 core targets significantly associated with CKD-aP. Notably, 33 key metabolites corresponding to these 44 core targets were found to be enriched in the tryptophan metabolism pathway. In patients with CKD, accumulated uremic metabolic products originating from the gut further demonstrate cytotoxic properties. These substances weaken the inner membrane of the intestinal epithelium, resulting in increased intestinal permeability. Bacteria and endotoxin from the gut can more easily enter the blood circulation. The increase in circulating endotoxin in turn triggers endotoxemia and a widespread inflammatory response, which further promotes the development of chronic kidney disease ( 33 ). Known gut-derived uremic toxins include indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate, 3-indoleacetic acid, trimethylamine oxide, and phenylacetylglutamine. Indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate and phenylacetylglutamine are related to the target EGFR. Intestinal microbiota produces a variety of metabolites such as Indoxyl sulfate, 3-indoleacetic acid, p-cresyl sulfate and kynurenine through the tryptophan metabolic pathway, and these metabolites accumulate in end-stage kidney disease and trigger multi-organ damage. Indoxyl sulfate and 3-indoleacetic acid are produced by the metabolism of tryptophan in the diet ( 34 ). Tryptophan is metabolized to indole by tryptophanase in intestinal bacteria such as Escherichia coli. Indole is absorbed in the intestine and sulphated in the liver to produce indoxyl sulfate. Indoxyl sulfate is normally excreted in the urine. Due to its strong binding to albumin, it cannot be effectively removed by conventional hemodialysis ( 35 ). Indoxyl sulfate is associated with elevated inflammatory biomarkers in patients with stage 3-4 chronic kidney disease ( 36 ). It has been shown that there is a positive correlation between IL-6 and indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate in hemodialysis patients ( 37 ). P-Cresol sulfate is a protein-bound uremic toxin produced by intestinal microbiota metabolism. It accumulates in patients with CKD and is closely associated with disease progression, cardiovascular complications, and mortality ( 38 ). Kynurenic Acid (KYNA) is a key metabolite in the tryptophan metabolic pathway, the Kynurenine Pathway, and is produced through catalysis by Kynurenine Aminotransferase ( 39 ). Tryptophan metabolites indole, indole derivatives and kynurenic quinoline can promote the release of cytokines such as IL-6 and IL-17 by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) ( 40 ). AHR is a helix-loop-helix transcription factor that binds to various ligands in vivo. It is one of the main receptors of renal injury induced by tryptophan metabolites. IS and KYN induce and aggravate renal injury by activating AHR signal transduction ( 41 ). Three tryptophanase families (Fusobacteriaceae, Verrucomiaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae) produce enzymes for indole and p-cresol ( 42 ). This suggests that gut microbiota can influence uremic toxin production and inflammation production through the tryptophan metabolic pathway leading to the occurrence of pruritus. However, it is important to note the dual roles of tryptophan-derived uremic toxins in CKD pathophysiology. While metabolites such as indoxyl sulfate and kynurenine are well-established contributors to systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and cardiovascular complications in CKD patients, certain tryptophan metabolites may also exhibit protective effects under specific conditions. For instance, moderate levels of kynurenic acid have been shown to possess neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties through aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) activation. Similarly, indole derivatives can promote intestinal barrier integrity and modulate immune responses when present at physiological concentrations. These observations suggest that the relationship between tryptophan metabolism and CKD-aP pathogenesis is complex and concentration-dependent, requiring careful consideration when developing therapeutic interventions targeting this pathway. Further research is needed to elucidate the threshold concentrations at which these metabolites transition from beneficial to detrimental effects in the context of chronic kidney disease.

Through molecular docking, we found that 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid (10-KOA) has good binding affinity with PPARG. 10-KOA is a biologically active unsaturated ketone fatty acid produced by Lactobacillus plantarum via biohydrogenation of linoleic acid. Genipin has good binding energy with CASP3. Genipin is the main active component of traditional Chinese medicine Gardenia (Gardenia jasminoides Ellis), a natural cross-linking agent extracted from Gardenia jasminoides, produced by the hydrolysis of Geniposide by gut microbiota β-glucosidase ( 43 ).

Recent studies have shown that it plays multiple roles in controlling CASP3 activity associated with cell apoptosis, is also involved in inflammatory responses and plays a protective function in organs ( 44 ). Research found that Genipin reduces the production of periodontitis-related inflammatory factors by activating signaling pathways through phosphorylation of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB) p65, extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK), and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MEK), mediated by IL-1β-induced production of IL-6 ( 45 ). Genipin promotes cell apoptosis and autophagy by enhancing the activity of CASP3 through inhibition of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby potentially providing a new strategy for the treatment of high-risk neuroblastoma ( 46 ). Urolithin a (UA) is the final metabolite produced by the gut microbiota from ellagitannins and ellagic acid, which are present in foods such as pomegranates and nuts. Recent studies have shown that UA has anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and mitophagy-activating effects, potentially offering benefits in the progression and complications of CKD ( 47 ). UA production depends on specific gut bacteria (such as Gordonibacter spp.), but CKD patients often experience gut dysbiosis, leading to decreased UA levels. UA may promote the growth of beneficial bacteria (such as Akkermansia muciniphila), reduce endotoxin (LPS) translocation into the bloodstream, and lower systemic inflammation ( 48 ). Toxicity tests indicate that 10-ket-12Z-octadecenoic acid does not cause liver damage or carcinogenicity, it may be promising drugs for treating CKD-aP.

In this study, we analyzed the microbiota-substrate-metabolite-target network. Our analysis revealed significant associations between IL6, 3-Indolepropionic acid, Tryptophan, Peptostreptococcus anaerobius, and Clostridium sporogenes, as well as between PPARG, 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid, Linoleic Acid, and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei. These findings suggest that these components play a substantial role in mitigating CKD-aP. There is a two-way interaction between the gut microbiota and chronic kidney disease. The buildup of toxins in CKD damages the gut barrier and disrupts the balance of gut bacteria. This gut dysbiosis, in turn, worsens kidney function and contributes to the development of related complications. Patients with CKD show a decrease in the numbers of anti-inflammatory microbes, such as those in the genera Roseburia, Prevotella, and Bacteroides. Conversely, there is an increase in pro-inflammatory microbes, including those belonging to the phyla Proteobacteria and Actinobacteria. These metabolic imbalances result in the accumulation of uremic toxins, triggering inflammatory responses and increasing oxidative stress ( 49 ). The gut microbiota of CKD-aP patients was significantly enriched for Pasteurellales, Pasteurellaceae, and Dialister, while that of non-CKD-aP patients was significantly enriched for Corynebacteriales. It is suggested that gut microbiota-host imbalance is a potential risk factor for pruritus in uremic patients ( 50 ). Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and linoleic acid (LA) are identified as key gut microbes and substances within the “M-S-M-T” network. Understanding the relationship between Lacticaseibacillus paracasei and linoleic acid is vital for developing novel therapeutic approaches for CKD-aP. LA, a long-chain fatty acid, plays significant roles in both health and disease. Its metabolism produces ceramide, a critical lipid for the skin barrier, which helps reduce transepidermal water loss. Notably, studies indicate that epidermal LA levels are reduced by 30%–50% in individuals with atopic dermatitis. LA is also instrumental in human metabolism and the immune system, with gut microbiota composition influencing immune stability. Th17cells, a T cell subset, differentiate from naive T cells upon stimulation by IL-6 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and subsequently secrete the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-17. Linoleic acid has been shown to inhibit Th17 cell differentiation while promoting Treg cell differentiation. Furthermore, LA is a crucial dietary metabolite of palmitic acid in the gut and can contribute to Th17/Treg cell imbalance ( 51 ). Linoleic acid strengthens the skin barrier by being incorporated into ceramides, modulating epidermal differentiation, and boosting lipid synthesis. Additionally, linoleic acid acts as a ligand to activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs). These PPARs play a role in various cellular processes, such as lipid metabolism, inflammation control, keratinocyte differentiation, and maintaining the integrity of the skin barrier. Disruptions in linoleic acid metabolism have been linked to the development of several skin conditions, including acne, atopic dermatitis, and psoriasis ( 52 - 54 ). Oleic acid demonstrates anti-inflammatory properties when acting on macrophages activated by Propionibacterium acnes, specifically by suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α ( 55 ). Linoleic acid is primarily generated through the metabolic activity of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei. This specific strain of Lacticaseibacillus paracasei exhibits potent anti-inflammatory properties when subjected to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) induction. Research conducted by Kwang-Soo Lee and colleagues has identified extracellular vesicles derived from this anti-inflammatory probiotic, termed LpEVs (Lacticaseibacillus paracasei extracellular vesicles), as promising anti-aging and antioxidant compounds capable of mitigating skin phenotypes and diseases associated with inflammation ( 56 ). Compared with a prior publication that explored gut-derived metabolites in general chronic kidney disease using network pharmacology ( 57 ), our work provides several key innovations. The earlier study identified a small set of metabolites (butyrate, equol, 3-indolepropionic acid and propionate) and PI3K/AKT-related pathways but did not address pruritus mechanisms or CKD-aP–specific inflammatory networks. In contrast, we specifically targeted CKD-associated pruritus, incorporated pruritus-related targets (IL-6, PPARG, CXCL8, CASP3, CYP1A1), and constructed a microbiota–substrate–metabolite–target (MSMT) framework. We also identified metabolites not previously reported—such as 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid and Genipin—and demonstrated their strong binding affinities to core pruritus-related proteins. Our findings therefore represent a novel extension of gut metabolite research into the pruritus domain, offering mechanisms and therapeutic candidates not addressed in prior CKD literature.

Several limitations of this study warrant acknowledgment. First, this investigation relies entirely on computational predictions and bioinformatics analyses without experimental validation. The metabolite-target interactions identified through molecular docking and the proposed mechanisms derived from pathway enrichment analysis require confirmation through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Second, the absence of clinical validation limits the immediate translational applicability of our findings. Future studies should incorporate animal models of CKD-aP to validate the therapeutic potential of candidate metabolites such as 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid, Genipin, and Urolithin A, as well as clinical trials to assess their safety and efficacy in human patients. Third, while the gutMGene database provides valuable microbial-metabolite associations, it may not capture the full diversity of gut microbiota-derived metabolites relevant to CKD-aP, particularly strain-specific metabolites or metabolites from underrepresented microbial populations. Fourth, individual variations in gut microbiota composition, dietary habits, genetic factors, and CKD severity may significantly influence metabolite production and bioavailability, which were not accounted for in this study. Finally, the molecular docking results, while suggestive of binding potential, do not fully capture the complexity of in vivo pharmacodynamics, including metabolite bioavailability, tissue distribution, and potential off-target effects. Despite these limitations, our study provides a comprehensive theoretical framework for understanding the role of gut microbiota metabolites in CKD-aP and identifies promising candidates for future experimental and clinical investigations.

6. Conclusion

This study employed network pharmacology approaches to explore the potential mechanisms by which gut microbiota metabolites affect CKD-aP. The findings provide further insights into the therapeutic potential of key target proteins, including those involved in the prevention of CKD-aP. The IL-17 signaling pathway may represent a promising direction for future research. Notably, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei metabolizes linoleic acid to produce a compound with anti-inflammatory effects and the ability to protect the skin barrier. This process may involve regulating the expression of the PPARG by 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid and presents potential as a probiotic intervention for CKD-aP.

Our findings reveal that gut microbial metabolites influence CKD-aP through multiple interconnected mechanisms: 1) modulation of core inflammatory targets including IL6, AKT1, and PPARG; 2) activation of immune-inflammatory signaling pathways, particularly IL-17, Toll-like receptor, and C-type lectin receptor pathways; and 3) regulation of tryptophan metabolism and its associated uremic toxins. The MSMT network analysis identified Lacticaseibacillus paracasei as a key microbial species that metabolizes linoleic acid to produce 10-keto-12Z-octadecenoic acid, which demonstrates strong binding affinity with PPARG and exhibits potential anti-inflammatory and skin barrier protective effects. These probiotic metabolites could suppress the expression of inflammatory genes and mitigate inflammation-mediated pruritus in chronic kidney disease. Future research directions should focus on: 1) experimental validation of predicted metabolite-target interactions through in vitro and in vivo studies; 2) clinical trials evaluating probiotic supplementation with Lacticaseibacillus paracasei or direct administration of candidate metabolites in CKD-aP patients; 3) investigation of optimal dosing, bioavailability, and safety profiles; and 4) exploration of personalized microbiome-based interventions considering individual variations in gut microbiota composition and CKD severity. These translational efforts may ultimately lead to novel microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies for managing this distressing complication of chronic kidney disease.
