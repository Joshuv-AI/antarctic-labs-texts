The role of serum uric acid in frailty among individuals with reduced renal function: a cross-sectional study base on CHARLS
Authors: Yuan Wang, Fan Zhang, Yi Chen, Yueyi Deng, Yifei Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Frailty is a common geriatric syndrome associated with adverse health outcomes, particularly in individuals with chronic kidney disease (CKD). However, the relationship between Serum uric acid (SUA) and frailty in individuals with reduced renal function remains unclear. This cross-sectional study utilized data from the 2015 wave of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Participants with self-reported CKD or an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² were included (N = 1,809). SUA levels were measured and categorized into tertiles (< 4.70 mg/dL, 4.70–6.03 mg/dL, and ≥ 6.03 mg/dL). Frailty status was assessed using a validated frailty index based on 30 health-related variables. Ordinal logistic regression and restricted cubic spline analysis were used to examine the association between SUA and frailty, adjusting for demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors. Higher SUA levels were significantly associated with frailty. In multivariable models, participants in the highest SUA tertile had increased odds of frailty compared to the lowest tertile (odd ratio [OR] = 1.354, 95% confidence interval [95% CI]: 1.028–1.783, P = 0.031). Restricted cubic spline analysis indicated a linear relationship between SUA and frailty index (P for overall = 0.037). Subgroup analysis showed a stronger association in participants aged < 65 years (highest vs. lowest tertile: OR = 1.872, 95% CI: 1.185–2.966, P = 0.007), while no significant association was observed in those aged ≥ 65 years (highest vs. lowest tertile: OR = 1.029, 95% CI: 0.719–1.473, P = 0.877). Elevated SUA levels are independently associated with frailty in individuals with reduced renal function, particularly among younger participants. These findings suggest that SUA may serve as a potential biomarker and therapeutic target for frailty prevention in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-16823-7.

Introduction

Frailty is a multidimensional syndrome characterized by a decline in physiological reserves and increased vulnerability to adverse health outcomes, including falls, hospitalization, and mortality1,2. It is particularly prevalent among individuals with chronic diseases, such as chronic kidney disease (CKD), where the interplay between reduced renal function and systemic metabolic disturbances exacerbates the risk of frailty3. As the global burden of CKD continues to rise, understanding the factors contributing to frailty in this population has become a critical public health priority4–6.

Serum uric acid (SUA), a metabolic byproduct of purine metabolism, has garnered increasing attention as a potential biomarker and modifiable risk factor in various chronic diseases, including CKD7. CKD alters SUA metabolism, often leading to hyperuricemia due to decreased renal excretion8. Beyond its role in gout, hyperuricemia has been implicated in the pathogenesis of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic syndrome, all of which are closely associated with frailty9. However, the relationship between SUA and frailty remains poorly understood, particularly in populations with reduced renal function.

Emerging evidence suggests that SUA may influence frailty through multiple mechanisms. Elevated SUA levels have been linked to oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are key contributors to the development of frailty10–12. Additionally, hyperuricemia may exacerbate sarcopenia, a core component of frailty, by impairing muscle protein synthesis and promoting muscle atrophy10. Despite these potential mechanisms, the association between SUA and frailty remains controversial in the literature8,13,14. Therefore, further research is needed to clarify the role of SUA in frailty, especially in the context of CKD.

In this cross-sectional study, we aim to explore the association between SUA levels and frailty among individuals with reduced renal function or self-reported CKD using data from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) wave 3. Specifically, we seek to determine whether elevated SUA levels are independently associated with frailty after adjusting for potential confounders, including demographic factors, comorbidities, and lifestyle behaviors.

Methods

This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines15.

Study population

The CHARLS is a nationally representative survey of adults aged 45 years and older in China16. The third wave of CHARLS (2015) was selected for this analysis, as it included comprehensive data on SUA levels, frailty status, and other relevant covariates. Participants with self-reported CKD or an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) based on cystatin C (eGFRcysc) below 60 mL/min/1.73 m² were included in the study. A total of 1,809 participants met these criteria and were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). Participants with missing data on SUA, frailty index, or key covariates were excluded.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and followed all relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their inclusion in the study.

Serum uric acid

SUA levels were measured from fasting blood samples collected during the CHARLS health examination. SUA was analyzed using an enzymatic colorimetric method, which is widely used for its accuracy and reliability17. SUA levels were treated as both a continuous variable and a categorical variable in the analysis. For categorical analysis, participants were divided into tertiles based on SUA levels: <4.70 mg/dL, 4.70–6.03 mg/dL, and ≥ 6.03 mg/dL. These cutoffs were determined based on the distribution of SUA levels in the study population.

Ascertainment of frailty status

Frailty status was assessed using a frailty index, which was calculated based on 30 health-related variables following the standard deficit accumulation approach18. These variables included self-reported health conditions, physical function, cognitive function, and psychosocial factors (Table S1). In our study, the frailty index was calculated by summing the number of existing deficits, dividing this total by 30, and multiplying the result by 10018. As such, the frailty index is represented as a continuous variable ranging from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating greater frailty. Participants were categorized into three frailty statuses based on established cutoffs: robust (frailty index < 10), pre-frailty (10 ≤ frailty index < 25), and frailty (frailty index ≥ 25). This classification has been validated in previous studies and is widely used in frailty research19,20.

Covariates

A wide range of covariates was included in the analysis to account for potential confounding factors. These covariates were selected based on their known associations with SUA levels and frailty. Demographic variables included age (years), gender (male or female), marital status (married or other), and education level (less than middle school, high school/vocational training, or higher education). Lifestyle factors included smoking status (never, past, or current smoker) and alcohol consumption (never, past, or current drinker).

Anthropometric measurements included body mass index (BMI), calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m²). Clinical variables included the presence of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, and heart disease, all of which were self-reported based on physician diagnoses. Laboratory biomarkers included fasting blood glucose, serum creatinine, cystatin C, hemoglobin A1c, and C-reactive protein. eGFRcysc was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation based on cystatin C levels21. All laboratory measurements were performed in certified laboratories using standardized protocols. Given the relatively low rates of missing data (Table S2), single imputation was employed to address missing values, with continuous variables imputed using the median and categorical variables imputed using the most frequent category22.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX) and R software 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population. Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) due to their non-normal distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Differences across SUA tertile were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Ordinal logistic regression was performed to examine the association between SUA tertiles and frailty status (robust, pre-frailty, frailty). Both univariable and multivariable models were constructed. The multivariable model adjusted for age, gender, marital status, education, smoking, drinking, BMI, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, heart disease, cystatin C, hemoglobin A1c, C-reactive protein, serum creatinine, fasting blood glucose, and baseline eGFRcysc. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were reported for each SUA tertile, with the lowest tertile serving as the reference group. The corrected variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to detect collinearity between variables (VIF greater than five means significantly collinearity) (Table S3).

Restricted cubic spline regression was used to explore the potential nonlinear relationship between SUA levels (continuous variable) and frailty index (continuous variable). The restricted cubic spline model included three knots placed at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of SUA levels. The overall significance of the association was assessed using the P-value for the model, while the P-value for nonlinearity was used to determine whether the relationship deviated from linearity.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings. First, the analysis was repeated after excluding participants with extreme SUA levels (top and bottom 5%). Second, the association between SUA and frailty was examined using SUA as a continuous variable in the logistic regression model. Third, potential interactions between SUA and key covariates (e.g., age, gender, BMI) were tested by including interaction terms in the regression models. Four, we excluded participants with missing covariate data to evaluate whether the results were influenced by incomplete data. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of included participants

Participants were grouped into tertiles based on SUA levels: <4.70 mg/dL (N = 651), 4.70–6.03 mg/dL (N = 555), and ≥ 6.03 mg/dL (N = 603) (Table 1). The median age of participants increased across tertiles (64.0, 66.0, and 69.0 years, respectively; P < 0.001). The proportion of males was significantly higher in the highest SUA tertile (65.7%) compared to the lowest tertile (36.3%; P < 0.001). Educational attainment differed across groups, with participants in the highest tertile more likely to have higher education (3.2%) compared to the lowest tertile (0.8%; P = 0.009). Marital status was similar across groups (P = 0.812). Smoking and drinking behaviors varied significantly: participants in the highest tertile were more likely to be past or current smokers and drinkers compared to the lowest tertile (P < 0.001 for both). BMI and the prevalence of hypertension were significantly higher in the highest tertile (median BMI: 23.8 kg/m²; hypertension: 55.2%) compared to the lowest tertile (median BMI: 23.2 kg/m²; hypertension: 37.9%; both P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in the prevalence of hyperlipidemia, diabetes, or heart disease among the groups. Blood glucose, serum creatinine, C-reactive protein, hemoglobin A1c, and cystatin C levels were all higher in the highest tertile (P < 0.001 for all). The frailty index was slightly lower in the middle and highest tertiles compared to the lowest tertile (P = 0.026). eGFRcysc decreased progressively across tertiles, with median values of 91.0, 69.6, and 53.9 mL/min/1.73 m² for the lowest, middle, and highest tertiles, respectively (P < 0.001).

The ordinal logistic regression analysis revealed that SUA levels were significantly associated with frailty status (Table 2). In the univariable model, participants in the second (4.70–6.03 mg/dL) and highest (≥ 6.03 mg/dL) SUA tertiles had higher odds of frailty compared to the lowest tertile (< 4.70 mg/dL), with ORs of 1.255 (95% CI: 1.012–1.557, P = 0.039) and 1.317 (95% CI: 1.065–1.629, P = 0.011), respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, the association remained significant for the highest tertile (OR = 1.354, 95% CI: 1.028–1.783, P = 0.031), while the middle tertile showed no significant association (OR = 1.169, 95% CI: 0.917–1.490, P = 0.208).

The restricted cubic spline analysis, using frailty index as the dependent variable and serum uric acid as the independent variable, showed a significant overall association (P for overall = 0.037). However, no significant nonlinear relationship was observed (P for nonlinear = 0.220), indicating a primarily linear association (Fig. 1).

In sensitivity analyses, excluding participants in the bottom 5% of SUA levels showed no significant associations between SUA tertiles and frailty after adjustment (middle tertile: OR = 0.934, 95% CI: 0.744–1.172, P = 0.556; highest tertile: OR = 1.205, 95% CI: 0.929–1.565, P = 0.160) (Table S4). Similarly, excluding the top 5% yielded non-significant results (middle tertile: OR = 0.895, 95% CI: 0.711–1.127, P = 0.346; highest tertile: OR = 1.000, 95% CI: 0.768–1.302, P = 0.998) (Table S5). When treated SUA as a continuous variable, the association remained significantly (OR = 1.096, 95% CI: 1.020–1.178, P = 0.013) (Table S6). Interaction analysis revealed a significant interaction with age (P = 0.016), but not with gender (P = 0.547) or BMI (P = 0.552). The complete case analysis likewise showed a significant association between the highest tertile of SUA and frailty (highest vs. lowest tertile: OR = 1.521, 95% CI: 1.125–2.058, P = 0.007) (Table S7). Given the significant interaction of SUA with age, we further performed subgroup analyses for age (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years).

In the subgroup analyses stratified by age, the association between SUA and frailty was stronger in participants aged < 65 years (Table 3). In this group, the highest SUA tertile was significantly associated with frailty in both univariable (OR = 1.833, 95% CI: 1.302–2.584, P < 0.001) and multivariable models (OR = 1.872, 95% CI: 1.185–2.966, P = 0.007). However, among participants aged ≥ 65 years, no significant associations were observed for middle or highest in multivariable models. Further restricted cubic spline curves are shown in Figure S2. As a supplement, we performed subgroup analyses by gender, BMI (< 28, ≥ 28 kg/m2), and eGFRcysc level (< 45, ≥ 45 mL/min/1.73 m²). The association between higher SUA and frailty was consistent across these subgroups (Table S8-Table S10).

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study based on data from the CHARLS, we investigated the association between SUA levels and frailty among individuals with reduced renal function. Our findings revealed that elevated SUA levels were significantly associated with frailty status, even after adjusting for a wide range of demographic, clinical, and lifestyle factors. These results provide important insights into the potential role of SUA as a biomarker and modifiable risk factor for frailty in individuals with impaired renal function.

Interpretation of findings

Our study demonstrated a positive association between higher SUA levels and frailty, with participants in the highest SUA tertile (≥ 6.03 mg/dL) exhibiting significantly higher odds of frailty compared to those in the lowest tertile (< 4.70 mg/dL). This association persisted after adjusting for potential confounders, suggesting that SUA may independently contribute to frailty risk. These findings are aligned with previous studies that have reported a link between hyperuricemia and frailty in older adults23,24. The restricted cubic spline analysis further supported a linear relationship between SUA levels and frailty index, indicating that even modest increases in SUA may be associated with greater frailty.

The mechanisms underlying the observed association are likely multifactorial. Elevated SUA levels have been implicated in oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, all of which are key contributors to frailty8. Hyperuricemia may also exacerbate sarcopenia, a core component of frailty, by impairing muscle protein synthesis and promoting muscle atrophy25. Additionally, SUA has been linked to metabolic disturbances, such as insulin resistance and hypertension, which may further increase frailty risk26–28. These mechanisms are particularly relevant in individuals with reduced renal function, as impaired kidney function can lead to the accumulation of SUA and exacerbate its deleterious effects.

Interestingly, our subgroup analysis revealed that the association between SUA and frailty was stronger in participants aged < 65 years compared to those aged ≥ 65 years. This finding suggests that younger individuals with elevated SUA levels may be more vulnerable to the adverse effects of hyperuricemia on frailty. One possible explanation is that younger individuals with reduced renal function may have a longer duration of exposure to elevated SUA levels, leading to cumulative damage over time. Alternatively, age-related physiological changes in older adults may attenuate the impact of SUA on frailty. Further research is needed to explore these age-related differences and their implications for clinical practice.

Comparison with previous studies

Our findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating a positive association between SUA and frailty. For example, García-Esquinas et al.23 conducted a 3.5-year follow-up study involving 2,198 participants aged 60 years or older. After multivariate adjustment, the ORs for frailty risk associated with the second and third tertiles of SUA levels, compared to the lowest tertile, were 1.18 (95% CI: 0.83–1.68) and 1.57 (95% CI: 1.11–2.22), respectively. For each 1 mg/dL increase in SUA concentration, the risk of frailty increased by 1.12-fold (95% CI: 1.00-1.24). Similarly, a cross-sectional study in a Korea cohort reported that higher SUA levels were significantly associated with frailty among older people (≥ 65 years)24. However, our study observed a U-shaped relationship in the subgroup population aged ≥ 65 years, while the results from Kang et al.24 indicated a predominantly linear relationship between the two. This discrepancy may be due to differences in study populations, frailty assessment methods, or statistical approaches.

In contrast to some studies that have suggested a protective effect of SUA in certain populations, particularly those with advanced age or severe frailty29, our results indicate that higher SUA levels are associated with greater frailty risk. These differences may reflect the complex and context-dependent effects of SUA, which can act as both an antioxidant and a pro-oxidant depending on the physiological environment30. In individuals with reduced renal function, the pro-oxidant effects of SUA may predominate, contributing to the observed association with frailty.

Clinical implications

The findings of this study have important clinical implications. First, SUA may serve as a useful biomarker for identifying individuals with reduced renal functions who are at increased risk of frailty. Routine measurement of SUA levels in clinical practice could help guide risk stratification and early intervention efforts. Second, our results highlight the potential value of interventions aimed at lowering SUA levels, such as dietary modifications, lifestyle changes, or pharmacological treatments (e.g., xanthine oxidase inhibitors). While the efficacy of SUA-lowering therapies in reducing frailty risk remains uncertain, our findings provide a rationale for further investigation in this area.

It is also important to consider the broader context of frailty management in individuals with reduced renal function. Frailty is a multifactorial syndrome that requires a comprehensive approach to prevention and treatment. In addition to addressing SUA levels, interventions should target other modifiable risk factors, such as physical inactivity, poor nutrition, and comorbid conditions. Multidisciplinary care models that integrate nephrology, geriatrics, and rehabilitation services may be particularly effective in mitigating frailty risk in this population.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of this study limits its ability to establish causality between SUA levels and frailty. While we observed a significant association between SUA and frailty, it remains unclear whether elevated SUA levels contribute to frailty or whether frailty itself leads to increased SUA levels. Longitudinal studies are required to verify the causal nature of this relationship. Second, despite adjustment for multiple covariates (age, sex, BMI, and chronic conditions), residual confounding cannot be excluded. Important determinants of both SUA and frailty-such as physical activity, detailed dietary intake (purines, fructose, alcohol), and medications that modify uric acid levels (urate-lowering therapy, diuretics)-were not comprehensively captured in CHARLS 2015 and could bias our estimates. The likely direction of bias is uncertain and may vary by factor (e.g., lower physical activity could raise SUA and increase frailty risk, potentially inflating the association), so our results should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in datasets with richer lifestyle and medication data. Third, SUA levels were measured only once in this study, which may not accurately reflect long-term fluctuations in uric acid levels. SUA levels can be influenced by short-term factors, such as recent dietary intake, medication use, or acute health conditions, potentially introducing measurement error. Fourth, the frailty index used in this study relies on self-reported health and functional status, which may introduce reporting bias. Participants might overestimate or underestimate their health conditions or functional impairments. Additionally, while the frailty index is a widely used measure, the specific variables included in its calculation may vary across studies, potentially affecting the comparability of results. Fifth, selection bias may have influenced our findings. This study included only participants from CHARLS with complete data, which may have excluded individuals with poorer health or missing data, leading to an underestimation of the association between SUA and frailty. Furthermore, the study population was limited to adults aged 45 years and older in China, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other age groups or populations with different ethnic or cultural backgrounds. Sixth, inconsistencies in sensitivity analysis highlight potential limitations in the robustness of our findings. For instance, when extreme SUA values (top and bottom 5%) were excluded, the association between SUA and frailty was no longer significant. This suggests that outliers may have disproportionately influenced the results, warranting cautious interpretation of the findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence of a significant association between elevated SUA levels and frailty among individuals with reduced renal function. These findings underscore the potential role of SUA as a biomarker and modifiable risk factor for frailty in this population. Further research is needed to confirm these findings, elucidate underlying mechanisms, and evaluate the efficacy of SUA-lowering interventions in reducing frailty risk.
