First report of bilateral avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral heads in a patient with dual factor V deficiency and factor V Leiden mutation: A unique coagulopathy intersection with therapeutic insights
Authors: Wei Li, Huibo Li, Xixi Dou, Deqiang Zheng, Chengyao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present what appears to be the first documented case worldwide of bilateral avascular necrosis (AVN) of the femoral heads in a young adult with the rare co-occurrence of congenital factor V (FV) deficiency and heterozygous FV Leiden mutation.

Case presentation

A 27-year-old, previously healthy man presented with a 2-month history of progressively worsening bilateral hip pain and restricted range of motion. He had no history of trauma, corticosteroid use, alcohol abuse, sickle cell disease or autoimmune disorder. Radiographic and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) confirmed advanced avascular necrosis (AVN) of both femoral heads, with evidence of structural collapse and deformity (Fig. 1A–E). Both hips were classified as ARCO stage IV. Further haematological analysis confirmed congenital factor V (FV) deficiency, with FV activity measured at just 2.7% (confirmed twice; mixing study corrected, ruling out an inhibitor). Liver function, platelet count, fibrinogen and D-dimer were normal, excluding acquired causes. Given the patient’s significant functional impairment and imaging findings, right total hip arthroplasty was scheduled (Fig. 1F–K). To manage bleeding risk, 20 units of cryoprecipitate were administered preoperatively and daily for 4 postoperative days, after which aPTT normalised to a mean of 42.30 s. Intraoperative blood loss was 350 mL with no excessive bleeding. Postoperative genetic analysis unexpectedly revealed the presence of a heterozygous FV Leiden mutation. FV Leiden is the most common inherited cause of thrombophilia and results from a point mutation (G1691A) in the FV gene (Fig. 1L). This mutation renders FV resistant to inactivation by activated protein C,1 leading to sustained thrombin generation, a hypercoagulable state, and an increased risk of venous thromboembolism.2 We propose that, despite the classical bleeding phenotype associated with the patient’s coexisting FV deficiency, the presence of the FV Leiden mutation introduced a subtle but chronic prothrombotic tendency. No other cause for AVN was identified (normal haemoglobin electrophoresis, negative ANA/ANCA).

Discussion

The femoral heads are uniquely susceptible to ischaemic injury due to their dependence on a limited number of end-arterial branches with minimal collateral blood supply.3 In this anatomically vulnerable context, even minor disturbances in microvascular flow – such as those caused by low-grade or subclinical thrombotic events – may be sufficient to trigger bone infarction.4 The contralateral hip (also ARCO IV) is managed conservatively with regular follow‑up; left THA will be offered if symptoms progress. In young patients (<30 years), THA carries a higher lifetime revision risk, but modern bearings have improved survivorship;5 the patient was fully counselled accordingly.

To our knowledge, no prior cases have reported avascular necrosis (AVN) in a patient with the rare coexistence of congenital FV deficiency and the FV Leiden mutation. This case broadens the clinical spectrum of coagulation disorders and highlights the importance of comprehensive thrombophilia screening, even in patients traditionally considered to be at elevated bleeding risk. It also raises the possibility that genetic interactions affecting coagulation pathways may play an underrecognised role in the pathogenesis of osteonecrosis.

Thrombophilia testing in this case was prompted by the unexplained prolonged aPTT and absence of typical risk factors, not as a routine screen. We suggest that a targeted thrombophilia screen (factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, antiphospholipid antibodies, protein C/S) may be considered after haematology consultation in young (<50 years) patients with idiopathic bilateral or multifocal AVN or an unexplained coagulation abnormality. However, routine screening is not recommended, and the clinical utility remains uncertain.

Conclusion

This first reported case of bilateral AVN in a patient with coexisting congenital FV deficiency and FV Leiden mutation highlights a potential thrombophilic contribution to AVN in young patients without classic risk factors. Clinicians should consider targeted thrombophilia screening in such atypical cases after haematology consultation.
