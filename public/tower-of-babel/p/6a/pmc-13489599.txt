Reappraising the Evidence for Term-Equivalent Age MRI Superiority in Preterm Infants
Authors: Mehmet N. Cizmeci, Frances M. Cowan, Linda S. de Vries
Published: 2026

With their meta-analysis, Bonezzi et al. [1] ask an important question: whether, in a preterm infant, a brain MRI performed early in the neonatal period or at term equivalent age (TEA) is more predictive of neurodevelopmental outcome at 12–36 months corrected age. The investigators concluded that the TEA-MRI offers higher predictivity than the earlier MRI. The study is the first to directly compare early and TEA-MRI across both motor and non-motor neurodevelopmental outcomes and integrates conventional structural MRI scoring with advanced volumetric and diffusion tensor imaging biomarkers. However, several important limitations weaken the strength of these conclusions and require consideration for clinical application.

Early versus TEA-MRI

There are several reasons why one would consider performing an early MRI. In a preterm infant with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy, an MRI performed during the diffusion window enhances recognition of deep grey nuclei injury. In large periventricular haemorrhagic infarction or severe cerebellar haemorrhage, it provides detailed information about additional injury to the contralesional hemisphere and whether the vermis is also involved. In cases with severe inhomogeneous echogenicity, diffusion-weighted imaging shows the extent of white matter injury (WMI) and additionally whether there is involvement of the corticospinal tracts. In all such infants, these early scan findings may help to decide whether the injury is severe enough to consider redirection of care and when it is, these infants will then not have a TEA-MRI, so the most severe cases would be missing for comparison [2, 3]. In infants with milder WMI, punctate white matter lesions will be easily recognized with diffusion-weighted imaging during the first week after an insult and be more abundant on the early MRI [4, 5].

In some of the studies in this meta-analysis, the early MRI was performed as late as 35–36 weeks postmenstrual age (PMA). In an infant born at 32 weeks’ gestation, this could possibly still be considered early, but in an infant born at 26 weeks who was not considered stable enough for an MRI for many weeks, it is hard to still consider an MRI at 34–36 weeks PMA as “early.” In a study from their own group assessing outcomes at 6 years corrected age, Mistry et al. [6] assessed 123 preterm infants with an early MRI at a mean PMA of 32.5 weeks and 114 of these infants also had a TEA-MRI. The global brain abnormality score had high specificity and low sensitivity on both early and TEA-MRI in predicting motor impairment and a high sensitivity and specificity in predicting cerebral palsy. A recent study by Selvanathan et al. [7] compared an early and TEA-MRI in 393 preterm infants. Moderate-severe WMI was present in 57 of these infants. Notably, moderate-severe and anterior WMI on early MRI, but not on TEA-MRI, was independently associated with suboptimal motor and cognitive scores at 36 months, directly challenging the conclusion of this meta-analysis.

MRI at TEA has the advantage of revealing features of dysmaturation and myelination [8]. Punctate white matter lesions will however be less abundant and cysts in the white matter may no longer be visible, with glial changes and ex-vacuo dilatation seen instead [5]. At TEA, a brain injury scoring system can be performed as was used in the study by Bonezzi et al. [1]. However, an MRI can be called TEA but is often performed when the infant is discharged from the NICU, at 36–37 weeks PMA, which is too early to assess myelination of the PLIC. It is worth noting that in the present meta-analysis, for intraventricular haemorrhage specifically, sensitivity was lower at TEA (26%) than at early MRI (35.6%), which is not surprising because of the evolution of these lesions over time. This underscores that the role of TEA-MRI is not uniform across all types of brain.

Limitations of the Meta-Analysis

One of the limitations of the present study is that only 9 of the 30 included studies reported extractable diagnostic accuracy data at both so-called early and late time points. Therefore, comparative analyses relied on a combination of paired and indirect approaches, which may have affected the results due to differences in population characteristics. When the analysis was restricted to paired data from the same cohorts, the superiority of TEA-MRI over early MRI became marginal and statistically non-significant. The TEA superiority seen in the indirect comparison likely reflects differences between cohorts, rather than a true prognostic advantage of imaging timing. Another limitation is that neurodevelopmental outcome was assessed at 12–36 months corrected age, which is quite early, especially when assessing cognitive outcome. It is well established that higher order cognitive disorders only become apparent at school age [9, 10].

In the present study, early MRIs were conducted closer to 35–36 weeks in most infants and cannot really be called early. It would have been helpful if the authors had divided their data into maturity subgroups as 3–4 weeks in a 26-week infant is different from the same postnatal age in a 32-week infant. Furthermore, the infants who had the earlier MRI were likely more stable and less severely injured and therefore could be taken to the scanner, while those who were less stable but with more injury were scanned later, making prediction of outcome at that later age easier. The authors themselves do acknowledge a number of limitations to their study, including major heterogeneity in MRI timing, and also variable MRI protocols, inconsistent lesion definitions, variability in lesion scoring systems across studies, varying outcome measures, a small number of studies for each biomarker, a predominance of qualitative over quantitative MRI measures, and potential selection bias introduced by exclusion of infants with large parenchymal haemorrhages in several included studies.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis, while addressing an important and clinically relevant question, is limited by the heterogeneous and frequently non-early timing of the early MRI and by the interpretation of indirect comparisons of the main findings. Its most important analysis, the paired comparison, shows only marginal and non-significant differences between early and TEA-MRI. Therefore, the conclusion that TEA-MRI is superior must be interpreted with considerable caution.
