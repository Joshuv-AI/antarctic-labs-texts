Magnetic Resonance Imaging Radiomic Analysis of Radiation-Induced Morphea of the Breast: A Proof-of-Concept Study
Authors: Jieying Wu, Joseph Bae, Chao Chen, Samuel Ryu, Daniel Lozeau, Alexander Stessin, Prateek Prasanna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeRadiation-induced morphea (RIM) is a very rare but devastating side effect of breast radiation therapy, characterized by progressive skin induration, pain, and discoloration, with no effective treatments currently available. This is a proof-of-concept study that aims to identify radiomic features from pretreatment magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans associated with the development of RIM in patients with breast cancer undergoing radiation therapy.Methods and MaterialsThis is a retrospective analysis of a single institutional registry of patients who received diagnosis of RIM following breast radiation therapy from 2008 to 2022. Clinical and histopathological data were reviewed. Pretreatment MRI scans of these patients and matched controls were analyzed. Radiomic features were extracted from whole breast and fibroglandular tissue regions of interest. A total of 528 radiomic features were compared between patients who developed RIM and those who did not, using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to identify statistically significant differences.ResultsWe evaluated 10 patients who received clinical diagnosis of RIM, with a mean age of 63 years (range, 44-75 years). Among these, 7 patients had biopsy-proven RIM. Both clinical and histologic findings were correlated with radiomic analyses. Forty percent of the patients had a history of autoimmune disorders, including hypothyroidism, Graves’ disease, systemic sclerosis, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Radiomic analysis identified 11 significant features, primarily related to tissue structure and texture. Nine of these features were from the contralateral breast, and 2 were from the ipsilateral breast.ConclusionsThis is a pilot study on a small sample that demonstrates that radiomic features extracted from pretreatment MRI scans can serve as potential predictors for the development of RIM in patients with breast cancer. The integration of clinical and histopathological data with radiomic analysis highlights the distinct changes in breast tissue architecture that precede RIM onset. These findings pave the way for the early identification of patients at risk, allowing for more personalized surveillance and management strategies.

Introduction

Adjuvant radiation therapy (RT) is the standard of care for most patients who received diagnosis of localized breast cancer undergoing breast conservation therapy. The majority of side effects of RT are considered relatively mild. Acute toxicities may include skin erythema, edema, and desquamation. Patients can be expected to have some persistent breast edema and fibrosis in the subacute period while long-term sequelae may include telangiectasia, hyperpigmentation, and induration. However, most of these do not impact patient quality of life. In fact, studies have shown that almost 90% of patients continue to report excellent or good global cosmetic outcomes compared to baseline at 3, 5, and 10 years after radiation.1

Radiation-induced morphea (RIM) is a rare but very severe side effect of breast RT.2 It has an evolving clinical presentation characterized by initial erythema and edema and later transitions to progressive induration, violaceous skin discoloration, and pain.3 While very few patients experience RIM, it can progressively cause devastating symptoms with no currently available effective treatments. Several studies estimate RIM incidence rates range from 1 in 500 to 1 in 3000 cases.4, 5, 6 However, these studies may underestimate its prevalence; 1 cohort of 5129 patients who underwent RT had a cumulative incidence of 1 in 378.7 The etiology and risk factors for RIM are poorly understood. No studies have yet validated predictive clinical, histologic, or radiological biomarkers predisposing patients to RIM, and to date, there are no established guidelines to diagnose or treat RIM. Given the rarity of this complication of breast irradiation as well as its severity, studying RIM and potential predictive biomarkers presents both an unmet need and a difficult challenge.

Radiomic features quantify subvisual characteristics in images and capture heterogeneity in image structure and texture. Radiomic features from magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans have been studied in the context of multiple cancers and have been demonstrated to correlate with multiple radiation-induced toxicities.8, 9, 10 Nearly all patients with breast cancer receive multiple medical imaging studies; radiomic features have been studied from mammography, ultrasonography, and MRI imaging to diagnose breast cancers and model patient outcomes following treatment.11, 12, 13 In these studies, researchers have identified textural and structural imaging features correlated with patient response to radiation and other therapies, enabling high-accuracy predictions for tumor recurrence and radiation-induced toxicities. These studies have also revealed that radiomic features specific to fibroglandular tissue (FGT) in the breast were often predictive of breast cancer outcomes, reflecting previously established characteristics of the role of breast density in breast cancer development and progression.

Although numerous studies have explored radiomic feature expression in breast cancer imaging, none have specifically focused on patients who developed RIM following RT. Because RIM is a condition of healthy tissue rather than cancerous tissues, we believe that it is important to study radiomic features from whole-breast and FGT regions of interest (ROIs) and that such features may be most correlated with RIM development. Specifically, we hypothesize that radiomic characterization of whole breast and FGT ROIs pretreatment MRI images will reveal meaningful differences in radiomic feature expression between patients with breast cancer who developed RIM following RT and those who did not.

We report a single institutional study of RIM cases, with full characterization of computational radiomic profiles for MRI images obtained from these patients as well as describing common clinical histories. We analyzed pretreatment imaging from patients with breast cancer and found differences in radiomic feature expression between those who developed RIM and those who did not. This is proof-of-concept evidence of predictive radiological biomarkers for RIM development in patients with breast cancer.

Methods and Materials

Data curation

We curated a database of all women who received diagnosis of RIM (clinical and/or biopsy-proven) following breast radiation at our institution from 2008 to 2022. Patients' medical history, treatment plan, and imaging were retrospectively reviewed. Our clinical diagnostic criteria for RIM included breast shrinkage, extensive induration of breast, and associated skin discoloration. Our pathologic diagnostic criteria included lymphocytic infiltration, prominent fibrosis, and collagen fibers.

For the initial RIM database, we selected patients with diagnostic pretreatment MRI imaging for radiomic analysis. The pretreatment MRI scans were selected to ensure they were acquired before the initiation of any form of systemic therapy, surgical intervention, or RT. This study adhered to ethical guidelines and was conducted with due regard for patient privacy and confidentiality. All procedures were performed in accordance with institutional regulations and ethical standards after approval from institutional review board.

Image acquisition

Pretreatment MRI images were first obtained from the institutional picture archiving and communications system for each patient with breast cancer who developed RIM. MRI images for patients with breast cancer who did not develop RIM were used as a negative control group. These control group patients were selected to match the RIM group in terms of age, breast cancer type, medical history, and radiation treatment dose (Table 1). The mean interval between MRI acquisition and radiation start date was 91 days. This duration reflects the standard care sequence: initial MRI at diagnosis, followed by biopsy, surgery, and recovery before starting RT. Image preprocessing and segmentation

Following image export, each scan was resampled to a 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm isotropic volume. Interpolation was performed using nearest neighbor resampling. Detailed information regarding the average pixel spacing and slice thickness of the studied scans is provided in Table E1. Pixel spacing was on average less than 1 mm whereas slice thickness was on average greater than 1 mm. A pre-existing V-Net model was then used to segment whole breast and FGT for each patient image using VIBRANT (Volume Image Breast Assessment) and T1 sequences as were available. Segmentations were visually inspected and manually corrected as necessary. To correct for intensity variation between patient MRIs, each scan was z-score normalized within breast segmentations. Specifically, the intensity value of each voxel within the breasts was normalized by subtracting the mean intensity and dividing by the SD of all intensity values within the breast. Radiomic feature extraction

Radiomic features were extracted from short tau inversion recovery and VIBRANT (Volume Image Breast Assessment) MRI sequences because these were the sequences in common among all the patients studied. The PyRadiomics software package was used to extract 3D radiomic features from both whole breast and FGT ROIs for both breasts for each patient. Studied features included those measuring image intensity, ROI morphology, and texture. Textural features were calculated from several gray-level image matrices including the gray-level size zone matrix which quantifies grouped regions of similar gray-level intensity, the gray-level run length matrix which quantifies persistent linear patterns in gray-level intensity, the gray-level co-occurrence matrix which captures heterogeneity in gray-level intensity, the gray-level tone difference matrix which measures contrasts in gray-level intensities, and the gray-level dependence matrix which captures the relationship between neighboring voxels’ gray-level intensities. Each of these features has been extensively studied in the context of medical imaging for patients with breast cancer. For Radiomics Quality Score 2.0, we report a total score of 14 of 17 (82%).

Statistical analysis

To identify which radiomic features may be correlated with RIM development, radiomic features with statistically significant differences in expression between patients who developed RIM and the matched RIM-negative controls were identified. To reduce the probability of identifying spurious correlations, features with high correlation (Pearson correlation coefficient >0.9) to one another were removed. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was then leveraged to analyze the association between radiomic feature expression and RIM development. Specifically, for each combination of ROI (whole breast, FGT) and breast side (ipsilateral, contralateral), radiomic features with statistically significant (alpha of 0.05) differences in expression between RIM and control patients were recorded.

Results

Clinical features

From January 2008 to December 2022, 10 patients developed RIM following breast RT at our institution. Of these patients, 1 received simultaneous bilateral radiation for synchronous bilateral breast cancer but developed RIM in only the left breast. Key clinical features of RIM observed in our clinic included significant breast shrinkage, extensive induration, and tenderness. All of these occurred not immediately after completion of RT but following a latency period during which acute dermatitis had resolved and the irradiated breast had appeared to be healing well. Then, at the time of RIM diagnosis, the irradiated breast was found to be considerably fibrotic on palpation and exhibited purple discoloration. With subsequent follow-up visits, the fibrosis in the affected breast softened on palpation or, at least, stabilized over the next 3-5 years for most but not all of the patients. Notably, one of the patients, whose symptoms continued to worsen, finally underwent total mastectomy as treatment for the RIM.

The cohort had a mean age of 63 years (range, 44-75 years). Mean latency of onset of RIM from last radiation treatment was 24.15 months (range, 5.3-59.6 months). In our study, there were no correlations observed between age, smoking history, body mass index, radiation dosage, or fractionation with the occurrence of RIM. Forty percent of patients had a documented history of autoimmune disorders. Hypothyroidism was the most prevalent autoimmune condition, present in 2 of 4 patients. The other autoimmune diseases included Graves’ disease, limited cutaneous systemic sclerosis, and systemic lupus erythematosus. Patients with autoimmune disorders did not have clinically more severe RIM than the other patients. Histopathological findings

Ten patients had a clinical diagnosis; 7 of these patients opted for biopsy with pathology consistent with histologic descriptions for RIM based on recent literature.15 Key pathologic features in our cohort include lymphocytic infiltrate, thickened collagen bundles, scattered or plump fibroblasts, and perivascular or sclerosing dermatitis.

Radiomic analysis

Seven patients in the cohort had pretreatment MRI scans as part of the evaluation process, and these images were used for radiomic analysis. In this study, we extracted 528 radiomic features from pretreatment MRIs to identify potential predictors of RIM. Among these 528, 11 radiomic features were found to be statistically significant predictors (P < .05) for the development of RIM. Nine radiomic features were from the breast contralateral to the RIM breast, and 2 radiomic features were from the breast ipsilateral to the RIM breast. To evaluate the predictive value of the studied radiomic features, machine learning classifiers were trained and evaluated in a leave-one-out cross-validation setting to predict RIM development following RT. To do so, radiomic features from each of the ROIs and MRI sequences studied were studied both individually and collectively. Before model training, highly correlated features (Pearson’s correlation coefficient >0.9) were pruned. The maximum relevance minimum redundancy feature selection algorithm was used to select features with the most information among training set samples for each round of cross-validation. The minority class (RIM+ patients) was also oversampled among the training samples. The entire data set was bootstrapped for 100 iterations to improve model robustness. This allowed the construction of CIs to assess statistical differences in performances between trained models. Clinical features of autoimmune status were also used for comparative analysis. Discussion

The first report of RIM was published in 1989 as a small series of 9 patients.16 In all 9, morphea began in the radiation field, and in a subset of 4 patients, it extended slowly beyond the treated area. It was found that radiation dose or severity of acute radiation toxicity did not correlate with the development of RIM. Since then, none of the published reports have elucidated the risk factors for RIM.17 Many practicing clinicians may be unfamiliar with it and are often baffled by its dramatic presentation. When a diagnosis is made, there is nothing that can be offered therapeutically. In this study, we sought to increase the awareness of this condition among practitioners and provide an analysis technique that will help preselect patients with predisposition for RIM development for increased surveillance.

Our study used radiomic analysis of pretreatment MRI images from patients with RIM. We found 11 radiomic features that significantly differed from those of matched controls. We believe that histologic changes specific to RIM are reflected more macroscopically as changes in breast tissue architecture that may be represented by subtle, subvisual changes on breast imaging identified via radiomic analysis. We also found that radiomic features were inconsistent across both breasts of the same patient. Because of this, extracting features from both breasts provided radiomic data pertinent to RIM.

Of 11 predictive radiomic features, primarily related to tissue structure and texture, 9 were detected in the breast contralateral to the breast that eventually developed RIM. It is interesting that the breast contralateral to RIM+ breast had more predictive radiomic features than the RIM+ breast itself. One potential explanation is that in the RIM+ breast, which underwent radiation and subsequently developed RIM, these features might be “masked” by features of peritumoral inflammation, hindering their consistent expression and predictability for RIM. Consequently, the RIM+ breast failed to provide consistent predictive radiomic feature expression because of the impact of tumor growth. In contrast, the contralateral breast, which remains tumor free, reflects the patient’s normal architectural characteristics, allowing the detection of distinct radiomic features. Notably, this was consistently the same for 1 patient who had bilateral breast irradiation. This suggests that with identical clinical and radiation parameters for both breasts, the development of RIM in only 1 breast may be attributed to variations in breast tissue characteristics.

In literature, several potential risk factors for RIM including patient age, RT parameters, and history of systemic sclerosis have been investigated but failed to correlate with RIM development.18 The potential role of autoimmunity in the pathogenesis of RIM has been brought up in several case reports and small case series.19, 20, 21 Similarly, in our study, nearly half of the patients had a history of autoimmune disease. Given the small sample size and paucity of studies, the role of autoimmunity has yet to be established.

The discrepancy in the existing literature regarding the true incidence of RIM is striking with estimates varying from 1 in 378 patients to 1 in 3000 patients. Other than clinicians’ unfamiliarity with this rare condition, some variability in presentation may also be contributory. From our thorough review of the existing literature, it remains unclear whether RIM should be considered a simple all-or-none clinical entity or graded based on its severity. In fact, none of the prior studies seem to have considered this question. From clinical surveillance of our patient cohort, it appears that RIM does, indeed, display some variability both in terms of its clinical severity and its eventual clinical course, with some but not all cases improving or, at least, stabilizing over time. While correlation of radiomic features with clinical severity of RIM is beyond the scope of our small cohort, proof-of-concept study, such efforts may be undertaken in the future if a multi-institutional image repository is created as a means to gather a larger case cohort for this rare disease entity.

Clinical and histopathological features of RIM distinguishing it from other long-term radiation effects like chronic radiation dermatitis and postirradiation fibrosis are equally important. In RIM, early timepoint histology shows lymphocytic infiltration around blood vessels and adnexal structures, while late phase is marked by the absence of adnexal fat tissue, increased lymphocytic infiltration, prominent fibrosis, and sclerotized collagen fibers.22 The absence of atypical fibroblasts in RIM distinguishes it from chronic radiation dermatitis.23,24 These subtle early changes in breast parenchyma can be picked up through radiomics. Clinically, a characteristic latency period may also be used to distinguish RIM from the other late sequelae of breast irradiation.

Our ability to detect radiomic features from the contralateral breast highlights a reference point for understanding RIM development. This has the potential to serve as an early predictor of RIM. To our knowledge, this is the first study to correlate RIM with radiomic attributes in a population of patients with breast cancer treated with adjuvant RT. While the findings presented contribute valuable insights into the potential association between radiomic features and RIM, the study has several limitations inherent in its design, most notably the small sample size and the retrospective nature of the data collection. The limited number of patients may also result in skewed correlations between radiomic features and RIM development.

Despite these limitations, this proof-of-principle pilot study provides a foundation for further research into the relationship between radiomic breast features and RIM development. Incorporating machine learning techniques, such as logistic regression or random forest algorithms, could provide a more systematic approach to evaluating the predictive utility of the 11 radiomic features analyzed. These methods can help identify model weights and assess the predictive value of each feature, potentially improving both statistical robustness and generalizability. For this rare condition with significant morbidity where large prospective studies are not feasible, this approach may prove particularly valuable.
