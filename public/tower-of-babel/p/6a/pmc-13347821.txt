Incidence and risk factors for permanent pacemaker implantation following aortic root surgery
Authors: Krishna Mani, Vinci Naruka, Eleonora De Laurentis, Mahmoud Abdellah, Emma Taylor, Mark Gallagher, Robert Morgan, Marjan Jahangiri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPermanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) remains a serious complication following aortic root replacement (ARR) and valve-sparing root replacement (VSRR). This is due to the proximity of the atrioventricular node and the bundle of His to the aortic valve annulus and the left ventricle. The data regarding PPI after aortic root surgery are sparse. We describe the incidence, indication and timing of PPI following conventional ARR and VSRR.Methods727 consecutive patients undergoing elective and urgent aortic root surgery (549 ARR, 178 VSRR) between 2006–2023 were studied. Patients requiring PPI after aortic root surgery were identified, along with the indication and timing. The primary endpoint was the incidence of new PPI implantation during index hospital admission.ResultsThe incidence of PPI was 1.8% (n=13), with incidences in the ARR and VSRR groups of 2.2% vs 0.6% respectively. PPIs were performed at a median of 9 days (range 3–14 days). Following ARR, PPIs were primarily indicated for complete heart block (67%). In the PPI group, 53% had bicuspid aortic valve (BAV), 62% were female and 62% had preoperative atrial fibrillation. The median follow-up was 7.6 years (range 0.5–14.5).ConclusionsConventional aortic root and valve sparing surgery carries a low risk of PPI (1.8%), particularly after VSRR. This study provides contemporary evidence of the risk of PPI requirement. BAV and female gender were associated with an increased risk of PPI.Keywords: Aortic Aneurysm; AORTIC VALVE DISEASE; Pacemaker, Artificial; Aortic Diseases

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Introduction

Aortic root replacement (ARR) and valve sparing root replacement (VSRR) involve manipulation of the aortic root, which is anatomically adjacent to critical conduction structures, such as the atrioventricular (AV) node and the bundle of His, thereby rendering the cardiac conduction system at risk of injury. Consequently, postoperative conduction abnormalities necessitating permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) are a recognised complication.1

Rates of PPI after isolated aortic valve replacement have been reported in the literature, ranging from 2% to 8%.2 3 PPI rates after ARR range from 3% to 12% within the first 30 days of surgery.4,6 Although several risk factors for PPI following ARR have been identified, including female sex, preoperative conduction disturbances, chronic comorbidities and bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) morphology, there remains a paucity in the data regarding the incidence, risk factors and indications for PPI and long-term PPI-related outcomes.4 6 These need to be considered during preoperative risk stratification to identify those at high risk and improve patient counselling. PPI also imposes an economic burden on healthcare systems, requiring allocation of hospital resources.7 8 The aim of this study is to determine the incidence of new PPI following aortic root surgery and to explore the risk factors and outcomes.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, single-centre study. All patients who underwent elective or urgent ARR and VSRR under the care of one surgical team between 2006 and 2023 were included. Emergency operations such as aortic dissections were excluded. The data were validated against the national database.

The primary outcome was new PPI during the index hospitalisation after bioprosthetic or mechanical ARR and VSRR. After discharge, patients underwent regular follow-up. Additional variables included primary indication for PPI and interval between surgery and PPI.

Preoperative assessment

All patients underwent trans-thoracic echocardiography (TTE), CT and/or cardiac MRI. The annulus measurements were obtained using TTE, measured in the long-axis view at the hinge point of the aortic valve leaflets in mid-systole. Coronary angiography was performed in patients older than 40 years of age. Each patient was discussed at a multidisciplinary team meeting consisting of at least one cardiac surgeon, cardiologist, vascular surgeon, radiologist and several residents from different disciplines. Patients with aortic root and ascending aneurysms >4.5 cm underwent surgery. In the earlier years of the study, non-connective tissue disease patients were operated on at the threshold of 5.0 cm. The 2024 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines currently recommend surgery in patients with a maximum aortic diameter ≥5.5 cm for isolated aortic aneurysm and in patients who are undergoing surgery for aortic valve disease with an aortic diameter ≥4.5 cm.9 After 2017, all patients underwent an assessment of the cross-sectional aortic area/patient height ratio, which influenced the indication for intervention.10 11

Operative technique

We previously described our operative technique in detail.12 13 It is a simple, reproducible technique, requiring minimal haemostatic material. Meticulous attention is given to the surgical technique. Several parts of the operation are performed by the residents. The types of valves and prosthetic material used for replacement of the ascending aorta, types of surgery, any concomitant procedures, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB), cross-clamp and circulatory arrest times were recorded. VSRR was performed using the Remodelling Technique.14

All patients received temporary atrial and ventricular epicardial pacing wires. If there was no need for pacing, they were removed on postoperative day 3 or 4. If pacing requirement persisted, the decision for PPI was made following discussions between the cardiac surgery and electrophysiology/cardiology teams. All permanent pacing devices were inserted by the electrophysiology team via an infra-clavicular, trans-venous (subclavian vein) approach.

Follow-up

All patients were followed up locally. Following discharge, the patients were first reviewed at 6 weeks with a routine examination, ECG and chest X-ray. They were reviewed at 6 months and subsequently annually. During annual reviews, repeat TTE, CT or MRI were performed. Annual pacing device checks were performed by the local electrophysiology team.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using the SPSS software (V.24 (IBM)). Data were presented as median (IQR) for continuous variables, and with count and frequency for categorical variables. To identify factors associated with PPI, logistic regression was performed. Univariate analysis was performed for variables relating to PPI. All variables were included in the multivariable model. Long-term survival was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Results

Baseline characteristics and operative details

A total of 727 patients underwent aortic root surgery. 549 patients underwent ARR, with biologic (n=365) or mechanical (n=181) prostheses and 3 patients underwent a homograft replacement. 178 patients underwent VSRR. Demographic and preoperative data are shown in table 1. The median age for all patients was 60 years (range: 18–84), and 30% were women. Bicuspid valves were present in 41% of patients. The majority of the patients (69%) had aortic stenosis and 35% had aortic regurgitation. Median annular size in VSRR was 23 mm (range 21–29).

Postoperative PPI

A total of 13 patients (1.8%) required PPI after surgery. PPI was performed at a median of 9 days after surgery (range 3–14 days). Eight patients were in preoperative atrial fibrillation (62%), eight were female (62%) and seven had a BAV (53%). 12 patients (92%) required PPI due to complete or second degree heart block after ARR.

In the ARR subgroup, 12 patients had PPI (2.2%). Indications for PPI in ARR included complete heart block (n=8 (67%)) and second-degree heart block (n=4 (33%)). In the VSRR subgroup, the incidence of PPI was significantly lower, with only one patient requiring PPI (0.6%).

The median length of stay after ARR was 7 days and after VSRR was 6 days. Length of postoperative stay was longer in patients requiring PPI at 12 days (range 9–18 (p=0.01)). There were no mortalities observed in patients who underwent PPI during the index hospital admission or in the subsequent follow-up period. The median follow-up was 7.6 years (range 0.5–14.5).

Multivariate analysis showed that factors independently associated with PPI included female sex (OR, 1.23; 95% CI 1.10 to 3.12; p=0.04), BAV (OR 1.98; 95% CI 1.54 to 3.23; p=0.01) and type of root surgery (ARR) (OR 0.56; 95% CI 0.24-0.89, p=0.002).

Discussion

In this single-centre study, we observed a low rate of PPI during the index hospital stay. PPI did not correlate with higher early or late mortality. Our data suggest that the majority of conduction disturbances occurred in patients who developed complete or second-degree heart block following ARR. ARR as type of surgery compared with VSRR was associated with a higher rate of PPI. This is likely due to conduction system injury, possibly from decalcification of the aortic annulus. The PPI rate was lower after VSRR compared with ARR. This difference may stem from VSRR with the remodelling technique, note requiring decalcification and less dissection of the left ventricular outflow tract compared with the reimplantation technique. In VSRR, the absence of prosthetic valves may reduce pressure on the conduction system, contributing to the lower PPI incidence. Further analysis indicated that females had higher risk of requiring PPI after ARR compared with males. This is similar to McMullen et al and Singh et al, thereby indicating female gender as a predictor of permanent pacemaker requirement.4 15 The reason for this remains unclear. This may be due to oversizing in women who tend to have smaller annuli.

There was a higher incidence of PPI in patients with BAV. Guglielmetti et al reported that BAV patients had a higher 30-day incidence of PPI (5.8% vs 1.6% in tricuspid aortic valve patients).16 This may be multifactorial including anatomical considerations of different BAV types with asymmetric cusps, variations in BAV-associated-aortopathy and anomalous coronary artery origins. There may be an alteration of the geometry of the aortic root and nearby structures like the AV node and bundle of His, thereby increasing the risk of conduction system injury during surgery. The rigid prostheses may also exert pressure on the membranous septum or AV node, particularly in BAV patients, whom the annulus may be smaller or irregularly shaped, increasing the susceptibility to conduction disturbances.

The hospital stay in patients who required PPI was significantly longer. This was partly waiting and hoping for return of sinus rhythm and partly waiting for fitness assessment and decision for suitability by the electrophysiology teams. The numbers are too small for any meaningful analysis and identifying risk factors.

Limitations of the study

Data were derived from a single centre. Despite this, the rates of PPI were consistent with rates from previous studies. The small number of PPI events limits the statistical power for identifying all potential risk factors and may reduce the precision of the observed associations.

Conclusions

The need for PPI after aortic root surgery is uncommon. Factors including female gender and BAV increase the likelihood of PPI. VSRR, using the remodelling technique, is associated with a very low likelihood of PPI compared with ARR.
