Application of machine learning to blood‐based biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease in Down syndrome
Authors: Patrick H. Luckett, Melissa Petersen, Sid O'Bryant, Mark Mapstone, Brad T. Christian, Benjamin Handen, Elizabeth Head, Beau M. Ances, for the Alzheimer's Biomarker Consortium‐Down Syndrome
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONBlood‐based biomarkers can improve Alzheimer's disease (AD) characterization in Down syndrome (DS). This study applied hierarchical clustering and machine learning–based feature selection to identify biomarkers associated with disease progression.METHODSCross‐sectional blood‐based biomarkers were analyzed from 211 DS participants (n = 79 cognitively stable [CS]; n = 72 mild cognitive impairment [MCI]; n = 60 AD dementia [DS‐AD]). These included markers of amyloid, tau, neurodegeneration, and inflammation. Clustering grouped biomarkers. Decision trees classified disease stage, and Shapley values identified the strongest predictors of disease stage.RESULTSThe strongest predictors overall were neurofilament light chain (NfL), tau/amyloid beta (Aβ)40, Aβ42/Aβ40, alpha‐2‐macroglobulin (A2M), and interleukin (IL)‐10. Within the CS group, NfL, tau/Aβ40, A2M, and IL‐10 were strong predictors. In MCI, Aβ42/Aβ40, NfL, A2M, and IL‐10 were strong predictors. In DS‐AD, Aβ42/Aβ40, NfL, and tau/Aβ40 were the top predictors. Cluster membership varied based on disease stage.DISCUSSIONThese findings reveal evolving biomarker signatures and clustering patterns across cognitive stages, underscoring their potential for disease monitoring.

INTRODUCTION

Down syndrome (DS) is caused by triplication of chromosome 21, and with an incidence rate of 1 in 700 newborns each year, making it one of the most common genetic disorders. 1 Due to the over‐expression of the amyloid precursor protein (APP) found on chromosome 21, individuals with DS have an elevated risk of developing Alzheimer's disease (AD), characterized by the development of amyloid plaques and tau neurofibrillary tangles. 2 , 3 , 4 By age 40, almost all individuals with DS have AD neuropathology, with most developing cognitive impairment by their mid‐50s.3– 6 More recently, clinical trials have been started specifically targeting amyloid pathology in DS individuals. Therefore, identifying biomarkers for AD pathology in DS will be crucial for monitoring disease progression, screening, and evaluating potential treatment responses in clinical settings.

Blood‐based biomarkers are relatively easy to acquire and are inexpensive as diagnostic markers. They offer economic benefits compared to imaging techniques like magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and positron emission tomography (PET), or cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). Imaging approaches require a participant to lie within a scanner, not move, and require sophisticated infrastructure that may not be available to all communities. 7 , 8 Similarly, CSF draws are often perceived as more intimidating and painful than blood draws due to the invasive nature of the procedure, the need for spinal access, and the potential for side effects like severe headaches and infection. In contrast, blood draws are less invasive and are less intimidating and distressing for patients, thus allowing for ease of implementation across different communities. This is especially true for patients who may be confused and/or easily agitated due to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or dementia due to AD. Thus, blood biomarkers are particularly appealing for monitoring and large‐scale screening in DS.

Numerous blood biomarkers have been studied in the context of various diseases. Specific to AD, amyloid beta (Aβ) 42 and 40 have been extensively studied, and are thought to reflect amyloid pathology in the brain. 9 , 10 Similarly, increases in plasma tau can indicate the development of neurofibrillary tangles. 9 , 11 Beyond these “hallmark” biomarkers of AD, measures of neuronal damage, most notably neurofilament light chain (NfL) 12 , 13 have been studied. However, elevation of biomarkers such as NfL is considered a measure of general brain disease and is not specific to AD. Additionally, a range of other biomarkers exists, which, while not directly indicative of DS or AD pathology, could serve as surrogate markers for underlying pathophysiological processes. 14 , 15 For example, alpha‐2‐macroglobulin (A2M), C‐reactive protein (CRP), eotaxin‐3 (CCL26), interleukins (IL‐10, IL‐18, IL‐5, IL‐6, IL‐7), serum amyloid A (SAA), soluble intercellular adhesion molecule‐1 (sICAM‐1), soluble vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1 (sVCAM‐1), thymus and activation‐regulated chemokine (TARC), and tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF‐α) measure various aspects of inflammation and the immune response. Other potential measures evaluate metabolism (e.g., beta‐2‐microglobulin [B2M], fatty acid‐binding protein 3 [FABP3]); cellular structure and migration (tenascin‐C [TNC]); and chemotaxis (T cell‐derived chemotactic factor [I‐309]). Given the multitude of biomarkers available, it is essential to determine the relative utility of each one in providing additional information compared to other biomarkers for DS. Ideally, identifying a small set of key blood‐based biomarkers that can accurately stage AD in DS would significantly enhance precision medicine.

Machine learning (ML) techniques, known for their ability to discern patterns from data, present a promising approach to identifying blood‐based biomarkers that accurately reflect AD progression in DS. Unlike traditional models limited by predetermined rules, ML algorithms adapt based on the data, are robust against complex feature interactions, and are sensitive to intricate association patterns. 16 The large volume of data and advancements in computational capabilities enhance the feasibility of applying ML to analyze biomarkers. We hypothesize that using advanced ML models on blood‐based biomarker data will identify the most relevant measures for staging disease progression in DS.

In this study, we used hierarchical clustering to group biomarkers based on similarity, decision trees to classify disease stage, and Shapley values to identify the strongest model predictors for 211 DS participants (n = 79 cognitively stable [CS]; n = 72 MCI; n = 60 dementia [DS‐AD]). This data‐driven approach provides insight into the behavior and utility of existing blood‐based biomarkers for DS, leading to improved diagnosis, screening, and clinical trial design by identifying the optimal biomarkers for evaluating therapies.

METHODS

Participants

This cross‐sectional study used data from DS participants (n = 211, 79 CS, 72 MCI‐DS; 60 DS‐AD) within the Alzheimer's Biomarker Consortium–Down Syndrome (ABC‐DS; https://www.nia.nih.gov/ research/abc‐ds). 17 All study procedures were approved under the respective institutional review boards, and informed consent and/or assent were obtained from all participants. Diagnostic groups were age matched to minimize the potential confounding influence of age on biomarker profiles.

Clinical classification

Clinical AD status was determined by consensus conferences following the recommendations of the American Association on Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities–International Association for the Scientific Study of Intellectual and Developmental Disabilities Working Group for the Establishment of Criteria for the Diagnosis of Dementia in Individuals with Developmental Disability. 18 Consensus was based on caregiver interviews; medical, clinical, and cognitive testing considered in reference to baseline scores and any recent major life transitions or events; and blinded to imaging, omics, and genetics data. 17 Participants were categorized as CS, MCI‐DS, dementia (DEM), or unable to determine (excluded from this analysis).

Blood assays

All plasma biomarkers were assayed using single‐molecule array technology (Simoa; Quanterix). Samples, calibrators, and controls used in the assay were equilibrated to room temperature in a biosafety cabinet for 1 hour. Room temperature samples were vortexed, then transferred into a Nunc 96‐well polypropylene deep well U‐bottomed plate (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and centrifuged at room temperature for 15 minutes at ~2,547 x g. After centrifugation, the supernatant was transferred into a 96‐well assay plate (Quanterix). Resorufin β‐galactopyranoside (RGP) was shaken on a Simoa Microplate Shaker at 30°C for a minimum of 30 minutes but no longer than 4 hours. A calibration concentration range was constructed using reagents provided and transferred into the 96‐well assay plate. All samples were then transferred into the 96‐well assay plate. The plate and all reagents were loaded into the HD‐X Automated Immunoassay Analyzer (HD‐X), which automated the assay and quantified target proteins in unknown samples using a two‐step immunoassay to generate a fluorescent signal. The HD‐X diluted the sample using the appropriate dilution factor with the provided sample diluent. Diluted samples then underwent a primary incubation with paramagnetic beads coated with a target antibody for protein capture and a biotinylated detector antibody for protein detection. After the incubation, the beads were washed and then mixed with a streptavidin‐β‐galactosidase (SBG) conjugate for enzyme labeling of the captured proteins. After a second wash, the beads were re‐suspended in a buffer containing RGP and transferred to a Simoa Disc, then sealed. SBG hydrolyzed the RGP substrate within the microwells to yield a fluorescent signal quantified by the Simoa optical system. The HD‐X Automated Immunoassay Analyzer generated a four‐parameter logistic curve with 1/y2 weighting from which unknown and control sample concentrations were calculated. See Table S1 in supporting information for all analytes evaluated. Further details can be found in Table S1 and online documentation (https://www.quanterix.com/publications/). 15 , 19

ML and statistical analyses

Analyses were performed in MATLAB R2023a. Clustering was performed with agglomerative hierarchical clustering. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering is a bottom‐up approach, in which each observation starts in its own cluster, and clusters are then merged as they move up the cluster tree. The distance metric used was the absolute value of the biomarker correlations. The linkage criterion, which determines the distance between sets as a function of the pairwise correlations between blood‐based biomarkers, used a weighted pair group method with arithmetic mean (WPGMA). 20

AD status (CS, MCI, or DS‐AD) was classified using decision trees. 21 The decision trees were optimized with 50 iterations of Bayesian optimization. Decision trees were selected to prioritize interpretability, rather than maximal predictive performance, which may be achieved through more complex, less interpretable models. Optimized variables include split criterion (Gini index or deviance), minimum leaf size, and maximum number of splits. In constructing the decision tree, variable selection was conducted using an interaction‐splitting technique. 22 This method used a curvature test to identify the strongest predictors by minimizing the p value of chi‐squared tests of independence between each predictor and the response, as well as between each pair of predictors and the response. All decision trees were trained with 10‐fold cross‐validation. Although multiple visits were available for a subset of participants, the analysis was cross‐sectional with respect to disease‐stage classification. Repeated observations were included to improve feature‐space characterization and modeling stability. Participant‐wise cross‐validation ensured that all observations from a given individual were confined to either the training or test set, preventing information leakage. Specifically, we used patient‐wise (grouped) cross‐validation. In every split, all observations from a given patient were assigned exclusively to either the training folds or the test fold (never both), and all hyperparameter tuning occurred only within the inner loop. This participant‐level split preserves independence between training and evaluation sets despite repeated measures, yielding an unbiased estimate of out‐of‐sample performance. 23 Of the 211 samples, 40 participants had multiple visits (18.96%). No participant had more than two samples in the dataset. Of the participants with two samples, 13 had matching cognitive scores, 12 transitioned from CS to MCI, and 15 transitioned from MCI to DS‐AD. After training, Shapley feature selection 24 , 25 was used to determine the importance of each feature for predicting AD status. Shapley values explained the contribution of a feature for a given sample by quantifying the marginal effect of including the feature in the model. A feature's Shapley value represents the average difference between the prediction and the model's average prediction across all possible combinations of features. The sum of all feature Shapley values represents the total difference between a given prediction and the average prediction. Overall feature importance was determined by computing Shapley values for each sample and averaging the results. Class‐specific averages and area under the curve were also calculated. Mean Shapley values were scaled to a range of [0,1], and features > 0.5 were considered strong predictors. Of note, this threshold is descriptive and does not imply some variation of statistical significance. To summarize how feature importance evolved across disease stages, these group‐wise means were treated as ordinal points along the disease continuum, and a robust linear regression was fit across stages. The slope of this regression was used as a summary measure of the rate of change in predictor strength across disease stage. This measure reflects change across the cognitive stage rather than temporal change.

Clustering

The elbow method consistently identified five clusters for the different groups (see supplemental material in supporting information). Figure 5A shows the clustering results using all available data. The first cluster comprised NfL, tau/Aβ40, Aβ42/Aβ40, A2M, B2M, and TNC. The second cluster consisted of sVCAM‐1, sICAM‐1, SAA, CRP, and IL‐6. The third cluster consisted of CCL26, IL‐7, TARC, IL‐5, TNF‐α, IL‐10, and I‐309. FABP3 and IL‐18 clustered separately. When examining cognitively stable individuals (Figure 5B), tau/Aβ40, Aβ42/Aβ40, B2M, TNC, sVCAM‐1, and sICAM‐1 comprised one large cluster, while SAA, CRP, IL‐6, CCL26, IL‐7, TARC, IL‐10, and TNF‐α formed another large cluster. Smaller clusters were observed for NfL and FABP3 together; IL‐18, I‐309, and A2M together; and IL‐5 as a separate cluster. For the MCI group (Figure 5C), IL‐6, CRP, SAA, B2M, I‐309, IL‐10, TNF‐α, and IL‐18 clustered together. Another cluster included IL‐5, tau/Aβ40, Aβ42/Aβ40, FABP3, and CCL26. TNC, sVCAM‐1, sICAM‐1, and A2M formed a separate cluster, with IL‐7 and TARC clustering together and NfL in its own cluster. In the DS‐AD group (Figure 5D), CCL26, IL‐7, TARC, TNF‐α, I‐309, IL‐10, IL‐5, and FABP3 clustered together. NfL, TNC, B2M, and A2M formed another cluster, followed by sVCAM‐1, sICAM‐1, SAA, CRP, and IL‐6. Tau/Aβ40 and Aβ42/Aβ40 were in their own cluster, as was IL‐18.

DISCUSSION

Numerous blood‐based biomarkers exist that may be relevant for studying DS. While standard amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration (AT[N]) measures are well‐established direct indicators of disease pathology, additional biomarkers, such as those related to inflammation, are important. These measures might reveal insights relevant to disease staging and clinical trials. This analysis used ML to identify the predictive ability and natural groupings of both the traditional and non‐additional blood‐based biomarkers in the context of DS. Decision trees effectively classified disease stages with high accuracy, and feature selection identified a subset of biomarkers most reflective of DS pathology based on disease stage. Our results suggest both the standard AT(N) biomarkers and biomarkers related to immune regulation and inflammation (A2M and IL‐10) are strong predictors of disease stage in DS. Further, hierarchical clustering revealed similarities and differences in biomarker behavior, which distinguished cognitive status.

ATN biomarkers (Aβ42/Aβ40, tau/Aβ40, NfL) were the top predictors of cognitive stage classification. Over the past several years, NfL has emerged as a reliable biomarker of neurodegenerative diseases. However, its non‐specific nature and the fact that it naturally increases with age, even in healthy individuals, are notable drawbacks. In DS, adults show an increase in NfL levels compared to controls, 27 and levels increase with the disease stage. 28 In our analysis, NfL was the top predictor (Figure 2) for each analysis. Because it was repeatedly the top predictor, there was no rate of change based on disease stage (Figure 3). Statistically, NfL showed the most significant difference, with accelerated growth leading to a median value nearly double for DS‐AD compared to CS. Our results confirm that NfL is a promising biomarker for disease staging in DS. 29 This makes NfL a more specific indicator of disease‐related neurodegeneration rather than age‐related changes, allowing for more accurate staging and monitoring of disease progression. In the clustering analysis, NfL was somewhat variable in terms of associating with other biomarkers across disease stages. It was generally associated with other strong blood‐based biomarker predictors (Aβ42/Aβ40, tau/Aβ40, and A2M). However, in the CS group, it only clustered with FABP3. FABP3 is involved in the transport of fatty acids and may also be a marker of neuronal injury. The presence of these biomarkers in a cluster indicates a potential link between neuronal damage and metabolic dysregulation. 30 , 31 In the MCI group, NfL was its own cluster. In the DS‐AD group, NfL was associated with TNC, B2M, and A2M. These biomarkers are involved in neuronal damage, extracellular matrix remodeling, immune response, and protease inhibition. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35

The tau/Aβ40 ratio was the second strongest predictor overall. Interestingly, tau/Aβ40 was a top predictor in the CS group, and Aβ42/Aβ40 was only a moderate predictor. In contrast, in the MCI group, the roles were reversed, with Aβ42/Aβ40 the strongest predictor and tau/Aβ40 only moderate. Finally, tau/Aβ40 was again a strong predictor in the DS‐AD group. Further, tau/Aβ40 significantly differed in the CS and the MCI groups, whereas Aβ42/Aβ40 did not. Generally, changes in Aβ are considered one of the earliest in AD‐related dementias. The fact that Aβ was only significantly different in more advanced stages might suggest that Aβ exhibits an exponential increase over time rather than being linear. Still, the rate at which accumulation occurs is lowest in CS, next highest in MCI, then becomes more pronounced in the DS‐AD group. On the other hand, tau may follow a more linear accumulation trend, resulting in a more noticeable difference between the CS and MCI groups. Further research is needed to understand these patterns fully. In any case, tau/Aβ40 was a stronger predictor of disease stage in both ML and statistical analysis. Tau/Aβ40 was consistently associated with Aβ42/Aβ40 in the clustering analysis, suggesting DS is not only a disease of amyloid but also tau. The CS group also clustered with TNC, B2M, sVCAM‐1, and sICAM‐1, immune response biomarkers, inflammation, and tissue remodeling. The MCI group also included IL‐5, FABP3, and CCL26, biomarkers of immune response regulation and inflammatory processes. These findings suggest that inflammation is associated with changes in tau pathology. Further studies are warranted to confirm and better understand this relationship.

Aβ42/Aβ40 was the third strongest predictor in our analysis overall (Figure 2A). Aβ has been well studied in the context of neurologic diseases related to AD and cerebral amyloidosis. In DS, the aneuploidy trisomy 21 results in significant overproduction of Aβ, leading to AD neuropathology in nearly 100% of cases. 36 Generally, the amount of Aβ in CSF and plasma decreases with disease severity, reflecting overproduction or impaired clearance in the brain. However, the results seen with plasma biomarkers in DS are more variable. 37 Some studies have shown a gradual decrease over time, 36 , 38 while others have shown the opposite trend as a function of time and disease stage. 6 , 39 , 40 In our data, plasma Aβ42/Aβ40 increased over time and was only significantly different for DS‐AD compared to the other two groups (Figure 4C). There was a slightly higher ratio of Aβ42/Aβ40 comparing CS to MCI, but this was not significant. This is why NfL and tau had higher feature weights. However, comparing MCI to DS‐AD, the Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio exhibited the most significant difference in predictor strength over time (Figure 3). It showed low predictor strength in the CS group, was moderately high in the MCI group, and was high in the AD group (Figure 2B‐D). Again, this corresponds with what is expected based on Figure 4C. While the mechanisms underlying this pattern cannot be determined from cross‐sectional plasma data alone, one possible explanation for the observed rise in plasma Aβ levels later in the disease is saturation of brain amyloid. In DS, chronic Aβ overproduction may eventually exceed the brain's capacity for deposition and clearance, leading to increased spillover into the bloodstream. Plasma Aβ trajectories in DS are known to be more variable and differ from the associated CSF markers, which typically show declining Aβ42 with increasing amyloid burden. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis generating rather than mechanistic.

Beyond ATN biomarkers, A2M and IL‐10 were strong predictors overall. A growing body of literature suggests that AD‐related pathogenesis is associated with an innate immune response and the release of inflammatory mediators. 41 , 42 , 43 A2M is a protease inhibitor and may be considered a genetic risk factor for AD‐related dementias based on its ability to mediate the clearance and degradation of Aβ. 44 Others have also suggested that A2M could affect tau pathogenesis by interacting with enzymes that regulate tau phosphorylation. 45 In our analysis, A2M was significantly lower in the CS group compared to the MCI and DS‐AD groups, but was similar for MCI and DS‐AD (Figure 4). Similarly, A2M showed the most significant decrease in predictor strength over time (Figure 3). These results are in line with past studies, which have demonstrated that markers of neuroinflammation tend to peak during the earlier stages of the disease. 46 In the clustering analysis (Figure 5), A2M tended to cluster with or near other strong predictors (Aβ42/Aβ40, tau/Aβ40, and NfL). However, this varied based on the disease stage. In the CS group, A2M was clustered with I‐309 and IL‐18, suggesting synergistic behavior with immune system regulation and inflammatory responses. In the MCI group, A2M was clustered with sVCAM‐1, sICAM‐1, and TNC, biomarkers involved with cell adhesion, extracellular matrix remodeling, and potential involvement in immune and inflammatory pathways. In the DS‐AD group, A2M was clustered with B2M, TNC, and NfL, biomarkers involving protease inhibition, immune modulation, and markers of neuronal injury and extracellular matrix remodeling. The clustering of A2M with various biomarkers across different disease stages highlights its role in the interplay among protease inhibition, immune responses, and neuroinflammation.

Conversely, IL‐10 showed a “U” behavior, with the highest levels observed in the CS group. IL‐10 is an anti‐inflammatory cytokine that contributes to immune homeostasis and promotes immune responses. Past research has shown similar results, in which adults with DS had increased inflammatory expression and decreased inflammatory mediators. 46 IL‐10 generally clustered with TNF‐α, IL‐5, IL‐7, CCL26, and I‐309, suggesting significant involvement in immune regulation and inflammatory processes. TNF‐α is a pro‐inflammatory cytokine involved in systemic inflammation, and IL‐5 and IL‐7 are cytokines that support the growth and differentiation of immune cells, particularly in the adaptive immune response. CCL26 and I‐309 are chemokines that recruit specific immune cells to sites of inflammation. This cluster indicates a coordinated response involving pro‐inflammatory and anti‐inflammatory mediators and factors that recruit and regulate immune cells, highlighting a complex interplay of immune regulation and inflammatory signaling through disease stages.

This study focused on biomarker discovery and interpretability rather than prediction optimization and, accordingly, used an interpretable decision tree framework. Future work will evaluate higher capacity models and interaction‐aware attribution methods (e.g., global Shapley), as well as integrative models incorporating demographic, genetic, and clinical variables. Further, while the present study focuses on biomarker discovery and model interpretability, this framework is inherently suitable for clinical deployment. Future work will focus on prospective validation and optimization of clinical workflows to enable real‐time application of this framework for disease staging, longitudinal monitoring, and clinical trial stratification. Interpretation of plasma biomarkers, particularly Aβ42/Aβ40, should be approached cautiously, as plasma measures reflect a combination of central and peripheral biological processes and are influenced by assay and systemic factors; thus, plasma Aβ should not be viewed as a direct surrogate for CSF or PET amyloid burden. Similarly, clustering of plasma biomarkers showed a weaker modular structure than prior CSF‐based analyses, consistent with the greater heterogeneity of blood‐based signals, and is therefore presented descriptively rather than as evidence of discrete biological modules.

CONCLUSION

In summary, our findings demonstrate that blood‐based biomarkers differentially associate with disease progression in DS. Markers of amyloid, neurodegeneration (NfL, tau/Aβ40), and inflammation (IL‐10, A2M) emerged as strong predictors, with varying predictive strength across disease stages. Clustering results further revealed that biomarker relationships reorganize with advancing pathology, reflecting underlying shifts in neurobiological processes. These results underscore the potential utility of blood‐based biomarkers for patient stratification, disease staging, and therapeutic monitoring in DS.
