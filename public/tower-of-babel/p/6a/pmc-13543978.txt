Sex differences in the expression of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) and its receptor: a review of contradictory and nuanced findings
Authors: Dinobi Offurum, Rebecca Lorsung, Radi Masri, Asaf Keller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Women are disproportionately affected by chronic pain, exhibiting greater incidence, severity, and duration of pain across a wide range of conditions. Women with chronic pain are also more likely to develop comorbid affective disorders, such as depression and anxiety. Although societal and environmental factors contribute to this sex bias, biological factors are also thought to play a role in these sex differences. One biological factor is calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), a neuropeptide with established roles in the pathogenesis of several chronic pain conditions, including migraine. A prevailing hypothesis is that differences in the expression of CGRP and its receptor are involved in sex differences in chronic pain. We explore the evidence for this by reviewing the literature. We find that only a few studies, typically with small sample sizes, address this hypothesis. In aggregate, these studies provide nuanced and contradictory evidence for sex differences in the expression of CGRP and its receptor. However, we present published data that support the conclusion that the CGRP pathway functions differently in males and females.

Introduction

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) is an ancient amino acid chain whose expression and functions appear to have been conserved in both vertebrates and invertebrates for at least 500 million years (Lafont et al., 2007; Mirabeau and Joly, 2013; Sekiguchi et al., 2016). CGRP modulates several cardiovascular, neural, immune, and metabolic processes (Labastida-Ramírez et al., 2019). Here, we focus on its established roles in the pathogenesis of chronic pain conditions, including migraine (Goadsby et al., 2017; Schou et al., 2017). Specifically, we explore the evidence that CGRP may be involved in sex differences in chronic pain.

Women are more likely than men to develop a variety of chronic pain conditions that affect different areas of the body, and women often exhibit more severe clinical pain (Mogil, 2012; Lipton et al., 2018; Scher et al., 2019; Osborne and Davis, 2022). This sex difference is particularly stark in migraine, where women show increased prevalence of migraine, as well as increased severity and persistence, relative to men (Stewart et al., 2008; Scher et al., 2019; Lipton et al., 2022).

These sex differences are likely driven by societal and cultural factors (Fillingim, 2000; Bartley and Fillingim, 2013). In addition, genetic, neuroimmune and neurobiological factors are thought to be involved in sex differences in chronic pain. Supporting a biological basis for these differences is their replication in preclinical models. For example, female rats display increased mechanical sensitivity after a lower dose of an “inflammatory soup” applied to the dura (Stucky et al., 2011). Similarly, treatment with the chemotherapy drug paclitaxel induces peripheral painful neuropathy, with female mice exhibiting greater mechanical hyperalgesia and thermal sensitivity than males (Cao et al., 2024). While the translation of sex differences into preclinical models supports a biological underpinning, the neurobiological mechanisms responsible for sex differences in pain disorders are largely unknown.

There is growing evidence that differences in CGRP expression and function may contribute to these sex differences. Direct application of CGRP to the rat or mouse dura mater produces cutaneous periorbital hypersensitivity, but only in females; Even high doses of CGRP produce no responses in males (Avona et al., 2019). Further, CGRP causes hyperalgesic priming—increased sensitivity to subsequent stimuli—only in female rats, and priming to a subthreshold dose of CGRP—by prior application of interleukin-6 or BDNF—occurs only in females (Avona et al., 2019; Paige et al., 2022). This hyperalgesic priming is suppressed by either CGRP receptor antagonists or a monoclonal antibody against CGRP (Paige et al., 2022). These sex-specific effects of CGRP do not appear to result from sex differences in vasoconstriction, as CGRP's effects on the vasomotor responses in meningeal arteries are similar between males and females (Edvinsson et al., 2024).

Consistent with these findings, Porreca and collaborators (Guzman et al., 2025) report that female, but not male, naïve mice display migraine-like pain behavior in response to CGRP applied to the dura. Further, they report that sensitizing the dura with repeated nitroglycerin causes sensitization to CGRP in both sexes, but at a 10 fold lower threshold in females.

Preclinical work supports sex differences in CGRP's role in pain beyond migraine. In models of neuropathic pain, sex-specific CGRP effects are also observed. In animals with spared nerve injury, CGRP antagonists can suppress mechanical hypersensitivity, but only in female mice. Consistent with this finding, intrathecally applied CGRP induces long-lasting mechanical hypersensitivity in female mice even at lower doses, whereas males exhibit transient responses and require higher doses (Paige et al., 2022). Similarly, spinal nerve ligation in rats results in mechanical sensitivity in both sexes, while CGRP antagonism resulted in a female-predominant rescue of emotional-affective responses (ultrasonic vocalizations) and anxiety-like behaviors (open field test) (Presto and Neugebauer, 2022).

That CGRP exerts sex-specific effects is also supported by clinical data. A comprehensive subpopulation analysis of data from FDA reviews by Porreca and collaborators (Porreca et al., 2024) concludes that anti-CGRP therapy is effective for acute migraine treatment in women, but provides no evidence of efficacy in men. However, CGRP monoclonal antibody treatment is equally effective for migraine prevention in both sexes (Porreca et al., 2024). Similar findings of sex-dependent sensitivity to CGRP are reported in preclinical models. For example, pain behaviors evoked after traumatic brain injury in mice are attenuated more effectively by CGRP receptor antagonists in females than in males (Navratilova et al., 2025).

The mechanisms by which CGRP exerts such sex differences are not fully understood. One hypothesis is that males and females release different amounts of CGRP during pain states. Technical barriers to measuring CGRP levels in the brain with high precision (discussed below) make it difficult to test this hypothesis. Another possibility is that CGRP has different effects on its postsynaptic targets in males and females. In the Discussion section we cite some studies that suggest that this may be the case. A third, more prevailing hypothesis is that these differences relate to sex differences in levels of expression of CGRP and its receptor.

A number of studies suggest that females may exhibit higher baseline levels of CGRP and certain CGRP receptor components in key pain-processing regions, such as the trigeminal system and synovial tissue, compared to males (Ji et al., 2019; Uchida et al., 2019; Maddahi et al., 2022; Cao et al., 2024). However, other studies report no sex differences in the expression of CGRP or its receptor components (Stucky et al., 2011; Presto and Neugebauer, 2022; Kemenesi-Gedei et al., 2023; Maddahi et al., 2024).

Our goal was to reconcile these conflicting findings by reviewing the literature, collating relevant data, and critically analyzing the results. We conclude that the jury is still out. The small number of studies, many with limited sample sizes, coupled with technical challenges in accurately measuring CGRP and its receptor components, currently make it difficult to determine whether sex differences exist in the expression of these molecules. Nonetheless, we highlight published evidence supporting the conclusion that the CGRP pathway functions differently in males and females.

Methods

We identified 101 publications in PubMed using the search query CGRP AND (“male and female” OR “men and women”). A similar search strategy used in the Consensus Research Database (consensus.app) identified a total of 1128 publications, 148 of which were deemed relevant. At this initial screening stage, an article was deemed relevant if it was an original empirical study that reported data on the expression of CGRP or a CGRP-receptor component in both male and female mammals. The stricter inclusion criteria described below were then applied to arrive at the final set of studies.

From these we selected articles that presented original data (excluding review articles), and that presented quantitative data, from both males and females, on expression levels of CGRP or CGRP receptor components in the central nervous system. We also included data from the trigeminal ganglion, dorsal root ganglion and the dura mater, as these are directly involved in the pathogenesis of chronic pain. Sixteen articles met the above criteria. All reported data from either mice or rats.

For studies that provided quantitative data of expression levels for each sex, we computed Hedges' g values as a measure of effect size (Hedges, 1981). It is generally accepted that values ≥0.2, ≥0.5, and ≥ 0.8 reflect small, medium and large effect sizes, respectively.

Results

CGRP

Most relevant studies quantified CGRP levels using antibodies for CGRP and western blots. Because CGRP concentrations are very low in most brain regions, it is usually necessary to pool data from several animals, or to analyze CGRP levels in the entire brain. These antibodies have also been used for immunohistochemistry, to provide semi-quantitative data on CGRP expression.

Dura

Guzman et al. (2025) applied CGRP to the dural surface of mice. This produced migraine-like pain behaviors in female, but not male, mice; a ten-fold lower dose was established as subthreshold in naïve female mice. The percentage of CGRP-positive neuronal fibers in the dura mater, examined 24 h after the CGRP application, was similar in male and female mice (Guzman et al., 2025). As described in the following section (Trigeminal ganglion), the percentage of CGRP-positive neurons in the trigeminal ganglion was higher in females.

To model chronic migraine in mice, Guzman et al. (2025) applied CGRP to the dural surface in mice that had previously received several injections of nitroglycerin (intraperitoneal; i.p.). This produced cutaneous allodynia in both sexes, that resolved in 8 to 11 days. When previously subthreshold doses of CGRP were applied to the dural surface of these mice, this elicited migraine-like nociceptive behaviors in mice of both sexes. However, the percentage of CGRP-positive fibers in the dura mater was similar in males and females.

Trigeminal ganglion

In the ophthalmic (V1) region of the trigeminal ganglion of mice that received supradural CGRP 24 h earlier (see above), the percentage of CGRP-positive neurons was higher in female mice than in males (Guzman et al., 2025). Priming with systemic (i.p.) nitroglycerin administration before the CGRP application (see above) was associated with a higher percentage of CGRP-positive neurons in the ganglia of both sexes (Guzman et al., 2025).

In the trigeminal ganglion of rats, Stucky et al. (2011) found no baseline sex differences in CGRP-encoding mRNA. Applying an “inflammatory soup” to the dura (as a preclinical model of migraine in rodents), decreased the expression of mRNA encoding CGRP in males, but not in females. Surprisingly, applying 20 μl buffered saline (as control) also resulted in an increase in CGRP mRNA, in both sexes. These authors did not test for sex differences in these changes; therefore, these data are not included in our Tables. Females showed increased mechanical sensitivity after the lower dose of the inflammatory soup, and these changes lasted longer than in males (Stucky et al., 2011). Thus, in this study the more pronounced and longer-lasting pain behavior in females was not accompanied by a corresponding baseline sex difference in trigeminal CGRP mRNA, indicating that the behavioral sex difference cannot be attributed to a difference in baseline CGRP expression in this region.

Similarly, in the rat trigeminal ganglion, Maddahi et al. (2024) found no sex differences in CGRP mRNA expression. The same study reported sex differences in the mRNA expression of the CGRP receptor component RAMP1; this is discussed in the CGRP receptor section below. An earlier study by Maddahi et al. (2022) used immunohistochemistry to compare the percentage of CGRP expressing neurons in the trigeminal ganglion and also reported no sex differences. Also consistent are findings by Kemenesi-Gedei et al. (2023) who report that the expression of CALCA, the mRNA which encodes the CGRP protein, is indistinguishable in male and female rats, and that a carrageenan-induced neuroinflammation of the trigeminal nerve evoked similar increases in CALCA mRNA levels in both sexes. The neuroinflammation was associated with higher scores in an orofacial operant test in females (Kemenesi-Gedei et al., 2023).

CGRP expression.

“Model” – pain model studied.

Spinal cord

In the dorsal horn of the mouse spinal cord, both CGRP protein expression and CGRP mRNA levels are higher in females (Cao et al., 2024). Treatment with the chemotherapy drug, paclitaxel, was associated with higher CGRP protein and mRNA levels in females (Cao et al., 2024). Note that all these results are based on only 3 animals per group. Paclitaxel treatment in this study resulted in higher scores of mechanical hyperalgesia and thermal sensitivity in females.

Dorsal root ganglion

Cohen et al. (2021) used acute and chronic mouse models of migraine (evoked by single or multiple nitroglycerin injections, respectively); these evoked mechanical hypersensitivity in both sexes. They then measured CGRP mRNA expression in the dorsal root ganglion (DRG). The authors kindly shared their raw data with us, to allow us to perform a direct sex comparison of mRNA levels. These levels were similar in both sexes (3.7 × 10−5 ± 1.9 × 10−5 in males, n = 5; 2.5 × 10−5 ± 1.4 × 10−5 in females, n = 4; p = 0.3; Welch's t-test). The authors do not report data from naïve mice. An antagonist of transient receptor potential cation channel subfamily C member 4 receptor caused similar reductions in CGRP mRNA in females and males (to 1.6 × 10−5 ± 4.7 × 10−6 in males, n = 5; 1.7 × 10−5 ± 5.9 × 10−6 in females, n = 3; p = 0.3; Welch's t-test).

The data reported by Ahlström et al. (2021) indicate that the proportion of CGRP-positive neurons is similar in the DRG of naïve male and female rats, and that neuropathic pain (spinal nerve injury model; SNI) produces similar reductions in the proportion of CGRP neurons in both sexes (Table 1). In this study, female rats exhibited more mechanical hypersensitivity after SNI (Ahlström et al., 2021). Similarly, Rees et al. (2024) report no sex differences in the proportion of neurons in the DRG of mice or rats that stain for CGRP. However, Yang et al. (1998) report a lower percentage of CGRP-positive neurons in the DRG of female rats at the cervical, lumbar and sacral segments, but no differences in DRGs from the thoracic segments.

Thus, three studies report no sex differences in CGRP expression in the DRG, whereas one reports lower CGRP expression in the DRG of female rats.

In contrast, Price and collaborators (Tavares-Ferreira et al., 2022) report higher CALCA expression in the DRG of female human subjects. Of note, the same study reports that nearly all human DRG neurons express CGRP, a marked difference from the rodent DRG that should be borne in mind when extrapolating these preclinical findings to humans.

Medulla

Expression levels of the CGRP-encoding gene in the medulla were reported to be higher in female than in male rats (Stucky et al., 2011). Applying an “inflammatory soup” to the dura (as a preclinical model of migraine in rodents) affected mRNA levels in inconsistent directions, depending on the inflammatory soup concentrations. However, applying buffered saline (as a control) had similar effects on CGRP mRNA (Stucky et al., 2011). Applying either an inflammatory soup or buffered saline to the dura decreased expression of CGRP mRNA. These findings suggest that, in this model of migraine, changes in CGRP expression are not causally related to the IS application (see above).

Amygdala

In the rat central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA), CGRP mRNA levels fluctuate at different times after spinal nerve ligation (SNL, a model of neuropathic pain). One week after SNL, CGRP mRNA levels were higher in males, but three weeks later they were higher in females (Presto and Neugebauer, 2022). CGRP antagonism in SNL rats resulted in rescued mechanical sensitivity in both sexes but showed female-predominant effects on emotional-affective responses (ultrasonic vocalizations) and anxiety-like behaviors (open field test) (Presto and Neugebauer, 2022).

In naïve rats, labeling density for CGRP is reported to be 2-fold higher in the amygdala of females (Li et al., 2024).

Hypothalamus

In the anteroventral periventricular nucleus of the medial preoptic area, female rats have a nearly 20-fold higher number of CGRP-immunoreactive neurons, compared to males (Herbison, 1992). This study also reported a 24-fold higher number of CGRP neurons in the medial preoptic nucleus, but no sex differences in the number of CGRP neurons in the ventrolateral regions of the medial preoptic area.

A subsequent study from the same laboratory reported a 3.5-fold higher number of cells expressing CGRP mRNA in female rats (Herbison and Spratt, 1995). These authors report that the relative expression levels of CGRP mRNA per neuron were similar in males and females.

In contrast, a third study from this group studied mediobasal hypothalamic nuclei, finding that male rats have a 2-fold higher number of CGRP neurons in the arcuate nucleus and the dorsomedial group (Leclercq and Herbison, 1996).

Thus, studies from the same group report that some hypothalamic regions have strikingly higher numbers of CGRP neurons in females, whereas other nuclei have higher numbers in males.

Whole brain

Li et al. (2024) assessed CGRP protein levels in a number of brain regions of rats, before and after producing traumatic brain injury (TBI) with a controlled impact over the parietal cortex (a model that produces chronic pain). In naïve females, CGRP measures were higher in cortical areas (by approximately 35%), amygdala (2-fold higher), and parenchymal vessels (58% higher). 30 days after TBI, CGRP levels were higher in each of these regions, compared to sham animals. Females had larger increases in CGRP levels in the peri-impact regions (by about 27%), but the increase in CGRP in other regions was similar in both sexes (Li et al., 2024). Application of a CGRP receptor antagonist suppressed the increase in CGRP levels after TBI, and the effects of the antagonist on CGRP levels were greater in female rats (Table 1). TBI was also associated with sex differences in behavioral outcomes in this study. Males exhibited increased anxiety-like and depressive-like behaviors, whereas females showed decreased anxiety and depressive-like behaviors after TBI. Female rats also have less severe structural outcomes after TBI. The authors interpreted these findings as evidence that higher levels of CGRP in females provide neuroprotection by diminishing oxidative stress (Li et al., 2024).

Rugarn et al. (1999) used radioimmunoassay to compare CGRP in male and female rats, presumably assaying entire brains. They report no significant differences, but provide no data. Therefore, we did not include this study in Table 1.

CGRP receptor

CGRP acts by activating a heterodimeric receptor composed of two membrane proteins: calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CLR) and receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1) (McLatchie et al., 1998; Dickerson, 2013; Edvinsson and Warfvinge, 2013). CLR is a member of the class B/Secretin family of G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs) that are activated by several neuropeptides, peptide endocrine hormones, and peptide paracrine factors (Hoare, 2025). The RAMP1 transmembrane protein confers ligand specificity of the CGRP receptor to CGRP (McLatchie et al., 1998; Hay and Pioszak, 2016). Further, physical association of CLR with RAMP is required for translocation of the functional heterodimer to the cell surface (McLatchie et al., 1998). A third CGRP receptor component, receptor component protein (RCP), a low molecular weight, hydrophilic, membrane-associated protein, is required to form a functional CGRP receptor (Luebke et al., 1996; Evans et al., 2000; Prado et al., 2001). RCP expression correlates with CGRP efficacy and enhances receptor function in response to CGRP (Naghashpour et al., 1997; Goharkhay et al., 2007).

Sex comparisons of the expression of these CGRP receptor components have been quantified, for the most part, using western blot for assessing protein levels or qPCR to quantify mRNA levels. Very few studies compared sex differences in these proteins after experimental manipulations, such as pain models.

Trigeminal ganglion

Maddahi et al. (2024) report that RAMP1 expression is higher in female rats, whereas receptor components CLR and RCP are expressed at similar levels in the trigeminal ganglion of both sexes. These authors also report a higher proportion of RAMP1-expressing neurons in female rats (Maddahi et al., 2022). In contrast, Stucky et al. (2011) find that RNA expression levels for all three CGRP receptor components are lower in female rats.

As discussed above, Stucky et al. (2011) report that both buffered saline and an inflammatory soup applied to the dura result in sex-specific pain-like behaviors. RAMP1 expression.

Thus, data on sex differences in the trigeminal ganglion are limited to studies in rats. These studies report no sex differences in the expression of CLR or RCP. Findings for RAMP1 are mixed: 2 studies report higher RAMP1 levels in females, whereas a third reports no sex differences.

Spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis

In the rat spinal trigeminal nucleus caudalis (SpVc), which processes pain and temperature signals from the face and head, there are no sex differences in any of the membrane fractions of CLR (cytoplasmic, detergent soluble, or detergent resistant) (Ji et al., 2019). In contrast, all three fractions of RCP are higher in females (Ji et al., 2019).

In the region of the medulla that contains the spinal trigeminal complex, mRNAs encoding each of the CGRP receptor components—RAMP1, CLR, and RCP—are reported to be lower in female rats (Stucky et al., 2011). As noted above, Stucky et al. (2011) report that both buffered saline and an inflammatory soup applied to the dura result in sex-specific pain-like behaviors. They further found that these treatments were associated with some—albeit inconsistent (see Table 2, Table 3, Table 4)—sex differences in the expression of CGRP receptor components in the medulla (Stucky et al., 2011).

Amygdala

After spinal nerve ligation, levels of both CLR and RAMP1 increase in females—but not males—at 4 weeks, whereas no changes are observed at 1 week (Presto and Neugebauer, 2022).

In most cases, data on a specific neuroanatomical region are obtained from only one or two experiments. The only regions for which there are three or more reports on CGRP levels are the rat trigeminal ganglion and the dorsal root ganglia (DRG). In the rat trigeminal ganglion, all four experiments report no sex differences. In the DRG, the reports are mixed. In mice, two experiments report no sex differences, while in the rat DRG, two experiments report no sex differences, while one reports lower levels in females. Most experiments of CGRP levels after pain-related manipulations were not replicated.

Similarly, most experiments of CGRP receptor component levels were not replicated. For CLR, one experiment reports lower levels in female rat trigeminal ganglia, and one reports no sex differences. For RAMP1 in rat trigeminal ganglia, one experiment reports lower levels in females, and two report higher levels in females.

In aggregate, eight experiments in naïve animals report higher levels of CGRP expression in females, eight report no sex differences, and two report lower levels in females. If we exclude data based on semi-quantitative immunohistochemistry, and count only data from studies that used quantitative protein or RNA measurements, four report higher levels of CGRP expression in females, four report no sex differences, and none report lower levels in females (Table 5).

For CGRP receptor components, two experiments in naïve animals report no sex differences, three report higher levels in females, and six report lower levels in females. All but one of these data are based on quantitative measurements of protein or RNA levels.

In conclusion, rodent studies appear to report contradictory and nuanced findings regarding sex differences in the expression of CGRP and its receptor components. This is likely related to a number of experimental constraints, some of which are discussed below.

Constraints

A consensus regarding sex differences in the expression of CGRP and its receptor cannot be drawn from these studies, likely due to several constraints. An obvious constraint is the lack of rigorous data testing for sex differences in CGRP and CGRP receptor expression. We identified only sixteen rodent studies that explicitly examined sex differences in these measures. Moreover, many of these studies used small sample sizes, which may be insufficient to detect true sex differences.

A related constraint is that our inclusion criteria required quantitative data from both sexes, which excluded studies conducted in only one sex. Such single-sex studies still provide useful descriptive information about CGRP and CGRP-receptor expression, but, by design, they cannot address sex differences directly. Therefore, we did not incorporate them into the comparisons above.

Difficulty measuring CGRP

Another limitation is that, like other peptides, CGRP levels are difficult to quantify. Microdialysis measurements are hampered by the small size of the rodent brain and the small CGRP transients in awake rodents. We are aware of only two groups that performed CGRP microdialysis in rats (Mathé et al., 1996; Angelucci et al., 2001; Gruber et al., 2001). These required 60 min of dialysate collection, and even then <1 fM/ml of CGRP was collected. Achieving similar results in the much smaller brains of mice (75% smaller volume) is even less feasible.

Several well-characterized antibodies against CGRP have been successfully employed in Western Blot analyses. However, due to the extremely low concentrations of CGRP in most brain regions, it is often necessary to pool data from a large number of animals or analyze CGRP levels throughout the entire brain. These antibodies have also been used for immunohistochemistry, providing semi-quantitative data on CGRP expression.

Because CGRP concentrations are extremely low in most brain regions, many studies have relied on measuring CGRP mRNA, CALCA, rather than protein levels. These data should be interpreted with the caveat that mRNA levels may not reflect protein expression due to post-transcriptional regulation, translational control, varying protein degradation rates, and experimental errors (Nie et al., 2006; Balakrishnan et al., 2022).

Radioimmunoassay can also be used to detect relatively low levels of CGRP in neuronal tissue (Chan et al., 2019). We are aware of only one study that used this approach to study sex differences in CGRP expression (Rugarn et al., 1999). As discussed above, they report no sex differences in (presumably) whole-brain samples.

CGRP receptor components have been measured using protein and RNA quantification methods. To our knowledge, there exist no reliable antibodies for immunohistochemistry of these components, a technical limitation highlighted in a recent article on RAMP1 antibodies (Hendrikse et al., 2022).

Hormones

Further, none of the studies we reviewed controlled for potential hormone influences, which may play a role in modulating CGRP expression, and may explain some of the observed sex differences, or lack thereof (Labastida-Ramírez et al., 2019). The role of hormones is supported by the fact that the increased female prevalence of migraine begins at the age of menarche, peaks at age 40 and diminishes as women approach menopause (Lipton et al., 2007; Stewart et al., 2008). Further, the frequency of migraine attacks increases around menstrual periods, in the first trimester of pregnancy and in the perimenopause (de Vries Lentsch et al., 2021).

Although mRNA levels of CGRP in the trigeminal nucleus and ganglion of rodents appear unchanged across the estrous cycle (Moussaoul et al., 1996; Puri et al., 2005), these levels are increased by ovariectomy, and then normalized with estrogen treatment (Aggarwal et al., 2012). Estrogen treatment can also reduce CGRP levels in the trigeminal nucleus caudalis (Pardutz et al., 2002). In contrast, in the pituitary, estrogen can increase CGRP expression (Gon et al., 1990). Studies in other brain regions report conflicting findings on the effects of estrogen on CGRP expression (Labastida-Ramírez et al., 2019).

In contrast, in healthy women, plasma CGRP levels are slightly higher during the periovulatory period, compared to the menstruation period (Raffaelli et al., 2021). Compared to men, plasma CGRP levels are higher in women, and even higher in women using contraceptive pills (Valdemarsson et al., 1990). These levels increase during pregnancy, and normalize after delivery, while postmenopausal women show a decrease in CGRP.

Taken together, it remains unknown whether the results of the rodent studies described here were affected by hormonal fluctuations.

Downstream effects of CGRP

Whereas the question of sex differences in the expression of CGRP and its receptor remains unanswered, CGRP may have different functional effects in the nervous system of males and females.

As in the present analysis, a recent review of preclinical studies on the effects of CGRP application on pain-like behaviors revealed nuanced findings (Lewter et al., 2025). The authors found some evidence that CGRP produces stronger pronociceptive effects in female rodents, but insufficient data to draw firm conclusions about whether CGRP antagonism produces sex-specific effects. The authors noted that very few of the studies they reviewed were conducted with adequate statistical power to determine sex differences. We identified a similar problem in the studies reviewed here.

However, as summarized below, emerging evidence suggests that CGRP release can have different downstream effects on male and female neurons.

CGRP effects on GABA currents

In spinal dorsal horn slices, bath-applied CGRP shifts GABAA reversal potentials to significantly more positive values – but only in female mice. As a result, CGRP reduces the ability of GABA to hyperpolarize and inhibit dorsal horn neurons in females, leading to higher pain metrics, compared to males (Paige et al., 2022).

We have shown that CGRP has sex-specific effects in the central nucleus of the amygdala (CeA), a key structure in the pathogenesis of chronic pain and anxiety. We have shown that the activity of CGRP-containing parabrachial afferents to CeA increases dramatically in chronic pain conditions (Smith et al., 2023), suggesting enhanced CGRP release in these conditions. We also found that CGRP release from the parabrachial terminals in CeA produces greater postsynaptic potentiation of parabrachial glutamatergic inputs to CeA neurons in females, compared to males (Lorsung et al., 2025). As a result, in females CGRP is expected to result in greater activation of the predominantly inhibitory neurons in the CeA. Coupled with a higher level of CGRP in CeA of females with chronic pain (Presto and Neugebauer, 2022), this potentiation of CeA neurons by CGRP may contribute to sex differences in chronic pain.

In conclusion, based on currently available evidence, it remains unclear whether the expression of CGRP or its receptor in the brain differs between males and females, or whether this expression changes in a sex-specific manner during various pathologies. The development of more sensitive assays, along with studies sufficiently powered to detect sex differences, will be critical to clarify this issue. More specifically, future studies would benefit from: (i) adequately powered designs, with an a priori power analysis for sex as a biological variable and with sex incorporated as a factor in the statistical analysis; (ii) measurement of both expression and function within the same study; (iii) control for hormonal status, including estrous-cycle staging and ovariectomy with hormone replacement, and parallel measures in both sexes where feasible; (iv) the use of validated, quantitative assays with explicit reporting of variance and, ideally, individual data points, given the documented unreliability of some antibodies and the general difficulty of quantifying CGRP (v) harmonization of regions, units, and reporting to enable future quantitative synthesis; (vi) access to specific and sensitive tools to quantify CGRP and its receptor components.

Nevertheless, whether or not CGRP and its receptor components are expressed in a sex-specific manner, emerging evidence does suggest that CGRP functions differently in the CNS of males and females. These differences may contribute to sex-specific differences in chronic pain.
