Preconditioning with StemRegenin 1 enhances human adipose stromal/stem cells proliferation, migration, and protection against antimycin A
Authors: Jing Zhao, Bin Yu, Julie Fradette, Borhane Annabi, Maria Petropavlovskaia, Nicoletta Eliopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Adipose-derived stromal/stem cells (ASCs) are promising for the treatment of many diseases, including tissue injury or degeneration associated with serious disease and high morbidity. The extent of cell therapy effectiveness, however, may be limited by the lower survival of implanted cells in environments of tissue damage. Therefore, strategies to improve cell survival are important, such as by pretreating/preconditioning cells with a beneficial agent. We investigated the pretreatment of human ASCs (hASCs) with StemRegenin 1 (SR1), a purine derivative used in clinical protocols for in vitro hematopoietic stem/progenitor cell expansion. We pretreated hASCs with SR1 and analyzed the resulting cells (SR1-hASCs) as compared to non-treated cells (NT-hASCs). We noted that treatment with SR1 significantly increased the proliferation and migration of hASCs, as well as their secretion of paracrine factors of interest, and did not affect their cell differentiation capacity. Furthermore, when these SR1-hASCs were subsequently exposed to antimycin A, a mitochondrial respiratory chain inhibitor, they showed significantly higher antioxidative, anti-apoptotic, and pro-survival abilities as compared to NT-hASCs. Since oxidative stress and other harsh environments result from tissue damage, our results support that the preconditioning of hASCs with SR1 may enhance their protective, reparative, and regenerative, and thus therapeutic, efficacy.

Introduction

Mesenchymal stromal/stem cells (MSCs) have demonstrated effectiveness for many preclinical and clinical applications. These cells provide several advantages for use in cell therapies. They are plentiful and accessible in humans of all ages; can be obtained with minimal morbidity from several sources, such as bone marrow and adipose tissue; can be easily expanded and manipulated in vitro; and can be implanted in patients.1,2 Also, efficacy and safety testing of these cells, such as karyotype analysis,3 can be conducted prior to their implantation in humans. Clinical trials have validated the safety of MSCs.4,5 Another advantage of MSCs is their ability to migrate/home to sites of tissue injury and exert their effects where needed.2,6

MSCs can lead to beneficial actions due to their wide differentiation capabilities as well as the numerous paracrine effects of their secretome.1,2,7 For instance, MSCs can participate in the protection and repair of tissue injury-induced damage through their ability to differentiate into various cell types and mainly through their paracrine effects.1,2,7,8 The latter is done through their secretion of factors protecting cells from apoptosis, inducing the proliferation of surviving cells, decreasing inflammation, promoting angiogenesis, and stimulating the differentiation of endogenous stem cells.1,2,7,8 Although early research on MSCs mainly involved bone-marrow-derived MSCs, there are now many other recognized sources, such as amniotic fluid, umbilical cord blood, and adipose tissue.1,2,7 Preclinical and clinical applications of MSCs are plentiful. For instance, in rodent models, we and other teams have shown the protective and reparative effects of MSCs, derived from bone marrow as well as from adipose tissue, for acute kidney injury.9,10,11,12,13,14 Also, MSCs have shown favorable clinical potential/outcomes, such as for graft vs. host disease and osteogenesis imperfecta.4,5 There are currently various conditions and numerous clinical trials, as listed on ClinicalTrials.gov, for which MSCs are being explored/used.1,2

Although cell therapy, such as with MSCs, shows promise in tissue protection, repair, and regeneration, it can be limited by the poor survival of implanted cells in environments of tissue damage.15 Enhancing the survival of the MSCs and their capacity for repair in stressful/harsh environments would improve their effectiveness. One strategy is to precondition, i.e., pretreat, these cells with a pharmacological agent with a pro-survival mode of action prior to their use or implantation. One such agent that we evaluated in the present study, using primary human adipose tissue-derived MSCs, is StemRegenin 1 (SR1).

SR1 is a purine derivative and aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) antagonist known for promoting the in vitro expansion of hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells.16,17,18 SR1 has proven to be clinically safe for use on stem cells subsequently implanted in patients.16,17,18 For instance, a phase 1/2 study demonstrated the safety and efficacy of SR1 added in the culture to expand CD34+ hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells from umbilical cord blood.18 It led to a 330-fold higher amount of CD34+ cells and subsequently to increased hematopoietic recovery without graft failure in patients (17/17) with hematological malignancies who were transplanted with these cells.18

However, very little is known about the effects of SR1 on other types of stem cells, including MSCs. We were therefore interested in revealing how SR1 may affect MSCs, more specifically human adipose tissue-derived MSCs,19,20 commonly referred to as human adipose-derived stromal/stem cells (hereon referred to as hASCs). In this novel study, we performed several analyses on hASCs following their preconditioning/pretreatment with SR1. For instance, our experiments revealed that SR1 significantly increased the proliferation and migration abilities of hASCs along with their secretion of hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), stem cell factor (SCF), and stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1). In addition, preconditioning hASCs with SR1 provided them with pro-survival, anti-apoptotic, and antioxidative protective effects when exposed to antimycin A (AMA) toxicity.

Results

Pretreatment with SR1 increases the proliferation of hASCs

To determine if preconditioning hASCs with SR1 affects their growth, cells were exposed to SR1 at 1 μM for 7–9 days and then assessed for their cellular proliferation ability over time. As shown in Figure 1A, the cells from all three donors revealed a significantly higher increase in cell proliferation at 11 days vs. 1 day after prior treatment with SR1, as compared to hASCs that were not pretreated with SR1 (p < 0.0001 in each case). Differentiation ability of hASCs is maintained after SR1 preconditioning

To establish whether pretreatment of hASCs with SR1 affects their differentiation ability, hASCs following 7- to 9-day exposure to 1 μM SR1 (SR1-hASCs), as well as NT-hASCs, were exposed to differentiation-inducing conditions in vitro. Results illustrated that the differentiation capacity of hASCs was maintained in cells that had been preconditioned with SR1. As shown in Figure 1D, the SR1-hASCs, in addition to the NT-hASCs, demonstrated osteogenic, adipogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation as determined by alizarin red S or oil red O staining of calcium deposits or lipid droplets or by Alcian blue positivity in differentiated cells, respectively.

Other than differentiation capacity, we also verified that exposure to SR1 for 7–9 days did not affect cell morphology or phenotypic markers. As shown in Figure S1, cell size and shape, as well as the distribution of actin filaments, were identical between NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs. In addition, similar expression of typical MSC markers was observed in NT and SR1-treated cells (Figure S2).

Treatment of hASCs with SR1 affects AHR and CYP1B1 gene expression

Since SR1 has been described as an antagonist of AhR, we first verified if SR1 inhibited the AhR-induced signaling and assessed the expression of cytochrome P450 1B1 (CYP1B1), a direct target of AhR, by RT-qPCR. As seen in Figure 2A (left), CYP1B1 expression was 0.06 ± 0.01, significantly lower than 0.94 ± 0.06 in NT-hASCs, by about 16-fold (p < 0.005), following the SR1 treatment, thus confirming the AhR antagonism by SR1. Interestingly, expression of AHR was increased at the same time by approximately 1.5-fold (Figure 2A, right, 1.16 ± 0.08 in SR1-hASCs as compared to 0.79 ± 0.02 NT-hASCs, p < 0.05). To verify if this change in mRNA led to an increase in AhR protein levels, western blot analysis was conducted using total proteins isolated from the cell lysates. As shown in Figures 2B and 2C, the expression of AhR is significantly stronger in SR1-hASCs as compared to that in NT-hASCs (by about 3.5-fold, p < 0.005). As seen in Figure 3A, even in the absence of SDF-1, there was a significantly higher amount of migrated SR1-hASCs vs. NT-hASCs, i.e., 34.04 ± 1.52 vs. 15.38 ± 0.76 (p < 0.0001). Levels of hASCs’ secreted factors are modulated by SR1 preconditioning

To reveal if pretreatment of hASCs with SR1 leads to an increase in secreted factors, the levels of HGF, SCF, SDF-1, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) were measured by ELISA using the conditioned media (CMs) from SR1-hASCs and NT-ASCs. Results showed a significant increase in HGF, SCF, and SDF-1 levels and a significant decrease in VEGF levels following SR1 preconditioning of the hASCs. As shown in Figure 3C, SR1-hASCs, as compared to NT-hASCs, were found to secrete 5.81 ± 0.79 ng of HGF per 106 cells as compared to 4.04 ± 0.52 (p < 0.0005), 1.16 ± 0.19 ng of SCF per 106 cells as compared to 0.93 ± 0.15 (p < 0.05), 11.99 ± 3.23 ng of SDF-1 per 106 cells as compared to 9.16 ± 2.23 (p < 0.05), and 14.61 ± 2.78 ng of VEGF per 106 cells as compared to 22.36 ± 5.21 (p < 0.05).

Pretreatment of hASCs with SR1 reduces AMA-induced effects on cell survival and apoptosis

We next aimed to discover if prior treatment with SR1 protects hASCs from toxic agent AMA, a blocker of the mitochondrial respiratory chain at the level of complex III. To evaluate any protection afforded by SR1 on AMA-induced effects on the survival/apoptosis of hASCs, SR1-hASCs and NT-hASCs were exposed to AMA for 7 days, and then cell viability and apoptosis were assessed, as described in the materials and methods.

With regards to cell survival, the results shown in Figure 4A indicate a significantly higher survival of SR1-hASCs as compared to NT-hASCs (p < 0001), i.e., a median of 66.00 vs. 53.50% cell survival, respectively. SR1 preconditioning of hASCs impacts protein expression after AMA exposure

To assess whether pretreating hASCs with SR1 affects the expression of proteins linked with cell survival, apoptosis, and oxidative stress, western blot analyses were performed on SR1-hASCs and NT-hASCs following 7-day AMA exposure. As shown in Figure 7A, hASCs preconditioned with SR1 showed significantly higher protein expression of phospho-p44/42 MAPK (P-Erk1/2), and Bcl-2 and lower expression of p21 (p < 0.01, p < 0.05, and p < 0.05, respectively). Importantly, expression of antioxidant enzymes, catalase and glutathione peroxidase 1 (GPx1), was significantly increased, as compared to NT-hASCs (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively). Discussion

In this study, we revealed the direct effects of SR1 on hASCs as well as the protective actions that SR1 preconditioning affords hASCs exposed to AMA toxicity.

First, pretreatment of hASCs with SR1 led to a significant rise in the proliferation of hASCs derived from each of the three donors. We also demonstrated that the capacity of hASCs to undergo tri-lineage differentiation upon specific induction was preserved following SR1 preconditioning.

Since SR1’s action has been described to involve the ligand-dependent transcription factor AhR, we assessed the gene and protein expression of the AhR in hASCs, as well as the expression of its direct target, CYP1B1,21,22,23,24 and confirmed that SR1 inhibited AhR signaling. However, we observed that preconditioning of the hASCs with SR1 significantly augmented expression of the AhR gene and protein, respectively. To the best of our knowledge, this finding has not been previously reported, and its significance is not yet clear. One can speculate that an increase in AhR expression is caused by the loss of the downstream signaling, potentially including a feedback loop. Interestingly, the AhR ligand TCDD prevented/reversed this SR1-induced increase in AhR expression in hASCs, thus suggesting that TCDD may compete with SR1 for the binding site on AhR. A study on human MSCs isolated from fresh bone marrow aspirates showed the implication of AhR signaling in cell proliferation, metabolism, apoptosis, and the cell cycle.23 Consistent with our findings on hASCs’ proliferation and CYP1B1 expression, SR1 was reported to augment MSCs’ proliferation, and when the activation of AhR was evaluated, SR1 was found to inhibit AhR, based on decreased mRNA and protein expression levels of CYP1A1 and CYP1B1.23 One report indicated that a long exposure of cells to SR1 may more significantly decrease CYP1B1, as occurred in human hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells after 7-day expansion with SR1.22 CYP1B1 is constitutively expressed in various tissues and plays multiple roles, including deactivation of toxins and metabolization of various exogenous and endogenous compounds, and has been reported to contribute to the generation of ROS.25,26 Inhibition of its expression may therefore underlie the therapeutic effects of SR1 observed in our study. A recent Viewpoint publication24 discussed the various implications of the AhR in MSCs, such as in human bone-marrow-derived MSCs, where the expression and activation of the AhR have been demonstrated,24,27 for instance, the potential in targeting AhR to delay MSC senescence and in developing improved treatments for various illnesses/conditions with aberrant AhR activity that are age related.24 Among its roles in MSCs, the AhR acts as a regulatory factor in MSCs’ immunomodulatory actions, and a recent study using bone-marrow-derived MSCs of Ahr+/+ and Ahr−/− mice showed that the mechanism is done through ubiquitination of the eukaryotic elongation factor-2 kinase.28

In addition, in our present work, we revealed that the migration ability of hASCs was significantly augmented by a pretreatment with SR1. Also, this increased migration was further enhanced in the presence of the chemotactic agent SDF-1, also known as CXCL12, while it was prevented by an antibody against the SDF-1 receptor CXCR4. SDF-1, which is also referred to as CXCL12, is involved in a variety of normal and pathological effects.29,30 It has been noted to act through binding to its two receptors, CXCR4 and CXCR7,30 to mediate the recruitment and migration/homing of MSCs and other stem cells as well.29,30

CXCR4 has also been demonstrated to be needed for MSCs’ migration/homing, such as in a study by Liu et al.29 on mouse bone-marrow-derived MSCs, where the effects following their preconditioning with hypoxia exposure included augmented CXCR4, an action also eliminated by an antibody against CXCR4, as we also observed. In another investigation, human bone marrow MSCs that were treated with heparin to block SDF-1/CXCR4 signaling showed decreased homing in a mouse ear-wound model.31 Further, increased levels of SDF-1 in the ovaries and serum of rats with premature ovarian insufficiency caused by chemotherapy led to a higher number of amnion-derived MSCs expressing CXCR4 homing to the ovaries after systemic administration, an effect prevented by hindering the SDF-1/CXCR4 axis.32 Improving cell migration is desired, as additional studies have reported that the ability of MSCs, including ASCs, to migrate to sites of injury augments cell survival and regeneration.33,34 Our current findings of SR1’s capacity to improve the migration of hASCs, and especially in the presence of SDF-1, are relevant for the utilization of SR1-pretreated hASCs for cell therapy, as the upregulation of SDF-1 has been reported to occur in environments (or as a consequence) of tissue damage/degeneration/hypoxia.35,36,37,38,39,40 For instance, in studies on ASCs’ administration in animal models of disease where SDF-1 was detected, such as in stress urinary incontinence and diabetic bladder dysfunction in rats, ASCs appeared to migrate to the injured urethra and bladder, respectively, thus to the sites of tissue damage.34,41,42

We showed that hASCs that were pretreated with SR1 secreted significantly more HGF, SCF, and SDF-1 under normal conditions, all of which are reported cell-protective factors. One study on human placenta-derived MSCs exposed to benzopyrene showed that SR1 increased the expression of HGF in these MSCs.43 In addition, they noted augmented levels of VEGF,43 whereas we saw lowered VEGF in hASCs. However, our observations were in unstimulated cells that were not exposed to benzopyrene or another toxic agent. In both studies, HGF was increased, which is favorable for various applications of MSCs, including hASCs, as HGF has been reported to exert several effects, such as increasing proliferation and suppressing apoptosis/senescence, as has also been observed with SCF and SDF-1.44,45,46,47

Moreover, to determine if preconditioning hASCs had any protective actions against a toxic agent, we subsequently exposed these cells to AMA, a blocker of the mitochondrial respiratory chain. AMA inhibits complex III of the mitochondrial electron transport chain, leading to overproduction of ROS and apoptosis.48,49,50 We showed that the AMA-induced toxic effects on hASCs were significantly decreased by prior pretreatment of these cells with SR1. SR1 preconditioning led to higher survival, as it exerted a protective effect on AMA-induced early apoptosis and late apoptosis or death of hASCs. We revealed a significant reduction in intracellular ROS formation in SR1-hASCs as compared to NT-hASCs exposed to AMA. Upon further analysis of the oxidative stress imparted by AMA exposure, we also noted that preconditioning of hASCs with SR1 significantly reduced the AMA-induced rise in p53, p21, and p27 levels in these cells. Thus, these results together indicate that the AMA-induced rise in intracellular ROS production, increased levels of tumor-suppressor and transcriptional regulator p53 and p53-regulated cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK) inhibitors p21 and p27 in hASCs, and cell apoptosis and death can be reduced/prevented by prior treatment of hASCs with SR1.

Further supporting that SR1 pretreatment can protect hASCs against oxidative damage and cell apoptosis/death, we looked at protein expression following AMA exposure and observed that SR1 affects the expression of proteins linked with cell proliferation, survival, and apoptosis, as well as with oxidative stress. More specifically, we observed significant rises in the expression of P-Erk1/2 and Bcl-2, a significant decrease in the expression of p21, in addition to significant increases in the expression of catalase and GPx1.

AMA has also been employed in other investigations on hASCs to induce hypoxic conditions and mitochondrial oxidative stress.51,52 In one study, instead of SR1, it employed the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine and demonstrated its protective actions against AMA.51 In a different study, also on hASCs, AMA-induced oxidative stress was reduced by co-exposure of cells to the mitochondrial-targeted antioxidant Mito-TEMPO.52 Several agents and/or preconditioning strategies have been reported to improve the beneficial properties and clinical potential of MSCs.29,53,54,55,56,57,58,59 Furthermore, other than MSCs, including ASCs, or hematopoietic stem/progenitor cells, human endothelial progenitor cells (EPCs) have also been evaluated following treatment with SR1.60 SR1 exposure afforded EPCs several protective effects against replicative cellular senescence and oxidative stress, including higher proliferation and migration as well as lower production of ROS and decreased expression of p53 and p21.60 The latter was also observed in our current study using hASCs. Our data reveal that SR1 treatment benefits not only human hematopoietic stem cells and EPCs but also ASCs and their effects. Since each type of stem cell or progenitor cell has its own set of properties, i.e., source, differentiation/paracrine abilities, and possible applications, our findings extend the applicability of SR1 pretreatment for enhancing cell therapy approaches. ASCs, with their abundance and ease of harvest, multilineage differentiation, and robust paracrine activity, are promising for a vast variety of applications, including tissue repair, regeneration, engineering, and metabolic and immunomodulatory therapies.20,61,62 Our study, therefore, indicates the potential of SR1 as a clinically translatable agent for preconditioning of hASCs to generate cell products with superior viability, migration capacity, and functional potency.

Moreover, our present investigations establish that treating hASCs with SR1 enhances their proliferative and migration abilities and secretion of several beneficial factors while preserving their differentiation capacity. In addition, when these SR1-preconditioned cells are exposed to AMA-induced toxicity, they exert higher antioxidative, anti-apoptotic, and pro-survival effects as compared to hASCs not treated with SR1, demonstrating a valuable role of SR1. Since oxidative stress and other non-favorable implantation environments result from tissue insult, damage, or degeneration, the results of our study support that preconditioning hASCs with SR1 may significantly augment their protective, reparative, and regenerative potential. Our in vitro studies on hASCs with SR1 may serve as an important pre-translational step that is needed to improve the efficacy of hASC-based therapies. We provided key and specific information and approaches that may be useful clinically to enhance efficacy in treating diseases or injuries where a diseased/damaged environment would impact hASC survival and/or effectiveness. We showed that pretreatment with SR1 before exposure to AMA increased the efficacy of hASCs in the relevant AMA in vitro model of a harsh environment. The overall aim of preconditioning stem/stromal cells, such as hASCs, with SR1 is to enhance their functionality in cell therapy applications.

In summary, our study shows that SR1 can be used as a versatile preconditioning agent for hASCs, as it can improve several beneficial properties of these cells. We demonstrated that SR1 can enhance hASCs’ proliferation, migration, and secretion of key trophic factors without affecting their differentiation potential, surface marker profile, or morphology. In addition, we revealed that SR1 preconditioning affords significant protection to these cells against mitochondrial/oxidative stress and apoptosis, hallmarks of the hostile surroundings cells often encountered after implantation. By ameliorating both the survival and functional potency of hASCs, SR1 preconditioning provides a promising approach to improve the therapeutic efficacy of various hASC-based therapies.

Materials and methods

Cell culture conditions

hASCs were kindly provided by Dr. Julie Fradette (Université Laval, LOEX, Quebec) according to protocols approved by the institutional review board of the Center de Recherche du CHU de Québec-Université Laval and after receiving informed written consent from the donors (#DR-002-1117). Cells were isolated from subcutaneous lipo-aspirated fat from three women donors aged 38, 40, and 46 with a mean body mass index of 22.4 ± 2.8 (SD), as previously described and used.19,53 The frozen hASCs were thawed and plated at 6,000 cells/cm2 in 15-cm-diameter cell culture dishes (Becton Dickinson [BD] Falcon, Franklin Lakes, NJ) in complete medium. The complete medium comprised DMEM/F12 medium (Invitrogen-Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Wisent, St. Bruno, Quebec). The culture medium from hASCs was replaced with fresh medium every 2–3 days. Once the cells attained a confluency of 80%, they were passaged and used for the described experiments at passages 3–5. Plated cells were maintained in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2.

Generation of SR1-pretreated hASCs

hASCs were plated in 15-cm-diameter cell culture dishes (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence (NT) or presence of SR1 (Cayman, Ann Arbor, MI) at 1 μM for 7–9 days, in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days, thus generating the NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs, respectively. We conducted a time course to determine the optimal duration for the preconditioning of the cells for our studies. Culturing for 7, 8, or 9 days was similarly optimal. We mainly used 7 days, but in some cases, due to timing, it went up to 9 days.

Quantification of hASCs’ proliferation

hASCs were plated at 500–1,600 cells/cm2 in 24-well plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM from day 1 for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. The number of viable cells in each of the wells over time was determined using the Alamar Blue (Invitrogen, Eugene, OR) assay on days 1, 4, 7, and 11 post-cell plating. Briefly, Alamar Blue was added to the cells at a 1:10 dilution. After 3 h of incubation at 37°C with 5% CO2, the supernatants were transferred to 96-well plates. The optical densities of the wells were measured at 570/600 nm with a standard spectrophotometer. The absorbance at 600 nm was subtracted from that at 570 nm. The cell-free blank value served as background and was subtracted from each value. Cell proliferation was represented as a fold change compared to day 1 post-cell plating.

Western blot analysis of hASCs for Erk1/2 and Akt expression

hASCs were plated in 10-cm-diameter tissue culture plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. The cells were then rinsed with cold DPBS, lysed with lysis buffer (20 mM Tris [pH 7.5], 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 2.5 mM sodium pyrophosphate, 1 mM beta-glycerophosphate, 1 mM Na3VO4, 1 μg/mL leupeptin, and 1 mM PMSF). Protein concentrations were determined by Bio-Rad Protein Assay kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA). Whole-cell lysates containing 15–30 μg total protein were separated by 4%–20% Precise Protein Gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, IL) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The membranes were then incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the solution of TBST (10 mM Tris·HCl [pH 8.0], 150 mM NaCl, and 0.05% Tween 20) supplemented with 10% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad Laboratories). Subsequently, the membranes were probed overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies of P-Erk1/2 and phospho-Akt (P-Akt) (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA). The membranes were then washed with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. The bound primary antibodies were visualized by incubating for 1 h with a suitable secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX). The membranes were washed and developed using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (ZmTech Scientific, Montreal, Quebec). Blots were later stripped with western blot stripping buffer (ZmTech Scientific) and reprobed with antibodies against total Erk (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) or total Akt (Cell Signaling Technology), which were used for normalization. Densitometry of bands was done using Fiji/ImageJ software.63 Phospho-to-total ratio was used for further quantification of the fold change in expression in SR1-hASCs over NT-hASCs.

Assessment of differentiation ability of hASCs

hASCs were plated in 6-well plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM from day 1 for 7–9 days, in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. When the cells reached 90% confluency, hASCs were incubated in differentiation media. For adipogenic differentiation, cells were maintained in complete medium supplemented with 1 μM dexamethasone, 0.5 mM isobutylmethylxanthine, 60 μM indomethacin (Sigma-Aldrich), and 5 μg/mL insulin (Jewish General Hospital) for 10 days and then stained with oil red O (Sigma-Aldrich, Oakville, Ontario). For osteogenic differentiation, cells were maintained in complete medium supplemented with 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, 200 μM L-ascorbic acid 2-phosphate, and 0.1 μM dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich) for 7 weeks and then stained with alizarin red S (Sigma-Aldrich). We chose to culture the cells for 7 weeks in the osteogenic medium in case any potential differences—especially in the later stages of differentiation, such as more extensive matrix mineralization—would not be observed after staining within a more typical 4-week period. This extended duration allowed us to more fully detect any potential effect of SR1 preconditioning on the osteogenic differentiation capacity of hASCs.

For chondrogenic differentiation, the StemXVivo Human/Mouse Chondrogenic kit (R&D Systems) was used. In brief, cells were resuspended in StemXVivo Chondrogenic Base Media, and aliquots of 2.5 × 105 cells were transferred to 15 mL conical tubes and centrifuged for 3 min at 300 × g to form pellets. Supernatants were carefully removed, and completed StemXVivo Chondrogenic Differentiation Media (0.5 mL) were added. The pellets were cultured for 3 weeks with a fresh chondrogenic differentiation medium change every 2–3 days. They were thereafter washed with cold PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 10 min at 4°C, stained with 1% Alcian blue 8GX (Sigma-Aldrich) in 3% acetic acid for 1 h at room temperature, and washed with 3% acetic acid. Images of the adipogenic and osteogenic differentiated cells were taken with a Leica DMIL microscope, while images of the chondrogenic pellets were taken using a Leica MZ10F microscope.

Cell migration assay

The effects of SR1 pretreatment on hASCs’ migration were investigated using Transwell chambers (BD Falcon, Le Pont-de-Claix, France) with an 8.0 μm pore size PET track-etched membrane. NT-hASCs or SR1-hASCs were plated in the inserts (upper chambers) at 2 × 104 cells/cm2. Serum-free medium alone or containing chemotactic agent human stromal cell-derived factor 1 (SDF-1) (Bio Basic, Markham, Ontario) at 150 ng/mL was added to the lower chambers. To assess the neutralization effect of anti-CXCR4, cells were pre-incubated with anti-human CXCR4 monoclonal antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX) at 10 μg/mL for 30 min at room temperature before the chemotaxis test. After removal of the anti-CXCR4 antibody, cells were further incubated for 18 h in a 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Non-migrated cells were removed from the upper surface of the membrane with a wet cotton swab. The migrated cells on the lower surface of the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 40 min and then stained using a solution of 0.3% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich) in 20% methanol. The cells were photographed under a Leica DMLB2 microscope using the 10× objective. Migrated cell numbers were counted in each randomly chosen microscope field. At least 13 random visual fields were used per group.

CM analysis

hASCs were cultured in complete medium, in the absence (NT) or presence of SR1 at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. The resulting NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs were each then sub-cultured in complete medium for 7 days in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. CMs from each cell preparation were collected and used to determine the secreted levels of human HGF, SCF, SDF-1, and VEGF using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the results are represented as ng/106 cells.

Cell survival assay

hASCs were cultured in complete medium, in the absence (NT) or presence of SR1 at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. The resulting NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs were each then sub-cultured in 24-well plates (BD Falcon) in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium (RPMI(−)) (Wisent) with low glucose (1,000 mg/L) in the presence of 7.5 μM AMA (Sigma-Aldrich), a blocker of the mitochondrial respiratory chain at the level of complex III, for 7 days in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Cell viability was measured by the Alamar Blue (Invitrogen) assay. In brief, Alamar Blue was added to the cells at a 1:10 dilution. After 3 h of incubation in a 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere, the cell supernatants were transferred to 96-well plates. The optical densities of the wells were measured at 570/600 nm with a standard spectrophotometer. The absorbance at 600 nm was subtracted from that at 570 nm. The cell-free blank value served as background and was subtracted from each value. Cell viability was represented as cell survival (%) compared to that prior to AMA exposure.

Apoptosis assay

hASCs were cultured in complete medium, in the absence (NT) or presence of SR1 at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. The NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs were then sub-cultured in 6-cm-diameter cell culture dishes (BD Falcon) and then treated with ischemic-mimicking conditions, i.e., serum-free and low-glucose (1,000 mg/L) RPMI medium in the absence or presence of 7.5 μM AMA for 7 days in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. All cells, including adherent and floating, were collected, centrifuged, and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells/mL in 100 μL 1× binding buffer (BD Biosciences Pharmingen, Mississauga, ON) to which 5 μL annexin V FITC and 10 μL propidium iodide (BD Biosciences Pharmingen) were then added. Following a 15-min incubation in the dark at room temperature, 100 μL 1×binding buffer was added per tube, and the cells were analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Data analysis was performed with FCSExpress (De Novo Software, Los Angeles, CA).

Intracellular ROS quantification

hASCs were plated in 15-cm-diameter plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence (NT) or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. Then, after removal of the medium, NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs were washed with DPBS and plated in 6-cm-diameter plates (BD Falcon). Afterward, these cells were treated with ischemic conditions, i.e., serum-free and low-glucose (1,000 mg/L) RPMI 1640 medium in the absence or presence of 7.5 μM AMA for 3 or 7 days in a 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. Cells were collected after trypsinization. Generation of ROS in the hASCs was measured using an oxidation-sensitive fluorescent probe, 2′7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) (Sigma-Aldrich), a method based on the ROS-dependent oxidation of DCFH-DA to DCF. More specifically, about 2 × 105 cells were collected and washed twice with PBS. They were then incubated with 15 μM DCFH-DA at 37°C for 20 min in the dark. Fluorescent DCF intensity was detected by a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences) when exposed to an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and an emission of 535 nm. Data analysis was performed with FCSExpress (De Novo Software). ROS levels are shown as the MFI of DCF.

Detection of p21, p27, and p53 by immunofluorescence

hASCs were plated in 15-cm-diameter plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM for 7–9 days, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. Then, cells were washed, sub-cultured in 4-well chamber slides (BD Falcon), and treated with ischemic-mimicking conditions, i.e., serum-free and low-glucose (1,000 mg/L) RPMI containing 7.5 μM AMA for 5 days in a 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified incubator. After removal of the treatment, cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. After aspirating the fixative, cells were permeabilized by ice-cold 100% methanol for 10 min at −20°C and then washed with PBS. The cells were incubated with blocking buffer (Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA), stained with primary antibodies against p21, p27, or p53 (Cell Signaling Technology), and then incubated with the secondary antibody, i.e., fluorochrome-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (H+L), F(ab')2 fragment (Alexa Fluor 594 conjugate) (Cell Signaling Technology), and finally counterstained with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Invitrogen, Eugene, OR). Fluorescence on slides was viewed under a Leica DM LB2 microscope (Leica Microsystems, Bannockburn, IL), and images were acquired using Leica Application Suite v.3.40 software (Leica Microsystems). 18–25 random fields per well were photographed. Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected using a threshold, and total fluorescence signal intensities of each image were determined using ImageJ software, represented as the raw integrated density (RawIntDen). DAPI-stained cells were counted with ImageJ.

Western blot analysis of hASCs exposed to AMA

hASCs were plated in 10-cm-diameter plates (BD Falcon) and cultured in complete medium, in the absence or presence of SR1 (Cayman) at 1 μM for 7–9 days in a humidified 37°C incubator with 5% CO2, with fresh medium changes every 2–3 days. Then, the medium was removed, and the NT-hASCs and SR1-hASCs were washed with DPBS and treated with 7.5 μM AMA in serum-free and low-glucose (1,000 mg/L) RPMI 1640 medium for 7 days in a 37°C with 5% CO2 humidified atmosphere. The cells were then rinsed with cold DPBS and lysed with lysis buffer, and the lysates were analyzed by western blot as described above but now using primary antibodies against P-Erk1/2, p21, Bcl-2, catalase, SOD1, HO-1, or GPx1 (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, MA). The membranes were then washed with TBS containing 0.1% Tween 20. The bound primary antibodies were visualized by incubating for 1 h with a suitable secondary antibody conjugated with HRP (Bethyl Laboratories, Montgomery, TX). The membranes were washed and developed using a chemiluminescent HRP substrate (ZmTech Scientific, Montreal, Quebec). Blots were later stripped with western blot stripping buffer (ZmTech Scientific) and reprobed with antibodies against total Erk (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA) or GAPDH (Cell Signaling Technology) as a loading control.

Statistical analyses

One-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc multiple comparison test, two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, or a t test was used depending on the experiment, as indicated in the figure legends. In all cases, p ≤ 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.
