Towards an enzyme cascade synthesis of the bulk chemical acrylic acid
Authors: Haodong Zhao, Chunying Wang, Shiqing Zhang, Qian Wang, Huanyu Chu, Jinying Yang, Lei Zhang, Zhanmei Xu, Bingzhao Zhuo, Cui Liu, Yufeng Mao, Lina Lu, Xu Zhang, Huiru Zhao, Yanhe Ma, Xiang Sheng, Yuwan Liu, Huifeng Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Acrylic acid is a bulk chemical predominantly produced via fossil-based processes. Here, we repurpose and engineer a thiamine diphosphate (ThDP)-dependent enzyme to catalyze direct \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− elimination from dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) for acrylic acid synthesis. Quantum chemical calculations reveal the catalytic mechanism involving isomerization, enol-ThDP intermediate formation, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− elimination, and hydrolysis. Using an automated high-throughput screening platform, we perform directed evolution and obtain a 13.6-fold improved variant, designated acrylic acid synthase (AAS). We establish an enzymatic methanol-to-acrylic acid (MAAP) pathway, achieving a titer of 4.3 g L−1, a productivity of 268.9 mg L−1 h−1, and 99.5% conversion. This work establishes a high-efficiency biomanufacturing paradigm for bulk acrylic acid, and expands the catalytic repertoire of ThDP-dependent enzymes for sustainable one-carbon conversion.

Introduction

Acrylic acid, a cornerstone industrial chemical, serves as the primary precursor for manufacturing diapers, coatings, paints, and adhesives1. According to industry projections, the market is anticipated to reach 8.16 million tons by 2025, with further expansion to 10.41 million tons by 2030, reflecting a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) exceeding 5% during the 2025–2030 period2. Currently, industrial acrylic acid production predominantly relies on the catalytic oxidation of propylene or propane3. However, these conventional petrochemical routes operate under energy-intensive conditions and generate environmentally detrimental byproducts, including CO2, acetic acid, and toxic acrolein4. Such challenges underscore the urgent need to develop sustainable alternatives that align with global decarbonization goals. Consequently, significant research efforts have been directed toward biosynthesis routes utilizing renewable feedstocks, aiming to mitigate environmental impacts while addressing the limitations of finite fossil resources.

Currently, four distinct biosynthetic pathways for acrylic acid production have been engineered within natural metabolic frameworks, including the glucose-glycerol pathway5, glycerol pathway6, malonyl-CoA pathway7, and β-alanine pathway8, respectively. Although substantial metabolic engineering efforts have been devoted, these native metabolism-derived pathways still demonstrate suboptimal performance in both titer and productivity. Herein, according to structural and functional screening, we identify and engineer a thiamine diphosphate (ThDP)-dependent enzyme to catalyze the direct elimination of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− from DHAP, yielding acrylic acid (Fig. 1a). By dissecting the catalytic mechanism of this enzyme and engineering it via directed evolution, we establish and refine an in vitro enzymatic route from methanol to acrylic acid. Our findings provide valuable engineering principles and rational strategies for constructing highly efficient biosynthetic pathways for acrylic acid.

Results

In silico mining of candidate proteins

Typically, the ThDP cofactor can catalyze the umpolung carboligation of aldehydes by forming a covalently bound enamine intermediate, which either condenses with another aldehyde molecule11, or undergoes the removal of hydroxyl groups from adjacent carbons to yield enol-ThDP compounds. Taking the repurposed phosphoketolase as an example12, after glycolaldehyde binds to ThDP, proton transfer and dehydration occur to form the enol-ThDP intermediate. This is followed by tautomerization to yield 2-acetyl-thiamine pyrophosphate (AcThDP). Finally, inorganic phosphate (Pi) attacks AcThDP to form acetyl phosphate. Similarly, when the substrate is D-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (D-G3P), its binding to ThDP is followed by proton transfer and dehydration to generate the enol-ThDP intermediate. This intermediate readily undergoes \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− elimination, leading to the formation of an acryloyl-ThDP complex, which can be feasibly hydrolyzed to produce acrylic acid (Fig. 1b). Furthermore, the standard Gibbs free energy change (ΔrG’°) for the conversion of D-G3P to acrylic acid was calculated to be −121.2 kJ mol−1 according to the Equilibrator database13. Accordingly, the production of acrylic acid from D-G3P via ThDP-dependent enzymes is feasible.

To identify possible proteins for the synthesis of acrylic acid from D-G3P, we employed a covalent ligand docking-based enzyme virtual screening strategy14. We selected the enol-ThDP covalent intermediate for covalent ligand docking, since it represents a critical intermediate in the conversion of D-G3P to acrylic acid. Initially, we identified all non-redundant protein structures bound to ThDP from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). To enhance the likelihood of discovering active enzymes, we further integrated homologous genes corresponding to these proteins from the AlphaFold Database (AFDB) (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). After removing redundant sequences, we established a curated dataset comprising over 500 unique sequences, which served as the search database for covalent ligand docking (Supplementary Fig. 1). The covalent docking protocol was implemented using RosettaLigand to evaluate the substrate-binding affinity of the enol-ThDP covalent intermediate with the proteins. After the three-stage docking and minimization process, the top 20 proteins were selected as potential candidates based on the DockingScore (Supplementary Table 1). Actually, these proteins exhibit diverse catalytic functions, encompassing decarboxylase, acetolactate synthase, and N2-(2-carboxyethyl)arginine synthase (CEAS), along with several functionally uncharacterized members (Supplementary Table 1).

Among these candidates (designated as ProThDP1-20), ProThDP4 and ProThDP20 were obtained as inclusion bodies when expressed in E. coli. ProThDP16 and ProThDP19 showed no detectable activity toward D-G3P. ProThDP2 emerged as the most active enzyme, generating 0.59 mM acrylic acid with a yield of 11.8% from 5 mM D-G3P (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). Although ProThDP2 remains functionally uncharacterized, it shares 75.92% sequence similarity with ProThDP1, a previously characterized CEAS known to catalyze the formation of N2-(2-carboxyethyl)arginine from D-G3P and L-arginine15. Using D-G3P and L-arginine as substrates, we demonstrated that ProThDP2 catalyzes the formation of N2-(2-carboxyethyl)arginine (Supplementary Figs. 4, 5), suggesting that ProThDP2 also belongs to CEAS. Given that DHAP and D-G3P are isomers capable of tautomerization mediated by acid-base amino acid residues16, it is possible to obtain protein skeletons capable of converting DHAP into acrylic acid based on the active ProThDP enzymes. We also tested the activity of these candidate proteins on DHAP. Interestingly, the results indicated that all active candidate enzymes could also facilitate the transformation of DHAP into acrylic acid (Fig. 1c). To rule out the possibility that the isomerization of DHAP to D-G3P was promoted by trace amounts of triose-phosphate isomerase (TIM) in E. coli, we subjected the purified ProThDP2 with the highest activity to LC–MS analysis (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). The results confirmed that ProThDP2 can catalyze the conversion of DHAP to acrylic acid without TIM.

Computational investigation of the catalytic mechanism

To elucidate the catalytic mechanisms, we performed a computational investigation of the reaction pathways underlying the formation of acrylic acid from DHAP. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations with the quantum chemical cluster approach have been proven to be a valuable tool for investigating the reaction mechanisms and selectivities of various enzymes17–21. In this study, we employed this approach for the mechanistic investigation. Among the active candidate enzymes, only ProThDP1 (PDB code: 2iht) has a resolved crystal structure. Therefore, ProThDP1 was chosen as the model enzyme, and its structure was used to construct the active site model, which includes DHAP, ThDP cofactor, and the residues forming the active site (Supplementary Fig. 8). The active site structure of ProThDP2, as predicted by AlphaFold2, is nearly identical to that of ProThDP1 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Consequently, the mechanistic insights obtained from DFT calculations on ProThDP1 are also applicable to ProThDP2. According to the proposed mechanism of CEAS15, D-G3P can be converted to an acryloyl-ThDP intermediate, which then undergoes nucleophilic attack by water followed by C–C bond cleavage to release acrylic acid. Therefore, the formation of acrylic acid from DHAP in the ProThDP1-catalyzed reaction requires an initial isomerization of DHAP to D-G3P. This isomerization is known to proceed via general acid-base catalysis, as described in the well-established mechanism of TIM16. Analysis of the ProThDP1 active site suggests that the ThDP ylide may serve as the catalytic acid-base moiety to facilitate proton transfer during DHAP isomerization to D-G3P. The proposed mechanism and the calculated energy profile are presented in Fig. 2a, b, respectively. The optimized structures of the intermediates and transition states involved in the reaction pathways are given in Fig. 2c, Supplementary Figs. 8, 10. The calculation results revealed that the ThDP ylide serves as a proton sink, facilitating proton transfer during the isomerization process (Fig. 2a). According to the calculated energy profile (Fig. 2b), the highest barrier (TS1) for the reaction from DHAP to D-G3P is 17.4 kcal mol−1, indicating that this process is energetically feasible. In the corresponding transition state, the key distances are 1.40 Å between the transferring proton and the C2 atom of ThDP, and 1.46 Å between the same proton and the carbon atom of D-G3P (TS1, Fig. 2c).Fig. 2Catalytic mechanism of acrylic acid biosynthesis from DHAP.a Proposed formation mechanism of acrylic acid from DHAP catalyzed by ProThDP1. b Calculated energy profiles for the formation of acrylic acid from DHAP catalyzed by ProThDP1. c Optimized structures of key transition states involved in the reaction pathway (TS1, TS6 and TS10). The transformation of D-G3P to acrylic acid initiates with the C-C bond formation by the nucleophilic attack of the ThDP ylide on the aldehyde group of D-G3P. This step is followed by two proton transfer events, resulting in the formation of the enolate-ThDP intermediate (Int7, Fig. 2a). This enolate-ThDP intermediate is well stabilized by an extensive hydrogen-bonding network involving residues E57, H120, R36, T80, Q121, and the amino group of the ThDP pyrimidine ring. This well-organized conformation effectively facilitates the C-O bond cleavage leading to the formation of acryloyl-ThDP intermediate. The role of the aminopyrimidine ring of ThDP in this process aligns with its well-established function as an acid-base group in ThDP-dependent enzymes19,20. Subsequently, the acryloyl-ThDP intermediate undergoes hydrolysis to yield acrylic acid. Notably, the leaving \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− was found to act as a mediator, facilitating the proton transfer from the H2O molecule to H120 (Fig. 2a). According to the energy profile of the overall reaction (Fig. 2b), the cleavage of the C–C bond to form acrylic acid (via TS10) represents the rate-limiting step, as it possesses the highest energy barrier among all steps in the reaction pathway. The calculated barrier for this step is 22.9 kcal mol−1 relative to Int7, which is the lowest-energy intermediate preceding TS10. The transition state structure is characterized by a C-C bond distance of 2.17 Å (Fig. 2c). Additionally, the proton transfer from the G3P-ThDP complex to the N4’ of ThDP (via TS6) exhibits a comparable energy barrier of 21.9 kcal mol−1, relative to Int5, suggesting it may also play a significant role in modulating the reaction kinetics. In TS6, the lengths of the C-H and N-H bonds involving the transferring proton are 1.37 Å and 1.36 Å, respectively (Fig. 2c).

Among the active site residues, only H120 directly participates in the chemical reactions. Furthermore, E57 has also been proven to play an important role in catalysis, as evidenced by its persistent hydrogen-bonding interaction with the N1’ of the ThDP aminopyrimidine moiety. This interaction is proposed to facilitate the proton transfer events involving the N4’ of ThDP during the reaction. Site-directed mutagenesis experiments indicated that E57 and H120 are indeed pivotal for enzyme activity. For example, E57G, E57L, H120A, H120G, H120I, and H120V mutations resulted in a drastic reduction or complete loss of enzymatic activity (Supplementary Fig. 11). Our calculation results provide a clear explanation for the ability of diverse ThDP-dependent enzymes to catalyze the conversion of DHAP or D-G3P to acrylic acid, given that the reaction requires the direct participation of only a single histidine residue from the enzyme.

Protein engineering of ProThDP2

To further enhance acrylic acid production from DHAP, we focused our protein engineering efforts on ProThDP2, as it exhibited the highest catalytic efficiency toward both D-G3P and DHAP. To identify more beneficial mutation sites, we proposed to expand the scope of amino acid residues targeted for directed evolution beyond the active site. Initially, 55 residues located within an 8 Å radius of the active site were selected (Supplementary Fig. 12). Additionally, we employed the protein language model ESM-1v to perform virtual saturation mutagenesis across the entire ProThDP2 sequence22 (Supplementary Fig. 13). Ultimately, 20 extra residues located outside the active site were selected.

Due to the absence of a high-throughput detection method for acrylic acid, we developed a high-throughput detection method based on acoustic droplet ejection-open port interface-mass spectrometry (ADE-OPI-MS), a cutting-edge technique that has recently emerged as a powerful tool for large-scale screening23. Preliminary experimental results confirmed the feasibility of ADE-OPI-MS for the high-throughput detection of acrylic acid (Supplementary Fig. 14). By integrating this high-throughput method for acrylic acid and automated instruments, we established an automated high-throughput screening platform (Fig. 3a), which was applied to single-point saturation mutagenesis on the above-mentioned 75 amino acid residues. Using this automated high-throughput platform, we can process 7,200 samples within 5 days, covering workflows ranging from DNA library construction, single-colony picking, and library amplification to protein expression, purification, reaction setup, and high-throughput detection. In this work, our directed evolution strategy involved identifying potential mutation sites via single-point saturation mutagenesis of 75 candidate residues. Utilizing NNK codon-based saturation mutagenesis, we identified nine candidate sites (3, 8, 12, 176, 403, 424, 433, 492, and 573) based on their average and maximum activity values (Supplementary Fig. 15). Subsequent sequencing of variants with enhanced activity at these sites revealed 42 mutants outperforming the wild-type enzyme (Supplementary Fig. 16). The validation results with purified enzymes revealed that the D424R variant exhibited the highest 1.8-fold increase in activity compared to the wild type among the three highly active mutants (D424T, D424R, and D424S) at position 424. We designated the D424R variant as ProThDP2-M1 (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 17). Using M1 as a template, we further introduced the remaining 39 beneficial mutants into M1. The results revealed that only four variants (D424R-P8C, D424R-T492G, D424R-G176R, and D424R-G176T) displayed superior activity compared to ProThDP2-M1 (Fig. 3b). D424R-P8C variant with the highest activity was designated as ProThDP2-M2 (Supplementary Fig. 18). Based on the results of the two-site combinatorial mutagenesis, the incorporation of T492G, G176R, G176T, A433S, and I573R into the M2 variant demonstrated the potential to further enhance the enzymatic activity of M2. Three-site combinatorial analysis revealed that ProThDP2-D424R-P8C-T492G and ProThDP2-D424R-P8C-G176R displayed higher activity than M2. ProThDP2-D424R-P8C-G176R mutant, which achieved a 4.7-fold increase over the wild type, was designated as ProThDP2-M3 (Supplementary Fig. 19). According to the results of the three-site combinatorial mutagenesis, we finally introduced T492G into M3. The results revealed that D424R-P8C-G176R-T492G (ProThDP2-M4) exhibited 7.3-fold higher relative activity than the wild type (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 20). The kinetic parameter kcat/Km of ProThDP2-M4 is 10.9 M−1·s−1, which is 13.6-fold higher than the starting enzyme ProThDP2 (Fig. 3c). Finally, we designated ProThDP2-M4 (Supplementary Fig. 21), which exhibited the highest acrylic acid synthesis capability, as an acrylic acid synthase (AAS).

Among the four mutation sites, D424R, P8C, and G176R mutation sites are positioned distal to the active site pocket, while T492G mutation site is located in the pocket with its side chain oriented away from the catalytic center (Supplementary Fig. 22). The D424R mutation replaces the native aspartate with arginine, enabling the formation of salt bridges with D260 and E401 (Supplementary Fig. 22), thereby reinforcing local structural integrity. The P8C mutation, situated at a putative dimer interface, introduces a cysteine residue that forms a new hydrogen bond with a histidine on the adjacent subunit (Supplementary Fig. 22). This inter-subunit interaction is predicted to increase the dissociation energy barrier of the dimer, thereby stabilizing the quaternary structure. The G176R mutation introduces a bulky, positively charged arginine residue, forming salt bridges with nearby acidic residues E10 or E179 (Supplementary Fig. 22). These electrostatic interactions likely rigidify a formerly flexible surface loop, suppressing non-productive thermal motions and contributing to overall stability. Collectively, the stabilizing effects of D424R, P8C, and G176R provide a clear structural rationale for the experimentally observed 12 °C increase in melting temperature (Tm) (Supplementary Fig. 23). The T492G mutation may reduce steric hindrance for substrate entry/product egress or accelerate adoption of the catalytically competent conformation. We propose that the dual enhancement of global stability (via distal mutations) and local flexibility (via T492G) underlies the superior catalytic efficiency of the engineered AAS.

In vitro biosynthesis of acrylic acid

The conversion of CO2 into acrylic acid not only addresses the carbon emissions inherent in traditional acrylic acid production but also serves as a pivotal pathway toward achieving carbon neutrality objectives. The hybrid pathway represents the optimal strategy for CO2 upcycling, integrating chemical (CO2-to-methanol) and biological (methanol-to-chemicals) conversion steps26. To establish an artificial pathway for acrylic acid biosynthesis from methanol, we employed the engineered formolase (FLS-M3) to catalyze the conversion of formaldehyde into dihydroxyacetone (DHA)27. By integrating FLS-M3 with our designed AAS (ProThDP2-M4), we constructed a methanol-to-acrylic acid pathway (MAAP) in a cell-free system (Fig. 4a). This streamlined approach offers a more efficient and practical route for acrylic acid synthesis from methanol. Furthermore, to ensure a robust initial driving force for acrylic acid biosynthesis, we utilized AOX from Pichia pastoris to catalyze the oxidation of methanol to formaldehyde. The hydrogen peroxide generated during this oxidation process is subsequently converted into water by catalase (CAT) from Bovine liver. To produce DHAP from DHA, we selected dihydroxyacetone kinase (DAK) from Kozakia baliensis, due to its lower sensitivity to formaldehyde28. With all pathway enzymes identified, we initially focused on characterizing the acrylic acid biosynthesis module from DHA. To confirm the catalytic capacity of the cell-free system, we investigated the performance of synthesizing acrylic acid with different concentrations of DHA. The results indicated that low concentrations of DHA (5–20 mM) were almost completely converted into acrylic acid, while as DHA concentration increased, the concentration of acrylic acid remained at around 30 mM (Fig. 4b). Based on the specific activity of AAS (ProThDP2-M4), its maximum catalytic capacity corresponds to the production of 29 mM acrylic acid (Supplementary Table 2). However, doubling the dosage of AAS (4 mg mL−1) did not improve the yield of acrylic acid (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Due to its conjugated double bond structure and acidic properties, acrylic acid can inhibit the activity of multiple enzymes in microorganisms8. Therefore, we focused on the effect of acrylic acid on the activity of pathway enzymes. To elucidate the mechanism of acrylic acid inhibition, we investigated the effects of acrylic acid and potassium acrylate on the production of acrylic acid from DHA. The results revealed that potassium acrylate exhibited negligible influence on acrylic acid biosynthesis, whereas acrylic acid itself demonstrated concentration-dependent inhibitory effects on its own production (Fig. 4c). This observation suggests product inhibition mediated by the acidic properties of acrylic acid. We increased the concentration of the potassium phosphate buffer. However, this had little effect on the yield of acrylic acid (Supplementary Fig. 25). Therefore, we decided to regulate the pH by adding KOH solution hourly. Finally, we achieved nearly complete conversion of 60 mM DHA to acrylic acid by increasing the dosage of AAS and adjusting the pH by adding 5 mM KOH solution hourly (Supplementary Fig. 24).

Following successful establishment of the acrylic acid biosynthesis module from DHA, we subsequently characterized the upstream DHA biosynthesis module from methanol. According to previous work and our testing28, formaldehyde can affect the activity of enzymes in the pathway (Supplementary Fig. 26). Therefore, the ratio of AOX to FLS-M3 is critical for DHA biosynthesis. With the dosage of FLS-M3 held constant, we screened varying concentrations of AOX. The results indicated that 0.25 mg L−1 AOX performed best and almost completely converted 180 mM methanol into DHA (Fig. 4d). After obtaining two synthesis modules, we intended to assemble the entire pathway. To avoid inhibition by high concentrations of methanol (Supplementary Fig. 26), we adopted a one-pot stepwise strategy. Methanol was first converted to DHA, and after 6 hours, PPK, DAK, and AAS were added to convert DHA into acrylic acid. Finally, we achieved the biosynthesis of acrylic acid from methanol within 16 hours, with a titer of 4.3 g L−1 (59.7 mM) and a yield of 99.5% (Fig. 4e). To our knowledge, this is the highest reported titer of acrylic acid biosynthesis.

Discussion

ThDP-dependent enzymes represent a class of multifunctional biocatalysts, which mediate a broad range of reactions, including the formation of C-C, C-N, C-S, and C-O bonds, as well as various decarboxylation reactions29. Moreover, the engineered ThDP-dependent enzymes have achieved a series of unnatural biotransformations, such as the carboligation reaction of formaldehyde12,30, the carboligation reaction of formyl-CoA31,32, the carboxylation reaction of methional33, the three-component radical cross-coupling reaction34, and the radical acylation reaction35,36. In this study, by engineering a naturally occurring ThDP-dependent enzyme, we successfully achieved the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− elimination from DHAP. Different from conventional elimination reactions, the engineered AAS encompasses isomerization, dehydration, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{\rm{H}}}}_{2}{{{\rm{PO}}}}_{4}^{-}$$\end{document}H2PO4− elimination15, and hydrolysis processes together, greatly diversifying and expanding the reaction scope of ThDP-dependent enzymes.

Acrylic acid is notoriously challenging to produce through microbial fermentation due to its inherent toxicity toward microbial hosts8,37,38. Until now, the yield of microbial production of acrylic acid has been limited to below 3% (Table 1). By leveraging this engineered AAS, we successfully established the MAAP pathway within a cell-free system, effectively circumventing the cytotoxicity associated with acrylic acid. This in vitro implementation facilitated an exceptional acrylic acid yield of up to 99.5%, underscoring the immense potential of the MAAP pathway for future applications. Although the MAAP pathway achieved a productivity of 268.9 mg L−1h−1–representing a 34- to 448-fold increase over reported pathways—its economic viability remains a significant challenge. The cost analysis indicates that the overall production cost could potentially be competitive with the peak market price of acrylic acid (Methods). This economic feasibility is primarily driven by leveraging high-cell-density fermentation with secretory host strains for enzyme production39, enhancing enzyme productivity, utilizing enzyme immobilization technology40, and harnessing electricity- or light-driven systems for ATP regeneration41,42. In the future, AAS could facilitate in vivo acrylic acid biosynthesis, provided that its toxicity toward microbial hosts is attenuated. This potential is supported by the mutant AAS-D301S, which almost completely abrogates the inherent ability of AAS to condense DHAP and L-arginine to form N2-(2-carboxyethyl)arginine (Supplementary Figs. 27–29). Methods

Preparation of candidate structures

To obtain an enzyme for the synthesis of acrylic acid from D-G3P, we screened 338 entries with the ThDP cofactor and its analogs in the Protein Data Bank (PDB). A total of 14 ThDP cofactor structural analogs have been identified by searching the PDB database using the ThDP SMILES string, including the entries with PDB IDs: V4E, M6T, A1LX0, TDW, THW, 5SR, 8PA, HTL, THV, TP8, 5GY, A1AEK, TOG, and 2TP. The protein structures with a single chain, RNA strands, or lacking key residues (Glu near the pyrimidine ring and phosphate group-binding residues) were removed. Then, we clustered and filtered the redundant sequences of all proteins using cd-hit50 (the cutoff value of sequence identities is set to 0.95), and the highest-resolution structure in each cluster was retained. Finally, 32 non-redundant protein structures were achieved. To further improve the likelihood of discovering active enzymes and enrich our structural database, we integrated the homologous genes corresponding to these 32 proteins from the AlphaFold Database (AFDB) (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk/). Following further removal of redundant sequences with cd-hit, a curated dataset comprising over 500 unique sequences/structures was established as the search database for covalent ligand docking.

Covalent ligand docking

The covalent ligand docking protocol was developed based on the RosettaSuite platform51,52. RosettaLigand employs the full atom modes of Rosetta to predict protein-small-molecule interactions; it allows backbone and ligand flexibility to improve the docking performance. The ligand D-G3P molecular file was downloaded from the PubChem database (CID: 729) and prepared using the protocol by adding hydrogen and assigning charges in UCSF Chimera software version 1.1253. The enol-ThDP covalent complex in the docking process was achieved by covalently attaching the carbonyl carbon atom of D-G3P to the C2 atom of ThDP cofactor by preparing a constraint file in which the bond type, bond length, angles, and torsions between D-G3P and ThDP were defined (Supplementary Note 1). The conformers of the D-G3P part of enol-ThDP were fully sampled using OpenBabel version 2.4.154. With an extra constraining definition applied for the key Glu residue, which provides an electron for the reaction and the pyrimidine ring of the ThDP molecule (Supplementary Note 2), the docking experiment ran through three stages, including a low-resolution docking stage, a high-resolution docking stage, and a final scoring stage (Supplementary Data 3). Finally, a total of 10, 000 docking models for each candidate protein were generated in the whole docking process. The DockingScore was defined as the difference value between the interface_delta_X and constraint scores (i.e. if_X_angle_constraint, if_X_atom_pair_constraint, if_X_coordinate_constraint, if_X_dihedral_constraint, if_X_metalbinding_constraint, DockingScore = |interface_delta - constrints_scores|). The docking pose with the highest DockingScore was selected as the final model. All the Rosetta applications were from Rosetta Suite version 3.5. The prepared protein molecule, Rosetta scripts, and options for the covalent docking are available in Supplementary Notes 1–2 and Supplementary Data 3. All figures were produced in PyMOL (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.3; Schro¨dinger).

Density functional theory (DFT) calculation details

All calculations were performed using the B3LYP-D3(BJ) density functional method as implemented in the Gaussian16 C.01 package55,56. The geometries of all the species involved in this study were optimized with the 6–31 G (d, p) basis set (Supplementary Data 4). Specifically, these species include the enzyme-substrate complexes (EIP: DHAP, E: DHAP, and E: G3P), all transition states (TSIP, TS1-TS10), all intermediates (Int1-Int10), and the enzyme-product complex (E:P). At the same level of theory for geometry optimization, the single-point SMD solvation with ɛ = 4.0 was calculated to estimate the effects of environment57, and the frequencies of optimized geometries were calculated to obtain zero-point energies (ZPEs). Then, single-point energy calculations using the larger basis set 6–311 + G (2 d, 2p) were performed based on the optimized structures to get more accurate electronic energies. The presented energies in this work are the large basis set ones, corrected for solvation and ZPE. The Cartesian coordinates of the optimized structures along the lowest-energy pathway are provided in Supplementary Data 4.

Expression and purification of enzyme with N-terminal His-tag

The coding genes for FLS-M3, DAK, PPK, and ProThDP from different species were inserted into the pET-28a expression vector using NdeI and XhoI restriction sites and expressed in E. coli BL21 (DE3). E. coli BL21(DE3) strains carrying expression plasmids were cultured at 37 °C in 1 L of 2YT medium with 100 mg L−1 kanamycin until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6. Subsequently, 0.5 mM IPTG was introduced to induce protein expression, and the temperature was lowered to 16 °C to prevent the formation of inclusion bodies.

After an additional 12 hours of incubation, the cells were harvested, washed twice, and resuspended in a 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (Kpi buffer) at pH 7.5. The cell suspensions were homogenized using a high-pressure homogenizer and then centrifuged at 14,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was applied to a pre-equilibrated Ni2+-NTA column. The proteins retained in the column were first eluted using a wash buffer (50 mM Kpi buffer, 50 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). Subsequently, the proteins in the column were eluted again with an elution buffer (50 mM Kpi buffer, 200 mM imidazole, pH 7.5). The proteins were subjected to desalting or diafiltration using Ultra-15 centrifugal filter devices.

Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA Protein Assay Reagent Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with BSA as the reference standard. Large-scale purification (1 L) typically yielded approximately 10–40 mg of the enzyme. The purified proteins were stored in a −80 °C freezer for use.

Reaction conditions

Reaction conditions for screening candidate proteins

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 candidate proteins, 5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM ThDP, and 5 mM D-glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate (D-G3P) or dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 1 hour in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer.

Acrylic acid synthesis from different concentrations of DHA

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 DAK, 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK, 2 mg mL−1AAS, 1 mM ThDP, 4 mM ATP, 5 mM MgSO4, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate, and 5–60 mM DHA. The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 12 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer. For reaction systems with DHA concentrations exceeding 30 mM, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate was supplemented after 4 hours of incubation.

Effects of AAS dosage on the yield of acrylic acid from DHA

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 DAK, 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK, 2 or 4 mg mL−1AAS, 1 mM ThDP, 4 mM ATP, 5 mM MgSO4, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate, and 60 mM DHA. After 4 hours, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added. The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 12 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer.

Effects of acrylic acid and potassium acrylate on the production of acrylic acid from DHA

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 DAK, 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK, 4 mg mL−1AAS, 1 mM ThDP, 4 mM ATP, 5 mM MgSO4, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate, 60 mM DHA, and 10–40 mM acrylic acid and potassium acrylate. After 4 hours, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added. The reactions were conducted at 37 °C for 12 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (the initial pH was 7.5).

Combined effect of AAS dosage and pH adjustment for the synthesis of acrylic acid from DHA

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 DAK, 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK, 4 mg mL−1AAS, 60 mM DHA, 1 mM ThDP, 4 mM ATP, 5 mM MgSO4, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate. After 4 hours, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added. KOH solution was added to the system every hour (with a final concentration of 5–6 mM). The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 12 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer.

Effects of AOX concentration on DHA synthesis

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 0.1–0.75 mg mL−1 AOX, 830 U mL−1 CAT, 5 mg mL−1 FLS-M3, 1 mM ThDP, 5 mM MgSO4, and 180 mM methanol. The reactions were conducted at 37 °C for 8 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer.

Effects of formaldehyde and methanol on AAS and PPK

Effects of formaldehyde and methanol on AAS were investigated. The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 1 mg mL−1 AAS, 5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM ThDP, 5 mM DHAP, 5/10 mM formaldehyde or 60/180 mM methanol. The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 1 hour in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer.

Effects of formaldehyde and methanol on PPK were investigated. The enzyme activity assay system, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM NADP+, 0.1 mg mL−1 PPK, 1 mM ADP, 1 U HK, 1 U G6PDH, 5 mM sodium hexametaphosphate, and 5 mM glucose. The changes in absorbance at 340 nm were monitored in real-time using a microplate reader. The control group contains 5/10 mM formaldehyde or 60/180 mM methanol.

Biosynthesis of acrylic acid from methanol

Initially, the reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 1 mL, included 180 mM methanol, 0.25 mg mL−1 AOX, 830 U mL−1 (0.004175 mg mL−1) CAT, 5 mg mL−1 FLS-M3, 1 mM ThDP, 5 mM MgSO4. The reaction system was conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C for 6 hours in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer. Subsequently, 1 mg mL−1 DAK, 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK, 4 mg mL−1 AAS, 4 mM ATP, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate were added to the above system and continued to react for 10 hours. Finally, 59.7 mM acrylic acid was produced. Note: after 7 hours, 5 mM KOH solution was added to the system every hour. After 10 hours, 6 mM sodium hexametaphosphate solution was added again.

Cost analysis

To demonstrate the technological and economic potential of the MAAP pathway in a cell-free system, we conducted a cost analysis. The primary cost drivers in cell-free systems include raw material cost, purified enzyme expenses, coenzyme regeneration, and product separation58. Conventionally produced methanol—derived from coal or natural gas—is priced at 200–400 USD/ton59. Based on a minimum methanol price of 200 USD per ton, the raw material cost is approximately 268 USD per ton of acrylic acid produced (shown below). With regard to coenzyme regeneration, sodium hexametaphosphate is currently employed for ATP regeneration in the system. The associated cost for ATP regeneration is estimated at 1,050 USD per ton of acrylic acid (shown below). Product purification, using m-xylene-assisted azeotropic distillation to isolate acrylic acid from the aqueous reaction mixture60, adds a relatively modest separation cost of approximately 55 USD per ton (shown below). Currently, laboratory-scale enzyme production is costly, with an estimated 1000 USD required to obtain 1 mg of purified enzyme39-clearly not economically viable for large-scale acrylic acid production. However, by leveraging high-cell-density fermentation with secretory host strains, the cost of purified enzymes is projected to drop significantly to 10–30 USD/kg39. Based on our modeling, enhancing enzyme productivity and utilizing enzyme immobilization technology could reduce the enzyme cost for producing one ton of acrylic acid to around 200 USD in the future (shown below). The overall production cost can be kept below 1,600 USD per ton. The market price of acrylic acid ranges from 1200 to 2420 USD/ton61. Although the production cost of acrylic acid could be competitive with the peak market price of acrylic acid in the future, commercial profitability remains uncertain, primarily due to the high cost of coenzyme regeneration. Looking ahead, we aim to develop alternative strategies for ATP regeneration-such as harnessing electricity or light-driven systems-that could dramatically reduce or even eliminate the reliance on expensive chemical regenerants41,42. Such innovations would significantly lower operating costs and unlock substantial economic potential for the MAAP pathway in a cell-free system.

Cost of methanol

The price of methanol is assumed to be 200 USD per ton. 1000 kg acrylic acid=1 × 106 g/72.06 g mol−1 = 1.39 × 104 mol. Calculating the required methanol: 1.39 × 104 mol × 3 ÷ 99.5% = 4.191 × 104 mol. Molar mass of methanol =32.04 g mol−1. Required mass of methanol: 4.191 × 104 mol × 32.04 g mol−1 = 1.34 ton. Therefore, the cost of methanol for producing 1 ton of acrylic acid = 1.34 ton × 200 USD/ton=268 USD.

Cost of pure enzymes

To achieve a significant reduction in the cost of pure enzymes, it is necessary to increase enzyme productivity and adopt immobilization strategies in the future. The productivity of the enzymes we calculated is 2 g L−1h−1, with 50 cycles of reuse, and the reaction system is 1000 L. The mass of acrylic acid produced by a batch of enzymes: 2 g L−1h−1 × 16 h × 50 × 1000 L = 1.6ton. Enzyme dosage: (0.25 mg mL−1 AOX + 0.004175 mg mL−1 CAT + 5 mg mL−1 FLS-M3 + 1 mg mL−1 DAK + 0.5 mg mL−1 PPK + 4 mg mL−1 AAS) ×1000 L = 10.754 kg. Enzyme dosage for producing one ton of acrylic acid: 10.754 kg /1.6 = 6.72 kg. Assuming a cost of 30 USD per kg of enzyme, the total enzyme cost for producing 1 ton of acrylic acid is 201.6 USD.

Cost of ATP regeneration

1000 kg acrylic acid=1 × 106 g/72.06 g mol−1 = 1.39 × 104 mol. Required sodium hexametaphosphate=1.39 × 104mol/5 = 2.78 × 103 mol. Molar mass of sodium hexametaphosphate=503.87 g mol−1. Required mass of sodium hexametaphosphate = 2.78 × 10^3 mol × 503.87 g mol−1 = 1.4 ton. Cost of sodium hexametaphosphate is 750 USD/ton in China. Therefore, the cost of ATP regeneration for producing 1 ton of acrylic acid = 1.4 ton × 750 USD/ton = 1050 USD.

Cost of product separation

The estimated separation cost for purifying one ton of acrylic acid from the aqueous reaction stream via m-xylene-assisted azeotropic distillation is 55 USD /ton. This calculation includes: External steam cost: Total energy consumption for separating one ton of acrylic acid is 3.34 GJ/t, equivalent to approximately 1.52 tons of industrial steam per ton of product. Therefore, steam cost is 38 USD/ton. Electricity (vacuum systems, pumps): 8 USD; m-Xylene entrainer loss: 1 USD; Polymerization inhibitors & maintenance: 6 USD; Equipment depreciation allocation: 2 USD.

Relative activity of combinatorial mutants

Determination of enzymatic kinetic parameters (Km, kcat) for wild-type (referred to as ProThDP2) and ProThDP2-M4(AAS)

The reaction mixtures, with a total volume of 0.2 mL, included 0.4 mg mL−1 ProThDP2 or AAS, 5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM ThDP, and 1–40 mM DHAP. The reactions were conducted at pH 7.5 and 37 °C in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer. For each substrate concentration, the total reaction time was set to 12.5 min, with sampling performed every 2.5 min. Product quantification was carried out via HPLC at five time points: 2.5, 5, 7.5, 10, and 12.5 min. The product concentration was plotted against reaction time for linear fitting. Since all curves yielded an R² value greater than 0.99, the slope of the fitted line was defined as the initial enzymatic reaction rate for the corresponding substrate concentration. Conditions: Aminex HPX-87H column (Bio-Rad), detection wavelength set at 200 nm, a mobile phase consisting of 5 mM sulfuric acid, a flow rate of 0.6 mL min−1, a sample volume of 100 µL, and a column temperature maintained at 40 °C.

Generation of the ProThDP2 single-site saturation mutagenesis library and site-directed mutants

The ProThDP2 plasmid (pET28a) was used as the template for the site-saturation mutagenesis library. For each site, mutant libraries were created using NNK-degenerate primers with the QuikChange PCR method. PCR conditions were: 98 °C for 5 min, followed by 31 cycles of 98 °C for 20 s, 55 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 2.5 min, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The libraries were cloned into pET28a via Gibson assembly and transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells (TransGen Biotech, China) for plasmid amplification. Single colonies were transferred to 96-well plates containing 150 μL of 2YT medium with 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin using the QPix460 colony picker (Molecular Devices, USA), and grown at 37 °C until OD600 reached ~1. The libraries were stored at −80 °C in 50% glycerol.

All site-directed mutagenesis of plasmids in this study was performed using the QuikChange PCR method. The DNA polymerases used for PCR amplification were purchased from Vazyme. PCR products were digested with Dpn I restriction endonuclease (Vazyme) and subsequently purified using a DNA purification kit (Zymo Research). The purified products were then assembled using the Gibson Assembly method (commercial kit from TransGen Biotech) and transformed into competent Escherichia coli DH5α or BL21(DE3) cells. After single colonies appeared, individual colonies were picked for cultivation and plasmid extraction. The extracted plasmids were verified by sequencing performed by GENEWIZ to confirm the mutational status and the correctness of the plasmid sequences. All primers were synthesized by GENEWIZ. The primer sequences used in this study are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

Screening the single-point saturation mutagenesis library of ProThDP2

Using the Biomek i7 liquid handler (Beckman Coulter, USA), 20 μL of glycerol stock culture was transferred into each well of a 96-deep-well plate containing 1.4 mL of 2YT medium supplemented with 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin. The plate was then incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 750 rpm. Subsequent steps (cell culture to OD600 ~ 0.6, protein induction with 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 16 hours) followed the protocol detailed in Fig. 3a. Cell pellets were harvested by centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 minutes and resuspended in 100 μL B-PER Bacterial Protein Extraction Reagent (Thermo Scientific, USA) plus 100 μL of 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5). Cell lysis was conducted at 4 °C in a high-throughput shaker at 200 rpm for 20 min. After lysis, 50 μL of nickel-coated magnetic beads (QianchunBio, China) were added, and His-tagged proteins were captured by incubating at 4 °C for 20 min with gentle mixing. The Kingfisher Presto system (Thermo Scientific, USA) was then used for automated bead washing (3× with 20 mM imidazole buffer). Then, a 200 μL reaction mixture (5 mM MgSO4, 1 mM ThDP, 10 mM DHAP in 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer, pH 7.5) was added to the bead-immobilized enzymes. Reactions were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 750 rpm for 8 hours. Following the reaction, the Kingfisher Presto system (Thermo Scientific, US) was used to isolate and remove the magnetic beads. The mixture was then subjected to centrifugation at 2300 g for 30 min, and 50 μL of the supernatant was dispensed into a 384-well plate using the Biomek i7 liquid handler (Beckman Coulter, USA). The plate was processed by centrifugation at 1,500 g for 10 min and horizontal agitation at 1000 rpm for 2 min. Analysis was performed using the EchoMS instrument (SCIEX, USA; Triple Quad 6500) configured for ADE-OPI-MS.
