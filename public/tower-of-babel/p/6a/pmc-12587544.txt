Patient, clinician, and researcher prioritization of postoperative outcomes for people living with kidney failure: a modified Delphi process
Authors: Nicole K. Brockman, Taylor Hecker, Cheryl Simoens, Nancy Verdin, Maoliosa Donald, Meghan J. Elliott, Danielle E. Fox, Matthew T. James, Sabrina Jassemi, Brenda R. Hemmelgarn, Paul E. Ronksley, Shannon M. Ruzycki, Tyrone G. Harrison
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeople with lived and living experience (PWLLE) of kidney failure have significantly high rates of surgery and postoperative complications. Despite these risks, there is lack of consensus on the most important postoperative outcomes for clinical care and research focused on this population. This study aimed to identify and compare the postoperative outcome priorities of PWLLE with those of clinicians/researchers.MethodsWe recruited PWLLE of kidney failure, clinicians and researchers from across Canada to complete a modified Delphi survey over three rounds between April and June 2024. Participants ranked 100 perioperative outcomes derived from literature, participant suggestions, and expert recommendations. Outcomes were evaluated for consensus using a 9-point Likert scale with predefined consensus criteria. Items not meeting consensus were either dropped or reconsidered in subsequent rounds. Median rankings of prioritized topics were compared between participant groups.ResultsOf 116 initial respondents, 91 consented to participate, with 64 (70%) completing all three rounds. Participants included 52 clinicians/researchers and 39 PWLLE. The top five prioritized postoperative outcomes were postoperative death within 30 days, intraoperative death, postoperative kidney allograft loss, septic shock, and 1-year postoperative mortality. Cardiovascular complications, dialysis dependency in individuals not requiring dialysis preoperatively, and risk of sepsis were also highly ranked. No significant differences were found in the top 10 priorities across participant groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionWe identified key postoperative outcomes for people with kidney failure undergoing surgery. This work will inform future research on perioperative care and the development of postoperative risk prediction tools for PWLLE of kidney failure.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04521-5.

Introduction

People living with kidney failure experience significantly more surgical interventions than people with normal kidney function [1]. They have also been shown to have higher risk for major complications in the postoperative period compared to those without chronic kidney disease, including higher risks of cardiac complications, stroke, infection, as well as risk of death and overall mortality [2–4]. Due to the association of surgery with increased risk of adverse postoperative outcomes, the decision to undergo surgical treatment is complex and patient-specific, emphasizing the need for detailed preoperative risk assessment. Unfortunately, there are few evidence-informed strategies available to effectively assess surgical risk for people with kidney failure, which limits the shared decision-making process between patients and healthcare providers. Furthermore, it remains unclear which outcomes are most important for patients with kidney failure undergoing surgical procedures.

Healthcare provider goals may not align with the priorities of individuals with lived experience of disease, highlighting the necessity of incorporating the unique perspectives of patients living with kidney failure when establishing research objectives. Though prior work has been done to identify patient prioritized outcomes in a variety of contexts, the priorities for perioperative outcome research for patients with kidney failure across all types of surgeries have not been described. The Standardized Outcomes in Nephrology (SONG) initiative [5] completed an international Delphi survey that prioritized outcome domains with respect to kidney transplant among both patient and healthcare provider stakeholders, some of which relate to the postoperative period [6]. Specific to the perioperative outcome domain, other work has identified patient-centred outcomes in older adults without kidney failure undergoing non-cardiac surgery [7], patients undergoing donation and transplantation [8] as well as patient priorities with respect to anesthesia and perioperative research [9]. However, these studies are not necessarily applicable to patients living with chronic kidney disease or kidney failure without a kidney transplant. Currently, the priorities for perioperative outcome research for patients with kidney failure across all types of surgeries are unknown. This is an important area of research as people with kidney failure have more comorbidities than the general surgical population [10, 11], have unique challenges in the perioperative period [12], have higher incidence of and different types of surgery [1], and have worse outcomes than people with normal kidney function [3, 11].

The purpose of this modified Delphi study was to identify the priorities for postoperative outcomes in people with kidney failure undergoing surgery from the perspectives of people with lived and living experience (PWLLE) of kidney failure (which includes both patients and their caregivers) and other stakeholders. Specifically, we identified and compared priorities for postoperative outcomes among multidisciplinary clinicians, researchers, and PWLLE of kidney failure and surgery.

Methods

Using the Canadian Society of Nephrology’s protocol for guideline topic prioritization [13], we conducted this modified electronic-Delphi (e-Delphi) study in alignment with established recommendations for using Delphi surveys in health-related research [14, 15]. The modified e-Delphi method was selected to facilitate global representation, collaboration, and consensus-building through technology. The reporting process adhered to the ACCORD (ACCurate Consensus Reporting Document) recommendations [16] (Supplementary Table 1). Ethics approval was obtained through the University of Calgary Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board (Ethics ID REB23-0749). Figure 1 provides an overview of our study process organized into 4 key stages: participant identification, literature search, e-Delphi survey rounds, and analysis of findings. All surveys were conducted online, beginning with an initial demographics survey and request for suggested outcomes to be included, followed by three rounds where participants ranked the items. In the second and third rounds, participants were presented with the median scores and percent agreement from preceding rounds for both major stakeholder groups (i.e., PWLLE as well as clinicians/researchers) of the remaining items.

Selection of participants and recruitment

Included participants were people living with kidney failure (based on estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] less than 15 ml/min/1.73m2 or receipt of kidney replacement therapy) that have had surgery, their caregivers (including family members), clinicians (physicians, nurses, and other providers from anesthesia, nephrology, internal medicine and surgery) and researchers from related fields. Eligible participants were adults from Canada with self-identified proficiency in reading and writing in either English or French. To recruit people with kidney failure, we employed broad recruitment strategies, engaging national, provincial, and local patient stakeholder groups, and snowball sampling to identify additional eligible individuals. PWLLE were compensated for their time with a gift card ($50 gift card or cash for those who completed all surveys, $25 for those who were not able to complete all surveys) per the recommendations from the Canadian Institutes of Health Research and Canadian Strategy for Patient-Oriented Research (SPOR) [17]. We recruited clinician and researcher participants by sending emails through national kidney, surgery, perioperative, and anaesthesia organizations, targeting members with clinical or research experience in the perioperative care of individuals with kidney failure. We aimed to have at least 20 PWLLE and 20 clinicians/researchers. Anticipating 50–70% drop out between rounds from prior work from our group, we estimated needing to recruit at least 130 individuals to participate in our first survey round.

Upon agreeing to participate and providing informed consent, participants were asked to complete demographic questions through a survey developed and administered via Qualtrics (Provo, Utah) [18], in English and French. For consenting patients or caregivers, we asked about their age, sex, gender identity, ethnicity, preferred language, location, kidney disease characteristics, surgical history and perioperative experience. Clinicians and researchers were asked similar questions, in addition to professional roles and experience related to the perioperative care for kidney failure populations.

Preparatory research

An initial literature search was conducted to identify relevant outcomes from similar groups conducting research prioritization exercises and relevant studies in the surgical population or in kidney related domains such as kidney transplant [5–9, 19, 20]. As part of the initial demographics survey, we asked participants to suggest up to 3 postoperative outcomes for consideration in the iterative e-Delphi process. All outcomes were reviewed by the research team for feasibility, and to eliminate duplicate topics. The final list of postoperative outcomes was used to create the first ranking survey.

Assessing consensus

The e-Delphi ranking survey process included three rounds, disseminated by e-mail in French and English to participants in April, May and June 2024. Participants received a weekly e-mail reminder to complete each round if they had not previously. Each survey was piloted for face and content validity, usability, clarity of the items, and length of the survey among research team members (including healthcare providers and patient partners) and several others external to the project design. Modifications were made before dissemination. TGH as senior author, and many of the co-authors, have expertise conducting e-Delphi prioritization studies, while this was the first Delphi study undertaken by NKB and TH. The survey was piloted to assess the usability of the Qualtrics interface, the clarity of the items, and length of the survey, and modifications were made before dissemination.

Items were organized into 12 categories (Kidney Specific Outcomes, Patient Disposition and Quality of Life Outcomes, Postoperative Care, Postoperative Mobility & Function, Post-operative Symptoms, Complications & Recovery, Infection & Sepsis, Anaesthesia, Mortality and Morbidity, Cardiovascular Outcomes, Pulmonary Complication, and Postoperative Cancer Surgery Outcomes). Participants rated each item on a 9-point Likert scale of importance, where 1 indicated strong disagreement and 9 indicated strong agreement with the item’s importance. The percentage agreement was calculated by determining the proportion of survey participants whose median ranking was either below 7 or greater than or equal to 7. For each item, consensus that the outcome is important was deemed as reached if (1) The median score was greater or equal to 7; and (2) the percent agreement was greater or equal to 75%. Items with a median rating of less than 7 and percent agreement less than 75% were dropped from further consideration. Items with a median rating of 7 or greater but did not meet percent agreement criteria (≥ 75%), it was evaluated again in the subsequent rounds. At the end of the three survey ranking rounds, items were identified as being of “uncertain importance” if they continued to not meet the consensus criteria for acceptance or rejection.

Round one (April 2024) included 100 perioperative outcomes; 72 outcomes were from the literature search, 10 outcomes were from patient recommendations, and 18 outcomes were from health care provider recommendations. Each item was evaluated using the consensus criteria listed above. Reminders were sent out every week for a total of 3 reminder emails to those that had not yet completed the survey. The round 2 survey (May 2024) included 37 items from round 1 that had a median ranking of 7 or higher and a percentage agreement less than 75%. In this round, the median overall score and percentage agreement from the previous round were provided to the participants. The final round of the Delphi surveys (round 3) contained 15 outcomes for review and was completed in June 2024. The steering committee was not involved in the decisions made by the consensus panel.

Analysis

We summarized participant demographics and reported participation rates across the rounds of the Delphi survey process. For each topic, we detailed its progression through the survey rounds, noting the round in which consensus was reached, the median score in that round or the final ranking round, and the percentage agreement. A priority score was calculated for each item that reached consensus by adding the Likert scores for that topic across participants in the round where it met consensus, and standardizing the total based on the number of respondents for that round. Median scores for each participant group were compared using a non-parametric test for equality of medians with a continuity correction, with p < 0.05 indicating a statistically significant difference. For all analyses we used Stata software v17 (StataCorp).

Results

Participant characteristics

Participants were recruited between August and December 2023 (n = 91). The three survey rounds were conducted in April (n = 78 participants, 84% participation rate), May (n = 59, 78%), and June 2024 (n = 64, 84%).

Of the 91 initial participants, 52 (57%) were either health care providers, allied health or researchers, and 39 (43%) were PWLLE of kidney failure (Table 1). Most were women (62%), with a median age of respondents of 40.5 years (IQR 10). The most frequent province/territories of residence were Ontario, Alberta and British Columbia (86%), and the majority (58%) of participants were White. Of the PWLLE group, 82% were patients themselves. Of the clinician and researcher group, most were physicians (73%), with a diverse group of respondents otherwise (Table 2). Most health care professionals practiced in the field of Nephrology (65%) and had worked in their primary clinical or research role for a median 10 years (IQR 11). Of the PWLLE group, most had a functioning kidney transplant (54%), or were on either peritoneal or hemodialysis (28%) (Table 3).

Perioperative outcome prioritization throughout Delphi process

Round one (April 2024) included 100 perioperative outcomes; 72 outcomes were from the literature search, 10 outcomes from PWLLE recommendations and 18 outcomes from clinician/researcher recommendations. The round 2 survey (May 2024) included 37 items from round 1. The final round of the e-Delphi survey (round 3) contained 15 outcomes for review and was completed in June 2024.

From the initial 100 topics from the literature and suggestions from participants, after the three survey rounds, 77 were deemed a priority, 13 were of uncertain importance, and 10 were rejected (Supplementary Table 2). The five highest priority items for all participants collectively based on mean priority rank were: (1) risk of postoperative death within 30 days of surgery, (2) risk of intra-operative death, (3) postoperative graft-loss (loss of kidney transplant because the transplant has failed), (4) septic shock (sepsis with shock requiring vasopressors), and (5) 1 year postoperative mortality (Table 4). The remaining top 10 prioritized topics focused on cardiovascular complications including risk of cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and major adverse cardiac events, as well as requirement for dialysis for people who were not dependent on dialysis preoperatively, and risk of sepsis.

Comparison of priorities between participant groups

There were no statistically significant differences between the two participant groups for any of the overall mean top 10 prioritized topics (Table 4). PWLLE prioritized perioperative mortality quite highly, in addition to sepsis and cardiac complications, which were relatively similar to clinician/researcher group. However, clinicians/researchers tended to place higher priority on other major cardiac or kidney complications (Supplementary Table 3). Errors in anaesthesia, organ or deep space infections, or neurologic infections were highly prioritized for PWLLE and not for clinician/researcher participants (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

We used a modified e-Delphi survey process to identify and prioritize postoperative outcomes for people with kidney failure undergoing various types of surgery, incorporating perspectives from individuals living with kidney failure, their caregivers, researchers, and multidisciplinary clinicians across Canada. In general, priorities were similar between two participant groups, with highest ranked postoperative outcome priorities including risk of mortality (postoperative or intraoperative death, cardiovascular death, one year risk of postoperative mortality), septic shock, cardiovascular complications (risk of myocardial infarct, non-fatal adverse cardiac event, major adverse cardiac event), and need for dialysis for people who were not dependent on dialysis pre-operatively.

Our finding that researchers, clinicians, patients and caregivers ranked mortality outcomes as their greatest priority correlated with a similar study on patient and caregiver identified priority outcome domains for patients undergoing kidney transplantation [6]. This consensus-building study also reported that patients and healthcare providers prioritized mortality, infection and cardiovascular complications as critically important outcomes.

In our present study, we additionally identified that septic shock and cardiovascular complications, were also prioritized highly between both PWLLE and clinician/researcher groups. A systematic review and meta-analysis conducted by Palamuthusingam et al.4 found a two- to five-fold increase in the odds of experiencing postoperative myocardial infarction or stroke in patients on chronic dialysis undergoing non-cardiac surgery, independent of the type of surgery. This group also identified that the risk of sepsis was more than two times higher in patients on chronic dialysis who underwent surgery, even after adjusting for factors such as age and comorbidities. Similarly, an observational study of over 300,000 patients revealed a clear inverse relationship between eGFR and risk of death and acute myocardial infarction (AMI) following ambulatory non-cardiac surgery [11]. This increased risk of outcomes found to be important to clinician/researchers and PWLLE underscore the critical importance of addressing and prioritizing high-risk postoperative outcomes, particularly in patients with chronic kidney disease or those undergoing dialysis, to improve patient safety and guide perioperative care strategies.

Although no significant differences were noted between the two groups’ priorities, important trends emerged. PWLLE tended to place greater emphasis on errors in anesthesia (e.g., wrong medication or dose). The reasons for this finding were not explored in this present study, though we hypothesize this may be attributable to patients’ lived experiences during medical procedures or fears, which might make anesthetic-related issues more prominent in their concerns. Statistically, the mortality or serious complication rate associated with anesthesia is less than 3 per 100,000 procedures. For example, a comprehensive analysis by the American Society of Anesthesiologists found that the risk of perioperative mortality in patients undergoing general anesthesia is below 1 in 100,000 in the United States [23], and similarly Schiff et al. [24] found an anesthetic complication rate resulting in death or serious complications of 26.2 per million procedures, with those with possible direct anaesthetic involvement being 7.3 per million cases. Despite these reassuring statistics, there remains a gap between the actual and perceived risks of anesthesia among patients.

Another noted trend was the higher priority that clinicians/researchers tended to place on outcomes related to the need for dialysis or graft function, which was ranked with higher priority to the clinician/researcher group than PWLLE. For example, clinicians/researchers ranked outcomes such as “Requirement of dialysis for people with kidney failure not on dialysis preoperatively (within 30 days of surgery)” and “Major adverse kidney events—mortality or dialysis of any duration, or >30% decline in eGFR from baseline in those not on dialysis preoperatively” among their top 10 priorities. In contrast, PWLLE ranked these outcomes much lower (53rd and 45th, respectively). This may be because patients with kidney failure already anticipate the possibility of dialysis and graft-related complications, making them less urgent or alarming. Further, most participants already had been in receipt of kidney replacement therapy, whereas people with kidney failure that had not been treated were not represented (Table 3). The lower priority of postoperative kidney-related complications identified by patients and caregivers contrasts with prioritized outcomes from other studies. For example, Howell et al. [20] identified and prioritized postoperative outcomes for kidney transplant recipients, with the highest ranked outcome being transplant survival, with participants indicating transplant survival as more important than their own survival. It is possible that these priorities may change if the epidemiology of these outcomes were presented to participants; this is an important future research direction as many outcomes have not been examined in the literature.

Our study differs from previous studies in several ways. A study looking at patient prioritized postoperative outcomes in patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery (not in the kidney failure population) highly prioritized outcomes such as disability score, length of hospital stay, days at home post-surgery [7], whereas these outcomes were ranked much lower in our study. However, in the mentioned study, the target population was older individuals greater than 65 years of age and thus may account for this shift in priority. In the kidney transplant domain, Allard et al.8 collected consensus on important research topics and identified priorities focused more on psychosocial aspects of disease, such as provider communication/education, cultural issues, psychological and social support to patients and caregivers, whereas these psychosocial outcomes were ranked much lower for our participants. This difference may reflect variations in the demographic and clinical characteristics of the populations studied, with our cohort potentially placing a greater emphasis on outcomes directly impacting physical health and functional recovery over psychosocial or long-term considerations. The methods used to identify candidate outcomes were different as well, which would have implications for the outcomes that participants were able to rank.

We used a modified e-Delphi process to achieve consensus among diverse groups, including individuals with kidney failure, caregivers, researchers, and clinicians, ensuring the findings reflect both lived experiences and expert knowledge, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The high participation rate (84%) across survey rounds and the alignment of priorities on severe outcomes, such as mortality and major complications, underscore the robustness and shared recognition of the most critical postoperative outcomes. Our study has limitations to consider as well. First, the perspectives of other key groups, such as nurses and allied health professionals, may have been underrepresented, limiting the diversity of viewpoints. Additionally, as we did not collect equity-related demographic data, we cannot assure the viewpoints of people from marginalized communities were adequately captured in this process. Approximately 36% of our participants were non-White, which is representative of the Canadian population but not those from other regions. Further, as the study was conducted exclusively in Canada, this may affect the generalizability of the findings to other regions or countries with different healthcare systems and practices as well. Even within Canada there are gaps in representation, with fewer participants from Quebec than other provinces. We focused our recruitment on the participant type and not on location or other variables; in future work, quota-based recruitment strategies to target these less represented provinces could be used. Finally, as consensus-based methods such as those used in our study are based on democratic principles of identifying priorities based on popular vote, people with marginalized perspectives may not be represented by our findings. To account for this, we purposefully recruited people with diverse demographic characteristics, and in particular, patients and their caregivers. We reported our findings stratified by participant group as well. Nevertheless, these limitations should be considered when interpreting the results of this study.

In conclusion, we identified and prioritized key postoperative outcomes for patients with kidney failure undergoing surgery, inclusive of diverse perspectives from patients, caregivers, healthcare providers, and researchers. The study highlighted shared concerns about severe morbidity and mortality, including mortality risk, septic shock, and cardiovascular complications, as critical outcomes for both patients and healthcare providers. Despite some differences in prioritization, there was strong alignment on the most pressing postoperative outcomes. The outcomes identified in this study will serve as important future research topics and are relevant as targets for postoperative risk prediction tools. For example, development of tools to target the most important outcomes to PWLLE and other groups will ensure tool relevance, and may inform shared-decision making and more personalized perioperative care options for individuals living with kidney failure.
