Troponin Screening and Major Cardiovascular Adverse Events During Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor Therapy
Authors: Imen Hamdi, Nathan El Bèze, Soledad Henriquez, Marie-Liesse Joulia, Raphaël Colle, Delphine Loirat, Nicolas Girard, Agnès Lillo Le Louet, Christophe Caussin, Mariana Mirabel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionDoes screening for troponin level elevation among patients with severe cancer stratify risk for immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI)–associated myocarditis compared with no screening?FindingsIn this cohort study including 859 patients with cancer, routine troponin screening was associated with lower mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in multivariable models, but no reduction in adjudicated MACEs after competing risk analysis or propensity score matching.MeaningThese findings support risk-adapted troponin screening based on baseline risk for ICI-associated cardiomyotoxicity (but not atherosclerosis risk) and clinical symptoms and suggest that screening for cardiovascular immune-related toxic effects should not rely on troponin levels alone.

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have markedly improved cancer prognosis, although this has come at the expense of potential cardiovascular (CV) immune-related adverse events (IRAEs). ICI-associated myocarditis has been in the spotlight due to initial high mortality rates1 and specific pathophysiology2 revealing the link between CV and muscular IRAEs, leading to the concept of cardiomyotoxicity.3 A recently published international registry grading algorithm for ICI-associated myocarditis severity is largely based on magnitude of troponin level elevation.4 Current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) 2022 cardio-oncology guidelines recommend systematic troponin screening for ICI-associated myocarditis regardless of symptoms or electrocardiographic changes.5 However, besides ICI-associated myocarditis, a wide range of CV events have been described during use of ICI therapies, with different pathophysiological pathways.2 Patients may experience worsening of underlying heart failure (HF),6 arrythmias,6 and atherosclerosis-related CV events.7

Approximately half of patients with cancer are now candidates for ICI therapy, which is often combined with other potentially cardiotoxic drugs as anthracyclines or vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors. By systematic screening of troponin levels during ICI treatment, the number of patients referred for suspected CV events during ICI treatment may increase drastically, with most presenting with nonsevere CV IRAEs, if any.8,9 Troponin remains a versatile biomarker; there are different assays and different cutoff values that render its generalizability difficult.10 Also, troponin levels may be raised in a variety of clinical scenarios, from HF to acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and may be confounded by kidney impairment, anemia, or obesity.11 Troponin screening may therefore lack specificity to detect ICI-associated myocarditis, a rare complication of ICI therapy with an incidence of approximately 1%.12 Increased troponin levels may lead to unnecessary interruption of ICI therapy, thereby compromising cancer prognosis in centers where expert cardio-oncology opinion may be lacking.

Studies focusing on systematic screening by troponin levels have mainly been single-center observational studies without a no-screen strategy comparison.13 By means of a multicenter retrospective study, we compared the outcomes of patients undergoing systematic troponin screening during ICI therapy with those of patients undergoing clinical assessment alone. Outcomes consisted of major adverse CV events (MACEs), including ICI-associated myocarditis, ACS, HF requiring hospital admission, CV death, and sudden cardiac death (SCD). Competitive risk (non-CV death as competitor) and propensity score–matched analysis were undertaken to mitigate confounders.

Methods

The TILT Study

The TILT study was a retrospective cohort of adult patients treated with ICIs (programmed cell death 1 [PD-1], programmed death ligand 1 [PD-L1], and/or CTLA-4 inhibitors) for solid cancer, either alone or in combination with other cancer therapies.14 The inclusion period was between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2022. Patients had been treated at either the Institut Montsouris or Institut Curie Paris, Paris, France, or Institut Curie Saint Cloud, Saint Cloud, France. Before the release of the 2022 ESC guidelines,5 clinical practice with regard to systematic troponin screening varied among clinicians. We compared 2 populations: patients undergoing systematic troponin screening (ie, ≥2 troponin level measurements during the first trimester of ICI therapy not motivated by clinical symptoms) and those not monitored by troponin screening. Troponin screening was not necessarily hospital based; therefore, assays and thresholds varied. Cross-sectional follow-up was undertaken between June 1, 2023, and June 30, 2024, to ensure a minimum of 6 months follow-up after the initiation of ICI therapy; data were censored on June 30, 2023.

This study complied with Good Clinical Practice, French regulations, and the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki15 and was approved by the Institut Montsouris, which waived the need for informed consent. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

Study Population

All patients 18 years or older treated with ICIs as a standard of care and who did not waive consent were eligible. Patients in double-blinded randomized clinical trials assessing ICIs were excluded. The indication for ICIs (curative, life extending, or palliative) was not considered, nor was the cancer site. A history of CV disease was defined by ACS, stroke, vascular peripheral events, acute or chronic HF, arrhythmias or conduction system diseases, or pulmonary embolism prior to ICI therapy.

Objectives and Outcome Measures

The primary objective of this study was to assess whether troponin screening was associated with MACEs, including ICI-associated myocarditis, ACS, HF requiring hospital admission, CV death, and SCD. We therefore compared MACEs between the screened and nonscreened populations. An independent medical adjudication committee (S.H., M.L.J., and A.L.L.) was deemed necessary to ensure accurate outcomes (ie, MACEs), including ICI-associated myocarditis because of the discrepant contemporary diagnostic criteria.5,16 The committee took into account the International Cardio-Oncology Society criteria5 and definite or probable criteria from the American Heart Association17 for ICI-associated myocarditis. For other MACEs, the contemporary ESC definitions were used as appropriate (ie, ACS18 and HF19).

Statistical Analysis

Patient characteristics are reported as mean (SD), median (IQR), or count (percentage) as appropriate. Continuous variables were compared using the paired t test or Wilcoxon–Mann-Whitney test, and categorical variables using the χ2 or Fisher exact test. Kaplan-Meier curves were plotted for all-cause mortality, CV events, and the composite MACE end point and compared using the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs. For multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models, covariates were selected a priori based on existing literature and clinical relevance, including age, sex, lung cancer, metastatic disease, prior CV disease, prior anticancer treatment before ICI initiation, ipilimumab exposure, concomitant ICI therapy, and systematic troponin monitoring. To account for competing risks, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were applied using non-CV death as the competing event. Competitive risk analysis was the preferred option over time-dependent Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, since cancer-related mortality was high in this population with advanced and mostly metastatic cancer. A propensity score for systematic troponin screening was estimated using a multivariate logistic regression model. We adopted an outcome-based covariate selection strategy: baseline variables with P < .20 in univariate survival analyses were included in the multivariate model. The model included age, sex, cancer type, metastatic status, prior CV disease, hypertension, diabetes, prior CV investigations, prior oncologic treatments, mediastinal irradiation, and ICI type. Patients were matched 1:1 using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement (caliper width 0.01 on the logit of the propensity score). Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences and visualized with a Love plot. This approach was chosen to ensure that the propensity score prioritized the most potent potential confounders while avoiding the inclusion of instrumental variables that could increase the variance of the estimate.

All statistical tests were 2 tailed, with P < .05 considered significant. Analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.2 (R Project for Statistical Computing).

Results

Study Population

From January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2022, medical records from 876 patients were manually reviewed, and 859 were included in the TILT study (Figure 1). Characteristics of the entire study population are depicted in Table 1. The mean (SD) age was 70.1 (10.8) years; 342 patients (39.8%) were female and 517 (60.2%) were male. The median follow-up was 15.5 (IQR, 7.2-30.2) months, while the median time to death was 3.3 (IQR, 1.3-7.2) months. Most of the study population was treated by ICIs for lung cancer (585 [68.1%]), followed by renal cell carcinoma (73 [8.5%]) and bladder cancer (58 [6.8%]). Most patients (751 [87.4%]) had metastatic cancer when starting ICI therapy. ICIs were at least a second-line therapy in 404 patients (47.0%) who had received previous cancer therapies. Most received PD-1 and/or PD-L1 inhibitors (808 [94.1%]), including pembrolizumab (395 [46.0%]), nivolumab (287 [33.4%]), and durvalumab (117 [13.6%]). Forty-seven patients (5.5%) received a combination of ICIs (ie, PD-1 and CTLA-4 inhibitors). Eight hundred twelve patients (94.5%) received a single ICI therapy; 333 (38.8%) received chemotherapy or target therapies in combination, most including platinum salts (278 [32.4%]) and vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitors (23 [2.7%]). A history of CV disease was present prior to ICI treatment in 212 patients (24.7%), led by ischemic heart disease in 88 (10.2%) and stroke in 23 (2.7%). CV risk factors included tobacco use (past or present) in 631 of 825 patients with data available (76.5%), hypertension in 307 (35.7%), and diabetes in 105 (12.2%). A total of 547 patients (63.7%) had undergone a CV assessment prior to ICI initiation (Table 1).

Study Population Comparison According to Troponin Screening

Patient characteristics differed markedly when comparing patients who underwent troponin screening (n = 441) with those who did not (n = 418). Patients deemed suitable by the oncology teams for troponin screening were significantly younger (mean [SD] age, 69.0 [10.0] vs 72.0 [11.0] years) (P < .001), received pembrolizumab or durvalumab (337 of 441 [76.4%]) (P < .001) more often, and received nivolumab (85 of 441 [19.3%]) (P = .32) or ipilimumab (17 of 441 [3.8%]) (P < .001) less often. More patients screened for troponin levels had lung cancer vs those who were not screened (392 of 441 [88.9%] vs 193 of 418 [46.2%]) (P < .001). Underlying CV disease and risk factors were similar apart from smoking status; smokers (past or present) less often underwent troponin screening (329 of 441 [74.6%] vs 319 of 418 [76.3%]) (P < .001). Patients receiving other cancer drugs combined with ICIs also underwent troponin screening more often (246 [55.8%] vs 65 of 418 [15.6%]) (P < .001) (Table 1).

Unadjusted Outcomes

During the study period, 484 patients (56.3%) died at a median follow-up of 3.3 (IQR, 1.3-7.2) months; of these, 436 deaths (90.1%) were from cancer, 30 (6.2%) were from other causes, 3 (0.6%) were from CV disease, and 15 (3.1%) were of unknown origin. Thirty-six of 484 patients (7.4%) patients died from IRAEs, intricated with cancer progression. Cancer progression was observed in 663 patients (77.2%) during the study period, leading to ICI therapy cessation. All-cause mortality was higher in unscreened patients (HR, 0.53 [95% CI, 0.44-0.64]) (P < .001) compared with those undergoing troponin screening (eFigure 1 in Supplement 1).

Forty patients (4.7%) presented with MACEs during the study period, including 16 with (1.9%) with HF, 16 (1.9%) with ACS (2 with elevated ST segment; 14 with nonelevated ST segment), 6 (0.7%) with ICI-associated myocarditis, 2 (0.2%) with stroke, 1 (0.1%) with Tako Tsubo syndrome, 1 (0.1%) with pericarditis, and 3 (0.3%) with SCD (eFigures 2 and 3 in Supplement 1). Among the patients with HF, 1 of 16 died from HF, and ICI was therapy stopped due to HF in 1 of these. Among the 16 patients with ACS, none died from ACS, and ICI therapy was withheld in 1 due to the ACS. In 6 patients diagnosed with ICI-associated myocarditis, 4 had their treatment stopped, and none died from myocarditis. In those with either pericarditis, stroke, or Tako Tsubo syndrome, none died from the CV event and all resumed ICI therapy. Of note, 136 patients (15.8%) experienced extra CV IRAEs at some point during therapy.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis including 855 patients with data available identified systematic troponin screening as an independent factor associated with less overall mortality combined with MACEs (HR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.45-0.70]) (P < .001). Other factors associated with overall mortality combined with MACEs included metastatic cancer (HR, 2.46 [95% CI, 1.75-3.74]) (P < .001) and prior cancer therapy (ie, ICI as second-line therapy or later) (HR, 1.31 [95% CI, 1.07-1.61]) (P = .01) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). Active troponin screening was associated with fewer MACEs on Cox proportional hazards regression multivariate analysis (HR, 0.44 [95% CI, 0.20-0.95]) (P = .04) (eTable 2 in Supplement 1). On Cox proportional hazards regression–specific analysis for CV events and cause of analysis, troponin screening was associated with fewer CV events on multivariate analysis (HR, 0.57 [95% CI, 0.46-0.72]) (P < .001), as well as metastatic cancer (HR, 2.71 [95% CI, 1.88-3.92]) (P < .001) and prior cancer therapy (HR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.10-1.68]) (P = .005) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

CV Outcomes After Competitive Risk Analysis

The Fine-Gray test showed no difference in the cumulative incidence of MACEs between groups (HR, 0.56 [95% CI, 0.20-1.55]) (P = .27). In contrast, prior CV disease (HR, 2.20 [95% CI, 1.03-4.72]) (P = .04), concomitant cancer therapy (HR, 3.52 [95% CI, 1.15-10.81]) (P = .03), and dual IC therapy by use of ipilimumab (HR, 6.97 [95% CI, 1.93-25.15]) (P = .003) remained associated with MACEs (Table 2).

CV Outcomes in the Propensity Score–Matched Population

After propensity score matching, the study population was divided in 2 groups of 177 with active troponin screening and 177 without troponin screening (covariate balance is available in eFigure 4 in Supplement 1). Briefly, mean (SD) age was 70.0 (11.0) years, 140 patients (39.5%) were female and 214 (60.5%) were male, 275 (77.7%) had lung cancer, 304 (85.9%) had metastatic disease, 79 (22.3%) were former or active tobacco users, and 91 (25.7%) had underlying CV disease before starting ICI therapy (Table 3). There were 223 deaths (63.0%) and 20 patients with MACEs (5.6%) in the propensity score–matched population. There was no significant difference in terms of MACEs between the active troponin screening and the nonscreened populations after propensity score matching (HR, 0.49 [95% CI, 0.20-1.24]) (P = .13) (Figure 2).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of 859 patients, mainly consisting of patients aged 70 years with metastatic lung or urinary tract cancer and treated with ICIs alone or in association with other cancer therapies, systematic troponin screening seemed to be associated with lower all-cause mortality and fewer MACEs in multivariate modeling analyses. However, these associations did not persist after competitive risk analysis or propensity score matching in a smaller subset of 354 patients. Taken together, the results suggest that the apparent benefit of routine troponin screening in unselected ICI populations may largely reflect selection and confounding rather than a direct effect of screening. Importantly, outcomes remained driven by cancer mortality in our mostly older population with metastatic solid cancer and a high atherosclerotic risk profile, often receiving other cancer therapies.

Our results apparently diverge from current ESC 2022 guidelines for troponin screening in patients receiving ICIs. The heterogeneity of ICI-associated MACEs must be considered: the low incidence of adjudicated ICI-associated myocarditis in our cohort (0.7%) and the predominance of other CV events (HF and ACS) question the yield of universal troponin screening. Furthermore, the role of troponin screening in detecting subclinical atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion has not been validated in cardio-oncology, nor in cardiology at large. Its utility in identifying HF is equally debatable in a population where dyspnea may be attributable to the underlying cancer or other confounders such as anemia or pulmonary embolism. In addition, troponin elevations in oncology are multifactorial thereby lacking specificity, arising from microvascular-led ischemia, HF, kidney impairment, other confounders such as anemia or pulmonary embolism, or concomitant use of potentially cardiotoxic agents.10

Pathophysiology of ICI-associated cardiomyotoxicity differs greatly from that of noninflammatory HF or atherosclerotic-related events.2 We adjudicated only 6 episodes (0.7%) of ICI-associated myocarditis in this retrospective study bearing no standardized diagnostic strategy, unlike other contemporary retrospective13 or prospective8,9,20 cohorts. Our results are therefore congruent with the recent work published by Salem et al.12 In a French nationwide analysis of more than 700 000 patients, the 6-month incidence of ICI-associated cardiomyotoxicities was similar to our results (0.7%-0.9%), with myocarditis and myositis co-occurring in 13% to 23%, and identified risk enrichers (thymoma, dual ICI therapy, prior thymic disorders or myasthenia, and advanced age) that strongly shaped early fatality. Our cohort’s low adjudicated myocarditis rate (0.7%) and predominance of nonmyocarditis CV events align with those population-level frequencies and underscore why universal troponin screening yielded little association with MACEs after rigorous adjustment. Our data support risk-adapted screening focusing on the high-risk phenotypes identified by Salem et al12 rather than systematic troponin screening. Furthermore, Power et al21 derived and externally validated an ICI-associated myocarditis severity risk score in the International ICI-Myocarditis Registry, in which incremental troponin level elevation, low QRS voltage, left ventricular ejection fraction of less than 50%, cardiomuscular symptoms, and thymoma were associated with short-term cardiomyotoxic events and death. Our findings fit this framework: troponin levels might be prognostic once myocarditis is overtly present, but routine screening of unselected ICI recipients is not associated with fewer MACEs, suggesting troponin monitoring may be deployed or targeted to patients at high risk of ICI-associated cardiomyotoxicity (as per Salem et al12) and for severity stratification (as per Power et al21), rather than used universally.

In a recent retrospective single-center study of 428 patients, Cheng et al13 systematically measured cardiac troponin I levels during ICI therapy and reported that 9.8% of patients developed elevations, with symptomatic patients experiencing a markedly higher risk of MACEs and all-cause mortality compared with asymptomatic patients. Importantly, ICI-associated myocarditis was detected in 6 of 428 patients, but no cardiac deaths occurred. The authors concluded that troponin screening combined with symptom-based triage facilitated early detection and safe continuation of immunotherapy in many cases. The study by Cheng et al13 emphasizes the importance of CV symptoms when assessing patients with raised troponin levels, as in the FAST TRACK ICI (FastTrack Cardiovascular Assessment for Suspicion of Cardiovascular Events on Immunecheckpoint Inhibitors) prospective cohort.8 Unlike the TILT study, the study by Cheng et al13 lacked a nonscreened comparator group, precluding assessment of whether screening alters outcomes relative to usual practice. Two additional recently published prospective cohort studies also lacked a comparator.9,20 In the TILT study, we compared patients undergoing systematic screening vs those managed without troponin monitoring. After careful covariate balancing and competing-risk analysis, we found no significant reduction in adjudicated MACEs with systematic screening, despite unadjusted signals favoring screening. These discrepancies may reflect differences in study design (single-center prospective vs multicenter retrospective), populations (notably cancer severity), and troponin assay standardization. Taken together, these recently published studies suggest that while systematic troponin testing can aid in risk stratification and clinical triage after elevation, its universal use across all ICI-treated patients may not confer a measurable reduction in MACEs.

Our findings, together with those of Cheng et al,13 suggest that troponin monitoring is most valuable not as a universal screening tool but as part of a structured triage pathway. Future prospective studies should compare risk-adapted vs systematic approaches, using standardized assays and integrated cardio-oncology care pathways,8 to determine whether screening improves patient-centered outcomes such as safe ICI continuation, avoidance of unnecessary interruptions, and reduction of MACEs. Randomized designs, although challenging, would provide the highest level of evidence to inform future guideline recommendations.

Strengths and Limitations

To our knowledge, this study includes the largest cohort of patients undergoing ICI to date to test the utility of troponin screening to reduce MACEs, with a contemporary comparator. Despite its retrospective design, propensity score matching and competing risk modeling likely mitigated confounding from measured covariates. Retrospective design is vulnerable to selection and information bias, although MACEs were centrally adjudicated. Our study, as most observational studies, is limited by immortal time bias, but we performed a competitive risk analysis in the attempt to lessen this bias. Despite propensity score matching, residual confounding cannot be excluded, and sample size reduction due to the propensity matching may have reduced our statistical power. For statistical power issues, we did not perform subgroup analysis by cancer type or cotreatments. Also, this secondary analysis was not prespecified in the initial methods plan, which conveys additional bias. The TILT study lacked statistical power to address ICI-associated myocarditis alone, given that its incidence is known to be barely 1%. The retrospective nature and lack of systematic workup (ie, including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging) did not allow us to identify subclinical ICI-associated myocarditis, the incidence of which may be higher. In addition, our results are based on a population mainly with lung, kidney, and bladder cancer, with a high or very high atherosclerosis risk profile, as in other cohorts.20 Our results cannot be expanded to populations at lower CV risk such as those with skin cancer, or patients at high risk of ICI-associated myocarditis.12 In addition, we did not collect data on underlying autoimmune disease in our study. We chose to consider troponin measurements as fixed time points and not an exposure, driven by clinical reasoning. Troponin assays and thresholds were not standardized across centers, reflecting practice in clinical settings in France. However, we acknowledge that this choice may appear as a methodologic flaw, albeit clinically relevant. Last, at the time of the inclusion period, cardio-oncology services were not fully structured in any of the centers; thus, patients did not undergo a specific health care pathway in the case of increased troponin levels.

Conclusions

In this multicenter cohort of patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors for advanced metastatic cancer in a clinical setting, systematic troponin screening was not associated with reduced all-cause mortality or fewer CV events after competing-risk analysis or propensity score matching. The low incidence of ICI-associated myocarditis and the predominance of alternative CV events highlight the limited yield of universal troponin screening. Troponin monitoring may be most valuable once CV toxicity is clinically suspected, or in patients at high risk of myocarditis. Future prospective studies should determine whether risk-adapted screening improves patient outcomes while avoiding unnecessary treatment interruptions.
