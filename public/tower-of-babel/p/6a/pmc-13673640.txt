Chinese couples’ lived experiences and decision-making on pregnancy continuation following prenatal anomaly detection: phenomenological and theory of planned behaviour insights
Authors: Li Chen, Xu Zhang, Wei Yin, Qiyu Qian, Dandan Yang, Songwei Feng, Hong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPrenatal detection of fetal anomalies creates significant emotional and decision-making challenges for expectant parents. International research has described shock, uncertainty, and distress following diagnosis, yet little is known about how such experiences unfold within Chinese cultural and family contexts. Family authority, lineage expectations, and concerns about stigma may shape how parents interpret the diagnosis and negotiate reproductive choices. Evidence remains limited regarding how Chinese couples experience emotional disruption, navigate family involvement, and make sense of the diagnosis during a period marked by uncertainty and moral pressure. This study sought to address this gap by exploring the lived experiences of Chinese couples following detection of a suspected or confirmed fetal anomaly.MethodThe qualitative phenomenological study employed Moustakas’ transcendental phenomenology(TP) and Ajzen’s Theory of Planned Behavior(TPB). Conducted in Suzhou City, situated in the Yangtze River Delta region of Eastern China, the research spanned from November 21, 2024, to February 15, 2025. The participants were 30 couples who had experienced prenatal anomaly detection in the past 12 months.ResultsAnalysis identified five themes that described how couples experienced emotional rupture, cultural pressure, and relational strain after receiving a prenatal anomaly diagnosis. Parents reported an abrupt loss of their anticipated future, marked by shock, temporal disruption, and efforts to regain a sense of control. Decision-making occurred within a landscape shaped by moral tension, clinical uncertainty, and unequal options. Cultural expectations related to filial piety, lineage, and family reputation influenced how parents interpreted the diagnosis and negotiated reproductive choices. Family and clinical support played complex roles by offering stability for some and creating additional pressure for others. The diagnosis also reshaped couple relationships, strengthening some bonds while exposing significant strain in others.ConclusionsChinese couples experiencing potential fetal congenital anomalies undergo intense emotions and employ various coping strategies. Their decisions are influenced by medical advice, cultural influences, and the availability of resources. By integrating the TP and TPB, this study examines their experiences and decision-making processes, emphasizing the need for culturally tailored support and counseling that respects their emotional and cultural contexts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12884-026-09286-9.

Introduction

Prenatal detection of fetal anomalies represents a significant clinical and emotional challenge for expectant parents across many settings [1–3]. Advances in prenatal screening, diagnostic imaging, and genetic testing have increased the frequency with which potential anomalies are identified, often at early stages of pregnancy [4, 5]. While advanced technologies create opportunities for informed decision-making and early intervention, they also introduce substantial psychological strain [6, 7]. International research has documented that parents commonly experience intense shock, fear, confusion, and grief immediately following the detection of a suspected anomaly [8, 9]. Many struggle to process complex medical information, to interpret uncertain prognostic implications, or to maintain a stable emotional state while awaiting follow-up evaluations [3, 10]. Emotional disruption often unfolds during a period with limited time for reflection, which complicates engagement with clinicians and amplifies the difficulty of navigating decisions regarding continuation or termination of pregnancy [6, 8, 11].

Although research in many countries has improved understanding of emotional responses to anomaly detection, most existing studies are rooted in Western cultural contexts [1, 12]. Family structures, social expectations, and decision-making norms in China differ substantially from Western settings, which suggests that findings generated elsewhere cannot be assumed to apply directly [13]. In China, pregnancy carries obligations and expectations that extend beyond the couple. Cultural norms emphasize filial piety, respect for elders, and the preservation of lineage [14, 15]. Decision-making during pregnancy often involves parents, in-laws, and wider family networks whose opinions may hold moral authority [16, 17]. Anxiety about maintaining family harmony, securing an ideal reproductive outcome, and preserving social respectability can shape how parents react to a diagnosis of potential fetal anomaly [14, 17, 18].

Prior research from China, though limited in scope, indicates that parents may experience additional psychological strain due to concerns about stigma surrounding disability and the belief that a child perceived as imperfect may diminish family standing [13, 19]. Many parents fear that raising a child with a disability will create long-term emotional and financial burdens that extend to the entire family system [13, 20]. Elders may also express strong views regarding continuation or termination of pregnancy, shaped by personal values, religious beliefs, or traditional expectations for family succession [13].

Parents may therefore find themselves negotiating conflicting viewpoints from multiple family members while simultaneously grappling with distress, uncertainty, and the need for urgent decision-making [13, 20]. This intersection of emotional, cultural, and relational pressures distinguishes the Chinese context and warrants deeper qualitative exploration [13, 21].

Despite the importance of cultural and familial dynamics in China, research that directly examines the lived experience of anomaly detection remains limited. Existing quantitative studies in China provide information about continuation and termination rates [22–24], but limited literature illuminates how parents interpret and navigate the diagnosis within their daily lives. A small number of qualitative studies have explored attitudes or decision rationales, yet they often focus on reactions rather than the deeper sense-making processes that unfold over time [1, 13, 25]. Little is known about how parents experience the immediate bodily and temporal shock of receiving the diagnosis, how they reconstruct imagined futures, or how they attempt to regain emotional grounding under conditions of medical uncertainty. Furthermore, existing studies often overlook the relational dimension, including how couples communicate, how responsibility for decisions is distributed, and how family authority shapes the negotiation of possible actions.

A theoretical void also remains. Much prior work describes parental emotions or documents decision outcomes, but relatively little research has examined how emotional responses intersect with cultural norms and social structures to shape the reasoning process [1, 8, 9, 13]. In China, where reproductive choices are often influenced by collective interests rather than individual preferences, an examination of such intersections is essential. A qualitative approach grounded in phenomenological inquiry is well suited for this purpose because it can capture the lived, embodied, and relational character of the experience. It allows exploration of how parents understand the diagnosis, how they experience the flow of time and emotion during uncertainty, and how cultural and familial forces shape their evolving interpretations.

A richer and more detailed understanding of the experiences of Chinese couples is needed to inform culturally responsive counseling practices and to strengthen communication between clinicians and families. Insight into the lived experience can guide more effective support for parents who confront emotionally and ethically complex decisions in a context shaped by strong cultural expectations and family authority. Such understanding is essential for improving the quality of perinatal care in China, particularly as diagnostic technologies continue to advance and as parents encounter increasingly complex information within compressed decision timelines.

Therefore, our study aimed to explore the lived experiences of Chinese couples following the detection of a suspected or confirmed fetal anomaly during pregnancy. The study seeks to understand how parents interpret and respond to the diagnosis, how emotional and temporal disruptions unfold, and how cultural norms, family expectations, and relational dynamics influence reasoning and decision-making within the context of their daily lives.

Materials and methods

Study setting

This study was conducted in Suzhou City, situated in the Yangtze River Delta region of Eastern China. Renowned for its rich cultural heritage and rapid economic development, Suzhou is one of the country’s most economically advanced cities.

The city features a diverse population comprising both native residents and migrant workers from various regions, providing a unique context for exploring the experiences of couples confronted with possible congenital fetal deformities. The advanced healthcare infrastructure in Suzhou, including state-of-the-art hospitals and clinics, facilitated the recruitment of participants and the collection of high-quality data. The study was conducted between November 21, 2024, and February 15,2025.

The healthcare system in Suzhou emphasizes comprehensive prenatal care and routine antenatal screening. Pregnant women are encouraged to attend regular check-ups, which include ultrasound examinations, blood tests, and other diagnostic procedures aimed at the early detection of fetal anomalies. Obstetricians and prenatal care providers in the region adhere to national guidelines for prenatal screening and diagnosis. When potential congenital anomalies are identified, couples receive counseling that covers medical information, possible interventions, and prognostic implications, enabling them to make informed decisions about their pregnancy.

Participants were fully informed about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits. They were assured that their participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw from the study at any point without any repercussions on their medical care. The measures taken to ensure confidentiality and data security were explained, and participants were provided with contact information for the research team and the ethics committee should they have any questions or concerns.

Study design

This study used a qualitative design informed by Transcendental Phenomenology (TP) and the Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB). This design was selected to capture the lived experience of parents who received a prenatal anomaly diagnosis and to understand how social expectations and perceived control shape decision-making. Phenomenology supported an in-depth exploration of emotional disruption, temporal disturbance, and shifts in identity as parents encountered diagnostic uncertainty. The TPB offered a complementary lens for examining how attitudes, perceived social expectations, and perceptions of choice influenced parents’ actions.

TP offers a systematic process, including epoche, phenomenological reduction, imaginative variation, and synthesis, that enables researchers to bracket personal biases and derive the essential structures of participants’ experiences through horizonalization and thematic clustering [26, 27]. This method ensured that our inquiry remained faithful to the phenomenon “as it appears” to participants, uncovering meanings and essences rooted in their own descriptions [28].

In parallel, we integrated Ajzen’s TPB to frame the examination of decision-making processes. TPB, an extension of the Theory of Reasoned Action, posits that behavioral intentions arise from three domains, attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, and has demonstrated robust predictive power across health-related behaviors. We designed interview questions and prompts specifically to elicit data on these TPB constructs, asking participants, for instance, to reflect on the perceived benefits and drawbacks of continuing the pregnancy (attitudes), the influence of family or societal expectations (subjective norms), and their confidence in managing medical, financial, and emotional challenges (perceived behavioral control) [29–31].

Sampling

Participants were selected using purposive sampling to ensure they met specific criteria relevant to the study’s objectives. Inclusion criteria included: (1) being either the mother or father from a couple recently informed (within the 12 months, at least one month) of a possible congenital fetal deformity during antenatal examinations; (2) pregnant women aged 25–45 years; (3) willing and able to provide informed consent; and (4) fluent in Mandarin Chinese to facilitate effective communication during interviews. Exclusion criteria were: (1) individuals known to be with severe mental illness or cognitive impairment that could interfere with their ability to participate; and (2) those experiencing extreme emotional distress that precluded participation, as determined by a healthcare professional.

Recognizing that mothers and fathers may have different perspectives and emotional responses, the study included either parent from each couple to capture a broader range of experiences. This approach aimed to provide a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon by including diverse viewpoints within the cultural and familial context.

Participants were recruited through obstetricians and prenatal clinics in Suzhou. Healthcare providers identified eligible individuals during routine antenatal visits or follow-up appointments after a potential congenital anomaly was detected. The providers introduced the study to potential participants, offering a brief explanation. Interested individuals were then approached by a member of the research team, who explained the study’s purpose, procedures, and ethical considerations in detail. Questions were answered thoroughly to ensure participants fully understood their involvement. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before proceeding with data collection. No incentives were offered, ensuring that participation was entirely voluntary and free from coercion.

The sample size was determined based on the concept of data saturation, which occurs when two consecutive additional interviews no longer yield new themes or insights. We determined saturation separately for phenomenological themes (after 26 interviews) and TPB-related decision constructs (after 30 interviews).

Data collection

Before initiating data collection, the interview guide was pilot-tested with two individuals meeting the inclusion criteria but not included in the final sample. This process helped refine the interview questions for clarity and relevance, ensuring they effectively elicited detailed responses about participants’ experiences. Adjustments were made based on feedback, enhancing the guide’s effectiveness (Interview guide provided as Supplement 1).

Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews conducted in private, comfortable settings chosen by the participants, such as a quiet communication room in the hospital. Each interview began with open-ended questions designed to encourage participants to share their stories in their own words. For example, participants were asked, “Can you describe your experience upon learning about the possible fetal anomaly?” and “What factors influenced your decisions following the diagnosis?” In addition, we included TPB-informed probes to capture: Attitudes (“What benefits or drawbacks did you associate with continuing the pregnancy?“); Subjective norms (“Whose opinions mattered most in your decision?“); and Perceived behavioral control (“How confident did you feel in managing medical, financial, or emotional challenges?“).

Only one participant from each couple was interviewed at a time. This approach provided a safe space for individuals to express personal thoughts and emotions without the influence or presence of their partner, potentially leading to more candid and insightful responses.

Given the sensitive nature of the topic, the interviewer was trained by a senior qualitative researcher through simulated interviews and guided feedback sessions that focused on sensitive communication, trauma-informed interaction, and management of participant distress. Procedures were in place to address any distress that might arise during the interview. If a participant exhibited signs of significant emotional discomfort, the interviewer offered to pause or terminate the session and provided information about available psychological support services. Participants were reminded that their participation was voluntary and that they could decline to answer any questions or withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences.

Interviews were audio-recorded with the participants’ consent to ensure accurate capture of their narratives. The duration of the interviews ranged from 20 to 34 min (average 23.3 min), depending on the depth of the participants’ sharing.

The recordings were transcribed verbatim.

Data were managed with strict confidentiality. Transcripts and recordings were anonymized by assigning code numbers to participants and removing any identifying information. The data were stored securely on password-protected computers, accessible only to members of the research team directly involved in the study.

Data analysis

Data analysis unfolded in two integrated stages that directly mirrored our dual theoretical frameworks. In the first stage, we employed Moustakas’ TP procedures to distill the essence of participants’ lived experiences [26, 27]. Analysis began with epoche, during which researchers set aside personal assumptions to approach the data with an open, receptive stance. We then conducted phenomenological reduction by reading each original Mandarin transcript in full, identifying each statement relevant to the experience of receiving a fetal anomaly diagnosis, and treating all such statements with equal value (horizonalization). Through imaginative variation, we explored structural meanings by considering different possible contexts and perspectives for each significant statement. These meaning units were organized into clusters that reflected common themes across participants. Finally, we synthesized textural descriptions (“what” was experienced) with structural descriptions (“how” it was experienced) to articulate a distilled, fundamental essence of the phenomenon [28].

In the second stage, we applied Ajzen’s TPB to explicate the decision-making processes described in the data. We revisited passages in which participants discussed their intentions, choices, and motivations, and systematically coded these segments according to TPB’s three core constructs: attitudes toward pregnancy continuation or termination, subjective norms shaped by family and societal expectations, and perceived behavioral control over medical, financial, and emotional resources. By mapping participants’ own words onto these domains, we were able to trace the pathways by which beliefs, normative pressures, and control perceptions influenced reported decision intentions and outcomes [29–31].

To ensure trustworthiness, we conducted member checking by sharing both phenomenological essences and TPB-based mappings with a subset of participants, who affirmed the accuracy and resonance of our interpretations. The research team maintained reflexive journals throughout the analysis to surface and bracket any emerging biases. An external qualitative methods expert reviewed the analytic stages, bolstering dependability and confirmability.

Rigor was supported through prolonged engagement during interviews, team-based analysis, and triangulation across participants and analytic perspectives. Dependability and confirmability were strengthened by maintaining an audit trail and by engaging in reflexive practice throughout the research process. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the study context and participants, and by demonstrating alignment between phenomenological insights and constructs from the TPB.

Confidentiality

Participants’ confidentiality was strictly protected throughout the study. Personal identifiers were removed from all data, and participants were assigned unique codes to anonymize their information. Audio recordings, transcripts, and other sensitive data were stored securely on encrypted, password-protected devices. Access to the data was limited to the primary research team members. Participants were informed of these measures and assured that their personal information would remain confidential and be used solely for research purposes.

Researcher’s role

The research team comprised professionals from diverse backgrounds, including specialists in antenatal management, a pediatric plastic surgeon, and experienced clinicians in gynecology and obstetrics. Every member received training in the qualitative research methods applied in this study.

The researchers recognized the importance of reflexivity in qualitative research, acknowledging how their backgrounds, beliefs, and experiences could influence the study. A reflexivity statement (Supplement 2) was prepared, detailing the researchers’ positions and potential biases. To manage these influences, strategies such as reflexive journaling were employed, allowing researchers to critically reflect on their interactions with participants and their interpretations of the data. Regular discussions among the research team facilitated the examination of differing viewpoints and helped ensure that the analysis remained rooted in the participants’ narratives. Researchers reflected on how their theoretical assumptions about lived experience and decision-making could influence coding in both the phenomenological and TPB phases.

Ethical considerations

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Affiliated Hospital of Soochow University (Approval Number: 2024-241137). All participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. The research adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and complied with institutional and national regulations regarding human subject protection and privacy.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

A total of thirty participants took part in the study, including twenty-five women and five men. Women were on average 31.7 years old, with ages ranging from twenty-five to forty-four. Men were on average 32.4 years old, with ages ranging from twenty-six to forty-seven. Most participants had completed an undergraduate degree or higher, while a smaller group had completed high school or lower levels of formal education. Two thirds of participants were employed at the time of the interview, while one third were not engaged in paid work. Participants received their prenatal anomaly diagnosis at an average gestational age of 25.6 weeks, with a range from sixteen to thirty-one weeks. The fetal conditions identified varied.

Chromosomal anomalies and congenital heart disease were the most common diagnoses. Renal anomalies, neurological or craniofacial malformations, and a set of additional conditions were also represented. Participants described a range of decision pathways following diagnosis. Sixteen chose to terminate the pregnancy, while fourteen chose to continue. This variation allowed exploration of how parents with different reproductive decisions interpreted the diagnosis and navigated emotional, cultural, and relational influences (Table 1).

Thematic findings

The analysis yielded five overarching themes illustrating how Chinese couples experienced and interpreted the emotional, cultural, and relational complexities following a prenatal anomaly diagnosis. Each theme captures a tension or meaning structure that shaped participants’ lived experiences and decisions.

Theme 1: “living in a collapsed future”: emotional shock, temporal disruption, and the work of re-anchoring the self

Participants described the moment of fetal anomaly detection as an ontological rupture: an abrupt dismantling of the assumed trajectory of pregnancy and parenthood. Rather than merely “receiving bad news,” they experienced the diagnosis as a profound disturbance in the continuity of time, identity, and embodied expectation. The language they used, such as “falling,” “freezing,” “numbness,” “emptiness” revealed a deep phenomenological disruption. The pregnancy, previously structured by routine anticipations (checkups, growth milestones, baby preparations), suddenly lost its anchoring role in everyday temporality.

Subtheme 1.1: the moment time stopped: shock, numbness, and perceptual disintegration

Participants consistently narrated the diagnostic moment as one in which the world momentarily stopped functioning in a recognizable way. Their descriptions revealed a collapse of sensory integration: they could see, but the visual world no longer made sense. They could hear the doctor, but meaning could not be processed. This breakdown reflected a sudden fracture of the “taken-for-granted future” embedded in pregnancy.

One mother described this moment of existential destabilization:“When the doctor told us, I felt like the ground had disappeared beneath my feet. I couldn't hear anything else he said after that moment. It was as if time stood still, and I was frozen in disbelief.”

“When the doctor told us, I felt like the ground had disappeared beneath my feet. I couldn't hear anything else he said after that moment. It was as if time stood still, and I was frozen in disbelief.”

Her imagery, “ground disappeared,” “time stood still”, illustrates how diagnosis shatters the embodied security of progressing toward motherhood.

Another participant described a split between bodily presence and cognitive absence, which is characteristic of traumatic discontinuity:“I remember staring blankly at the ultrasound screen, not understanding what the doctor was pointing out. My mind just wouldn't accept that something could be wrong with our baby.”

“I remember staring blankly at the ultrasound screen, not understanding what the doctor was pointing out. My mind just wouldn't accept that something could be wrong with our baby.”

The inability to “accept” the information reveals a temporary suspension of meaning-making capacity. In phenomenological terms, her “natural attitude” toward pregnancy, expecting normality, was suddenly undermined.

A third participant extended this dissociation beyond the clinic, suggesting a lingering sense that she had been displaced from her own life:“I walked out of the clinic in a daze. The bustling streets felt surreal, like I was watching someone else's life unfold.”

“I walked out of the clinic in a daze. The bustling streets felt surreal, like I was watching someone else's life unfold.”

In this case, the diagnosis punctures not only medical space but the participant’s lived world, creating a feeling of alienation from everyday reality.

Subtheme 1.2: emotional oscillation: living between hope and dread

Following the initial rupture, participants entered a prolonged period of emotional volatility. Their narratives showed oscillation, not progression. They vacillated between contradictory emotional states: moments of fragile hope, sudden floods of despair, temporary numbness, and intrusive anxiety. This oscillation reflects a continuous re-encounter with uncertainty.

One participant described this daily emotional instability vividly:“The emotional rollercoaster hasn't stopped. Some days, I wake up feeling a glimmer of hope, only to be engulfed by overwhelming sadness by the afternoon. It's exhausting.”

“The emotional rollercoaster hasn't stopped. Some days, I wake up feeling a glimmer of hope, only to be engulfed by overwhelming sadness by the afternoon. It's exhausting.”

This oscillation was exacerbated by the cyclical nature of clinical interactions. Ultrasound appointments became emotionally charged, sites where hope and terror collided. Another woman explained how repeated encounters with medical spaces intensified her distress:“Every prenatal appointment filled me with dread. The fear of hearing more bad news consumed me. Sleep became elusive, and the constant anxiety was debilitating.”

“Every prenatal appointment filled me with dread. The fear of hearing more bad news consumed me. Sleep became elusive, and the constant anxiety was debilitating.”

Instead of reassurance, the appointments reactivated trauma, reinforcing a sense of precariousness.

Over time, some participants began slowly accommodating the emotional weight. This did not reflect acceptance in a linear sense. Rather, it was a gradual process of integrating grief into daily life:“Acceptance didn't come easily. It was a slow process of acknowledging my feelings, allowing myself to grieve, and finding small moments of peace amidst the chaos.”

“Acceptance didn't come easily. It was a slow process of acknowledging my feelings, allowing myself to grieve, and finding small moments of peace amidst the chaos.”

This demonstrates their processes of meaning-making, the gradual rebuilding of emotional coherence amid chaos.

Subtheme 1.3: coping as reclaiming agency: emotional expression, spiritual practices, and functional immersion

Participants sought a wide range of coping strategies, talking, praying, working, seeking solitude, each representing attempts to rebuild psychological grounding and reclaim a sense of control. The strategies were not simply “stress management” but ways of restoring agency in a world that had suddenly become uncertain.

Several participants described the need to express overwhelming emotions safely and to be heard without judgment:“Talking to my sister helped immensely. She listened without judgment and let me pour out all my fears and frustrations. Those conversations were a lifeline.”

“Talking to my sister helped immensely. She listened without judgment and let me pour out all my fears and frustrations. Those conversations were a lifeline.”

This emotional ventilation functioned as a counterweight to the internalized pressure to “stay strong,” which many participants felt.

Others sought spiritual containment and symbolic meaning, using prayer and ritual as tools to stabilize the emotional self:“I found comfort in visiting the temple. Lighting incense and praying gave me a sense of peace, even if just for a little while. It felt like I was doing something meaningful.”

“I found comfort in visiting the temple. Lighting incense and praying gave me a sense of peace, even if just for a little while. It felt like I was doing something meaningful.”

This ritual provided existential orientation when biomedical uncertainty felt overwhelming.

Still others strategically relied on routine and productivity to occupy mental space and avoid intrusive thoughts:“Keeping busy with work was my way of coping. Staying occupied meant I didn't have to dwell on the situation constantly. It was a temporary escape.”

“Keeping busy with work was my way of coping. Staying occupied meant I didn't have to dwell on the situation constantly. It was a temporary escape.”

Theme 2: “choosing amid unequal choices”: moral deliberation, clinical uncertainty, and the emotional labor of decision-making

For participants, deciding whether to continue or terminate the pregnancy was not a matter of choosing between two clear paths. Instead, they confronted a landscape of “unequal choices,” each weighted with emotional, moral, and relational consequences. Participants described decision-making as an iterative, exhausting process, one that required moving between medical information, imagined futures, family expectations, and their own shifting emotional capacities.

Rather than a rational calculation, decision-making was narrated as a profound moral experience: trying to determine which path would cause “less harm,” “less regret,” or “less suffering” for their child, themselves, and their families. Underneath this deliberation lay a deep struggle to balance hope, fear, responsibility, and cultural ideals of what constitutes a “good parent.”

Subtheme 2.1: medical information as anchor and overwhelm: navigating prognostic ambiguity

Medical information played a dual role. On one hand, it helped participants create some structure within uncertainty. On the other, the sheer complexity of genetic terminology, probabilities, and prognostic ranges created confusion, fear, and emotional overload. Participants frequently described medical consultations as moments when they desperately sought clarity but were simultaneously overwhelmed by the implications of what they heard.

One couple expressed how comprehensive explanations grounded them emotionally:“Our doctor was patient and thorough. He didn't rush us and took the time to explain every detail, which made us feel more informed and less alone.”

“Our doctor was patient and thorough. He didn't rush us and took the time to explain every detail, which made us feel more informed and less alone.”

This sense of being “not alone” underscores how clinical communication shapes parents’ emotional security.

Another participant described the emotional difficulty of receiving frank but painful information:“The medical team's honesty was hard to hear, but I appreciated their transparency. Knowing all the potential outcomes helped us weigh our choices realistically.”

“The medical team's honesty was hard to hear, but I appreciated their transparency. Knowing all the potential outcomes helped us weigh our choices realistically.”

Honesty became a paradoxical comfort, distressing but necessary for decision-making.

For others, the clinical language itself became a barrier, contributing to a sense of drowning in complexity:“Some of the medical jargon was overwhelming, but the doctor broke it down into terms we could understand. That clarity was crucial.”

“Some of the medical jargon was overwhelming, but the doctor broke it down into terms we could understand. That clarity was crucial.”

The need for digestible information reveals how medical communication intersects with emotional processing. In all cases, the way clinicians framed uncertainty profoundly influenced how parents interpreted risk, imagined the child’s future, and evaluated their options.

Subtheme 2.2: ethical and practical weighing: balancing suffering, hope, responsibility, and feasibility

Faced with uncertain prognoses, parents engaged in deeply ethical reasoning. They imagined future suffering, both the child’s and their own, and weighed it against the possibility of survival, resilience, and unexpected positive outcomes. Their deliberations reflected a struggle to reconcile biomedical probabilities with cultural expectations of parental responsibility and moral duty. One participant articulated the ethical dilemma starkly:“Learning that our child would need multiple surgeries with uncertain outcomes made us question if it was fair to bring them into the world to suffer.”

“Learning that our child would need multiple surgeries with uncertain outcomes made us question if it was fair to bring them into the world to suffer.”

This captures the moral calculus many faced: the fairness, dignity, and ethical justification of continuation.

Another participant shifted attention to the long-term practical realities of caregiving:“We thought about the long-term care required and whether we had the resources, both emotionally and financially, to provide the support our child would need.”

“We thought about the long-term care required and whether we had the resources, both emotionally and financially, to provide the support our child would need.”

For some women, the calculus also involved their own physical safety:“The doctors mentioned risks to my health if we continued the pregnancy, which added another layer of complexity to our decision.”

“The doctors mentioned risks to my health if we continued the pregnancy, which added another layer of complexity to our decision.”

In their case, maternal risk adds another moral dimension: self-preservation versus perceived duty.

Across narratives, decision-making emerged as moral labor involving imagined futures, ethical obligations, and evaluations of what constituted a life worth living, weighed not abstractly, but through the lens of personal and cultural values.

Subtheme 2.3: negotiating differences within the couple: emotional divergence, conflict, and co-construction of choice

Decision-making was rarely a unified process. Many couples discovered emotional or interpretive differences rooted in divergent coping styles, gendered expectations, or distinct relationships to the imagined child.

Several couples described disagreements that arose when partners processed uncertainty differently:“My husband wanted to continue the pregnancy, holding onto hope for a miracle, while I was leaning toward termination to prevent potential suffering.

“My husband wanted to continue the pregnancy, holding onto hope for a miracle, while I was leaning toward termination to prevent potential suffering.

This illustrates how partners may inhabit different emotional timelines, one anchored in hope, the other in protective caution.

Intergenerational voices added further complexity:“Our parents had strong opinions, offering advice based on tradition and their own fears. Balancing their input with our own feelings was incredibly challenging.”

“Our parents had strong opinions, offering advice based on tradition and their own fears. Balancing their input with our own feelings was incredibly challenging.”

Such external pressures force couples to negotiate not only with each other but with extended family systems and cultural norms.

Some couples sought formal support to mediate the tensions:“We attended counseling together, which helped us communicate more openly and find common ground in our decision.”

“We attended counseling together, which helped us communicate more openly and find common ground in our decision.”

Counseling supported couples in articulating fears and aligning divergent perspectives.

One participant distilled the core dilemma that many couples described:“We were caught between hope and despair. The medical facts were stark, but our hearts weren't ready to let go. Making a decision felt impossible.”

“We were caught between hope and despair. The medical facts were stark, but our hearts weren't ready to let go. Making a decision felt impossible.”

Theme 3: “carrying the weight of lineage and ‘face’”: cultural obligations, intergenerational authority, and the meaning of disability

Participants described the diagnosis not solely as an individual or couple-level crisis but as a family-wide moral event deeply embedded in Chinese sociocultural expectations. Their narratives revealed the enduring influence of traditional values, filial piety, lineage continuity, and the collective maintenance of “face”, in shaping how they interpreted the diagnosis, anticipated judgment, and evaluated their options.

Subtheme 3.1: Disability through a cultural lens: Burden, stigma, and threats to lineage

Participants struggled with how disability is perceived and treated in contemporary Chinese society. Their accounts reflected fears of social stigma, exclusionary attitudes, and diminished opportunities for children with disabilities, all of which shaped how they imagined their child’s future and how they believed others would view their family.

One participant articulated the pressure to uphold ideals of family excellence:“In our culture, there's a strong emphasis on family lineage and perfection. The idea of having a child with a disability felt like we'd be letting everyone down.”

“In our culture, there's a strong emphasis on family lineage and perfection. The idea of having a child with a disability felt like we'd be letting everyone down.”

This highlights how disability is not only a medical condition but a cultural disruption of familial expectations.

Another participant expressed chronic worry about societal judgment and the child’s future quality of life:“I believe every life is valuable, but I couldn't ignore the harsh realities of how people with disabilities are treated here. It made me worry constantly.”

“I believe every life is valuable, but I couldn't ignore the harsh realities of how people with disabilities are treated here. It made me worry constantly.”

This acknowledges compassion and moral conviction while simultaneously confronting external realities of social exclusion. The sentiments reveal that decisions were not made in a vacuum. They were shaped by anticipated stigma, cultural expectations of “successful parenthood,” and concerns about burdening the lineage.

Subtheme 3.2: intergenerational pressure: expectations, authority, and the constraints of filial piety

Parents and in-laws were often deeply involved in the decision-making process. Their opinions carried significant emotional weight due to norms of filial piety and family-centered decision-making. Participants felt compelled to consider elders’ wishes, whether or not they aligned with their own.

Some described pressure toward termination based on the desire for a “healthy heir”:“My parents urged us to think about having another child instead, emphasizing the importance of a healthy heir. It was painful to hear.”

“My parents urged us to think about having another child instead, emphasizing the importance of a healthy heir. It was painful to hear.”

Others faced strong pressure in the opposite direction, to continue the pregnancy regardless of prognosis:“His family was adamant that we should do everything possible to ensure the child was born, regardless of the prognosis.”

“His family was adamant that we should do everything possible to ensure the child was born, regardless of the prognosis.”

The opposing positions illustrate how intergenerational authority can push couples into difficult emotional positions, forcing them to navigate conflicting ethical propositions.

One participant described this negotiation as a nearly impossible balancing act:“Balancing respect for our elders with making the right choice for our own family was an impossible tightrope.”

“Balancing respect for our elders with making the right choice for our own family was an impossible tightrope.”

This tension emphasizes the cultural expectation to honor parents’ wishes while also struggling to uphold personal emotional limits, marital unity, and parental responsibility.

Theme 4: “support as stabilizer and stressor”: the ambivalent roles of family and professional networks

Support systems profoundly influenced how participants experienced and managed the diagnosis. Yet support was rarely uniformly positive. While practical help and emotional presence offered relief and containment, unsolicited advice, pressure, or emotionally charged reactions from others often intensified stress.

Participants’ experiences illustrate the duality of support within collectivist family structures: closeness can nurture, but it can also constrain.

Subtheme 4.1: family support as sanctuary, and occasionally suffocation

For many, family members provided much-needed practical and emotional support. The presence of parents or in-laws often brought comfort and relief during a destabilizing time:“My mother-in-law stayed with us, cooking meals and helping around the house, which relieved some of our daily burdens.”

“My mother-in-law stayed with us, cooking meals and helping around the house, which relieved some of our daily burdens.”

Yet the same family members sometimes became sources of stress when support turned into directive guidance, unsolicited advice, or emotional overinvolvement:“While their intentions were good, constant advice from relatives became overwhelming. We needed space to process.”

“While their intentions were good, constant advice from relatives became overwhelming. We needed space to process.”

This duality demonstrates how collectivist family norms, care, responsibility, interdependence, can create both connection and constraint.

Subtheme 4.2: professional empathy and holistic care as emotional anchors

Healthcare professionals shaped the emotional climate of the diagnostic journey. Participants valued clinicians who acknowledged their distress, offered psychological space, and treated them as whole persons rather than solely as medical cases.

One participant described counseling as a turning point in her emotional coping:“The counselor provided a safe space to express my fears and frustrations. Her empathy made a significant impact.”

“The counselor provided a safe space to express my fears and frustrations. Her empathy made a significant impact.”

Another praised the holistic approach of their doctor:“Our doctor didn't just focus on the medical aspects; he cared about how we were coping emotionally.”

“Our doctor didn't just focus on the medical aspects; he cared about how we were coping emotionally.”

The experiences demonstrate the importance of relational attunement in clinical care, and demonstrate how empathy can stabilize parents navigating uncertainty.

Subtheme 4.3: seeking information as a strategy to reclaim control

Participants often felt disoriented by uncertainty. Actively searching for reliable information became a key strategy to regain agency and reduce helplessness.

Some engaged in exhaustive question preparation:“I came to appointments with a list of questions. Understanding every detail was important to me.”

“I came to appointments with a list of questions. Understanding every detail was important to me.”

Others described how unclear information increased anxiety:“When information was unclear, it heightened my anxiety. Clear communication from the medical team was essential.”

“When information was unclear, it heightened my anxiety. Clear communication from the medical team was essential.”

Finally, external resources helped parents reconstruct a sense of mastery over the situation:“Access to brochures and reputable online resources allowed us to educate ourselves beyond the consultations.”

“Access to brochures and reputable online resources allowed us to educate ourselves beyond the consultations.”

Information-seeking in this case operated not just cognitively but emotionally as a way to counter fear with structure.

Theme 5: “a crisis that reshapes bonds”: strengthening, straining, and transforming relationships

The prenatal diagnosis acted as a relational crucible that transformed couple and family dynamics. Across narratives, relationships were neither uniformly strengthened nor consistently strained. Instead, participants described a complex reconfiguration of roles, intimacy, communication, and emotional labor. Crisis became both a mirror revealing vulnerabilities and a catalyst intensifying connection.

Subtheme 5.1: deepened intimacy through shared adversity

For many couples, the shared experience of crisis fostered emotional closeness and renewed relational commitment. Facing uncertainty together created a sense of solidarity and increased mutual empathy.

One participant expressed the strengthening of their partnership:“Facing this challenge together brought us closer. We learned to support each other in ways we hadn't before.”

“Facing this challenge together brought us closer. We learned to support each other in ways we hadn't before.”

Another echoed the idea of hardship revealing love and endurance:“Going through this hardship revealed the depth of our commitment. It reaffirmed our love and partnership.”

“Going through this hardship revealed the depth of our commitment. It reaffirmed our love and partnership.”

Support also came from friends, creating a wider network of emotional containment:“Friends rallied around us, offering support without us even having to ask. Their kindness was overwhelming.”

“Friends rallied around us, offering support without us even having to ask. Their kindness was overwhelming.”

The accounts show how crisis can reaffirm relational bonds, strengthen emotional attunement, and create new forms of relational meaning.

Subtheme 5.2: strain, disconnection, and the exposure of fault lines

For others, the diagnosis exposed or exacerbated relational tensions. Stress reduced patience, blurred communication, and heightened emotional sensitivities. Participants described arguing more, withdrawing emotionally, or struggling to understand each other’s coping styles.

As one couple explained:

“The stress sometimes led to arguments. We were both hurting and didn't always know how to help each other.”

“The stress sometimes led to arguments. We were both hurting and didn't always know how to help each other.”

Conflicts extended beyond the couple when family members disagreed about the “right” decision:

“Our relationships with some family members became strained due to disagreements over what we should do.”

“Our relationships with some family members became strained due to disagreements over what we should do.”

Crucially, participants did not describe the tensions as failures but as reflections of the intensity of the situation, evidence of how crisis stretches relational resources but can also prompt growth and renegotiation.

Discussion

This study identified five core phenomenological themes, namely emotional impact; decision‑making process; cultural and societal influences; support systems; and impact on relationships, among Chinese couples informed of possible fetal anomalies. Participants described an initial shock and denial, a protracted emotional journey, and varied coping strategies.

Decision‑making hinged on medical advice, prognostic information, and couple negotiation. Deeply rooted cultural norms around lineage and “face” shaped perceptions of disability, while formal and informal support systems mediated resilience. Relationship dynamics oscillated between strengthened bonds and conflict. Mapping these themes onto the TPB revealed that attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control jointly informed intentions and choices regarding pregnancy continuation.

Our findings confirm the intense, non‑linear emotional trajectory documented in previous qualitative work on prenatal anomaly diagnoses [32–34]. Participants’ initial shock and dissociation align with global reports of traumatic stress reactions following fetal anomaly notification [33]. The ongoing oscillation between hope and despair reflects grief-processing models that emphasize cyclical rather than stage‑based emotional responses [32]. Importantly, the breadth of coping mechanisms, from social sharing to spiritual practices, mirrors patterns identified in both Chinese and international settings, where social support and faith‐based strategies mitigate distress [34]. However, the depth of prolonged anxiety and its intrusion into daily life underscores the need for sustained psychological interventions beyond the immediate post‑diagnosis period.

Participants’ deliberations showcased the interplay of clinical data and personal values. Consistent with prior meta‑syntheses, clarity and compassion in provider communication emerged as critical to informed decision‑making [32, 35, 36]. Negotiation within couples, often under the watchful eye of extended family, reflects collectivist dynamics unique to East Asian contexts, augmenting but also complicating individual autonomy [37]. When mapped to TPB, attitudes toward continuation were shaped by ethical considerations of child suffering and maternal health risk; subjective norms were driven by filial expectations; and perceived behavioral control hinged on resource availability, which advances the prior work by offering an explanatory framework for how these factors coalesce into actionable intentions [37].

Traditional Chinese values of filial piety and “face” exerted powerful normative pressure, amplifying stigma associated with disability [38, 39]. Unlike Western studies where disability rights movements may temper negative perceptions [40], our participants feared societal ostracism and family dishonor if they continued an “imperfect” pregnancy [33]. The legacy of the one‑child policy continues to reverberate, as couples perceive heightened obligation to produce a healthy heir [33, 41, 42]. These contextual factors underscore that culturally tailored counseling must explicitly address ancestral and policy‑driven expectations.

Our results highlight the dual nature of support. Family assistance offered practical relief but sometimes amplified pressure through well‑intended advice [33]. Healthcare professionals who combined medical expertise with emotional empathy served as critical buffers against isolation [34, 43, 44]. Consistent with social exchange theory, participants sought both functional (information, instrumental aid) and emotional support; deficits in either domain exacerbated anxiety [34, 45]. The urgent desire for clear, comprehensive information aligns with global evidence that decisional conflict diminishes when patients receive tailored educational resources [34, 46, 47]. These findings call for integrating structured peer support and professional counseling into prenatal care pathways.

The study revealed that facing fetal anomaly could both strengthen and strain partnerships. Shared adversity fostered emotional intimacy and revealed latent resilience, akin to phenomena of post‑traumatic growth described in other perinatal contexts [48, 49]. Yet persistent uncertainty and divergent coping styles precipitated conflict in some dyads, echoing findings that parental communication breakdown can worsen psychological outcomes [50, 51]. Interventions should therefore incorporate couple‑focused therapy to reinforce mutual support and facilitate joint decision‑making.

Clinical implications

This study highlights the importance of clinical care that recognizes the emotional disruption, cultural expectations, and relational pressures experienced by Chinese couples following a prenatal anomaly diagnosis. Healthcare professionals should be attentive to the profound shock and temporal disturbance that many parents described, and provide space for emotional processing before presenting complex information. Clear, paced communication using accessible language is essential, particularly when discussing probabilistic outcomes and residual uncertainty. Clinicians should be aware that decision-making often involves extended family members and may be influenced by filial piety, lineage expectations, and concerns about preserving family reputation. Supportive care should therefore include guidance on navigating intergenerational dynamics and balancing family input with the couple’s emotional capacity. Sensitivity to stigma surrounding disability is critical, and clinicians should avoid language that reinforces social judgement or assumptions about burden. Counseling should help parents articulate their own values and concerns, while acknowledging that cultural norms may shape how they express distress or seek support. Engagement with both partners during consultations can strengthen shared understanding and reduce relational strain. Overall, clinical practice should integrate emotional containment, cultural awareness, and structured communication strategies to support couples as they navigate complex decisions in a context shaped by strong social and familial expectations.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several strengths. The use of a phenomenological design allowed a detailed and sensitive exploration of how Chinese couples experience a prenatal anomaly diagnosis. The approach supported close attention to emotional disruption, temporal disturbance, and relational meaning-making that would be difficult to capture through quantitative methods alone. The dual theoretical orientation helped integrate experiential accounts with the social and cognitive influences that shape decision-making. The study setting, a major prenatal diagnostic center, made it possible to recruit participants soon after receiving the diagnosis, which strengthened recall accuracy and provided insight into immediate responses. The inclusion of couples provided a more complete understanding of how shared decision-making unfolds within family systems. Rigorous analytic procedures, team-based coding, and reflexive discussion supported depth and credibility in interpretation.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Participants were recruited from a single tertiary hospital, which may limit transferability to families who seek care in rural settings or lower level facilities. Most participants had access to specialized prenatal services and may have had more resources and support than parents in under-resourced regions. The study included couples who agreed to participate in a sensitive and emotionally demanding interview, which may introduce selection bias. Interviews were conducted once, rather than longitudinally, so the findings capture experiences within a relatively narrow timeframe. The study relied on self-reported accounts that may be influenced by distress, cultural norms regarding emotional expression, or reluctance to disclose conflict with extended family. Although the research team engaged in reflexive practice, researcher positionality may still have shaped interpretation.

Conclusions

Chinese couples facing possible congenital fetal anomalies navigate a profoundly turbulent journey marked by intense shock, prolonged anxiety, and diverse coping strategies. Their decisions hinge on a dynamic interplay of clear, compassionate medical guidance; cultural imperatives around lineage, “face,” and stigma; and the practical realities of resource availability. By combining Moustakas’ TP with Ajzen’s TPB, this study both illuminated the rich textures of lived experience and explained how attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control coalesce into concrete pregnancy decisions. These insights underscore the necessity of culturally tailored, longitudinal psychosocial support; empathetic, plain‑language counseling; and decision‑aids that address values, familial pressures, and resource planning.

Ultimately, our findings offer a robust, theory‑informed foundation for designing interventions and policies that honor the emotional complexity and cultural context of Chinese couples as they confront one of the most challenging decisions of their lives.
