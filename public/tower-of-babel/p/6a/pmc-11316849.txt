Comparison of production performance and meat quality characteristics between Guizhou Black goats and F4 generation hybrids of South African Kalahari Goats
Authors: Min Yao, Lingle Chang, Suyun Xu, Jing Zhang, Ping Li, Bing Tian, Li Luo, Danpin Yang, Qingmeng Long, Xiao Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study spanned 6 years and 4 generations, involving the progressive crossbreeding of South African Kalahari Goat (SK) and Guizhou Black Goat (GB) over three generations, followed by cross fixation F3 with F1 in the fourth generation, accompanied by the use of molecular markers technology to select a high fertility population, resulting in the creation of a hybrid goat, BKF4 (11/16 SK lineage and 5/16 GB lineage). A comparative evaluation of the BKF4 hybrid breed and its parental breeds was conducted. Reproductive and production parameters of GB, SK, and BKF4 goat groups were monitored, including lambing rate (LR), survival rate (SR), daily weight gain at 3 months of age (DWG), and adult body weight (ABW) (n = 110, 106, 112 per group). In addition, dressing percentage (DP) (n = 12 per group) and analyses of amino acids (n = 8, 6, 10 per group) and fatty acids (n = 6 per group) were conducted to evaluate meat quality indicators. Results: (1) Reproductive and production performance: The index of LR reached 199%, significantly higher than GB and SK (p ≤ 0.001), with a SR of 95.0%, markedly higher than SK (p ≤ 0.001); DWG was 276.5 g, ABW reached 56.6 kg and with a dressing percentage (DP) of 54.5%, they are significantly surpassing GB (p ≤ 0.001). (2) Regarding meat quality: pH45-value and crude protein content (CP) increased, while intramuscular fat content increased compared to GB and ash content decreased. The amino acid composition was similar to GB, but the taste was more similar to SK. However, there were some negative impacts on fatty acid composition and functionality. (3) PCA analysis revealed that: BKF4 exhibited superior meat quality compared to GB and SK, influenced by two key factors contributing 83.49% and 16.51% to the explained variance, respectively. The key factors affecting meat quality include intramuscular fat (IMF), nutrient index (NI), PUFAs/MUFAs, n-6FAs, and drip loss (DL).Keywords: Guizhou black goat, South African Kalahari Goat (SK), Hybridization, Performance, Meat qualitySubject terms: Reproductive signs and symptoms, Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Chemical genetics

Introduction

Goats are universally acknowledged as an important agricultural resource, furnishing meat and derivative products essential for human consumption1,2. Their meat is particularly valued for unique nutritional qualities, composition, and sensory properties3–7. Since the late twentieth century, globalization has facilitated the widespread crossbreeding of local goat breeds with genetically superior international breeds, aimed at enhancing production performance and increasing their economic, ecological, and social value2,8–12. This crossbreeding strategy not only introduced desirable genetic traits but also preserved the adaptability and phenotypic characteristics of local breeds, thereby significantly improving the nutritional value and meat quality of goats8,13. In the realm of molecular breeding, genes such as Bone Morphogenetic Protein 15 (BMP15), Growth Differentiation Factor 9 (GDF9), Dynein Axonemal Heavy Chain 1 (DNAH1), and Retinol Binding Protein 4 (RBP4) have been identified as significantly associated with reproductive traits in goats, providing promising markers for selecting individuals with enhanced reproductive capabilities14–17.

This study employed molecular breeding and four-generation progressive crossbreeding between the native Guizhou Black goats (GB) of China and South African Kalahari goats (SK) to develop a high-quality hybrid breed, BKF418. While significantly enhancing the reproductive and production performance of the parent breeds, the meat quality of the hybrids was rigorously evaluated. These efforts are crucial for the sustainable development of the goat meat industry and provide valuable insights for the strategic breeding of local goat crossbreeding12.

Materials and methods

Animal management and experimental design

This section outlines the methodology used to manage and select animal subjects for the study. Step 1: Base Selection. Initial populations of high-reproductive-performance Guizhou Black Goats and South African Kalahari Goats were established using molecular techniques targeting the BMP15, GDF9, and RBP4 genes14. Step 2: Four generations of Crossbreeding Selection involved the breeding of Guizhou Black Goats (GB), South African Kalahari Goats (SK), and their crossbreeds, including BKF1 (1/2 SK ♀ × 1/2 GB ♂, ma (♂) ≧ 45 kg, ma (♀) ≧ 40 kg), BKF2 (3/4 SK ♀ × 1/4 GB ♂, ma (♂) ≧ 45 kg , ma (♀) ≧ 40 kg), BKF3 (7/8 SK ♂ × 1/8 GB ♀, ma (♂) ≧ 50 kg, ma (♀) ≧ 45 kg), and BKF4 (Crossover fixation BKF3 x BKF1: 11/16 SK × 5/16 GB, ma (♂) ≧ 55 kg and ma (♀) ≧ 50 kg )8,19. Breeding criteria comprise high lambing rate, superior body size and quality, high meat yield, strong adaptability, and selecting individuals with stable hereditary traits verified through pedigree and lineage records. Breeding took place at the Guizhou Kalahari Goat Breeding Company in Qinglong County, Guizhou Province, China, and lasted for six years. Males and females were grazed separately, with natural pasture as the primary feed supplemented with meat goats feed purchased from the Yingmei Group. The developed BKF4 goats inherit characteristics from the specialized breeding line of Guizhou Black Goats and South African Kalahari Goats, exhibiting comparable environmental adaptability to the local Guizhou Black Goats. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from Guizhou University (EAE-GIU-2021-P009) and animal experimental ethics regulations were followed according to ARRIVE guidelines. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Performance metrics such as lambing rate (LR), Survival rate (SR), daily weight gain at 3 months of age (DWG), and adult body weight (ABW) were meticulously recorded from 328 parallel samples, with group sample sizes of 110, 106, and 112 for GB, SK, and BKF4, respectively12,20. All gexperimental samples were randomly chosen from healthy animals.

Slaughter and assessment of meat quality

Standard commercial procedures were adhered to for goat slaughter to ensure consistency in data collection. Dressing percentage (DP) was assessed following the protocol established by Reynolds et al.21, and based on the average of 12 replicates. Muscle samples (approximately 300 g each) were precisely taken from the longissimus dorsi muscle (LD) between the 12th and 13th thoracic vertebrae within 30 min post-mortem for analysis of meat quality and nutrient content22. The pH45-value was measured according to Martins J.M et al. by using a Testo-205 pH meter (TESTO, Testo Group, Germany)12,22. Drip loss, expressed as the percentage weight decrease, was determined by comparing the weights of samples before and after storage at 4 °C for 48 h23. Moisture was obtained after drying samples in an oven at 105 °C for 24 h, while Crude protein content (CP) was determined by Kjeldahl-nitrogen × 6.25. Ash content was determined after incineration at 550 °C for 6 h. Intramuscular fat (IMF) was determined gravimetrically after Soxhlet lipid extraction using a SoxtecTM 2055 automated system (FOSS, Denmark). Amino acid analysis was conducted using the S-433D Amino Acid Analyzer (SYKAM, Germany), while fatty acid analysis utilized the Agilent Technologies 7890A Gas Chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, USA). The methods described by Ali Mahabbat et al. and Śmietana Natalia et al. were followed24,25. Each type of analysis was conducted with a minimum of three replicates per sample.

Amino acid analysis

In this study, the sums of Essential Amino Acids (EAAs), Conditionally Essential Amino Acids (CEAAs), Non-Essential Amino Acids (NEAAs), Amino Acids with Pharmacological relevance (PAAs, tagged with “#”), Branched-Chain Amino Acids (BCAAs, the collective levels of Ile, Leu, and Val), and Total Amino Acids (TAAs) were calculated. Additionally, the ratios of PAAs/TAAs, EAAs/TAAs, and EAAs/NC (NC = NEAAs + CEAAs) were also determined. Using the FAO/WHO amino acid scoring model, the Ratio of Amino Acids (RAA), Ratio Coefficient (RC), Relative Standard Deviation (RSD), Score of Ratio Coefficient (SRC), Essential Amino Acid Index (EAAI)26, Biological Value (BV), Nutritional Index (NI) and Protein Efficiency Ratio (PER) were calculated for all samples. The calculation formulas are as follows: RAA = amount of a specific essential amino acid/recommended amount; RC = RAA of a specific essential amino acid/mean RAA of all essential amino acids; SRC = 100—RSD × 100, where RSD represents the relative standard deviation of RC; EAAI=RAA1×RAA2×⋯RAAnn; BV = [(1.09 × EAAI − 11.7]27; NI = (EAAI × Protein (%)/100), n represents the number of essential amino acids, n ≦ 826,28; PER = − 0.468 + 0.454( Leu) − 0.105 (Tyr)29. The taste attributes of amino acids, including ‘umami (U)’, ‘bitter (B)’, ‘sweet (S)’, and ‘aromatic flavor (A)’, were evaluated according to established literature, with the respective threshold values (TV) listed in Table 125,30.

Fatty acid analysis

After Fatty Acids (FAs) identification, the sums of Saturated Fatty Acids (SFAs), Monounsaturated Fatty Acids (MUFAs), Polyunsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs), Essential Fatty Acids (EFAs, labeled with “#”), Unsaturated Fatty Acids (UFAs), Total Fatty Acids (TFAs) and the PUFAs/SFAs, PUFAs/MUFAs, UFAs/TFAs, n−6/n−3 ratios were calculated. Several indices were utilized in the quantification of fatty acids, including the Unsaturation index (UI), Atherogenic index (AI), Thrombogenic index (TI), Hypocholesterolemic Fatty Acids (DFA), Hypercholesterolemic Fatty Acids (OFA), and the hypocholesterolemic/hypercholesterolemic ratio (h/H). Calculation formulas for these indices are as follows: UI = 1 × ∑MUFA (%) + 2 × ∑DUFA (%) + 3 × ∑TUFA (%) + … n × ∑NUFA (%); AI = (C12:0 + (4 × C14:0) + C16:0)/(∑MUFA + ∑PUFAn−3 + ∑PUFAn−6)31; DFA = UFA + C18:0; OFA = C12:0 + C14:0 + C16:0; TI = (C14:0 + C16:0 + C18:0)/((0.5 × ∑MUFA) + (0.5 × ∑PUFAn−6) + (3 × ∑PUFAn−3) + ( ∑PUFAn−3/∑PUFAn−6))25,32;(h/H) = [∑(C18:1n−9, C18:1n−7, C18:2n−6, C18:3n−3, C20:3n−6, C20:4n−6)]/[∑(C14:0 + C16:0)]; n−6/n−3 = ∑PUFAn−3/∑PUFAn−620,33,34. The AI and TI indices indicate the impact of fatty acids on cardiovascular risk, while the h/H ratio reflects the functional influence of fatty acids on cholesterol metabolism25.

Statistical analysis

The collected data underwent statistical analysis using Excel for initial organization and SPSS version 21.0 for advanced computations. One-way ANOVA was used to assess differences among data sets, followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons. Results were presented as mean ± standard error (Mean ± SE) with significance levels set at *p ≤ 0.05 or **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001. Significant differences were observed among values marked with different superscripts within the same row. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to evaluate meat quality indicators in all samples, generating a composite score to depict their impact and evaluation.

Ethical statement

Ethical review and approval were waived for this study, due to the compliance with national and/or European Regulations on animal husbandry and slaughter, as reported in the Materials and Methods section.

Results and analysis

Performance and carcass characteristics

Table 2 and Fig. 1A display the performance and carcass characteristics of the BKF4 hybrid, which resulted from molecular selection for high reproductive traits and hybrid breeding practices. BKF4 exhibited notable improvements in lambing rate, daily weight gain at 3 months, adult body weight and dressing percentage compared to the GB and SK groups (p ≤ 0.001). Notably, BKF4 demonstrated a lambing rate increase of over 45.5% and more than doubled the daily weight gain at 3 months compared to the purebred Guizhou Black Goat (GB). Moreover, BKF4 maintained the exceptional survival rate exceeding 95%, indicative of robust disease resistance. The hybrid also achieved a more than 15.5% increase in adult weight gain.

Amino acid composition

Table 4 outlines the analysis of 16 amino acids in the samples, including 7 essential amino acids (EAAs), 4 conditionally essential amino acids (CEAAs), 5 non-essential amino acids (NEAAs), and 9 pharmacologically relevant amino acids (PAAs). Levels of CP, EAAs, CEAAs, PAAs, PAAs/TAAs, EAAs/NC, and EAAI, as well as SRC, NI, and BV, were significantly higher in GB and BKF4 compared to SK (p ≤ 0.001), with BCAAs and TAAs also showing a significant increase (p ≤ 0.05). Additionally, lysine, methionine, threonine, valine, phenylalanine, glycine, and proline levels in GB and BKF4 were significantly higher than those in SK (p ≤ 0.01), while arginine and alanine were also significantly elevated (p ≤ 0.05). Conversely, histidine was significantly lower in GB and BKF4 compared to SK (p ≤ 0.001), and serine was significantly lower (p ≤ 0.05). The amino acid profile of BKF4 closely resembled that of Guizhou Black Goat (GB). There were no significant differences in PER values among the three groups. In terms of taste intensity, GB and BKF4 exhibited significantly higher umami, (U + S + A)/B values and aromatic flavor compared to SK (p ≤ 0.01), higher sweetness (p ≤ 0.05), and lower bitterness (p ≤ 0.05). The B:U:S:A profile indicated that BKF4 was more similar to GB.

Referencing Table 5, the analysis revealed that the essential amino acid (EAA) profile of BKF4 closely mirrors that of its purebred progenitors, aligning well with the FAO/WHO amino acid (AA) standards and demonstrating high nutritional value. The EAAI’ values recorded for the samples stood at 93.12, 72.22, and 87.51 for GB, SK, and BKF4, respectively, reflecting a comparatively superior amino acid profile in BKF4 to SK.

Fatty acid composition

In beef and lamb, the intramuscular fat in muscle contains 45–48 g/100 g of saturated fatty acids (SFAs), 35–45 g/100 g of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs), and up to 50 g/100 g of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs)36. Table 6 presents the fatty acid profile identified in the intramuscular fat of the studied goat groups, revealing 18 different types of fatty acids. There are 6 types each of SFAs, MUFAs, and PUFAs, showing significant inter-group differences (p ≤ 0.01). Specifically, SFAs increased from 38.9 to 49.3%, MUFAs decreased from 48.2 to 42.9%, and PUFAs decreased from 12.8 to 7.9%, indicating an adverse shift in relative content. Furthermore, IMF, BCFAs, EFAs, UFAs, TFAs, n − 6, UI, DFA, OFA, and h/H all demonstrate the highest content in SK, followed by GB, and the lowest in BKF4 with significant differences (p ≤ 0.01). Both AI and TI values are markers for evaluating the risk of cardiovascular diseases. Higher values indicate a higher risk of cardiovascular diseases for individuals, yielding disappointingly negative outcomes. Despite this, n − 3 content in BKF4 significantly surpasses that in GB, particularly with a remarkable increase in three key bioactive fatty acids: ALA, EPA, and DHA, leading to a substantial reduction in the n − 6/n − 3 ratio from 28.9 in GB and 18.20 in SK to 6.56 in BKF4. In terms of individual fatty acids, most fatty acids exhibit a similar trend, except for eicosenoic acid.

Discussion

Reproductive and production performance

Cross breeding is an important way to improve livestock performance9,19. Reproductive and production traits are economically significant in animal production. Enhancing these traits signifies enhanced productivity and profitability and is typically regarded as the top priority in breeding programs38,39. Through three generations of progressive hybridization and molecular breeding, the lambing rate of BKF4 goats achieved 199% via the technology of cross-fixation. This rate notably surpasses that of the parent breeds. Additionally, BKF4 goats maintained a high survival rate of 95.1%, demonstrating commendable performance in environmental adaptability and animal well-being. In terms of growth performance, BKF4 goats benefit from the genetic characteristics of South African Kalahari goats, with an average daily weight gain of 276.5 ± 25.4 g at three months of age, twice the growth rate of Guizhou black goats. Slaughter performance stands as a pivotal metric for assessing livestock production capabilities. In this aspect, the adult body weight of BKF4 goats increased from 49 kg to 56.6 kg, and the dressing percentage escalated from 40.4 to 54.5%. This resulted in a profit surge of over 20%, notably boosting effectiveness.

Meat quality indicators

pH45-value and drip loss (DL) are crucial parameters in assessing meat quality, influenced by factors including breed selection, nutrition levels, and feeding practices40. Post-slaughter, heightened glycolytic activity in muscles accelerates ATP hydrolysis and muscle glycogen breakdown to lactate, causing a decline in muscle pH and subsequent meat degradation41,42. The rate of pH decrease is directly linked to the pace of biochemical changes in meat, impacting important quality traits like water loss, color, tenderness, and flavor43. A higher pH leads to slower anaerobic glycolysis of muscle glycogen, less water exudation loss, enhanced protein stability, and improved meat freshness44. Additionally, pH45-value is closely related to drip loss, as it can lower pH close to the protein's isoelectric point, reducing negative charges on fibers and lessening electrostatic repulsion. Lower pH levels also create larger gaps between myosin and actin filaments and decrease muscle fiber space45. Drip loss is also influenced by intramuscular fat (IMF) content and distribution, with higher IMF values associated with decreased drip loss2. This parameter serves as a key indicator of muscle water-holding capacity and significantly impacts the loss of marketable meat and its quality, affecting nutritional value, color, texture, flavor, and juiciness12. Within the three groups studied, the pH45-values were 5.47 for GB, 6.18 for SK, and 6.46 for BKF4, whereas DL values were recorded at 4.47% for GB, 3.6% for SK, and only 1.44% for BKF4. Furthermore, the ash contents of SK and BKF4 are statistically similar at 4.29% and 3.65%, respectively, with corresponding moisture levels of 74.65% in GB and 73.4% in BKF4. The decreased drip loss in BKF4 compared to the parental strain is in line with previous research findings46,47, suggesting potentially enhanced juiciness in BKF4. The ash content, negatively correlated with the values of pH45, DL and IMF, primarily consisting of minerals such as inorganic salts and metallic elements, serves as an indicator of the mineral content in food. Minerals are crucial for tissue formation and maintaining normal physiological functions, and they must be obtained from dietary sources as they cannot be synthesized by the human body. Thus, the content of mineral elements in food is of significant importance48. Compared to GB, an increase in ABW is associated with decreased ash content and elevated protein content in BKF4, consistent with the findings of A. Zullo et al.49, where the increase in protein content may also be attributed to a reduction in moisture content50.

IMF plays a crucial role in palatability and consumer preferences of meat, showing a positive correlation with tenderness, flavor, juiciness, and overall acceptability37,51–53, and it is linked to variables such as age, gender, and diet54. Meat fat and moisture content typically demonstrate a negative correlation55. Consumer preference for goat meat significantly increases at an IMF level of 3%, peaks at 4%, and continues to rise with higher IMF content37. Optimal flavor richness is achieved when the IMF ranges between 3.4 and 4.5%48. Sensory scores for juiciness, overall liking, flavor, and tenderness of loins increased across the IMF range of 2.5% to 7.0% by 10.7, 10.0, 9.1 and 5.9 units on a 0–100 scale56. The IMF of the BKF4 group is 4.4%, which falls within consumers' preferred range. Previous research has shown that the expression levels of the H-FABP and ADSL genes can influence the content of IMF and IMP, indirectly contributing to the formation of muscle tenderness, juiciness, flavor, and freshness40,57.

Protein and amino acids

In the context of dietary needs regarding nitrogen balance and growth, amino acids (AAs) are nutritionally defined as essential (indispensable), conditionally essential or nonessential (dispensable) for human protein synthesis and specific physiological structures and functions26,58,59. High-quality proteins are characterized by a complete range of amino acids and a balanced proportion of essential amino acids (EAAs). BKF4 demonstrates higher levels of CP, EAAs, CEAAs, PAAs, BCAAs, TAAs, EAAI, SRC, NI, BV compared to SK, showing overall superiority in amino acid content and quality, with no significant difference between BKF4 and GB. BCAAs, including leucine, isoleucine, and valine, which cannot be synthesized by the human body, are often consumed by athletes or supplemented to healthy neonates, enhancing the activity of glutathione transferase (GST) and catalase58. Lysine, as a therapeutic amino acid, plays a crucial role in promoting growth, enhancing mineral absorption, and boosting immune function60. Arginine can promote the secretion of endogenous substances such as white blood cells and macrophages by the immune system, enhancing immunity48. Following the FAO/WHO recommended model, BKF4's essential amino acid content has increased from 38.4% and 44.17% respectively to 51.53%. BKF4's EAAI value is 87.51, slightly lower than the GB group's 93.12 but significantly higher than the SK group's 72.22, indicating an overall enhancement in the meat quality of the hybrid goats. With the highest NI and BV values among the three groups, this suggests an improved amino acid profile and value of the meat from the hybrid goats. Furthermore, CP values exhibit positive correlations with EAAI and BV, as well as with bitterness, sweetness, umami, and aromatic flavor. Regarding flavor, amino acids, inosine, and peptides are crucial flavor precursors in meat, and their breakdown enhances the sensory attributes and taste of meat46. The balance of bitterness (B), umami (U), sweetness (S), and aromatic flavors (A) (B:U:S:A ratio) in the three groups of goats suggests that the flavor profile of BKF4 is more similar to that of SK. Additionally, the increased levels of glutamate, lysine, serine, and alanine in the muscle significantly enhance the flavor (or aroma) of goat meat61.

Intramuscular fat and fatty acids

Intramuscular fat is predominantly composed of fatty acids, which are influenced by various factors such as species, cultivar, climate, and management, posited to render goat meat relatively healthier compared to other red meats5,51. In the present study, the intramuscular fat (IMF) content of BKF4 (ranging from 3.17 to 5.63 g/100 g) falls between those of GB (2.46 to 3.64 g/100 g) and SK (3.24 to 8.92 g/100 g). It is positively correlated with n − 3, UI, h/H, DFA, TI, and AI, while displaying a negative correlation with n − 6. Overall, the levels of SFAs, MUFAs, PUFAs, BCFAs, and TFAs decreased, altering the ratios of SFAs:MUFAs:PUFAs:EFAs (GB vs SK vs BKF4, 1:1.01:0.24:0.23 vs 1:1.24:0.33:0.33 vs 1:0.87:0.16:0.16). This demonstrates a reduction in the proportions of MUFAs, PUFAs, and EFAs, leading to a lowered PUFA/SFA ratio (GB: 19.78% to 28.42%; SK: 31.45% to 34.83%; BKF4: 14.62% to 17.28%), significantly deviating from the recommended ratio of ≥ 0.4562–65. Specific fatty acid contents varied, with some at intermediary levels and others significantly lower than those of the parental breeds. These discrepancies resulted in lower UI, DFA, h/H values, and higher AI, TI, leading to deteriorated meat quality, consistent with previous research findings38,61. Encouragingly, n − 3 FAs increased in the hybrid offspring, with detectable levels of EPA and DHA that were absent in GB. Meanwhile, n − 6 FAs such as C18:2n6 and C20:4n6 decreased, reducing the n − 6/ n − 3 ratio from 26.41 to 31.39 in GB and 17.26 to 19.14 in SK, to 5.34 to 7.78 in BKF4. The recommended n − 6/ n − 3 ratio is usually ≤ 463,64,66. Studies have shown that a change in the n − 6/ n − 3 ratio from 1:1 to 15–20:1 may lead to metabolic disorders, causing symptoms such as insulin resistance, hyperinsulinemia, obesity, while n − 3 FAs and monounsaturated fatty acids have a protective effect against heart disease67. C18:3n3 plays a key role in reducing blood lipids and blood sugar, antioxidation, antibacterial and antiinflammatory, and the development of brain and retinal43,68. Additionally, extensive research indicates that saturated fatty acids (SFA) and trans fatty acids heighten cardiovascular risk69, with excessive SFA intake potentially raising levels of both high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), thereby increasing the risk of atherosclerosis and coronary heart disease70. Elevated levels of PUFAs/SFAs and oleic acid (comprising approximately 95% of MUFA) are beneficial in reducing serum cholesterol, lowering the risk of atherosclerosis, and preventing heart diseases71,72. Replacing 1% of dietary SFAs with polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) is believed to reduce low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol by 2% or 3%73. Furthermore, unsaturated fatty acids (USFAs) are known for their beneficial impact on the flavor, texture, and nutritional value of goat meat, with a significant correlation between the n − 6/ n − 3 ratio and meat flavor. Additionally, the oxidation of stearic acid (C18:0) and other saturated fatty acids can generate volatile compounds, influencing consumer acceptability6,74.

Research indicates that the FA composition of ruminant edible fats is mostly determined by complex interactions between dietary factors and rumen metabolism75. Meat produced from forage-fed animals is considered superior to grain-fed counterparts, being rich in medium-chain fatty acids and antioxidants, with certain plant species potentially enhancing this further36,51.Furthermore, supplementation of fats or specific mixed feeds can enhance meat nutritional quality, increasing levels of oleic acid, linoleic acid, linolenic acid, and long-chain PUFAs (such as DHA and EPA), elevating n − 3 FA levels and anticancer properties64,76. Hence, adjusting dietary practices, feeding schedules, and other critical factors such as stress conditions can modulate and alter the fatty acid composition, thereby enhancing the quality of hybrid goat meat77. Integrating edible quality traits into goat genetic improvement programs and employing novel methods such as molecular breeding and non-destructive detection techniques (such as near-infrared spectroscopy, ultrasound, CT imaging) hold promise for screening goats with specific fatty acid phenotypes23,78,79.

Conclusions

The hybrid goat BKF4, bred through four generations of crossbreeding and six years of advanced molecular techniques, demonstrates high adaptability, strong reproduction and production performance, and notable economic benefits. In terms of meat quality, BKF4 outperforms the South African Kalahari Goat but slightly trails the Guizhou Black goat. It shows high protein content, increased intramuscular fat, enhanced water retention, and reduced ash content. Despite the negative effects on fatty acid composition and functionality, BKF4's amino acid profile resembles that of the Guizhou Black goat, and its taste is akin to the South African Kalahari Goat (SK). This prompts the need for improvements through molecular selection, non-destructive testing, and dietary adjustments in future phases.
