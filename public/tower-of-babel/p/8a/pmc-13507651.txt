“It's confusing as hell”: a qualitative study of barriers accessing poverty alleviation benefits through the US Earned Income Tax Credit
Authors: Wendi Gosliner, Heena Shah, Kaitlyn Jackson, Lia C H Fernald, Rita Hamad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is the largest US poverty alleviation program, with positive effects on health equity. Over 20% of eligible households do not receive benefits.MethodsIn this qualitative study, we explored benefits of and barriers to EITC receipt among EITC-eligible families through analysis of 40 semi-structured interviews with EITC-eligible parents in California using the immersion-crystallization method.ResultsWe identified 3 categories of findings: (1) perceived benefits of EITC; (2) administrative barriers leading to low EITC take-up among eligible families, eg, learning, compliance, and psychological barriers; and (3) suggestions to improve EITC take-up. Participants reported the EITC helped families meet needs, make larger purchases, and alleviate perceived stress. Learning barriers were most salient, especially lack of information or understanding. Participants described compliance barriers (eg, tax-filing rules) and psychological barriers (eg, complexity, cognitive load, stress). Participants suggested that the EITC could be improved with more and clearer information, a streamlined system to automatically provide benefits to those eligible, and increased benefit levels.ConclusionThe EITC supports social determinants of health among recipients, yet is difficult to obtain for too many. Improving program knowledge and streamlining access for eligible households may improve take-up and health equity.Keywords: Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), poverty alleviation, qualitative analysis, administrative burden, health disparities

Introduction

Poverty is a pervasive and persistent determinant of health inequities, with recognized impacts on mental and physical well-being across the life course. While the poverty rate in the United States is 10.5% overall, it is 18% among families with children and higher among Black (29%) and Hispanic families (25%).1 Low socioeconomic status and financial insecurity have been associated with increased risk of depression, anxiety, and other mental health conditions.2 Poverty can also increase high-risk health behaviors and chronic stress, and prevent people from affording health-promoting resources such as healthy food, high-quality housing, and health care.3-5

The largest US poverty alleviation program for families with children is the federal Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), which disburses approximately $60 billion annually to approximately 25 million families.6 Distributed as a tax refund, the average benefit is over $2500 per family and the maximum exceeds $6000. The EITC raises millions of families out of poverty by providing cash and incentivizing employment (because it is contingent on working).7 EITC receipt is associated with improved birth outcomes, child development, and mental health and reduced smoking; effects often are larger for marginalized subgroups such as Black women and those with lower educational attainment.8-15 Potential mechanisms linking the EITC with physical and mental health include increased resources for food and housing and reduced debt.16,17

Despite the recognized health benefits of the EITC, in 2019 (the year of our study), 80% of eligible families nationally actually received the EITC refunds for which they were qualified, and California's take-up rate was lower than the national average at 79.1%.7,18 Take-up of some US safety net programs were slightly higher than the EITC; for example, in 2019, take-up of the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children was 84% among infants but only 46% overall,19 whereas for the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program, take-up was 82% in 2019 and 88% in 2022.20 Take-up was nearly 88% for the expanded Child Tax Credit in 2021,21 a program also administered by the tax-filing system. In addition to the federal EITC, over half of states provide their own supplemental EITC benefits. For example, despite the relatively large California EITC (CalEITC) benefit, take-up was estimated at only 53% in 2017,22 leaving $2 billion in unclaimed federal and state benefits in California alone.23

Research is increasingly focusing on reasons for incomplete take-up of this critical poverty alleviation benefit. Prior work has primarily utilized administrative data (eg, tax records) to propose possible explanations: lack of awareness; confusion around eligibility criteria, especially for families with nontraditional structures (eg, divorced parents); and administrative burdens of tax filing itself, particularly for individuals with low incomes who are not required to file taxes.22,24-26 Administrative burdens refer to challenges imposed on people that make it difficult to access or maintain a benefit for which they qualify, either intentionally or due to poorly designed systems. Administrative burdens can also harm health through increasing frustration,27 and by limiting access to well-established health-protective programs such as food assistance, income supports, and Medicaid.28

Theoretical work on administrative burdens impeding safety net program take-up more broadly categorizes barriers to take-up into 3 groups—learning (ie, lack of understanding, awareness, and information), compliance (ie, work related to and rules of the application process, travel, paperwork, and/or documentation), and psychological costs (ie, complexity, cognitive load, stress related to the application process)29,30—although there is limited work examining the EITC in particular. In a recent quantitative analysis of EITC barriers among California families with low income, nearly 1 in 10 did not receive the EITC because they did not file taxes, perhaps because they had income below the filing threshold.31 Among tax filers, lower likelihood of EITC receipt was associated with being younger, speaking a primary language other than English, and lower EITC awareness. Another qualitative analysis found that respondents could not differentiate the portion of their tax refund that had come from the EITC, and that the complexity and fluidity of finances and family life in low-income households was a source of uncertainty during tax season. These factors suggest that, like other programs, administrative burdens including learning, compliance, and psychological costs may also explain incomplete EITC take-up. To date, limited research has investigated the lived experiences of EITC-eligible individuals to better understand their perceived barriers to take-up.

The present study seeks to fill this gap, using qualitative interviews with EITC-eligible individuals to provide detailed accounts of experiences with EITC as well as barriers to EITC receipt, and applying the theoretical framework on administrative burdens to understand more about the learning, compliance, and psychological costs preventing EITC take-up. Additionally, this study examines the ways in which families perceive the EITC's benefits and their ideas for how to improve the system to facilitate EITC receipt among eligible people. Given the US federal government's “Burden Reduction Initiative,” under the Biden administration, specifically focused on increasing take-up of safety net programs,32 this study provides critical evidence to inform ongoing policymaking to ensure that families facing economic disadvantages can access supports to achieve health equity.

Data and methods

Study sample

This qualitative study is a sub-study of the Assessing California Communities’ Experiences with Safety Net Supports (ACCESS) Study, a larger cross-sectional study that interviewed caregivers of young children in California during August 2020–May 2021. A brief review of study methods are included here, and further details are included in the Supplemental Methods.31 Sample recruitment for ACCESS was conducted in partnership with community-based organizations. We also utilized snowball sampling, asking study participants to invite others to participate. We sought to recruit an EITC-eligible sample, and therefore, inclusion criteria for ACCESS were as follows: income within eligibility limits for the EITC based on marital status and number of dependents and immigration status consistent with EITC eligibility rules.33 Respondents who participated in the ACCESS study from January 29 to March 18, 2021 (n = 137) were invited to answer open-ended EITC-focused questions for this sub-study (Table 1), from which n = 40 were selected for transcription and inclusion in the final analytical sample (Figure 1, Supplemental Methods). All 40 participants were determined to be EITC-eligible for the 2019 tax year.

Data analysis

The qualitative analysis was conducted following an immersion-crystallization approach.34 Three members of the research team (WG, HS, KJ) read through the full analytic sample of 40 transcripts (immersion), then reflected on emerging patterns or themes (crystallization) and repeated the process throughout the analysis. HS drafted a codebook after the initial read of transcripts, including deductive codes based on research questions and inductive codes that emerged from the data. WG and KJ reviewed the codes. All discussed and refined the codebook together before HS coded all transcripts. The team met regularly to discuss the coding approach, modify codes as needed, and address discrepancies in understanding or interpretation. We used Dedoose software version 8.4.47 to organize, code, and manage data.(35) WG and HS then conducted an iterative process of reviewing excerpts, sorting them into categories and subcategories using peer-debriefing to refine and validate interpretations.

Twenty-eight (70%) participants received the EITC in 2019 and 12 (30%) did not. Of the 12 nonrecipients, 5 filed taxes in 2019 and 7 did not. Nonrecipients had less education and lower income. Other characteristics were generally similar across groups.

Three primary categories of findings arose from qualitative interviews: participants’ perceptions of (1) the benefits of the EITC, (2) administrative burdens limiting take-up, and (3) suggestions for improvement. Most themes arose from EITC recipients, as few nonrecipients were familiar enough with the EITC to discuss it in depth.

The 3 main benefits discussed included that the EITC supported basic needs, the receipt of the EITC as an annual lump sum allowed a pathway for larger purchases or savings, and the program helped alleviate perceived stress related to finances. Three kinds of barriers to take-up were also discussed: learning barriers, including lack of knowledge and misperceptions about the EITC; compliance barriers, including those related to the work of filing taxes, claiming the EITC, and tax rules that do not require individuals below a given income threshold to file taxes; and psychological barriers related to the complexity and cognitive load of EITC filing. Participants suggested that addressing learning barriers, minimizing compliance barriers, and increasing the EITC refund could enhance take-up among EITC-eligible families. Benefits of the EITC

The EITC helped meet basic needs

Most participants reported that the EITC supported their basic needs, including rent, food, utility, and other bills; paying down debt; and purchasing necessities such as shampoo and toothpaste. One EITC recipient said, “It's helpful, any bit of extra income I can get is helpful” (P01, female, 37 years, White, EITC $3100). Several expressed that they used the EITC to purchase goods or services for their children or family. A recipient shared, “… it can help with bills that are piling up … it can help with procuring childcare or something that you need to go to work or school. [It] can help with buying clothes” (P02, female, 27 years, Black, EITC $3500). While most participants appreciated support from the EITC, some said it was not helpful or worth the effort of filing, particularly those whose refunds were small. One EITC recipient who received $400 said, “It did not really feel noticeable … it was $200 or something, it's very little. So it did not … really affect our family at all” (P03, female, 35 years, other race/ethnicity, EITC $400). Even among those who received larger EITC refunds, many reported they were so behind on paying for basic necessities that the money got swallowed up quickly to cover their needs. One recipient said, “… if it was just me getting some additional monies, then we just use it for bills” (P04, female, 42 years, Black, EITC $3500). Another participant who received less than $100 in EITC benefits said, “… we’re still struggling families … it hasn’t affected our day to day … there's so much that needs to be done with it, it's still just a drop in the bucket” (P05, female, 37 years, Black, EITC <$100).

Lump-sum refund enabled savings or larger purchases

A few people reported that the lump-sum EITC refund allowed them to buy larger, more expensive items that they otherwise would not be able to afford, like furniture. Some reported being able to use it to save money or that is was just easier to get an annual lump sum. As 1 participant who received $5800 in EITC benefits said, “… it's easier to manage … a one-time payment … around this time I will be expecting this money, and it … will help what's going on” (P06, male, 39, Black, EITC $5800).

EITC refund allowed families to worry less about money

A number of EITC recipients reported that receiving it helped them feel less worried—for example, “it makes me less worried about paying bills” (P01, female, 37 years, White, EITC $3100) or “I don’t have to worry so much about getting basic things for my daughter” (P07, female, 32 years, Latina, EITC $2900). This feeling was more common among participants who received larger refunds.

Administrative burdens create barriers to EITC take-up

Learning barriers

The overwhelming majority of participants described learning barriers as the most salient and prominent reasons for suboptimal EITC take-up (Table 3). The barriers emerged primarily as a lack of knowledge as well as misperceptions. Knowledge of EITC was limited, and this was a critical component of incomplete take-up, understanding, and appreciation of the EITC. Many had not heard of the EITC prior to the interview despite receiving large benefit amounts, reporting, “I learned about it today” (P08, female, 24 years, Latina, EITC $5700). When asked what they knew before participating in the interview, another respondent who received the EITC remarked, “I didn’t know anything” (P09, female, 34 years, Latina, EITC $1600). Learning barriers included lack of information about the existence of the program, eligibility requirements, and application procedures. For example, a nonrecipient commented, “… I just knew that a lot of us could qualify for it on our taxes, but I didn’t know what the qualifications were … or we had to apply or just with our tax information they would give it to us. I just didn’t know what it consisted of or how to get it” (P10, female, 23 years, Latina, tax filer, EITC nonrecipient). Another who received the EITC highlighted that not knowing about free tax preparation assistance was a barrier, saying, “… a lot of people don’t know that there is assistance, free assistance [for tax preparation]” (P11, female, 48 years, White, EITC $5500). This participant acknowledged that knowing about the program's existence was not sufficient for access, given the lack of awareness about resources available for accessing the program.

Some participants revealed misperceptions or provided inaccurate information about the program—for example, thinking it was only for people with children or confusing it with other tax credits. One said, “I thought earned income was the part where you file taxes, where you get earned income credit for your children …. The earned income credit that you earn for your children … taking care of them all 12 years” (P12, female, 29 years, other race/ethnicity, EITC $1800). Another interviewee who did not get the EITC despite filing taxes said, “I thought it was the tax child rebate credit … I thought that came because of the pandemic” (P10, female, 23 years, Latina, tax filer, EITC nonrecipient). Most participants who knew about the EITC reported learning about it from their tax preparer or filing taxes themselves. One non-filer said, “I got the total understanding from you today but hearing about it is when I went in to prepare … my taxes every year” (P13, female, 21 years, other race/ethnicity, didn’t file taxes). Others reported learning about it through word-of-mouth, government websites or other government programs, social media, flyers, or workplaces. Another non-filer said, “I learned from it through seeing other family members do their taxes … but I felt like it's the same, they know it, but don’t really know the details of it” (P14, female, 24, Latinx, didn’t file taxes). When asked why they thought eligible people would not apply, the majority said it was because people did not have enough information. One said, “They don’t know about it. Most people that I’ve talked to assume that if they didn’t make enough to file for taxes that they basically didn’t know about the income tax credit” (P11, female, 48, White, EITC $5500). Participants did not suggest that people are not exerting the necessary effort to learn about the EITC, but rather, that passive learning about the benefit is inherent in the program structure but active work to apply for it is inconsistent with this design.

Further, another learning cost emerged when discussing that those who do not make enough income to be required to file taxes remain unaware that they may still be eligible for the EITC. One EITC recipient said, “… a lot of people don’t realize that they would qualify for it, because they don’t technically have to file because they don’t meet that income threshold” (P11, female, 48 years, White, EITC $5500). Another non-filer said, “… it’s a little bit concerning, maybe disappointing, because there’s not a lot of information that people just generally offer in terms of that type of stuff .… Like I said, I only know if I’m actually applying, so it’d be nice if it was some way that I can be notified, that I could benefit from that before I would start an application process” (P16, male, 32 years, Black, didn’t file taxes). Thus, multiple types of learning barriers, including lack of information about the program's existence, eligibility requirements (including for those with low income), and application steps, prevented full EITC access among study participants.

Compliance barriers

Participants shared mixed sentiments about the requirement to file taxes to receive the EITC. Some reported that the link between receiving the EITC and participating in the tax system was a benefit since they were filing taxes anyway and getting money back felt like an incentive to file or a bonus for doing so. Comments included that tax filing “is a good thing,” and getting a refund means you get “money in return” for filing your taxes. Others reported the requirement to file taxes to receive the EITC was a drawback and a reason that eligible people missed out on benefits. Most themes arose from EITC recipients discussing why others did not receive benefits, as few nonrecipients knew enough about the EITC.

Two main compliance themes emerged. First, respondents noted that filing taxes takes time, resources, and energy, and getting the EITC requires additional time within the tax-filing process. Related to tax filing, 1 participant who received the EITC said, “It’s just that sometimes, for some reason, it takes just sitting down and … having the time to do paperwork and uninterrupted paperwork, that’s important. It’s hard to do, so that’s kind of the setback” (P07, female, 32 years, Latina, EITC $2900).

A second compliance theme related to the fact that the families with the lowest incomes and greatest need are not required to file taxes, and therefore, see little benefit in completing the paperwork and processes required. As a result, lower income households are more likely to miss out on the EITC due to not filing taxes, even if they are aware of programs like the EITC. For example, a few people mentioned that they thought this may have led many people erroneously think that filing taxes would be a burden without benefit. One EITC recipient said, “… people don’t file because they don’t earn that much during the year and they feel like they don’t have to, but then again, here’s this credit that you could have gotten regardless” (P15, female, 29 years, Latina, EITC $3500).

Even for tax filers, many felt the system allowed too many ways to make mistakes and miss the opportunity to get the EITC despite being eligible. One participant said that, after learning they had not received the EITC despite thinking they had, “[I feel] confused … I thought I got it and … I guess I didn’t” (P17, female, 22 years, Latina, tax filer, EITC nonrecipient). Another nonrecipient said, “I’m sad … I wish I would’ve got it, but now I know this year to ask about it” (P18, female, 33 years, Latina, tax filer, EITC nonrecipient). Similarly, some participants mentioned that working in the gig economy presented barriers because it was difficult to collect the needed paperwork from the employer. People may not have the time to navigate the required systems to retrieve such paperwork like W-2s or may not even have the appropriate household resources like a computer or printer. One EITC-recipient said,

I think there’s a lot of people that work in the gig economy and sometimes it’s difficult to get W-2, or print them out, or if you’re doing things on your own, human error or they’re not aware of the EITC … but sometimes they just might not have the time to read and understand everything or they’re in a rush … or they don’t have the help that to reach out to or time to reach out for help. (P07, female, 32 years, Latina, EITC $2900).

Psychological barriers

The complexity of tax filing with its many rules and opaque processes creates psychological barriers to people filing taxes and claiming the EITC specifically. Participants reported the challenge of understanding the tax rules and particularly the rules for EITC filing being a barrier to EITC receipt. One participant who received the EITC said, “… I do know people that don’t do their taxes, just because it's too overwhelming for them to do it” (P19, female, 35 years, Latina, EITC $2000). These psychological barriers can cause individuals not to receive governmental poverty alleviation supports that could improve their lives. One participant who received less than $100 in EITC benefits said,

Yeah, you can read these words, that doesn’t necessarily mean you understand it. Tax code language is very specific language, which is confusing as hell to the average lay person, right? It doesn’t actually make a lot of sense, it’s just as confusing as legal jargon, because it’s meant to be confusing …. It’s not necessarily contacts and subject matter that makes sense to the average person … and then there’s people who don’t file taxes for so many reasons. Fear, confusion, not seeing the benefit or feeling like it benefits them. (P05, female, 37 years, Black, EITC <$100).

The complexity of filing taxes and applying for EITC benefits, and the confusion surrounding it, was associated with a variety of fears regarding EITC take-up that were mentioned by a handful of participants. These included fear of having to continue filing every year once people were in the system, fear of owing money, fear of negative implications related to citizenship status, and fear of having to repay the money to the government someday. One EITC recipient said, “Maybe people think it’ll get deducted someday or … it might count against them if they get some kind of government help” (P04, female, 42 years, Black, EITC $3500). These examples highlight how complex rules and opaque processes led some people to avoid filing taxes and receiving the EITC because they were concerned it would harm them.

Suggestions for improving EITC take-up

Participants offered multiple suggestions for increasing EITC take-up. The 2 most common related to overcoming learning barriers by providing more information and increasing the EITC refund amount. To reduce learning barriers, participants suggested developing mechanisms to inform people about the EITC, including mass texts with links to eligibility information, commercials, flyers, educational videos, and billboards. Generally, respondents suggested making information more available to help families understand eligibility and potential benefits. One nonrecipient said, “Maybe inform more families about it and make sure they get it” (P17, female, 22 years, Latina, tax filer, EITC nonrecipient). Another who received the EITC said, “… people aren’t applying for it, and they’re qualified … like providing more information for families around tax season, so people understand that they can apply for it” (P01, female, 37 years, White, EITC $3100). Some participants suggested who should provide the information. One participant who did not file taxes and therefore did not receive the EITC suggested that other safety net programs could provide information about the EITC, saying,

If you’re receiving Calworks [California's Temporary Assistance for Needy Families], Supplemental Security Income and not associating that with filing taxes. So, if you do qualify technically, you may not know it, so they can provide that information. Letting folks know what you may qualify for, if you look into it. I think more people will do it. (P16, male, 32 years, Black, didn’t file taxes)

The other suggestion that participants raised frequently was increasing the amount of the EITC. One said, it “could be a bigger amount” (female, 22 years, White, EITC $200, CalEITC nonrecipient). Another who received the EITC suggested increasing the amount and frequency of payments:

…it should be enough to where it’s like they calculate how much it cost to raise a … child throughout the entire year, which cost tons…like say it takes $30,000 to, to raise a child and you’re only making $20,000 … $10,000 should be split up in small amounts throughout the year. (P02, female, 27 years, Black, EITC $3500)

In response to a question about frequency of EITC disbursements, many reported liking the annual lump sum. However, some suggested it would be more beneficial if benefits were spread throughout the year. Others did not express a preference. One who received the EITC said, “Well, I can see the benefit for both .… For me, I use it just to pay off or pay down a credit card, but I can see like if you were to receive it throughout the year, I may not have to put some of my expenses on credit cards and then therefore not have to pay a lump-sum at the end of the year” (P20, female, 60 years, Black, EITC $6500). Another EITC recipient said, “I think that would be better. It’s money in the pocket every month. It would also make it a lot easier knowing that we’re going to get some money, because there’s times where … it is a month-to-month basis, just because we are living paycheck to paycheck” (P21, female, 35 years, other race/ethnicity, EITC $400).

Discussion

This is among the first qualitative studies in recent years to examine perceptions and barriers to take-up of the EITC—the largest US poverty alleviation program with proven health benefits. In terms of perceived benefits, the EITC helped families with young children meet basic needs, enabled them to make larger purchases when they receive the lump-sum refund, and helped alleviate perceptions of financial stress. These findings are consistent with quantitative studies that have examined spending patterns among EITC recipients and found that debt reduction and big-ticket purchases are common uses of the EITC refund.16,17 One prior historical qualitative study of EITC recipients found similar spending on larger purchases, although that study was focused on evaluating preferences for lump-sum vs advanced EITC payments.36

Our study goes beyond examining the benefits of the EITC, also eliciting details of the many barriers to receiving this critical poverty alleviation support. While most EITC recipients expressed gratitude for the program, those who did not receive it expressed sadness and disappointment. While most study participants received the EITC, 30% missed out on this critical poverty alleviation benefit. Study participants overwhelmingly described that the most salient barriers were learning barriers, primarily lack of knowledge. Our study found a lack of program knowledge both among people who did and did not receive the EITC, indicating that program knowledge is not necessarily required to receive the benefits. If participants’ tax preparers knew to file for the benefit, many received it without knowledge about it themselves. Yet, others who did not know about it either failed to apply for it or failed to file taxes, not realizing they were eligible for this critical support.

Our findings are consistent with prior qualitative work that found that EITC recipients viewed their tax refund as a whole and were unable to differentiate the EITC portion specifically.37 These findings are also consistent with criticisms of the safety net system that posit that this lack of knowledge violates a basic economic principle: that complete information is needed for people to make optimal decisions. We learned from study participants that lack of information and difficulty accessing information led people to missed opportunities to support health and well-being. This lack of knowledge represents a market failure.29

At the same time, compliance barriers (eg, work of filing taxes, adhering to income eligibility rules, time to fill out forms) and psychological barriers (eg, complexity and cognitive load leading to stress, anxiety, and fear) also prevented participants from filing taxes and/or claiming the EITC. This is consistent with a prior randomized controlled trial among 1 million California tax filers, which found that interventions to increase awareness of the EITC were not sufficient to increase take-up, likely due to these and other administrative hurdles.38 This is also consistent with work finding that younger individuals and those who do not speak English as a primary language are less likely to file taxes and/or receive the EITC, likely because of inexperience or challenges with tax filing.31 Indeed, although all tax filers, regardless of socioeconomic status, experience some compliance costs when filing taxes, our findings highlight that, among our sample of low-income families, compliance costs were exacerbated due to lack of access to resources (eg, computers, printers, affordable tax-filing services). These compliance costs were particularly highlighted in respondents’ reports of others’ experiences.

In terms of suggested solutions, participants recommended addressing learning barriers, minimizing compliance barriers, providing more support for tax filing, and increasing the minimum EITC refund size as potential ways to improve take-up of the support. These suggestions emphasize how the current design of the EITC puts much of the burden of refund receipt on the end-user to navigate a complicated bureaucracy. Such suggestions are further supported by a series of recent experiments that have documented that changing communication of bureaucratic rules and requirements (eg, providing examples, simplifying language) can improve the accuracy of meeting application requirements and even application rates.30,39,40 Another experiment among Danish adults found that the use of bureaucratic language when describing program eligibility rules exacerbated learning, compliance, and psychological costs.39 With this context in mind, 1 possibility for reducing administrative burdens is to avoid the application process all together, automatically distributing the EITC to eligible households, or to provide a simplified way to claim the refund, similar to the automatic distributions of the Child Tax Credit (CTC) in 2021 that increased CTC take-up.41 A number of study participants suggested this solution; it may be particularly pertinent for those individuals who do not make enough money to be required to file taxes yet who are EITC eligible. Previous studies have demonstrated potential interventions to increase take-up—for example, official letters from the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) about free tax preparation42 and IRS notices to EITC-eligible families reminding them of their eligibility.43 Nevertheless, these findings, coupled with the results from the present analysis, suggest that program awareness alone may not be enough to improve EITC take-up, and that the way in which requirements and eligibility are structured and communicated may be the most salient option for improving how citizens apply for and benefit from programs.

No strong consensus emerged about whether the EITC is more beneficial in its current annual lump-sum distribution vs monthly distributions, in contrast to historical work that found a preference for the lump-sum distribution.36 However, we did find that, although most participants appreciated support from the EITC, some who received small refunds said it was not helpful or worth filing, further supporting larger lump-sum payments. Lump-sum EITC payments have been shown to be associated with cyclical food insecurity across the year as short-term benefits wane.44 The current study was conducted before the 2021 temporary expansion of the CTC, which was distributed as monthly payments. Future studies could examine whether families’ opinions of EITC disbursement frequency changed after experiencing monthly CTC payments.

This study has several strengths and limitations. The qualitative findings are an important complement to prior research using administrative and survey data, producing nuanced insights into barriers faced by EITC-eligible families and potential solutions to incomplete take-up. Reviewing participants’ tax forms to verify EITC eligibility and receipt also reduced measurement error in defining the study sample and identifying participants actually receiving EITC benefits. Further, capturing EITC benefit levels from tax forms allowed us to understand differences in experiences of people who receive smaller vs larger refunds. Nevertheless, the findings may not generalize beyond California or beyond the study time frame, which occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic when the safety net policy landscape was particularly dynamic.45 Also, there were only a few people in some participant categories (eg, racial/ethnic backgrounds and tax-filing status), and most findings and quotes arose from EITC recipients vs nonrecipients, which may mean that we have limited information on sentiments among these groups.

Conclusion

The EITC is the largest US poverty alleviation program for families with children, with proven potential for addressing social determinants of health and health equity. Yet, the complexity of the EITC disbursement system—namely, the tax-filing process—leaves many eligible families without this potentially beneficial support. Our study provides timely evidence based on EITC-eligible participants’ lived experiences to inform ongoing policymaking and support health equity. The federal government has historically taken steps to address these hurdles to safety net program participation—for example, as part of the “Burden Reduction Initiative” under the Biden administration.32 Another proposed (but not passed) bill in 2022 focused on simplifying tax filing, especially for tax credits like the EITC.46 Continuing to innovate to ensure that all eligible people receive the nation's primary poverty alleviation benefit will promote public health and health equity.
