Enhancing Crohn’s disease management during pregnancy: innovations in therapeutic approaches and cutting-edge monitoring practices
Authors: Mustafa Goksu, Ozan Karadeniz, Aycan Orler, Pinar Kadirogullari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the current practices and highlight the importance of ongoing innovation in managing Crohn’s disease (CD) during pregnancy.MethodsA prospective observational study was conducted at Kanuni Sultan Suleyman Training and Research Hospital, encompassing patients managed from 2023 to 2024. The study included 43 pregnant women diagnosed with Crohn’s disease who were monitored throughout the prepartum and postpartum periods. Clinical data regarding disease duration, medication use, intestinal surgery history, maternal complications, and neonatal outcomes were collected and analyzed. Key variables included the need for a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, and neonatal birth weight.ResultsA cohort of 43 patients with Crohn’s disease was analyzed for maternal and neonatal outcomes. The median age of the cohort was 33.7 ± 4.8 years, with a median disease duration of 6.8 ± 3.9 years. Vaginal delivery (VD) was achieved in 51.2% of cases, while 48.8% required a cesarean section. Assisted reproductive techniques (ART) were used in 9.4% of patients. Maternal complications included gestational diabetes (2.3%), gestational hypertension (4.7%), cervical insufficiency (2.3%), and intrauterine fetal death (4.7%). Premature birth (< 37 weeks) occurred in 11.6% of cases, and 25.6% of newborns required NICU admission. Notably, 20.9% of patients had a history of intestinal surgery, and 37.1% received medications such as corticosteroids, antibiotics, or infliximab.ConclusionPregnant women with Crohn’s disease generally have favorable outcomes, especially when disease activity is well-controlled. Active disease during pregnancy, however, is associated with increased maternal and neonatal complications. Close monitoring and individualized management are crucial to optimizing pregnancy outcomes in this population.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, Inflammatory bowel disease, Management, Pregnancy, Therapy

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD), a chronic and relapsing variant of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), predominantly affects the gastrointestinal system, with global prevalence rates showing a marked increase, particularly among women of childbearing age [1]. Navigating CD during pregnancy introduces distinct challenges due to the complex interplay between disease activity, maternal health, and fetal development. Evidence suggests that approximately 25% of women with IBD may conceive while their condition remains active, thereby exacerbating pregnancy-related complications [2].

Women with CD during pregnancy are confronted with heightened risks of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, such as preterm birth, low birth weight, and cesarean delivery, particularly when disease activity is present [3, 4]. Attaining and sustaining disease remission before conception and throughout gestation is paramount, as women in remission generally experience pregnancy outcomes comparable to those seen in the general population [5]. Nevertheless, achieving a balance between effective disease management and the safety profile of medications during pregnancy remains a significant concern for both patients and healthcare providers.

Recent advancements in therapeutics, including biologics and immunomodulators, have markedly improved CD management during pregnancy. Research has indicated the relative safety of specific biologic treatments, such as anti-TNF agents, which diminish disease activity without posing significant risks to fetal health [6, 7]. Moreover, advancements in non-invasive monitoring techniques, like fecal calprotectin assays and advanced imaging modalities, have enhanced disease surveillance, enabling prompt interventions that contribute to improved maternal and neonatal outcomes [8].

This manuscript aims to document our clinical experience and outcomes in managing 43 pregnant women diagnosed with Crohn’s disease from 2023 to 2024. Additionally, it provides an exhaustive review of strategies designed to optimize health outcomes for both mothers and their offspring.

Materials and methods

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Kanuni Sultan Suleyman Training and Research Hospital Obstetrics and Gynecology Department. The study period spanned from December 2023 to September 2024. The objective was to evaluate maternal and neonatal outcomes in pregnant women diagnosed with Crohn’s disease, focusing on the impact of disease activity, medication use, and prior surgical interventions during the prepartum and postpartum periods. The study was approved by the same institutional review board with an approval number KAEK/2023.12.202 and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study followed the guidelines outlined in the Helsinki Declaration.

The study enrolled 43 pregnant women with a confirmed diagnosis of Crohn’s disease who were regularly monitored throughout their pregnancies and postpartum periods. Inclusion criteria required a documented Crohn’s disease diagnosis before pregnancy and ongoing follow-up at the hospital’s obstetric and gastroenterology clinics. Exclusion criteria included incomplete medical records and any co-existing systemic conditions that could impact pregnancy outcomes (e.g., lupus or rheumatoid arthritis).

Clinical data were prospectively collected during routine prenatal visits and postpartum follow-ups. For each patient, the following information was recorded: patient demographics and clinical characteristics, including age, body mass index (BMI), duration of Crohn’s disease, history of intestinal surgery (e.g., bowel resection), and history of assisted reproductive technologies. Medication use was documented, specifying the type and duration of therapies such as corticosteroids, immunomodulators, and biologics. Maternal outcomes assessed included mode of delivery (vaginal or cesarean), gestational age at delivery, and maternal complications such as preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, or Crohn’s disease flares requiring intervention. Neonatal outcomes were also collected, including birth weight, Apgar scores, gestational age, prematurity, and the need for neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission.

Patients were evaluated regularly by both gastroenterology and obstetrics teams. Neonatal assessments were conducted immediately after birth and during NICU admission, if applicable. The primary outcome measures included maternal complications, gestational age at delivery, mode of delivery, neonatal birth weight, and NICU admission. In secondary outcomes, the use of biological drugs such as infliximab (used by 2.3% of patients), which demonstrated safety and efficacy in maintaining remission during pregnancy, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and fecal calprotectin, used in 30% of cases to assess disease activity, were included.

In the analysis of research data, SPSS Inc, Chicago, USA (SPSS v28.0) software was used. Descriptive statistics for categorical variables in the study were provided in frequency and percentage. In contrast, descriptive statistics for quantitative variables were given as mean and standard deviation (minimum-maximum).

Vaginal delivery (VD) was the mode of delivery in 51.2% of cases (n = 22), while cesarean delivery (CD) was performed in 48.8% (n = 21). Of the 21 CD cases, 5 (23.8%) were associated with prior intestinal surgery, primarily ileocecal resection, though no direct causation was established. ART were used in 9.4% of cases, with 4.7% undergoing intrauterine insemination (IUI) and 4.7% in vitro fertilization (IVF).

Complications included gestational diabetes mellitus (2.3%), gestational hypertension (4.7%), cervical insufficiency (2.3%), and intrauterine fetal death (4.7%). Prematurity (< 37 weeks) was observed in 11.6% of the cases, and 25.6% of newborns required admission to the NICU. The mean weight of newborns was 3203 g, ranging from 1997 g to 4800 g. Fetal growth restriction (FGR) and anomalies were observed in one case and two cases, respectively. The anomalies included one case of cardiac malformation and one case of limb anomaly, with no clear association to medication use based on available data.

Oral corticosteroids were used by 20.9% of patients, and antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) were administered in 16.2% of cases. No patients in this cohort had documented fistulas or perianal disease. A notable proportion of patients (20.9%) had a history of intestinal surgery, primarily ileocecal resection (ICR).

Secondary outcomes included the use of biologics like infliximab (used by 2.3% of patients), which aligns with recent advancements showing safety and efficacy in maintaining remission during pregnancy, and non-invasive monitoring with fecal calprotectin and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), used in 30% of cases to assess disease activity without fetal risk.

Discussion

Antepartum monitoring

Prenatal monitoring at regular intervals is essential for the well-being of both the mother and the fetus for pregnant women with Crohn’s disease (CD). The Crohn’s disease activity at the time of conception has a major impact on the outcomes of pregnancy. The presence of active Crohn’s disease at the onset of pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of adverse outcomes, including miscarriage, preterm delivery, low birth weight, and small-for-gestational-age (SGA) infants [9]. In contrast, women who are in remission at the time of conception generally experience pregnancy outcomes comparable to those of healthy women [10].

Regular assessment of clinical symptoms and inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and fecal calprotectin, is essential for effective antenatal surveillance and indicates disease activity [11]. It is more advantageous to use modern imaging techniques like MRI and ultrasound during pregnancy, as they enable diagnosis without exposing the fetus to ionizing radiation. In our study, 30% of patients underwent MRI or fecal calprotectin testing, which facilitated timely interventions, consistent with Kammerlander et al. findings on non-invasive monitoring efficacy [8]. The imaging frequency should be adjusted based on the severity of the condition and any variations in symptoms. Additionally, serial ultrasound assessments are recommended to monitor fetal development and detect potential indicators of FGR, a frequent complication associated with active Crohn’s disease [12]. Effective care optimization requires collaboration between gastroenterologists, obstetricians, and maternal-fetal medicine specialists.

Maternal risks and management

The risk of maternal complications is higher among women with Crohn’s disease who are pregnant, particularly if the disease is not well-regulated. Heightened Crohn’s disease activity elevates the risk of complications, including anemia, venous thromboembolism (VTE), malnutrition, and the development of pregnancy-specific conditions such as preeclampsia and gestational diabetes [13]. Moreover, pregnancy-related exacerbations may require corticosteroid treatment, increasing the chances of gestational diabetes, hypertension, and premature membrane rupture [14].

The primary goal of addressing maternal risks is to maintain the disease in a state of remission. Throughout gestation, the majority of treatments for Crohn’s disease, such as aminosalicylates, immunosuppressants like azathioprine, and biologic therapies such as anti-tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents, are generally regarded as safe [15]. Infliximab and adalimumab, which are biological agents, are commonly used and effectively reduce flare-ups without significantly raising the risk of congenital anomalies [16]. However, corticosteroids should be reserved for short-term management of acute flare-ups due to the potential risks they present to both the mother and fetus [17]. It’s important to carefully monitor iron supplementation, as anemia and iron deficiency are common due to both disease-related malabsorption and increased iron needs during pregnancy [18]. For women with moderate to severe disease activity, VTE prophylaxis should be considered, given the increased risk of thromboembolic events in this population [19].

Fetal risks and management

The fetal risks in pregnancies with Crohn’s disease are mainly linked to the mother’s disease condition. Adverse outcomes such as preterm birth, low birth weight, FGR, and an elevated risk of stillbirth have been associated with inadequately managed CD during pregnancy. However, effectively managing the illness can substantially reduce the chances of these complications, emphasizing the importance of maintaining remission during pregnancy. Our prematurity rate (11.6%) was slightly lower than reported in Mahadevan et al. (15%), possibly due to effective use of biologics and non-invasive monitoring in our cohort [3]. Our study’s FGR rate (2.3%) was lower than reported in Shand et al. (5%), likely due to effective disease control with biologics and regular monitoring [4].

Pharmacological management plays a crucial role in balancing maternal health and fetal safety. Methotrexate, a drug used in some Crohn’s disease treatment regimens, is contraindicated during pregnancy due to its teratogenic effects and must be discontinued well before conception [20]. Thiopurines such as azathioprine have been shown to pose minimal risk of teratogenicity and are considered safe for use after the first trimester of pregnancy [21]. Biologic therapies, particularly anti-TNF agents, are also regarded as safe during pregnancy [22]. Consistent fetal monitoring with ultrasounds is crucial for monitoring growth and identifying possible issues like FGR or oligohydramnios [23].

Delivery type

The mode of delivery in women with Crohn’s disease is influenced by several factors, including disease activity, location, and the presence of perianal involvement. In the absence of perianal disease, VD is generally considered safe and is the preferred option for many women with CD [24]. For women with active perianal disease, fistulas, or substantial rectal involvement, cesarean delivery is typically recommended to avoid perineal trauma, which may worsen perianal disease or lead to long-term complications, such as fecal incontinence.

A systematic review found that cesarean section rates are higher among women with CD compared to the general obstetric population, particularly in those with a history of fistulizing disease or perianal complications. In cases where the disease is under control and there is no active perianal involvement, VD may still be an option. Therefore, decisions about the mode of delivery should be individualized, and multidisciplinary input from obstetricians, gastroenterologists, and, when necessary, colorectal surgeons should guide the delivery plan.

Postpartum monitoring and breastfeeding

The postpartum period represents a time of heightened vulnerability for women with Crohn’s disease, as disease activity often flares within the first year after delivery. Studies show that up to one-third of women with CD experience a flare in the postpartum period, particularly if they discontinue medication during pregnancy or soon after childbirth [25]. Monitoring should include regular assessments of clinical symptoms, inflammatory markers, and nutritional status, as postpartum women with active disease are at risk for anemia, malnutrition, and dehydration.

Breastfeeding is generally encouraged in women with CD, even for those on biological therapies, as drugs like infliximab and adalimumab are minimally secreted into breast milk and have not been shown to harm the infant. However, some medications, such as methotrexate, are contraindicated during breastfeeding and must be avoided. Given the increased risk of postpartum flare-ups, close follow-up with a gastroenterologist is essential. Preventative measures, including prompt resumption of maintenance therapy after delivery, may help reduce the risk of relapse. Furthermore, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which are commonly used for postpartum pain management, should be avoided as they may exacerbate Crohn’s disease symptoms.

Medical treatment during pregnancy

Maintaining disease remission during pregnancy is paramount to preventing complications. The majority of CD medications are safe for use during pregnancy, although some, such as methotrexate, Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, ozanimod, and allopurinol, are not recommended due to their teratogenic potential [26]. Aminosalicylates, including mesalamine, are among the safest drugs for use during pregnancy and have not been associated with an increased risk of birth defects.

Thiopurines, including azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine, can be continued in pregnancy. However, some studies suggest that their use may increase the risk of low birth weight, especially when used in high doses or in combination with other immunosuppressants. Biologic therapies, especially TNF inhibitors, are highly effective in maintaining remission and are considered safe during pregnancy. Our study’s use of infliximab (2.3%) reflects a shift toward biologics, which have improved outcomes compared to older corticosteroid-based approaches (Mahadevan et al.) [3]. Nevertheless, there is debate about whether biologics should be discontinued in the third trimester to reduce neonatal exposure. Some experts recommend continuing these therapies throughout pregnancy to avoid maternal flare-ups, as the risks of untreated active disease often outweigh the potential risks to the fetus.

Corticosteroids, such as prednisone, are often used for acute flare-ups but should be used sparingly due to the increased risk of gestational complications, including gestational diabetes, hypertension, and premature delivery. Ultimately, the goal of treatment during pregnancy should be to maintain remission, and any decisions regarding medication use should involve close collaboration between the patient, gastroenterologist, and obstetrician to ensure both maternal and fetal safety. As a limitation of our study, remission status was not specifically recorded. It is obvious that our knowledge on this subject will be strengthened with more comprehensive studies in the future.

Conclusion

Managing CD in pregnancy is multifaceted, requiring pre-conception counseling, maintenance of remission, careful pharmacological management, and coordinated follow-up across specialties. Advances in biological therapies have significantly improved pregnancy outcomes, but challenges remain, particularly in predicting postpartum flares and managing drug safety in breastfeeding. Ongoing research and long-term studies are essential to optimizing care for both mother and child in this unique patient population.
