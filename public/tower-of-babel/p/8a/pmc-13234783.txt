Phosphorus-Free and Self-Extinguishing Cellulose Paper Achieved via Short-Chain Carboxylate-Functionalized Mn/Ni Nanoparticles
Authors: Qing Lin, Haoran Jiang, Yong Hu, Ziwen Dong, Song Kong, Yangli Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Addressing the practical predicament of uneven global distribution of phosphate resources, this study focuses on transition-metal-based short-chain perfluorocarboxylates (Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA) and systematically investigates their flame-retardant properties, aiming to develop efficient novel phosphorus-free flame retardants. The results of micromorphological characterization indicate that Mn-TFA forms a porous network structure, and Ni-TFA exhibits a polyhedral morphology, both of which possess distinct crystallinity attributed to the coordination interaction between metal ions and carboxylate groups. Flame-retardant performance evaluation demonstrates that samples display excellent flame-retardant effects: their flame-retardant performance is significantly superior to that of ammonium dihydrogen phosphate (the core active component of commercial flame retardants) at all tested concentrations. Particularly under medium-concentration loading conditions, the flame-retardant paper can achieve a self-extinguishing effect. The results of TG-IR-MS combined analysis show that the generation rates of CO and CO2 in the fuel system are significantly accelerated under the action of the flame retardants, indicating that the flame-retardant process is dominated by the condensed-phase effect.

Introduction

Cellulose is widely used in construction, agriculture, printing, and other fields. However, its application is limited by its inherent high flammability, which has driven the development of effective flame-retardant (FR) modification methods for cellulose-based paper. For instance, treating paper substrates with guanidine phosphate or diammonium phosphate has successfully imparted flame-retardant properties. Specifically, impregnation of corrugated paper with a silicon–nitrogen-phosphorus-based formulation has been shown to achieve efficient flame retardancy, while ammonium dihydrogen phosphate impregnation significantly increases the limiting oxygen index of paper and reduces combustion intensity. Coating is another common technique, typically involving the deposition of a flame-retardant layer on the paper’s surface. Notably, an aluminum phosphate/montmorillonite nanocomposite coating has yielded remarkable results, with a 96.77% reduction in burning time and an 85.59% reduction in burning length at an optimal additive dosage of 18.3%. Despite these advances, a fundamental limitation persists: coated paper only provides transient protection against short-term flame exposure as flame retardants are primarily surface-localized and rapidly consumed. Thus, impregnation remains the preferred and reliable method for endowing cellulosic paper with a robust flame-retardant performance.

Currently, flame retardants used in paper products are predominantly reliant on phosphorus-containing compounds, whose primary raw material is high-quality phosphate rock. As a nonrenewable strategic mineral resource, phosphate rock exhibits multiple inherent disadvantages, including highly concentrated global distribution, unbalanced regional endowment, significant variations in ore grade, and prominent constraints from mining and environmental protection. These factors severely threaten global phosphorus resource security and the sustainable development of related industries, leading to its official classification as a critical raw material by the U.S. Geological Survey. Against this backdrop, experimental exploration of the formulation design of novel phosphorus-free flame retardants is imperative. Studies have demonstrated that transition metals possess exceptional flame-retardant capabilities. For instance, zeolite-based flame retardants loaded with manganese compounds have been developed, and the flame-retardant efficiency of transition-metal compounds such as Mn­(C5H5)2 and Cr­(C5H5)2 has been reported to be 35 times and 75 times higher than that of phosphorus-containing compounds, respectively.

Perfluorinated compounds (PFCs) are commonly used as flame-retardant components. Among them, perfluorocarboxylic acids (PFCAs) are organofluorine compounds with the chemical formula C n F2n+1CO2H. Unlike halogenated alkanes (e.g., Halon 1301 and 1211), PFCAs exhibit zero ozone depletion potential. A major drawback of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA, where n = 7) lies in the toxicity of its salts. PFOA-related compounds have been listed in the Stockholm Convention on Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs) due to their persistence, bioaccumulation, and adverse effects on the environment and organisms. Researchers have summarized the global usage and production of aqueous film-forming foams (AFFF), reporting that the estimated global emissions of AFFF during 1965–1974 ranged from 50 to 100 tons. In contrast, short-chain PFCAs (n ≤ 6) have negligible bioconcentration factors and thus have been removed from the POPs list since 2019.

Trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), a short-chain organic carboxylic acid (n = 1), is a common ligand for metal cations. This study aims to prepare phosphorus-free flame-retardant paper by synthesizing complexes of manganese and nickel with trifluoroacetic acid, respectively, and loading them onto cellulose paper via an impregnation method. The flame-retardant efficiency is compared with that of commercial phosphorus-containing flame retardants to demonstrate the superior performance of the novel phosphorus-free flame-retardants.

Synthesis and Characterization of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA

Raw Materials

The chemical reagents used in the laboratory were purchased from the commercial sources without additional processing or purification. MnCl2 with purity ≥99% and NiCl2·6H2O with a purity of 98% were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. The 99% TFA, ADP, and NaOH were provided by Sinophem Shanghai Co., Ltd. The water used was deionized water produced in our laboratory.

Sample Preparation of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA

The MnCl2 (5.0 g, 40 mmol) and excess TFA (5.0 × 10–2 dm3, 0.65 mol) were added to a double-necked round-bottomed flask that was dried in the oven. When supplied in excess, TFA is both reactant and solvent. The deionized water (5.0 × 10–4 dm3) was added dropwise to dissolve the MnCl2. The resulting mixture was stirred under 300 rpm for 28 h. The reaction then took place in an atmosphere of Ar. After the mixture was slowly cooled to room temperature and evaporated, it was washed three times with n-hexane and then dried for 8 h in a vacuum dryer at 60 °C to remove TFA. The obtained Mn-TFA product is 8.68 g pink crystal.

NiCl2·6H2O (9.15 g, 38 mmol) was filled in a double-necked round-bottomed flask dried in an oven, and TFA (5.0 × 10–2 dm3, 0.65 mol) was added in excess as the reactant and solvent. Then, water was added to dissolve the nickel chloride completely. The mixture was stirred in Ar (300 rpm) for 28 h at reflux temperature. After cooling to room temperature, the solution was evaporated and crystallized, washed with n-hexane, and then vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 8 h. The Ni-TFA product (10.64 g) is a light green crystal. Characterization Results

SEM is used to characterize the microstructure of the two samples. The SEM model is Gemini450, and the sample was sprayed with gold for 100 s before the test. It can be seen from Figure a–c that Mn-TFA has an obvious porous network structure, which is a massive solid with a diameter of 100 μm, and its surface pores are about 2 μm. As shown in Figure d–f, Ni-TFA is a polyhedron shape with good crystallization, and its particle size is about 2 μm, which is much smaller than Mn-TFA, indicating that it has better dispersion. There are no holes on the surface of Ni-TFA similar to that of Mn-TFA, but there are obvious folds on the surface of Ni-TFA, which should be attributed to fluoride bridging.

SEM images of the Mn-TFA (a–c) and Ni-TFA (d–f).

The SEM test relies on the original sample without further processing, and the original sample powder may appear to have the phenomenon of agglomeration and compaction. To explore the fine morphology and structure of the sample particles as small as possible, we further studied the samples dissolved in ethanol after ultrasonic treatment by TEM. After ultrasonic treatment, the sample was dispersed to be small enough. As can be seen from Figure a–c, Mn-TFA is an irregular shape with a diameter of about 200 nm, but it is still a porous network structure, which is consistent with the SEM results, and its pore size is about 20 nm. Ni-TFA is a spherical particle with a diameter of 400 nm, rather than a polyhedral shape, which may be caused by fluoride shedding and grinding on the surface of the sample during ultrasonic treatment. The nanoscale of the two retardants determines their better dispersion in the gas phase during the combustion inhibition process. For flame retardants, smaller particle size and more loose mesh structure are conducive to rapid action of flame to exert an inhibition effect, so Mn-TFA may have better flame inhibition effect than Ni-TFA based on physical properties alone.

TEM images of the Mn-TFA (a–c) and Ni-TFA (d–f).

The literature shows that there are three characteristic peaks in the infrared spectrogram of TFA, which appear near 1170, 1447, and 1783 cm–1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of the C–F bond, the symmetric stretching of the CO2 – group, and the asymmetric stretching of the CO2 – group, respectively. , FTIR curves of the Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA are illustrated in Figure , for which the tested sample was mixed with KBr at a mass ratio of 1:80 and was molded into transparent slices. The two synthesized samples all have the three characteristic peaks of TFA mentioned above, except that the peak positions are slightly offset after the complexation reaction, but the overall trend is consistent. Their CO2 – symmetric stretching peak and asymmetric stretching peak can be observed at 1401 and 1626 cm–1.

FT-IR spectra of the Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA.

The reason for this phenomenon is that TFA, as a ligand, coordinates with manganese ion and nickel ion, respectively, through carboxylic acid oxygen, resulting in the reduction of electron density of CO2 – group after complexation and the reduction of absorption frequency. The coordination types of CO2 – groups and metal cations can be determined by the difference in the positions of the symmetrical and asymmetric stretching peaks of CO2 – groups in the FT-IR spectra, that is, Δv = v as–v sym. In the FT-IR data of the coordination of TFA and metal cation, the coordination type is unidentate, ionic, and bridging coordination corresponding to the Δv value of 264–382, 202, and 147–245 cm–1, respectively. The Δv value of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA is about 225 cm–1 in Figure , that is, the coordination form of TFA and metal cation in two samples is bridging.

XRD tests are used to characterize the crystalline properties of the materials. Figure illustrates the XRD patterns of prepared Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA. The peaks of the two materials are sharp and obvious, and these peaks mostly appear in a small-angle range, which indicates that these two prepared retardant materials have a good crystal structure. That is, the internal atomic and molecular arrangements of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA are periodic and orderly, and the impurities unrelated to flame retardant are less, which further verifies the success of retardant synthesis.

XRD patterns of the Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA.

XPS survey was used to explore the chemical composition of synthesized Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA samples. Figure demonstrates the surface elemental composition of these two samples. The obvious peaks of C 1s, O 1s, and F 1s at 285.19, 532.16, and 688.04 eV can be found in both curves, which means that C, O, and F are the main elements in these two retardants. As illustrated in the spectra of Mn-TFA, there is an evident peak at 641.98 eV, corresponding to the binding energy of Mn 2p. The Ni 2p peak can also be observed at 855.40 eV in the XPS curve of Ni-TFA. This indicates that the carboxyl group in TFA has successfully complexed with metal cations Mn and Ni.

XPS survey spectra of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA.

To further analyze the valence states of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA, Avantage was used to fit the characteristic peaks of C 1s, F 1s, Mn2P, and Ni2P. The XPS fine spectra of elements of the two samples are shown in Figure . The fine spectra of C 1s and F 1s of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA are similar, indicating that there is no significant difference between the valence states of these two elements in samples. Mn 2p in Mn-TFA has three peaks at 653.48, 646.88, and 641.48 eV, corresponding to the binding energy of standard manganese. Ni 2p in Mn-TFA has four peaks at 881.58, 872.78, 860.88, and 855.38 eV, corresponding to the standard nickel. According to the results of peak fitting, Mn-TFA contains 49.65% of C, 27.32% of O, 17.36% of F, and 5.67% of Mn, and Ni-TFA contains 59.03% of C, 19.14% of O, 12.37% of F, and 9.46% of Ni, respectively. This further explains the complex reaction between carboxylic acid and metal cation and verifies that the transition metal element with flame-retardant effect is introduced successfully into the retardant samples. The performance of flame retardants is closely related to their chemical compositions. Compared with the effective flame-retardant components of the two new retardants, the content of F + Mn in MN-TFA is 23.03%, and the content of F + Ni in Ni-TFA is 21.83%, so the former may have better flame-retardant performance.

XPS survey spectra of (a) C 1s, (b) F 1s, (c)Mn 2p for Mn-TFA and (d) C 1s, (e) F 1s, (f) Ni 2p for Ni-TFA.

The thermal stability of two retardant samples was characterized by TG tests, in which the temperature range was 30–800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min under the N2 atmosphere. The thermogravimetric curves of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA are shown in Figure a. The initial decomposition temperature of both samples is lower than 80 °C, and the process of weight loss continues with the increase in temperature. It indicates that they can start pyrolysis and evaporation when the initial combustion temperature is not high, which plays a role in inhibiting the combustion process. As illustrated by Figure b, both samples show a significant and frequent mass loss at 30–200 °C and 600–800 °C. Such phased weight loss was mainly related to the damage of the sample surface structure and the volatilization as well as further degradation of solvent molecules or unreacted ligands. The residual carbon content of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA at 800 °C is 30.65% and 25.81%, respectively, indicating that Mn-TFA has better thermal stability, which is crucial for the retardant to exert the condensate phase inhibition performance.

(a) TG and (b) TGA curves of the Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA.

Combined with the above characterization results of physical and chemical structures of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA, Mn-TFA has a smaller microscale and porous structure, contains more effective flame-retardant components in the chemical composition, and has excellent thermal stability, so it should have a more efficient flame-retardant efficiency. The next step was to conduct experiments to confirm this view.

Results and Discussion

The loading of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA onto cellulose paper substrates was accomplished through an impregnation process. Subsequently, the flame-retardant performance of the modified papers was quantitatively assessed using the downward fire spread rate (DFSR), an experimental protocol specifically designed for evaluating fire resistance in cellulosic materials. ,

Results of DFSR

The DFSR results of cellulose papers contain two novel retardants, and the commercial ADPs are shown in Figure . Among them, with the increase of concentration, the DFSR in all cases of V Mn‑TFA, V Mn‑TFA, and V ADP decreases gradually, in which, V ADP stabilizes at value 0.83 when C k of ADP reaches the 25.3 × 10–4 mol/g. It is clear that the V k of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA decreases faster than that of ADP when increasing C k, and V k of Mn-TFA has the highest sensitivity to the C k. V k = 0 is defined as self-extinguishing, i.e., the cellulose samples doped with retardant will automatically extinguish quickly after ignition. It can be seen that the flame will self-extinguish once the Mn-TFA adsorption reaches the 19.5 × 10–4 mol/g. The same thing will happen when the concentration of Ni-TFA reaches 26.8 × 10–4 mol/g. It implies that with the same concentration of retardant, the retardant efficiency of Mn-TFA is much better than that of Ni-TFA, and the concentration required for Mn-TFA to achieve self-extinguishing is 37% lower than that of Ni-TFA.

DFSR of Mn-TFA, Ni-TFA, and ADP.

Overall, the experimental results indicate that Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA have better flame-retardant effects than ADP, and among them, Mn-TFA shows the best flame-retardant performance. To further illustrate this phenomenon, the instantaneous flame morphology and flame temperature are discussed below.

Figure shows the snapshots of flame morphology obtained from different cellulose samples, considering the adoption with different retardants. As shown in Figure a, when a pure cellulose paper burns, the flame is bright and luminous with light yellow as the main color. The flame of the cellulose sample containing Mn-TFA is much dimmer than that of pure cellulose, and the root of the flame is blue from a side view as shown in Figure b, which is caused by the incomplete combustion due to the retardant effect. In Figure c, although the flame of the cellulose sample containing Ni-TFA is not as dim as that in Figure b, the flame height is reduced and the flame structure is incomplete. From the side view, there is also an apparent blue flame at the root because of incomplete combustion. The flame morphology of the cellulose sample containing ADP shown in Figure d is not much different from that of the pure fiber sample. Although the ADP flame is faint, it still shows an obvious yellow color. During the combustion, air molecules around the burning zone are heated and move at a high speed, which give off a bright light. The heat energy released by combustion is concentrated around the reaction zone, and the surrounding air is heated. The faster air molecules move will lead to a brighter light. Judging from the morphology of the flame, a brighter flame means a higher flame speed.

Instantaneous flame morphology in cases of (a) pure cellulose paper, (b) cellulose paper with Mn-TFA, (c) with Ni-TFA and (d) with ADP.

The above results indicate that Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA have a more obvious influence and intervention on the burning flame structures compared with the traditional retardant ADP, that is, they show a stronger combustion inhibition effect.

During the suppression of flames, a retardation cooling effect is typically observed. However, direct use of thermocouples to monitor temperature changes during the fire spread process can compromise the accuracy of the DFSR due to contact between the thermocouple and the sample. To quantify this outcome accurately, thermocouples are positioned 20 mm away from the sample. Although the measured temperatures at this distance may be lower than the actual flame temperatures, they do not impact the comparative analysis of cooling effects among different retardants.

Figure displays the flame radiation temperature. In Figure a, during the combustion of pure cellulose paper, the radiation temperature at the initial position registers at 177 °C. However, there is a notable decrease in radiation temperatures at subsequent downstream locations (TC1 and TC2), which measure 99 and 83.6 °C, respectively. This reduction can be attributed to the thermocouple initially being influenced solely by flame radiation. As the flame propagates downward, the temperatures detected at the TC1 and TC2 thermocouples are governed by a combined effect of radiation heating and convective cooling caused by the gas flow ahead of the flame front, which is accountable by both radiation and convection effects.

Transient profiles of flame radiation temperature curves measured in three different points (shown in Figure ) for cases of (a) pure cellulose, (b) cellulose paper with Mn-TFA, (c) with Ni-TFA, (d) with ADP.

Comparatively, the temperature profiles obtained for the cellulose samples containing retardants show a mild change at the three successive positions, as shown in Figure b–d. This could be explained by that the combustion and the resulting radiation temperature are now more dominated by the inhibition effect for the samples containing the retardant. At the first point TC3, the measured temperature values are dramatically reduced when the retardants are considered. Specifically, the radiation temperature of the first position of the samples containing Mn-TFA, Ni-TFA, and ADP decreased by 96%, 79.1 and 67.2 °C, respectively, compared with that of pure cellulose. It is further confirmed that the reduction effect of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA is better than that of commercial phosphorus-containing retardant ADP, and the Mn-TFA has the best inhibition performance.

The vapor fuel generated by the pyrolysis of the solid fuel at the preheating zone is ignited to form a diffusion flame when the flame propagates downward on the cellulose paper. The combustible vapors from the combustion of cellulose and the gaseous products volatilized by the retardant are generated between the flame zone and the unburned zone. If the adsorbed retardants inhibit the oxidative pyrolysis and degradation of residual carbon in the condensation state of cellulose, the formation of cellulose volatile steam will be influenced and the spread rate of flame will be decreased. However, DFSR can be inhibited if Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA evaporate and decompose into gaseous substances that can capture combustion radicals at flame temperature. It is an important goal to explore the inhibition process of new flame retardants. Therefore, it is not comprehensive and unreasonable to speculate about the inhibition process of retardants only from the results of the inhibition experiment. The TG-IR-MS test was used to analyze the detailed flame-retardant process of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA.

Results of TG-IR-MS

Dry cellulose powder was thoroughly mixed with Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA in a nitrogen-filled glovebox. The flame retardant loadings in the mixed powder were set to the maximum concentrations that could be adsorbed by the cellulose paper samples in the inhibition tests, that is, C Mn‑TFA = 28.3 × 10–4 mol/g and C Mn‑TFA = 27.9 × 10–4 mol/g. Thermogravimetry–infrared spectroscopy–mass spectrometry (TG-IR-MS, TL-9000) was employed to analyze the gaseous products released during sample pyrolysis and the possible flame-retardant pathway. The experiments were performed in an air atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min from 30 to 800 °C.

Figure presents the TG-DTG curves of neat cellulose powder and composite samples incorporated with Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA, respectively. The pyrolysis process of neat cellulose can be divided into three stages: dehydration, pyrolysis, and charring. Specifically, the dehydration stage occurs at 40–250 °C, during which cellulose loses approximately 1 wt % of bound water. As the samples were sufficiently dried prior to testing, the water loss peak in the DTG curve is not obvious. The temperature range of 250–370 °C corresponds to the main pyrolysis stage, where about 95 wt % mass loss takes place with vigorous pyrolysis reactions. Unstable functional groups decompose into small and large molecular fragments, which further condense into volatile products at elevated temperatures. The charring stage spans 370–850 °C, where the weight loss rate slows down remarkably with increasing temperature, eventually forming carbonaceous residues.

TG and TGA curves of the mixed samples.

As shown in Figure , neat cellulose exhibits a char yield of only 1.92% at 800 °C, whereas the introduction of flame retardants significantly modifies the thermal weight loss behavior of cellulose. Both Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA effectively suppress the rapid thermal weight loss of cellulose near 350 °C, smooth the TG curves, and increase the char yields to 23.84% and 20.52%, respectively. The distinct effects of these two flame retardants on the thermal stability of cellulose can be further explained in combination with the thermogravimetric results in Figure . Mn-TFA itself presents a higher char yield (30.65%) than Ni-TFA (25.81%), thus leading to a more pronounced improvement in the thermal stability of cellulose.

The core intrinsic flame-retardant process lies in the cooperative catalytic charring effect in the condensed phase. On the one hand, the trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) component provides acidic active sites that catalyze dehydration, dehydrogenation, and chain scission of cellulose in the condensed phase, promoting the conversion of C–H and C–O bonds into CC, aromatic, and fused-ring structures, thereby initiating and enhancing char formation. Meanwhile, the acidic sites inhibit the generation of small flammable gases during cellulose pyrolysis, retaining more carbon in the solid phase to form dense char residues. On the other hand, the transition metals Mn and Ni, with abundant empty d orbitals, act as highly efficient dehydrogenation catalysts. They abstract hydrogen atoms from polymer chains to generate carbon radicals, which further facilitate C–C cross-linking, cyclization, and aromatization reactions, rapidly constructing a continuous and stable carbon skeleton structure.

The structural characteristics of cellulose determine the large difference between different components and the different fracture modes of functional groups and the different fracture reforming modes of various bonds. After the addition of retardants, the pyrolysis process of cellulose will also produce different macromolecular coagulable volatile substances and small-molecule substances. Three-dimensional (absorbency-wave-temperature) infrared absorbance spectroscopy was used to analyze the changes of various functional groups and gases of cellulose samples containing Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA during pyrolysis.

Figure presents the FT-IR spectra of the three samples at the maximum pyrolysis rate, and the analysis of functional groups corresponding to each characteristic absorption peak and the reaction mechanism is as follows: the absorption peak at a wavenumber of 3590 cm–1 corresponds to the bending vibration of the O–H–O bond, which is the characteristic absorption peak of H2O. The C–H stretching vibration peak at 2910 cm–1 is attributed to CH4. This absorption peak can only be observed in the pure cellulose sample but not in the samples added with flame retardants, indicating that the added flame retardants can effectively inhibit the release of CH4 during the pyrolysis of cellulose. The characteristic absorption peak at 2360 cm–1 corresponds to the bending vibration of the OCO bond in CO2, which is the characteristic peak of CO2. Comparison shows that the intensity of the absorption peak at 2360 cm–1 in the samples added with Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA is significantly higher than that in the pure cellulose sample, indicating that the introduction of flame retardants can promote the release of CO2 during the pyrolysis of cellulose. As an inert gas, CO2 can effectively isolate the oxygen required for combustion, thereby exerting an ideal flame-retardant effect.

FT-IR curves of pyrolysis products of the mixed samples at maximum degradation rates in the TG-IR experiments.

The absorption peaks at 1742 cm–1 and 1404 cm–1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of the CO bond and CC bond, respectively, which are characteristic absorption signals of hydrocarbons, indicating that a small amount of flammable hydrocarbon products is generated during the pyrolysis of cellulose. The C–C/C–O stretching vibration peak at 1060 cm–1 corresponds to the small-molecule carbon-containing products generated after combustion. This characteristic peak is most prominent in the pure cellulose sample, while no obvious absorption peak is observed near 1060 cm–1 in the samples added with Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA. The above phenomena indicate that the addition of prefabricated flame retardants can effectively inhibit the release of small-molecule carbon-containing products (with C–C/C–O structures) during the pyrolysis of cellulose, thereby achieving the effect of combustion inhibition. Its main mechanism of action is as follows: the flame retardants undergo chemical reactions with the active free radicals generated during the pyrolysis of cellulose, blocking the transmission of the combustion chain reaction and thereby reducing the generation of flammable small-molecule products. This flame-retardant strategy has important practical application value in the fields of fire prevention and control as well as material flame retardancy.

In addition, characteristic fluctuation signals appear at a wavenumber of 3916 cm–1 in the Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA samples, which are not detected in the pure cellulose sample. Combined with relevant studies on the thermal decomposition of HF, it can be determined that this signal is attributed to HF. As a typical hydrogen-containing halogen-free radical quencher, HF can efficiently trap highly active free radicals critical to the combustion chain reaction in the combustion gas phase, interrupting the transmission process of the free-radical chain and inhibiting flame spread and heat release from the source. At the same time, the generation process of HF consumes the combustible free radicals and heat in the system, reducing the intensity of gas-phase combustion, which plays an important role in improving the flame-retardant efficiency of materials and inhibiting combustion spread. It should be noted that HF is toxic; therefore, in the actual industrial application process of this new type of flame retardant, it is necessary to focus on its toxic mechanism and safety prevention and control measures.

Figure shows the 3-dimensional trend of FTIR curve changes of three samples heated from room temperature to 800 °C. The most obvious difference between pure cellulose and the sample with retardant is the characteristic absorption peak of CO2. The CO2 peak of pure cellulose appears and persists near 450 °C, but the signal is always weak. However, for the samples containing Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA, their characteristic peaks of CO2 are significantly different from the peak of cellulose. As can be seen in Figure b,c, the CO2 peak of the samples containing retardants appeared at the beginning of pyrolysis and gradually increased at a high rate, and Mn-TFA has higher CO2 peak intensity and rising rate than Ni-TFA. Upon addition of retardants, an observed increase in CO2 production during cellulose pyrolysis or combustion typically signals an enhancement in inhibition effectiveness. This is because the inclusion of retardants likely facilitates a more complete combustion reaction, thereby reducing the formation of long-chain hydrocarbons and other potentially harmful gases. The purpose of adding retardants during cellulose pyrolysis is usually to decrease the generation of volatile fuels required for flame propagation. An ideal retardant promotes the earlier decomposition of cellulose materials into less flammable small molecules or directly to ash, while also increasing the CO2 output. The significant increase in the release of CO and CO2 indicates that the oxidation pathway of hydrocarbons is altered during combustion, with a notable enhancement in incomplete oxidation. This experimental phenomenon is highly consistent with the free-radical scavenging mechanism in the gas phase, indirectly confirming that HF can inhibit gas-phase combustion by quenching active radicals in the combustion chain reaction, thereby exhibiting a potential gas-phase flame inhibition effect.

Evolution of the thermolytic products of (a) cellulose, (b) cellulose + Mn-TFA, and (c) + Ni-TFA in the TG-IR experiments.

Figure illustrates the mass spectrometry results for three samples at their maximum heating rates. From these spectra, characteristic signals for H2O, CO, and CO2 were distinctly identified. Since the loss of moisture primarily originates from the thermal decomposition of cellulose fuel, there are no significant differences in the peak values of the H2O signal among the sample groups. However, a notable difference is observed in the intensity of the CO and CO2 mass spectral signals between the samples containing the two retardants. Specifically, the signal strengths for CO and CO2 in the Mn-TFA sample are notably higher than those in the Ni-TFA sample. This outcome suggests that Mn-TFA has a propensity to promote incomplete pyrolysis reactions in cellulose fuel, leading to an increased production of carbonaceous species. Such characteristics demonstrate that Mn-TFA exhibits enhanced efficiency in flame-retardant performance.

Intensity of products of (a) cellulose, (b) cellulose + Mn-TFA, and (c) + Ni-TFA decomposition at maximum degradation rates was obtained in the TG-MS experiments.

On the basis of the identification and analysis of gas-phase products discussed earlier, it is evident that the performance of flame retardants is manifested through both physical and chemical effects. Physically, flame retardants are uniformly adsorbed onto the sample surface, influencing combustion propagation. However, since there are no significant differences in the particle size and morphology between the two flame retardants, the physical effects do not play a dominant role in affecting the DFSR.

The core difference between Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA lies in their metal atomic composition. As confirmed by previous TG experiments, manganese complexes with TFA exhibit superior thermal stability. This characteristic is crucial for the DFSR process that primarily relies on condensed-phase flame retardancy as it can significantly slow down the spread of flames in the devolatilization area, thereby demonstrating enhanced performance.

Durability Performance

To evaluate the durability of flame retardants under humid conditions, the flame-retardant paper was exposed to 25 °C and 80% relative humidity for 7 days, after which it was retested using the DFSR method under the same conditions. The resulting samples were designated as Mn-TFA­(H), Ni-TFA­(H), and ADP­(H). As shown in Figure , comparative analysis of the obtained data revealed that high-humidity exposure exerted no significant effect on the flame-retardant performance of any tested sample. Although the DFSR values decreased slightly, these changes corresponded only to a minor improvement in combustion inhibition, which was attributed to the adsorption of a very small amount of moisture within the sample matrix. This observation not only demonstrates the excellent hydrolytic stability of the modified cellulose paper substrate but also indicates that it retains its flame-retardant function after moisture absorption.

Remarks

The precursor TFA employed in the synthesis of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA exhibits a certain toxicity to fish and aquatic invertebrates. Moreover, the thermal decomposition of these two complexes generates HF, which also poses certain toxicity. These toxicity issues represent key constraints for their practical application as flame retardants, and further systematic investigations of their toxicity are therefore required. Prior to the present study, no literature has reported the combustion inhibition performance of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA. Despite the aforementioned limitations, this work lays the foundation for the future preparation of novel perfluorocarboxylate-based flame retardants.

Conclusions

Two novel phosphorus-free flame retardants, Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA, were successfully synthesized in this study and immobilized on cellulose paper substrates via an impregnation method. Their novelty lies in abandoning the traditional phosphorus-containing flame retardant approach and adopting short-chain carboxylate-functionalized Mn/Ni nanoparticles with low bioaccumulation, which addresses the problem of uneven global distribution of phosphorus resources and provides a new green flame-retardant pathway. Characterization confirmed that they exhibit porous network and polyhedral nanostructures, respectively, with distinct crystallinity attributed to the coordination between metal ions and carboxylate groups; XPS analysis verified the effective coordination, with the surface fluorine contents of Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA being 17.36% and 12.37%, respectively. Thermal degradation studies showed that the residual carbon contents reached 30.65% and 25.81%, respectively, during the dominant pyrolysis stage at 600–800 °C, indicating a significant improvement in thermal stability.

Comparative fire performance evaluation showed that Mn-TFA exhibited comparable effectiveness to commercial ADP at low loadings, while both Mn-TFA and Ni-TFA were significantly superior to ADP at high concentrations (efficiency order: Ni-TFA > Mn-TFA > ADP), and self-extinguishing of the paper could be achieved at medium concentrations. TG-IR-MS analysis revealed that the flame retardants promoted CO2 release and inhibited the release of flammable CO, with the flame-retardant process being dominated by the condensed-phase effect. The excellent flame inhibition effect was supported by the combined action of the physical barrier formation and chemical gas-phase inhibition. This short-chain perfluorocarboxylate-based transition-metal system achieved a breakthrough in phosphorus-free flame retardancy of cellulose paper, providing a high-performance alternative to traditional phosphorus-containing formulations.
