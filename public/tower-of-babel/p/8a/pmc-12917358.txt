Introduction

Sleep is a fundamental element of health which accounts for about one-third of our lives, and is as crucial as nutrient intake and physical exercise.1 2 It maintains the cognitive function, immunity, metabolism and emotional stability which directly impacts one’s overall well-being.1 Chronic sleep deprivation can be associated with detrimental health outcomes such as greater susceptibility to cardiovascular disorder, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, cancer and psychological disorders.1,3 Buysse proposed a description of sleep health which is: ‘Sleep health is a multidimensional pattern of sleep-wakefulness, adapted to individual, social and environmental demands, that promotes physical and mental well-being. Good sleep health is characterised by subjective satisfaction, appropriate timing, adequate duration, high efficiency and sustained alertness during waking hours.’4

Despite the fact that adolescents require approximately 9.25 hours of sleep and adults aged 18–60 years require at least 7 hours of sleep in a day, many of them continue to get less sleep, leading to a chronic sleep debt.5 Over the past years, sleep quality among young adults has been declining, as revealed by numerous studies and reports. Approximately 36% of teenagers and adults have poor sleep quality.6 In a study conducted between 2012 and 2014 among 1051 adult participants, 14.9% were diagnosed with insomnia.7

The Sleep Heart Study reported that those who had poor and short sleep were at 29% higher risk of cardiovascular disease.8 Food intake also increases with inadequate sleep, which is explained by elevated ghrelin production, an appetite hormone. Consequently, inadequate sleep has been linked to higher body mass index (BMI).5 9 Poor sleep has also been associated with insulin resistance, leading to increased risk of diabetes.10 Poor sleep may also be associated with an increased risk of cancerous tumour development.5

University students often face psychological and lifestyle adjustments as they transition to adulthood, impacting their habits, coping skills and well-being. Over half of college students experience poor sleep quality, and sleep issues such as insomnia, difficulties initiating sleep and/or maintaining sleep are frequent among them.11 12 Sleep quality, which is related to sleep hygiene, has been found to be poor in this population across different countries. In American cohorts, it was 27%13 while in Asian cohorts, it was approximately 36%,14 going as high as 69%.15

Among university students, medical students are one subset that seems particularly susceptible to sleep problems, perhaps due to the length and complexity of their studies and the high level of stress they are under.16 According to reviews, 52%–55% of the medical students reported poor sleep quality.17 18 Spanning across continents, it was 65.1% in Europe, 59.9% in America, 54.5% in Africa, 47.4% in Asia and 30.5% in Oceania.

Long-term use of benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine hypnotic medications with low addictive potential is commonly used for induction and maintenance of sleep. However, regular use of benzodiazepines can compromise quality of life by contributing to tolerance, dependency and toxicity.19 20 This situation promotes exploring alternative non-pharmacological interventions to improve sleep quality.

One well-recognised non-pharmacological treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). Despite being the first-line treatment, CBT remains underused which can be attributed to both patient-centred and system-based factors. The time and expense involved are patient-related barriers, and the scarcity of qualified CBT for insomnia specialists is a significant system-based challenge.21 22

Owing to these challenges, one explores other non-pharmacological interventions that can aid in enhancing the quality of sleep. An example of such an intervention is Yoga Nidra, which is a form of pratyahara (sensory withdrawal) technique that is both simple and accessible. The fundamental principles of practising Yoga Nidra were outlined in the book by Swami Satyananda Saraswati, a renowned teacher from the Bihar School of Yoga in Munger, Bihar, India. He described Yoga Nidra as a ‘systematic method of inducing complete physical, mental and emotional relaxation, where relaxation is attained by turning inward, away from external experiences.’23 Often referred to as a ‘yogic power nap,’ it aids in restoration and conservation of energy by cooling the body following daily activities.24 The practice follows a structured sequence of steps that initially require conscious effort but gradually become more intuitive and automatic with experience.25 The steps in Yoga Nidra are mentioned in Box 1.

Yoga Nidra has been studied as a therapeutic intervention for various medical conditions. Its ease of practice has helped in its acceptance as a treatment option for many ailments such as in managing menstrual abnormalities,26 combat-related post-traumatic stress disorder,27 anxiety and depression,25 26 28 and diabetes,29 and it has also been studied for its effect on sleep.22 24 30 31

Rationale

Yoga Nidra, a structured relaxation technique, has demonstrated potential in improving sleep quality by reducing stress, improving autonomic regulation and promoting deep relaxation. A standard Yoga Nidra practice lasts about 30 min. Medical students rarely have time for relaxing techniques due to their busy schedules, demanding coursework and erratic sleep patterns. Given that a standard Yoga Nidra session typically lasts around 30 min, it may be difficult for them to incorporate into their daily routine.

To make Yoga Nidra more accessible within their busy schedules, a shortened 12 min Hindi version could be highly beneficial. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first empirical investigation in India evaluating the efficacy of short-duration Yoga Nidra for improving sleep quality in students at a tertiary healthcare centre in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand.

Study objective

This trial aims to evaluate and compare the efficacy of a 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention in improving sleep quality among medical undergraduate students at a tertiary healthcare centre in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand, India. The results will be compared with those of a control group which will receive a 4-week sleep education.

Research questions

What is the efficacy of a 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention compared with sleep education on sleep quality among medical students with poor sleep quality?

Hypotheses

We hypothesise that compared with sleep education, participation in a 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention will be associated with improved sleep quality. We also hypothesise that participation in a 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention will have improvements in all physiological and biochemical parameters.

Methods and analysis

Trial design

We propose a two-group parallel randomised controlled trial (RCT). After participants complete their baseline assessment, they will be randomly allocated to one of the two groups: (1) the 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention group or (2) the 4-week sleep education control group. This study protocol follows the ‘Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials 2013,’ recommended guidelines for a clinical trial protocol32 (figure 1).

Trial setting

This trial will be conducted at a tertiary healthcare centre in Rishikesh, Uttarakhand, India. The Yoga Nidra intervention will take place in the Department of Community & Family Medicine at the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Rishikesh, facilitated by a trained yoga therapist.

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility screening will be conducted by a trained research officer (RO). To be eligible for participation in this trial, participants must be aged 18 years or older and currently enrolled as undergraduate students at AIIMS, Rishikesh. Additionally, the participants must have a Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) score >5.

Interventions

Yoga Nidra

Participants randomly allocated to the Yoga Nidra group will receive 4-week Yoga Nidra sessions at the Department of Community & Family Medicine. The sessions will be adjusted to the timings of the college schedule of students. Participants will be guided to attend three sessions per week with a trained yoga therapist. The Yoga Nidra audio adapted from Esther et al.31 has been translated to Hindi and subsequently validated. The audio includes the following stages:

The Yoga Nidra audio will be pre-recorded with a duration of 12 min. The session will be conducted in a soundproof room, with minimal ambient lighting during the daytime. The participant will lie on an 8 mm thick yoga mat, remaining in Shavasana (corpse pose) throughout the session, with palms facing upwards.

Sleep education (control)

Participants randomly assigned to the control group will receive reminders three times in a week for 4 weeks regarding sleep hygiene education. This education will cover healthy sleep practices and urge the participants to adhere to guidelines that improve their sleep quality (eg, maintain a regular sleep schedule, avoid caffeine, regular exercise and remove noise from the surroundings during sleep).33 Reducing the possibility of the Hawthorne effect—that is, making sure all participants receive the same amount of attention—is one of the reasons for providing the sleep education on a regular basis. To minimise group contamination, control group participants will be advised not to participate in any form of yoga or meditation practices during the 4-week intervention.

Measures to maintain adherence

Every week an RO will make contact with each Yoga Nidra group participant to discuss any issues related to adherence (eg, motivation). The RO will follow-up at a 2-week mid-intervention to assess adherence and/or adverse events, if any. Attendance of the Yoga Nidra group participants will be recorded after each session.

Primary outcome

Sleep quality will be assessed using PSQI questionnaire. PSQI is a widely used and effective tool for measuring the sleep patterns and quality in adults. It covers seven components: (1) subjective sleep quality; (2) sleep latency; (3) sleep duration; (4) habitual sleep efficiency; (5) sleep disturbances; (6) use of sleep medication; and (7) daytime dysfunction. The seven component scores are summed to obtain a global PSQI score which ranges from 0 to 21. Poor sleep quality is indicated by higher scores; a score >5 indicates significant sleep disturbances.

Sample size

In the absence of published RCTs on Yoga Nidra for college students, the power calculation was based on data from RCT examining the effect of Yoga Nidra on sleep quality among chronic insomnia patients.34 A sample size of 76 participants per group would provide a power of 90% (two-tailed α=0.05) detecting an effect size of 0.53 on the primary outcome (PSQI). Expecting a dropout rate of 10% and 1:1 allocation, the total number of participants estimated at baseline is 160.

Recruitment

Detailed information about the trial will be provided to the potential participants before enrolment that guarantees the recruitment of suitable candidates for the trial. The research team will be responsible for the recruitment process. To achieve adequate participant enrolment, direct communication through faculty and student representatives will also be used to raise awareness. The trial will be announced in classes and a Google Form link containing the screening questionnaire will be shared with the students. The link will contain general information about the trial, including its purpose. Participants will be provided with a consent agreement with assurance of confidentiality that the personal information will be kept secure and used solely for the intended purpose of the study.

Following the consent agreement, the detailed screening questionnaire will be made available to the participants which includes the following sections: demographic data, PSQI, medical history, ASSIST and submit section. Based on the screening results, participants will be recruited in either of the two groups. Participants with PSQI scores >5, and ASSIST category with no intervention and brief intervention will be recruited in the study.

Randomisation and blinding

The principal investigator will generate a randomisation list using an online software (2025 GraphPad). The allocation sequence, while maintaining confidentiality, will be transferred into sealed opaque envelopes by a third party who is not involved in the study. Sealed opaque envelopes will be opened by the RO immediately after baseline assessments. Participants will be told not to discuss group assignment with follow-up assessors. The outcome assessors are blinded to group allocation to minimise bias and maintain the integrity of the data collection process.

Data collection

Baseline assessment

This assessment will be performed by the RO prior to group allocation. Baseline characteristics will include data such as age, gender, religion, year of study, marital status, screentime usage (total screentime, laptop/tablet time, social media usage and screentime before sleep), PSQI, HRV, RR, PR, BMI, BP, anxiety disorder and depressive disorder, and collecting samples for RBS, lipid profile, IL-6 and salivary cortisol.

Follow-up assessments

All the participants will be followed up at the end of the first week, second week and end of intervention. Assessments at the end of the first week will include HRV data, RR, PR, BMI, BP, RBS and salivary cortisol. RBS levels at the end of the first week will be assessed by the finger prick method by a glucometer (On Call Plus). Assessments at the end of the second week will include HRV data, RR, PR, BMI, BP and salivary cortisol. Endline post-intervention assessment will include PSQI, HRV, RR, PR, BMI, BP, anxiety disorder, depressive disorder and levels of RBS, lipid profile, IL-6 and salivary cortisol.

Data management

A unique study identifier will be allocated to each of the study participants at inclusion. MS Excel worksheet will be used to store data. Authorised personnel only will be allowed to handle the collected data.

Statistical methods

All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat approach, meaning all participants assigned to a specific intervention group will be included, regardless of adherence to the protocol. The data will be analysed using SPSS V.26. The sample will be described using descriptive statistics such as mean, SD, median, minimum, maximum and range. The Shapiro-Wilk test will be used to assess data normality. Analysis will follow standard methods for RCTs comparing the two groups (Yoga Nidra intervention and control). The hypotheses relating to intervention efficacy will be tested using general linear models to assess changes over time within and between groups. Additionally, regression analyses will be conducted to identify predictors of treatment outcomes. Non-parametric methods will be used for comparisons in cases where data exhibit substantial skewness.

Assessment of harms

All the participants will be advised to notify the study coordinator in the event that they encounter any adverse events. If there are any adverse events, the principal investigator will decide whether to discontinue the study or the participant. Furthermore, participants have the right to withdraw from the study at any point in time.

Patient and public involvement

Medical students were consulted regarding the questionnaire and provided feedback. Based on their insights, questions were revised to enhance clarity and comprehension. At this stage, no additional student involvement is planned for the project.

Ethics and dissemination

The study obtained approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (AIIMS, Rishikesh) on 27 May 2022 (AIIMS/ie,C/22/231) and the trial has been prospectively registered in Clinical Trials Registry-India: CTRI/2022/07/044426. Informed consent (online supplemental material) will be obtained from students who choose to participate, ensuring their voluntary involvement in the project. The opportunity to withdraw participation at any moment, confidentiality and voluntariness will all be made explicit. In order for their non-identified data to be included in aggregate analyses for publication in a scientific journal, participants will give written informed consent. No identifying or personal information of any kind will be shared. The results of the study will be presented to stakeholders at conferences and seminars, and published in scientific journals.

Discussion

This paper outlines the background and design of a RCT aimed at evaluating the efficacy of a short 4-week Yoga Nidra intervention in comparison to sleep education on sleep quality among medical students in a tertiary healthcare centre in Uttarakhand, India. To the best of our knowledge, this trial will be the first to evaluate the effects of 12 min Yoga Nidra for sleep quality among medical students. The findings from this study will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals as well as national and international academic conferences.

The 12 min Yoga Nidra can be easily integrated into the busy schedule of the everyday life of medical students. If Yoga Nidra proves to be effective in improving sleep quality among medical students, it could provide a practical, non-pharmacological solution to address sleep disturbances, helping students manage academic stress and irregular sleep patterns more effectively.

To enhance participant engagement and reduce dropout rates, the research team will implement motivational strategies and provide timely reminders to participants about the intervention and sample collection dates. Given that this trial is conducted in a single-centred setting, we must consider that it may not accurately represent a larger population, limiting generalisability to all medical students. Future studies should consider multicentred trials across diverse geographical locations or academic settings in order to improve applicability of the results. Another potential limitation of this study is the lack of long-term follow-up, and thus, sustained effects of the intervention over time cannot be assessed. Additionally, while the PSQI is a widely accepted and validated tool in sleep research, it relies on self-reported data, which introduces certain limitations. In particular, subjective measures such as sleep duration and sleep onset latency have been shown to correlate only moderately with objective assessments like actigraphy or polysomnography. This discrepancy may be due to individual differences in sleep perception or recall accuracy. Therefore, the possibility of response bias or misperception influencing the reported outcomes cannot be ruled out. Future studies may benefit from incorporating objective sleep measures alongside self-reports to strengthen the validity of the findings.

Trial status

Screening was initiated in July 2022, followed by the commencement of recruitment in September 2024. Data collection is estimated to be completed in September 2025.
