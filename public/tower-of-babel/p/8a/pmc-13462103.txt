Optimizing AI implementation for surgery: recommendations for infrastructure and deployment in the operating room
Authors: Ariana Walji, Caterina Masino, Stephanie Williams, Spencer Gable-Cook, Jimmy Qiu, Pouria Mashouri, Divya Kewalramani, Mayur Narayan, Tyler J. Loftus, Wagner H. Souza, Amin Madani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recent years have seen a surge in the development of Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies to enhance intraoperative decision-making and improve patient safety. Yet, real-world evidence on their implementation remains limited. This study evaluated the impact of AI deployment in the operating room across key implementation domains—usability, cost, and carbon footprint—using cloud and edge-based infrastructures. Twenty-three end-users (surgeons, residents, fellows, operating room nurses) from a multi-site teaching hospital in Canada participated in system usability testing, assessed with validated scales and open-ended feedback. Cost and carbon footprint were assessed based on operational metrics. Results showed greater deployment task completion with Cloud but increased physical strain, carbon emissions, and operational costs compared to Edge after 396 h of use. Despite similar usability ratings, participants suggested improvements to the Cloud setup and both systems’ interface. Findings provide evidence-based recommendations toward the implementation of sustainable, user-centered AI systems in surgical practice.

Introduction

Human errors in healthcare settings remain a major cause of preventable patient harm and death, with higher prevalence observed in specialties such as intensive care and surgery1. A recent study on over 1000 surgical in-patients from hospitals across the United States found that nearly 60% of adverse events were potentially preventable, while over 20% were probably or definitely preventable2. Of the many root causes of complications throughout the perioperative stage, errors in judgment and decision-making at the time of surgery are a key factor leading to suboptimal patient outcomes3,4. Although many protocols (e.g., surgical safety checklist) are employed to reduce the risk of surgical complications, the persistence of preventable complications highlights the need for new, adaptive strategies to improve surgical quality and patient safety5. There is therefore a critical need for innovative and scalable tools to optimize surgical decision-making and improve surgical outcomes.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has increasingly become integrated into the surgical domain, particularly through its ability to automate complex computer vision tasks using imaging data and real-time surgical video6. A growing number of applications have been reported, with algorithms developed for surgical instrument tracking, anatomical and pathological structure recognition, and objective performance assessment7–10. For example, GoNoGoNet is a deep learning model trained to delineate safe and unsafe zones of dissection during laparoscopic cholecystectomy11. It has demonstrated performance comparable to expert surgeons across multiple validation studies and may potentially reduce the risk of major bile duct injury during surgery12.

Despite the growing interest in leveraging AI tools for surgical guidance, previous studies revealed challenges in translating new technologies into the operating room (OR) environment13–15. These translation barriers are associated with a lack of consideration for: (1) workflow integration, (2) assessment with end-users, and (3) broad system-level impacts. First, integration of technology with existing healthcare environments and workflows is often ignored during the technology design process16,17. As a result, introducing such tools into the dynamic and complex environment of the OR faces resistance due to concerns over compromising patient safety and surgical efficiency16. Second, many robust technologies are designed without incorporating stakeholder feedback14, despite evidence that new technologies significantly impact staff workload and job satisfaction18. Insufficient human-factor-based assessment for new technologies may result in poor usability and increased human error17,19, furthering resistance to adoption. Third, the lack of consideration for broader system-level impacts (especially costs and environmental sustainability) often compromises effective translation14,20,21.

When it comes to AI tools to support safe surgical decision-making, current literature almost exclusively reports on model development and validation, with a paucity of studies addressing its real-world deployment and clinical implementation22,23. There is an urgent need to understand how these tools impact end-users in the OR and how to integrate them in a safe, effective, and sustainable way.

This study critically evaluates the experiences of expert surgical staff deploying AI in a high-fidelity simulated OR. Specifically, it compares two well-adopted and scaled infrastructures for real-time AI implementation, including Cloud and Edge computing systems, to investigate AI’s impact in the OR across three critical factors: (1) usability, (2) cost, (3) carbon footprint. Cloud operates through remote network servers and involves a web-based platform with a laptop for AI deployment in this study. Conversely, Edge operates locally in the OR and involves a platform that can deploy AI inference onto the surgical monitors. This study offers novel evidence on translational indicators that could potentially predict challenges and guide recommendations for structured, safe, and effective application of AI systems in surgical settings.

Results

Comparative usability testing of AI deployment through Cloud versus Edge systems was conducted in a high-fidelity simulated OR. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy served as the surgical use case for AI, along with the GoNoGoNet AI model for real-time inference in laparoscopic cholecystectomy11,12,22. A cohort of 23 representative end-users participated in system usability, user ergonomics testing pertaining to the different monitor setups, and user experience. OR nurses did not take part in the ergonomics evaluation phase. Costs and carbon footprint were also calculated.

Comparative usability testing

System usability

Participants achieved a higher completion rate for AI deployment tasks when using Cloud (median 100%, IQR 100–100%) compared to Edge (median 80%, IQR 80–80%). The median difference of 20% between Cloud and Edge was statistically significant (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, W = 3.0, p = 2.16 × 10−5).

Participants’ System Usability Scale (SUS) scores revealed greater usability for Edge (mean = 69.67, SD = 16.74) than Cloud (mean = 65.33, SD = 16.61). However, the paired mean difference between Cloud and Edge of −4.3 (SD = 16.29) was not statistically significant (paired t-test, t(22) = −1.28, p = 0.214). User ergonomics

Participants reported higher workload scores with Cloud for all National Aeronautics and Space Administration-Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) subcategories compared to Edge. Significant mean differences between Cloud and Edge were found in the “physical” (mean difference = 17.33, SD = 14.38, paired t-test, t(14) = 4.670, p value = 0.0004) and “frustration” subcategories (mean difference = 14.33, SD = 15.91, paired t-test, t(14) = 3.489, p value = 0.0036). There were no statistically significant differences in the “mental”, “temporal”, “performance”, and “effort” subcategories (all p > 0.05). User experience

All participants provided feedback about their overall experience of each system. Three participants did not provide suggestions for the improvement of Cloud, whereas two participants did not provide suggestions for Edge.

Qualitative data were obtained in line with well-established frameworks to guide and evaluate the implementation of interventions into real-world settings, such as the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR), which identifies five key domains: Inner Setting, Outer Setting, Intervention Characteristics, Process, and Characteristics of Individuals24. Our thematic analysis of user experience feedback following AI exposure led to the identification of eight key themes: setup, interface, workflow, visualization, usability, decision-support, effectiveness, and innovation concept. Similar analysis of participants’ suggestions for improvement revealed the same key themes (excluding effectiveness and innovation concept) in addition to ergonomics and scalability.

Regarding feedback related to user experience, the highest frequencies of positive feedback pertained to the Innovation Concept of Cloud (9) and Edge (14), where participants noted: “[Enjoyed the real-time feedback [with Cloud]” and “[The Edge system is] very sophisticated”. Conversely, Cloud received the greatest frequency of negative responses regarding its Workflow (5), where another participant noted: “it’s distracting having to look outside the visual field”. In comparison, Edge received the greatest frequency of negative feedback regarding its Interface (5), where a participant mentioned: “improve flow [of mobile app] from selecting model to [model] activation”. As for feedback on potential improvement, the Cloud system had the greatest frequency of suggestions pertaining to Setup (13) and Interface (13), where one participant suggested the need for “better integration with the existing OR setup” and another suggested: “provide compatibility to overlay view on active screen”. The Cloud system also received several suggestions regarding Visualization (7), where some participants commented on the need to “reduce flickering” pertaining to the model output. Conversely, the highest frequency of improvement suggestions for Edge was related to Interface (16), where one user noted: “the app needs work to make it more user-friendly”. Cost

The Cloud system at our institution operates through Amazon Web Services (AWS), which does not have specific upfront costs. However, if users or other institutions choose to purchase a separate smart device to deploy the web application in the OR, this may be considered an upfront cost. High-performance laptops can range anywhere from ~$1500 Canadian Dollars (CAD) to $3000 or more. In comparison, the Edge system has a single upfront cost based on the system hardware, comprised of several components, including a GeForce RTX 4070 GPU (Graphics Processing Unit), which costs approximately $810 CAD. Together, these components cost approximately $2561 CAD. With higher performance GPUs (e.g., NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation), these costs can go as high as $6800 US Dollars (USD) or more (https://marketplace.nvidia.com/en-us/enterprise/laptops-workstations/nvidia-rtx-6000-ada-generation).

Based on AWS monthly pay-as-you-go streaming charges, operational costs for the Cloud system include ~$4425.55 CAD per month for four Tesla T4 GPU servers, ~$172.83 CAD per month for the head node, and storage service fees of ~$0.07 CAD per month. Conversely, the Edge system is not affiliated with storage service fees, as processing and data storage occur locally. However, both systems include additional operational expenses for energy consumption. Using on-peak electricity rates in Ontario provided by Toronto Hydro in April 2025 of 15.8 cents/kWh (https://www.torontohydro.com/for-home/rates) and estimated energy consumption for the Cloud of 0.69 kWh per hour, the Cloud system costs an additional 10.90 cents per hour or $78.49 CAD per month. In contrast, AI streaming through the Edge system consumes 0.18 kWh per hour, which costs an additional 2.84 cents per hour ($20.45 CAD per month). When the Edge system is idle, it consumes 0.10 kWh of electricity per hour, thereby costing an additional 1.58 cents per hour ($11.38 CAD per month). Carbon footprint

Carbon footprint for AI deployment was calculated based on operational emissions. Electricity consumption intensities for 2025 reported by the Government of Canada indicate that Ontario produces 38 g CO2e per kWh of electricity (https://www.canada.ca/en/environment-climate-change/services/climate-change/pricing-pollution-how-it-will-work/output-based-pricing-system/federal-greenhouse-gas-offset-system/emission-factors-reference-values.html). This carbon emission grid includes large consumers such as hospitals with ORs, and hence was used to estimate the carbon emissions for deploying AI through Cloud and Edge based on their respective electricity consumption values. Given that Cloud consumes roughly 0.69 kWh of electricity per hour when running AI models, it emits ~26.22 g CO2e every hour. Idle energy consumption values are not publicly disclosed by AWS servers and thus cannot be calculated in our analysis. Edge consumes 0.10 kWh of electricity per hour when idle and 0.18 kWh per hour when running AI inference. This results in 3.8 g CO2e every hour when idle, compared to 6.84 g CO2e for every hour that AI is streaming live in the OR. Using reports from a multi-site study for operating room carbon emissions, a baseline for electricity-related emissions was established based on the Vancouver General Hospital’s grid carbon profile (23 g CO2e/kWh)25. That entails annual carbon emissions of 534,194 kg for energy consumption across 22 ORs (including lighting, plug loads, HVAC, etc.), which equates to approximately 2.8 kg CO2e per hour per OR. Relative to this baseline, Cloud AI deployment represents ~0.9% of emissions per OR, whereas Edge represents ~0.13% when idle and ~0.24% when running AI.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the real-world implementation of AI in the OR across key factors associated with the adoption and integration of new healthcare technologies. Through usability testing of Cloud and Edge AI deployment across different surgical end-users, several key findings emerged. First, system usability results suggest that Cloud may be more intuitive based on higher task completion rates, although similarity in SUS ratings implies that participants perceive the systems to have comparable usability. Next, greater physical and frustration scores with Cloud during ergonomics testing, along with higher reports for neck strain, suggest that the system’s dual-monitor setup may impair surgeon comfort in the OR. Analysis of end-user feedback revealed positive user experiences with both systems, although most suggestions for improvement pertained to the setup and interface of Cloud and the interface for Edge. Cost analysis revealed lower upfront costs for deployment on Cloud, but higher costs over time, suggesting that Edge AI implementation may be more financially feasible for institutions. Lastly, carbon footprint analysis revealed greater carbon emissions from Cloud, suggesting that AI deployment through Edge may be more environmentally sustainable.

The first aspect of end-user testing examined the usability of both systems. Findings revealed significantly greater AI deployment tasks completed with Cloud, suggesting that Cloud may be easier for staff to navigate, minimizing the need for sophisticated training on specialized hardware. However, the lack of significant differences in SUS scores between Cloud and Edge suggests that end-users perceive AI deployment through either infrastructure to be suitable in the OR. By focusing on system usability testing, this study aimed to fill a recognized gap in surgical AI research26. This form of end-user evaluation is vital to ensure user-friendly and efficient systems that best support the needs of both healthcare teams and patients27 and to inform improvements to system design and setup prior to implementation26. Our data suggests that both systems are strong candidates for deployment of AI in the OR, although modifications to simplify the tasks required for AI deployment with Edge may improve its overall usability and facilitate the potential consolidation of AI-based assistance as a component of surgical standard of care28.

The field of surgery has a high prevalence of surgeon musculoskeletal disorders, with reports as high as 100% for surgeons performing laparoscopic surgery due to factors such as monitor positioning29. This risk is especially concerning when we consider longer surgical cases and the cumulative ergonomic strain over a surgeon’s career, given that musculoskeletal disorders are linked to increased healthcare utilization by surgeons, diminished physical capacity to operate, reduced productivity, and, in some cases, the need to modify practice patterns, limit certain procedures, or take temporary or permanent leaves30. It is therefore essential to consider ergonomics when implementing new surgical technologies to reduce the risk of additional sensorimotor strain during surgery, including minimally invasive procedures31–33. Ergonomic testing revealed significantly greater physical strain and frustration with Cloud than Edge, as well as significantly greater exertion in the neck region. This may be due to Cloud being set up with a secondary monitor for AI inference, which required users to turn their head away from their main monitor (Fig. 5). These findings suggest the use of a secondary monitor to deploy AI in the OR may not be ideal—regardless of whether the model is making inference on a cloud or edge device—given the potential for physical strain on the surgeon. In addition, alternating visual-cognitive attention foci could impair patient safety, as distractions in the OR have been associated with unintended surgical errors34–36. Providing feedback on the main monitor that can be turned on or off at the surgeon’s discretion, as was used with the Edge setup (Fig. 6), may reduce cognitive distractions and physical strain imposed by dual-monitor setups, thereby mitigating some of the risks associated with shared attention during surgical procedures37,38.Fig. 5Cloud ergonomics testing involved AI inference accessed through a web application on a laptop, positioned to the left of the surgical monitor displaying the same video feed.A medical manikin with ports to insert laparoscopic tools was placed on the patient bed for surgical simulation.Fig. 6Edge ergonomics testing involved an edge device (i.e., workstation) connected to the surgical scope tower, allowing for AI inference to be displayed as a visual overlay on the surgical monitor.A medical manikin with ports to insert laparoscopic tools was placed on the patient bed for surgical simulation.

Written feedback on user experience and suggestions for improvement to each system revealed several key themes. As shown in Fig. 3, both systems received the greatest frequency of positive feedback relating to the theme of Innovation Concept, although the Edge system received more positive comments for this theme overall. This suggests that participants were open to the novelty and unique applicability of each system’s use in surgery, which may support their integration into the OR. However, Cloud received the highest frequency of negative feedback regarding Workflow, with comments about the distractions caused by having the AI displayed on a secondary monitor in the OR. Distractors in the OR are a major challenge that can impair team efficiency and lead to error39 and therefore need to be addressed to mitigate cognitive overload, common among surgical staff40,41. These findings aligned closely with the suggestions for improvement with the Cloud system, which had the highest frequency of responses relating to Setup and Interface. Therefore, the advantages of being able to control the AI inference as an overlay on the main monitor without requiring its continuous display on a secondary monitor may better support the OR workflow. Furthermore, Cloud received negative feedback regarding Visualization, where users commented on the model output instability, causing distractions. These observations may be the result of Cloud having additional latency because data must travel between the local device and the remote cloud server over a network. In contrast, the Edge does not require remote network servers, allowing for smooth and consistent model output, which may better support end-users. Despite this advantage, the Edge system received the highest frequency of negative user experience feedback and improvement suggestions regarding its interface, where participants noted challenges with the mobile application to control the AI (Fig. 7). Some examples of improvement suggestions include changing the “eye” button to something more understandable to users, such as writing out the word “pause” instead. These suggestions are consistent with previous reports that challenges with the user interface are the most prevalent issue regarding usability testing of new healthcare devices27. There is an essential need to fully evaluate the costs associated with integrating new surgical technologies, as inadequate assessment can lead to underestimating the investment required for successful implementation43,44. Despite this recognized importance, implementation costs for hospital-based technologies are highly underreported45. Given that the OR is already one of the costliest environments in healthcare46, it is imperative to conduct a thorough cost analysis associated with deploying AI technologies into the surgical environment to ensure financially sustainable implementation. Based on our analyses, streaming through Cloud using AWS has no upfront costs and relatively low costs per hour, making it a strong choice for occasional AI streaming in the OR (e.g., temporary second opinion decision-support). While Edge requires greater investment in upfront costs for the workstation hardware, hourly operational costs are significantly lower than Cloud, likely due to the higher GPU demand intrinsic in Cloud. As a result, Edge becomes financially advantageous after 396 h of use (Fig. 4), which equates to roughly 33 surgical workdays, assuming 12 h of AI streaming per day to align with the average OR shift length. Assuming roughly 30-min periods of AI deployment per case (e.g., 30 min of decision support during the hepatocystic dissection of a laparoscopic cholecystectomy) and up to six cases per week, monthly operational costs for Edge are roughly 229 times less than those of Cloud, supporting that Edge is substantially more cost-effective over time. As the OR environment is becoming increasingly digitized and AI tools are being increasingly integrated into all phases of the patient care journey, many institutions will seek to invest in the integration of robust Edge compute infrastructure to support large-scale AI use for end-users, which may ultimately be more cost-effective at an institutional level.

Given the growing body of literature focused on the environmental impact of AI20,47, this study also explored the carbon footprint associated with AI deployment in the OR. Accounting for carbon emissions associated with AI deployment in the OR is vital to align with growing healthcare focus on environmental sustainability or “green AI” approaches48,49. From our analyses, the Edge system emits approximately 6.9 times less CO2e per hour than the Cloud system when idle and ~3.9 times less CO2e per hour when running AI inference. These findings suggest that AI deployment with Edge-based systems has a lower carbon footprint and is likely to be more sustainable for the environment in the long run, which aligns closely with reports from previous literature on edge computing as a strategy for reducing greenhouse gas emissions50. Energy consumption for the Edge system when idle can be addressed by unplugging the system overnight when elective surgeries have been completed to avoid unnecessary carbon emissions, as suggested in previous works20.

This study has limitations. First, the implementation analyses were based on internally developed Cloud and Edge-based systems. Therefore, the results may vary if an institution chooses to develop its own systems with different specifications (e.g., edge devices with different GPUs). Additionally, the differences in setup for the Cloud and Edge systems were specific to this study (e.g., the Cloud system involving a secondary monitor to deploy AI from the web interface). This setup reasonably reflects how Cloud-based systems are commonly deployed, often requiring a secondary laptop or monitor due to network latency and web-application incompatibility with OR monitors that are usually display-only51,52, whereas Edge systems, functioning as local hardware, are capable of overlaying AI outputs directly onto the surgical monitor52. However, it is possible for institutions to implement different setups depending on their workflow preferences. We thereby acknowledge that the findings pertaining to ergonomics for each system setup may differ depending on local institutional preferences. Despite these limitations, the findings lay a solid foundation to understand the general differences between two well-used deployment systems and different setups in the OR so that institutions can begin reflecting on how to best integrate AI. Furthermore, the sample size for certain role types was small due to difficulties in recruiting available participants, such as fellows. However, our total sample size of 23 participants was still above the minimum recommended sample size of 10–20 participants to detect usability issues53. Lastly, the ergonomics testing phase involved a short, simulated surgery scenario, which may not be truly representative of the physical strain or workload caused by the different monitor setups that may affect a surgeon user during a real case. However, this was intentional, as participants had limited availability and additional time was required for survey completion. The purpose of this testing phase was to prompt participants to consider how different setups could potentially cause strain in a real OR case. Future implementation studies could assess AI deployment across each system during live surgical cases to evaluate how well they integrate within the true OR workflow and address any limitations before full adoption.

Based on the key implementation factors assessed in this study, we included a checklist to guide institutions when preparing to implement AI into the OR (Table 2). Furthermore, we provided key findings from the study (Table 3) and considerations for selecting between Cloud and Edge deployment infrastructures (Fig. 8). Ultimately, the decision for the optimal deployment infrastructure will depend on an institution’s specific priorities and constraints. Altogether, this study lays the groundwork for understanding the multifactorial impact of AI deployment in the OR and provides evidence-based practice recommendations to guide institutions in safe, effective, and sustainable AI integration in surgery. Institutions seeking to integrate AI into their operating rooms must consider the diverse impacts it can have on surgical end-users and system-level factors (e.g., cost, environment) to ensure long-term feasibility and sustainability. Without addressing this concern, the field of surgery is unable to fully leverage the potential of AI without introducing risks to surgical workflow and imposing financial and environmental burdens. Methods

This study was approved and carried out in accordance with institutional guidelines for a Quality Improvement study at University Health Network (UHN) (ID# QIRC 24—0998). Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study design overview

This prospective post-only study consisted of a single 30-min visit (15 min Cloud system testing, 15 min Edge system testing). Comparative usability testing was conducted in a high-fidelity simulated OR to evaluate system usability, ergonomics, and user experience pertaining to AI deployment with the Cloud (version 1.0) and Edge (version 1.0) systems developed at University Health Network (UHN) in Toronto, Canada. For study purposes, an open-source laparoscopic cholecystectomy was used in both Edge and Cloud scenarios as the use case for AI deployment. Both systems were also configured with the GoNoGoNet AI model, which provides real-time feedback as a colored overlay on the study video (“green” for safe dissection zone, “red” for unsafe dissection zone)11,12,22. Post-study, participants were asked to complete surveys regarding the usability of each system, although nurses were not required to complete the section on ergonomics.

Participants

The study recruitment period was from January 2025 to March 2025. Representative end-users (surgeons, fellows, trainees, and OR nurses) of any age, sex, gender, and experience level were recruited from Toronto General Hospital and Toronto Western Hospital. Participants were recruited through an email to the hospital staff distribution lists.

Experimental setup

High-fidelity simulated operating room

The simulated OR consisted of items found in a standard OR for laparoscopic procedures, including a surgical scope tower, high-resolution laparoscopic monitors, and a patient bed54. The study video of the laparoscopic cholecystectomy procedure was displayed on the surgeon’s monitor. The Cloud and Edge systems were both set up and deployed in this OR environment during user testing.

AI deployment systems

Cloud computing system

The first intervention is a Cloud computing system which operates through remote network servers. This study employs a web-accessible platform running on four independent NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPUs on our institution’s cloud with the following test performance: 28.05 frames per second (FPS). The display latency of 37.17 ms combined with network latency of 64.50 ms results in total end-to-end latency of 101.68 ms when running live AI inference. This study employed a laptop (13-inch Macbook Pro with macOS 14.3.1) to run the web application for AI inference, as shown in Fig. 5.

Edge computing system

The second intervention is an Edge-based system, operating through local graphical processing units (GPUs). The Edge system pipeline consists of three parts (Fig. 9): (1) a user interface tablet with a mobile application for selecting and deploying AI (Fig. 7), (2) a high-performance moveable workstation with NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4070 for real-time processing, (3) a sterile field monitor (integrated with the scope tower) to display the AI inference. The study’s Edge system test performance results are as follows: 19.02 FPS with a display latency of 52.57 ms for running real-time AI inference. Procedure

To minimize order effects, the order for testing each system was counterbalanced for participants (e.g., Participant 1 tests Cloud then Edge, Participant 2 tests Edge then Cloud, etc.).

A research coordinator provided all participants with a standard overview of each AI system before guiding them through a series of familiarization tasks to deploy AI inference on the study video. Assistance was not provided to the user unless they were unable to advance to the next task independently. Except for nurses, all participants engaged in an additional study phase on ergonomics, which involved a 5-min simulated laparoscopic surgery. Participants were instructed to emulate the surgical actions in the study video using a mock laparoscopic setup (Figs. 5 and 6). Participants were able to interact with the AI inference at their discretion. For Cloud testing, this involved looking over to the laptop, which displayed the synchronized study video with the AI model feedback. For Edge testing, participants had to request that the AI overlay on their main monitor be turned on or off at their discretion.

Following the simulation, all enrolled participants completed a post-study survey on the overall usability experience associated with each system. Surgeons, fellows, and residents had an additional survey focused on ergonomics to inform physical strain and perceived workload in response to interactions with each system’s monitor setup.

Outcome measures

System usability

The usability of each AI system was assessed with deployment task completion rates and the SUS. Task completion rates are often used in usability studies to assess the effectiveness of a system and are expressed as the proportion or percentage of tasks completed over all tasks attempted within a given time period55. The SUS consists of ten multiple-choice questions ranked on a five-point Likert scale. Score calculations for SUS were computed following standard procedures as described elsewhere56.

User experience

Following exposure to AI models, participants provided written feedback on user experience and suggestions for improvement. The feedback content was analyzed using a thematic approach for the identification of themes that should be prioritized towards successful implementation, in a similar fashion to well-established frameworks such as the CFIR24. Frequency counts were used to analyze the number of responses pertaining to each theme for user experience feedback and suggestions for improvement.

Ergonomics

The impact of AI on surgical staff ergonomics was assessed with the NASA-TLX and Borg CR-10 rating scale. NASA-TLX uses a 100-point scale ranging from 0 (low workload) to 100 (high workload), and assesses six subcategories: mental demand, physical demand, temporal demand, performance, effort, and frustration57. Borg CR-10 is a category-ratio scale where users can rate their perception of physical exertion in relation to a given task on a scale ranging from 0 (nothing at all) to 10 (maximal)58, which was provided to participants alongside a body map to rank the intensity of perceived exertion to respective body parts59.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro–Wilk testing was employed to assess the normality of the distribution of quantitative data. Given the paired design of the study, significance testing employed the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed data and a paired t-test for normally distributed data. All statistical analyses were performed using Python software (version 3.10.11) and libraries such as Pandas, NumPy, and SciPy for data processing. The Matplotlib library was also used for data visualization. As for qualitative outcomes, participant free-text responses were manually segmented into distinct ideas. Next, two independent reviewers categorized the ideas into distinct themes. Feedback related to user experience were further classified as positive or negative. Once consensus was reached on the themes for each participant response, frequency counts were used to quantify the number of participants who provided feedback for each theme. Frequency distribution visualizations were also generated using Matplotlib.
