Novel specific anti‐ESM1 antibodies overcome tumor bevacizumab resistance by suppressing angiogenesis and metastasis
Authors: Nannan Kang, Buxi Fan, Yao Sun, Peilin Xue, Yu Liu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractSuppressing tumors through anti‐angiogenesis has been established as an effective clinical treatment strategy. Bevacizumab, a monoclonal antibody, is commonly used in various indications. However, two major challenges limit the long‐term efficacy of bevacizumab: drug resistance and side effects. Bevacizumab resistance has been extensively studied at the molecular level, but no drug candidates have been developed for clinical use to overcome this resistance. In a previous study conducted by our team, a major finding was that high expression of ESM1 in bevacizumab‐resistant tumors is associated with an unfavorable response to treatment. In particular, an increase in ESM1 expression contributes to heightened lung metastasis and microvascular density, which ultimately decreases the tumor's sensitivity to bevacizumab. In contrast, the silencing of ESM1 results in reduced angiogenesis and suppressed tumor growth in tumors resistant to bevacizumab. We put forward the hypothesis that targeting ESM1 could serve as a therapeutic strategy in overcoming bevacizumab resistance. In this study, a variety of anti‐ESM1 antibodies with high affinity to human ESM1 were successfully prepared and characterized. Our in vivo study confirmed the establishment of a bevacizumab‐resistant human colorectal cancer model and further demonstrated that the addition of anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies to bevacizumab treatment significantly improved tumor response while downregulating DLL4 and MMP9. In conclusion, our study suggests that anti‐hESM1 monoclonal antibodies have the potential to alleviate or overcome bevacizumab resistance, thereby providing new strategies and drug candidates for clinical research in the treatment of bevacizumab‐resistant colorectal cancer.Keywords: anti‐angiogenesis, anti‐ESM1 antibody, bevacizumab resistance, colorectal cancer, DLL4

INTRODUCTION

Therapies targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) have been among the most effective approaches in the antiangiogenic class for the treatment of various cancer types over the past four decades. Antibody drugs such as bevacizumab and other VEGF inhibitors have demonstrated both safety and effectiveness in clinical trials, leading to their approval for use in cancer treatment. Bevacizumab was the first VEGF‐blocking monoclonal antibody to gain approval from the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of various forms of cancer, including non‐small cell lung cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, glioblastoma, renal cell carcinoma, and ovarian cancer. Clinical trials have demonstrated that bevacizumab can significantly prolong both overall survival and progression‐free survival in these tumor types, making it a valuable treatment option for patients with advanced or metastatic disease. 1 , 2 The benefits of using bevacizumab may be limited by development of drug‐resistant mechanisms through alternative pathways. Numerous studies have investigated the molecular mechanisms that contribute to bevacizumab resistance in various types of cancer, including lung cancer, ovarian cancer, colorectal cancer, breast cancer, and glioma. 1 , 3 , 4 , 5 Several biological mechanisms could explain this phenomenon. For instance, tumor cells may release proliferative and invasive molecules, as well as angiogenesis‐activating exosomes, to promote the activation of angiogenesis signaling pathways. 6 Components from the tumor microenvironment, both cellular and non‐cellular, could also contribute to bevacizumab resistance. For example, hypoxia inducible factors such as HIF1α, 7 myeloid‐derived suppressor cells (MDSC), 5 tumor‐associated macrophages (TAM), 8 and cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAF), 9 may induce drug resistance. Therefore, novel drug regimens that combine bevacizumab with inhibitors of alternative pro‐angiogenesis pathways to counter bevacizumab resistance are currently under investigation. The drug combination therapy is regarded as superior over bevacizumab monotherapy to delay or overcome anti‐VEGF therapeutic resistance. It improves bevacizumab efficacy and prolongs the survival of patients.

It has been reported that the activation of the DLL4/Notch signaling pathway in multiple contexts is the underlying cause of tumor progression and contributes to the resistance to anti‐VEGF therapy. 3 Numerous clinical tumor models have demonstrated a strong correlation between DLL4 expression and tumor progression, as well as treatment resistance. Multiple studies have indicated that high levels of DLL4 expression 10 , particularly within tumor tissue, are positively correlated with tumor invasiveness and poor prognosis in patients with colorectal cancer, gastric cancer, and breast cancer. 11 , 12 , 13 The study found a major correlation between increased DLL4 expression in ovarian cancer patients 14 and increased Notch intracellular cleaved domain (NICD) in colorectal cancer patients with a poor response to anti‐VEGF therapy. 3 Moreover, studies have shown that inhibiting the Notch/DLL4/Hes pathway can lead to a decrease in angiogenesis and tumor growth in human colon tumors. 15 There are currently three bispecific antibodies targeting DLL4 and VEGF under clinical trials: OMP‐305B83, ABT‐165, and CTX‐009. 16 , 17 , 18 These antibodies have shown promise in blocking the DLL4 and VEGF pathways, resulting in efficacy against advanced solid tumors. Further clinical trials are needed to determine their safety and efficacy in cancer treatment.

Although DLL4 block heightened the effect of anti‐VEGF therapy, DLL4 antibody monotherapy caused serious on‐target off‐tumor effects, including hepatotoxicity and pulmonary hypertension during preclinical practice, which limited the flexibility of DLL4 mAb in combination with other angiogenic inhibitors. Recent studies from our group and others show that endothelial cell specific molecule‐1 (ESM1) is an upstream regulatory molecule of DLL4. 19 , 20 ESM1 is a secreted proteoglycan with a 20‐kDa protein core and a dermatan sulphate (DS) chain. Research has shown that the in vivo functions of ESM1 include the following. First, ESM1 can compete with ICAM1 for binding to LFA‐1, affecting the recruitment of circulating lymphocytes to inflammatory sites. 21 Second, ESM1 is involved in the progression of various vascular system diseases, such as cardiovascular disease 22 and chronic kidney disease. 23 Third, recent studies have identified the role of ESM1 in promoting tumor metastasis and angiogenesis, 24 including promoting tumor proliferation and metastasis though AKT/NF‐κB/Cyclin D1 activation 25 and VEGFA binding to endothelial cells VEGFR in a ESM1 dose‐dependent manner. 22 Finally, ESM1 has a VEGF‐independent role in embryonic development and tumor formation. ESM1ko mice have defects in the embryonic vascular system and die, 26 whereas overexpression of ESM1 increases tumor malignancy and radiotherapy tolerance. 24 However, despite extensive research revealing the role of ESM1 in promoting tumor metastasis and angiogenesis at tumor sites, no effective ESM1 inhibitors have been reported so far. Here we reported a series of monoclonal antibodies with high affinity for human ESM1 and investigated the effect of anti‐ESM1 mAbs combination with bevacizumab to overcome bevacizumab resistance in vitro and in vivo in established drug‐resistant models of colorectal cancer and triple negative breast cancer. We also investigated the molecular mechanisms involved. The goal of our study is to develop methods for improving the effectiveness of bevacizumab and to identify strategies for overcoming resistance to this drug. Our findings may lead to the identification of a promising target for cancer therapy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animal experiments

Mice were purchased from the Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University (Nanjing, China), and experiments were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of China Pharmaceutical University (Nanjing, China) (Ethical approval codes: 2021‐07‐014, 2021‐07‐013, and 2021‐12‐019). Female Balb/c nude mice (6–8 weeks old) were injected subcutaneously on the left side with 1 × 107 HCT116R cells or 3 mm3 tumor tissue (n = 6). The mice were treated with anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies 1‐2B7, 1‐6D11, 7‐9B10, and 7‐9C5 and/or bevacizumab (bevacizumab 10 mg/kg i.p., anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibody 20 mg/kg i.p. once a week), fluorouracil (23 mg/kg, i.p., three times/week) and PBS. Mice were killed after 2–3 weeks of treatment, and body weight and tumor volume were measured every 2–3 days.

Cell cultures and treatments

The human colorectal cancer cell line HCT116 and human breast cancer cell line MDA‐MB‐231 were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA) and were maintained under standard culture conditions in DMEM containing 10% FBS (Invitrogen) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

The primary HUVECs used in the study were provided by He Gou. The HUVECs were isolated from the umbilical cord and cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECM) (Sciencell) containing 10% FBS at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator.

Construction of ESM1 expression vector

To obtain esm1 cDNA, reverse transcription PCR was performed on HUVECs using the HiScript II 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Vazyme Biotech). The cDNA was then inserted into the multiple cloning site of the pCDH vector, and a 6 × His tag was added to the C‐terminal end for subsequent protein purification.

Expression of ESM1 with HEK‐293F cell line

To express ESM1, 293T cells were transfected with pCMV‐VSVG, psPAX2, and pCDH plasmids. After 24 and 48 h, the culture supernatant was collected and added to HEK‐293F in the presence of 4 μg/mL polybrene (Yeasen). After 48 h, the subcultured infected HEK‐293F cells were screened for stress tolerance through selection in 1 μg/mL puromycin (Yeasen) to obtain an ESM1‐stable expression cell line. Finally, ESM1 was purified using a Ni column (Thermo Fisher) affinity chromatography system and stored in PBS containing 5% (m/v) trehalose (Yeasen).

Preparation of anti‐ESM1 mAbs

Female BALB/c mice that were 6–8 weeks old were immunized with hESM1 mixed with complete Freund's adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich) and incomplete Freund's adjuvant (Sigma Aldrich) until the serum titer reached 1.28 million. The cells were then fused with mouse myeloma cells Sp2/0‐Ag14 using the hybridoma technology to generate monoclonal antibodies. After subclonal screening and ELISA identification, 12 monoclonal antibodies against human ESM1 were obtained.

SDS‐PAGE and western blot analysis

The purified protein antigens and antibodies were analyzed using a 12% resolving gel, and the resulting protein bands were stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. For western blot analysis, the protein samples were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a wet transfer system (Bio‐Rad). The membranes were then incubated with a 1:2000 dilution of anti‐ESM1 polyclonal antibodies (ab103590; Abcam) after being blocked by 5% nonfat milk Tris Buffered Saline with 20% Tween (TBST), followed by incubation with goat anti‐mouse IgG H&L (ab150117; Abcam) secondary antibodies. Finally, the protein bands were detected using a chromogenic‐based detection system (Tanon).

ELISA

The affinity of the anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies produced was determined through an indirect ELISA affinity assay developed by Beatty. To perform the assay, a microtiter plate was coated with 100 μL of ESM1 protein antigen, which was diluted to two concentrations ([Ag′] = 1 μg/mL and [Ag] = 2 μg/mL) with coating buffer. Phosphate buffered solution with 20% Tween (PBST) was used as the washing buffer, and 3% BSA in PBST was used as the blocking buffer. Purified anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies were added to the wells at varying concentrations after the washing and blocking steps. The secondary antibody used was anti‐mouse IgG H&L (ab6789; Abcam), and 100 μL of TMB was added to initiate the color development reaction. After completion, 100 μL of 2 mol/L H2SO4 was added to each well to terminate the reaction, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices). We used the detection results to generate a quartile curve and calculated the antibody affinity constant using the Beatty formula: Kaff = (n–1)/2(n[Ab′]t–[Ab]t), where [Ab]t and [Ab′]t represents the EC50 values of high and low concentrations of antigen, respectively. The parameter n is derived from the ratio of [Ag′] to [Ag].

MTT assay

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were cultured in ECM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. HUVEC were seeded in 96‐well plates at a density of 5000 cells per well and incubated for 24 h at 37°C under 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. After incubating for 12 h, we treated the cells with a combination of anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies (200 μg/mL) and bevacizumab (100 μg/mL, 200 μL per well) for 24 h. Subsequently, 20 μL MTT was added into each well and incubated in the microplate for 4 h. Next, we added DMSO to each well and mixed well. Finally, the absorption of each well in A490 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo Fisher).

Migration and invasion assays

Cells were seeded in the upper chamber at a concentration of 5 × 103 cells per well on DMEM‐diluted matrigel (50 μL) (Corning). Anti‐ESM1 antibodies were added to the lower chamber in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS. After 24 h of incubation at 37°C, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with crystal violet dye. The migrated cells were located on the lower surface of the filter, and the results were obtained by averaging the total number of cells from three fields of view.

Tube formation assay

Cells were cultured in DMEM‐diluted matrigel (100 μL) (Corning) at a concentration of 5 × 103 cells per well. VEGF and ESM1 were added to each well at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL as blank controls without cytokines. Then, bevacizumab (250 μg/mL) was added to each well individually, as well as in combination with 1‐2B7 (200 μg/mL), 1‐6D11 (200 μg/mL), 7‐9C5 (200 μg/mL), or 7‐9B10 (200 μg/mL). An equal volume of PBS was used as a negative control. After 24 hours of incubation at 37°C, tube formation in each group was observed under a microscope, and the number of branches formed per random 9–13 fields of view was counted.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Tissue sections that were fixed with 4% formaldehyde were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against CD31 (GB11063‐1; Servicebio) and VEGFA (19003‐1‐AP; Proteintech, USA). Following primary antibody incubation, the tissue sections were subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies, including goat anti‐rabbit IgG H&L (ab150077; Abcam) and goat anti‐mouse IgG H&L (ab150117; Abcam). After the incubation with secondary antibodies, the slides were washed and then treated with DAB (G1212‐200T; Servicebio) chromogen for 3‐5 min, resulting in a dark brown color. The tissue sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin and scanned at ×40 magnification using a Nano Zoomer 2.0 HT (Hamamatsu Photonics K. K.).

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as means ± standard deviation using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. All experiments were independently replicated at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Preparation of anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies based on the hybridoma technique

Endothelial cell specific molecule‐1 is a secreted proteoglycan whose glycosylated structure makes it preferable to use mammalian expression systems. To obtain the natural ESM1, HEK293 cells were selected as an ESM1‐stable expression cell line. At first, the human ESM1 gene was cloned by reverse transcription PCR (Figure 1A) and inserted into the pCDH plasmid (Figure 1B). Next, we constructed an HEK293‐hESM1 stable overexpression cell line and evaluated the ESM1 level by dot blot. Compared with HEK293‐control cells, ESM1 was highly expressed in the supernatant of HEK293‐hESM1 cells (Figure 1C). We further evaluated hESM1 by western blot. The bonds of human ESM1 with or without dermatan sulfate are shown in Figure 1D. Then, we performed immunization in Balb/c mice by hESM1 assisted with complete or incomplete Freund's adjuvant (Figure 1E). As shown in Figure 1F, the titers of antibodies from 10 mice immunized with the hESM1 were significantly higher than mice treated with PBS at dilution times of 1,280,000, which allowed the cells derived from murine splenocytes to fuse with mice myeloma Sp2/0‐Ag14 cells. Finally, 12 anti‐hESM1 monoclonal antibodies were obtained and purified by protein‐A chromatography (Figure 1G). To investigate whether the anti‐hESM1 monoclonal antibodies could bind to the natural hESM1 secreted by tumor cells, we collected the cell culture supernatant of the MDA‐MB‐231overESM1 cell line established in our previous research 20 used as natural hESM1 antigen. The results showed that 1‐2B7, 4‐3C2, 7‐7D7, 7‐9C5, and 7‐7C3 had comparable binding ability with ESM1 compared with the positive control (commercial anti‐ESM1 polyclonal antibody, Abcam), and the binding ability of 4‐3B4, 4‐2F4, 7‐2E6, and 7‐9B10 is higher and 1‐6D11, 4‐2F1, and 4‐5B3 is lower than the positive control (Figure 1H). Together, these data suggested that 12 anti‐hESM1 monoclonal antibodies were prepared successfully and had binding ability to native hESM1.

Anti‐hESM1 mAbs have high affinity binding to ESM1

Next, we defined the subtype of the antibodies. Antibody subtype identification experiments showed that all 12 anti‐hESM1 monoclonal antibodies’ heavy chains were IgG1, and light chains were κ (Figure S1A). To further evaluate the affinity constant of the antibody to ESM1, we performed non‐competitive indirect ELISA and calculated the affinity constant of each antibody. The results demonstrated that the affinity constant of 1‐2B7, 1‐6D11, 4‐2F4, 4‐3C2, 7‐9B10, 7‐9C5, and 7‐2E6 is 0.37, 0.45, 0.24, 0.61, 0.56, 0.89, and 1.3 nM, respectively (Figure S1B and Figure 2A), which had high affinity and was adaptive for neutralizing hESM1 in the tumor microenvironment. When measuring the affinity constants of 4‐3B4, 4‐5B3, 7‐7C3, and 7‐7D7, it was found that even using high concentrations of antibodies could not make the fitting curve reach the plateau phase, so the affinity constant could not be measured by ELISA. This also indicates that these antibodies have a low affinity for ESM1. Our preliminary research indicates that ESM1 can promote vascular formation by stimulating endothelial cell proliferation and migration. To screen antibodies for subsequent experiments, different antibodies were individually used to treat HUVECs cultured in medium containing ESM1, and their ability to inhibit HUVEC proliferation was observed. As shown in Figure 2B, the antibodies 1‐2B7, 1‐6D11, 7‐9B10, and 7‐9C5 displayed the best inhibitory effects. Therefore, they were chosen for further experiments. However, this result suggests that the efficacy of ESM1 antibodies alone is not particularly significant.

To investigate the binding of anti‐ESM1 antibodies to ESM1 within tumor tissues, we utilized a transwell chamber to co‐culture endothelial cells and bevacizumab‐resistant tumor cells to simulate the tumor microenvironment in vivo. This approach was chosen because endothelial cells and tumor cells are the main sources of ESM1 in the tumor microenvironment. First, flow cytometry was performed to assess the binding of anti‐ESM1 antibodies to ESM1 on both extracellular tumor cells and endothelial cells. As shown in Figure 2C, represented by the antibody 1‐2B7, when endothelial cells and tumor cells were co‐cultured, anti‐ESM1 antibodies could bind to the ESM1 secreted by both cell types. However, compared to endothelial cells, the antibodies exhibited a greater tendency to bind to ESM1 in bevacizumab‐resistant tumor cells. To investigate the selectivity of anti‐ESM1 antibodies in binding to ESM1 within the tumor microenvironment in vivo, we labeled tumor cells with Ki67, endothelial cells with CD31, and ESM1 antibodies with Cy3 fluorescent dye. This allowed us to detect the source of ESM1 antigen that the ESM1 antibodies bind to. We found that the anti‐ESM1 antibodies bound more to ESM1 in tumor sites and less to ESM1 in vascular sites (Figure 2D). These results not only suggest that anti‐ESM1 antibodies can bind to ESM1 antigens at different levels, including protein, cell, and tissue but also indicate that anti‐ESM1 antibodies may have a preference for binding to ESM1 in tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment.

ESM1 is increased in bevacizumab‐resistant colorectal cancer

An applicable model is necessary to measure the in vitro and in vivo activity of anti‐ESM1 antibodies. We have developed a triple‐negative breast cancer bevacizumab‐resistant balb/c‐nude mouse model in our previous study, 8 and the RNA‐Seq analysis of bevacizumab‐resistant tumor cells revealed that ESM1 in drug‐resistant tumor cells increased significantly and promoted metastasis and angiogenesis in vitro and in vivo. To determine the generalizability of the association between bevacizumab resistance and increased ESM1 across bevacizumab indications, we induced a bevacizumab‐resistant model in colorectal cancer based on continuous bevacizumab administration (Figure 3A). The tumor was transplanted when it became aggressive until bevacizumab failed to inhibit tumor growth. As shown in Figure 3B,C, bevacizumab treatment effectively suppressed the tumor growth in the first 3 weeks compared with control. Interestingly, there was no difference in tumor progression between bevacizumab administration and PBS treatment in 9–11 weeks. We further compared the tumor growth rate in mice treated with bevacizumab and PBS from 3 to 11 weeks, respectively, and found that the growth rate remained unchanged in the control group while it increased continuously in the bevacizumab‐treated group, indicating that the tumor acquired bevacizumab resistance (Figure 3D). We evaluated the CD31 and VEGF A in bevacizumab‐treated mice at time points of 3 weeks and 11 weeks. There is a significant increase in CD31 and VEGF A at 11 weeks compared with 3 weeks (Figure 3E,F). Finally, we evaluated the changes in ESM1 expression compared with control and immunohistochemistry, indicating that ESM1 was increased in the bevacizumab‐treated tumor (Figure 3G). Thus, those results indicate that bevacizumab resistance in mouse models is accompanied by increased ESM1 expression.

Anti‐ESM1 mAbs significantly inhibited bevacizumab resistance‐induced angiogenesis in vitro

Proliferation and migration of endothelial cells are the first two steps in angiogenesis, and metastasis from the primary site to other sites is a critical step in tumor progression. We have demonstrated in detail that overexpression of ESM1 significantly promoted endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and tumor metastasis in a bevacizumab‐resistant TNBC model. 20 To confirm the importance of ESM1 inhibition for remission or reversal of bevacizumab resistance‐induced tumor progression, we isolated bevacizumab‐resistant breast and colorectal cancer cell lines from bevacizumab‐resistant tumor‐bearing mice, and named MDA‐MB‐231R and HCT116R, respectively, which can be used as the cells model with high ESM1 expression (Figure 4A). To examine whether the anti‐ESM1 mAbs could inhibit bevacizumab‐resistant cells induced by endothelial cell proliferation and migration, we cultured HUVECs with cellular supernatant of MDA‐MB‐231R and HCT116R with or without anti‐ESM1 mAbs and bevacizumab and detected HUVEC proliferation and migration by MTT assay and wound healing assay. As expected, treatment by anti‐ESM1 mAbs combined with bevacizumab significantly inhibited the proliferation of HUVECs compared with the bevacizumab, and we further found that there was no significant difference between bevacizumab with the PBS‐treated group (Figure 4B,C). Similarly, anti‐ESM1 mAbs combined with bevacizumab significantly inhibited the migration of HUVECs compared with the bevacizumab (Figure 4D,E). In addition, the combination of anti‐ESM1 antibodies with bevacizumab significantly inhibited tube formation in endothelial cells, compared to using bevaxumab alone (Figure 4F). The activation of the DLL4‐Notch pathway plays a critical role in tumor resistance to bevacizumab, leading to the development of multiple DLL4/VEGF bispecific antibodies currently in clinical research. In our previous study, we found that ESM1 can regulate the high expression of DLL4 molecules. Therefore, we aimed to investigate whether anti‐ESM1 mAbs inhibit angiogenesis by suppressing DLL4 expression. Indeed, anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibodies significantly inhibited tumor DLL4 expression (Figure 4G,H), suggesting that anti‐ESM1 antibodies suppress bevacizumab resistance by inhibiting the ESM1‐DLL4 signaling axis.

Combination of anti‐hESM1 mAb and bevacizumab significantly inhibited the growth of bevacizumab‐resistant colorectal cancer in vivo

We then focused on the impact of anti‐ESM1 mAbs on bevacizumab‐resistant mice. It has been shown in our previous research that anti‐ESM1 mAb 1‐2B7 can significantly increase the sensitivity of bevacizumab‐resistant TNBC to bevacizumab. 20 A bevacizumab‐resistant model of colorectal cancer (CRC) in vivo was established and treated with bevacizumab, anti‐hESM1 antibodies (1‐2B7/7‐9B10/7‐9C5) combined with bevacizumab, 5‐fluorouracil and PBS (Figure 5A). Once the tumor diameter was over 1 cm in any group, all mice were humanely killed, and tumors were collected. 5‐fluorouracil or bevacizumab alone failed to inhibit bevacizumab‐resistant tumor colorectal tumor progression, while 1‐2B7, 7‐9B10, or 7‐9C5, compared with bevacizumab, remarkably suppressed HCT116R growth compared with the control (Figure 5B). Interestingly, it appears that tumors exhibit chemotherapy insensitivity after acquiring bevacizumab resistance in bevacizumab‐resistant models (Figure 5C). Moreover, there was no weight loss in mice administered bevacizumab and anti‐hESM1 antibodies relative to the control group (Figure 5D), which preliminary indicated the safety of the combination therapy. ESM1 neutralization also caused significant necrosis in the tumor (Figure 5E). We concluded that anti‐ESM1 antibody 1‐2B7, 7‐9B10, or 7‐9C5 can significantly enhance the efficacy of bevacizumab in a CRC bevacizumab‐resistant model.

Anti‐hESM1 antibodies significantly lessened tumor metastasis

A preceding finding of our study is that overexpression of ESM1 enhanced the metastatic ability of TNBC in vivo, which was explained by ESM1 promoted MMP9 and DLL4 expression, as well as epithelial–mesenchymal transition. 20 We detected the impact of anti‐hESM1 antibodies on tumor metastasis in MDA‐MB‐231R and HCT116R. As shown in Figure 6A, anti‐ESM1 antibodies significantly inhibited MDA‐MB‐231R and HCT116R cell invasion (Figure 6A,B) and suppressed MMP9 expression (Figure 6C,D). Furthermore, we selected the most effective antibodies, 1‐2B7 and 7‐9C5, in two different drug‐resistant models to investigate their inhibitory effects on tumor lung metastasis. As shown in Figure 6D, antibody therapy following intravenous injection of tumor cells inhibited the lung metastasis of breast cancer and colorectal cancer by 1‐2B7 and 7‐9C5, respectively. Therefore, we conclude that anti‐hESM1 antibodies have a unique ability to inhibit tumor metastasis by regulating MMP9.

DISCUSSION

There are several unresolved issues related to the use of anti‐VEGF drugs in clinical cancer treatment, including compatibility with other drugs, drug resistance, and treatment duration. Therefore, various therapeutic strategies have been developed for use in conjunction with anti‐VEGF therapy. Combining VEGF blockade with the inhibition of Ang‐2 or PD‐L1 has the potential to overcome resistance to bevacizumab therapy in glioblastoma and advanced hepatocellular carcinoma. 4 , 27 Clinically, bevacizumab combined with chemotherapy is often used for patients with advanced metastatic tumors. Both our previous studies and this research have demonstrated that bevacizumab‐resistant triple‐negative breast cancer and colorectal cancer may be insensitive or unresponsive to chemotherapy drugs such as docetaxel and fluorouracil. This could be explained by several factors, including genetic mutations in the tumor, changes in the tumor microenvironment, and alterations in the expression of certain proteins involved in drug metabolism and transport. First, ESM1 upregulated angiogenesis‐related factors to enhance microvessel density (MVD), causing tumors to obtain more oxygen and nutrient support, which accelerated tumor metabolism and growth. Moreover, ESM1 promoted tumor metastasis by regulating the expression of MMP9, thereby reshaping the extracellular matrix and resulting in the weakened efficacy of chemotherapy drugs. Furthermore, ESM1 could induce DLL4 upregulation, and high levels of DLL4 might induce a highly functional vascular network with more pericyte coverage to sustain the growth of the tumor. 28 We hypothesized that the vascular structures formed at the tumor site under the action of ESM1 might affect the penetration of chemotherapy drugs in the tumor.

DLL4 is an important molecule that induces angiogenesis and is associated with poor prognosis in oncological diseases. 29 , 30 , 31 Various antibody drugs targeting DLL4 have been used in clinical trials for cancer treatment since 20, but DLL4 monoclonal antibody cannot be used alone because of its severe toxicity. Therefore DLL4 blockade, used to enhance inhibition of angiogenesis by antagonizing VEGF, is presented with VEGF/DLL4 bispecific antibodies. Anti‐VEGF/DLL4 bispecific antibodies show positive preclinical and clinical benefits and a large reduction in side effects of DLL4 blockade alone. In this study, ESM1 is the upstream regulatory molecule of DLL4, and our study shows that the ESM1 monoclonal antibody has a good safety profile in mice. An antibody combination or bispecific antibody administration are both feasible therapeutic strategies to complement the treatment to enhance anti‐VEGF antibody resistance by inhibiting the DLL4/Notch pathway.

In conclusion, we prepared several monoclonal antibodies with high affinity against human ESM1 using hybridoma technology in this study and verified the effect of anti‐ESM1 monoclonal antibody combined with bevacizumab on the remission of bevacizumab resistance in bevacizumab‐resistant breast cancer and colorectal cancer models (Figure 5F), which provided research ideas and a basis for bevacizumab resistance research, and also provided potential candidate antibodies for alleviating or overcoming the issue of bevacizumab resistance.
