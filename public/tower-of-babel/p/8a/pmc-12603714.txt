Trends in the use of opioid and non-opioid analgesics after TBI with and without polytrauma
Authors: Andrew J Medvecz, Amelia W Maiga, Stacie Dusetzina, Oscar D Guillamondegui, Andrew D Wiese
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyze whether outpatient opioid and non-opioid analgesic prescribing after traumatic brain injury (TBI) admission has varied over time among commercially insured patients in the USA.Setting2016–2022 Merative MarketScan commercial claims.ParticipantsCommercially insured, opioid-naïve adults aged 18–64 hospitalized for TBI and/or long bone fracture (LBF).DesignRetrospective observational study.Main measuresWe identified patients filling an opioid, benzodiazepine, muscle relaxant, gabapentinoid or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug within 30 days of discharge by injury type (TBI, LBF, both). We calculated the difference in prescription prevalence from 2016 to 2022 among patients with TBI compared with the difference among patients with LBF to estimate the change in prescribing practices over time and then used modified Poisson regression to adjust for confounders.ResultsWe identified 15,928 patients with TBI only (39.4%), 22,912 patients with LBF (56.6%) and 1,615 patients with both TBI and LBF (4.0%). The proportion of patients filling an opioid was 45.4% after TBI, 75.4% after LBF only and 72.3% after both TBI/LBF. Among all patients, opioid fulfillments were lower in 2022 compared with 2016 (54.6% vs 69.9%; adjusted relative risk (aRR) 0.79 (95% CI 0.77 to 0.82)). Patients with all injury types observed a decrease in opioid prescribing (TBI/LBF: −19.0% (95% CI −27.5% to –10.6%); TBI only: −17.2% (95% CI −20.2% to –14.2%); LBF only: −13.7% (95% CI −15.8% to –11.5%)). Gabapentin use increased from 2016 to 2022 (6.8% vs 19.3%; aRR 2.80 (95% CI 2.52 to 3.11)) whereas muscle relaxant use did not change (14.5% vs 13.5%; aRR 0.93 (95% CI 0.85 to 1.02)).ConclusionOpioids remain a significant component of postdischarge pain management for hospitalized patients with TBI, with and without LBF, although the prevalence of opioid prescription has declined.Level of evidenceLevel IIIKeywords: Brain Injuries, Traumatic; Opioid-Related Disorders; pain management

Introduction

Opioids have a significant role in the management of pain for patients experiencing traumatic injuries. Acutely, trauma patients often receive opioid pain medication to allow for diagnosis and treatment of their injuries. Similarly, for patients presenting with traumatic brain injury (TBI), inpatient care often involves pain control with the use of opioid medications, either for pain directly related to the brain injury or other associated injuries which are concurrently presented.1 2 However, many patients with traumatic injuries, including those with TBI, can develop chronic pain after their initial hospitalization.2,5

There has been increasing attention on the opioid epidemic and the role of initial opioid prescribing on the risk of persistent opioid use and subsequent opioid use disorder. The addictive potential of opioids after one initial prescribed course is well documented.6,9 Surgical procedures and admissions for traumatic injury are the inciting event for many patients to fill an opioid prescription.10,14 Alam et al identified the potential for these short-course postoperative prescriptions for low-risk operations to subsequently progress to persistent opioid use.13 Further, there is some evidence that patients with TBI may be at increased risk for the development of substance use disorder (SUD).15 SUD is also a risk factor for head trauma and TBI.16 A study of American armed forces identified patients with TBI as having higher risk for discharge due to alcohol or drug use.17 The recognition of risks associated with even short-term opioid use has led to numerous quality improvement initiatives to decrease opioid exposure after operation, including through multimodal pain management seeking to decrease opioid requirements through concurrent prescription with non-opioid alternatives.18,20

Despite the recognition for potential harm, the prevalence of opioid analgesic prescribing among patients with acute TBI has not been quantified.21 We sought to characterize the trend of outpatient opioid and non-opioid analgesic prescribing after admission for TBI. We benchmarked prescribing practices for TBI against those for long bone fracture (LBF) to better understand the opioid prescribing rates of both populations. Contrary to TBI, opioid prescribing practices have been evaluated after orthopedic injury, and prescribing guidelines have been established to minimize opioid-related harms.21

Methods

We identified a retrospective cohort of patients hospitalized for TBI, LBF, or both TBI and LBF (TBI/LBF) using the MarketScan commercial claims database (March 1, 2016 through November 30, 2022). The cohort included adult patients hospitalized with a diagnosis of TBI (crushing injury or fracture of skull, intracranial hemorrhage/contusion) or LBF (humerus, femur, or tibia) identified by ICD-10 codes (online supplemental table 1).22 We chose LBF as a comparator group as opioid prescriptions are common for these fractures and there is a typical resolution of opioid requirement once fractures heal. We excluded individuals without continuous insurance enrollment or evidence of prescription drug coverage from 6 months (183 days) before admission date through day 30 after discharge to include only those for whom we had complete ascertainment of selection criteria and analgesic prescription fills during the study period. We excluded patients with evidence of pregnancy or prior TBI or LBF in the 6 months prior to the admission date for the qualifying TBI or LBF hospitalization (table 1). We also excluded individuals less than 18 years old and those with evidence of in-hospital death, as well as those with a length of stay greater than 14 days as patients with longer stays may have had more extensive injuries and have different pain management requirements at discharge due to the extended length of stay in the hospital. Furthermore, we excluded patients with evidence of an outpatient prescription for opioids, muscle relaxants, gabapentinoids, or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the 6 months before admission through day 1 before discharge to exclude individuals with prevalent analgesic use prior to hospitalization. As all of our variables and outpatient analgesic use were measured from MarketScan claims data, the lack of evidence for a covariate meant that the individual did not have a history of that condition or use of that medication, and so this information was not considered missing. As such, there were no variables with any missing data in our final analytic dataset.

We characterized the proportion of patients during each study year (2016–2022) with evidence of filling a prescription for opioids (excluding buprenorphine and methadone formulations), muscle relaxants, gabapentinoids, or NSAIDs in the 1 day before through 30 days after discharge by injury type (TBI, LBF, or TBI/LBF). To provide an estimate of the change in prescribing practices over time, we calculated the absolute difference in prescription prevalence in 2022 compared with 2016 by drug and injury type. Additionally, we used separate modified Poisson regression models with robust SEs to calculate the relative risk of filling a prescription for each drug in each year (using 2016 as reference) with 95% CIs adjusted for patient sex, age, length of stay, fracture and TBI type, as well as discharge disposition (discharged to home vs not discharged to home). In planned secondary analyses, we examined trends in the average morphine milligram equivalent (MME) of the initial discharge opioid prescription overall, by injury type and by use of each of the different non-opioid analgesics. Additionally, we examined intracranial injuries separately from other head injuries and characterized prescribing trends by predefined US regions in the MarketScan data (eg, Northeast, North Central, South, West). We also conducted sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our study findings to the inclusion of individuals hospitalized greater than 14 days and to the examination of prescribing patterns in the 1 day before through day 60 after discharge (restricted to individuals with continuous enrollment through day 60 after discharge).

Results

Among the 64,393 patients in our sample, 37.2% filled at least one outpatient prescription for one of the study analgesics of interest in the 6 months prior to hospitalization (figure 1). Among those that were analgesic-naive at the time of TBI or fracture hospitalization in our primary study population (n=40,455), we identified 15,928 patients with TBI only (39.4%), 22,912 patients with LBF (56.6%), and 1,615 patients with TBI/LBF (4.0%). Furthermore, 67.9% filled at least one of the four medication classes in the 30 days after discharge (LBF only: 78.6%; LBF/TBI: 77.5%; TBI only: 51.4%).

Study population flow diagram.

Patients hospitalized with TBI (with or without LBF) were more likely to be male and younger than patients hospitalized with LBF alone (table 1). Individuals hospitalized with both TBI/LBF had the longest length of stay compared with LBF or TBI only (5.5 days for TBI/LBF, 3.7 days for LBF only, 3.8 days for TBI only). Tibia fractures were the most common fracture type among both those with LBF only and TBI/LBF (50.5% and 41.1%, respectively).

Across the study period, the proportion of patients filling a postdischarge opioid was 45.4% after TBI only, 75.4% for patients with fracture only, and 72.3% for TBI/LBF. Non-opioid analgesic medications were less commonly prescribed than opioids in the 30 days after discharge regardless of injury type. Muscle relaxants, gabapentin, and prescription NSAIDs were filled by 14.9%, 14.2%, and 8.5% of those with LBF only; 13.5%, 9.7% and 6.3% for those with TBI only, and 26.1%, 23.4%, and 10.5% for those with TBI/LBF (table 1).

In the entire cohort, opioid use was significantly lower in 2022 compared with 2016 (54.6% vs 69.9%; difference: −15.3% (95% CI −17.1% to −13.5%)). The proportion of patients filling a discharge opioid was lower for patients with TBI only compared with LBF only and TBI/LBF throughout the study period (figure 2). Similar declines were observed across each injury type in 2022 compared with 2016 (LBF only: 67.0% vs 80.7%; difference: −13.7% (95% CI −15.8% to –11.5%); TBI only: 35.7% vs 52.9%; difference: −17.2% (95% CI −20.2% to–14.2%); LBF/TBI: 62.3% vs 81.3%; difference: −19.0% (95% CI −27.5% to –10.6%)) (table 1). The filling of muscle relaxant prescriptions declined only among those with TBI only in 2022 compared with 2016 (−3.2% (95% CI −5.2% to –1.1%), whereas there was a substantial increase in gabapentinoid prescriptions observed in 2022 compared with 2016 overall (difference: 12.5% (95% CI 11.3% to 13.7%)) (table 1). The prevalence of NSAID prescriptions increased only among those with LBF only (2.6% increase (95% CI 1.2% to 3.9%)), whereas the prevalence declined among those with TBI only (−1.8% decrease (95% CI −3.2% to –0.3%)). We observed similar findings among those with both intracranial injuries and other head-related injuries (online supplemental figure 1).

In the drug-specific regression models adjusted for patient age, sex, length of stay, fracture and TBI type, and discharge status, the relative risk of filling an opioid prescription was 0.79 times lower (95% CI 0.77 to 0.82) in 2022 than in 2016, whereas the relative risk of filling a gabapentinoid prescription was 2.80 times higher (95% CI 2.52 to 3.11). No significant change in the filling of a muscle relaxant or NSAID was observed in 2022 compared with 2016 (adjusted relative risk (aRR) 0.93 (95% CI 0.85 to 1.02) and aRR: 1.15 (95% CI: 0.99 to 1.32), respectively).

In addition to decreases in opioid prescribing prevalence over time, the median strength (measured in MME) of opioid prescriptions filled after discharge declined substantially across all injury types (median MME of 375 in 2016 vs 180 in 2022) and among those using opioids only or in combination with non-opioid analgesic classes (figure 3). Specific to those with TBI only, the median MME declined from 225 in 2016 to 135 in 2022 among those receiving opioid analgesics only, with similar declines among those filling both an opioid and non-opioid analgesic prescription (opioid and muscle relaxant: 300 MME in 2016 to 150 MME; opioid and gabapentinoid: 425 MME to 150 MME; opioid and NSAID: 225 MME to 97.5 MME; opioid and at least two other analgesics: 387.5 MME to 150 MME). Study findings were consistent in the sensitivity analyses that included individuals with a hospitalization length of stay greater than 14 days and that examined prescribing patterns up through day 60 after discharge. Trends were visually consistent across US regions for each analgesic type, though prescribing of opioids, muscle relaxants, and gabapentin was consistently lower in the Northeast region compared with other regions (online supplemental figure 2).

Discussion

The prevalence and dose of the initial discharge opioid prescription filled after hospitalization for TBI has declined over time. This trend may reflect numerous efforts that have been made in the community and nationwide. In the past decade, there has been increased recognition of the role that physicians’ prescribing practices have made on the opioid epidemic.23,26 The US Department of Health and Human Services has outlined a strategy for targeting opioid overdose preventions that is anchored by the primary prevention objective of reducing clinically inappropriate prescribing.27 There have been efforts for education programs among general and orthopedic surgery providers regarding appropriate amounts of postoperative opioid prescriptions.21 28 Data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention reflects a significant decrease in the dispensation rate in the USA.29 However, significant variability in prescribing practices remains among regional and economic disparities that result in continued high rates of opioid distribution by physicians.

Despite this decline in opioid prescribing in our cohort and the efforts nationwide to reduce opioid prescribing, we identified that opioids remain a significant component of post-discharge pain management in TBI whether used alone or in combination with other analgesics. Importantly, we also observed similar trends and median dose of the initial discharge opioid prescription regardless of whether only opioids were prescribed or whether they were prescribed in combination with other analgesics. These findings add to the very limited prior literature regarding the medication types that physicians are prescribing for acute management of pain after TBI. The prevalence of opioid prescribing after admission for acute TBI has had limited study as identified by a scoping review conducted by Starosta and colleagues.1 One group has identified that 41% of opioid-naïve patients and 87% of patients with preadmission opioid use requiring intensive care unit admission after TBI were prescribed opioids at 1 month after discharge, data that is consistent with the findings from our study.30 Notably, a significant portion of these patients had persistent use at 12 months postdischarge (19% opioid naïve, 56% preadmission opioid users). Even among patients with mild TBI, 16% of patients were being prescribed opioids for headaches 3 months after discharge.31 This lack of study has highlighted the need for data-driven prescribing guidelines regarding opioid dosing and multimodal analgesia after hospitalization for TBI.

Many studies have identified the development of chronic pain after TBI. Although many patients develop pain from injuries that occurred concomitantly with the TBI, a significant portion of patients report pain that is not injury-related.32 The mechanism for chronic pain after TBI is not well known, but many patients with a history of TBI in their lifetime report persistent pain as well as chronic persistent prescription opioid use.4 A study of American veterans from the Iraq and Afghanistan wars found patients with severe TBI sequelae were more likely to receive short-acting and long-acting opioids for chronic pain, and the effect was compounded with concomitant diagnoses for post-traumatic stress disorder and depression.33 If an opioid prescription is used for the initial management of the TBI, it may be these initial admission and subsequent prescriptions that are predisposing patients to the development of persistent opioid use.

We observed that the decline in opioid use over time coincides with subsequent moderate increases in gabapentinoid prescribing after TBI, with the largest increases observed among patients hospitalized for TBI/LBF. Prior epidemiologic literature identified a tripling of the gabapentin prescribing rates in some cohorts.34 Much of the literature regarding the efficacy of gabapentinoids has come from studies of perioperative outcomes, but results have been mixed. Hah et al identified a faster time to opioid cessation for mixed surgical patients randomized to receive gabapentin in the perioperative period.35 A more recent meta-analysis identified no significant analgesic effect for perioperative gabapentinoids as well as no effect on chronic postoperative pain.36 We did not observe a significant change in the use of muscle relaxers in our cohort over the study period. The benefits of these drugs in TBI have not been well-studied despite a dramatic rise in their prescribing in the past two decades.37 Much of the efficacy for muscle relaxers was seen in low back pain as an alternative to benzodiazepines,38 but they have also been found to benefit patients with acute pain with opioid reduction after rib fractures.39

Our cohort is advantageous because it provides data on prescription fills rather than on the prescriptions alone. The prescription fills data may provide a more accurate representation of what patients require for postinjury analgesia. Opioid fill rates vary from 40% to 79% with numerous factors influencing patients’ ability to obtain prescribed analgesics, including insurance status.40 41 This population is continually insured, so we think the opioid prescription fill rate approaches the prescribing rates and reflects the proportion of discharging physicians choosing to prescribe opioid and non-opioid analgesics.

There are several limitations to this study. This is a large, retrospective analysis of a commercial claims database that lacks the granularity of specific patient characteristics, injury details, and hospital events that may contribute to the need for more or less opioid prescriptions. We accounted for this variability by limiting the length of stay to 14 days as patients with longer stays may have had more extensive injuries, requiring more opioids at discharge, or whose longer time course led to less opioid requirement given the extended time from injury. This data set is limited to continuously insured patients between 18 and 65 years old. Although being uninsured is associated with greater risk for opioid-related death, we note that most opioid-related deaths are in patients with health insurance.42

Conclusion

Patients with TBI are being prescribed significant amounts of opioid medications after initial admission for TBI. Total mean dose of the prescribed opioids for TBI decreased in recent years and non-opioid analgesic prescribing increased. Additional research is necessary to analyze whether changes in opioid prescribing and the increasing use of non-opioid analgesic strategies have been associated with improved effectiveness in pain control or with reductions in opioid-related harms among patients with TBI.

Transparency, rigor and reproducibility statement

As this is a retrospective analysis of the MarketScan commercial claims database, no preregistration was conducted. The analysis plan was not formally preregistered. Due to a lack of similar previous studies and the article’s goal of evaluating trends in prescribing practices, no sample size calculations were made. Data were analyzed using SAS Enterprise Guide, V.8.2.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA) and Stata statistical software (release 18.0, StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA). The MarketScan commercial claims database was obtained from a third party (Merative Marketscan) and so data from the study is not publicly available. Analytic code used to conduct the analyses presented in this study is not available in a public repository, but is available by emailing the corresponding author.
