Molecular impact of NOTCH signaling dysregulation on ovarian cancer progression, chemoresistance, and taxane response
Authors: Kamila Koucka, Alzbeta Spalenkova, Karolina Seborova, Tereza Tesarova, Marie Ehrlichova, Ivona Krus, Petr Holy, Lukas Rob, Martin Hruda, Jiri Bouda, Alena Bartakova, Vendula Smoligova, Iwao Ojima, Lei Chen, Hersch Bendale, Marcela Mrhalova, Katerina Kopeckova, Pavel Soucek, Radka Vaclavikova
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) face high mortality due to late diagnosis, recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance. The NOTCH signaling pathway plays a critical role in cancer progression. This study analyzed NOTCH pathway deregulation in EOC patients and its response to taxane treatment in vitro and in vivo. In tumor cells of EOC patients, a significant upregulation of NOTCH1/3/4 and JAG2 and a downregulation of the NOTCH2 gene were found. The observed high levels of NOTCH3 mRNA were also confirmed at the protein level. In contrast, we observed a significant association of low NOTCH4 expression with the presence of peritoneal metastasis and shortened platinum-free interval. In the resistant in vitro cell line model, significant upregulation of NOTCH signaling pathway, namely NOTCH3, was observed after treatment with experimental Stony Brook taxanes (SB-Ts), with high efficacy against paclitaxel-resistant ovarian tumor cells. The administration of SB-Ts also caused NOTCH3 upregulation in an effective combination regimen with paclitaxel in comparison to paclitaxel alone and untreated control in the in vivo cell-derived xenograft mouse model of resistant ovarian cancer. Knockdown of the NOTCH3 gene caused higher sensitivity of resistant cells to taxanes, suggesting that NOTCH3-specific inhibition may potentially bring therapeutic benefits in resistant ovarian carcinoma. Based on our results, we suggest the NOTCH3 gene as a potential target for preclinical studies on resistant ovarian tumors. The current study also highlights the NOTCH4 gene as a potential predictive biomarker of therapeutic response in ovarian cancer.Keywords: NOTCH signaling pathway, ovarian cancer, chemoresistance, prognosis, dysregulation, taxanes

Introduction

Ovarian carcinoma ranks as the eighth most prevalent form of cancer affecting women on a global scale, its incidence is approx. 10.3 per 100,000 women [1,2]. The majority of these cases manifest as epithelial ovarian carcinomas (EOCs), with the dominant high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSC) histological subtype, which is the most aggressive, afflicting 70–80 % of all EOC patients [3,4]. Worldwide, the 5-year survival rate of women with stage 1–2 reaches approximately 70–95 %, while approximately 20–45 % of patients will survive their cancer for 5 years or more after being diagnosed. Due to the late diagnosis of ovarian cancer at advanced stages and many patients developing recurrent disease due to induction of chemotherapy resistance, treatment options are limited [1,5–7].

The standard management of EOC currently consists of a combination of cytoreductive surgery and adjuvant chemotherapy with regimens combining platinum derivatives, mainly carboplatin or cisplatin, with taxanes [8–10]. Among conventional taxanes, paclitaxel (PTX) is included in regimens for systemic chemotherapy of advanced ovarian carcinoma [8,11,12]. For patients with pathogenic BRCA1/2 mutations, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have been incorporated into the therapy of recurrent EOC [8,11,13–15]. Nevertheless, both intrinsic and acquired resistance of tumor cells hinder the clinical effectiveness of such treatment.

Multidrug resistance is a highly complex process, where classical mechanisms of action of platinum derivatives or taxanes are disrupted or altered by many factors. Among factors responsible for developing resistance to platinum derivatives and taxanes are alterations in drug targets, membrane transporters, or drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., CYP2C8, CYP3A4, CYP3A5), drug detoxification through cellular thiols lower sensitivity to apoptosis, alterations in DNA damage repair (DDR), and altered proliferation, summarized in [16,17]. Thus, new therapeutic options, targets, and compounds effective in resistant types of tumors are urgently needed.

Our previous work has shown that the new generation taxoids, “Stony Brook taxanes” SB-T-121605 and SB-T-121606 [18], exert high cytotoxic potency against human PTX-resistant ovarian cancer cell line NCI/ADR-RES in vitro. The high efficacy of SB-T-121605 and SB-T-121606 was confirmed in vivo—in murine drug-resistant ovarian carcinoma cell-derived xenograft (CDX) model, particularly for a combination regimen with PTX (1 mg/kg of SB-T and 9 mg/kg of PTX). The third generation SB-Ts are highly promising drug candidates for the effective treatment of resistant ovarian carcinomas and eventually other aggressive tumors, e.g., pancreatic carcinomas as recently published by us [19].

Deregulation of different signaling pathways including the NOTCH signaling pathway may also contribute to the development of resistance to therapeutic agents. The NOTCH signaling pathway is considered relatively highly conserved across all species. The canonical pathway is formed by four receptors (NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, and NOTCH4), three delta-like canonical Notch ligands (DLL1, DLL2, and DLL3), and two jagged canonical Notch ligands (JAG1 and JAG2). The binding of ligands to NOTCH receptors activates the pathway, which plays roles in somatogenesis, cell differentiation, and body development. NOTCH pathway disruption causes several disorders, e.g., CADASIL and Alagille syndrome, or pancreatitis [20]. In both solid tumors and hematological malignancies, the NOTCH pathway has mainly an oncogenic role [21, 22].

In ovarian cancer cells, several published studies demonstrated associations between overexpression of different NOTCH pathway members and drug resistance, although by different mechanisms of NOTCH pathway activation [23–26]. Connections of overexpression of NOTCH1/2/3/4, DLL3, and JAG1 with poor survival of ovarian carcinoma patients, advanced disease stage, or metastatic process were reported [27–29]. Members of the pathway are relatively frequently altered (approx. in 22 %, e.g. amplification of JAG1/2 or amplification/mutation of NOTCH3), mainly in the HGSC subtype [30]. A potential role of NOTCH1 and NOTCH3 in chemosensitivity to platinum-based therapy was observed [31,32], suggesting that the most promising targets from the pathway, after their functional confirmation in vitro and in vivo, can contribute to the improvement of therapy of resistant ovarian carcinomas.

This study focused on analyzing the gene expression profile of the NOTCH signaling pathway in a cohort of EOC patients with the aim of assessing their utility as molecular biomarkers of resistance and potential therapeutic targets. The next goal was to analyze the deregulation of the NOTCH pathway in ovarian carcinoma cell lines resistant to PTX after treatment with PTX and new experimental SB-Ts in vitro. Furthermore, we validated the results in the in vivo model of resistant ovarian carcinoma cell line-derived mouse xenografts (CDX). Finally, the functional role of the most promising target was investigated in the resistant ovarian cancer cell line model by its gene manipulation.

Material and methods

Patients

The current investigation involved the examination of tumor tissue samples from 149 patients diagnosed with EOC at University Hospital Kralovske Vinohrady, Motol University Hospital in Prague, and University Hospital in Pilsen, Pilsen, Czech Republic, spanning the period from 2009 to 2016. Additionally, 17 control samples of ovarian tissues without any morphological indications of ovarian carcinoma were included in the study. The control samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgery for reasons unrelated to malignancy. The tissue samples collected during surgery were subjected to histopathological analysis following standard diagnostic protocols. The samples were immediately frozen and stored at −80°C until the use.

The following clinical data were retrieved from medical records: age at diagnosis, FIGO stage, tumor grade, EOC subtype, expression of the proliferation marker Ki-67 as a percentage of tumor cells positive (only available for patients from Motol University Hospital), presence of peritoneal metastases, and tumor residuum after surgery. Clinical data included also systemic chemotherapy treatment (regimens, treatment period, and the number of cycles). Resistance status was determined as the platinum-free interval (PFI) in months, which represents the time between the last platinum dose and the detection of disease relapse, progression, death caused by malignancy, or last follow-up [33,34]. Overall survival (OS) represents time between the surgery and death of the patient. These details are in Table S1. Patients with PFI ≤ 6 months were considered platinum-resistant, and patients with PFI ≥ 12 months were platinum-sensitive. Twenty patients had PFI between 7 and 10 months and were classified as partially platinum-sensitive [35]. These patients were tentatively included in the resistant group.

All patients were provided with comprehensive information regarding the objectives of the study and gave written consent to participate. The study design was approved by the Ethics Committees of the University Hospital Kralovske Vinohrady, Motol University Hospital in Prague, and University Hospital in Pilsen, Czech Republic.

Chemicals

For in vitro experiments, PTX was obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MA, USA). Experimental Stony Brook Taxanes (SB-Ts) were developed and synthesized by the Ojima Research Group at the Institute of Chemical Biology & Drug Discovery at Stony Brook University in Stony Brook, NY, USA [36–38]. The second (SB-T-1216) and third generation (SB-T-121605, and SB-T-121606) SB-Ts were selected. The structure of these novel taxanes is shown in Figure S1 [39,40]. The taxanes were dissolved in DMSO to create stock/working solutions before use, with a concentration of 40 mM of the PTX stock and 10 mM of each SB-T stock. Working solutions were prepared just before use and the DMSO concentration incubated with cells did not exceed 0.5 %. For in vivo experiments, the infusion solution of PTX - Paclitaxel EBEWE 6 mg/ml - was provided by Ebewe Pharma (Unterach am Attersee, Austria).

Cells and culture conditions

The SKOV-3 human ovarian carcinoma cell line, sensitive to PTX (referred to as SKOV-3/SEN), was obtained from Cell Lines Service (Eppelheim, Germany). To develop a PTX-resistant variant, SKOV-3 cells were cultured long-term in a growth medium with gradually increasing concentrations of PTX. This process resulted in the creation of the resistant SKOV-3/RES cell line, which was cultivated in a final concentration of 300 nM PTX. Both cell lines were grown in McCoy’s 5 A Medium (PAN-Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany) supplemented with L-glutamine (300 mg/l), NaHCO3 (2.0 g/l), penicillin (100 U/ml), streptomycin (100 μg/ml), sodium pyruvate (1 mM), HEPES (15 mM), and 10 % fetal bovine serum. For the SKOV-3/RES cell line, 300 nM PTX was added to culture media. Cultivation was conducted at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5 % CO2. Resistant phenotype of SKOV-3/RES cells was previously validated by analysis of the expression of ABCB1 (P-gp), a major player in drug resistance, at both mRNA and protein levels [41]. Both sensitive (SKOV-3/SEN) and resistant (SKOV-3/RES) cells were passaged twice a week, up to the 40th passage. Regular checks for Mycoplasma contamination were performed using the MycoAlert® Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Lonza, Basel, Switzerland) and cell line authenticity was verified through STR analysis performed by Generi Biotech laboratory (Hradec Kralove, Czech Republic).

Cell viability

Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 103 into a 96-well plate and left to incubate overnight. The following day, the cells underwent treatment with different concentrations of PTX (30 000 – 0.03 nM for SKOV-3/SEN and 200 000–0.3 nM for SKOV-3/RES), SB-T-1216 (30 000–0.03 nM), SB-T-121605 (30 000–0.003 nM) or SB-T-121606 (30 000–0.003 nM). The cells were incubated with taxanes for a total of 72 h. Following incubation, cell viability was assessed fluorometrically using the Cell-Titer-Blue® viability assay (Promega) on the Infinite M200 plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Analysis of cell cycle

A total of 1 × 105 cells were plated onto a 24-well plate and allowed to adhere overnight. The next day, the culture medium was refreshed either with fresh medium (control) or medium containing different concentrations of PTX (30 nM for SKOV-3/SEN and 4500 nM for SKOV-3/RES), SB-T-1216 (10 nM for SKOV-3/SEN and 50 nM for SKOV-3/RES), SB-T-121605 (10 nM for SKOV-3/SEN and 30 nM for SKOV-3/RES) or SB-T-121606 (5 nM for SKOV-3/SEN and 10 nM for SKOV-3/SEN). After 24, 48, and 72 h of incubation, the culture medium was aspired and BD Cycletest Plus DNA Kit containing propidium iodide (Becton Dickinson, East Rutherford, New Jersey, USA) was added to each well [42]. Fluorescence readings were taken using the BD FACS-Verse flow cytometer (Becton Dickinson). For every sample, a total of 20 000 single-cell events were captured. The data were then analyzed with the BD FACSuite software (Becton Dickinson). All the measurements were conducted in three separate biological replicates.

Analysis of cell migration

The migration potential of SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES cell lines was monitored using an xCELLigence RTCA DP instrument with CIM-plates 16 (ACEA Biosciences, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Cells were serum-starved for 4 days in culture medium containing 5 % FBS, next 4 × 104 cells per well in serum-free media were seeded in the upper chamber of the CIM-plate 16. The lower chamber of the CIM-plate 16 was filled with a medium containing 10 % serum as a chemoattractant. PTX or experimental taxanes were present both in the upper and lower chamber of the plate in sublethal concentration (corresponding to IC12.5 of each compound). Cell migration was monitored for 24 h. All experiments were repeated three times.

Further, cells were diluted to 3 × 105 cells/ml and 70 μL of the suspension was pipetted to each well of 2-well Culture-Insert (Ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany). The next day, the culture insert was removed from adhered cells to create the wound. The Petri dish with cells was filled with the medium itself (control) or medium containing sublethal concentration (corresponding to IC12.5) of each taxane. The size of the wound was monitored by CytoSMART™ Lux2 (Lonza) and the analysis of wound closure was performed by ImageJ software with the Wound healing size tool plugin [43].

Gene expression in cell lines

Cells (at a density of 5 × 105) were plated into 60 mm Petri dishes and left to incubate overnight. The following day, the cells underwent treatment with PTX, SB-T-1216, SB-T-121605, or SB-T-121606; control cells were maintained in medium alone without any treatment. The concentrations were selected based on the IC50 value, supplemented with additional concentration values, ensuring that one common concentration was included for all taxanes, except for PTX in the resistant cell line, where the IC50 differed by 2–3 orders of magnitude, to ensure that the effect of the taxanes would be captured effectively. After 48 h of treatment, cells were collected by trypsinization, washed twice with PBS, and then resuspended in RLT buffer from the AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germantown, Maryland, USA) and stored at −80°C for subsequent RNA and protein extraction (chapter 2.8). Expression at the mRNA level (2.9) and protein level (2.10) was then measured in the prepared samples.

Cell-derived xenografts

The study involving mouse xenografts was performed by investigators possessing the authorization to work with experimental animals. The experimental procedure was compliant with Czech jurisdiction regulation of experimental use of animals (Act No. 246/1992 Coll.) on the protection of animals against cruelty, as amended).

Immunodeficient female athymic Nude Crl:NU(NCr)-Foxn1nu mice aged 4 – 6 weeks were obtained from Charles River Laboratories (Freiburg, Germany) and housed in a pathogen-free environment. Cell suspension (100 μl) containing 5 × 106 SKOV-3/RES or SKOV-3/SEN cells in PBS was injected subcutaneously into the dorsal flanks of each mouse. Application of taxanes was initiated after tumors reached a size of ~100 mm3.

In vivo treatment with paclitaxel and SB-Ts

In total, 30 xenografts were prepared. SKOV-3/SEN cells were used for the preparation of 15 xenografts, and another 15 xenograft models were prepared from SKOV-3/RES cells. Both the sensitive and resistant xenograft groups were divided into three groups of five mice (six group in total), with one being treated with DMSO in tissue culture water (1.5 %, control groups), the second one with PTX only (10 mg/kg), and the third one with the combination of PTX (9 mg/kg) and SB-T-121606 (1 mg/kg). Therapeutics (PTX or SB-T-121606) were administered intraperitoneally in a total volume of 100 μl twice a week for three weeks. Control groups received 100 μl of vehicle (1.5 % DMSO in sterile water for tissue culture) (PAN-Biotech) intraperitoneally twice a week for three weeks as well. Animals were sacrificed after six cycles of treatment or based on their physical condition during the treatment. During the experiment the change in xenograft body weight and tumor volume was monitored before each application. Tumor volume was calculated by following the standard formula (width2 × length)/2. Resected tumors were preserved in RNA later (Sigma-Aldrich) and stored at −80 °C until further processing.

Isolation of nucleic acids and cDNA synthesis

Tumor tissue samples obtained from both animal models and ovarian carcinoma patients, as well as control samples from women without ovarian malignancy, were homogenized using a mortar and pestle in the presence of liquid nitrogen. The AllPrep DNA/RNA/protein Mini kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was utilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions to isolate total RNA, DNA, and protein from the tissue and cell culture samples. The quantification of RNA was performed using the Quant-iT™ RiboGreen™ RNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen™, CA, USA) and the Infinite M200 fluorescence reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Austria). To assess the quality of RNA, the RNA integrity number (RIN) was determined using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using 0.5 μg of total RNA with the use of the RevertAid First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (MBI Fermentas, Vilnius, Lithuania), following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality of the cDNA was confirmed through PCR amplification of the Ubiquitin C fragment, as previously described [44].

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was conducted using the TaqMan® Gene Expression Assays (ThermoFisher, Waltham, MA, USA). List of the assays used for selected genes (NOTCH1, NOTCH2, NOTCH3, NOTCH4, JAG1, JAG2, DLL1, DLL3, and DLL4) can be found in Table S2. Highly stable expression of reference genes PPIA, YWHAZ, and UBC was used to normalize the results in in vitro, in vivo models, and in patients with ovarian carcinoma.

The qPCR reaction mixture for all samples consisted of 1 μL of 5 × Hot FirePol Probe qPCR Mix Plus (ROX) (Solis BioDyne OÜ, Tartu, Estonia), 0.25 μL of 20 × TaqMan Gene Expression Assay, 1.75 μL of nuclease-free water, and 2 μL of 8-times diluted cDNA, making a final reaction volume of 5 μL. The PCR reaction was performed using the 384-well position ViiA7 Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA).

The cycling parameters for all reactions included an initial hold at 50 °C for 2 min, followed by a denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min. This was followed by 45 cycles consisting of a denaturation step at 95 °C for 15 s and an annealing/extension step at 58 or 60 °C for 60 s (for detail see Table S2). The non-template control (NTC) contained water instead of cDNA. Negative cDNA synthesis controls (RNA transcribed without reverse transcriptase) were also included to identify possible carry-over contamination. Each sample was analyzed in duplicates, and samples with a standard deviation of duplicates greater than 0.5 Ct were re-analyzed. The design of the qPCR study adhered to the MIQE guidelines [45].

Immunoblotting analysis of protein expression

Protein samples were extracted from both control and treated cells, animal and human patient samples using the AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini Kit (Qiagen), and their concentrations were determined with the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Infinite M200 plate reader. For analysis, 20 μg of total protein was loaded onto a 7.5 % (w/v) polyacrylamide gel, underwent electrophoresis, and was then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. The membranes were initially blocked for 60 min using Blocker™ BLOTTO in TBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by an overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in Blocker™ BLOTTO in TBS with 0.2 % Tween®20 (v/v). After rinsing with TBS containing 0.1 % Tween®20 (v/v), the membranes were incubated for 60 min with secondary antibodies at room temperature. The proteins assessed included actin (mouse monoclonal antibody, AC-40, cat no. A3853, Sigma Aldrich) as a control, along with NOTCH3 (rabbit monoclonal antibody, D11B8, #5276 Cell signaling, Danvers, Massachusetts, USA). The secondary antibodies used were IRDye® 800CW Goat anti-Rabbit and IRDye® 680RD Goat anti-Mouse IgG (Li-cor, Lincoln, Nebraska, USA). Protein visualization was performed with the Odyssey® Fc Imaging System (Li-cor), and quantification was done using Image Studio version 4.0.21 (Li-Cor).

Detection of NOTCH4 protein expression in ovarian tissue samples was conducted as described above with the NOTCH4 rabbit monoclonal antibody (EPR18049, Abcam, UK). The assessment of NOTCH4 expression in control and treated cancer cells, as well as in animal tumor samples, was performed through membrane reprobing due to the lack of sufficient protein for additional membranes. Membranes were rinsed with ultrapure water, incubated for 40 min at room temperature with 1 × Restore™ Fluorescent Western Blot Stripping Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), then rinsed again with ultrapure water and subsequently with TBS. The effectiveness of stripping was verified using the Odyssey® Fc Imaging System (Li-cor). Stripped membranes were blocked using Blocker™ BLOTTO in TBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. Protein visualization was carried out using the Odyssey® Fc Imaging System (Li-cor), and quantification was performed with Image Studio version 4.0.21 (Li-Cor).

Knockdown of NOTCH3 transcript level by locked nucleic acids

Transfection of SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES cells was performed by Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen™) and by Antisense LNA GapmeRs (Qiagen). GapmeR for NOTCH3 was designed by GeneGlobe platform – designID (LG00820881-DDA, product sequence 5 ´–3 ´- TTACGGCATGGTGAGG) targeting 5-prime site of target. As a negative control for the experiment, Antisense LNA GapmeR Negative Control A (LG00000002-DDA, product sequence 5 ´–3 ´- AACACGTCTATACGC) was used, and as a positive control of successful transfection AllStars Hs Cell Death Control siRNA (Qiagen) was used. For gene expression analysis, 1 × 105 cells were plated into 12-well plates and for cell survival 5 × 103 cells were plated into 96-well plates. Cells were reversely transfected with 30 nM of GapmeR or negative/positive controls according to the manufactureŕs protocol, medium containing the transfection complex was replaced after 24 h of incubation with growth media only and then incubated for 72 h. After that, cells were harvested for isolation by AllPrep DNA/RNA/Protein Mini kit (Qiagen). Further handling of samples is described in Chapters 2.10 and 2.11. For the survival analysis, the same procedure was followed as with cells without GapmeRs, as described in Chapter 2.4. Briefly, cells were seeded, treated with taxanes (PTX, SB-T-1216, SB-T-121605, SB-T-121606) for 72 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Blue® assay on an Infinite M200 plate reader. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The IC50 values were determined using the GraphPad Prism v10.2 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Values are displayed as means ± SD of three independent experiments. Statistical comparison of IC50 values was made by the two-tailed Student’s t-test.

In order to estimate the differences in gene expression resulting from treatment, both in vitro and in vivo, the fold change was calculated using the comparative Ct method as described by Livak and Schmittgen [46]. Relative quantification was performed using the 2−ΔCt method, while fold change (FC) calculation in groups divided by treatment with taxanes was determined done the 2−ΔCt method [46,47]. Statistical comparison between treated and untreated tumor cells and xenograft groups was conducted using the two-tailed Student’s t-test in GraphPad Prism. The statistical analysis of expression results in cell lines and xenografts was performed on 2−ΔCt values, with significance defined as a p-value smaller than 0.05 and an FC value smaller than 0.6667 or greater than 1.5. The transcript levels of target genes were normalized to the reference genes mentioned in Table S2.

Statistical comparison of tumor volume in in vivo models was conducted using the two-tailed Student’s t-test.

In the cohorts of ovarian carcinoma patients, mean Ct values of duplicates, normalized to reference genes, were utilized to calculate differences in transcript levels between tissue types. The 2−ΔCt method and standard deviation were employed for relative gene expression normalization [47]. Associations between transcripts and clinical data, such as age at diagnosis, histological subtype of ovarian carcinoma, histological grade, FIGO stage, Ki-67 in tumor cells expression as a percentage, resistance to therapy, and death, were assessed using non-parametric tests including the Mann-Whitney, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman rank tests. Survival functions were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier (log rank test). All continuous variables were divided by the median for survival analyses. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v16.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) or GraphPad v10.2 software (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA, USA). The false discovery rate (FDR) test by Benjamini and Hochberg [48] was applied to control the Type I error in single gene expression analyses, and adjusted p-values (q-values) are provided for each comparison except for the in vitro and in vivo analyses.

Results

EOC study population

Patients characteristics

The expression profile of NOTCH pathway genes was measured in tumor tissue of a cohort of EOC patients (N = 149) and control ovarian tissue (N = 17). Clinical data of EOC patients are in Table S1. On average, patients with EOC were diagnosed at the age of 60 ± 11 years. A majority of them had the HGSC subtype (81 %), grade 3 tumors (77 %), and advanced stage III or IV (81 %). In total, 104 (70 %) patients had their samples collected during the initial surgical procedure before undergoing any chemotherapy (referred to as the Pre-treatment Group). The remaining 41 (28 %) patients had their samples collected during surgery after receiving neoadjuvant cytotoxic therapy (NACT) involving PTX and platinum derivatives, as detailed in Table S1 (referred to as the Post-treatment Group).

To assess resistance status, we categorized all tumor samples based on the PFI. EOC patients with available clinical characteristics were grouped into those resistant to platinum treatment (N = 24; PFI <6 months), those partially responsive to platinum treatment (N = 20; PFI 6–12 months), and those fully responsive to platinum treatment (N = 93; PFI >12 months). Among all treated patients, 118 experienced disease progression, and 57 died. The median PFI (± SD) was 21 ± 26 months. We also used tissue samples from 17 non-cancer patients who did not exhibit morphological signs of primary ovarian carcinoma in their ovaries (ovarian or uterine leiomyoma, benign ovarian cysts, cervical or endometrial carcinoma, sarcoma, and benign cystadenofibroma) as control samples.

NOTCH pathway expression in EOC tumors and non-malignant ovarian tissues

We compared mRNA levels of the NOTCH signaling pathway in the cohorts of EOC patients (N = 149) and control ovarian tissues without the presence of malignant cells (N = 17). Expression of all NOTCH pathway genes except DLL3 was detected in both types of ovarian tissue. In ovarian tumor tissue, the highest levels were found for the NOTCH3 gene followed by NOTCH1/2 and JAG1 genes. When we compared ovarian tumors and non-malignant tissues, we found that the mRNA levels of NOTCH1, NOTCH3, NOTCH4, and JAG2 genes were significantly higher and that of NOTCH2 lower in tumor tissue than in non-malignant ovarian tissue. These results also passed FDR correction for multiple testing (q=0.031). The most significant upregulation was found for NOTCH3 and JAG2 genes (Fig. 1). Comparison of expression levels of NOTCH signaling pathway genes between EOC pretreatment and post-treatment tumors showed no statistically significant difference.

The expression of NOTCH1, NOTCH4, JAG1, and DLL4 was lower in the HGSC subtype compared to other histological subtypes of EOC (Fig. 2A). A significant increase of JAG1 expression was found in higher grade (grade 3) compared to lower grades (grade 1 and 2) (Fig. 2B). NOTCH4 and DLL4 expression was higher in less advanced stage I–III tumors compared to stage IV. (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, the expression of the NOTCH3 gene showed a statistically significant positive correlation with the age of patients (p = 0.031, R2=0.175).

Functional study

In vitro effect of conventional paclitaxel and experimental SB-Ts on proliferation, migration, and cell cycle of SKOV-3 cell line and its resistant subline

In the in vitro study, we compared cell viability in the presence of PTX and experimental SB-T derivatives. The IC50 values of all tested taxanes ranged from 5 to 37 nM after 72 h of incubation in the original SKOV-3 cell line (SKOV-3/SEN) (Figs. 5A, 5B). In the PTX-resistant subline (SKOV-3/RES), the IC50 value of PTX was 3 orders of magnitude higher (4.386 nM after 72 h), whereas in the case of the SB-T derivatives studied, the values ranged between 12–74 nM (Figs. 5A, 5B). Thus, all SB-Ts were slightly less effective in the resistant cell line than in the sensitive cells (relative resistance ratio between 2.4 and 3.6, Figs. 5B, 5C), but they were by 2–3 orders of magnitude more potent than PTX, whose relative resistance ratio was 118. Overall, among the SB-Ts compared, the most potent was the third generation SB-T-121606, whose IC50 value was the lowest in both the sensitive cell line (5 nM) and the resistant one (12 nM) and the resulting relative resistance ratio (2.4) was also the lowest (Figs. 5B, 5C).

In order to analyze changes in the cell cycle caused by PTX and experimental SB-Ts, we incubated SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES cells with all tested taxanes for 24, 48, and 72 h and measured the proportion of cells in each cell cycle phase by flow cytometry. The concentration selected for each taxane corresponded to the IC50 value. The subG0 population, indicative of apoptotic or damaged cells, was significantly elevated in both sensitive and resistant cells treated with PTX and all SB-Ts after 24, 48, and 72 h of treatment, suggesting treatment-induced cell death (Fig. 5D). The amount of SKOV-3/SEN cells in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle was significantly increased in cells treated with SB-T-121605 (p < 0.001) and SB-T-121606 (p < 0.001) after 24 h of incubation. However, after 48 h of incubation, the increase of cells in the G2/M phase was significant only for SB-T-121606 (p = 0.005), and after 72 h of incubation, the difference in the G2/M phase subpopulation was not significant for any tested condition. Similarly, in the SKOV-3/RES cell line, the G2/M population of cells was significantly increased in SB-T-121605 (p = 0.027) and SB-T-121606 (p = 0.008) treated cells after 24 h. In cells treated for 48 h, only SB-T-121606 treatment showed a significantly increased G2/M population of cells (p = 0.011), but the significance for SB-T-121605 was lost (p = 0.504). Representative histograms showing cell cycle distribution are provided in Supplementary Figure S3.

Additionally, we analyzed the effect of SB-Ts on cell migration. A significant reduction in migration potential after 24 h monitoring of the SKOV-3/SEN cell line in the presence of SB-T-1216 (p < 0.001), SB-T-121605 (p = 0.008), and SB-T-121606 (p = 0.014) was observed. This trend was not statistically significant for cells grown in the presence of PTX (p = 0.180, Fig. 5E). Analysis of cell migration by wound healing assay showed a decrease of cell migration for all four taxanes tested, but it was statistically significant only for cells cultured in the presence of SB-T-1216 (p = 0.039), SB-T-121605 (p = 0.048) and SB-T-121606 (p = 0.020) (Figs. 5G, 5H). For the SKOV-3/RES cell line, a significant reduction in the migration potential in the presence of SB-T-1216 (p = 0.017), SB-T-121606 (p = 0.038), and PTX (p = 0.005) was found. The migration in the presence of SB-T-121605 was beyond the threshold of significance (p = 0.053) because of higher variability between the three experiments (Fig. 5E). The wound healing assay showed significantly lower migration in SKOV-3/RES cells incubated with PTX (p = 0.029), SB-T-1216 (p = 0.011), SB-T-121605 (p = 0.019), and SB-T-121606 (p = 0.011) (Figs. 5I, 5J). Overall, the migration potential of the PTX-resistant cell line was lower compared to the sensitive subline (Fig. 5F).

Effects of NOTCH3 expression downregulation by GapmeR technology

Function of NOTCH3 was investigated by a gene manipulation (knock down) experiment in SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES sublines in vitro. Antisense oligonucleotides (GapmeRs) successfully downregulated the NOTCH3 gene expression in both studied sublines. The results show that all tested taxanes effectively reduced cell viability in both SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES cell lines (Figs. 6A, 6C). When comparing IC50 values with cells without suppressed NOTCH3 expression, it is evident that downregulation of the NOTCH3 gene leads to an increase in efficacy of the tested taxanes. This was statistically significant in SKOV-3/SEN treated with PTX (Fig. 6B), and in SKOV-3/RES in the case of PTX, SB-T-1216, and SB-T-121605 (Fig. 6D). The comparison of untreated controls with cells treated with 30 nM GapmeRs targeting the NOTCH3 transcript showed statistically significant downregulation of target expression in SKOV-3/SEN (p = 0.017) and SKOV-3/RES (p = 0.017) sublines (Fig. 6E).

In vivo expression changes of NOTCH signaling pathway after treatment with paclitaxel or combination of paclitaxel with SB-T-121606

Following in vitro experiments, we analyzed the expression of all NOTCH pathway genes in tumor tissue samples harvested from CDX murine in vivo models derived from SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES sublines treated with PTX and SB-T-121606.

Discussion

Resistance of tumor cells to conventional taxanes is one of the major obstacles to successful treatment of patients with ovarian cancer. Currently, new therapeutics and molecular targets are needed to improve patient outcomes. The NOTCH signaling pathway plays a crucial role in the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, development, and tissue homeostasis [49]. Dysregulation of the NOTCH signaling pathway was found in diverse malignancies including ovarian cancer [26,50–52]. Moreover, the NOTCH signaling pathway is implicated in conferring chemoresistance to tumor cells [31,32,53]. Therefore the members of the NOTCH signaling pathway have pontential as prognostic and predictive biomarkers or therapeutic targets. In the initial part of the study, we described the expression profile of the NOTCH signaling pathway and its impact on prognosis and survival in EOC patients. In the subsequent functional study, we investigated the expression profile of NOTCH signaling pathway genes in sensitive and resistant ovarian cancer models treated with conventional PTX and synthetic derivatives SB-Ts, previously demonstrated as potent killers of resistant tumors [18,19], in vitro and in vivo.

Few studies have addressed the NOTCH signaling pathway in ovarian cancer to date. Its dysregulation in the HGSC histological subtype [54, 55] and association with poor survival of ovarian carcinoma patients, advanced stages of disease, or metastatic process was reported [26,50, 51,56–58]. Very recently, important role of the NOTCH pathway in the progression of ovarian cancer, e.g., by regulating immune cells, endocrine resistance, Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, and oxidative phosphorylation, was revealed [56].

Our study shows the association of low NOTCH4 gene expression with the more aggressive HGSC subtype, FIGO stage IV, presence of metastases, and shorter PFI of EOC patients, suggesting that its low levels may help predict poor treatment response and bad prognosis of patients. Our data corroborate previous findings of the correlation of high NOTCH4 mRNA levels with favorable overall survival of ovarian cancer patients [57], association of NOTCH4 gene expression with FIGO stage [56], and a recent analysis of the TCGA dataset confirming that high NOTCH4 expression is relevant for good prognosis of the disease [59]. Therefore, NOTCH4 has been included in a seven-gene risk model for risk stratification and prediction of survival outcomes in patients with ovarian serous carcinoma [59].

Despite the above-demonstrated prognostic potential, very little is known about the functional relevance of NOTCH4 expression for ovarian cancer progression and therapy outcome. The effects of NOTCH4 overexpression vary by tumor type, but recent studies suggest that, for example in colorectal cancer cell lines, NOTCH4 overexpression suppresses cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while inducing apoptosis [60]. In triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells, knockdown of glutamate-rich WD-repeat-containing protein 1 (GRWD1) suppressed proliferation, invasion, and migration and promoted apoptosis by inhibiting the NOTCH pathway, particularly NOTCH1, and NOTCH4 [61]. Our study adds new data to this fragmented prior information, obtained mostly from in vitro studies. We used a newly established in vivo CDX mouse model of resistant ovarian tumors, produced using the PTX resistant SKOV-3/RES subline, for monitoring the NOTCH pathway after taxane treatment. A significant upregulation of the NOTCH4 protein after the application of PTX and its combination with highly effective experimental SB-T-121606 was observed in this model, which supports the suggested favorable role of NOTCH4 found by us and others [55,56,58,59] in prognosis of EOC patients, and may serve as biomarker of their survival. Further, results from tumor volume analysis supported results from our previous studies, showing high efficacy of the combination of PTX with novel SB-T-121606 in ovarian and pancreatic carcinoma [18,19].

The highest gene expression levels of the whole pathway were found for the NOTCH3 gene. It was significantly overexpressed in EOC tumors compared to non-malignant ovarian tissues. NOTCH3 is one of the four receptors of the NOTCH signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in cell communication and is involved in various cellular processes, including differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis. Aberrant expression of NOTCH3 has been found to be common in different solid tumors and high NOTCH3 expression has been shown to predict poor OS and PFS in EOC patients [26,62–65]. Moreover, NOTCH3 expression was correlated with advanced TNM stage and histological type, lymph node metastases, distant metastases, high pathological grade, ascites and chemoresistance to PTX/platinum based chemotherapy [63,65]. Similarly, we found that the NOTCH3 gene was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to non-tumor tissues, indicating its possible role in tumor progression. This finding aligns with the results reported by Park et al. [62]. However, we did not find any other significant correlations between NOTCH3 expression and clinicopathological data in our study.

In the available literature, the association of NOTCH3 expression with poor therapy response and resistance in ovarian cancer is also supported by functional studies. NOTCH3 protein overexpression rescued the OVCAR-3 ovarian cancer cells from apoptosis [66] or induced epithelial-mesenchymal transition process and attenuated carboplatin-induced apoptosis in OVCA429 cells [67]. Additionally, inhibiting NOTCH3 activity through the use of either siRNAs or γ-secretase inhibitors significantly increases the sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to cisplatin [32]. Furthermore, higher NOTCH3 expression has been repeatedly associated in the literature with resistance to platinum [62] and PTX [68], particularly through mechanisms involving increased expression of ATP-dependent transporters [62], promotion of anti-apoptotic processes [68], and maintenance of stem-like cell properties [69]. Conversely, NOTCH3 inhibition has been linked to increased chemotherapy sensitivity, reduced cell viability and migration, and enhanced apoptosis [68]. Our results are in agreement with these studies because after the knockdown of the NOTCH3 gene, sensitivity to the used PTX and SB-Ts increased in both SKOV-3/SEN and SKOV-3/RES cells. In the SKOV3-RES cell line, the expression of NOTCH3 was higher, which is related to the resistance to the PTX, as shown in in vitro results. Higher expression of NOTCH3 was observed also after with PTX or SB-Ts treatment, the results are significant in SKOV-3/RES in both in vitro and in vivo models. To the best of our knowledge, a study similar to ours conducted in vivo has not yet been published, making direct comparisons impossible. In summary, these results indicate that NOTCH3-specific inhibition sensitizes resistant ovarian cancer cells to PTX and/or platinum treatment.

In order to provide more solid evidence to the assumptions mentioned below, we performed functional study using knockdown (KD) of the NOTCH3 gene by GapmeRs. This KD highly significantly enhanced the sensitivity of SKOV-3/RES to PTX and the sensitive subline SKOV-3/SEN as well. This result suggests that specific inhibition of NOTCH3 could be a potentially effective strategy to sensitize resistant ovarian tumors to therapy. In comparison to the NOTCH3 gene, the inhibition of NOTCH4 did not prove effective in this manner in our ovarian cancer models. Interestingly, a Phase II clinical trial of the single agent RO4929097, an unspecific γ secretase inhibitor of NOTCH signaling, has been conducted in 45 EOC patients. However, RO4929097 had insufficient activity as a single-agent in platinum-resistant ovarian cancer [70]. Our new data warrant further study of NOTCH inhibitors in resistant ovarian tumors, perhaps combined with platinum or taxane derivatives.

The study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. A broader context, such as the role of epigenetics or main oncodriver genetic alterations like TP53 mutations, was not explored, which could provide a deeper understanding of the observed phenomena. Additionally, the investigation was limited to a specific set of models, and incorporating a wider range of cell lines or using more complex systems like patient-derived xenografts or organoids is planned for future research, as well as functional analyses focused on other promising members of the NOTCH pathway, such as NOTCH1, NOTCH2, JAG1, JAG2, and DLL4. On the other hand, the study offers significant benefits. Particularly the functional analysis conducted using SKOV-3 SEN/RES models, with a focus on in vivo applications, provides a unique new platform for studying mechanisms of resistance and sensitivity. Furthermore, the discrepancy observed between mRNA and protein levels in some cases invites discussion, suggesting potential regulatory mechanisms at the post-transcriptional or post-translational level, which merit further investigation.

In conclusion, our main findings based on patient and functional data suggest that NOTCH4 may act as a predictive biomarker of therapeutic response in EOC. Additionally, NOTCH3 seems to be a potential drug target in the therapy of resistant EOC. In vitro and in vivo findings in resistant ovarian models revealed that after treatment of conventional PTX (4500 nM) or new SB-Ts (30 nM), significant deregulation of NOTCH signaling pathway genes occurs, including upregulation of NOTCH3. Knockdown of the NOTCH3 gene caused higher sensitivity of ovarian cancer cells to taxanes, suggesting that NOTCH3 modulation and targeting may represent potential therapeutic improvements in resistant ovarian carcinoma. Follow-up studies should incorporate patient-derived xenografts and organoids to enhance understanding of individual variability of patients’ therapy response, prognosis, and complexity of tumor environment, including also the diversity of EOC subtypes.
