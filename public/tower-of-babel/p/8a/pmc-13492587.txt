Genome‐wide association and genomic prediction of anthracnose (Colletotrichum dematium) resistance in spinach
Authors: Raja Sekhar Srungarapu, Ainong Shi, Carlos A. Avila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnthracnose, caused by Colletotrichum dematium, has emerged as a major foliar disease that threatens spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) production. In this study, a diverse panel of 266 accessions was evaluated under field conditions to dissect the genetic architecture of anthracnose resistance. Substantial phenotypic variation was observed, with disease severity indices (DSIs) ranging from 1 (highly resistant) to 10 (highly susceptible). A total of 20 accessions showed moderate to high resistance (DSI ≤ 4), representing valuable resistance sources. A genome‐wide association study (GWAS) identified 20 significant single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). The most consistent marker (SOVchr3_19667279) explained up to 66.8% of phenotypic variance, and several associated SNPs were located within or near putative defense‐related genes. Genomic prediction (GP) using multiple models demonstrated that predictive accuracy increased when a set with more SNPs was used. The genomic best linear unbiased prediction model achieved the highest accuracy (r = 0.92), while the Bayesian ridge regression model attained the best predictive accuracy (r = 0.51) when using 20 GWAS‐derived SNPs, highlighting the value of integrating association mapping with prediction approaches. Cross‐population prediction performed well (r = 0.58–0.71), whereas across‐population prediction showed reduced accuracy (r = 0.10–0.14), indicating the influence of genetic background on model transferability. This integrative genomic study provides novel insights into the genetic basis of anthracnose resistance in spinach and demonstrates the potential of GWAS and GP in accelerating resistance breeding.

INTRODUCTION

Spinach (Spinacia oleracea L.) is one of the most widely consumed and cultivated leafy vegetable crops, known for its high nutritional value and health‐promoting properties (Cao et al., 1996; Howard et al., 2002). It is a rich source of vitamins, antioxidants, and minerals, including folate, iron, phosphorus, sodium, calcium, and ascorbic acid (vitamin C), making it an important component in global diets (Bunea et al., 2008; Yosefi et al., 2010). Global consumer demand for spinach has increased steadily, due to its culinary versatility and recognized health benefits (Cao et al., 1996; Howard et al., 2002; Morelock & Correll, 2008). It is a cool‐season crop typically grown in temperate and subtropical climates, with global production of 34.11 million tonnes (Mt) (FAOSTAT, 2023). China is the largest producer (31.72 Mt), contributing over 92% of the total global output, followed by the United States (0.42 Mt), Turkey (0.23 Mt), and Japan (0.20 Mt) (FAOSTAT, 2023). The global spinach cultivation area spans over 0.94 million ha (Mha), reflecting the rising demand for fresh and processed spinach (FAOSTAT, 2023). In the United States, spinach is cultivated on 29,542 ha with a productivity of 14,317 kg/ha, primarily concentrated in California (52,900 ha), followed by Arizona (12,200 ha) (U.S. Department of Agriculture, 2024). Despite its economic and nutritional importance, spinach production is challenged by several biotic stresses, particularly foliar diseases that significantly reduce both yield and market quality (Correll et al., 1994; B. Liu et al., 2017, 2020).

Anthracnose, caused by Colletotrichum dematium, is one of the most devastating fungal diseases affecting spinach (Correll et al., 1994; B. Liu et al., 2017, 2020). It is prevalent in regions with warm temperatures and high humidity, creating conducive conditions for rapid disease development and dissemination (Correll et al., 1994; B. Liu et al., 2020). The fungal pathogen causes sunken lesions on leaves, petioles, and stems, which often coalesce and lead to defoliation, wilting, and ultimately, severe crop losses (Correll et al., 1994; Kurt et al., 2016; B. Liu et al., 2020; Washington et al., 2006). Yield reductions of up to 50% have been reported in severely infested fields, particularly under warm and humid environments, which favor rapid pathogen proliferation (Cannon et al., 2012; Correll et al., 1994; Koike & Correll, 1993). The wide host range of fungal pathogen, including vegetables, legumes, cereals, and fruit trees, further complicates its management due to cross‐infection and pathogen persistence in crop rotations (Correll et al., 1994; Dal Bello, 2000; L. Liu, Jin, et al., 2016; Smith & Aveling, 1997; Washington et al., 2006; Yoshida & Shirata, 1999). Moreover, the emergence of multiple Colletotrichum species complexes with increased aggressiveness has further complicated disease management, especially in organic production systems where fungicide use is restricted (B. Liu et al., 2021; F. Liu, Tang, et al., 2016).

Traditional disease management strategies, including fungicides and cultural practices, offer some degree of control against the pathogen, but host plant resistance remains the most sustainable and environmentally friendly strategy for anthracnose disease management (Awika et al., 2020; B. Liu et al., 2020). Thus, the development of genetically resistant spinach cultivars remains the most promising long‐term solution for mitigating anthracnose‐related losses. However, breeding for disease resistance in spinach has been constrained by its narrow genetic base, high heterozygosity, dioecious reproduction system, and the polygenic quantitative traits influenced by genotype × environment interactions (Janick & Stevenson, 1955; Morelock & Correll, 2008). Moreover, phenotypic screening for disease resistance is labor‐intensive, environmentally variable, and often not feasible at early breeding stages (Simko et al., 2021). Hence, there is a critical need for genomic tools that can accelerate the identification of resistance loci and improve selection accuracy in spinach breeding programs.

Spinach is a diploid species (2n = 2x = 12) belonging to the family Amaranthaceae, with a relatively small genome (∼989 Mb), which has been recently sequenced and annotated, providing valuable insights into gene families, sex determination, and stress response pathways (Arumuganathan & Earle, 1991; Cai et al., 2021; Collins et al., 2019; Ellis & Halsted, 1890; Hirakawa et al., 2021; X. Ma et al., 2022; Xu et al., 2017). Advances in high‐throughput genotyping and statistical genomics have enabled the dissection of complex traits through genome‐wide association studies (GWASs). GWAS leverages natural genetic diversity and historical recombination events in diverse germplasm panels to identify single‐nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with traits of interest at high resolution (Davey et al., 2011). In spinach, GWAS has been applied to explore traits such as leaf traits (J. Ma et al., 2016), mineral content (Ji et al., 2024; Qin et al., 2017; Rameneni et al., 2025; Rueda et al., 2022), oxalate concentration (Shi, Mou, & Correll, 2016; Xiong et al., 2025), leaf miner resistance (Alatawi et al., 2024; Shi & Mou, 2016), Downy mildew resistance (Bhattarai et al., 2020, 2021; Bhattarai, Olaoye, et al., 2022; Bhattarai, Shi, et al., 2022), Verticillium wilt resistance (Bhattarai, Shi, et al., 2022; Shi AiNong et al., 2016; Shi, Mou, Correll, Motes, et al., 2016), Fusarium wilt resistance (Gyawali et al., 2025), Stemphylium leaf spot resistance (Shi, Mou, Correll, Koike, et al., 2016), white rust resistance (Shi et al., 2022), and anthracnose resistance (Awika et al., 2020) have been demonstrated using diverse spinach accessions; their application to disease resistance, particularly anthracnose, remains limited. However, there is a significant knowledge gap regarding the genetic basis of anthracnose resistance in spinach. To date, no comprehensive GWAS has been reported for this trait, limiting the development of molecular markers for resistance breeding. Complementary to GWAS, genomic prediction (GP) offers an opportunity to improve breeding efficiency for polygenic traits by using genome‐wide markers to estimate the breeding values of individuals in the breeding population (Meuwissen et al., 2001). GP models such as (1) stepwise regression, (2) ridge regression, (3) Bayesian estimation models such as Bayes A (BA), Bayes B (BB), Bayes LASSO (BL), (4) semi‐parametric regressions like kernel‐based regression and neural networks, and (5) machine learning methods like random forest (RF) and support vector machine (SVM) models (Singh & Singh, 2015) have been successfully applied in other leafy vegetables, legumes, and cereals for agronomical, biotic, and abiotic stress‐related traits (Bartholomé et al., 2022; Jain et al., 2017; A. M. J. Lee et al., 2024; Sandhu et al., 2022; Simko et al., 2021; Susmitha et al., 2023). The integration of GWAS and GP provides a synergistic framework to not only identify resistance loci but also to predict the performance of the accessions in breeding populations with high accuracy.

Given the economic importance of spinach and the increasing threat posed by anthracnose, this study aims to fill a critical gap in understanding the genetic architecture of anthracnose resistance in spinach. Specifically, the objectives are to (1) evaluate the phenotypic variation for anthracnose resistance among a diverse panel of 266 spinach accessions, (2) identify SNP markers associated with anthracnose resistance using multiple GWAS models, (3) identify putative candidate genes linked to resistance loci, and (4) assess GP accuracy using various GP models using both significant and randomly selected SNP sets. This integrated genomic approach is expected to provide valuable genetic resources and insights for breeding anthracnose‐resistant spinach cultivars and contribute to sustainable disease management strategies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

A diverse panel of 266 spinach (S. oleracea L.) accessions obtained from the USDA National Plant Germplasm System (NPGS) for GWAS and GP of anthracnose resistance. These accessions represent a wide geographical distribution across 30 countries. The majority (86.84%) of accessions originated from Turkey (75), the United States (37), Afghanistan (19), China (19), Unknown (19), Macedonia (15), India (10), Iran (10), Hungary (8), Syria (7), Belgium (6), and Georgia (6), and among others (Table S1).

Experimental design and disease evaluation

A virulent isolate of C. dematium, the causal agent of anthracnose, was collected from diseased spinach plants in a commercial field near Uvalde, TX, and maintained on potato dextrose agar (PDA) (Difco Laboratories Inc). Inoculation was performed as described by Awika et al. (2020). Briefly, for inoculum preparation, oat grains were autoclaved twice in vented mycology spawn bags (MycoSupply Inc), supplemented with sterile water (200 mL/kg of oat), and inoculated with 10‐day‐old PDA cultures (approximately twelve 5 × 5 mm plugs per bag). The inoculated bags were incubated at room temperature for 10 days with daily agitation to promote uniform fungal colonization. Spinach seedlings were grown in a randomized complete design. Each plot consisted of 30 plants at 4 cm between plants and 50 cm between plots and grown under standard agronomic practices. At 21 days after emergence (4–5 true leaf stage), each plant was inoculated with ∼90 g of oat‐based fungal inoculum, manually applied over the canopy. To ensure optimal disease development, inoculations were performed in the morning dew, followed by light sprinkler irrigation once daily for five consecutive days. Disease severity index (DSI) was assessed 28 days post‐inoculation using a 1–10 scale. Briefly, the surface area covered was averaged per plant and then totaled as a percent on a plot basis. The per‐plot percentages were then converted to nominal scores of 1–10, where a score of 1 denotes 0%–10% infection, 2 denotes 11%–20% infection, and so on up to a score of 10 denoting 91%–100% infection. The raw scores were retained to preserve biological interpretability and consistency with prior spinach disease‐resistance studies. Mean disease scores were used in downstream analyses.

Genotyping and SNP filtering

DNA was extracted from fresh leaves pooled from 5 to 10 plants per genotype. High‐quality DNA from each sample was fragmented into ∼350 bp fragments using a Covaris Ultrasonic Processor prior to sequencing. DNA library construction included end repair, A‐tails addition, purification, polymerase chain reaction amplification, and library quality assessment. Libraries were paired‐end sequenced using whole‐genome resequencing (WGR) technology on the Illumina NovaSeq platform, achieving 10× coverage of the spinach genome and generating approximately 10 Gb of sequencing data per sample at BGI (https://www.bgi.com/). The spinach genome annotations of Monoe‐Viroflay genome annotation (Cai et al., 2021) (http://spinachbase.org/ftp/genome/Monoe‐Viroflay/) were used as a reference for mapping the WGR data of the 470 spinach genotypes with Burrows‐Wheeler aligner software (BWA v0.7.8‐r455). SAMtools (v 0.1.19‐44428 cd) was utilized to sort the BAM files and remove duplicate reads. The program Picard (v 1.111) (Li & Durbin, 2009) was used to merge BAM files from the same sample, and the GATK software (v 3.5) (McKenna et al., 2010) was chosen to detect and filter SNPs and InDels. Approximately 16 million raw SNPs were identified across the 470 spinach genotypes.

In this study, 266 of the 470 spinach genotypes were used for GWAS and GP. Filtering and keeping the SNPs with minor allele frequency (MAF) >2%, missing allele <5%, and heterogeneous rate <10%, we retained 120,817 high‐quality SNPs based on the Monoe‐Viroflay genome reference (Figure S1), which were subsequently used for GWAS and GP in this study. A relatively low MAF threshold was chosen to avoid excluding rare resistance alleles, as high resistance was observed in only a small number of accessions. All genomic sequences generated for these spinach accessions have been deposited in the National Center for Biotechnology Information Sequence Read Archive under BioProject ID: PRJNA860GP. The SNP dataset is available on Figshare at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.29923271.

Principal component analysis and genetic diversity analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) and a neighbor‐joining (NJ) phylogenetic tree were implemented in GAPIT3 (Genomic Association and Prediction Integrated Tool version 3) (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021; https://zzlab.net/GAPIT/index.html) to examine population structure among the 266 spinach accessions based on 120,817 SNPs. PCA was performed using 2–10 components, and the NJ phylogenetic tree was constructed to visualize clustering patterns for 2–10 groups, following the approach described by Shi et al. (2022). To further assess genetic diversity among spinach accessions with anthracnose resistance, phylogenetic trees were constructed using a subset of 6000 SNPs in MEGA 7 (Kumar et al., 2016), following the maximum likelihood method. Parameters used were consistent with those previously reported in (Shi et al. 2022; Shi, Mou, Correll, Motes, et al., 2016).

Genome‐wide association studies

GWAS for anthracnose resistance in the 266 spinach accessions were conducted using multiple statistical models to enhance the detection of true marker–trait associations (MTAs) and to compare model performance. A total of 120,817 SNPs were used for analysis. Five models were implemented in the GAPIT3 package in R: GLM (generalized linear model), MLM (mixed linear model), MLMM (multiple loci mixed model), FarmCPU (Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification), and BLINK (Bayesian‐information and Linkage‐disequilibrium Iteratively Nested Keyway) (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021). In addition, three models (GLM, MLM, and FarmCPU) in rMVP (memory‐efficient, visualization‐enhanced, and parallel‐accelerated tool for GWAS) package (Yin et al., 2021) (https://github.com/xiaolei‐lab/rMVP), and three models (SMR [single marker regression], GLM [with PCA correction], and MLM [with PCA + Kinship corrections]) were used in TASSEL (trait analysis by association, evolution, and linkage) 5. A significance threshold for identifying MTAs was determined using the Bonferroni correction (p = 0.05/120,817), corresponding to a logarithm of odds (LOD) score threshold of 6.38 (Shi et al., 2022). The primary objective of employing multiple models and pipelines (GAPIT3, rMVP, and TASSEL) was cross‐validation of MTAs, rather than model comparison per se. SNPs repeatedly detected across multiple models and software platforms were prioritized as robust loci. Manhattan plots and quantile–quantile (Q‐‐Q) plots were generated to visualize marker significance association and to assess model performance and fit. Additionally, t‐test results for significant SNPs were obtained using Visual Basic for Applications in Microsoft Excel 2016.

Candidate gene identification

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) between SNP loci was evaluated using both genetic distance (cM) and physical distance (bp). Pairwise LD among SNPs was calculated as the squared allele‐frequency correlation (r 2) using TASSEL 5 (Bradbury et al., 2007), and LD visualization was performed with Haploview (Barrett et al., 2005). Chromosome‐level LD decay rates for the six spinach chromosomes were adopted from Alatawi et al. (2025).

For genomic regions surrounding GWAS‐identified significant SNP markers, local LD decay was estimated by plotting pairwise r 2 values against physical distance (bp). The LD decay rate was defined as the physical distance at which the average r 2 declined to half of its maximum value (Kim et al., 2007; Lam et al., 2010). These locally estimated LD decay distances were used to delimit candidate genomic regions whenever possible. When LD decay could not be reliably estimated for a specific SNP‐associated region, the chromosome‐specific LD decay value was applied.

Candidate genes were identified within the defined LD blocks flanking each significant SNP. LD heatmaps for SNP regions containing candidate genes were generated using Haploview (Barrett et al., 2005) based on the Monoe‐Viroflay spinach reference genome. Genome annotation data were obtained from SpinachBase (http://www.spinachbase.org/) or via FTP access (http://spinachbase.org/ftp/genome/Monoe‐Viroflay/).

Genomic prediction analysis

GP was performed under several complementary scenarios to evaluate the predictive potential of genome‐wide markers and GWAS‐derived loci for anthracnose disease severity in spinach. Three major GP frameworks were implemented: (1) diagnostic self‐prediction using the entire panel, (2) cross‐validated prediction using randomly selected SNP sets of varying densities, and (3) GWAS‐derived marker‐based GP using both cross‐ and across‐population schemes. A schematic representation of the GP workflow, including population partitioning and validation schemes, is provided in Figure S2.

Genomic prediction using GAPIT3 for the entire panel (self‐prediction)

The GAPIT3 software package was used to estimate genomic estimated breeding values (GEBVs) using genomic best linear unbiased prediction (gBLUP) and GWAS‐assisted genomic BLUP (GAGBLUP, previously referred to as maBLUP) (J. Wang & Zhang, 2021) (https://zzlab.net/GAPIT/index.html). In this analysis, the full panel of 266 spinach accessions was used as both the training population (TP) and validation population (VP) to predict GEBVs for the DSI of anthracnose resistance. This self‐prediction scenario was included only as a diagnostic assessment to evaluate whether the available phenotypic and genotypic data contained sufficient signal for GP. Because TP and VP are identical, prediction accuracy (PA) from this scenario is expected to be inflated and is not intended to represent true predictive performance or breeding application.

Genomic prediction using different randomly selected SNP sets

PA for the DSI of anthracnose resistance was evaluated using six GP models: Bayes A (BA), Bayes B (BB), Bayesian LASSO (BL), Bayesian ridge regression (BRR), RF, and SVM. All analyses were conducted in the R environment following previously described procedures (Ravelombola et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2022, 2025). The SVM model was implemented using the kernlab R package (Karatzoglou et al., 2004). Bayesian models were run with 5000 iterations with a burn‐in of 2000 iterations using the BGLR package (Pérez & de Los Campos, 2014). The RF model was implemented using 100 decision trees using the randomForest package (Liaw & Wiener, 2002).

Sixteen randomly selected SNP sets were generated, ranging in size from 6 to 120,817 SNPs (r6, r20, r50, r100, r200, r500, r1000, r2000, r5000, r10000, r15000, r20000, r30000, r50000, r100000, and all120817). The four Bayesian models (BA, BB, BL, and BRR) were applied to all 16 SNP sets, whereas RF and SVM were evaluated using SNP sets from r6 to r15000.

PA was assessed using a fivefold cross‐validation strategy, in which four folds served as the TP and one fold served as the VP. Each model‐SNP set combination was replicated 100 times. PA was calculated as the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) between GEBVs and observed phenotypic values, and mean r‐values and standard errors (SEs) were computed across replications. Boxplots summarizing model performance across SNP sets were generated using the ggplot2 package in R. No bootstrapping was applied.

Genomic prediction using GWAS‐derived SNP markers

GWAS‐derived SNP markers from the entire panel

GWAS was conducted using five models (GLM, MLM, MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) implemented in GAPIT3, three models (GLM, MLM, and FarmCPU) implemented in rMVP, and three models (SMR, GLM, and MLM) implemented in TASSEL 5. SNPs significantly associated with DSI were identified from each model using the full panel of 266 spinach accessions. These GWAS‐derived SNP markers were subsequently used as marker sets for GP. PA was evaluated using a fivefold cross‐validation framework with six GP models: BA, BB, BL, BRR, RF, and SVM, following the same procedures used for the randomly selected SNP sets. PA was calculated as the correlation between GEBVs and the observed phenotypic values. Because the GWAS analysis was performed using the entire dataset prior to cross‐validation, the validation subsets were indirectly included in the marker discovery step. As a result, the reported PAs may be subject to some degree of upward bias due to potential data leakage and should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Cross‐ and across‐population prediction using GWAS‐derived SNP markers from 80% of the panel

To further evaluate the predictive value of GWAS‐derived markers, the full panel of 266 spinach accessions was randomly divided into 80% TP (213 accessions) and 20% VP (53 accessions). GWAS was performed only in the TP using the five models (GLM, MLM, MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) in GAPIT3. SNP markers with −log10(p) > 6.38 were selected for GP. GEBVs were estimated using six models: BA, BB, BL, BRR, RF, and SVM. Two prediction scenarios were evaluated: across‐population prediction, where SNP markers identified from the TP were used to predict the VP, and cross‐prediction, where SNP markers were used to predict the same TP. Each GP model was replicated 100 times per run. This procedure was repeated five times using independent random TP‐VP partitions, and the final PA was calculated as the mean r‐value across repetitions. Boxplots illustrating the performance of each GP model across SNP sets were generated using the ggplot2 package in R.

GWAS‐derived SNP markers using GAGBLUP in GAPIT3

GP was also conducted using the GAGBLUP model implemented in GAPIT3. The 266 accessions were randomly divided into 80% TP (213 accessions) and 20% VP (53 accessions), and phenotypic values of the VP were masked during model training. PA was calculated as the correlation between GEBVs and observed phenotypic values in the VP. This procedure was repeated five times, and mean r‐values were used to assess model performance under both cross‐ and across‐prediction scenarios.

RESULTS

Phenotypic variation in anthracnose resistance

Substantial phenotypic variability was observed among the 266 spinach accessions evaluated for anthracnose resistance. DSI, assessed on a 1–10 scale, ranged from 1 (highly resistant) to 10 (highly susceptible), with a mean score of 8.25 and a standard deviation of 1.89 (Figure 1). The frequency distribution exhibited skewness toward higher susceptibility, indicating that most accessions were moderately to highly susceptible to C. dematium (Figure 1). This broad variation supports the suitability of the panel for GWAS and GP analyses.

Distribution of anthracnose disease severity index among 266 spinach accessions.

Notably, 20 accessions from 266 accessions exhibited disease scores ≤4, including four highly resistant accessions, NSL32629, PI648954, NSL28216, and NSL42771, with scores ≤2.0 (Table 1) collected/originated from the United States, indicating their suitability as parents in breeding programs to develop anthracnose‐resistant hybrids and cultivars. Other resistant accessions included five originated from United States (NSL184378, NSL6089, NSL184379, NSL6085, and PI648951), two accessions (PI176778 and PI171862) from Turkey, one (NSL28218) from Sweden, one (PI491261) from Greece, six accessions (PI648943, PI418978, PI433212, PI648941, PI648942, and PI648948) from China, and one (PI274065) from unknown origin showed resistance with DSI ≤ 4 (Table 1). These accessions represent valuable genetic resources for breeding anthracnose‐resistant spinach.

Distribution of SNPs, principal component analysis, and genetic diversity analysis

After quality control and filtration (MAF > 2%, missing allele < 5%, and heterogeneous rate < 10%), a total of 120,817 SNPs were mapped across six chromosomes of spinach and further utilized for downstream analyses (Figure S1). Chromosome 3 was mapped with a high number of SNPs (31,051), followed by chr4 (24,947), chr5 (24,166), chr1 (16,277), and chr6 (15,890), while chr2 (8486) had fewer SNPs (Figure S1). PCA of the SNP dataset revealed the presence of population structure within the panel. The first three principal components (PC1, PC2, and PC3) explained 32.18%, 5.25%, and 1.79% of the total genetic variation, respectively, collectively accounting for 39.22% of the variability (Figure 3A,B). The inclusion of these PCs as covariates in GWAS models was essential to account for confounding due to structure. A genotype‐based phylogenetic analysis using the NJ method drawn by GAPIT3 reflected into three sub‐clusters in the evaluated spinach germplasm (Figure 3C). Based on the three clusters (Q1 to Q3), the largest group, Q1, contains 235 (88.3%) accessions, with most accessions from Turkey (75), the United States (37), and from other countries. Q2 with 17 (6.4%) accessions primarily from India (8), followed by China (3), Japan (3), Afghanistan (1), Korea (1), and Pakistan (1) (DSI ≤ 4). Q3, with 14 (5.3%) accessions, was dominated by accessions from China (8), followed by one accession from Afghanistan, Japan, Korea, Nepal, Taiwan, and Thailand. Notably, resistant accessions were distributed across all three groups, with 16 in Q1, one in Q2, and three in Q3. Kinship analysis showed that the pairwise kinship coefficients among the accessions ranged from 0.0 to 2.0, indicating varying degrees of relatedness and confirming the necessity of correcting for both population structure and relatedness in association mapping (Figure 3D).

Genome‐wide association analysis (GWAS)

GWAS for anthracnose resistance in 266 spinach accessions identified 20 SNPs significantly associated with disease severity using multiple models implemented in GAPIT3, rMVP, and TASSEL 5 programs with a significance threshold set at LOD (−log10 p) ≥ 6.38, based on a Bonferroni‐corrected genome‐wide significance level with their corresponding t‐test (Table 2 and Table S2, Figure 4 and Figures S4 and S5). SOVchr3_19667279 on chr3 was the most consistent and significant SNP identified across BLINK (LOD = 6.58), FarmCPU (LOD = 6.25), MLMM (LOD = 9.01), MLM (LOD = 5.86), GLM (LOD = 6.44), rMVP, and TASSEL, with an average LOD > 5.5 across the GWAS models (Table 2). This marker explained a substantial proportion of phenotypic variance explained (PVE) (up to 66.81%) and represents a major‐effect locus underlying resistance to C. dematium. The highly significant SNP SOVchr6_42532193 on Chr6 also surpassed the significance threshold across multiple models: BLINK (LOD = 9.37), FarmCPU (LOD = 6.09), MLMM (LOD = 8.84), MLM (LOD = 5.64), GLM (LOD = 6.30), rMVP (LOD > 5.5), and TASSEL (LOD > 4.7), explaining up to 11.43% of PVE, reinforcing the reliability of this association (Table 2). On chr1, SNP (SOVchr1_34595149) was detected exclusively by the BLINK model with a LOD of 7.47, exceeding the threshold and explaining 9.53% of PVE. Three additional SNPs on chr1 (SOVchr1_26510561, SOVchr1_26510563, and SOVchr1_26510597) were identified with LOD > 5.5 by FarmCPU, rMVP‐FarmCPU, and GLM in both GAPIT3 and TASSEL. On chr2, SNP SOVchr2_94539004 was significant in FarmCPU (LOD = 6.21) and GLM and TASSEL GLM (LOD = 6.41), explaining 16.5% of PVE (Table 2). The integration of results from rMVP (GLM, MLM, and FarmCPU) and TASSEL provided strong cross‐validation of highly significant SNPs. Particularly, SNPs on chr3 and chr6 were consistently supported by all three pipelines used in this study. Markers such as SOVchr3_41343, SOVchr6_42532188, and SOVchr6_42532190 on chr3 and chr6, though not surpassing the strict genome‐wide threshold in all models, showed high LOD values (up to 6.69) in rMVP and TASSEL, indicating suggestive signals potentially involved in polygenic control (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

Anthracnose, a destructive foliar disease in spinach caused by C. dematium, represents a major constraint to spinach production worldwide, necessitating the identification of resistant germplasm and elucidation of the underlying genetic architecture (Correll et al., 1994; B. Liu et al., 2017, 2020). This study presents a comprehensive examination of anthracnose resistance in spinach through GWAS and GP, utilizing multiple statistical models and diverse germplasm. The integration of phenotypic, genotypic, and statistical approaches facilitated the identification of significant MTAs and the evaluation of GP accuracy, providing valuable insights for breeding programs focused on developing disease‐resistant cultivars.

The extensive phenotypic variation observed among the 266 spinach accessions for anthracnose resistance, with DSI values ranging from 1 to 10 and a mean of 8.25, underscores the quantitative and polygenic nature of anthracnose resistance (Awika et al., 2020; Correll et al., 1994; B. Liu et al., 2020). Due to logistical constraints, the average disease severity score was used for analysis. Nevertheless, this score exhibited wide phenotypic variation and clear discrimination among accessions, making it suitable for GWAS analysis. The skewed distribution of DSI toward higher susceptibility suggests that resistance is relatively rare in the global spinach gene pool, reinforcing the need for focused germplasm characterization and selection. Notably, 20 accessions exhibited resistance (DSI ≤ 4), including four highly resistant lines (DSI ≤ 2) originating from the United States. These accessions represent valuable sources of resistance alleles for future genetic improvement and pre‐breeding efforts in spinach.

Phylogenetic analysis based on genome‐wide SNP data (120,817 SNPs) provided important insights into the genetic structure and geographical stratification of the spinach germplasm panel. Using the NJ method implemented in GAPIT3, the 266 accessions were grouped into three major sub‐clusters (Q1–Q3), reflecting both geographic origin and genetic divergence (Figure 3C). The largest cluster, Q1, contained 88.3% of the accessions and consisted predominantly of germplasm from Turkey and the United States, two major contributors to global spinach germplasm collections. In contrast, clusters Q2 and Q3 contained genetically distinct accessions primarily originating from South and East Asia, including India, China, and Japan. Importantly, resistant accessions were distributed across all three clusters, suggesting that anthracnose resistance is not confined to a single gene pool or geographic region but may have arisen independently in multiple populations. Similar geographically structured resistance patterns have been reported in spinach for white rust resistance (Shi et al., 2022) and Fusarium wilt (Gyawali et al., 2025).

DSI values exhibited skewness; however, ordinal disease scores are commonly used directly in GWAS of disease resistance to preserve biological interpretability and consistency with previous spinach disease‐resistance studies (Shi et al., 2022). Mean disease severity scores were used for analysis rather than BLUPS. Although averaging across replications reduces environmental variance, BLUP‐based phenotypes can better account for experimental design and improve signal‐to‐noise ratios in association mapping (Piepho et al., 2008). Future GWAS analyses incorporating BLUPs derived from multi‐environment trials may further refine MTAs and improve the robustness of GWAS results.

Using multiple GWAS models (GLM, MLM, MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK) implemented in GAPIT3, rMVP, and TASSEL 5, this study identified 20 significant SNPs associated with anthracnose resistance across six chromosomes (Table 2). Slight differences in LOD values between software tools likely reflect variations in model implementation, parameter estimation methods, and handling of missing data, even though both programs use similar ridge‐regression frameworks (Kaler et al., 2020). The relatively low MAF thresholds used in this study may increase the risk of false positives, highlighting the importance of cross‐model validation and independent confirmation of key loci (Shi et al., 2022).

Among the significant SNPs identified, SOVchr3_19667279 on chromosome 3 emerged as the most consistent and robust marker across multiple models and platforms, explaining up to 66.81% of phenotypic variance. Its detection across GLM, MLM, MLMM, FarmCPU, and BLINK models reinforces its potential biological relevance and importance as a major locus for anthracnose resistance (Table 2). From a breeding perspective, the locus tagged by SOVchr3_19667279 represents a promising candidate for MAS. Another SNP, SOVchr6_42532193 on chromosome 6, was also consistently detected across multiple models and explained up to 14.23% of phenotypic variance, suggesting an additive or complementary effect with the major locus on chromosome 3. SNPs detected by only a single model may reflect either model‐specific sensitivity or false positives; therefore, candidate loci and GP marker sets were primarily derived from SNPs supported by multiple models to reduce the likelihood of spurious associations.

Significant SNPs on chromosomes 1, 2, and 6 were identified by specific GWAS models, suggesting potential model‐dependent sensitivity or smaller effect sizes (Table 2). These loci may contribute to the polygenic architecture of anthracnose resistance, consistent with the understanding that quantitative disease resistance often involves both major and minor genes acting additively or epistatically (Corwin & Kliebenstein, 2017; Poland, 2010). The detection of SNPs across multiple chromosomes further supports the complex genetic architecture underlying anthracnose resistance in spinach (Awika et al., 2020; Bhattarai, Shi, et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2024). The identification of several SNPs across multiple GWAS platforms (GAPIT3, rMVP, and TASSEL 5) further strengthens the reliability of these associations.

Functional annotation of significant SNPs identified several candidate genes potentially involved in anthracnose resistance. The gene SOV1g005480, linked to multiple SNPs on chromosome 1, encodes a Bms1‐type G domain‐containing protein involved in ribosome biogenesis (Table 3). Although its direct role in pathogen resistance has not been well established, ribosomal proteins and associated factors have been implicated in plant stress responses and immune signaling (Hang et al., 2018; Lee et al., 2024; Wegierski et al., 2001). Another candidate gene, SOV3g013880, located near significant SNPs on chromosome 3, encodes a general transcription factor 3C polypeptide 5‐like protein. Although its direct involvement in disease resistance remains unclear, transcription factors play key roles in regulating plant defense responses and stress signaling pathways (Sharma et al., 2024).

Significant SNPs on chromosome 6 were associated with the gene SOV6g010910, which encodes a jasmonate‐induced protein homologous to S. glauca. This suggests a potential role in JA‐mediated defense signaling. JA is a key phytohormone involved in plant defense against necrotrophic pathogens, including Colletotrichum species, the causal agents of anthracnose (Campos et al., 2014; Roychowdhury et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2017). The proximity of this gene to the consistently significant SNP SOVchr6_42532193 supports a potential functional relationship with anthracnose resistance and highlights it as a promising candidate for future functional validation.

The polygenic nature of anthracnose resistance observed in this study is consistent with previous reports in spinach and other crops where multiple loci with moderate effects contribute to disease resistance (Awika et al., 2020; Bhattarai et al., 2022; Thomas et al., 2024). These findings highlight the need for integrated breeding strategies combining MAS and GS approaches.

This study also evaluated the potential of GS for anthracnose resistance using multiple GP models. Predictive performance varied depending on the model and marker set used, consistent with previous findings demonstrating that model choice strongly influences GP accuracy (Alemu et al., 2024). The highest GP accuracy (r = 0.92) was achieved using the gBLUP model, followed by GAGBLUP (Figure 6). However, self‐prediction typically overestimates predictive accuracy and therefore should not be used to evaluate breeding utility. Greater emphasis was placed on cross‐validation and independent validation scenarios, which provide more realistic estimates of predictive performance.

The strong performance of gBLUP is consistent with its ability to effectively capture additive genetic variance in traits controlled by many loci with small effects (Crossa et al., 2017; X. Wang et al., 2018). Similar results have been reported for white rust resistance in spinach (Shi et al., 2022) and in other crops where gBLUP performs well for moderately to highly heritable traits (Crossa et al., 2017, 2025).

The use of GWAS‐derived marker sets (m20, m7, and m6) significantly improved PA compared with randomly selected SNP sets (Tables 4 and 5). This supports the strategy of incorporating trait‐associated markers into GS pipelines to enhance predictive efficiency by prioritizing informative markers and reducing noise from non‐informative loci (Bhattarai et al., 2022; Spindel et al., 2016). In addition, PA for randomly selected SNP sets plateaued beyond approximately 5000 SNPs, consistent with observations in other crops indicating an optimal marker density for GP (Juliana et al., 2019; Spindel et al., 2016).

PA declined substantially when moving from within‐population to across‐population prediction scenarios. Within‐population predictions produced accuracies ranging from r = 0.58 to 0.71, whereas across‐population predictions decreased to r = 0.10–0.14. A similar decline was observed with the GAGBLUP model, indicating potential overfitting of the GP model. These results highlight the strong influence of genetic relatedness between training and VPs on prediction performance. Reduced PA across populations has been widely reported and is commonly attributed to differences in allele frequencies and LD patterns between populations (Norman et al., 2018; Poland & Rutkoski, 2016; Shi et al., 2022). Although the GWAS successfully identified loci associated with the trait, the current GP models show evidence of overfitting and therefore have limited predictive utility for selecting unobserved accessions.

It should also be noted that the GWAS‐derived SNP markers used in the GP analysis were identified using the entire dataset prior to cross‐validation. Consequently, validation subsets were indirectly involved in the marker discovery step, which may lead to inflated estimates of PA due to potential data leakage. Future studies should address this limitation by conducting GWAS within each training fold during cross‐validation or by using independent populations to provide a more rigorous evaluation of GP performance.

Overall, the integration of GWAS and GP provides valuable insights for spinach breeding. The anthracnose‐resistant accessions identified in this study represent useful genetic resources for developing resistant cultivars. Future research should focus on fine‐mapping and functional validation of candidate genes, evaluating resistance stability across environments, and integrating validated markers into GS pipelines to accelerate breeding progress.
