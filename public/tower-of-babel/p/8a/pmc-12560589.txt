Epidemiological trends and age-period-cohort effects on urolithiasis incidence across the BRICS-plus from 1992 to 2021
Authors: Fangzhi Yue, Jianlin Liu, Liang Guo, Chu Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrolithiasis is a substantial public health concern, affects 10–15% of the world’s population and imposing significant health and economic burdens. The BRICS-plus nations (Brazil, Russian Federation, India, China, South Africa, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, Iran, and Ethiopia) represent a block of countries with a fresh and invigorating approach to global health. This study aims to evaluate the epidemiological trends and geographic variations in the urolithiasis burden across these nations.MethodsData on the number, all-age rate, age-standardized rate, and relative change in urolithiasis incidence from 1992 to 2021 within BRICS-plus were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) 2021. Relationships between the age-standardized rate incidence and the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) were assessed using Pearson correlation analyses. Additionally, age-period-cohort modeling was employed to estimate net drift, local drift, age, period, and cohort effects over the past three decades.ResultsFrom 1992 to 2021, total urolithiasis cases increased by 41.89%, while the age-standardized incidence rate decreased by 20.72%, globally. Most BRICS countries, excluding Russian Federation, exhibited a declining trend in the age-standardized incidence rate over the past three decades. Russian Federation registered the highest age-standardized incidence rate (3525.62 per 100,000 population) in 2021, whereas the most substantial reduction of 44.32% was observed in China. The annual net drift ranged between − 2.47% in China to 1.16% in Brazil among the ten countries. A significant positive correlation was found between the incidence rate of urolithiasis and SDI values. Countries exhibited similar age effect patterns, with rates showing an initial increase followed by a decrease with advancing age, along with varying period and cohort effects, indicative of differential control measures and temporal burden trends.ConclusionThe age-standardized incidence rate of urolithiasis showed an overall declining trend across the BRICS-plus from 1992 to 2021. Significant health inequalities persist among these nations, which might partially be attributed to disparities in socio-economic circumstances. Furthermore, our findings emphasize the necessity for targeted interventions across age, period, and cohort dimensions and can guide the design of tailored public health strategies for specific nations and demographics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-025-04525-1.

Introduction

Urolithiasis is a prevalent urological condition characterized by the formation of solid crystals within the kidneys, ureter, bladder, or urethra [1, 2]. As a substantial public health concern, it affects 10–15% of the world’s population [3]. This condition is notorious for episodes of excruciating pain (renal colic), which, alongside associated symptoms like hematuria and nausea, severely disrupts daily activities, sleep, and overall well-being. Additionally, Management of urolithiasis, which spans from medical expulsive therapy to minimally invasive procedures and complex surgical interventions [4], contributes significantly to increased healthcare costs and a substantial burden on healthcare systems worldwide [5, 6]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for in-depth and timely assessments of the escalating disease burden associated with urolithiasis to inform public health strategies, optimize therapeutic algorithms, and guide healthcare infrastructure planning effectively.

Previous studies based on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2019 data have outlined the substantial heterogeneity in the global burden of urolithiasis, particularly highlighting disparities in healthcare access and evidence-based decision-making in developing regions [7–9]. Addressing these disparities requires a focused examination of representative and influential groupings within the global south. As World Health Organization (WHO) Director General Margaret Chan declared, Brazil, Russian Federation, India, China, and South Africa (BRICS) represent a block of countries with a fresh and invigorating approach to global health [10]. Its recent expansion to include Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, Iran, and Ethiopia (BRICS-plus), has created a unique coalition of major economies undergoing rapid socioeconomic transition. Characterized by vast populations, diverse healthcare systems, and varying socioeconomic conditions [11, 12], these nations represent a critical mass facing multifaceted health challenges rooted in their development contexts. Focusing on the epidemiological characteristics of urolithiasis across BRICS-plus therefore offers a unique lens to understand the evolving burden of diseases strongly influenced by lifestyle and environmental factors within rapidly developing populations, and to inform targeted interventions both within these nations and for similar settings globally.

However, traditional descriptive epidemiological reports have limitations in capturing the complex temporal trends and drivers of urolithiasis [7–9]. The Age-Period-Cohort (APC) model is particularly advantageous for understanding how various demographic and temporal factors interact to influence disease burden, providing a compelling framework for urolithiasis investigation [13]. Moreover, the recent update to the GBD 2021 database offers a timely opportunity to further investigate the burden of urolithiasis across the BRICS-plus nations [14, 15]. Data from GBD 2021 facilitates the monitoring of country-specific epidemiological dynamics, providing critical evidence to inform targeted programs and policies aimed at improving urolithiasis-related health outcomes.

This study aims to provide a comprehensive assessment of the urolithiasis burden across the BRICS-plus nations from 1992 to 2021. By employing an APC modeling approach, it deciphers the distinct effects of age, period, and cohort on incidence trends, thereby generating crucial insights for targeted public health planning.

Methods

Data sources

This study utilized the GBD 2021 dataset, accessible through the Global Health Data Exchange GBD Results Tool (https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2021). The GBD 2021 provides estimates of disease burden for 371 conditions across 204 countries and territories, capturing key dimensions including incidence, prevalence, mortality, disability, and associated risk factors [14]. Significant updates in this iteration encompass the incorporation of 19,189 novel data sources for disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), the addition of 12 new conditions, and methodological refinements [14]. Crucially, it also integrates the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on global health metrics [16].

In the GBD 2021, urolithiasis is defined as abnormal formation of crystalline masses along the urinary tract, commonly from calcium compounds, uric acid, struvite, or cystine, generally presenting with waves of severe abdominal or flank pain, hematuria, nausea, or painful or difficult urination, using the International Classification of Diseases, both 9th edition (ICD-9: 592-592.9 and 594-594.9) and 10th edition (ICD-10: N20-N23.0) [16]. Figure 1 illustrates the key steps in modelling the incidence of urolithiasis for the GBD 2021 study. The process involved synthesizing data from multiple administrative sources, applying bias adjustments, and using meta-regression tools to produce standardized estimates. A clear and complete description of data extraction can be found in the Supplementary file. Our analysis specifically extracted global and BRICS-plus national data on urolithiasis incidence counts, all-age incidence rates, and age-standardized incidence rates. Data spanned the period 1992–2021 and encompassed age groups from 15 to 94 years. Cases below age 15 were excluded due to their rarity or absence within the dataset. To calculate the 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), the model selection, parameter estimation, and data quality features from the GBD database were employed [16]. These UIs represent the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles derived from 1000 draw-level simulations of the posterior distribution. Moreover, an updated Socio-demographic Index (SDI) for BRICS-plus countries was provided in the GBD 2021. Comprehensive details on GBD 2021 methodologies are documented elsewhere [14–16]. The utilized data are publicly available and de-identified, with ethical oversight and waiver of informed consent provided by the University of Washington Institutional Review Board (study number: 9060).

Data analysis

Temporal and spatial trend analysis of urolithiasis incidence

We assessed the burden and trends of urolithiasis incidence globally and within BRICS-plus nations from 1992 to 2021. Key metrics analyzed included: total incident cases, all-age incidence rate, age-standardized incidence rate, and relative percentage changes over time. The association between national SDI values and age-standardized incidence rate across the BRICS-plus countries was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient. Furthermore, the age-specific burden distribution was evaluated by stratifying incident cases into four age brackets (15–39, 40–59, 60–79, and 80–94 years) and computing the proportion of total cases within each stratum.

Age period cohort modelling analysis

Input data comprised GBD 2021 estimates of urolithiasis incidence and corresponding population counts for each country/region. The APC model inherently faces a non-identification problem due to the exact linear dependency among age, period, and cohort, which complicates the unique estimation of their individual effects. To solve this problem, we adopted the Intrinsic Estimator (IE) method proposed by Yang et al. [19] and Fu [20]. The IE method decomposes the three temporal trends by imposing geometric orthogonality constraints on the parameter space, effectively separating age, period, and cohort effects while minimizing bias and improving estimation efficiency. Compared to other models, the IE method is considered to provide the best fit [21]. Detailed methodological information is available in other publications [13, 19–21]. Consistent 5-year intervals for age and period were required. Accordingly, the population aged 15–94 years was categorized into 16 age groups (15–19, 20–24, …, 90–94). To enhance temporal specificity and account for short-term fluctuations (e.g., pandemic effects), incidence and population data were centered on mid-year points for six discrete periods (1994, 1999, 2004, 2009, 2014, 2019) rather than using 5-year averages [13, 22]. This structure defined 21 consecutive birth cohorts, spanning mid-year birth intervals from 1900 to 1904 (median 1902) to 2000–2004 (median 2002).

The key output metrics from the APC model include net drift, local drift, the longitudinal age curve, and period and cohort relative risks (RR). Net drift captures the overall log-linear trend of the incidence rate across the entire population by both period and cohort. Local drift outlines the trend for each specific age bracket. The longitudinal age curve depicts the expected age-specific rates for a reference cohort, adjusted for period effects. Period RR and cohort RR measure relative risks across different periods and cohorts, respectively, adjusting for both age and the other comparative (period or cohort). APC modeling was performed using the National Cancer Institute’s web tool (https://analysistools.cancer.gov/apc/) [23]. Subsequent data visualization and statistical analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3). Statistical significance of model coefficients was assessed using two-tailed Wald χ² tests.

Results

Incidence of urolithiasis trends from 1992 to 2021

Table S1 presents the population, total incidences, all-age incidence rate, age-standardized incidence rate, and net drift of urolithiasis for the world and the ten BRICS countries. Over the last thirty years, the number of incidences due to urolithiasis experienced an increase, from 74695.24 thousand (95% UI 61573.54 to 91868.87) in 1992 to 105983.78 thousand (95% UI 88349.36 to 128645.16) in 2021, corresponding to a 41.89% increase (Table S1 and Fig. 2). The age-standardized incidence rate declined from 1567.56 (95% UI 1289.79 to 1934.44) per 100,000 population in 1992 to 1242.84 (95% UI 1034.94 to 1506.99) per 100,000 population in 2021, marking a reduction of 20.72% (Table 1; Fig. 2). The estimated global incidence rate decreased, on average, by just under 1% per year over this period (net drift: -0.84%; 95% CI -0.93 to -0.75).

The incidences of urolithiasis have significantly increased in all BRICS-plus, with the exception of the Russian Federation (Table 1, Table S1 and Fig. 2). A particularly notable increase was observed in the United Arab Emirates, which showed the most substantial rise, remarkably by 680.30%. In 2021, the all-age incidence rate and age-standardized incidence rate for urolithiasis ranged from 514.47 (95% UI 412.74 to 640.99) per 100,000 population and 696.03 (95% UI 567.88 to 864.58) per 100,000 population in Ethiopia to 4834.33 (95% UI 4099.14 to 5655.11) per 100,000 population and 3525.62 (95% UI 2964.71 to 4180.46) per 100,000 population in Russian Federation, respectively. A declining trend was exhibited by five countries from 1992 to 2021, and the most substantial reduction of 44.32% was observed in China. Conversely, Brazil experienced a more significant rise, at 55.92%, while Iran records a modest increase, at 1.10%. According to the APC model estimates, an annual net drift was observed, varying from − 2.47% (95% CI -2.59 to -2.35) for China to 1.16% (95% UI 0.95 to 1.38) for Brazil within ten countries (Table 1). The narrow confidence intervals around most net drift estimates strengthen our confidence in the reported trends. Additionally, a significant positive correlation was identified between the age-standardized incidence rate of urolithiasis and SDI values (r = 0.32, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Time trends in urolithiasis incidence across different age groups

Figure S1 and Table S2 depict the annual percentage change (local drift) in urolithiasis incidence rates for each five-year age group from 15 to 94 years. Globally, negative values, indicating a reduction in burden, were observed across almost all age groups. This pattern was also evident in China, South Africa, and Ethiopia. In contrast, six nations (Brazil, the Russian Federation, Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the United Arab Emirates, and Iran) exhibited increases in incidence rates, as shown by positive local drift values for most age groups. India displayed a divergent trend, with positive values in lower age groups (under 55) but negative values in higher age groups (55 and over). Notably, females in the Russian Federation, particularly older women, exhibited a higher frequency of positive drift values than males.

Figure S2 depicts age-stratified trends in urolithiasis case numbers over time. The global burden of urolithiasis occurred predominantly in young and middle-aged groups, particularly among individuals under 60. This age distribution was consistent across all BRICS-plus countries and remained relatively stable from 1992 to 2021. However, a notable shift has occurred in recent years in the United Arab Emirates, where an increasing proportion of cases has transitioned from the younger (15–39 years) to the middle-aged (40–59 years) population.

Age, period and cohort effects on urolithiasis incidence

Figure 4 and Table S3 depict the age effects, revealing a consistent inverted U-shaped pattern across all nations. The incidence rate of urolithiasis initially increases, peaks in young and middle-aged adulthood, and then declines in older age groups after adjusting for period effects.

Discussion

This study identifies notable fluctuations in the burden of urolithiasis both globally and within BRICS-plus countries during the period from 1992 to 2021. Analysis has indicated a substantial 41.89% increase in the number of incidences; however, the age-standardized incidence rate has declined by 20.72%. Significant variations were observed among the ten BRICS members. The multifaceted epidemiology of urolithiasis is further elucidated by these findings, which reflect considerable health disparities and highlight potential strategies for mitigating its impact across different age groups, time periods, and birth cohorts within BRICS-plus.

The significant positive correlation between the SDI and urolithiasis incidence presents a critical paradox. While higher SDI typically signifies improved overall welfare, it appears to be linked to an increased burden of urolithiasis in many emerging economies. This correlation can be attributed to two primary factors. Firstly, rapid economic development often precipitates detrimental lifestyle changes, such as increased consumption of animal protein, sodium, and processed sugars, coupled with more sedentary behaviors and rising rates of obesity and metabolic syndrome [2, 24–26], which are all recognized risk factors for urolithiasis [5]. Secondly, enhanced healthcare infrastructure and diagnostic capacity in economically developing regions might lead to more frequent diagnoses and reports of urolithiasis cases [2]. The experience of Gulf nations like Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates illustrates this paradox. In these countries, economic diversification and urban sprawl have fueled the lifestyle changes mentioned above [27, 28]. Moreover, investments in medical infrastructure have often prioritized diagnostic and treatment capabilities over primary prevention and public health education, thereby exacerbating a focus on managing rather than preventing the disease [29]. In contrast, China serves as an instructive exemplar of how proactive public health policy can counteract these risks. China’s consistent decline in period and cohort effects reflects the success of national health strategies, such as the Healthy China 2030 initiative, which promoted better dietary habits and higher health literacy among citizens [30–32]. This demonstrates that economic development does not inevitably lead to a higher disease burden if accompanied by targeted health strategies. Consequently, public health strategies for nations experiencing rapid economic growth should extend beyond infrastructure investment to include: the integration of urolithiasis risk-factor screening into primary care, the establishment of national stone registries to better monitor the epidemic and evaluate interventions, and regulatory measures to reduce the salt and sugar content in processed foods. Such a multi-faceted approach is necessary to ensure that economic development does not inadvertently exacerbate the burden of non-communicable diseases like urolithiasis. Meanwhile, a country like Ethiopia faces a different set of challenges, where progress in reducing the disease burden is likely constrained by more fundamental limitations in healthcare access and infrastructure [33, 34], affecting both genuine risk and diagnostic reporting accuracy. It must prioritize strengthening foundational public health and primary care systems. A key step is to integrate basic screening for urolithiasis symptoms into existing community health programs to improve case detection and management.

Egypt’s increase is partly explained by limited healthcare access, challenges in preventive education, and economic fluctuations affecting public health priorities [5, 35]. Unique geographical environment and cultural practices, like rich in oxalates, low fluid intake in arid regions, and dehydration in hot climates, further contribute to the upward period effects historically noted in this region [36–39]. Beyond improving healthcare access, policy interventions in Egypt should prioritize community-level education on dietary modification to reduce oxalate and salt intake, alongside public awareness campaigns promoting adequate hydration, particularly for outdoor workers in its hot climate. In contrast, several nations demonstrate how policy interventions can mitigate this burden. In Iran, initial increases driven by demographic transitions were successfully reversed following the implementation of the Health Sector Evolution Plan after the 2010s, highlighting the impact of responsive healthcare policy [40, 41]. South Africa’s mixed trends also coincide with health initiatives aimed at addressing systemic disparities, suggesting policy innovations hold potential for stabilizing the burden [42]. India’s complex pattern, characterized by periods of decline punctuated by a rise, mirrors a complex interplay of geo-environmental factors, improved but uneven healthcare access, and persistent rural-urban health disparities [43–45]. Similarly, the rising trend in Brazil likely reflects the compounded effect of widespread shifts towards obesogenic diets and insufficiently disseminated preventive healthcare measures, particularly in underserved regions [46–48]. This underscores an urgent need for policies that not only raise national awareness but also explicitly bridge the geographic and socioeconomic disparities in access to primary prevention. Our analysis also emphasizes key demographic risk patterns. The peak rates of incidence among young and middle-aged groups align with existing epidemiological studies [2, 7–9]. Individuals in these age groups often exhibit peak metabolic rates and are frequently subjected to behavioral lifestyle and occupational factors conducive to stone formation [41, 43, 49–51]. The Russian Federation has achieved a measurable reduction in its age-standardized incidence rate of urolithiasis over the thirty decades, suggesting partial success of past public health or clinical efforts. Despite these advances, its high incidence rate still deserves attention. This may be due to the maintenance of structural and demographic risk factors previously associated with the high disease burden in the region, including the maintenance of traditional diet patterns such as meat intake [52], high alcohol intake [53], and cold climate conditions that hinder adequate liquid intake. Of particular concern is the rising incidence among older females, a trend potentially driven by post-menopausal physiological changes, higher susceptibility to urinary tract infections, and gender-specific differences in healthcare-seeking behavior [54, 55]. These findings highlight the need for integrating targeted, prevention-focused strategies, particularly those addressing the needs of high-risk subgroups, within broader national health initiatives.

The evolving epidemiological landscape directly informs clinical practice and long-term surgical planning. Our findings project a growing demand for urological interventions across the BRICS-plus nations, driven not only by rising absolute case numbers but also by the characteristic age distribution of the disease. The concentration of urolithiasis cases among young and middle-aged adults, a pattern which is consistently observed across most studied countries, implies that patients will likely require surgical care during their peak productive years. This creates a paramount need for minimally invasive procedures that offer rapid recovery and minimal disruption to employment and daily life, ensuring broader socioeconomic benefits beyond clinical outcomes. Furthermore, in countries like the United Arab Emirates where the burden is shifting toward middle-aged groups, the healthcare system must prepare for decades of sustained management for these populations. To efficiently address this escalating and decades-long surgical burden, healthcare systems must prioritize investment in expanding capacity for and training in these minimally invasive techniques. Recent advancements, such as the comparative study by Stefano et al. demonstrating the efficacy of robotic pyelolithotomy [56], illustrate how surgical innovation can improve management of complex cases. Integrating such sophisticated technologies will be crucial for optimizing outcomes and resource utilization in the face of the demand highlighted by our findings.

A significant advancement of this study is the application of the APC model to the most recent GBD 2021 data, yielding novel insights beyond previous reports based on GBD 2019. Our analysis reveals important long-term shifts in disease burden through cohort effects. For instance, the declining cohort risk in China signals the success of enduring public health investments, while the rising risk in younger cohorts in Gulf nations points to a growing future burden rooted in early-life factors. Although our period analysis (ending in 2021) does not capture the COVID-19 pandemic’s immediate impact, the consistent pre-pandemic trends established here provide a crucial baseline for evaluating future disruptions to urolithiasis care. Furthermore, this study accurately quantifies changes in the age distribution of the burden from 1992 to 2021 across BRICS-plus. This in-depth assessment enhances our understanding of the complex interplay between natural aging, historical trends, and public health policies in addressing urolithiasis. However, our findings must be interpreted in the context of several limitations inherent to the GBD study design. First, while GBD 2021 is the most comprehensive effort to model global disease burden, its estimates rely on the availability and quality of underlying data, which may be sparse or unreliable in some regions within the BRICS-plus countries. This can lead to underreporting and significant uncertainty in estimates. Second, the modeling assumptions, though robust, may smooth over subnational variations or short-term fluctuations, potentially masking local epidemics or the impact of specific health policies. However, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using 5-year average data (Figures S3-S6) to assess the robustness of our findings to temporal smoothing, and the consistent results strengthen confidence in the observed trends. Third, as an ecological study, our analysis at the national level cannot be directly used to infer causal relationships at the individual level. Finally, while the APC model is powerful, its estimates can be complex to interpret and are subject to the inherent identifiability problem, despite our use of the IE method to mitigate this issue.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive analysis of the urolithiasis burden in BRICS-plus countries from 1992 to 2021. We observed a general decline in the age-standardized incidence rate, yet substantial health disparities persist among these nations. These disparities may be partly attributable to differences in socioeconomic circumstances. Our findings underscore the complex interplay of age, period, and cohort effects in shaping urolithiasis trends. The distinct patterns observed in each country, shaped by unique socioeconomic, cultural, and policy contexts, highlight the necessity for tailored public health strategies. We recommend that future research investigate the impact of targeted policies and interventions to inform a more comprehensive approach to managing urolithiasis in these rapidly developing nations.
