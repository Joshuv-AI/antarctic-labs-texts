The reduced cortical bone density in vertebral bodies: risk for osteoporotic fractures? Insights from CT analysis
Authors: Yong Yang, Feng Liao, Xingbo Xing, Nianxi Liao, Dawei Wang, Xin Yin, Yihao Liu, Jidong Guo, Li Li, Huadong Wang, Chunyan Li, Yang Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is a corresponding increase in the prevalence of osteoporosis and related fractures with the aging population on the rise. Furthermore, osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) may contribute to higher patient mortality rates. It is essential to conduct research on risk factors for OVCF and provide a theoretical basis for preventing such fractures.MethodsWe retrospectively recruited patients who had spine CT for OVCF or back pain. Demographic and CT data were collected. Quantitative computed tomography (QCT) software analyzed the CT data, using subcutaneous fat and paraspinal muscles as reference standards for BMD processing. BMD of cortical and cancellous bones in each patient’s vertebral body was determined.ResultsIn this study, 144 patients were divided into non-OVCF (96) and OVCF (48) groups. Non-OVCF patients had higher cortical BMD of 382.5 ± 52.4 to 444.6 ± 70.1 mg/cm3, with T12 having the lowest BMD (p < 0.001, T12 vs. L2). Cancellous BMD ranged from 128.5 ± 58.4 to 140.9 ± 58.9 mg/cm3, with L3 having the lowest BMD. OVCF patients had lower cortical BMD of 365.0 ± 78.9 to 429.3 ± 156.7 mg/cm3, with a further decrease in T12 BMD. Cancellous BMD ranged from 71.68 ± 52.07 to 123.9 ± 126.2 mg/cm3, with L3 still having the lowest BMD. Fractured vertebrae in OVCF patients (T12, L1, and L2) had lower cortical bone density compared to their corresponding vertebrae without fractures (p < 0.05).ConclusionsT12 had the lowest cortical BMD and L3 had the lowest cancellous BMD in OVCF patients, with T12 also having the highest incidence of osteoporotic fractures. These findings suggest that reduction in cortical BMD has a greater impact on OVCF than reduction in cancellous BMD, along with biomechanical factors.

Introduction

With the aging population on the rise globally, there is a corresponding increase in the prevalence of osteoporosis and fractures related to it, including fractures of the spine, hip, wrist [1, 2]. Among these fractures, osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) are the most common, accounting for nearly 70% of cases [3]. OVCF tend to occur earlier than other major osteoporotic fractures, such as hip fractures. OVCF can result in intense pain, alterations in balance, muscle exhaustion, an increased risk of falling, and subsequent fractures [2]. Consequently, OVCF significantly diminishes an individual’s quality of life. Furthermore, there is a possibility that OVCF can lead to higher mortality rates in patients [2, 4].

The current understanding of bone loss and osteoporosis has predominantly focused on trabecular bone, leading to a perspective referred to as ‘trabeculocentric‘ [5]. This perspective has been shaped by the belief that trabecular bone is more metabolically active due to its larger surface area and the common occurrence of fractures in skeletal sites primarily composed of trabecular bone, such as vertebrae [6]. However, recent research has challenged these assumptions by highlighting the significance of the cortex in the appendicular skeleton and vertebral body, which accounts for the majority of bone loss in later life [7, 8]. Additionally, it has been observed that 80% of fractures are non-vertebral and primarily occur in sites that contain cortical bone, such as the proximal femur and distal radius [6]. Interestingly, patients with ankylosing spondylitis exhibit increased cortical bone mineral density, decreased cancellous bone mineral density (BMD) [9], and rarely experience OVCF in early stage. This raises the question of whether cortical porosity or cancellous porosity plays a more prominent role in the occurrence of OVCF.

Another methodological factor that contributes significantly to the prevailing trabeculocentric perspectives is the larger scale of trabeculae (around hundreds of microns) compared to cortical pores (around tens of microns) [6]. This size disparity has made trabeculae more easily detectable through non-invasive imaging, particularly in recent years with advancements in imaging technology. However, as imaging techniques have improved, there has been an increasing recognition of the role played by cortical bone in terms of bone loss [10, 11]. For this study, computed tomography (CT) data for patients were obtained from the Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS). And the CT data was imported into a quantitative computed tomography (QCT) software package for processing BMD [12, 13]. The objective of this study is to investigate the significant loss of cortical or cancellous bone in patients with OVCF and determine whether cortical porosity or cancellous porosity has a major impact on the occurrence of OVCF.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

This study was waived by the ethical committee of the Fourth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital. Data was retrospectively extracted from the PACS and Radiological Information System (RIS/HINACOM) of patients who underwent CT examinations between January and December 2022 at the Fourth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital. All patients included in this study were of Asian ethnicity from China. CT images were included in DICOM 3.0 format. Descriptive data, such as gender, age, and body mass index, of the patients need to be collected.

The study population was classified into two groups: those non- osteoporotic compression vertebral fractures (non-OVCF) and those with OVCF. And two groups of patients aged between 60 and 90 were included. To be included in the non-OVCF group, patients underwent surgical treatment for conditions such as lumbar disc herniation, lumbar spinal stenosis, or lumbar spondylolisthesis. Prior to surgery, routine lumbar CT was performed, which included the T12 vertebral body. Additionally, patients had to have no history of osteoporotic fractures or vertebral body shaping surgery, no previous implant retention such as pedicle screws in the vertebral body, and no underlying diseases such as mandatory spondylitis, fluorosis, or osteosclerosis, and so on.

The inclusion criteria for patients with OVCF are as follows: Patients should have no obvious vertebral compression fractures resulting from trauma, but should include patients with fragility fractures caused by falls. Additionally, they should have a history of low back pain within one month, exhibiting a high signal on MRI lipolysis indicative of a recent single vertebral fracture, and must have been admitted to the hospital for a complete CT examination. Also, patients had to have no history of vertebral body shaping surgery, no previous implant retention such as pedicle screws in the vertebral body, and no underlying diseases such as mandatory spondylitis, fluorosis, or osteosclerosis, and so on. Patients with old vertebral fractures and those who did not display a high signal on MRI lipolysis were excluded from the study.

Computed tomography and ROI placement

Computed tomography data for patients who underwent the examination for any reason using GE Discovery CT750 HD were obtained from the PACS for evaluation. All personal information, including patient name, patient number, and accession number, was anonymized. Information, including scan protocol and reconstruction parameters in DICOM tag, were retained. The CT scan technique parameters were 120 kVp, 10 mAs, and 1.0–1.25 mm slice thickness. A standard bone tissue convolution filter was used in the reconstruction.

Spine CT images with a slice thickness of 1.0–1.25 mm were inputted into BMD calculation software (Yizhun-BMD v1.1). The region of interest (ROI) was defined as the spongy bone zone in the central plane of each vertebra, parallel to the end plate (Fig. 1). The ROIs were automatically segmented by a deep-learning algorithm, excluding parts with abnormalities such as implants, tumors, and bone spots. The placement of the ROIs was confirmed by a group of radiologists with at least 3 years of experience in interpreting skeletal CT images.

Determining bone mineral density

For the BMD processing, we utilized a QCT software package (Yizhun-BMD v1.1) that employs subcutaneous fat and paraspinal muscles as reference standards instead of an external phantom with a known mineral content. The correction technique in the software is based on the fact that muscle and subcutaneous fat possess a known linear attenuation coefficient (µ), allowing for the correction of measured CT values. Subsequently, the corrected CT bone value is converted into an equivalent BMD (mg/cm3). The final BMD value for the current examination is determined by the volume-weighted average of the selected ROIs.

Statistical analysis

Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data. Differences between the two groups were evaluated with unpaired Student’s t test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism v8.0.2, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Cortical and cancellous BMD were assessed in patients with non-OVCF

This study included a total of 96 patients with non-OVCF, of whom 32 were males and 64 were females, with an average age of 76 ± 10 years and an average BMI of 22.77 ± 1.96 (kg/m2). The cortical BMD from the T12 vertebral body to the L4 vertebral body was 382.5 ± 52.4, 403.4 ± 60.1, 421.1 ± 62.7, 431.6 ± 61.1, and 444.6 ± 70.1 mg/cm3. The cortical BMD was found to be lowest in the T12 vertebral body, and there was a significant difference between the cortical BMD of T12 and L1 (Fig. 2).

The cancellous BMD was found to be lowest in the L3 vertebral body, and there was a significant difference between the cancellous BMD of L3 and T12 (Fig. 2).

The position of the injured vertebra and its cortical and cancellous BMD were assessed in patients with OVCF

This study included a total of 48 patients with OVCF, comprising 17 males and 31 females, with an average age of 76 ± 10 years and an average BMI of 22.80 ± 2.39 (kg/m2). The male to female ratio in the OVCF group was 0.55:1, while in the non-OVCF group it was 0.5:1, with no statistically significant difference between the two groups (P > 0.05). Among them, there were 2 cases of T8 vertebral fractures, 3 cases of T10 vertebral fractures, 3 cases of T11 vertebral fractures, 13 cases of T12 vertebral fractures, 12 cases of L1 vertebral fractures, 8 cases of L2 vertebral fractures, 3 cases of L3 vertebral fractures, and 4 cases of L4 vertebral fractures (Fig. 3).

The cortical BMD of the injured vertebrae, upper vertebrae, and lower vertebrae were 366.1 ± 54.3, 369.3 ± 147.7, and 374.9 ± 54.6 mg/cm3, respectively. There was no significant difference in the cortical BMD between the injured vertebral body and the upper or lower vertebral bodies (Fig. 3).

The cancellous BMD of the injured vertebrae, upper vertebrae, and lower vertebrae were 142.5 ± 100, 86.08 ± 38.35, and 83.29 ± 57.9 mg/cm3, respectively. Significant differences were observed between the cancellous BMD of the injured vertebral body and that of the upper vertebral body, as well as between the injured vertebral body and that of the lower vertebral body (Fig. 3).

This may be because the cancellous bone of the injured vertebral body becomes compressed after the fracture, leading to an increase in cancellous BMD compared to that of the adjacent vertebral body. On the other hand, the cortical bone, which was not compressed, did not experience significant changes in BMD.

Cortical and cancellous BMD were assessed in patients with OVCF

To clarify the role of cortical and cancellous BMD in vertebral osteoporotic fractures, we conducted further measurements in 48 patients with OVCF. The mean cortical BMD from T12 to L4 was 365.0 ± 78.9, 397.5 ± 147.8, 404.2 ± 110.2, 429.3 ± 156.7, and 420.0 ± 94.9 mg/cm3, respectively (Fig. 4). The cortical BMD of T12 vertebral body was still the lowest, with a significant difference compared to that of L3. The mean cancellous BMD from T12 to L4 was 112.9 ± 173.6, 123.9 ± 126.2, 107.1 ± 117.7, 71.68 ± 52.07, and 100.1 ± 114.0 mg/cm3, respectively. The cancellous BMD of L3 vertebral body was still the lowest, with a significant difference compared to that of L1 vertebral body (Fig. 4).

A comparison was made between the cortical BMD of fractured and non-fractured vertebral bodies in patients with OVCF

In patients with OVCF, we conducted a further examination of the cortical BMD of the T12, L1, and L2 vertebral bodies, which are known for their high incidence of fractures. The cortical BMD of the unfractured T12 vertebral body was measured at 354.6 ± 53.2 mg/cm3, while the fractured T12 vertebral body had a cortical BMD of 325.6 ± 40.4 mg/cm3 (P<0.05). For the unfractured L1 vertebral body, the cortical BMD was 365.5 ± 48.4 mg/cm3, whereas the fractured L1 vertebral body had a cortical BMD of 328.3 ± 23.4 mg/cm3 (P<0.01). Lastly, the cortical BMD of the unfractured L2 vertebral body was found to be 384.7 ± 52.0 mg/cm3, while the fractured L2 vertebral body had a cortical BMD of 355.9 ± 21.7 mg/cm3 (P<0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) is currently the clinical gold standard for diagnosing osteoporosis [14]. DXA provides a T-score value to compare the aBMD of an individual with the average value for healthy 30-year-old women for diagnosing osteoporosis. However, due to the planar nature and limited resolution of DXA, it cannot detect cortical porosity. To overcome this limitation, clinical CT can be utilized to provide a 3D or volumetric assessment of cortical and trabecular bone density (volumetric BMD (vBMD); mg/cm3). By incorporating a reference phantom with known bone mineral densities (typically hydroxyapatite (HA) or dipotassium phosphate (K2HPO4)) in clinical CT images, it is possible to convert CT Hounsfield units (HU) into equivalent vBMD. This technique is commonly known as quantitative CT (QCT) [15]. Hence, we evaluated the cortical and cancellous BMD of vertebral bodies in patients with or without osteoporotic fractures using clinical CT.

Our results revealed significant findings that the cortical BMD in thoracic 12 was the lowest, while the cancellous BMD in lumbar 3 was the lowest among patients who did not experience osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. Additionally, the probability of osteoporotic fractures occurring in the thoracolumbar region is the highest. In the past, there have been few reported imaging data prior to the occurrence of osteoporotic fractures, and the imaging data after the fracture cannot truly reflect the pre-fracture situation. Patients with lumbar degenerative diseases in our hospital who require surgical treatment undergo routine preoperative lumbar CT examinations. We collected lumbar CT data from these patients and analyzed it. We observed that the patient had the lowest cortical bone density in the twelfth thoracic vertebra, with a gradual increase in cortical bone density from the twelfth thoracic vertebra to the fourth lumbar vertebra. This finding aligns with the incidence of OVCF in patients. Interestingly, the patient exhibited the lowest cancellous BMD in the third lumbar vertebra and the highest cancellous BMD in the twelfth thoracic vertebra, which contradicts the expected pattern for OVCF. These results suggest that cortical bone may play a more significant role in OVCF compared to cancellous bone. Simultaneously, there is a rise in mechanical stress at the junction of the thoracic and lumbar spine, which reaches its peak at the twelfth thoracic vertebra or the first lumbar vertebra [16–18]. Alternatively, mechanical support from the rib cage and paravertebral muscles may aid in reducing the risk of OVCF in the middle thoracic and lower lumbar spine [18, 19]. To gain further clarity on how the cortical and cancellous BMD of the vertebral body changes during OVCF, we also collected and analyzed CT imaging data from patients who had experienced such fractures.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the thoracolumbar spine is at a high risk for OVCF [15, 20–22]. A decrease in BMD, along with biomechanical factors, is one of the main causes of osteoporotic fractures in the thoracolumbar spine. Following a vertebral fracture, the compression of cancellous bone leads to a significant increase in its BMD compared to adjacent vertebral bodies. However, this increase does not accurately reflect the BMD of cancellous bone prior to the fracture. Simultaneously, we observed that the cortical bone of the injured vertebrae in osteoporotic fractures did not exhibit an increase in BMD when compared to adjacent vertebrae, which can reflect the cortical BMD before the fracture. This study included 48 patients with OVCF, including 13 cases of T12 fractures (27%), 12 cases of L1 fractures (20%), and 8 cases of L2 fractures (17%), which together accounted for 64%. In this study, we discovered that the cortical BMD of the T12 vertebral body was the lowest among patients with OVCF. Additionally, we observed that the cancellous BMD of the L3 vertebral body was also the lowest. However, the T12 vertebral body exhibited the highest incidence of osteoporotic fractures. This result indicates that, in the process of osteoporotic fractures, the decrease in cortical BMD plays a more significant role than the decrease in cancellous BMD.

To clarify the important role of decreased cortical BMD in osteoporotic fractures, we investigated the vertebral bodies (T12, L1, L2) with higher incidence of osteoporotic fractures. It was found that the cortical BMD of the vertebral bodies (T12, L1, L2) in patients with osteoporotic fractures was significantly lower than that of the vertebral bodies without fractures. The cortical bone accounts for 30–60% of the vertebral body and supports 45–75% of the axial load during compression [23]. The cortex of the femoral neck contributes 40–90% of the bending rigidity [24]. The removal of trabeculae from the femoral neck only resulted in a 7% reduction in fracture load [25], indicating the dominant role of cortical bone in this area.

Our study had several limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design of the study in a single spine center unavoidably led to bias in patient selection and data collection. Although this aligns with the probability of osteoporotic fractures in the thoracolumbar region reported in previous literature [20], a larger sample size would better reflect clinical reality. Thirdly, the DXA could not be estimated due to the potential increase in pain caused by transporting patients with vertebral osteoporosis fractures. Prospective, multicenter, and extensive research is needed to better understand the important role of reduced cortical BMD in the occurrence of OVCF.

Conclusion

In the population without OVCF, the lowest cortical BMD is observed from T12 to L4, with the thoracic 12 region showing the lowest density. Additionally, the lumbar 3 vertebral body has the lowest cancellous BMD. In patients with OVCF, there is a further decrease in cortical BMD in the thoracolumbar vertebrae, which is associated with a higher probability of osteoporotic fractures in this specific region. Notably, the T12 vertebral body has the highest incidence of osteoporotic fractures. It is suggested that, when osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures occur, the decrease in cortical BMD plays a more significant role than the decrease in cancellous BMD.
