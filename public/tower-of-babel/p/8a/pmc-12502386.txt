Prevalence of intrinsic capacity impairment and associated factors among retired older people in Cameroon
Authors: Marie-Josiane Ntsama Essomba, Joel Kevin Andela Ebidiye, Jean Jacques Noubiap, Jules Motto Ndoumbe, Félicité Djuikwo Teukeng, Maturin Tabue Teguo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly identification of older adults at increased risk for functional decline remains challenging in the majority of African countries. We aimed to determine the prevalence of intrinsic capacity (IC) impairment and associated factors among a group of retired Cameroonians.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included retired individuals aged 60 years or older. We collected sociodemographic data and geriatric syndromes. The Integrated Care to Older People (ICOPE) screening tool was used to assess the domains of IC. Multivariable regression analysis was used to assess factors associated with IC impairment.ResultsA total of 375 older adults underwent baseline step 1 screening (42.7% Male; mean age 67.9 ± 6.03 years), of whom 356 (94.9%) had a positive result. Step 2 assessment was conducted in 281 participants (78.9%), confirming IC impairment in 182 individuals (48.5%). The most frequently impaired domains were cognition (49.8%), vision (44.8%), and hearing (43.1%). In univariable analysis, participants with IC impairment were more likely to be older (mean age 69.03 ± 6.58 vs. 66.8 ± 5.04 years; p = 0.037), have lower educational level (p< .001), report comorbidities (p =.031), a history of falls (p = .035), and sleep disorders (p = 0.019). Male participants were more likely to have preserved IC compared to females (p =.033). After adjusting for age and sex, factors associated with IC impairment included low educational level [odds ratio (OR) 3.04; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.4–6.7], history of falls (OR 4.02; 95% CI: 1.11–14.6), and sleep disorders (OR 2.9; 95% CI: 1.4–6.2).ConclusionIdentification of older at risk of IC impairment in Cameroon may help tailor further policies for Healthy Aging taking into account available resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06421-7.

Introduction

By 2050, one fifth of the world’s population will be over 60, of which 80% will live in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1]. As the proportion of older people increases worldwide, health systems in LMICs need a policy shift to meet the challenge of optimising care of the ageing population. Although several evidence support the importance of equitable distribution of the available healthcare resources across age groups, early identification of older adults at increased risk for functional decline remains challenging in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA). Indeed, despite efforts to improve level of care, mortality remains high among older people in SSA, owing to multimorbidity but the role of frailty has also been pointed out [2, 3]. According to a systematic review conducted in 2021, the prevalence of physical frailty in Many African countries varies between 10 and 37% and was higher in comparison to other regions of the world [4]. However, rely solely on frailty manifestations to identify older adults with declining functional reserves may result in missed opportunities for earlier intervention. Thus, programmes to promote primary prevention in the community are not only relevant, but also necessary to deliver appropriate patient-centered interventions. Furthermore, family solidarity remains the main support to afford healthcare-related expenses but it is not enough, especially when dealing with impaired functioning of older adults. Based on the concept of Healthy Ageing, the World Health Organization(WHO) has published guidelines to promote Integrated Care for Older People(ICOPE) in the community [5]. ICOPE provides tools and guidance to community health workers to develop community-based, integrated-care approaches to support older people intrinsic capacity and functional ability. The program consists on the early detection of the decline on domains of intrinsic capacity(IC), including impaired locomotion, malnutrition, visual impairment, hearing loss, cognitive impairment and depressive symptoms [5, 6]. Several studies provide valuable insights on implementation of the ICOPE approach to screen IC impairment in other settings [7–9]. ICOPE can serve as a template for further national guidelines to allocate available healthcare resources, taking into account the growing older population in many African countries. Fortunately, there has been an increase in capacity building, manpower development and institutionalization of geriatric care in SSA. In Senegal, the government has introduced the « Plan Sesame », a national free health care program for older people and the number of geriatric units is keep increasing in the country [10, 11]. In Nigeria, dedicated geriatrics wards with trained interdisciplinary teams have been build up since 2016 and training in geriatric medicine have been included in the residency program [12]. In 2019, the prevalence of frailty was estimated to be 36% among community-dwellers aged 55 years and above in Cameroon [13]. The high prevalence of age-related conditions including cognitive impairment [14] and sarcopenia [15] is also a matter of concern as geriatric medicine is still at its infancy and for now policies on ageing are not available in Cameroon. There is evidence that frailty is linked to dysregulation of multiple systems including stress-response, metabolism and musculoskeletal systems that are also involved in IC decline [16, 17]. Decline in IC may underlie the decreased homeostatic reserves that culminate in older adults living with frailty. Screening for IC impairments identifies older adults at higher risk of incident frailty and incident disability [18]. The construct of IC appears to provide valuable predictive information on an individual’s subsequent functioning [19]. Thus, we aimed to determine the prevalence and factors associated with IC impairment in a group of older people in Cameroon.

Methods

Study design and setting

We carried out a cross-sectional study from February to June 2024 in Yaounde, the capital of Cameroon. The participants were recruited among retired older people At the Yaounde 3 district Tax Department and in the 4 centers of the National Social Insurance Fund. Instituted in 1967, The National Social Insurance Fund provides social insurance for employees in the private and public sectors in Cameroon.

Sampling

Given the absence of representative data on intrinsic capacity impairment in Cameroon, the prevalence of impairments was considered unknown for the sample size calculation. Using Cochran’s formula and a prevalence of intrinsic capacity impairment of 84.3% reported in India [20], a 95% confidence level, and a Margin of error of 5%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 204. Accounting for a 10% non-response rate, the final target sample size was set at 227. Due to financial constraints, a convenience sample of retirees was made. We ask volunteers during the period when they usually come in these centers to collect their retirement pension.

Study population

Inclusion criteria

We included individuals aged ≥ 60 years who provided informed written consent.

Non-inclusion criteria

Participants who presented with clinical conditions that would impede assessment such as severe dementia, severe Parkinson’s disease or severe disability were not included.

Method of data collection

Demographic and clinical data

Demographic data included: age, sex, marital status, current activity and educational level (illiterate, primary, secondary, university). Clinical data included comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes, osteoarthritis, and the number of drugs. Geriatric syndromes including history of falls during the 12 past months, sleep disorders, polypharmacy and activities of daily living (ADLs) dependency were recorded. Polypharmacy was defined as the concomitant use of more than 5 medications. Functional ability assessement was derived from the Katz et al. ADLs tool [21]. Dependency was defined as the presence of one or more incapacity to perform ADLs.

Domains of intrinsic capacity assessment

IC were assessed using the ICOPE tool which includes the screening of the six key domains as proposed by WHO [5]: cognition, locomotion, vitality, vision, hearing and psychosocial well-being. The ICOPE screening tool has been validated in various settings, including low and middle-income countries, with a good construct validity and clinical utility [19, 22].

Any impairment reported for one of the six IC domains with the step 2 assessment was given a score of 1, otherwise 0. The IC composite score was then calculated by adding the score of the six domains, ranged from 0 to 6. The lower IC composite score indicates a greater IC.

Data analysis

Data were analysed with the Statistical Package for Social Sciences for Windows (SPSS 23.0, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Quantitative variables are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and proportions. Quantitative variables were compared using the Student’s T test or U-Mann Witney test when needed. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-squared test or Fisher exact T test as needed. Variables with p-value < 0.1 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable regression model. Associations are reported as odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI), with the group without IC impairment as the reference group. A two-tailed p-value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The study included 375 participants (42.7% Male, mean age 67.9 ± 6.03 years). Most participants resided in urban areas (76.8%), were married (47.5%) and achieved secondary education (49.1%). All participants were retirees but 39.2% of them had other income producing activities. Comorbidities were present in 75.5% of participants. Following the in-depth assessment, 182 participants (48.5%) were found to have an IC impairment. The median (IQR) IC composite score was 2 [1–3]. Cognitive decline was observed in 49.8% (140/281) of participants, with a median (IQR) Mini-Cog score of 2 [1–3]. Impaired locomotion was reported in 19.9% (56/281), with a mean SPPB score of 5.5 ± 2.9. Impaired vitality was found in 4.3% (12/281), with a mean MNA-SF score of 11.5 ± 2.1. Depressive symptoms were present in 21.4% (60/281), with a median (IQR) GDS-15 score of 5 [3–7]. Visual and hearing impairments were reported in 44.8% (126/281) and 43.1% (121/281) of participants, respectively.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of IC impairment and its associated factors among a group of Cameroonian retirees. At step 1 screening, the Majority of participants presented with At least one alert indicating potential IC impairment. Following the step 2, a high prevalence of confirmed IC impairment was observed with cognition, vision and hearing as the most frequently affected domains. Compared to those with preserved IC, participants with IC impairment were older, predominantly female, had lower educational level, more comorbidities, and were more likely to report a history of falls and sleep disorders. After adjusting for age and sex, low educational level, previous falls and sleep disorders remained independently associated with IC impairment.

The high prevalence of IC impairment found in our study is consistent with previous studies. In France, 94% of older adults were screened positive At step 1, with 90% confirmed to have IC impairments At step 2 [8]. In a cross-sectional study conducted in India, IC impairment was present in 84.3% of older community-dwellers [20]. In China, a prevalence of 69.1% was observed using step 1 [28] and 39.9% using a full assessment in a larger cohort in 2021 [29]. The most common impaired domains in our study were cognition, hearing and vision similar to findings in Singapour [22]. Other studies found different domains to be predominant: locomotion, vision and hearing in India [20], vitality and cognition were in France [8]. These discrepancies are likely due to variations in assessment tools, populations, and definitions of IC impairment. In this study, we used the validated stepwise ICOPE approach, using step 1 as a community-level screening tool and step 2 as a structured, in-depth assessment. The step 1 screening tool has been validated for its ability to correctly identify IC impairment, which permits assessors to be confident to refer older adults and undergo full assessment when the screening tests yield positive results [19, 30]. Despite growing interest on the IC concept, both its definition and assessment in the clinical setting remain challenging. However, evidence suggests that IC offers a broader reflection of an older person’s health beyond comorbidities or chronological age [19, 31]. It underscores physiological reserve and resilience of an individual, thereby promoting a life-course approach to health rather than a disease-centered one.

Our participants were retired, urban-dwelling older adults, many of whom remained economically active. This profile is comparable to other study populations in India, China and Brazil [9, 20, 31]. The presence of IC impairment among relatively healthy, independent older adults highlights the limitations of current clinical practices in Cameroon, which tend to focus on overt frailty. Indeed, current approach in our setting focus on reacting to clinically manifest diseases. Older people will refrain seeking medical advice unless they exhibit symptoms. Delaying care until functional decline arise can result in missed opportunities for early interventions, especially in a context where universal health coverage is limited and out-of-pocket health expenses are common. Although the IC construct intend to promote an integrated approach with functional ability of older people as a key element, its implementation is not effective in many African countries. In Cameroon, geriatric medicine is still at its infancy and aging specialists are scarce. Our findings suggest that we should focus on the diversity of older adults even those who are independent. Programmes to promote early identification of older people at risk in the community would probably implies empowerment for meaningful community engagement of all the stakeholders.

We observed that women were more likely to have IC impairments than men—a trend consistent with previous studies [19, 32–34]. In Cameroon, older women appear to be more vulnerable. In a cross-sectional study including 403 Cameroonians aged 55 and over, women were more likely to live alone and to have frailty [35]. In another cross-sectional study involving 104 community-dwellers aged 55 and above, women were more likely to have low muscle strength than men [14]. Biological and social factors may account for these disparities. There is evidence that dysregulation of musculoskeletal system and low-grade inflammation may overlap with IC domains [36, 37]. It has been suggested that sex differences in inflammation as indicated by elevated C-reactive protein and fibrinogen [38] may also contribute to sex differences in IC trajectories. The role of socioeconomic characteristics in gender differences in self-reported health and functional limitations among older adults in Cameroon has also been pointed out [39] as in other settings [34].Our findings highlight the importance to address gender-specific disparities in ageing. Low level of education was associated with IC impairment in our study, reinforcing existing literature’s findings that socioeconomic factors play crucial roles in determining health outcomes in later life and influencing the overall process of healthy ageing [34, 40, 41]. In Cameroon, low education correlates with poor health outcomes and limited access to preventive care [39]. Education enhances economic opportunity, access to healthcare and adequate nutrition—all of which may influence IC trajectories.

History of falls was another associated factor to IC impairment. Our findings are supported by studies from China and Europe that identified lower IC scores and specific domain impairments as predictors of falls. Shen et al. showed that declined IC composite scores were associated with increased risks of falls in hospitalized older adults in China(OR 0.64, 95%CI 0.57–0.72) [42]. Liu et al. demonstrated that impaired vitality, locomotion, and psychology domains predicted the incidence of future falls within two years [43]. In community-dwelling octogenarians from the Aging and Longevity in the Sirente geographic area (IlSIRENTE) study, there was a strong association between high IC and lower odds of falls (OR 0.31, 95% CI 0.16–0.60, p <.001). Individuals with higher IC were less likely to report recent falls, with locomotion being an independently associated domain [33]. Falls are influenced by multiple factors among which the complex interaction between cognitive, musculoskeletal and sensory components is essential to ensure ambulation. Indeed, cognitive decline may impair hazard perception, while sensory losses may lead to reduced confidence regarding their environment and apprehension towards engaging locomotion. Consequently, older adults may limit their activities to prevent injuries, which can result in progressive functional decline. Furthermore, poor nutritional status may lead to decreased muscle mass and function. Older adults may therefore exhibit slow gait and poor balance, thereby increasing the risk of recurrent falls.

Sleep disorders were also independently associated with IC impairment. While this association is underreported, growing evidence supports links between poor sleep and frailty. A cross-sectional study conducted in China showed a high prevalence of sleep disorders among community-dwelling older adults and its findings also supported the hypothesized association between sleep disorders and frailty [44]. A systematic review with meta-analysis published in 2023 showed that older adults with poor sleep quality have 1.49-fold higher odds of being frail compared with their counterparts [45]. Another systematic review with meta-analysis conducted in 2024 found that sleep duration disorders and poor sleep quality were associated with pre-frailty and frailty in community-dwelling older adults in several countries [46]. It has been hypothesized that sleep disorders could result in altered metabolic and inflammatory pathways, such as decreased levels of insulin-like growth factor-1 and increased level of interlukin-6 and C-reactive protein, which are involved in low-grade inflammation, a hallmark of frailty [47]. Sleep disorders can lead to symptoms such as fatigue, gait disturbances and poor physical activity, which can impede with domains of IC. On the other hand, poor sleep quality could cause psychological, social isolation or loneliness. This suggest that sleep disorders may have both direct and indirect effects on domains of IC including locomotion and psychological well-being. Further research are needed to characterize the impact of sleep disorders on IC status in older adults.

Strength and limitations

To the best of our knowledge this is the first study to comprehensively assess IC and associated factors in Cameroon using the full ICOPE assessment. Our study highlights the necessity to pay Attention on disparities in the ageing population in Cameroon especially with regards to gender-related inequities. Special Attention should be paid to the older adults with less education, or those having sleep disorders and history of falls because they were more likely to have IC impairment. Our findings suggest that ICOPE can serve as a template for further national guidelines to allocate available local resources to address the ongoing demographic transition. A comparison of baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants is presented in Supplementary Table 1. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding age, sex, marital status, education level, income-generating activities and comorbidities. These findings support the internal validity of the study results. However, we acknowledge that the convenience sampling employed may have introduced selection bias and limits the generalizability of our findings. Older retirees who were more accessible, socially engaged may have been overrepresented while those who were homebound, with poor socioeconomic support or residing in rural areas may have been underrepresented. The latter are potentially prone to intrinsic capacity impairment suggesting that the observed prevalence may underestimate the true burden in the broader population.

Conclusion

This study showed a high prevalence of IC impairment in this group of retirees in Cameroon. Our findings suggest that preventing falls, diagnosing and managing sleep disorders may delay the transition to IC impairment. The association of level of education and IC suggests the possible long-term impact of education on Healthy Aging. Despite growing interest, screening and of IC impairment into clinical practice remains challenging in our setting. Larger population-based studies are needed to monitor IC trajectories and assess other outcomes including incident frailty and disability.
