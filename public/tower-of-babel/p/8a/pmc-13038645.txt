A theoretical bayesian decision tree model for prioritizing RA-TKA under constrained resources robotic-assisted versus conventional total knee arthroplasty
Authors: Francisco Endara Urresta, Carlos Peñaherrera-Carrillo, Alejandro Barros Castro, Camilo Helito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRobotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (RA-TKA) has demonstrated superior alignment accuracy and reduced variability compared to conventional techniques. However, the clinical indications for RA-TKA remain poorly standardized, often driven by surgeon preference or institutional availability rather than patient-specific complexity. There is a need for an objective model to guide case selection and promote rational use of robotic systems. We developed a theoretical Bayesian decision tree model based on literature-derived conditional probabilities. The model integrates clinical variables (age, body mass index [BMI], coronal alignment, deformity severity, ASA classification) and system-level factors (robotic access) to estimate the posterior probability of benefit from RA-TKA. Simulated clinical scenarios and a Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 virtual patients were used to evaluate model behavior and sensitivity. Coronal deformity ≥ 10° and BMI > 35 were the most influential variables, while robotic access acted as a binary gatekeeper. In simulated scenarios, posterior RA-TKA recommendation probabilities ranged from 14.6% to 89.2%, depending on complexity and access. The Monte Carlo simulation yielded a mean recommendation probability of 53.7% (SD 21.2%), with strong discriminatory performance. Sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the model across input variations. This Bayesian model provides a transparent, interpretable framework for RA-TKA indication. It supports evidence-based, individualized decision-making and offers a platform for standardizing the use of robotic technology. Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has undergone substantial evolution over the past two decades, both in implant design and surgical technique. Among the most significant innovations is the incorporation of robotic assistance, which has emerged as a promising adjunct to improve surgical precision, restore mechanical alignment, and minimize variability in component positioning. Robotic-assisted TKA (RA-TKA) enables preoperative 3D planning and intraoperative adjustments based on soft-tissue behavior, offering a level of customization previously unattainable with conventional instruments. Clinical studies have reported improved radiographic outcomes, reduced outlier rates in coronal and sagittal alignment, and more consistent joint line restoration in patients undergoing RA-TKA. Although long-term survivorship and functional superiority remain subjects of ongoing investigation, early data suggest that robotic assistance may be especially beneficial in patients with complex deformities or biomechanical alterations [1–3].

Despite these potential advantages, the clinical indications for RA-TKA remain poorly defined. In current practice, the choice to utilize robotic technology is frequently influenced by surgeon preference, institutional policy, or logistical availability, rather than by standardized clinical criteria. As a result, patients who may derive the greatest benefit from the technology—such as those with severe coronal malalignment, excessive body mass index (BMI), or altered joint kinematics—may not consistently receive it. Conversely, RA-TKA may be employed in patients for whom conventional techniques would yield comparable outcomes, thereby contributing to unnecessary healthcare expenditure. This variability in clinical decision-making is further exacerbated by disparities in infrastructure and resource allocation, particularly in low- and middle-income health systems where robotic platforms may not be universally available [1–3].

There is therefore a pressing need for a reproducible, transparent, and clinically grounded algorithm to guide the appropriate indication of RA-TKA. An ideal decision model should integrate patient-specific factors—including age, BMI, degree of preoperative deformity, and limb alignment—as well as system-level variables such as hospital access to robotic systems and surgical expertise. Such a model would enable rational allocation of advanced technology based on anticipated clinical benefit, rather than arbitrary or economically driven considerations [3–5].

Bayesian decision trees represent a powerful and underutilized approach to clinical decision-making in orthopedic surgery. These models combine probabilistic reasoning with hierarchical classification, allowing clinicians to estimate the likelihood of benefit from a given intervention by sequentially integrating input variables. Unlike traditional regression models, Bayesian trees can accommodate conditional dependencies among predictors, handle uncertainty explicitly, and update themselves as new evidence emerges. Their intuitive structure and visual clarity make them especially well-suited for clinical environments, where decision support tools must be both interpretable and actionable [3–5].

In this context, we propose the development of a theoretical Bayesian decision tree to guide the indication of robotic-assisted versus conventional TKA. Rather than relying on retrospective institutional data, the model is constructed from a synthesis of published clinical evidence, incorporating key predictors identified in the literature. Through this approach, we aim to simulate decision pathways that reflect real-world variability and to visualize how specific clinical and institutional factors modulate the probability of benefit from robotic assistance.

We hypothesize that a Bayesian decision model, based on literature-derived clinical and logistical variables, can provide a quantifiable and objective framework to support the selective indication of RA-TKA. In this framework, the primary endpoint of the model is not functional outcome or implant survivorship, but the probability of achieving improved technical accuracy in component positioning and limb alignment, as reported in studies comparing robotic and conventional instrumentation. The model therefore estimates the probability of technical benefit, defined as a reduced likelihood of alignment outliers and improved reproducibility of component placement.

Methods

Conceptual framework

Bayesian decision trees are probabilistic graphical models designed to support decision-making under conditions of uncertainty. In the clinical setting, they offer a structured methodology to combine prior knowledge—often derived from clinical studies or expert consensus—with individual patient data to estimate the likelihood of a particular outcome or the appropriateness of a given intervention. Unlike traditional classification or regression trees, which segment data through deterministic splits at predefined thresholds, Bayesian trees assign conditional probabilities to each decision node. This structure allows the model to explicitly represent uncertainty and to update its output dynamically as new evidence becomes available.

One of the key advantages of Bayesian decision trees over linear regression models is their ability to incorporate non-linear and conditional interactions between variables. For example, the effect of obesity on the technical complexity of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) may vary substantially depending on the degree of preoperative deformity or coronal alignment. Bayesian models accommodate such interdependencies naturally, without assuming independence or linearity. Moreover, unlike conventional algorithms that often act as black boxes, Bayesian trees produce a transparent and interpretable structure. Each node and branch can be traced and clinically justified, making them suitable as decision-support tools in surgical environments, particularly where interpretability is essential for adoption by orthopedic surgeons.

Bayesian decision trees are particularly valuable in situations where randomized trials are impractical, and clinical decision-making depends on multiple interacting variables with different levels of evidence. Their graphical format facilitates communication between clinicians and supports shared decision-making processes, especially in technology-intensive procedures such as robotic-assisted TKA.

For clarity, the target outcome of the model is technical surgical precision, rather than patient-reported clinical improvement.

To facilitate clinical interpretation, a provisional probability threshold was defined to guide decision-making. In this framework, a predicted probability of benefit from RA-TKA ≥ 60% was considered a reasonable threshold for recommending robotic assistance, whereas probabilities below this value would favor conventional instrumentation. This threshold was selected to represent a balance between technical benefit and resource utilization and should be interpreted as an initial heuristic value subject to refinement during future clinical validation studies.

Such simulations offer preliminary insight into how the theoretical tree could function in a real-world decision-support setting.

Definition of benefit

In the present theoretical framework, “benefit” is defined as the probability of achieving improved technical accuracy in component positioning and limb alignment when using robotic-assisted TKA compared with conventional instrumentation. However, this concept must ultimately be anchored to measurable outcomes for clinical validation.

For future validation studies, a potential primary endpoint could be the achievement of the planned mechanical or functional alignment within a predefined tolerance range (e.g., ± 3° from the planned coronal alignment), a threshold commonly used in arthroplasty literature to define alignment outliers. Secondary endpoints could include reduction in component positioning errors, improved early postoperative alignment consistency, or association with patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) and revision rates.

The present model therefore estimates the probability of technical advantage, which must be prospectively validated against these measurable surgical outcomes.

Functional outcomes such as patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), pain scores, or implant survivorship were intentionally not incorporated into the model because these outcomes require prospective clinical datasets and long-term follow-up. The current framework should therefore be interpreted as estimating the probability of technical advantage, rather than direct clinical superiority.

Instead, benefit is defined as the relative probability of achieving improved technical accuracy in component positioning and limb alignment, based on published evidence demonstrating reduced alignment outliers with robotic-assisted TKA compared with conventional instrumentation. Therefore, the model estimates the probability of technical advantage, rather than direct clinical superiority.

Derivation of conditional weights

Conditional weights were not extracted as direct quantitative values from individual studies. Instead, they were derived through a structured synthesis of trends reported across the literature regarding predictors of alignment variability and surgical complexity in total knee arthroplasty.

For each variable, studies reporting increased rates of alignment outliers, component malposition, or technical difficulty were identified. The relative magnitude of each weight was then assigned proportionally based on the consistency and strength of association reported across these studies.

For example, obesity and severe coronal deformity were assigned higher positive weights because multiple comparative studies have demonstrated increased alignment variability in these patient groups when conventional instrumentation is used [11–15]. Conversely, ASA class was assigned a small negative modifier because robotic workflows may involve longer setup times, which could be less favorable in higher-risk patients.

To explore the internal probabilistic behavior of the model across a broader population, a Monte Carlo simulation was conducted using 10,000 virtual patients. Clinical inputs were randomly sampled from distributions consistent with population data: age followed a Gaussian distribution (mean = 67, SD = 9), BMI followed a truncated Gaussian (mean = 31, SD = 4, bounds = 20–45), and coronal alignment was categorized (neutral: 50%, varus: 35%, valgus: 15%). Severe deformity (> 10°) was present in 30% of cases, ASA class III + in 35%, and robotic access in 60%.

The simulation yielded a mean posterior probability of RA-TKA recommendation of 53.7% (SD: 21.2%). Notably, 18.6% of patients had a recommendation probability > 80%, whereas 29.1% scored < 30%. The remaining 52.3% fell between 40 and 70%, illustrating how the model distributes recommendation probabilities across varying clinical scenarios. The mildly right-skewed shape of the distribution highlights the influence of clustered high-complexity patients with access to robotic systems.

Robotic system availability, while binary, had an absolute effect: its removal reduced RA-TKA probability to zero in all simulations, regardless of patient complexity. Finally, a comparison with empirical decision-making highlights the model’s potential value. In many institutions, RA-TKA is allocated based on logistical factors—such as operating room availability, scheduling, or surgeon preference—rather than formal clinical criteria. This often leads to a misalignment between surgical complexity and resource allocation, resulting in either underuse of robotics in high-need cases or overuse in low-complexity scenarios. By contrast, the Bayesian model delivers quantifiable, individualized, and evidence-informed recommendations. It aligns high-cost interventions with anatomically or technically demanding profiles, improving both cost-effectiveness and clinical consistency. The decision tree also enhances transparency and communication among surgical teams, anesthesiologists, and patients, supporting its integration into multidisciplinary preoperative workflows.

Future retrospective validation against institutional registries could confirm whether this model improves alignment between theoretical benefit and actual utilization of RA-TKA. Discussion

This study introduces a theoretical Bayesian decision model to guide clinical decision-making in the selection between robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty (RA-TKA) and conventional TKA. By integrating conditional probabilities derived from the literature with clinically relevant input variables, the model provides a structured, interpretable, and scalable framework to estimate the probability of technical benefit of RA-TKA, defined as improved alignment accuracy and reduced positioning variability compared with conventional instrumentation. across diverse patient scenarios. Unlike black-box predictive algorithms, the decision tree design emphasizes clinical transparency, making it readily interpretable and adaptable to surgical practice [6–10].

External validation using real-world registry or prospective datasets represents the essential next step before clinical implementation.

Interpretation of key variables

The results of the simulated cases, Monte Carlo distribution, and sensitivity analysis underscore the hierarchical influence of certain clinical parameters. Among them, coronal deformity ≥ 10° and obesity (BMI > 35) consistently emerged as the most influential predictors of recommendation for RA-TKA. These variables reflect technical challenges where robotic precision is most valuable—specifically, in achieving accurate bone resection and restoring mechanical alignment in anatomically complex knees. This is consistent with prior studies demonstrating increased risk of component malposition and alignment outliers in obese patients and those with pronounced varus or valgus deformities when using conventional jigs [11–15].

Conversely, age and ASA class had comparatively modest standalone effects but played important modifying roles. Younger age (< 65 years) implies longer prosthesis life expectancy and greater activity levels, making alignment precision more relevant for long-term outcomes. ASA class, while not directly predictive of RA-TKA benefit, informs perioperative risk tolerance and can shift the decision threshold, especially in borderline cases.

A particularly important finding was the binary influence of robotic system availability. Regardless of the calculated benefit, if a robotic platform is not accessible, the model defaults to recommending conventional TKA. This reflects a critical real-world constraint and highlights the importance of distinguishing between theoretical benefit and practical feasibility [16–20].

These dynamics support a stratified and patient-specific approach to robotic TKA indication, contrasting with current trends of either overutilization based on institutional marketing or underutilization due to cost barriers.

Clinical applicability in resource-constrained settings

The proposed model is designed to function not only as a theoretical framework but also as a decision-support tool in real-world clinical environments, particularly in systems where robotic access is limited or selectively available. In such settings, the tree may be used to prioritize patients who are most likely to benefit from robotic precision—such as those with high BMI, severe deformity, or younger biological age—thereby promoting rational and equitable use of high-cost technology [20–22].

Its visual, interpretable structure allows for easy integration into preoperative discussions, including multidisciplinary surgical boards or patient education sessions. Rather than replacing clinical judgment, the model complements it, offering a data-driven second opinion that is easily auditable and consistent. Moreover, it could be embedded within electronic surgical planning tools or mobile apps to facilitate point-of-care decision-making [20–22].

The introduction of a probability threshold further enhances the practical applicability of the model. By translating probabilistic outputs into a binary recommendation framework, surgeons can interpret model predictions in a clinically actionable manner. In the current conceptual framework, a threshold of approximately 60–70% probability may represent a reasonable range for prioritizing robotic assistance. However, the optimal threshold should ultimately be determined through external validation studies evaluating whether higher model-predicted probabilities correspond to improved surgical accuracy or patient outcomes.

An important conceptual distinction should be made between clinical indication and technological availability. The present model was designed to estimate the probability of technical benefit from robotic-assisted TKA based primarily on patient-specific factors. Institutional access to robotic platforms was included only as an implementation constraint reflecting real-world practice environments.

Ideally, the model should first generate a clinical recommendation independent of resource limitations, thereby identifying patients who would theoretically benefit most from robotic assistance. In this way, the framework could serve not only as a surgical decision-support tool but also as a strategic instrument for healthcare systems to identify unmet technological needs and justify the acquisition of robotic platforms in centers treating high-complexity patient populations.

Economic context and resource prioritization

Robotic-assisted TKA represents a resource-intensive technology whose adoption is constrained by economic and logistical factors. Several economic evaluations have suggested that robotic platforms may become cost-effective primarily when applied to patients with increased surgical complexity, where improved alignment accuracy or reduced revision risk may offset the additional costs of the technology. For example, Hickey et al. reported that robotic TKA may provide the greatest economic value when selectively used in high-risk subgroups, particularly patients with severe deformity or elevated BMI. Similarly, Ritter et al. demonstrated that cost-effectiveness improves when robotic systems are applied to cases with increased technical difficulty, rather than routine low-complexity arthroplasty.

These findings align with the results of the present model, in which severe coronal deformity and obesity emerged as the most influential factors increasing the probability of benefit from RA-TKA. In this context, the Bayesian decision framework may provide a rational strategy for prioritizing robotic technology toward patient populations where both clinical and economic value are likely to be maximized.

The need for model localization and economic integration

An important consideration in interpreting the present model is the need for local calibration and economic integration. Robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty is a resource-intensive technology whose adoption is influenced not only by clinical complexity but also by economic constraints, reimbursement structures, and healthcare system organization.

The current model focuses on identifying patient characteristics associated with increased technical complexity in TKA. However, the real-world implementation of robotic technology depends on multiple contextual variables that were not incorporated into the present framework. These include the capital cost of robotic platforms, disposable instrumentation expenses, operating room time, and maintenance contracts.

Additionally, the economic sustainability of robotic-assisted arthroplasty varies substantially across healthcare systems. In fee-for-service models, additional procedural costs may be partially offset through reimbursement structures or increased surgical volume. In contrast, healthcare systems operating under fixed Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payments or global budgets may not reimburse the additional cost associated with robotic technology.

Patient demographics and disease patterns also vary significantly across geographic regions. Populations with high prevalence of obesity or severe deformity may derive greater relative benefit from robotic precision compared with populations where arthroplasty is performed in younger or post-traumatic patients.

For these reasons, the present framework should be interpreted as a conceptual decision-support model requiring local calibration rather than a universal decision tool. Future implementations should incorporate regional cost data, reimbursement structures, and population-specific epidemiology to recalibrate the conditional weights of the model.

Comparison with existing literature

While robotic systems for TKA have demonstrated benefits in alignment precision and early postoperative recovery, there is limited consensus regarding formal indication criteria. Most existing literature focuses on outcomes comparison rather than surgical selection. A recent Delphi consensus study among orthopedic surgeons acknowledged this gap, citing the absence of validated protocols to determine who should receive RA-TKA and why [22–24].

Previous attempts to stratify surgical indication—such as scoring systems based on deformity, function, or imaging parameters—often rely on retrospective data and offer limited predictive transparency. Regression-based models may identify correlates of success but do not inherently guide real-time decisions. The present study contributes a novel visual-probabilistic approach, explicitly built to simulate clinical reasoning and adaptable to future data integration [24–26].

Ethical and conceptual implications of technology prioritization

A conceptual and ethical consideration arises when interpreting the present model. If robotic-assisted TKA consistently demonstrates superior precision in component positioning and limb alignment compared with conventional instrumentation, one could argue that the technology represents the technically optimal approach for all patients. In that sense, every patient undergoing TKA may theoretically benefit from the enhanced accuracy provided by robotic systems.

However, the practical reality of orthopedic practice is that robotic platforms remain limited by economic cost, infrastructure requirements, and operating room logistics. Consequently, the primary challenge facing healthcare systems is not whether robotic assistance is technically advantageous, but how to allocate a scarce and resource-intensive technology in a rational and equitable manner.

The purpose of the present Bayesian framework should therefore not be interpreted as identifying the only patients who benefit from robotic surgery. Rather, the model is intended to identify patients in whom the incremental technical advantage of robotic assistance is most likely to have meaningful clinical impact, thereby supporting the prioritization of robotic resources when universal access is not feasible.

In this sense, the model functions as a resource prioritization tool rather than a restriction of clinical indication. Its goal is not to justify a lower standard of care for less complex cases, but to provide a transparent and evidence-informed method for allocating advanced technology in environments where availability is constrained.

As robotic platforms become more widely available and cost barriers decrease, the role of such prioritization frameworks may diminish. Until then, structured allocation strategies may help ensure that patients with the highest technical complexity—such as those with severe deformity or obesity—are not disadvantaged by purely logistical decision-making.

Importantly, the framework does not imply that conventional TKA is an inferior standard of care. Conventional techniques remain safe and effective when performed by experienced surgeons. Rather, the model recognizes that robotic technology may offer incremental precision benefits whose relative value increases in technically demanding cases.

Economic and health system context

An important limitation of the present model is the absence of explicit economic variables. Robotic-assisted total knee arthroplasty represents a capital-intensive technology associated with additional costs including platform acquisition, maintenance contracts, disposable instrumentation, and potential increases in operative time during the learning phase. These economic factors significantly influence whether robotic technology can be sustainably implemented within a given healthcare system.

The financial viability of robotic TKA varies substantially across healthcare environments. In fee-for-service systems, the additional costs may be partially offset by higher reimbursement or increased procedural volume. In contrast, in systems operating under fixed Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payments or global budgets, the added cost of robotic technology may not be reimbursed, limiting its adoption regardless of potential clinical benefit.

Consequently, the present model should not be interpreted as a universal decision tool but rather as a conceptual decision-support framework that identifies patient characteristics associated with increased technical complexity. The actual implementation of robotic technology must ultimately be evaluated within the economic constraints of each healthcare system.

Population demographics and disease patterns

Another important consideration is the variability in patient populations across geographic regions. The distribution of osteoarthritis severity, obesity prevalence, deformity patterns, and post-traumatic knee pathology differs significantly between countries and healthcare systems. For example, populations in North America may demonstrate higher obesity prevalence, whereas other regions may present greater proportions of post-traumatic arthritis, inflammatory disease, or younger patients requiring arthroplasty.

These epidemiological differences may influence both the prevalence of technically demanding cases and the relative value of robotic precision. As a result, the conditional weights applied in the present model should be interpreted as illustrative rather than universal. Local calibration using institutional datasets would be required to ensure accurate representation of population-specific risk profiles.

Surgeon experience and learning curve

The model also assumes a stable technical benefit associated with robotic assistance. However, multiple studies have demonstrated a learning curve associated with the adoption of robotic TKA systems, during which operative times may be longer and workflow efficiency may be reduced. Surgeon experience, institutional volume, and familiarity with the robotic platform may therefore influence the magnitude of benefit achieved in practice. These variables were not incorporated into the current theoretical framework but represent important factors that should be considered in future refinements of the model.

Future directions

The most important next step will be external validation using real-world clinical registries to determine whether patients with higher model-predicted probabilities of benefit actually demonstrate improved surgical accuracy or clinical outcomes.

Future iterations of the model should separate the clinical recommendation layer from the implementation layer, allowing the framework to function as a true indication tool independent of institutional availability.

Validation of this theoretical framework is a crucial next step. Retrospective application of the model to institutional registries could allow comparison between actual RA-TKA utilization and model-based recommendations. Metrics such as alignment accuracy, complication rates, and short-term outcomes could serve as endpoints to evaluate whether alignment with model recommendations predicts improved surgical results [36–38].

Alternatively, prospective multicenter implementation—where the model is used in real time to inform surgical planning—could help assess its clinical utility, user acceptance, and cost-effectiveness. Such studies may also reveal opportunities to refine the model by incorporating additional variables such as preoperative functional scores, limb length discrepancies, or radiographic severity of osteoarthritis [37–39].

Finally, integration into digital health platforms, including electronic medical records or surgical planning software, could allow real-time automation, broader adoption, and iterative learning using machine learning techniques. A hybrid model combining Bayesian reasoning with neural network updates could offer both transparency and dynamic performance.

If validated, this model could inform international guidelines on RA-TKA indications, helping ensure that robotic technology is used where it provides the greatest clinical and economic value, while preserving access and sustainability across healthcare systems.

Future research should focus on integrating cost-effectiveness variables, healthcare reimbursement models, and local patient demographics into the Bayesian framework. Incorporating these parameters would allow development of region-specific calibration models capable of adapting the decision tree to different healthcare environments.

Prospective validation should compare model predictions with measurable surgical endpoints such as alignment deviation from preoperative plans, rates of alignment outliers (> 3°), postoperative component positioning accuracy, and association with early patient-reported outcome measures. Integration of such endpoints would allow calibration of the probabilistic outputs and refinement of decision thresholds for clinical implementation.

Role of robotic access as an implementation constraint

In the present framework, robotic access was incorporated as an implementation constraint rather than a determinant of clinical indication. While current clinical practice is frequently influenced by institutional availability, the objective of the model is to estimate patient-specific technical advantage independent of logistical limitations. Future iterations of the model should therefore generate a primary clinical recommendation based on patient characteristics alone, allowing institutions to identify unmet technological needs and better justify resource allocation.

Conclusion

This theoretical Bayesian decision tree model provides a structured and objective approach to integrating key clinical variables to estimate the probability of technical benefit from robotic assistance, while acknowledging institutional availability as a secondary implementation factor, the model quantifies the probability of technical benefit and provides a potential threshold-based framework to guide robotic case selection on a case-by-case basis. Unlike current practice patterns that often rely on subjective judgment or institutional constraints, this model offers a reproducible framework grounded in evidence-based probabilities.

Its application could help prioritize the use of robotic platforms for patients with the highest technical complexity, thereby enhancing surgical precision, optimizing resource utilization, and promoting equity in access to advanced technology. In doing so, the model supports a more rational allocation of high-cost surgical innovation, particularly in settings with limited availability.

Although theoretical, this framework provides a theoretical decision-support structure whose clinical value must be confirmed through external validation using real-world datasets, including retrospective validation using institutional databases and prospective multicenter trials. It may also serve as a scaffold for more complex algorithms incorporating functional outcomes, cost-effectiveness, or real-time decision support tools.

Ultimately, by offering clinicians an interpretable and adaptable tool, the model contributes to the development of standardized clinical pathways and evidence-based guidelines for the use of robotics in knee arthroplasty.
