Advancing Retinal Vessel Analysis: Reproducibility of Myogenic Constriction and Assessment of Regulatory Capacity
Authors: Eva Fleischlin, Christoph Hauser, Walthard Vilser, Lukas Streese, Henner Hanssen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurate characterization of retinal microvascular autoregulation requires reliable assessment of both vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive responses. Although blood pressure (BP)-induced vasoconstriction is well recognized, its measurement lacks reproducibility data, limiting its translational applicability. This study aimed to establish and validate a standardized framework for quantifying BP-induced vasoconstriction and to introduce the control range as a novel metric for retinal microvascular regulatory capacity.MethodsHealthy adults were assessed twice, one week apart. Retinal vessel diameters were continuously measured during isometric handgrip exercise at sub- and maximal contractions to induce BP elevations. Changes in arterial vessel diameters were extracted by subtracting the mean of the lowest 3 seconds from the baseline diameter. Additionally, flicker light–induced dilation was measured, and the range between maximal dilatation and constriction was calculated. Intra-individual reproducibility was assessed via intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).ResultsTwenty-four participants (13 males, 11 females; 43.4 ± 15.1 years) were included. The average reduction in vessel diameter during the maximal handgrip phase was −6.15 ± 3.89 µm (P < 0.01). The intra-individual ICC was 0.87 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.74–0.95). Based on the independent behavior of vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive responses, the quantified control range was defined as the dynamic range between maximal flicker-induced dilation and pressure-related constriction.ConclusionsThis standardized approach enables reproducible quantification of retinal myogenic constriction. The control range may serve as a measure of microvascular regulatory capacity.Translational RelevanceA standardized and reproducible assessment of retinal myogenic constriction and regulatory capacity has the potential for clinical implementation in the risk stratification of cardiovascular disease.

Introduction

The eye offers a unique opportunity to non-invasively assess systemic microvascular structure and function. The retinal microvasculature is often referred to as a “window to the heart,” as it is closely linked to systemic cardiovascular (CV) health.1,2 Changes in retinal vessel diameters, such as narrowing of the small retinal arteries and widening of the small retinal veins, are associated with increased CV risk.3 Moreover, retinal vessel diameters have been linked to several CV risk factors, including hypertension, obesity, and dyslipidemia.4–8 Although static vessel analysis (SVA) derived from fundus photographs provides valuable information on retinal vessel morphology and indirectly on vascular function, the functional status of the retinal microvasculature can be directly assessed using dynamic vessel analysis (DVA). This technique measures the response of small retinal arteries and veins to flicker light stimulation.9 Growing evidence suggests that the response of the small retinal arteries and veins to flicker light stimulation is associated with CV risk and may serve as a valuable CV risk prediction tool.10–12 Compared to DVA, vasoconstrictive mechanisms are less frequently quantified, resulting in an incomplete characterization of retinal vascular regulation. Blood pressure (BP)-induced vasoconstriction, commonly referred to as the Bayliss effect, describes the intrinsic myogenic response of blood vessels to increased transmural pressure.13 This mechanism maintains stable end-organ perfusion despite fluctuations in systemic BP and protects the downstream microvasculature.13 Myogenic constriction represents a microvascular autoregulatory mechanism in response to increases in transmural pressure under experimental conditions. Therefore, throughout this paper, we refer to this response as myogenic constriction, while acknowledging its conceptual relation to the Bayliss effect originally described by Bayliss.13

Robinson et al.14 were among the first to introduce the measurement of retinal vessel vasoconstriction in response to BP changes following isometric exercise. They investigated the effects of increasing BP due to different isometric exercise stimuli, including squatting, leg extension, and handgrip, on retinal vessel diameters and flow velocity using laser Doppler velocimetry.14 Blum et al.15 then demonstrated that DVA can quantify BP-induced vasoconstriction in small retinal arteries in response to isometric exercise. Moreover, they highlighted the potential clinical relevance of this response in patients with diabetes. Subsequently, several research groups investigated retinal arteriolar constriction during acute BP elevations in both healthy individuals and patients with metabolic and vascular disease.16–20 Given the importance of microvascular regulation in ocular pathologies, glaucoma may also be associated with disturbances in vascular autoregulation. However, to date, research has primarily focused on vasodilatory responses, whereas data on myogenic constriction in glaucoma remain limited.21–23 Furthermore, an age dependency of BP-induced constriction was shown with a delayed and attenuated response at a higher age.24,25 Importantly, all available studies have reported a wide inter-individual variability in BP-induced constriction responses.

Although DVA is a well-standardized method for assessing flicker-induced vasodilation, the assessment of BP-induced myogenic constriction in the retinal microvasculature has been less standardized to date, and methodological approaches vary across the literature. Consequently, reporting practices and outcome metrics for myogenic constriction differ, which may limit comparability among findings. As a key methodological limitation of previous studies, exercise-induced BP increases were often elicited using standardized workloads not tailored to individual strength capacity. This may result in heterogeneous BP responses and reduce comparability among participants. Therefore, the present study introduced an individualized isometric handgrip protocol prescribed as a percentage of maximal voluntary contraction. Given that myogenic autoregulation predominantly occurs at the level of arteries, which actively regulate vascular tone in response to changes in perfusion pressure, the analysis was restricted to small retinal arteries. The aim of this study was to evaluate this approach for quantifying retinal arterial myogenic constriction and to determine the reproducibility of the vascular response

Methods

Study Design

This monocentric study was conducted at the Department of Sport, Exercise and Health (DSBG) of the University of Basel. The participants were measured at two time points, at least 1 week apart, with a maximum interval of 10 days between measurements. The two appointments were conducted at the same time of day to exclude possible diurnal variations. This study was carried out in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki26 and was approved by the Ethics Committee of North-Western and Central Switzerland (EKNZ-2021-00086). Participants signed written informed consent prior to starting the measurements.

Recruitment and Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Recruitment was conducted during an open-house event at the DSBG where visitors were invited to participate. Individuals 18 years or older were eligible to participate. Before the first measurement, their medical history was assessed. Exclusion criteria included untreated CV disease; any acute or chronic eye disease such as macular degeneration, cataract, diabetic retinopathy, glaucoma, or any other ocular disease; and elevated intraocular pressure (intraocular pressure [IOP] ≥ 21 mm Hg).

Sample Size Consideration

This study was designed as a methodological reproducibility study, with the primary aim of estimating variability and reliability of retinal myogenic constriction measurements rather than testing a specific intervention effect. Therefore, no formal hypothesis-based sample size calculation was performed; instead, the sample size was chosen to allow estimation of intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) from repeated measurements. Accordingly, 24 participants underwent repeated measurements on two separate days.

Preparation

Participants were instructed to adhere to standardized pre-assessment conditions. In accordance with the standard operating procedures described by Streese et al.,27 participants were instructed to refrain from exercise for 24 hours prior to testing. Furthermore, alcohol and caffeine consumption were prohibited for 12 hours and food intake for 2 hours preceding the assessment.27

Measurements

After obtaining informed consent and checking for inclusion and exclusion criteria, resting BP was measured in a seated position following a 10-minute rest period. IOP was assessed using a rebound tonometer (iCarePRO; iCare Finland OY, Vantaa, Finland). Six IOP measurements were taken; the highest and lowest values were excluded, and the remaining four were averaged. Subsequently, pupil dilation was induced with one drop of tropicamide 0.5%. By default, measurements were performed in the right eye; however, if this was not feasible (e.g., due to focusing difficulties or unilateral ocular pathology), the left eye was assessed instead. After 10 minutes of rest, the dilation status of the pupil was assessed. Due to interindividual variations in axial length and refractive status, the retinal image scale differs between individuals, which can introduce inaccuracies in absolute vessel diameter measurements. To account for this, absolute diameters were estimated based on Gullstrand's normal eye. This reflects a schematic model of the human eye with standardized anatomical dimensions, assuming that 1 measuring unit corresponds to 1 µm. However, when calculating relative changes between measurements, the influence of individual image scaling is minimized. Furthermore, retinal vessels assessed in this study were classified as small arteries rather than arterioles, as their diameters exceeded 100 µm.

Dynamic Vessel Analysis

DVA was performed in accordance with the standard operating procedures described by Streese et al.27 The participants rested in a seated position for 10 minutes before the DVA measurement. Following a 50-second resting phase, three cycles of flicker stimulation (20 seconds of diffuse luminance flicker with green light at 12.5 Hz) and recovery (80 seconds) were conducted. Arterial segments were marked in the upper temporal quadrant one to two optic disc diameters away from the optic disc edge and were consistently assessed at the identical anatomical location across both examinations by manual alignment. The continuous measurement of the diameter for the marked vessel segments was performed using retinal vessel analyzer (RVA) software (Imedos Health GmbH, Jena, Germany) and a modified fundus camera (FF450; ZEISS, Jena, Germany). The analysis of the recorded data was carried out according to the procedures outlined by Streese et al.27 The maximal diameter was determined from the median curve across the three flicker phases. It was defined as the maximum value occurring within the last 10 seconds of flicker stimulation and up to 3 seconds after cessation of the flicker.

Retinal Vessel Analysis for the Quantification of Myogenic Constriction

Prior to the assessment of myogenic constriction, maximal left-handed grip strength was measured using a dynamometer adjusted for participant comfort. Participants were instructed to perform a maximal contraction for 3 to 5 seconds with verbal encouragement. The test was repeated, and the higher value was used to determine target levels for the subsequent submaximal handgrip phase. Grip strength was recorded using the Leonardo Mechanograph GF (Novotec Medical GmbH, Pforzheim, Germany); Leonardo Mechanography v4.4 BAS software was used for both maximal and continuous grip strength measurements. A short rest period was implemented before starting the myogenic constriction protocol to normalize BP after the maximum handgrip measurement. The continuous assessment of the vessel diameter was performed using a modified FF450 fundus camera and the RVA software. The participants were seated at a comfortable height to minimize head movement. The same vessel segments as in the DVA were marked with the RVA software. Vessel diameters were measured continuously at a rate of 25 images per second. After an initial resting phase of 50 seconds, the first cycle of the handgrip task began, asking participants to press the dynamometer with 30% of their maximal strength measured in the preceding maximal hand grip task. The examiner gave verbal feedback to ensure the participant was in the desired range. After 30 seconds at 30% of their maximal strength, the participants were verbally motivated to press as hard as possible for the following 30 seconds to achieve a maximal BP increase. After 80 seconds of rest, the cycle was repeated once. This hand grip protocol is depicted in Figure 1. The data analysis process is further explored later, as it was defined as part of this study.

Continuous BP Measurement

In nine participants, a beat-to-beat BP measurement system was put in place to visualize the BP peaks relating to the handgrip exercise. This was measured by the Finometer PRO (Finapres Medical Systems B.V., Enschede, Netherlands).

Data Analysis

All data analyses were conducted using R 5.4.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For participant characteristics, means and standard deviations (SDs) were calculated. Following initial screening, participants with insufficient data quality were excluded from the analysis. Exclusion criteria included inability to maintain visual focus, excessive head movement during measurement, or reduced video quality. For the remaining participants, the better of the two measurement cycles was subjectively selected based on predefined criteria, such as lower signal variance, fewer artifacts, and fewer missing data points.

Myogenic Constriction Analysis Methods

To identify the most reliable method for determining the minimum vessel diameter, multiple data processing approaches were tested. Day-to-day reliability of these methods was assessed using ICCs, calculated with a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement and single measurements. This model estimates the consistency of measurements at two fixed time points within the same participants. The single-measurement ICC was chosen to evaluate the reliability of individual measurements rather than group means.28 To visually assess day-to-day reliability, Bland–Altman plots were used to illustrate the agreement between repeated measurements. Details on the calculation methods are provided in the Supplementary Materials. Based on the ICC calculations, we identified the method using the mean of the lowest consecutive 3 seconds during the maximal handgrip phase and the subsequent 10 seconds as the most reliable and therefore selected it for further analysis.

Calculation of Vessel Diameter Changes

To assess recovery of vessel diameters, successive 5-second intervals following the minimum were averaged and compared with resting values. Resting, minimum, and post-minimum values were statistically compared using paired t-tests. Additionally, absolute and percentage changes from resting diameters were calculated.

Sub-Group Analysis With BP

For participants with continuous BP recordings, the change in vessel diameter per 10-mm Hg increase in systolic BP was calculated. This was done by dividing the change in vessel diameter by the maximum change in BP, then multiplying by 10. The maximum BP change was defined as the difference between the mean BP during the 30 seconds preceding the handgrip exercise and the mean of the three highest consecutive BP values recorded during the handgrip exercise.

Control Range

The control range was defined as the difference in magnitude between maximum flicker-induced dilation and maximum pressure-induced constriction. Two calculation methods for the control range were systematically compared based on the ICCs. The method with the higher lower-bound 95% confidence interval (CI) was then chosen for further analysis. The methods are described in detail in the Supplementary Materials.

We propose a first linear approximation of the regulatory range combining dilatory and constrictive responses. To quantify this regulatory capacity of the marked retinal vessel segments, the absolute diameter change (µm) obtained during the DVA measurement was added to the absolute diameter change (µm) observed during the myogenic constriction measurement. The resulting value was then normalized to the respective mean resting vessel diameter of both measurements. Mean and SD were calculated, and reproducibility was assessed via ICCs, calculated with a two-way mixed-effects model for absolute agreement and single measurements.

Regression Analyses

Four simple linear regression analyses were conducted to identify factors influencing the measured change in vessel diameter. With myogenic constriction as the outcome variable, the predictors were arterial flicker-light–induced dilation (aFID), maximal handgrip strength, BP elevation during measurement, and age. Additionally, a separate linear regression was performed with the control range as the outcome variable and age as the predictor variable to assess the age dependency of this new parameter. For these analyses, baseline measurements of all participants with sufficient data quality were included.

Results

A total of 31 participants were initially enrolled in the study. Four were excluded due to missing data at one or both time points, and three were excluded due to insufficient quality of retinal vessel measurements. Thus, 24 participants were included in the final analysis. In 21 participants, the right eye was examined, and in three participants the left eye was assessed. The participants had a mean age of 43.4 ± 15.1 years, ranging from 20 to 64 years. Additional sample characteristics are depicted in Table 1.

Changes in Arterial Vessel Diameter

An example measurement from one participant is depicted in Figure 2, including the continuous handgrip force recording, the corresponding BP response, and the simultaneous retinal vessel diameter measurement. Maximal handgrip exercise induced a reduction in retinal vessel diameter of −6.15 ± 3.89 µm, corresponding to a relative decrease of −5.38 ± 3.21%. Additional myogenic constriction parameters are presented in Table 2. After termination of the handgrip task, the vessel diameter values gradually approached resting values within 30 seconds. After reaching a minimum of 106.59 ± 13.85 µm, the diameter increased to 109.93 ± 14.52 µm in the first 5-second interval. This upward trend continued across subsequent intervals: 110.35 ± 14.59 (5–10 seconds), 110.49 ± 14.80 µm (10–15 seconds), 110.30 ± 14.30 µm (15–20 seconds), 111.37 ± 14.66 µm (20–25 seconds), and 111.35 ± 14.55 µm (25–30 seconds), closely approaching the resting value of 112.74 ± 14.81 µm. These changes were statistically significant compared with both the minimum and resting values, indicating that the diameter rose significantly in the first 5 seconds after the minimum and did not return to resting values over the 30 seconds following the minimum. BP Sub-Analysis

For a subgroup of nine participants, BP was analyzed on a beat-to-beat basis during the measurement. Resting systolic BP, averaged over 30 seconds, was 138 ± 21 mm Hg. During the maximal handgrip phase, systolic BP increased to 187 ± 29 mm Hg, representing a mean rise of 49 ± 21 mm Hg. In this subgroup, resting arterial vessel diameter was 105.32 ± 11.86 µm, decreasing to a minimum of 99.65 ± 10.96 µm during the maximal handgrip phase. This corresponds to a mean reduction of 5.68 ± 3.64 µm. On average, each 10-mm Hg increase in systolic BP was associated with a decrease of 1.23 ± 0.90 µm in arterial vessel diameter.

Associations of aFID, Handgrip Strength, BP Elevation, and Age With the Myogenic Constriction Response

Four simple linear regression analyses were conducted. The predictors investigated were aFID, maximal handgrip strength, and maximal BP elevation during the measurement. The regression results for each predictor are shown in Table 3. aFID, maximal handgrip strength, and BP elevations were not significant predictors of the myogenic constriction response. aFID and handgrip strength explained little to no variance, whereas the BP increase accounted for 20% of the variability, although this did not reach statistical significance. Age was a significant predictor of myogenic constriction, with higher age associated with a less pronounced myogenic constriction capacity. The model with age as a predictor accounted for 24.1% of the variance in the myogenic constriction capacity. The regression analyses are presented as scatterplots in Figure 4.

Day-to-Day Reliability

The day-to-day reliability of the difference in vessel diameter from the rest period and the minimum showed an ICC of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.74–0.95), indicating a moderate level of consistency across repeated measurements. Approximately 87% of the variability in the measurement was attributable to differences between subjects, and the remaining 13% was due to within-subject variability or measurement error. The day-to-day mean absolute error (MAE) between the baseline and follow-up measurement was 1.55 ± 1.18 µm. The agreement between the two measurement time points was visually assessed using Bland–Altman plots (Fig. 5). Control Range

The combination of the absolute maximal dilation and absolute maximal constriction enabled the derivation of a new parameter, the control range, representing the maximal functional span of vasoactive responses in that vessel. This parameter reflects the total range between flicker-induced dilation and myogenic constriction. The more reproducible method for calculating the range was to add the absolute change measured during DVA to the absolute maximal change during myogenic constriction. The range in vessel diameters was 12.05 ± 5.14 µm, corresponding to a mean range of 10.61% ± 3.80% relative to the average resting diameter. In line with the approach used for myogenic constriction, the age dependency of the control range was assessed. Age was not associated with the control range expressed as a percentage of the resting diameter. In the linear regression, the effect of age was small and non-significant (β = −0.001; 95% CI, –0.002 to 0.001; P = 0.146). This regression analysis included only 21 participants, as three had to be excluded because different vessel segments were marked in the DVA and the myogenic constriction measurement.

Discussion

Reproducibility of Myogenic Constriction

This study is the first, to our knowledge, to evaluate the reproducibility of a novel approach for non-invasive assessment of myogenic constriction and regulatory capacity in the retinal microcirculation. The ICC of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.74–0.95) for measuring myogenic constriction capacity can be classified as moderate.28 The MAE between the two time points was 1.55 ± 1.18 µm, which indicates that changes in intervention studies must be higher to have a meaningful effect.

The mean constriction in vessel diameter was –6.15 ± 3.89 µm (P < 0.01), which corresponds to a constriction of −5.38% ± 3.21%. This magnitude of constriction is comparable to that reported by Blum et al.15 and is of similar extent to the vasodilation observed during flicker light–induced dilation in the current sample (4.95% ± 2.07%). The vessel diameter reached the minimum on average at 0.44 ± 9.68 seconds before the pressure phase ended, corresponding to the expected BP peak. In contrast to previous studies, this study aimed to elevate BP using an isometric exercise adapted to the participant's individual strength level, based on a percentage of their maximal voluntary contraction. Earlier investigations of the myogenic constriction in the retinal circulation employed a standardized isometric task, such as holding a predefined weight with outstretched arms, regardless of individual strength capacity.15 The approach used in this study, involving a hand dynamometer, enables a strength stimulus tailored to each participant. This individualized approach is expected to elicit more comparable BP responses across participants by minimizing variability due to differences in muscular strength. Therefore, in the regression analysis in the present study, the maximal handgrip strength was not a significant predictor of the arterial myogenic constriction response.

The BP increase during the hand grip phase explained 20.4% of the variance in the myogenic constriction measurement. There was a negative correlation between the vessel response and the BP response during the myogenic constriction measurement, but it was not statistically significant. Yet, it is important to note that the sample size for this specific regression analysis was very small. Future studies should therefore consider including BP elevation as a potential confounding variable.

Comparison With Flicker Light–Induced Dilation

Our results show that flicker light–induced dilation, a commonly used indicator of endothelial function in the retinal microcirculation,9 does not significantly explain variation in myogenic constriction. Although both mechanisms reflect aspects of microvascular tone regulation, they operate via distinct physiological pathways. Our study is not mechanistic in nature, but we would like to hypothesize about the potential mechanisms involved. From a mechanistic standpoint, flicker light–induced dilation is primarily mediated by nitric oxide (NO) released by endothelial cells. Other vasoactive substances such as adenosine, prostaglandins and K+ also play a role in the vessel reaction.9,29 In contrast, the myogenic constriction is an endothelium-independent mechanism driven by vascular smooth muscle cells.30 Increases in intraluminal pressure lead to the activation of non-selective cation channels in the smooth muscle cell membrane, causing membrane depolarization and subsequent calcium influx, which triggers contraction.31 Although the exact mechanisms of pressure-induced channel activation remain unclear, there is evidence suggesting a role for transient receptor potential (TRP) channels in this process.32,33 These TRP channels appear to interact closely with G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), linking mechanical stretch to smooth muscle contraction.30 Inter-individual variability in the timing of the initial dilatory response may obscure this response in the median arterial vessel response curve (Fig. 2).

Myogenic Constriction and CV Risk

Although flicker light–induced dilation has been well established in association with CV risk factors, evidence linking the myogenic response to CV disease or end-organ damage remains limited.34 To date, blood glucose levels and diabetes are the only risk factors studied in relation to both flicker light–induced dilation and myogenic constriction. The myogenic response is impaired under both acute and chronic hyperglycemia,16,18 and its capacity further declines with increasing severity of diabetic retinopathy.35 Similarly, individuals with diabetes show reduced flicker light–induced dilation, even before the onset of clinical retinopathy.36 Thus, glucose regulation and diabetes currently represent the only cardiometabolic factors known to influence both vascular responses.35–37 The impact of other CV risk factors on myogenic constriction remains largely unexplored. Understanding these relationships of CV risk factors with the vascular responses could enhance our ability to predict and characterize early vascular dysfunction. In addition, interventional evidence suggests that myogenic constriction is modifiable, with improved glycemic control leading to enhanced vascular responses.17 In the context of hypertension, assessing the myogenic constriction may provide valuable insights, as it reflects the ability of end organs to autoregulate and counteract increases in transmural pressure and therefore BP peaks.13 Impaired myogenic constriction responses to BP fluctuations may therefore serve as a predictor of end-organ damage and could help predict the development of cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, renal, and ophthalmological complications associated with hypertension and other CV risk factors.

Emerging evidence suggests that different vascular phenotypes exhibit distinct vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive response patterns. For example, in patients with CV disease, small retinal arteries may be pre-constricted, potentially blunting the myogenic constriction response while preserving normal flicker light–induced dilation in case of normal endothelial function.38 Conversely, in individuals with high physical activity levels, vessels may be pre-dilated, potentially limiting the vasodilatory reserve but enhancing the capacity for constriction due to increased mechanical reserve.39 These examples highlight how individual differences in vascular tone or structural remodeling could differentially affect dilation and constriction capacities.9 Consequently, combining both flicker and myogenic assessments may offer a more nuanced picture of microvascular health.

Taken together, these findings suggest the need to consider multiple, complementary assessments of microvascular function rather than relying on a single proxy. Future studies should explore whether the joint assessment of vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive responses improves early detection and characterization of microvascular dysfunction, particularly in populations at increased CV risk. This dual approach may also provide valuable insights into end-organ damage based on early microvascular abnormalities.

Control Range

Therefore, the new combined metric “control range” is proposed. If we assume that flicker-induced dilation and pressure-induced constriction describe the maximum changes in vessel diameter in healthy individuals, then their combined assessment provides an estimate of the maximal control range within which a vessel can actively regulate its diameter. The quantification of the control range using absolute deviations from resting diameter for both responses yielded a mean control range of 12.05 ± 5.14 µm, corresponding to 10.61% ± 3.80% of the average resting diameter. This calculation method yielded good reliability for absolute values (ICC = 0.84; 95% CI, 0.65–0.93), whereas expressing the control range relative to resting diameter resulted in a lower reliability (ICC = 0.75; 95% CI, 0.49–0.89). According to established interpretation guidelines, these confidence intervals indicate moderate-to-excellent reliability for absolute values and poor-to-good reliability for relative values, highlighting substantially greater uncertainty in the normalized approach.28 The lower reliability in the relative values is likely due to the additional variability introduced by the resting vessel diameter. Importantly, this method is inherently dependent on the resting diameter and thus influenced by the current state of the vessel. We feel it is necessary to discuss another alternative for measuring the control range and briefly review the respective benefits and pitfalls of the methods. A discussion of the alternative measurement of the control range is provided in the Supplementary Materials.

As no correlation was observed between dilation and constriction capacities, this newly proposed metric integrates both mechanisms to provide a more comprehensive representation of retinal microvascular function. By integrating both dilatory and constrictive responses, the control range provides a more complete depiction of retinal microvascular behavior than either parameter alone. Future studies are needed to determine how this measure performs across different cohorts and whether it adds value in identifying microvascular impairment.

Age Dependency

In the present study, myogenic constriction exhibited a pronounced age dependency, with older participants showing reduced autoregulatory responses. This finding is consistent with previous assessments of retinal microcirculation27 and aligns with the results reported by Blum et al.24 and Jeppesen et al.25 where age-dependent changes in the myogenic constriction response were observed. Blum et al.24 reported that, in younger participants, even moderate increases in BP induced vasoconstriction, whereas in older participants larger increases in BP were required to elicit a vasoconstrictive response. Similarly, Jeppesen et al.25 found that the myogenic constriction response is reduced from midlife onward. The age-related decline in myogenic constriction may be partially attributed to increased vascular stiffness, resulting from a reduction in elastic fibers, an increase in collagen deposition, elevated calcium accumulation, and loss of vascular smooth muscle cells due to decreased cellular proliferation with age.40–42 This reduction in smooth muscle cells is further exacerbated by inflammation and vascular calcification.43 Blum et al.24 therefore suggested that changes in vessel diameter should be evaluated using slope-based measures, as these are independent of age. Similarly to the slope-based measures, no significant association was found between age and the control range, suggesting that this parameter may represent a promising marker that is less influenced by age.

Limitations

Due to the high complexity of the measurement, it currently requires a skilled operator to perform it. This complexity, for both the patient and the examiner, may also contribute to the data quality dropout rate. However, this limitation could be addressed in the future through the automation of certain processes, such as the use of audio signals to guide the patient when the handgrip force falls outside the desired range.

Additionally, the interpretation of myogenic constriction per BP increase is limited by the small number of participants with simultaneous BP recordings, which reduces the statistical power of findings within this subgroup. Furthermore, beat-to-beat BP measurement at the finger may be affected by the contralateral handgrip exercise. As handgrip intensity increases, the opposite hand often tenses involuntarily, potentially introducing artifacts that compromise the accuracy of the BP data. Moreover, mean resting BP was in the prehypertensive range, indicating that the study participants were not entirely free of cardiovascular risk factors. This should be considered when comparing these data with those from other study populations.

Conclusions

Our study introduces a novel approach to assessing BP elevation–induced myogenic constriction and the regulatory capacity of small retinal arteries. The good day-to-day reliability of the myogenic constriction measurement supports its application in both longitudinal and cross-sectional study designs, underscoring its potential as a valuable tool for evaluating microvascular function. Importantly, the lack of association between flicker light–induced dilation and myogenic constriction suggests that both vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive mechanisms should be considered as distinct yet complementary aspects of microvascular function. Therefore, these two measurements were combined into a new metric termed “control range.” Given the central role of myogenic constriction in blood flow regulation, its relationship with various CV diseases warrants further investigation to explore its diagnostic and predictive value. Future studies should aim to explore associations with CV risk factors in diverse populations and determine whether dual-modality assessments improve CV risk stratification or early disease detection.
