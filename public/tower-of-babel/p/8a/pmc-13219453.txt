Integrating Pythagorean fuzzy numbers into the graph model to resolve water resource conflicts in the Yellow River Basin
Authors: Yichan Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractConflicts over sustainable water resource development trigger social unrest, constrain economic development, and exacerbate geopolitical tensions. The graph model for conflict resolution (GMCR) provides a feasible tool for resolving such disputes. However, the complex uncertainty of such conflicts makes it difficult for traditional GMCR to yield satisfactory solutions for each decision-maker (DM). In this paper, Pythagorean fuzzy numbers are incorporated into the graph model framework to express DMs’ true preferences. For the first time, a Pythagorean fuzzy graph model is proposed to solve complex real-world conflicts. A new preference-ranking method accounting for trust relationships supplements traditional GMCR to determine the true preferences of individual DMs with common interests (composite decision-makers). DM weights are calculated based on point degree centrality and proximity centrality. A group decision matrix is obtained using the Pythagorean fuzzy weighted average operator, and comprehensive scores are calculated using Romanian selection and the group score matrix to rank alternatives. On this basis, new stability definitions collectively called Pythagorean fuzzy stability are proposed in GMCR. Finally, the model is successfully applied to conflicts over sustainable water resource development in the Yellow River, expanding the theoretical scope of GMCR and providing feasible solutions for resolving water resource conflicts.Keywords: Graph model for conflict resolution, Pythagorean fuzzy values, Trust relationships, Conflicts over sustainable water resource development, Yellow RiverSubject terms: Engineering, Environmental social sciences, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Conflicts over sustainable water resource development often arise from the uneven distribution of resources and sharp increases in water demand, leading to intense competition between regions and industries1. Since water resources are scarce, it is difficult to simultaneously meet the needs of agriculture, industry, and residents, which impacts economic production and quality of life2. Furthermore, excessive exploitation and poor management of water resources provoke ecological degradation, threatening the ecological health of water environments and reducing biodiversity3. Competition for water resources intensifies social conflicts, leading to cross-regional and inter-departmental conflicts that threaten social stability and sustainable development4,5.

The Yellow River Basin is regarded as the “mother river” of China, providing a central focus of politics, culture, and the economy. Conflicts over sustainable water resource development in the Yellow River Basin damage the environment, affect agricultural and residential water use, and trigger social conflicts between provinces. The traditional approach to resolving such conflicts mainly involves environmental and engineering strategies. However, this approach overlooks the decision-making behavior of decision-makers (DMs) and their interactions within uncertain conflict environments6,7. The uncertainty in DMs’ preference cognition and the complex nature of conflict exacerbate the difficulty of conflict resolution.

In this paper, by combining two classical decision analysis methods with uncertainty preferences, we propose policy suggestions from a management perspective to resolve conflicts over sustainable water resource development. Our approach safeguards the needs and interests of each DM in this dispute and ensures the strongest consensus during the decision-making process.

It is widely accepted that human beings and their collective organizations are inherently competitive. Conflicts arise as a result and take on various forms, including conflicts over sustainable water resource development8. Conflict harms both individuals and social stability. Scholars have developed several conflict analysis methods to resolve this real-life negotiation problem9. First, the game theory model established a foundation for future research. Metagame analysis was then developed to calculate equilibria based on DMs’ preferences10. However, the increasing complexity of conflicts makes it difficult to determine DMs’ precise preferences, so existing methods often fall short. Thus, a more practical and more intuitive method called the graph model for conflict resolution (GMCR) has been suggested for conflict analysis theory11,12.

GMCR has two main advantages. First, it combines qualitative and quantitative methods; second, it requires only relative preferences—but not precise utility values—for analysis. GMCE proceeds through modeling and analysis3. First, we determine four basic elements; next, we conduct a conflict analysis by applying the four basic stability definitions to GMCR. The use of stability concepts in GMCR not only characterizes behavioral sharing and benefit interaction among DMs but can also resolve real-life conflicts and shape effective policy13,14.

The preferences of DMs for all states in the traditional GMCR are assumed to be crisp preferences15,16. This means that the preference degree of each DM between any two states is clear and definite. Nevertheless, complex conflicts, such as those over sustainable water resource development, are often accompanied by conflicting backgrounds that are difficult to accurately define, as well as information asymmetries among DMs, producing significant uncertainty in their preference relationships17–19. This makes it difficult for traditional GMCR to solve conflicts involving uncertain preferences20,21. It is crucial to depict DMs’ uncertain preference relationships in conflicts and obtain resolution solutions in uncertain environments. Thus, multiple types of uncertainty preferences have been incorporated into the GMCR framework. Corresponding definitions of uncertainty stability have been derived, providing an effective analytical tool for solving complex conflicts.

A new type of uncertainty preference called the unknown preference, symbolized by “~”, was introduced by Li, et al.22. Subsequently, the most common fuzzy preference types were incorporated into the analytical procedure of GMCR23. GMCR also offers a theoretical paradigm for studying other uncertain preference variants, including gray24, incomplete fuzzy reciprocal25, probabilistic26, and intuitionistic preferences14. Fuzzy set theory was established by Zadeh27. Atanassov28 expanded this idea and proposed intuitionistic fuzzy set theory, which uses membership and non-membership degrees to represent DMs’ degrees of affirmation or negation toward a scheme. Yager29 then proposed Pythagorean fuzzy sets, which extend the available range to decision-making situations in which the summation of membership and non-membership degrees may surpass 1, yet their squared total is no more than 1. This provides DMs with a more relaxed decision-making environment. At the same time, some advanced fuzzy decision-making methods have also been applied to environmental management, providing reference for the discussion in this article30,31.

Numerous studies have examined decision-making methods based on Pythagorean fuzzy sets since their introduction. For example, Akram, et al.32 proposed the Pythagorean fuzzy approximation ideal solution ranking method to resolve group decision-making problems. Pythagorean fuzzy numbers provide a scientific way to characterize the uncertainty of DMs33–35. However, no existing GMCR research has considered the superiority of Pythagorean fuzzy sets in characterizing uncertainty, which greatly limits GMCR’s capacity to solve complex problems.

We use Pythagorean fuzzy sets to express the uncertainty of DMs within the GMCR logical framework. The sets are first integrated into GMCR to develop rational stability definitions in an uncertainty negotiation environment. The graph model with Pythagorean fuzzy numbers can enrich the scope of conflict analysis research and compensate for the shortcomings of existing research. Specifically, it allows us to resolve conflicts over sustainable water resource development.

In traditional GMCR, the preference ranking method constructed for all states of each DM is based on clear preferences. This approach has two important shortcomings. First, when DMs’ preferences are uncertain, the existing preference-ranking methods become difficult to apply36. Second, the situation in which DMs involved in conflicts form composite decision-makers (CDMs) was not taken into account37,38. With the development of information technology centered on the internet, it has become increasingly convenient for single DMs aggregated into CDMs to exchange information, especially when social networking software provides a platform for social interaction, communication, and information exchange. Such platforms enhance the close connections between DMs, allowing social relationships such as trust and influence to be magnified and manifested in actual decision-making activities, thereby forming a social network with certain characteristics39,40. When composed of trust relationships, this is called a trust network.

In a group decision-making environment, due to factors such as abundance of information and different DM levels, there are corresponding differences in levels of trust between decision experts, which hinders decision-making consensus41. For example, Li and Wei42 proposed a two-stage dynamic influence model based on social networks. This model can account for consensus formation within group decision-making under conditions of incomplete information. Thus, accounting for trust relationships among DMs during preference ranking helps optimize consensus formation in social network environments, yielding more satisfactory results43–46.

In this paper, we introduce Pythagorean fuzzy numbers into social networks, accounting for both objective attribute weights and decision expert weights. We propose a Pythagorean fuzzy preference ranking method that considers trust relationships. A group of definitions collectively called Pythagorean fuzzy stability in GMCR is proposed to resolve conflicts over sustainable water resource development in the Yellow River and to design feasible policy suggestions in which stakeholders achieve a win-win situation for all. In social networks, different vertexes and edges are generally used to represent interlinked social relationships between DMs. Vertices represent social actors, and edges represent relationships between them, such as trust and cooperation51,52. Definition 13

Social networks are typically represented in the form of a directed graph G(V, E), where V={v1, v2,…, vm represents the various DMs, and E represents the directed edges in the network graph. (vi, vj)∈E indicates a direct trust relationship between DMs vi and vj. Social networks can also be represented in the form of adjacency matrices. The adjacency matrix of a directed graph G(V, E) is represented by D=(dij)m×m.

In social network analysis, centrality is the characteristic of a vertex and comes in three types: degree, intermediate, and near-centrality. Degree centrality represents the centrality of a vertex in a local area, reflecting the direct connectivity and interactivity between this vertex and other vertexes. Relative degree centrality designates the ratio of the absolute centrality of a vertex to its maximum possible degree in the network graph. In a directed graph, the degree of a vertex comprises its in-degree and out-degree. The in-degree is the number of edges directed toward the vertex, reflecting the degree to which it is trusted by others. The out-degree is the number of edges departing from the vertex, reflecting its direct connections to others.

Proximity centrality is an indicator used in network analysis to measure the power of vertexes in a network. The proximity centrality of vertexes represents those that are not controlled by others. The closer a vertex is to the center, the greater the power of the corresponding vertex and the greater its influence. The proximity to the center of a vertex is the reciprocal of the sum of the shortest path distances of that vertex to all other vertexes in the social network graph.

Pythagorean fuzzy stability definition of GMCR

Four new stability definitions—PFNash, PFGMR, PFSMR, and PFSEQ—have been proposed in GMCR with Pythagorean fuzzy values. Our newly proposed definition of stability details the interests and trends of DMs in uncertain decision-making environments as well as the interactive feedback process with opponent DMs.

The PFNash definition of stability does not require the movement of an opponent DM to be deduced from the opponent’s initial state. Thus, the PFNash definition is identical for GMCR with two DMs and GMCR with multiple DMs at the same time. This definition requires only one step of movement to determine the stability of the focus state, which is one-step strong stability. Finally, a focus state is considered to be PFNash stable for each DM in conflicts and is thereby a global PFNash stable result.

PFGMR stability definitions fully consider the irrational counterattacks of opponents. This counterattack behavior affects the main DMs’ judgment, impacting the conflict stability of the given state. However, the focus DMs in PFGMR stability definitions do not consider opponents’ preferences. In other words, it is only through the two-step movement of the DMs that we can determine whether a given state satisfies PFGMR stability, which is weak. Finally, when a given state belongs to PFGMR stability for each DM, the state is the global PFGMR stable solution.

PFSMR stability definitions are constructed and developed using PFGMR stability definitions. Specifically, compared to PFGMR, PFSMR stability definitions integrate an additional step based on the original two-step movement. This means that DM k will counterattack the opponent’s irrational counterattack behavior to ensure stability at state s. Thus, PFSMR stability definitions belong to three-step mobile stability and weak stability. When a focus state s is considered PFSMR stable for all DMs, that state is the global PFSMR stable solution.

Within GMCR with Pythagorean fuzzy values, a state s is PFSEQ for DM k iff s1∈, there exists another state s2∈ that possesses s≻ks2, and it is represented as s∈.

PFSEQ stability and PFGMR stability are formally similar. The difference is that in PFGMR, the opponent DMs adopt irrational counterattacks, while in PFSEQ, DMs only accept rational counterattacks by opponents. Therefore, PFSEQ stability is identifiable with two-step mobile stability and weak stability. Furthermore, when a focus state is considered PFSEQ stable for all DMs, the state is the global PFSEQ stable solution.

Within GMCR with Pythagorean fuzzy values, for a given state s, s is considered a Pythagorean fuzzy equilibrium (PFE) for each DM if and only if it is a Pythagorean fuzzy stable state for each DM according to any types of stability selected from Definitions 17 − 20.

Application to conflicts over sustainable water resource development in the Yellow River Basin

Background

Ningxia Province is one of the five major autonomous regions in China. This province is small but faces serious problems, such as ecological fragility, water resource scarcity, and challenges in industrial transformation and upgrading—especially in the Yellow River Basin (Fig. 3). The ecology in this basin is extremely fragile and marked by frequent natural disasters. On the one hand, due to factors such as climate change and human activities, the ecosystem has been severely damaged, causing serious problems, such as forest degradation, desertification, soil erosion, ecological imbalance, and frequent natural disasters—especially floods—which have seriously impacted locals. On the other hand, amid large-scale urbanization and agricultural development in the Yellow River Basin, high-quality development has been insufficient, highly polluting enterprises are concentrated, and industrial enterprises cause serious damage to the local ecological environment, hindering sustainable development in the local economy and society.

The chemical industry in Ningxia, which includes the development of coal chemicals, petrochemicals, fertilizers, new chemical materials, and other materials, has developed rapidly and become an important economic pillar of the local economy. This industry has a large economic total, high correlation, and strong driving force. However, with the deepening of industrialization and the proliferation of enterprises, wastewater discharge into rivers has become a major ecological challenge. Once harmful substances in industrial wastewater are discharged into rivers, they not only endanger the survival of aquatic organisms but also threaten the human living environment. In 2021, the Central Ecological and Environmental Protection Inspection Team discovered that many enterprises in the fine chemical industry park located in Pingluo County, Ningxia Province, demonstrate illegal utilization of Yellow River water resources and prominent pollution hazards. The local protection bureau and the government immediately took relevant actions to restrict the discharge of pollutants by enterprises, demanding that they raise their wastewater treatment standards.

Significance of the research

First, the Yellow River Basin is an important water source in China, especially in the northwest and northern regions. A shortage of water resources directly affects agriculture, ecology, and social stability. One of the core goals of water resource management is to reduce soil erosion and drought phenomena and to restore the ecological environment through scientific planning and management.

Second, water resources are crucial for urban, industrial, and agricultural development in the Yellow River Basin. Reasonable water resource management could not only improve the efficiency of water use but also inject momentum into the local economy. For agriculture, optimizing the use of irrigation resources and improving water recycling can ensure stable grain production and increase farmers’ income. For industry, reducing water pollution and conserving water can enhance the green production of enterprises, attract green investment, and promote industrial structure upgrading. For cities, optimizing water supply systems and sewage treatment facilities—and thereby enhancing stabilizing the water resource supply—promotes urbanization and high-quality regional economic development.

The Yellow River Basin encompasses multiple provinces and regions with differences in water distribution and demand. The management of water resources thus faces a significant challenge in cross-regional coordination. By establishing effective regional cooperation mechanisms, water resources in the Yellow River Basin can be distributed fairly and reasonably. To achieve the goal of “overall planning and coordinated development,” it is necessary to promote cooperation between upstream and downstream areas and between left and right banks. Conflicts caused by competition for water must be avoided. In addition, local governments, enterprises, and all sectors of society need to come together, implement public education and participation, form a joint effort to solve water resource problems, and ensure that water governance can be balanced with social harmony and long-term development.

Significance of the study

In water resource conflicts, stakeholders can effectively identify and understand the needs, interests, and positions of all parties through conflict analysis results, in order to develop targeted solutions. Firstly, conflict analysis can help stakeholders identify the root causes of conflicts, including unequal distribution of water resources, disputes over usage rights, environmental impacts, and socio-economic factors. This process not only reveals surface issues, but also delves into potential conflicts of interest. Secondly, by establishing a dialogue platform and negotiation mechanism, all parties can engage in rational discussions based on data and facts, seek common interests, and promote cooperation and compromise. This kind of dialogue not only helps to reduce misunderstandings and hostility, but also enhances trust and encourages all parties to participate in the formulation of solutions. Finally, utilizing the analysis results to promote the optimization of policy formulation and management measures, making the utilization of water resources more sustainable, effectively alleviating conflicts, and achieving a win-win situation for all parties. At the same time, stakeholders can also use the results of conflict analysis to carry out public propaganda and education, raise community awareness of the importance of water resource management, further promote the participation of all sectors of society, and form a more comprehensive and long-term conflict resolution mechanism. Through these comprehensive measures, stakeholders can achieve more efficient coordination and cooperation in complex water resource management, laying the foundation for regional sustainable development.

Conclusions and future works

The Yellow River not only harbors a profound historical and cultural heritage but also plays a pivotal role in the country’s economic development, ecological security, and social stability. The management of water resources in the Yellow River Basin is necessary to ensure ecological well-being, sustainable economic development, and long-term societal stability. Through scientific governance and rational allocation of water resources, the environment can be effectively improved. Additionally, economic coordination and development can be promoted, achieving a win-win situation for society, the economy, and the environment. However, the complexity and uncertainty of conflicts over sustainable water resource development in a dynamic environment require DMs to demonstrate flexibility and adaptability in the decision-making process. This is the only way to ensure the fair and sustainable allocation of water resources while promoting regional coordination and sustainable development.

The novel method proposed in this article, which we call the Pythagorean fuzzy graph model, can resolve conflicts in complex and uncertain environments. Specifically, GMCR with Pythagorean fuzzy values provides a new approach for resolving conflicts over water resources. The GMCR method can help identify the core issues in such conflicts and clarify the needs and positions of all stakeholders. By analyzing the interests and emotional tendencies of all parties, this method helps identify effective solutions, thereby reducing misunderstandings and conflicts. Ultimately, this method helps promote rational communication between parties, enhance trust and cooperation between DMs, and minimize disputes.

The government can protect the legitimate rights and interests of each DM in conflicts over sustainable water resource development by formulating sound laws and regulations, providing mediation platforms, and ensuring fair, just handling procedures. The conflicts examined in this article involve only two DMs, ignoring the role of third-party DMs, such as the government. Therefore, in future research, we will consider more complex conflicts over water management involving multiple DMs, including the government, and provide rational policy recommendations in this extended context.

The limitation of this study is that the newly proposed preference acquisition method is relatively complex and has a limited scope of application. In addition, the future research direction is to enhance model adaptability test its effectiveness in other mangrove regions, and new preference acquisition methods can be proposed.
