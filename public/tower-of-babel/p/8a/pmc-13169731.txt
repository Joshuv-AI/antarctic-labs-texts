Behind hypertension in white-coat heroes: a cross-sectional study exploring the link between burnout, occupational factors with hypertension in Kinshasa’s healthcare workers, Democratic Republic of Congo
Authors: Rodrigue Khonde, Sandra Tricas-Sauras, Aline Labat, Bernard Kianu, Eric Mafuta, Guillaume Kiyombo, Yves Coppieters
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHypertension remains a leading cause of cardiovascular diseases worldwide. Unfavorable working conditions may lead to detrimental effects on employees’ health and work well-being. Healthcare workers are often exposed to sustained high stress levels due to the demands of their occupation, making them more susceptible to burnout. Despite their commitment to caring for others, their health can also be compromised. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among healthcare workers in Kinshasa, focusing on occupational factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted through a structured questionnaire based on the modified WHO STEPwise and Maslach Burnout Inventory - Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel among healthcare workers selected via multistage stratified random sampling in Kinshasa’s hospitals from December 2023 to January 2024. Weight, height, blood pressure, and fasting blood sugar were measured. The prevalence of hypertension was determined. All associated variables with hypertension were included in a multivariable logistic regression analysis at the 5% significance level.ResultsOf 566 healthcare workers enrolled in the study, 55.0% were female and 45.0% were male, with a mean age of 39.0 ± 10.4 years. The prevalence of hypertension was 23.3% (95% CI: 19.9–27.0), with 57.6% (95% CI: 48.7–66.1) of hypertensive participants unaware of their diagnosis at the time of data collection. In the multivariable analysis, the socio-demographic and lifestyle factors associated with hypertension were age ≥ 40 years (aOR = 3.48, 95% CI: 2.02–5.98), physical inactivity (aOR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.02–2.80), being overweight (aOR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.05–3.06), and being obese (aOR = 3.80, 95% CI: 2.06–7.01). Regarding occupational factors, seniority ≥ 10 years (aOR = 3.41, 95% CI: 1.94-6.00), night shift work (aOR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.85–8.78), and burnout syndrome (aOR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.23–3.31) were associated with hypertension.ConclusionBeyond lifestyle, occupational conditions contribute to hypertension among HCWs. Addressing hypertension requires tackling both lifestyle behaviors and modifiable work-related factors. Integrating healthy practices such as regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, and routine blood pressure monitoring with structured shift schedules, stress management programs, and supportive workplace environments can protect cardiovascular health, prevent burnout, and strengthen HCWs’ efficiency and patient care quality.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27191-w.

Background

Hypertension is one of the leading modifiable causes of premature mortality and a major contributor to the burden of cardiovascular disease in the world [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of individuals with hypertension is estimated to have doubled from 650 million to 1.3 billion between 1990 and 2019 [2]. The most affected are people aged between 30 and 79 years, two-thirds of whom live in low- and middle-income countries, and almost half of the people who have hypertension are currently unaware of their condition [2, 3]. While the prevalence of hypertension has decreased in high-income countries, there is a rising trend in low- and middle-income countries [4].

Although the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC) lacks national data on the prevalence of hypertension, despite the assumption of an ongoing epidemiological transition in low- and middle-income countries, the routine monthly data from the District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2) over the past five years (2019 to 2023) indicate a 15% increase in hypertension incidence, rising from 13.2% to 15.2% in the general population [5].

The recognized risk factors for hypertension can be categorized into two primary groups: modifiable (tobacco use, excessive alcohol consumption, an unhealthy diet, and a sedentary lifestyle [3, 6] and non-modifiable (sex, age, genetic predispositions, such as a family history of hypertension, and race/ethnicity) [4, 7]. Besides these recognized conventional risk factors, work environment factors have also been identified as significant contributors. Several working conditions have been reported to influence blood pressure among workers, including job stress [8], noise exposure [9], shift work [10], short sleep/sleep deprivation [11], workload, and long working hours [12]. These conditions contribute to an increased cardiovascular risk, and in the present study, we focused on some of them.

Burnout is identified as a phenomenon specific to the occupational environment resulting from chronic exposure to job stress [13]. This is characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment, particularly in occupations that frequently deal with others in their work [14]. It affects all occupations, in particular healthcare workers (HCWs) [15]. As a result, HCWs, who provide care and services to patients to improve their health, themselves face high demands at several levels, making them more susceptible to burnout, especially given the high-stress conditions of their work environment [16].

The problem of burnout among HCWs in low- and middle-income countries, including sub-Saharan Africa, has largely been overlooked, as attention has been mainly focused on occupational health and safety concerns related to various occupational hazards, particularly biological ones, accounting for almost half of the cases, according to the scientific literature [17]. However, a worker experiencing burnout would be much more exposed to other occupational risks [16]. Although the available studies in the African context are limited, the current studies show that burnout is a serious health problem among HCWs in sub-Saharan Africa [18].

Previous studies have shown that burnout is associated with various health consequences, including health behaviors (alcohol consumption and smoking) [19], as well as mental health disorders [20]. However, the relationship between burnout and cardiovascular health, especially hypertension, remains largely under-documented [21].

To our knowledge, the scarce studies conducted in the DRC on work environment factors associated with hypertension mainly focused on job stress [22–24] without exploring other consequences resulting from this prolonged exposure to job stress, such as burnout. As essential components of the healthcare system, HCWs are crucial in managing and providing health care to the populations. Their ability to perform optimally in their roles is closely linked to their health status, directly affecting their productivity and efficiency. Hypertension, which affects thousands of people, does not spare HCWs. Combating non-communicable and promoting healthy working conditions and the well-being of these HCWs is consistent with the objectives of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs 3.4, SDGs 8.5 SDGs 8.8) adopted by nations, including the DRC. Epidemiological efforts and disease prevention strategies in the DRC remain primarily focused on infectious diseases despite the growing evidence of an ongoing epidemiological transition in low- and middle-income countries [5]. Comprehensive data on the factors associated with hypertension in HCWs would allow for the planning and implementation of interventions targeting this occupational category. Given the gaps mentioned above, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among HCWs, emphasizing occupational factors, including burnout.

Methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional study with data collection conducted from December 2023 to January 2024 among selected HCWs in Kinshasa’s General Referral Hospitals (GRH).

Sampling methods and participants

The study participants in the survey included physicians and nurses from Kinshasa, selected through a multistage stratified random sampling method.

Kinshasa is divided into 24 municipalities, organized within four administrative districts. These municipalities have 35 health zones, each containing a General Referral Hospital (GRH) or a hospital functioning as a GRH. First, the 35 GRH were identified and classified within the 24 municipalities. Three municipalities were then randomly chosen from each district. In each selected municipality, one GRH was either directly chosen if it was the only one or randomly selected if there were two. The sample size was proportionally allocated across the four districts. Finally, participants were randomly chosen using a systematic polling approach based on comprehensive lists of HCWs from each hospital.

The sample size was determined using an estimated prevalence of hypertension (p) of 41.9% [25], a 95% confidence level (t) with a standard value of 1.96, a 5% margin of error (m), and a design effect of 1.5, with an additional 10% for non-response. Inclusion criteria were any HCW (physician or nurse) employed at a designated Kinshasa GRH who agreed to participate. A total of 566 HCWs from 12 GRHs in Kinshasa were included in the study.

Data collection and study variables

Data were collected via face-to-face interviews using an electronic questionnaire that combined the modified WHO STEPwise questionnaire (Supplementary material 1) and the Maslach Burnout Inventory - Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel questionnaire (MBI-HSS-MP) (Supplementary material 2). This questionnaire was integrated into smartphones using the Open Data Kit (ODK) Collect application, allowing trained interviewers to enter data directly.

The use of random sampling helped to minimize recruitment bias. At the same time, non-response rates were reduced due to the study’s interest, appropriate interview length (30 to 45 min), and participants’ flexibility in selecting a suitable survey time. Training sessions for interviewers and pre-testing the reliability and validity of data collection tools contributed to reducing measurement bias. A pretest involving 60 HCWs, representing approximately 10% of the total study population, was conducted in two GRHs that were not included in the main study.

The questionnaire was structured into three main parts: the initial section collected sociodemographic, lifestyle, and occupational information, while the second and third focused on measurements, including anthropometric data (weight, height) and blood pressure readings, as well as fasting blood sugar levels.

The dependent variable was hypertension. The independent variables were sociodemographic, lifestyle, and occupational variables.

Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, marital status (unmarried, comprising single, divorced, and widowed; and living with a partner, including married and cohabiting), educational level, household size, and place of living. Lifestyle variables covered behavioral factors (smoking, alcohol consumption, fruit and vegetable consumption, and physical activity), anthropometric measurements (body mass index “BMI”), and biological parameters (fasting blood sugar). Occupational variables incorporated the occupation (physician or nurse), department (non-surgical, including internal medicine and pediatrics, and surgical, comprising surgery and gynecology-obstetrics), employment contracts (permanent and temporary), seniority, night shifts, burnout syndrome, and hypertension knowledge.

Operational definitions of variables

Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg and/or use of current antihypertensive therapy and/or known hypertension [26]. A self-reported history of hypertension diagnosed by an HCW was used to define “known hypertension”. Using a digital blood pressure monitor OMRON X2 Basic (HEM-7121 J-EO, Omron Healthcare Co., Ltd., Kyoto, Japan), blood pressure was measured three times on the same arm at intervals of two to three minutes. The participant’s arm was kept at heart level and their feet flat on the floor, following a rest period for a minimum of 10 min after they had been seated. The mean of three separate measurements determined the systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings (in mmHg) [27].

Age was dichotomized into two groups: <40 years and ≥ 40 years. This threshold was selected based on evidence that blood pressure tends to increase from around 40 years of age [28], and for analytical purposes, to ensure more balanced groups and avoid overly wide age intervals.

Smoking status among HCWs was determined by any reported use of cigarettes or similar substances within the last 12 months [29].

Consideration of HCWs as alcohol consumers was based on consumption of alcoholic beverages within the 30 days before the survey. According to the WHO, this consumption is defined as four standard glasses per day for women and six for men per day, with each standard glass containing 10 g of ethanol. A standard glass corresponds to a typical beer bottle (285 ml), a medium-sized glass of wine (120 ml), an aperitif (60 ml), or a small glass of liqueur (30 ml) [30].

Fruit and vegetable consumption was assessed through respondents’ self-quantification of their portions. Participants were given explanations concerning the meaning of a portion of fruit and/or vegetables (80 g) [30]. The average daily consumption was determined by multiplying the number of fruit or vegetable portions consumed per day by the number of days per week they were eaten, then dividing by 7. Daily intake was deemed sufficient if one of the following conditions was met: an average daily consumption of ≥ 5 portions of either fruits or vegetables or a combined average daily intake of both fruits and vegetables totaling ≥ 5 portions per day [30].

BMI was calculated by dividing the weight in kilograms (kg) by the square of the height in meters (m²), with the final value presented in kg/m² [31]. Weight for each participant was obtained using an electronic scale, SECA 813 (SECA GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), well-calibrated, ensuring they wore light clothing and no shoes. Height was measured to the nearest centimeter using a portable wall scale, SECA 213 (SECA GmbH & Co. KG, Hamburg, Germany), with participants standing shoeless, backs against the wall, heels together, and looking straight ahead. Following the WHO obesity classification, BMI was categorized into three groups: normal weight (< 25 kg/m²), overweight (25–29.9 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30 kg/m²) [32].

Diabetes was defined as having a fasting blood sugar level of ≥ 126 mg/dL and/or being on oral antidiabetic medication [33]. Fasting blood sugar measurement was obtained after at least 8 h of fasting using a digital OneTouch Verio Reflect blood sugar monitor.

Physical activity was assessed using the Ricci and Gagnon (R&G) scale [34], which considers sedentary behaviors, leisure physical activities, and daily physical activities, physical activity was assessed. Based on their scores, participants were categorized into two groups: inactive (score < 18) and active (score ≥ 18) [34].

Night shift work was defined as a work schedule carried out during nighttime hours rather than the conventional 8-hour daytime shift [35].

The assessment of hypertension knowledge focused on knowledge of hypertension risk factors, medical and lifestyle management, and complications. Each correct answer was awarded a score of 1, while incorrect or unknown responses were assigned a 0. A total knowledge score was generated and standardized to 100, with knowledge levels classified as low (< 60%), moderate (60–79%), and high (≥ 80%).

Burnout syndrome was assessed using the MBI-HSS-MP, a widely used instrument to assess burnout, especially for HCWs, and it consists of 22 items [36]. The scale includes three dimensions: (1) emotional exhaustion, (2) depersonalization, and (3) personal accomplishment. Each item evaluates a specific domain on a 7-point scale, ranging from “never” to “every day”. Scores range from 0 to 54, 0 to 30, and 0 to 48, respectively, for emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment. High scores on the emotional exhaustion (≥ 27) and depersonalization (≥ 13) subscales, or a low score on the personal accomplishment subscale (≤ 31), strongly indicate symptoms of burnout. Participants were considered to have burnout at the time of the survey if they had a score ≥ 27 on the emotional exhaustion subscale and/or ≥ 13 on the depersonalization. This criterion is the most widely used in studies to define burnout [37].

Internal consistency of the MBI-HSS-MP dimension score was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (acceptable values are above 0.7) [36]. For the whole MBI-HSS-MP, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79. Reliability testing of the MBI subscales performed well, with Cronbach’s alpha of the emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and personal accomplishment subscales being 0.88, 0.80, and 0.73, respectively. In this study, burnout was analyzed as a global construct rather than by individual dimensions, as the main objective was to investigate factors associated with hypertension, with burnout considered as one of the occupational exposure variables.

Statistical analysis

For quantitative data, statistical analyses were conducted using Stata software version 17. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to verify the normality of continuous variables. Means and standard deviations were used to describe continuous variables, whereas categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages.

Multivariable logistic regression models were constructed to investigate the sociodemographic, lifestyle, and occupational variables associated with hypertension. The final model included all variables that showed significance in the bivariate analyses. A non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test was used to determine the best-fitting model. Multicollinearity was assessed by testing the variance inflation factor (VIF), where a VIF above 5 indicated multicollinearity. The strength of associations was quantified using odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. For all analyses, statistical significance was set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Sociodemographic and lifestyle characteristics, and prevalence of hypertension

Among the 566 HCWs participants, 311 (55.0%) were female and 255 (45.0%) were male. The mean age was 39.0 ± 10.4 years, with nearly 60% being under 40 years. Most participants (93.1%) had a university degree, and nearly three-fifths (57.8%) lived with a partner. In almost half of all cases (47.7%), the household size was between 4 and 6 residents, and about 73.0% were tenants. Regarding lifestyle factors, the majority of HCWs were non-smokers (91.5%) and had insufficient fruit and vegetable consumption (90.1%). Additionally, 63.1% were physically inactive, and 44.2% had consumed alcohol within the past 30 days. BMI measurements indicated that 33.0% were overweight, 16.8% were obese, and diabetes was found in 10.8%. The prevalence of hypertension was 23.3% (95% CI: 19.9–27.0), and 57.6% (95% CI: 48.7–66.1) of those with hypertension were unaware of their condition at the time of the survey.

Bivariate associations of sociodemographic and lifestyle factors with hypertension

In bivariate analysis, being aged ≥ 40 years (cOR = 4.93, 95% CI: 3.23–7.53), married or living with a partner (cOR = 2.46, 95% CI: 1.60–3.78), drinking alcohol (cOR = 1.53, 95% CI: 1.04–2.26), physically inactive (aOR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.10–2.58), overweight (aOR = 2.50, 95% CI: 1.56–4.01), and obesity (aOR = 5.76, 95% CI: 3.39–9.80) were more susceptible to have hypertension (Table 1).

Occupational characteristics and their association with hypertension

Among participants, 303 (53.5%) were nurses and 263 (46.5%) were physicians. Slightly more than half (54.1%) were in non-surgical departments, with the majority being permanent hospital employees (88.9%). Nearly three-quarters had less than ten years of seniority (73.5%), and 61.1% had night shift work ranging from 1 to 12 nights per month. Over a quarter (27.9%) of HCWs were found to have burnout syndrome, and less than a tenth (9.2%) had a low level of knowledge about hypertension (Table 2).

Multivariate analysis

The sociodemographic and lifestyle risk factors identified for hypertension among HCWs in Kinshasa were age ≥ 40 years (aOR = 3.48, 95% CI: 2.02–5.98), physical inactivity (aOR = 1.69, 95% CI: 1.02–2.80), overweight (aOR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.05–3.06), and obesity (aOR = 3.80, 95% CI: 2.06–7.01). Regarding occupational variables, the identified risk factors for hypertension were seniority ≥ 10 years (aOR = 3.41, 95% CI: 1.94-6.00), night shift work (aOR = 5.00, 95% CI: 2.85–8.78), and burnout syndrome (aOR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.23–3.31).

Discussion

This study has examined the prevalence of hypertension and its associated factors among HCWs in Kinshasa. The prevalence of hypertension was 23.3% (95% CI: 19.9–27.0), and the identified sociodemographic and lifestyle factors associated with hypertension were age, physical activity, overweight, and obesity, while seniority, night shift work, and burnout syndrome were occupational factors associated with hypertension.

Prevalence of hypertension

The prevalence of hypertension in our study was similar to findings reported in Nigeria among HCWs [38]. However, it was higher compared to the findings reported in Angola [39]. The variability in results may be explained by several factors, such as the characteristics of the study population, geographical location, sector of activity, and working conditions.

Given their awareness and understanding of hypertension and its prevention measures, we might have expected a lower prevalence of hypertension among HCWs. However, a recent study reported a lower prevalence of hypertension in the general population at 15.2% [5].

More than half of HCWs did not know they were hypertensive at the time of the study. Known for its asymptomatic nature and insidious onset, hypertension is often discovered only after complications occur, especially without routine screening. The high prevalence among HCWs is alarming, given that they are the ones treating hypertensive patients and are essential in preventive measures through the guidance they provide to their patients.

Association of hypertension with sociodemographic and lifestyle factors

In this study, older age (≥ 40 years) was associated with hypertension. Similar findings have been reported elsewhere among HCWs in Angola [39] and Brazil [40]. Blood pressure is known to increase with age after the forties as a result of progressive physiological changes in the cardiovascular system [28]. Our findings are consistent with the results of other studies.

The chance of being hypertensive was associated with physical inactivity. These findings were also reported elsewhere [41]. Strong evidence supports that physical activity reduces blood pressure in both normotensive and hypertensive adults. For those with hypertension, it also reduces the risk of progression to cardiovascular diseases [42]. Although they are well-informed about the negative impacts of physical inactivity, a large proportion of them are inactive, as our findings show (63.1%). Factors contributing to this physical inactivity include prolonged periods of sitting, shift work, and the stressful nature of their work, as reported elsewhere [43].

The chance of having hypertension was 1.79 times higher in overweight and 3.80 times higher in obese HCWs compared to those with normal weight. The risk of obesity for HCWs is higher due to factors related to the demands of their work, such as shift work with disrupted sleep patterns to provide healthcare services, leading HCWs to have unhealthy eating habits, an increased appetite, and reduced levels of physical activity. These conditions promote obesity, factors associated with the chance of developing hypertension [44].

Association of hypertension with occupational factors

HCWs with seniority > 10 years had a higher chance of being hypertensive, as they were three times more likely to have hypertension compared to those with less than 10 years. Although seniority is naturally correlated with age, our multivariate analysis showed that the association remained statistically significant even after adjusting for age. This finding suggests that, beyond the effect of chronological aging, prolonged exposure to occupational stressors and unfavorable working conditions associated with seniority may be potentially harmful to their cardiovascular health. These findings align with those reported in South Africa [45] and Cameroon [46].

HCWs with night shift work were more likely to have hypertension. These findings are in line with another study in Brazil among HCWs [40]. Night shift workers usually have a daily routine that markedly differs from their daytime counterparts. Besides experiencing disturbed sleep, their social interactions and leisure activities post-work and on weekends are also impacted [47]. These social and family aspects are essential for managing the pressure and stress resulting from work. In addition to reduced sleep duration, they are also known to have increased intake of coffee, hypnotic medications, and alcohol, as well as a sedentary lifestyle, factors contributing to the risk of hypertension [40]. However, many HCWs opt for night shifts due to the additional financial compensation they receive. As a result, they often take on additional night shifts in multiple hospitals, leading to prolonged working hours and insufficient rest periods. In our study, some HCWs performed up to 12-night shifts per month, which could contribute to the increased disruption mentioned above.

The odds of hypertension among HCWs with burnout syndrome were two times higher compared to those without burnout syndrome. This association was also reported in Chile [21] and Taiwan [48]. Burnout, a phenomenon resulting from prolonged exposure to job stress, could explain the mechanism behind the increased blood pressure and risk of hypertension through the autonomic nervous system and the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, the main systems responding to human stressors [49]. Although individuals could develop coping strategies to manage their stress, prolonged exposure to stress eventually leads to a lasting imbalance between demands and the individual’s resources to deal with them, leading to illnesses such as burnout [49, 50]. In the hospital environment, HCWs are confronted with stressors associated with their jobs, particularly those linked to the demands of patient care services, night duty, and patient interactions. The healthcare sector is often associated with arduous working conditions and high levels of stress due to daily patient interactions. The combination of fatigue, stress, and insufficient physical and mental rest contributes to an increased risk of errors and prolonged exposure to stress [51]. Thereby, this prolonged exposure to stress exposes HCWs to an increased chance of developing hypertension, and in our study, more than a quarter of HCWs were burnt out (27.9%).

As highlighted in a recent study, disease prevention efforts and epidemiological priorities in the DRC remain primarily focused on infectious diseases despite emerging data suggesting an epidemiological transition in low- and middle-income countries, including the DRC [5]. Within the Congolese workforce, which includes a large portion of the population, health promotion activities in the workplace are unstructured, even in hospitals where HCWs, the key actors in the health system, are employed. The DRC’s legislation requires companies to organize occupational health services or to subscribe to these services from hospitals when they cannot provide them internally [52]. Therefore, establishing occupational health services and promoting healthy lifestyles as well as workplace well-being in these hospitals through improved working conditions for HCWs could have a beneficial effect on other occupational sectors, as long as these HCWs play an important role in disease prevention, including hypertension.

Strengths and limitations

This study has both strengths and certain limitations that should be considered. First, this study is distinguished by its investigation of both the typically known factors of hypertension and the occupational factors associated with hypertension among HCWs in Kinshasa, with an emphasis on the occupational health dimension, in a low- and middle-income country undergoing an epidemiological transition, such as the DRC. The second aspect to emphasize is that burnout, also observed within the Congolese work environment, is a contributing factor to hypertension. Moreover, this study highlights the need to promote occupational health initiatives focused on work conditions leading to prolonged job stress among HCWs, which could have detrimental health consequences.

Despite these strengths, this study has some limitations that should also be mentioned. Firstly, this study focused exclusively on physicians and nurses, restricting the generalizability of the findings to the entire HCWs. Secondly, only active HCWs present at hospitals during the survey were included, which could introduce a potential healthy worker effect; individuals with poorer health status or on sick leave were not represented. Although the potential confounding effect of occupational category (physicians and nurses) was assessed and found not to materially affect the results, a residual healthy worker bias cannot be entirely excluded. Finally, while the MBI scale offers significant information regarding burnout syndrome in research or workplace assessment frameworks, its diagnostic results are not considered to have direct clinical value.

Conclusion

Our study emphasizes that protecting the cardiovascular health of HCWs requires an integrated approach addressing both healthy lifestyle and occupational factors that contribute to hypertension among HCWs. Promoting healthy behaviors—such as regular physical activity, balanced nutrition, maintaining a healthy weight, and routine blood pressure monitoring—should be combined with practical workplace interventions. Hospitals can implement structured shift planning to prevent excessive night work, enforce adequate rest periods between shifts, develop and establish stress management programs, and foster a supportive work environment that minimizes chronic occupational stressors.

Implementing these measures can have a direct impact on HCWs’ well-being and professional performance. Targeted interventions, including on-site exercise facilities, nutrition counseling, regular health assessments, and workshops on stress management, can reduce fatigue, prevent burnout, and lower the risk of hypertension and other cardiovascular diseases. By enhancing workforce resilience and functional capacity, such strategies improve the quality and safety of patient care, strengthen HCWs efficiency, and support the long-term sustainability of healthcare services.
