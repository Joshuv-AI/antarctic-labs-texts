Transcriptomic insights on differential gene regulations in viable but non-culturable (VBNC) bacteria under chlorine and UV disinfection
Authors: Mandy Lok Yi Tang, Eunice Yuen Man Chung, Erin Yee Nam Yu, Stanley Chun Kwan Lau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacteria entering viable but non-culturable (VBNC) state as a stress response to disinfection is one of the major challenges in sewage treatment, as these bacteria evade culture-based detection methods while retaining metabolic activities, pathogenicity, and the possibility to resuscitate, raising concerns for the public health risks of effluent discharge. The formation of VBNC cells and their gene regulations among different intrinsic and extrinsic conditions (e.g. stress factors, bacterial species and environmental conditions) are yet to be investigated. To facilitate the understanding of bacterial stress tolerance and adaptation under disinfection, we conducted a multifaceted investigation into the VBNC formation and differential gene expression of two commonly used fecal indicator bacteria (Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecium) under the effect of different disinfectants (chlorine and UV-C), strains (sewage-isolated and ATCC reference), and surrounding media (effluent and PBS).ResultsThe formation of VBNC cells was observed after disinfection across all experimental conditions. Notably, the proportion of VBNC cells in effluents after chlorine treatment reached over 90% of the total population. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were observed in the transcriptomes of treated cells (containing viable cells without culturable cells) compared to the untreated cells. UV-C treated bacteria primarily upregulated carbohydrate metabolism, DNA repairing, and toxin-antitoxin systems, whereas chlorine-treated bacteria upregulated exopolysaccharide production, phosphotransferase system, and secondary carbon metabolism while suppressing central carbon and other energy metabolism. Both chlorine- and UV-C treated cells exhibited downregulation of DNA replication, transcription, and the biogenesis of RNA, ribosome and chromosome. However, most DEGs related to bacterial secretion system were exclusively upregulated in E. coli; only Type IV pili components were upregulated in E. faecium. Tetracycline and vancomycin resistance genes were particularly upregulated in the E. faecium strains isolated from sewage but not the laboratory reference strains, indicating the strain-to-strain variations in metabolic activities. Besides, more DEGs were identified in cells suspended in effluents than in PBS under chlorination, showing different extent of transcriptomic shifts between media.ConclusionsThis study revealed distinct responses and adaptive mechanisms of bacteria against chlorine and UV-C disinfection. The presence of VBNC populations at extreme dosages highlighted their high tolerance against disinfectants. Moreover, the alterations in gene expression profiles suggested some gene regulations associated with the non-reproductive and energy conserving traits in stressed cells, and reflected the potential risks of bacterial pathogenicity in disinfected effluents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05441-6.Keywords: Gene expression, VBNC, Chlorination, UV radiation, Transcriptome, Fecal indicator bacteria, Sewage, Disinfection, Functional pathway, Stress response

Introduction

In the pursuit of sustainable water management, sewage treatment is critical for mitigating environmental pollution and protecting public health from waterborne pathogens. Chlorination and ultraviolet (UV) radiation are prevalent disinfection technologies employed in sewage treatment facilities worldwide to reduce microbial loads in effluents before discharge. To assess the treatment efficacy and potential health risks of effluent discharge, the concentrations of fecal indicator bacteria (FIB) including Escherichia coli and Enterococcus spp. are routinely monitored as surrogates for pathogen loads. However, a huge challenge arises from the transition of these bacteria into viable but non-culturable (VBNC) state under disinfection, which is a physiological condition where cells maintain metabolic activity and potential virulence but are unable to reproduce and form colonies on culture media, escaping detection by conventional culture-based water sanitation assessment protocols [1, 2].

Bacteria entering the VBNC state is a survival tactic under stressful conditions and thus a widespread phenomenon in sewage treatment works. Studies have documented a high prevalence of VBNC cells in effluents after disinfection, including a great variety of pathogens possessing numerous virulence genes [3–5]. Their presence is particularly alarming for public health, as certain proportion of VBNC cells are able to resuscitate in a relatively favorable environment after disinfection, such as the human gut or downstream water bodies, potentially triggering infections, antibiotic-resistant outbreaks, and chronic diseases [6, 7].

Previous studies on VBNC bacteria have mainly focused on the phenotypic or metabolic changes of a particular species under a certain stress condition but fall short of comparing the changes under different situations such as induction methods, species or environmental conditions, which hinders a complete understanding of formation and behaviour of VBNC bacteria. This limitation has important implications to sewage disinfection processes, where chlorine and UV-C disinfection are both prominent stress factors that induce VBNC populations, yet they have completely different mechanisms of cellular damage. Chlorine attacks bacteria by oxidizing proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, leading to the damage of multiple cellular components, membrane permeabilization and metabolic disruption [8]. In contrast, UV-C (100–280 nm), which is commonly used for sewage disinfection, primarily causes DNA damage in bacteria via pyrimidine dimer formation, halting replication while preserving membrane integrity [9, 10]. Given the distinct cellular targets of these disinfectants, whether and how VBNC bacteria respond differently to chlorine and UV-C treatments are of fundamental scientific interest and carry important public health implications, as these bacteria would be discharged to the environment and affect the ecosystem. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the gene expression shifts of chlorine- and UV-C induced VBNC bacteria in sewage to decipher their stress-specific survival strategies and their potential impact.

In this study, two types of FIB, E. coli and Enterococcus faecium, were isolated from municipal effluents and then exposed to different dosages of chlorine and UV-C (254 nm) to examine their changes in physiological states (i.e. culturable, viable and dead) and the proportions of induced VBNC populations. Transcriptomic profiling of untreated versus treated cells (containing viable cells without culturable cells) was utilized to gain insights into the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and the associated functional changes upon the entry of VBNC state. In addition to indigenous sewage strains, two reference strains (E. coli ATCC 25922 and E. faecium ATCC 19434) were included to compare the differences between sewage-isolated and laboratory reference strains under different disinfection stresses. Furthermore, the experiments were conducted in two different media which were phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and sterilized effluent. They served for a comparison of the gene expression profiles between a controlled, relatively nutrient-limited condition (i.e. PBS) and a complex, nutrient-rich condition (i.e. sterilized effluent). This study aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis by incorporating different types of disinfectants, dosages, bacterial strains, and environmental conditions. In particular, the differential gene expression which may be associated with the non-reproductive, pathogenicity and energy conserving characteristics of VBNC cells were examined. The findings facilitated the understanding of bacterial stress tolerance and adaptation under sewage disinfection, providing insights for the development of strategies against the formation of VBNC bacteria and risk assessment of effluent discharge.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of E. coli and E. faecium from sewage

As a coastal city, 80% of the population in Hong Kong use seawater for toilet flushing. The majority of seawater-based sewage produced is treated using chemically enhanced primary treatment (CEPT). In contrast, the remaining population uses freshwater for flushing and produces freshwater-based sewage that is treated using activated sludge process (secondary treatment). The E. coli and E. faecium strains used in this study were obtained from the undisinfected effluents collected from Stonecutters Island Sewage Treatment Works (using CEPT to treat seawater-based sewage) and Stanley Sewage Treatment Works (using activated sludge process to treat freshwater-based sewage). The operational parameters of the two sewage treatment works were indicated in Supp. Table 1.

The collected effluents were filtered through 47 mm diameter, 0.45 μm pore-sized nitrocellulose membranes, which were then incubated on CHROMagar™ ECC agar plates at 37 °C for the growth of E. coli and mEI agar plates at 41 °C for the growth of Enterococcus [11]. After 24 h, putative colonies of E. coli and Enterococcus, which appeared as blue colonies on both types of agar, were transferred to Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) and grown at 37 °C for 24 h. The cultures were stored in 25% glycerol at -80 °C. After that, the identities of isolates were confirmed using PCR detection of gene markers (Supp. Figure 1). For the identification of E. coli, malB and bglY gene markers were used to eliminate other bacteria or cryptic clades of Escherichia (clades II to V), which are evolutionary distinct but not distinguishable from E. coli using conventional diagnostic tools [12]. The use of bglY gene marker to classify cryptic clades was illustrated in Supp. Figure 2 as reference. For the identification of Enterococcus strains, tuf, ddl and a strongly conserved non-coding sequence were used to differentiate E. faecium, E. faecalis and other Enterococcus species [13–15]. The information of respective primer sequences, amplicon sizes, reaction mixtures and thermal cycles is provided in Supp. Table 2.

To extract the genomic DNA of the isolates, each colony was first suspended in 100 µL of nuclease-free water, heated at 99 °C for 20 min and cooled on ice for 5 min, followed by centrifugation at 8,000 x g for 10 min. The supernatant containing DNA was then collected and stored at -20 °C. Reference strains of E. coli (ATCC25922), E. faecium (ATCC19434) and E. faecalis (ATCC 19433) were used as positive controls for the PCR amplification. All the PCR products were subjected to gel electrophoresis in a 1.5% agarose gel at 120 V for 25 min in Lithium Acetate Borate (LAB) buffer, then stained with SYBR™ Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen™) for 20 min and visualised using a UV Gel Documentation System (Bio-Rad). Confirmed E. coli and E. faecium isolates were classified into genotypes using rep-PCR and (GTG)5-PCR DNA fingerprinting, respectively [16, 17]. The PCR products were resolved by gel electrophoresis and the digital gel images were then imported into the software Bionumerics v7.0 (Applied Maths) to determine the similarity cut-off for genotype classification. One E. coli strain and one E. faecium strain with genotype found present in both effluent types were chosen as representatives for experiments, together with two reference strains (E. coli ATCC25922 and E. faecium ATCC19434).

Preparation of bacterial cell suspension and medium for chlorine and UV-C treatments

Escherichia coli strains were cultured in LB while E. faecium strains were cultured in TSB at 37 °C for 16–18 h with shaking at 220 rpm. The overnight cultures were then inoculated into fresh medium in the ratio of 1 : 9 at 37 °C with shaking at 220 rpm until reaching mid-log phase (O.D.600 absorbance = around 0.4 for E. coli and 0.8 for E. faecium). The cells were harvested by centrifuging 10 mL of cell suspension at 3,000 x g for 10 min and discarding the supernatant. The cells were then washed with PBS twice and resuspended in 10 mL of PBS.

Sterilized CEPT effluents were prepared by filtering through 0.22 μm pore-sized polycarbonate membranes, then autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min and stored at 4 °C before use. Temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen (DO), pH and turbidity of the sterilized effluents and autoclaved PBS were measured in triplicate using a Yellow Stone Instrument 6600-M (YSI Inc.), while the total nitrogen (TN), ammonia (NH3), nitrite (NO2−), nitrate (NO3−), total phosphorous (TP), phosphate (PO43−), and silicate (SiO32−) were measured in triplicate according to the APHA standard methods [18]. The measured physicochemical parameters were summarized in Supp. Table 3.

Chlorine treatment

Before carrying out the experiments, all the glassware were pretreated to remove chlorine demand by soaking in 15–20 mg L− 1 of reagent-grade sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) solution (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h, followed by thorough rinsing with Milli-Q water three times. The prepared cell suspensions of each bacterial strain were transferred to pretreated glass beakers and diluted 100 times with either autoclaved PBS or sterilized effluents. The estimated culturable concentrations of bacterial cell suspensions based on the O.D.600 absorbance were around 6 log10 mL− 1 for effluents and 7 log10 mL− 1 for PBS. A higher cell concentration was used for PBS as the bacterial reduction by chlorine in PBS was supposed to be more rapid than in effluents. Five dosages of NaOCl solution were applied to the bacterial cells suspended in PBS (E. coli: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 mg L− 1; E. faecium: 0, 1, 2, 3, 4 mg L− 1)., whereas four dosages were applied to cells suspended in effluents (both E. coli and E. faecium: 0, 4, 8, 12 mg L− 1). These dosages were determined by preliminary tests to capture significant reduction of culturable cells for each type of bacteria and medium. NaOCl solution was added to the cell mixtures with continuous mixing by magnetic stirrers. Each dosage was performed in triplicate. Autoclaved PBS and sterilized effluent without adding bacterial suspension served as negative controls. The chlorine reaction was stopped by adding 0.05% of 1 M sodium thiosulphate (Na2S2O3) after 40 min, which is within the range of contact time among sewage treatment plants (around 30–60 min). Total residual chlorine (TRC) were measured in triplicate at 40 min using the N, N-diethyl-p-phenylenediamine (DPD) method with a chlorine colorimeter (YSI Inc.) before the addition of Na2S2O3.

UV-C treatment

The prepared bacterial cell suspensions were diluted 100 times with autoclaved PBS or effluents to an estimated culturable concentrations of 6 log10 mL− 1 bacterial cells based on the O.D.600 absorbance. After the dilution, 20 mL of the suspension was transferred to each 90 mm sterile polystyrene Petri dish (Thermo Scientific™). Autoclaved PBS and effluent without bacterial cells served as negative controls. The dishes were placed in the center of an UV crosslinker (Fisherbrand™, Fisher Scientific), which contained five UV-C lamps (254 nm, 8 W each) with a distance of 15 cm above the surface of the dishes. Both E. coli and E. faecium bacterial cells were exposed to UV-C dosages of 0, 3, 5, 10, 20, 40, 60, 120, 300 and 1500 mJ cm− 2 in individual dishes. These dosages were determined by preliminary tests to capture a significant reduction of culturable populations. The extremely high dosage of 1500 mJ cm− 2 was added to examine if there would be further reduction in the viable populations. All treatments were conducted in triplicate.

Enumeration of culturable, viable and dead cells

Bacterial suspensions were subjected to the measurements of culturable, viable and total cells immediately after the chlorine or UV-C treatment. Culturable cells were enumerated by using membrane filtration methods [11]. The samples were filtered through 0.45 μm pore size nitrocellulose membranes and incubated in the dark for 24 h on CHROMagar™ ECC agar at 37 °C for culturing E. coli and mEI agar at 41 °C for culturing E. faecium. The filtered volumes of samples ranged from 1 to 100 mL, with serial dilutions up to 107-fold in PBS. Blue colonies appeared on the agar plates after incubation were reported as colony forming units (CFU). All the measurements were conducted in triplicate and averaged. The detection limit for this assay was 1 CFU 100 mL− 1.

Viable and dead cells from both chlorine and UV-C treatments were enumerated using flow cytometry. Different sample preparation protocols were applied to chlorine and UV-C treated cells before using the flow cytometer due to the differences in bactericidal mechanisms of two disinfectants. Chlorine inactivates bacterial cells by rapidly oxidizing cell membranes and intracellular components [19, 20]. Therefore, the viability of chlorine-treated cells was assessed based on membrane integrity using LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ bacterial viability kit (Invitrogen™), which stained viable and dead cells differently. However, this method is not suitable for UV-treated cells, as UV-C inactivates bacterial cells by cross-linking DNA without damaging cell membranes. Therefore, the viability of UV-C treated cells was assessed based on their biosynthesis capability using direct viable count (DVC) method [21, 22]. Cells were considered viable if they demonstrated the ability to elongate when provided with nutrients for growth and an antibiotic to arrest cell division [23].

For sample preparation, chlorine-treated cells were stained using LIVE/DEAD™ BacLight™ bacterial viability kit, which contains SYTO 9 (green-fluorescent) that stained all cells and propidium iodide (PI) (red-fluorescent) that stained presumably dead cells with compromised membranes. Equal volumes of SYTO 9 and PI were mixed and 0.6 µL of the mixture was added to 200 µL of chlorine-treated cells. After incubation in dark at room temperature for 15 min, the sample were spiked with 50 µL of 10-fold diluted CountBright™ Absolute Counting Beads (Invitrogen™) as control. The concentrations of viable populations of chlorine-treated cells would be calculated by subtracting the number of SYTO 9-stained bacteria (total) with the number of PI-stained bacteria (dead). In contrast, UV-C treated cells were subjected to DVC procedure, which stimulated the elongation of viable cells but not the dead cells [21, 22]. In this procedure, the UV-C treated cells were incubated with 0.05% yeast extract and levofloxacin at minimum inhibitory concentrations (E. coli sewage strain: 0.5 µg mL⁻¹, E. coli reference strain: 1 µg mL⁻¹, both E. faecium sewage and reference strains: 6 µg mL⁻¹) in the dark at 37 °C (3 h for E. coli, 6 h for E. faecium). In this treatment, yeast extract served as a nutrient for viable cells to grow, while levofloxacin prevented cell division. After the treatment, 0.5 mL of samples were stained with 1 µg/mL acridine orange (AO) for 15 min at room temperature to produce green fluorescence for distinguishing bacteria from debris. The samples were then spiked with 10 µL of 10-fold diluted counting beads as control. All the samples were prepared in triplicate.

After sample preparation procedures, both chlorine and UV-C treated cells were subjected to the detection in a multicolour flow cytometer (FACSCelesta™ Flow Cytometer, BD Biosciences) equipped with three lasers of excitation wavelengths at 405 nm (violet), 488 nm (blue) and 640 nm (red). Green fluorescence was detected via a 530/30 nm bandpass filter, while red fluorescence was detected via a 695/40 nm bandpass filter. Forward Scatter (FSC) and Side Scatter (SSC) were used to measure the relative cell sizes and their internal complexity, respectively. To ensure instrument performance, quality control was conducted via BD Cytometer Setup and Tracking (CS&T) using CS&T beads (BD Biosciences) at a low flow rate of 12 µL min⁻¹ prior to measurements. The determination of optimal polygonal gates for distinguishing viable and dead populations required different cell controls for each treatment. For chlorine treatment, untreated cells and cells treated with 100 mg L⁻¹ NaOCl for 20 min were used as the viable and dead cell controls, respectively. Meanwhile for UV-C treatment, untreated cells and cells treated with 75% ethanol for 30 min served as the viable and dead cell controls, respectively. All the measurements of viable and dead cells were performed in triplicate with 15,000–25,000 signal events recorded for each replicate. The final concentrations of cells were adjusted by the recovery of counting beads as in Eq. 1.

Transcriptome sequencing and data processing

Based on the results of culturable and viable concentrations, triplicate samples of untreated cells and triplicate samples of treated cells which contained viable cells without any detectable culturable cells for each strain in each medium were selected for transcriptome sequencing. The chlorine and UV-C dosages used for the treated cells were indicated in Supp. Table 4. Untreated cells were those subjected to identical experimental procedures except exposure to chlorine or UV-C. The cell suspensions of these samples were filtered through 0.22 μm pore size polycarbonate track etched (PCTE) membranes (GVS Filter Technology) immediately after treatment, with filtered volumes of 10 mL for untreated cells and 200 mL for treated cells. The membranes were placed into Zymo ZR BashingBead lysis tubes added with 1mL of DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research). RNA was extracted by using ZymoBIOMICS™ DNA/RNA Miniprep kit (Zymo Research) following the manufacturer’s procedures. The transcriptomic sequencing was conducted on Illumina NovaSeq platforms by Novogene (Tianjin, China), to generate 10 millions of 150 bp paired-end reads per sample. Read base quality analysis was conducted using FastQC v0.11.5. Low-quality bases and adapter sequences were trimmed using Fastp v0.23.1 [24]. Raw reads with adapter contamination, > 10% of uncertain nucleotides or > 50% of low quality nucleotides (base quality score less than 5) were discarded. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) reads were removed using SortMeRNA v4.3.4 [25]. After quality control, an average of 1.2 × 107 filtered reads was yield for each sample and over 97% reads were over Q30. Clean reads were mapped to reference genomes (E. coli ATCC 25922 genome NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_000401755.1 and E. faecium ATCC19434 genome NCBI RefSeq assembly GCF_001544255.1) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 in reference-based assembly mode using default settings [26]. Functional assignments were performed using eggNOG-mapper v2.1.9 for gene ontology (GO) terms and KEGG pathway annotations [27]. Relative expression levels of transcripts were calculated in transcripts per million (TPM) by using Salmon v1.8.0 to account for both transcript length and sequencing depth.

The 3-dimensional non-metric multidimensional scaling (nMDS) plot based on Euclidean distance of the TPM values of expressed genes was constructed using the MASS package in R software v4.5.1. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified by using DESeq2 [28], with the criteria of log2 fold change > |1| and FDR-corrected p-values (q-value) < 0.05. Volcano plots showing the fold change and significance of DEGs were constructed using VolcaNoseR [29].

Results

Induction of VBNC state under different dosages of UV-C and chlorine

Both UV-C and chlorine treatment reduced culturable cell concentrations as dosages increased, regardless of the bacterial species and media used (Figs. 1 and 2). Under UV-C treatment, the culturable concentrations of E. coli strains dropped from around 6 log10 CFU mL− 1 to below the detection limit as the dosage reached 10 mJ cm− 2 in PBS and 20 mJ cm− 2 in effluents (Fig. 1). Culturable E. faecium also became undetectable, but at higher dosages up to 120 mJ cm− 2 in effluents. Similarly, under chlorine treatment, the culturable concentrations of E. coli and E. faecium dropped from around 6–7 log10 CFU mL− 1 to below detection limit (Fig. 2). Chlorine dosages required to eliminate culturable cells in effluents were much higher than those in PBS, especially for E. coli, which required at least 5 times higher dosages. In addition to the differences in disinfectant resistance between bacterial species and media, strain-to-strain variations were also observed. For instance, the UV-C dosage required to eliminate the culturable E. faecium sewage strain in PBS was 6 times higher than that for the reference strain. Higher dosages were also required to eliminate the culturable sewage strains comparing to the reference strains in all chlorine experiments.

In contrast, the reduction in viable concentrations of E. coli and E. faecium under UV-C and chlorine was less pronounced. For UV-C treatment, the reduction in viable concentration was within 1 log10 after exposing to 300 mJ cm− 2. Even after the exposure to extremely high dosage of 1500 mJ cm− 2, the reduction of both bacteria only reached around 2 log10. Over 4 log10 cells mL− 1 of viable cells remained in all UV-C experiments. For chlorine treatment, the reduction of viable E. coli and E. faecium cells was negligible in effluents, while the reduction of viable E. coli and E. faecium cells in PBS was around 1 and 3 log10 respectively after treating with the highest dosages. The E. faecium reference strain in PBS had the lowest viable counts after chlorine treatment, which was approximately 4 log10 cells mL− 1.

The concentrations of VBNC cells was determined as the difference between the concentrations of viable and culturable cells. Around 90% of the E. coli and E. faecium in effluents were in VBNC state even after treated with the highest chlorine dosage, corresponding to 6–7 log10 cells mL− 1. The TRC measured at the end point (40 min) of all chlorination experiments ranged from 0.03 to 1.81 mg L− 1 in PBS and 0.04–1.09 mg L− 1 in effluents. Although the proportions of VBNC cells induced by UV-C in effluents were lower, they still reached over 40% of the total populations when the culturable proportions were undetectable.

Transcriptome profiling of treated and untreated cells

The nMDS plot depicted the dissimilarity in the transcriptomic profiles of treated and untreated cells of E. coli and E. faecium in chlorine and UV-C treatments, with a stress value of 0.02 (Fig. 3). Distinct separation was observed between E. coli and E. faecium, reflecting species differences in gene expression. The variations of gene expression between chlorine and UV-C treatment were more pronounced in E. coli than in E. faecium. Moreover, the divergence in transcriptomes between treated and untreated cells was greater in E. coli, especially for the chlorine treatment.

Blue in colour: chlorine treatment, red in colour: UV-C treatment; circle in shape: E. coli, square in shape: E. faecium; hollow figure: untreated cells, filled figure: VBNC cells

The average number of annotated genes were 4714 in E. coli strains and 2447 in E. faecium strains. Differential expression analysis identified significantly up- and down-regulated genes in treated cells comparing to untreated cells under UV-C and chlorine treatments (log2FC > |1| and q-value < 0.05) (Figs. 4 and 5). For UV-C treatment, the percentages of downregulated genes over the total number of annotated genes in different strains and media ranged from 0.36 to 6.54%, while that of the upregulated genes ranged from 0.12 to 4.74% (Fig. 4). Downregulated genes consistently outnumbered upregulated genes across all strains and media. For chlorine treatment, the percentages were 0.04–34.07% for downregulated genes and 0.20-33.31% for upregulated genes (Fig. 5). Among all the experimental conditions, chlorine-treated E. coli reference strain in effluent exhibited the greatest number and proportion of DEGs. Overall, the total number of DEGs in chlorine-treated cells was > 3 times higher than in UV-C treated cells. Furthermore, chlorine-induced DEGs had higher average fold changes than those induced by UV-C.

The percentages of common DEGs over total DEGs in UV-C and chlorine-treated cells were low. In E. coli, only 203 downregulated genes (13.01% of all downregulated genes) and 75 upregulated genes (6.25% of all upregulated genes) were shared between two treatments. Similarly, in E. faecium, only 34 downregulated and 40 upregulated genes were shared, corresponding to 5.99% and 10.55% of all downregulated and upregulated DEGs.

Functional analysis of DEGs in UV-C treated cells

Considering the DEGs in UV-C treated cells of all strains and media, the genes were primarily associated with metabolism and genetic information processing (Fig. 6). Numerous genes coding for DNA repair and recombination proteins were upregulated, particularly the rec and din genes involving in the SOS response. Two genes dinD and dinI encoded for DNA-damage-inducible proteins, with dinI showing a high log2FC of 5.16. Recombination proteins recA and recN, along with the regulatory protein recX, were also upregulated, with recN having a log2FC of > 3. In contrast, DEGs associated with DNA replication, chromosome and nucleoid partitioning proteins, transcription machinery, and the biogenesis of mRNA, tRNA and ribosome were predominantly downregulated. Key examples included DNA replication initiation and elongation factors (dnaABGN), cell division proteins (ftsALNQWX), tRNA modification factors (trmBDL, tsaBC) and rRNA modification factors (rsmBE, rlmAEI).

Pathways labelled in red indicated that they had more upregulated genes than downregulated genes, while those in blue indicated that they had more downregulated genes than upregulated genes

For metabolism, many DEGs were involved in the carbohydrate and amino acid metabolic pathways. More genes were upregulated than downregulated in these pathways. For carbohydrate metabolism, the upregulated genes spanned through glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, citrate cycle, pyruvate metabolism, fructose and mannose metabolism, and so on. All the genes in phosphate acetyltransferase-acetate kinase pathway (interconverting acetyl-CoA to acetate) were upregulated. Meanwhile, various types of amino acid metabolism were also upregulated, such as aspartate, glutamate, valine, leucine and tryptophan. The entire pathway of converting chorismite to tryptophan was upregulated. Conversely, the genes involved in the metabolisms of glycans, terpenoids, polyketides and nucleotides were mostly downregulated. The genes waaDJLOPY responsible for the biosynthesis of lipopolysaccharide core region were all downregulated.

In addition, the toxin-antitoxin system (TA system) showed 10 upregulated genes, such as hokCD, ldrABCD, vapC, bssSR and bhsA. ClpA in E. coli and clpP in E. faecium which assisted the degradation of antitoxins were also upregulated. The related multiple stress resistance protein bhsA had the highest log2FC (6.64) among all DEGs. Apart from the functional pathways classified in KEGG, an uncharacterized protein ybiJ was upregulated with a high log2FC of 6.12, which was assigned to the GO annotation of DNA damage response.

Functional analysis of DEGs in chlorine-treated cells

For the DEGs in chlorine-treated cells of all strains and media, the regulations of some carbohydrate metabolic pathways were different from that of UV-C treated cells (Fig. 7). Most of the DEGs in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, citrate cycle, pentose phosphate pathway and pyruvate metabolism were downregulated. Genes involved in the Embden-Meyerhof pathway of glycolysis (converting glucose to pyruvate), conversion of oxaloacetate to fructose-6P in gluconeogenesis, complete citrate cycle and pentose phosphate pathway were all downregulated. In contrast, many genes were upregulated in the metabolisms of other types of carbohydrates and related sugar acids, such as fructose, mannose, galactose, ascorbate and aldarate. Especially, the genes for the degradation of ascorbate to D-xylulose-5P were all upregulated, which included the ulaABCDEF operon and ulaG cistron. The log₂FC of ulaA and ulaB were 5.30 and 4.49, respectively.

Numerous metabolic pathways showed downregulation, including oxidative phosphorylation, methane and sulfur metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, amino acid metabolism (threonine, methionine, histidine), and biosynthesis of lipopolysaccharide, peptidoglycan, terpenoid backbone, and polyketide sugar units. For example, cytochrome bd ubiquinol oxidase subunits cydX and cydB in the oxidative phosphorylation pathway were downregulated with log₂FC of -6.00 and − 4.00, respectively. For assimilatory sulfate reduction, 6 genes (cysCDHIJN) throughout the entire pathway and the sulfate transporter cysP were downregulated. The biosynthesis of purine and pyrimidine (both DNA and RNA) and amino acids including threonine, methionine and histidine were also completely downregulated. Notably, many genes in exopolysaccharide biosynthesis were upregulated, particularly those for production of colanic acid (wcaABCEFIJKL), which is a crucial component of biofilm. Another upregulated metabolic pathway is the biosynthesis of siderophore group nonribosomal peptides, such as yersiniabactin and enterochelin.

Consistent with UV-C treatment, the DEGs of chlorine-treated cells associated with DNA replication, chromosome and associated proteins, transcription machinery, the biogenesis of mRNA, tRNA and ribosome were predominantly downregulated. The ribosome-associated inhibitor raiA in ribosome biogenesis had the greatest log₂FC of -9.21 among all DEGs. However, in contrast to UV-C treated cells, the DEGs for DNA repair and recombination proteins were mostly downregulated, encompassing systems for single- and double-strand break repair and translesion DNA synthesis.

Regarding signaling and cellular processes, many genes were upregulated in the phosphotransferase system (PTS) for membrane transport. Apart from the aforementioned ulaABC that were responsible for transport and phosphorylation of L-ascorbate, celABC that were responsible for transporting cellobiose were also highly upregulated with a log₂FC of > 3.8. Meanwhile for the bacterial motility, chemotaxis proteins such as cheABRWYZ were downregulated, whereas the flagellar assembly proteins including Type III secretion proteins and the C, M, S, P and L rings (fliFGHIJLMNOPR and flgAHI), as well as the fimbrial proteins in pilus system (fimACDFGHI), were all upregulated. Several genes related to the antimicrobial resistance were also upregulated, but their mechanisms differed by the type of species, which will be addressed in next section.

Strain differences and medium effects

Some functional pathways exhibited different gene regulations between species. In E. coli, a total of 59 genes encoding Type I–VI secretion system components (except Type IV) were upregulated in chlorine-treated cells, including adhesin protein transporter (lapBCE), general secretion pathway protein (gspACDEFGHIJKLM), Type IVa pilus homolog protein (hofBCMNOPQ), flagellar export apparatus (fliFHINOPR) and Imp/Vas secretion system core components (impBCGHJKL and vasDGJL). Furthermore, most of the genes in chaperone-usher system and curli biogenesis were upregulated in E. coli, including the fimACDFGH, papACDEFGK, ecpCD and csgABCEF. In contrast, the upregulation of secretion systems in E. faecium was only limited to competence-related DNA transformation transporter (DNA-T) core components, comprising comEA, comEC, comFA, comFC, comGA, comGC and comGF.

Distinct types of antimicrobial resistance genes were also upregulated in E. coli and E. faecium under chlorination. Tetracycline resistance gene (tetA) and vancomycin resistance genes (vanY and vanR) were upregulated in sewage-isolated E. faecium strain, whereas various multidrug resistance modules, including efflux pumps (acrABEF and mdtEF) and repression of OmpF porin (marA), were upregulated in the E. coli reference strain. The upregulation of secretion systems and antimicrobial resistance genes in both species occurred predominantly in effluents.

For both chlorine and UV-C treatments, various types of transcriptional regulators were downregulated, but they were mainly exhibited in E. coli cells. There were 83 and 28 genes being downregulated in E. coli under chlorine and UV-C treatments, respectively. Most of them belonged to araC, lysR, deoR, lacI and tetR families. In contrast, only 14 and 5 genes were downregulated in E. faecium under chlorine and UV-C, respectively.

Overall, the number of DEGs identified in effluents were higher than that in PBS, especially under chlorine treatment. This pattern extended across diverse functional genes such as enzymes, structural components, regulators and transporters. Notably, most of the upregulated genes associated with prokaryotic defense systems and antimicrobial resistance were observed in the effluents.

Discussion

Factors affecting bacterial inactivation under UV-C and chlorine treatment

The results demonstrated different extent of resistance of E. coli and E. faecium against UV-C treatment (Fig. 1). Enterococcus faecium exhibited much stronger resistance to UV-C compared to E. coli, requiring up to 12 times higher dosage to render culturable cells undetectable. This aligns with previous findings that Gram-positive bacteria, such as Enterococcus spp., were generally more resilient to UV-C disinfection than Gram-negative bacteria like E. coli, due to the thicker peptidoglycan layer that absorbed and scattered UV-C radiation from penetrating into the cells [30, 31].

Chlorination required higher dosages to reduce bacterial cells in effluents compared to PBS (Fig. 2), which can be attributed to the higher levels of total nitrogen and ammonia in effluents (Supp. Table 3). Ammonia reacted rapidly with chlorine to form chloramines, which had lower disinfecting power and reduced the bioavailability of free chlorine. The effluents also contained organic compounds which further consumed chlorine through oxidation, therefore the chlorine available for bacterial inactivation was reduced, decreasing the overall disinfection efficacy.

Strain-to-strain variations were observed in both UV-C and chlorine treatments. The sewage-isolated strains of E. coli and E. faecium required equal or higher disinfectant dosages for the elimination of culturable cells compared to laboratory-maintained reference strains. It is consistent with the findings in other study which showed that laboratory-maintained strains were more susceptible to chlorination than those isolated from sewage or environmental sources [32]. We attribute the difference in tolerance of sewage and reference strains to the selective forces in their source environment: sewage strains had been challenged by many stressors during previous primary or secondary treatment processes and developed various adaptive traits, while the reference strains were adapted to relatively stable conditions with limited environmental pressures through long-term propagation in laboratories. Stressors that bacteria might encounter during sewage treatment processes included heavy metals, antibiotics, and pharmaceuticals, as well as fluctuations in temperature, pH, salinity, and oxygen level. It is generally believed that bacteria with prior exposure to stressors would develop greater tolerance to other stressful conditions by adaptive mechanisms involving long retention proteins, phenotypic changes, and epigenetic modifications [33, 34]. The acquired adaptive traits may be unspecific but offer great advantages for bacteria to survive in other harsh conditions. Therefore, the sewage strains might become more resistant to the stress of disinfection due to previous exposure to other stressors. As the reference strains used in this study (E. coli ATCC 25922 and E. faecium ATCC 19434) are standard strains recommended in EPA protocols and widely used as a model in many disinfection and toxicological studies, the observed differences in disinfectant resistance between sewage and reference strains suggested that laboratory-maintained strains might not be able to reflect the actual stress tolerance of bacteria in the field, emphasizing the importance of using field-isolated strains for the determination of disinfection outcomes.

In this study, the dosages used for both UV-C and chlorine treatments in effluents covered the general ranges of dosages used in real-world wastewater treatment systems, which are around 30–100 mJ cm-2 of UV-C and 5–12 mg L⁻¹ of chlorine. However, it is observed that the viable cell concentrations in effluents had limited reduction at high dosages. Especially for UV-C, the viable cells of both species remained above 4 log10 mL⁻¹ at extreme disinfectant dosages (1500 mJ cm⁻2), given that the wavelength of UV-C used in this study (254 nm) is considered as the conventional germicidal UV that is commonly applied for disinfection. These results reflected that the viable populations are very difficult to be eradicated in sewage treatment plants even with the use of extremely high dosages. Furthermore, the induction of VBNC state was pronounced under low dosages of UV-C and chlorine, which is consistent to previous findings [35, 36]. Although increasing the dosages could reduce the percentages of VBNC cells in total population, significant amounts of VBNC cells persisted. Therefore, the discharge of disinfected effluents can potentially introduce large quantities of VBNC bacteria into the environment, threatening the ecosystem as the cells maintain metabolic activities and may actively reproduce after resuscitation.

Comparison of the transcriptomic shifts in UV-C and chlorine-treated bacteria

Transcriptomic sequencing of UV-C and chorine-treated cells, which contained viable cells without detectable culturable cells, was used to explore the gene expression profiles of VBNC cells. However, the results might not solely represent VBNC cell because a considerable amount of dead cells was also observed in the samples subjected to sequencing. Although the presence of dead cells should not have strong impact on the transcriptomic analysis due to the absence of active gene expression and rapid degradation of RNA in dead cells, especially under the damage exerted by UV-C and chlorine treatments [37], residual RNA from the inactivated cells could potentially contribute to the sequencing pools given that the RNA extraction was carried out immediately after treatments. Therefore, the transcriptomic shifts observed in treated cells might also reflect the bacterial stress responses induced by applied treatments, rather than being unequivocally attributable to VBNC cells.

The transcriptomic shifts in treated cells revealed the distinct bacterial stress responses between different species and disinfectants. First, the number and proportion of DEGs found in E. coli were higher than E. faecium in most cases (among different disinfectants, strains and media) (Figs. 4 and 5). This implied greater sensitivity and transcriptional flexibility of E. coli under stress. This disparity might arise from the larger genome size of E. coli (ca. 4.5–5.5 Mb) compared to E. faecium (ca. 2.5–3.5 Mb), allowing more regulatory elements and redundant genes to be activated in response to the stress [38]. Also, as shown in the dose-response curves (Figs. 1 and 2), E. coli strains were more vulnerable to disinfectants than E. faecium, as the thinner cell wall structures of Gram-negative bacteria are generally more susceptible to the intrusion of stressors. Therefore, UV-C and chlorine treatment induced broader gene expression changes in E. coli. Second, higher proportions and fold changes of DEGs were found in chlorine-treated cells than UV-C treated cells, indicating that chlorine introduced greater perturbation to the gene expression profiles of bacteria. It could be attributed to the differences in the reaction mechanisms of two disinfectants. Chlorine species oxidized a wide range of biomolecules including proteins, lipids and DNA, causing massive damage to cellular components, whereas the major effect of UV-C was forming pyrimidine dimers in DNA. The observation aligned with the propensity of chlorine for generating large-scale systemic metabolic reconfiguration, contrasting to UV-C’s localized, DNA-centric responses.

The low proportions of common DEGs in chlorine- and UV-C treated cells suggested that the two disinfectants activated and repressed different sets of genes in bacteria. Under UV-C radiation, the cells showed significant upregulation of SOS response (Fig. 6), which could be activated by the accumulation of single stranded DNA from replication blockages caused by UV-C induced pyrimidine dimers [39, 40]. This system entailed the participation of over 50 genes to perform diverse functions to counteract the DNA damage, involving DNA repair, lesion bypass and cell division 0arrest [40–42]. Among the key genes for the initiation of response, the highly upregulated genes recA and recN played critical roles to mediate strand invasion and tethering activity [43, 44]. The cell division inhibitor sulA which extended time for genome repairing, was also found to be upregulated in the E. coli sewage strain (log₂FC > 4), supporting the prolonged non-reproductive property of VBNC cells. Another modules with upregulated genes in UV-C treated cells were the TA systems, which are also strongly related to the characteristics of VBNC physiological state. Each TA system consists of a toxin that inhibits cell growth by affecting cellular processes like transcription, translation and replication, and an antitoxin that neutralizes the effect of toxin [45]. The antitoxin is labile and can be degraded under stress to liberate the toxin. Increased expression of TA genes in stress-induced persister cells (stress-tolerant, growth-arrested yet culturable) has been widely documented in literatures, especially for the type II TA system [46–49]. While VBNC has been considered as a deeper dormant state than persistence which shares many physiological similarities, some studies suggested TA activation may have a role in the bacterial dormancy of VBNC cells upon exposure to stress [50–52]. Our study showed that numerous TA genes in UV-C induced VBNC bacteria had significant changes in gene expression. For instance, many toxin-coding genes were upregulated, including hokCD which extend the lag phase of cell growth for cells to adapt and respond to the stress, Ldr which blocks translation and causes growth inhibition, and vapC toxins which cleave RNAs and suppress protein synthesis [53–56]. Also, clpAP proteases could enhance the degradation of various antitoxins like parD and mazE, leading to reversible growth arrest [57–59]. Related factors like bssSR also showed an increase of gene expression in VBNC cells, induced by the overproduction of toxin [60]. These results suggested that the TA activities of UV-C treated cells were mediated through the upregulation of toxin-coding genes and proteases which target antitoxin, and these elevated toxins in TA systems could be linked to the characteristic of VBNC cells, i.e. inhibited cell division.

Both UV-C and chlorine-treated cells showed substantial changes in the expression of metabolic pathways (Figs. 6 and 7), but the regulations differed remarkably between disinfectants. UV-C treated cells appeared to upregulate the metabolism of numerous carbohydrate sources, including the central carbon metabolism and other carbon sources. The upregulated phosphate acetyltransferase-acetate kinase pathway governing the energy generation of pyruvate from glycolysis suggested that the bacteria utilized carbon sources like glucose to fuel cellular processes for survival. In contrast, the pathways of central carbon metabolisms tended to be downregulated in chlorine-treated cells, implying that the cells did not prefer obtaining energy from common carbon sources. Instead, metabolisms of other carbon sources were upregulated. Notably, the ula genes for degradation of ascorbate were highly upregulated, acting as a putative energy source of carbon. The switching from metabolizing highly-energetic glucose to alternative carbon sources in VBNC state was also observed in a study working on Bacillus subtilis [61]. Meanwhile, many other types of metabolism were downregulated in chlorine-treated cells, suggesting that the chlorine-treated cells featured a global suppression in metabolic pathways. It is consistent with the extensive disruption of electron transport systems and metabolic enzymes in bacteria by reactive chlorine species. On the other hand, some metabolic pathways which conferred advantages for bacterial survival under oxidative stress had upregulated DEGs, especially the synthesis of exopolysaccharide (EPS). The production of EPS, particularly colanic acid, is involved in cell adhesion and biofilm formation, which has been shown to have a great protective effect against chlorine penetration [62].

Apart from the regulations of metabolism, chlorine-treated cells also showed increased gene expression for phosphotransferase system (PTS). This system plays an important role in carbohydrate transport and phosphorylation, and carries diverse functions such as metabolic regulation, biofilm formation and antibiotic resistance [63]. It was shown that an intact PTS in E. faecalis could promote resistance to stresses like hydrogen peroxide and acid [64]. The elevation of PTS might be beneficial for bacteria to utilize different carbon sources and support the regulation of stress responses against chlorine.

Differences in transcriptomes on species, strain and medium levels

Comparing the transcriptomic alterations in E. coli and E. faecium, distinct gene regulations in the bacterial secretion systems were observed between the species after chlorine treatment. Many DEGs were exclusively upregulated in E. coli because most of the systems were specialized in Gram-negative bacteria, comprising both inner and outer membrane components [65, 66]. It is noted that many operons which coded for the outer membrane (OM) and extracellular proteins like hof, csg and fim were upregulated in E. coli. In contrast, major inner membrane (IM) proteins like sec and tat did not have significant upregulation in either E. coli or E. faecium. These observations can be attributed to the thick peptidoglycan layer in the cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria which offered protection to the cytoplasmic membrane transport systems, whereas the thin cell wall and OM of Gram-negative bacteria were more susceptible to the damage of chlorine oxidation. The upregulation of secretion systems among the general suppression of metabolic pathways in E. coli suggested the higher priority of energy allocation towards repairing these systems in VBNC Gram-negative bacteria. The upregulation of secretion system under chlorine stress and enhanced molecular secretion in VBNC Gram-negative bacteria were also observed in several previous studies [20, 67, 68]. These findings suggested the importance of maintaining the functions of bacterial secretion pathways during VBNC state, as it provided adaptive advantages to the cells under hostile environment such as nutrient acquisition, environmental sensing, communication, pathogenicity and biofilm formation. Meanwhile, it is observed that the DNA-T core components were particularly upregulated in E. faecium. These components were required for constructing surface appendages called Type IV pili (T4P) which involve in adhesion, motility, and natural transformation [69, 70]. They can also facilitate horizontal gene transfer through the uptake of DNA.

The transcriptomic variations of sewage-isolated and reference strains did not have a distinct pattern across experimental conditions, suggesting that the impact of previous growing conditions on the alterations in gene expression profiles might not be as strong as that of the types of disinfectant and bacteria. However, strain variations exhibited and some functional genes of greater concern to humans were upregulated in certain strain. For example, the antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) of tetracycline and vancomycin were upregulated in the sewage-isolated strain of E. faecium, which are two antibiotics heavily used against bacterial infection. The elevated expression of ARGs in VBNC sewage strains is a potential risk to public health as these bacteria can evade detection in sewage monitoring and be discharged to the environment, resulting in the spreading of ARGs and evolution of multidrug-resistant bacteria. On the other hand, it is observed that the number of DEGs was always higher in effluents than in PBS under chlorination, showing that the transcriptomic shifts in VBNC bacteria tended to be amplified in the effluents. It is suspected that effluents might exacerbate the bacterial stress responses and elicit more cellular interactions with the environment, because effluents contained many resources like nutrients, organic substances and anthropogenic substances that could support metabolism and provide building blocks, giving more opportunities for bacteria to adjust their cellular activities to enhance survival and recovery. Also, most of the upregulated prokaryotic defense systems and antimicrobial resistance genes occurred in bacterial cells suspended in effluents, indicating the resilience and threats of the VBNC cells in sewage. The comparisons of sewage-isolated to reference strains and effluents to PBS illustrated the differences in interpreting the metabolic activities and potential influence of VBNC cells using lab-based models and under real-world scenarios, showing the selective gene regulations in different strains and environment.

In this study, the viability of VBNC cells was determined by their membrane integrity and cell elongation capacity (an indication of metabolic activity) after chlorine and UV-C treatment, respectively. The use of transcriptomics also reflected that the cells maintained an active state of gene expression. These features of possessing intact membranes, metabolic activity and gene expression are common characteristics of VBNC cells [71]. However, since different methods were used to determine the viability of chlorine- and UV-C treated cells, the differences observed in the transcriptomes of these two types of cells might be caused by different selection criteria for their physiological condition or metabolic potential, rather than the effects of disinfectant type. To facilitate comparison between treatments, keeping a consistent viability assessment method is recommended. Given that the cell elongation capability assessed by DVC method may require stable membrane structures, this method might not be suitable for chlorine-treated cells as chlorine can cause sublethal damage to membranes. Other methods for assessing the metabolic capability of VBNC cells can be conducted, such as CTC reduction, ATP measurement, and esterase activity assays.

Conclusions

This study revealed the tolerance of E. coli and E. faecium under UV-C and chlorine disinfection, suggesting how species-specific traits, environmental matrices, and strain origins affected the inactivation efficacy. The persistence of VBNC populations across a wide range of disinfectant dosages indicated that VBNC cell formation can be readily induced during disinfection processes. This implies that simply increasing the disinfectant dosage may not be an effective strategy for eliminating them in real-world applications. Preventing VBNC formation requires a deeper understanding of the mechanisms driving induction and metabolic survival. In this study, the transcriptomic analyses highlighted the specific gene regulations in bacteria treated by different disinfectants. These alterations in gene expression profiles give insights into the survival mechanisms of VBNC bacteria, suggesting some possible directions for developing targeted and effective control strategies. For instance, countermeasures could include reducing the availability of major energy sources such as carbohydrates or weakening their defense against disinfectants through disrupting the synthesis of EPS. In addition, the VBNC bacteria might exhibit upregulated expression of genes associated with pathogenicity and defense systems. It posed ecological and public health risks as they might disrupt the stability of microbiomes and accelerate the development of multi-drug resistant strains. Further investigation into the long-term ecological impact and resuscitation triggers of VBNC cells in discharged effluents is essential to safeguard public health.
