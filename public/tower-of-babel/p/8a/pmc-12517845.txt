Association between eight anthropometric indices and chronic low back pain: A cross-sectional study from the NHANES 2009–2010
Authors: Ruliu Xiong, Chengji Liu, Zhixiong Zhang, Xing Chen, Jiwei Xiang, Xingmao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Emerging evidence establishes a robust association between obesity and chronic low back pain (CLBP) development. This cross-sectional study systematically evaluated 8 body composition metrics and their associations with CLBP risk using data from 4751 participants (mean age: 43.03 ± 0.33 years; 50.61% female) in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. Following National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey protocols and established formulas, we computed 8 anthropometric indices: body mass index, waist circumference, waist-to-height ratio, body roundness index, weight-adjusted waist index, conicity index (CI), relative fat mass, and a body shape index. Multivariate regression analysis and restricted cubic splines were used to analyze the associations between these 8 anthropometric indices and CLBP prevalence. Stratified analyses evaluated subgroup variations, while receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was employed to quantify the predictive validity of these anthropometric indices. Analytical findings revealed significant associations between CLBP and all adiposity metrics except a body shape index. Specifically, in the model adjusted for all covariates, per 1 z-score increase in waist circumference, WtHR, body mass index, weight-adjusted waist index, body roundness index, relative fat mass, CI was a significantly correlated with elevated CLBP risks of 27% (odds ratio [OR]: 1.27, 95% CI: 1.15–1.41; P < .001), 26% (OR: 1.26, 1.14–1.40; P < .001), 25% (OR: 1.25, 1.13–1.37; P < .001), 19% (OR: 1.19, 1.06–1.34; P = .004), 25% (OR: 1.25, 1.14–1.38; P < .001), 44% (OR: 1.44, 1.21–1.71; P < .001), and 22% (OR: 1.22,1.09–1.36; P < .001), respectively. Restricted cubic splines analyses established linear dose-response relationships between these indices and CLBP risk (P-value for nonlinearity ≥ .253 for all). Among all adiposity metrics evaluated, the CI yielded the highest area under the curve value (0.5976, 95% CI: 0.573–0.622) in receiver operating characteristic analysis, indicating enhanced discriminatory capacity. Subgroup and interaction analyses validated the consistency of these findings. This study revealed that various anthropometric measures are significantly associated with the risk of CLBP development. Therefore, integrating these anthropometric factors into early preventive strategies may significantly reduce the incidence of CLBP and improve public health outcomes.

1. Introduction

Chronic low back pain (CLBP) is a common and challenging health disorder,[1] characterized by persistent pain and discomfort in the lower back lasting for 12 weeks or longer.[2] As the most prevalent form of chronic pain,[3] CLBP has emerged as a leading cause of functional impairment and disability globally.[4] Notably, low back pain (LBP) ranks as the 5th most common reason for physician visits in the United States, with prior research indicating that 80% of people will experience it at some point in their lives.[5] By 2020, the global prevalence of LBP had surpassed 500 million cases, with projections indicating this figure could exceed 800 million affected individuals worldwide by mid-century.[6] Furthermore, LBP demonstrates significant recurrence tendencies, as research indicates nearly 70% of affected individuals develop renewed episodes during the 1st year after the initial manifestation.[7] The recurrence of LBP frequently leads to persistent disabling pain, severely impairing patients’ quality of life and contributing to work absenteeism and reduced productivity, thereby imposing a substantial economic burden on society. LBP thus constitutes a pressing worldwide health priority.[8] LBP stems from a complex web of risk factors such as obesity, prolonged sitting, physical labor, and poor posture.[9,10] It is worth noting that physical activity may also have a preventive effect on the occurrence of LBP. Strengthened trunk muscle strength can reduce sudden forces on the intervertebral discs, which would otherwise potentially damage the annulus fibrosus.[11] When no identifiable source (such as fractures, cancer, infections, or ankylosing spondylitis) is detected, it is labeled nonspecific LBP.[12] As the burden of CLBP intensifies and research progresses steadily, deeper exploration of its risk factors is required to advance clinical insights for potential therapeutic applications.

Obesity has emerged as an escalating global pandemic, imposing a substantial burden on human health systems worldwide. Despite concerted international public health initiatives, no nation has successfully curbed the upward trajectory of overweight and obesity prevalence rates among adult populations to date.[13] As a well-established risk factor, obesity has been extensively studied and validated in LBP contexts.[14–16] The 2021 Global Burden of Disease study analysis revealed that a substantial proportion of years lived with disability attributable to LBP stemmed from 3 modifiable lifestyle-related risk factors: occupational ergonomic stressors, tobacco use, and elevated body mass index (BMI). Notably, elevated BMI demonstrating a rapidly escalating impact on LBP-associated years lived with disability.[17] Although BMI remains the standard obesity measure, its limitations in assessing body fat distribution and metabolic risks have prompted researchers to develop new evaluation methods combining visceral fat measurement and body composition analysis.[18–20] Waist circumference (WC) is a simple yet clinically valuable anthropometric indicator. It is recommended by authoritative institutions such as the National Institutes of Health, World Health Organization, American Heart Association, and International Diabetes Federation as a key indicator for assessing central obesity, used to screen for the risk of metabolic diseases and cardiovascular diseases.[21] Substantial evidence has revealed that elevated WC is independently associated with an increased risk of developing multiple comorbidities, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM), hyperlipidemia, and LBP.[22] However, this assessment has certain limitations: on the one hand, the cutoff values for determining whether WC is excessive vary depending on gender and ethnicity; on the other hand, there is controversy regarding the optimal anatomical location for measuring WC.[23] Besides, akin to the widely utilized waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), this anthropometric parameter fails to accurately delineate both subcutaneous adipose tissue and visceral adipose tissue deposition patterns. Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, while capable of accurately distinguishing muscle and fat content, is difficult to adopt as a simple and cost-effective tool for large-scale population screening or routine health monitoring due to its reliance on high-cost equipment, stringent testing conditions (e.g., subject positioning and operator expertise), and unavoidable exposure to ionizing radiation.[24] To delineate compartment-specific fat distribution, emerging anthropometric indices have been developed over recent decades for adiposity assessment, with particular emphasis on central adiposity. A novel anthropometric parameter, the body roundness index (BRI), has been validated to predict both body fat percentage and % visceral adipose tissue.[20] Emerging evidence highlights its significant association with risks of multiple chronic conditions, positioning BRI as a promising alternative to BMI for comprehensive adiposity assessment.[25,26] Similarly, another emerging class of anthropometric indices, the weight-adjusted waist index (WWI) and a body shape index (ABSI), has been validated to correlate with risks of multiple diseases.[27,28]

While these novel adiposity metrics have proven valuable for cardiovascular and metabolic risk assessment, their role in CLBP remains poorly defined. Existing studies predominantly focus on conventional measures like BMI, overlooking critical body fat distribution patterns. To address this gap, we systematically compared 8 anthropometric indices – BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, WWI, conicity index (CI), relative fat mass (RFM), and ABSI – using the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) data. Through comprehensive predictive performance analysis, this study aims to identify optimal adiposity indicators for CLBP risk stratification in U.S. adults.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and participants

The NHANES is a nationally representative health study utilizing a complex multistage sampling design to collect health status, nutritional data, and risk factor information from the noninstitutionalized U.S. population. Approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board, this survey obtains informed consent from all participants prior to data collection. Therefore, no additional ethical approval was required for the secondary analysis conducted in this study. This investigation utilized open-access NHANES datasets available via the CDC website: www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes.

2.2. Exclusion criteria

The study population was derived from a single survey cycle of NHANES 2009–2010, as only this cycle could identify diagnoses that met the criteria for CLBP. Initially, 10,537 participants were enrolled. Exclusion criteria comprised: individuals aged < 20 years (n = 4319), missing anthropometric measurements (weight, height, or WC; n = 41), and unavailable CLBP diagnostic records (n = 944). After applying exclusion criteria, the final analytical cohort consisted of 4751 eligible participants. Figure 1 delineates the participant selection flowchart.

2.3. Definition of CLBP

CLBP identification was operationalized through self-reported data from the Arthritis Questionnaire (ARQ_F) module of the NHANES. Firstly, ARQ010 is primarily used for screening chronic back pain. To ensure data reliability, only participants who provided complete responses to this question were retained for inclusion in the analysis. The diagnosis of CLBP is based on 2 additional questions in the questionnaire: “Was there one time when you had pain, aching, or stiffness in your low back on almost every day for 3 or more months in a row?” (ARQ024D) and “Do you still have low back pain, aching, or stiffness?” (ARQ022AD). Dual affirmative responses were mandatory for CLBP case definition.

2.4. Anthropometric index calculation

Anthropometric measurement serves as a fundamental tool for assessing nutritional status, general health, and potential health risks in both pediatric and adult populations. To ensure high-quality data acquisition, NHANES personnel undergo rigorous training in standardized examination protocols, including scheduled equipment calibration and precise measurement documentation. 2.5. Covariates

Covariate selection was informed by prior research and established epidemiological evidence, incorporating demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and health conditions.[29–31] Age was analyzed as a continuous variable and subsequently stratified into 3 subgroups for analysis: ≤40 years, 41 to 60 years, and > 60 years. Sex was categorized as male or female. Race/ethnicity included 5 groups: Mexican American, Other Hispanic, Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, and Other Race-Including Multi-Racial. Educational attainment was stratified into 3 tiers: less than high school, high school/equivalent, and college or above. Marital status was dichotomized into “Married or living with partner” and “Living alone,” reflecting cohabitation status. The poverty-income ratio (PIR) was categorized as < 1.3, 1.3 to 3.5, or ≥ 3.5. Smoking status classified participants as never (<100 cigarettes), former (≥100 cigarettes with cessation), or current smokers (≥100 cigarettes with continued use). Alcohol intake was defined as ≥ 4 drinks/day (yes/no). Physical activity levels were determined by metabolic equivalent of task-minutes/week thresholds: <500 (low physical activity) or ≥ 500 (high physical activity). Hypertension was defined by self-reported medical history or an average systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mm Hg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mm Hg. DM was defined by self-reported diagnosis, hypoglycemic medication use, or meeting anyone biochemical thresholds: hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5% or fasting blood glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL or postprandial 2-hour blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL.

2.6. Statistical analysis

The statistical procedures strictly complied with NHANES analytical protocols, incorporating NHANES-recommended survey weights to appropriately address the complex multistage clustered sampling framework. First, we conducted an intergroup comparison of the baseline characteristics between participants in the CLBP group and those in the non-CLBP group. Continuous variables were expressed as means ± standard errors, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies with proportions (%). Between-group comparisons utilized t-test for continuous measures and chi-square test for categorical variables. In this study, 8 anthropometric variables were z-score transformed as follows: z score = (index − index_mean)/index_sd.

In this study, 3 multivariate logistic regression models were used to explore the correlations between 8 anthropometric indices and CLBP. Model 1 examined unadjusted associations; Model 2 adjusted for demographic covariates (age, sex, and race/ethnicity); Model 3 further incorporated socioeconomic factors (education level, marital status, and PIR), behavioral variables (smoking status, alcohol intake, and physical activity), and clinical comorbidities (DM and hypertension). Moreover, nonlinear relationships were assessed through restricted cubic splines (RCS) with 4 knots in fully adjusted models. Subsequently, stratified multivariate regression was employed to evaluate the subgroup analyses stratified by gender, age, ethnicity, educational level, marital status, and PIR. And the log-likelihood ratio test model was used to assess the subgroup heterogeneity. Finally, the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the predictive ability of the 8 anthropometric indices for CLBP, and the area under the curve (AUC) values were compared. All computations were conducted using R statistical software (version 4.4.1 ; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with survey package implementation. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed statistical significance threshold of P < .05.

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study population, comparing participants with CLBP and non-CLPB groups. Among 4751 participants, 4182 (88.02%) were categorized as non-CLPB and 569 (11.98%) as CLBP. Significant differences were observed across multiple demographic and health-related indicators. The CLBP group demonstrated a higher mean age, though sex distribution did not differ significantly (P = .608). Racial composition analysis revealed a significantly higher proportion of non-Hispanic Whites in the CLBP group. Educational attainment varied markedly, with the CLBP group exhibiting a higher prevalence of less than high school education and a lower proportion of college-educated individuals. Health behavior analyses indicated a significantly higher rate of current smoking and low physical activity in the CLBP group, while alcohol intake showed no significant association (P = .109). Chronic disease burden was notably elevated in the CLBP group, with higher prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes. Anthropometric measures, including weight, WC, BMI, WHtR, WWI, BRI, RFM, and CI were higher in the CLBP group (all P < .05). Although a statistically significant difference in the ABSI was observed (P = .008), the absolute mean difference between groups was minimal. Height did not differ significantly (P = .668). These findings suggest that CLBP is associated with advanced age, higher cardiometabolic risk, obesity-related indices, lower educational attainment, and adverse health behaviors.

3.2. The association between the anthropometric indices and CLBP

Table 2 summarizes the multivariate logistic regression analysis evaluating associations between 8 anthropometric indices and CLBP. In the unadjusted Model 1, all indices exhibited significant positive associations with CLBP (all the P-value < .001) and CI had the highest odds ratio (OR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.29–1.55) across all anthropometric indicators. After adjusting for sex, age, and race (Model 2), all the indices retained statistical significance (all the P-value < .001), though effect sizes slightly attenuated. Notably, RFM had the highest OR in Model 2 (OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.35–1.87). In the fully adjusted Model 3 (accounting for sex, age, race, marital status, education, alcohol intake, smoking status, hypertension, and diabetes), RFM maintained the highest OR among all indices (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.21–1.71, P < .001). Additionally, WC, WHtR, BMI, BRI, and CI remained robustly associated with CLBP (all the P-value < .001). Conversely, ABSI lost significance (OR = 1.06, 95% CI: 0.95–1.19, P = .277), indicating its association may be confounded by covariates. WWI showed reduced but persistent significance (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.06–1.34, P = .004). Furthermore, after adjusting for all covariates in Model 3, RCS analysis revealed no evidence of nonlinear relationships between the 8 anthropometric indices and CLBP (P-value for nonlinearity ≥ .253 for all). Specifically, WC, WHtR, BMI, BRI, WWI, RFM, and CI demonstrated significant linear associations with CLBP (all the P-value for overall association ≤ .034). In contrast, ABSI showed no significant association with CLBP (P-value for overall association = .601; Fig. 2).

3.3. Subgroup and interaction analyses

The subgroup analyses further elucidated the consistency and heterogeneity in the associations between 8 anthropometric indices and CLBP risk. As shown in Figure 3 the results demonstrated consistent positive associations of all 8 anthropometric measures with CLBP prevalence across all subgroups. Additionally, interaction analyses revealed a sex-specific interaction was observed for the ABSI, which exhibited a notably stronger association with CLBP in male participants (per z-score increase: OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.29–1.71; P-interaction = .007). Similarly, RFM demonstrated subgroup heterogeneity, showing enhanced associations with CLBP prevalence among individuals living alone (per z-score increase: OR = 1.40, 95% CI: 1.22–1.59; P-interaction = .049) and those with a PIR < 1.3 (per z-score increase: OR = 1.42, 95% CI: 1.24–1.64; P-interaction = .032), with both interactions reaching statistical significance.

3.4. ROC analysis

The diagnostic performance of 8 anthropometric indices discriminating individuals with CLBP was quantitatively assessed through ROC curve analysis with AUC (Fig. 4). As shown in Table 3, the AUC for CI, WC, WHtR, BRI, WWI, BMI, ABSI, and RFM were 0.5976, 0.5944, 0.5877, 0.5877, 0.5855, 0.5725, 0.5679, and 0.5659, respectively. These results indicate that CI and WC demonstrate enhanced predictive accuracy, with marginally superior AUC values, while WWI exhibited the highest sensitivity (0.710) for reducing false negatives and RFM achieved the highest specificity (0.793) for excluding non-CLBP cases. Although no index reached an excellent predictive threshold (AUC > 0.70), the balanced performance of CI and WC supports their utility as primary screening tools for CLBP risk assessment in clinical settings.

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the NHANES 2009–2010 to conduct the 1st systematic evaluation of associations between 8 anthropometric indices (BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, WWI, CI, RFM, and ABSI) and CLBP prevalence. Our analysis revealed robust positive associations between increases in body measurement parameters and CLBP risk. In fully adjusted models accounting for demographic, lifestyle, and clinical covariates, 7 anthropometric measures (except for ABSI) maintained statistically significant relationships with CLBP risk and RCS analyses demonstrated linear dose-response relationships with significant associations. ROC curve analysis identified CI as the most discriminative indicator for CLBP prevalence, demonstrating the highest diagnostic accuracy among all evaluated parameters. Notably, WC demonstrated comparable discriminatory capacity while offering distinct practical advantages due to its simpler measurement protocol and greater clinical accessibility.

Long-standing epidemiological observations have hypothesized a potential association between overweight/obesity and increased risk of LBP and CLBP, though the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated.[15,32,33] The high risk of CLBP associated with obesity is often attributed to mechanical stress loading. Spine surgeons predominantly focus on pathological conditions associated with intervertebral discs, with substantial clinical evidence establishing lumbar disc degeneration as the most prevalent etiology of LBP and a leading cause of disability worldwide. Notably, nerve root compression secondary to lumbar disc herniation not only manifests as severe radicular pain but may also progress to potentially debilitating neurological sequelae, including lower extremity weakness and in severe cases, paralysis. A Norwegian HUNT cohort study tracking follow-up data from 30,327 participants aged 30 to 69 with LBP showed that the risk of developing CLBP increased with higher BMI levels in both women and men.[15] Additionally, the probability of symptom recurrence or persistence in patients with CLBP also rose with increasing BMI. Another study involving 300 LBP patients from Pakistan, with a mean age of 51.24 ± 18.12 years, indicated that approximately 75% of the patients were overweight or obese, and this association was stronger in women than in men.[33] A systematic review of 56 studies involving populations from different countries and regions across various age groups also found a positive correlation between obesity and the occurrence and severity of LBP.[32] Interestingly, an MRI-based analysis of 1634 participants with LBP (mean age 51 years) revealed that obesity increased the likelihood of disc herniation and spinal stenosis.[34] Additionally, a meta-analysis examining the association between overweight or obesity and LBP in participants aged 6 to 18 years suggested that excess weight may represent a risk factor for LBP in children and adolescents.[35] These studies primarily used BMI as the indicator of obesity. Our research findings support these perspectives and provide large-sample data validation for these mechanistic associations, demonstrating that elevated BMI exacerbates the risk profile for CLBP. Compared to these studies, our cohort, based on the NHANES database, examined the relationship between obesity and CLBP in a large and diverse sample of U.S. adults, was relatively young adults (mean age 43.03 years). This evidence-based association suggests that in the clinical prevention and management of CLBP, weight management should be incorporated into comprehensive intervention strategies, with proactive control of this modifiable risk factor.

However, BMI fails to accurately assess body fat distribution, particularly metabolically active visceral fat, is a significant shortcoming in epidemiological research.[36] Consequently, there is a pressing need to adopt more precise adiposity metrics that can more effectively evaluate the risk of developing CLBP. Recently, an increasing number of studies have started to focus on the relationship between other obesity indices and LBP.[30,37–42] A systematic review and meta-analysis investigating adiposity-LBP associations demonstrated that elevated WC, waist-to-hip ratio, body fat rate, or total body fat mass consistently correlate with increased LBP risk, irrespective of normal-range BMI status.[21] Furthermore, several novel indices have also been increasingly applied to investigate the association between obesity and the risk of developing related disorders. The BRI demonstrates superior capability in distinguishing muscle mass from adipose tissue, exhibiting heightened sensitivity to alterations in body shape and fat distribution.[20] Recent studies have extensively validated its clinical utility, with evidence suggesting that BRI may serve as a more reliable predictor of cardiovascular outcomes and mortality in patients with metabolic syndrome compared to BMI.[43] However, no prior research has systematically investigated the association between BRI and CLBP. Our study fills this critical knowledge gap by demonstrating a significant positive association between BRI and the risk of CLBP development. The CI, proposed as a metric of body fat distribution, increases proportionally with abdominal adiposity accumulation. While existing literature confirms a robust association between CI and gallstones risk, its relationship with CLBP remains unexplored.[44] Our findings reveal a stable and statistically significant positive correlation between CI and CLBP risk, with CI demonstrating superior predictive performance (AUC = 0.5976) relative to conventional anthropometric measures.

Although prior studies have suggested positive associations between the WWI/ABSI and LBP, their specific relationships with CLBP remain unclear.[30,42] Intriguingly, our fully adjusted models revealed that ABSI lost statistical significance in predicting CLBP risk. Originally developed to refine WC measurements by capturing its additive predictive value beyond BMI, ABSI initially demonstrated unique advantages in all-cause mortality prediction.[45] However, unlike other novel indices, ABSI fails to differentiate fat mass from lean mass and exhibits limited efficacy in chronic disease prognostication. This aligns with previous observations where ABSI-gallstones correlations diminished after covariate adjustment.[46] Our results corroborate this phenomenon, though the underlying mechanisms warrant further etiological exploration. RFM, as another novel obesity indicator, also exhibits unique advantages. It was 1st proposed by Orison in 2018. Currently, existing studies have demonstrated that RFM is associated with the risks of female infertility, gallstones, psoriasis, and asthma.[47–50] Currently, no study has denoted associations between RFM and CLBP. Our study revealed a significant linear relationship between RFM and CLBP risk, addressing this gap in the literature. Specifically, after adjusting relevant covariates, compared with other indices, the z-value derived from the transformation of RFM shows a more prominent OR in relation to the incidence risk of CLBP. This association is likely mediated by inflammation, as the occurrence of LBP is always accompanied by a series of local inflammatory responses.[51] Previous research has suggested that persistent pain associated with diseases may be caused by changes in the properties of peripheral nerves.[52] Tissue injury is usually accompanied by the activation of nociceptors or nonneuronal cells, along with the accumulation of released endogenous factors, including neurotransmitters, prostaglandins, leukotrienes, cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), IL-6, and tumor necrosis factor α.[53] These factors are collectively referred to as the “inflammatory soup.” Nociceptors, by recognizing these components, enhance their sensitivity to temperature or touch, which constitutes one of the causes of persistent pain. Some studies have found that inflammatory markers partially mediate the relationship between RFM and the risks of certain diseases, such as psoriasis and asthma.[47,50] Inflammation may plausibly serve as a partial mediator in the relationship between RFM and CLBP, though this hypothesis necessitates further empirical validation.

5. Strengths and limitations of this study

This study offers significant advantages and unique contributions. Firstly, by being the 1st to investigate the associations between 8 obesity indices and CLBP in the American population, it reveals the widespread correlations between different obesity indices and CLBP. Secondly, the study employs diverse analytical frameworks through population stratification studies and multivariate regression models, accounts for covariates such as demographic and clinical factors, and identifies robust correlations between body composition metrics and CLBP, thus strengthening the validity of the study conclusions. Thirdly, the large sample size and national representativeness of this study enhance the reliability and generalizability of its results. Fourthly, we compare the predictive efficacy of these 8 indices and identify the CI as a relatively prominent obesity index for recognizing CLBP. This study reveals the potential risks of different types of obesity in the onset of CLBP, indicates the potential clinical significance of obesity, a modifiable risk factor, for the prevention of CLBP, and calls on medical professionals (such as clinicians) to attach importance to individualized body weight management for people with different types of obesity, thereby reducing the incidence risk of various diseases.

However, it should be acknowledged that this study has several potential limitations and considerations. First, the cross-sectional study design limited our ability to establish causal relationships between obesity and the incidence of CLBP. Future studies should adopt longitudinal designs to better understand the directionality of this association. Second, the use of self-reported measures for diagnosing CLBP may introduce reporting bias. Subsequent research should incorporate comprehensive clinical diagnoses to mitigate this bias. Third, since all study participants were recruited from the United States, our findings may not be generalizable to other countries, and caution should be exercised when extrapolating the conclusions to other populations. Additionally, although this study adjusted for several covariates to address potential confounding effects, some residual confounding factors – such as dietary habits, medication use, and other unmeasured variables – might still influence the conclusions. Future investigations should aim to account for these residual confounding factors.

6. Conclusion

In this cross-sectional study, anthropometric indices including BMI, WC, WHtR, BRI, WWI, CI, RFM, and ABSI exhibited statistically robust associations with CLBP prevalence. Specifically, in the fully adjusted model, RFM (per 1 z-score increment) had the strongest correlation with CLBP while the CI and WC demonstrated excellent discriminatory ability in detecting the risk of CLBP. This study highlights the potential benefits of weight management in preventing the occurrence of CLBP.
