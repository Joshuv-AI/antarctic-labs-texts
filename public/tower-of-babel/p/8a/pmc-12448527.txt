Teleconsultation: Ophthalmology reaching every nook and corner
Authors: Ankeeta A Thali, Jagadeesh K Reddy, Vandhana Sundaram, Mithun Thulasidas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study evaluates the impact of teleconsultation services in rural communities across India through a retrospective analysis. Teleconsultation units, also referred to as vision centres (VCs), are established in multiple rural regions, each serving a population of 20,000 to 50,000. These units are equipped with a slit lamp and a fundus photography system, and are managed by trained optometrists. Communication with ophthalmologists at base hospitals is facilitated through apps, namely Skype and Remidio. Data collected from 41 such units across 9 states over 18 months, from April 2022 to September 2023, were analysed. A total of 1,91,194 patients were examined during this time. Of these, 30,840 (16.13%) were prescribed and dispensed spectacles, 57,425 (30.03%) received medication and were followed up with at the VCs, and 23,623 (12.35%) underwent free cataract surgery. Additionally, 48,657 patients (25.44%) were referred to the base hospital for further management of more complex conditions. Given India’s low doctor-to-patient ratio and the challenges in accessing quality eye care in rural areas, these findings suggest that teleophthalmology is a powerful tool in bridging the healthcare gap. It not only brings accessible and affordable eye care to underserved populations but also helps reduce the burden on tertiary care centres.

Telemedicine refers to the use of technology to deliver healthcare remotely. As a “technologically forward” field, ophthalmology allows for its widespread use in the form of teleconsultation to reach out to the remote population who do not have easy access to eyecare. Although one of the earliest uses of telemedicine was in the late 1950s and early 1960s in the field of psychiatry,[1] the first project in teleophthalmology was published in 1975.[2] Since then, it has gradually gained popularity and acceptance, with a major impact during the Covid-19 pandemic. Here, we aim to analyze the impact of teleconsultation in India’s rural community.

Methods

Our tertiary eye care hospitals (which function as the base hospitals), located in various states in the country, have teleconsultation units set up in rural communities that we refer to as “vision centers” (VC). Each VC caters to a population of around 20,000 to 50,000 and is equipped with a slit lamp photograph system, a fundus photograph system, Schiotz tonometer, trial sets, retinoscopes, an optometrist, and good internet connectivity for communication. Besides this, they also have a sphygmomanometer, glucometer, a computer with an electronic medical record system, and an optical shop. The optometrists are trained for a year to provide basic eyecare, which includes history taking, examination, refraction, and using software applications. They take a detailed history for every patient coming to the VC, check the intraocular pressure, do their refraction, and take their slit lamp and fundus photographs. The cost of teleconsultation for a patient is ₹75 at most VCs. However, in tribal areas, a subsidized rate of ₹30 per patient is applied to ensure affordability and access.

The setup at the base hospital consists of an exclusive room equipped with two to three computers with good internet connectivity. It is attended by two to three qualified ophthalmologists and two staff nurses at a time. Teleconsultation is then carried out with the help of two applications, Skype and Remedio. The doctor at the base hospital communicates with the patient via video call on Skype and views the photographs sent by the optometrist using Remedio. The timings are from 9 a.m. to 6 p.m., 6 days a week.

A systematic retrospective analysis of the electronic medical records was performed over a period of 18 months, from April 2022 till September 2023, on data obtained from 41 VCs of 10 of our hospital units spread across 9 states in India, as shown in the figure below [Fig. 1]. These data include the number of patients examined, treated, and referred to the base hospital. In addition, the demographic profile of these patients, the conditions being treated, and the number of patients who underwent surgical intervention were also analyzed. The categorization of visual impairment is based on the World Health Organization’s World Report on Vision (2019).[3] Being a descriptive study, the analyzed data have been represented in terms of percentage (%).

Results

A total of 1,91,194 outpatients visited the 41 VCs during the 18-month period from April 2022 till September 2023, with an average of 10,366 per month. Among these, the highest number of patients (85,626; 44.78%) were seen at the VCs in Tamil Nadu, which has the largest network of VCs, comprising 17 centers in total.

Out of the total number of outpatients that visited the VCs, 81,002 (42.36%) were male and 1,10,192 (57.63%) were female. The majority of patients (24.50%/46,843) were found to belong to the older age group (>60 years), while the least (23,325,12.2%) belonged to the 0–15 years age group [Table 1]. A total of 48,169 patients (25.19%) had severe visual impairment (<6/60 to 3/60) [Fig. 2].[3]

The three most common diagnoses included lens-related pathologies (33.61%), refractive errors (16.13%), and ocular surface pathologies (12.07%). Among the lens-related pathologies, cataract was found to be the most common diagnosis, while among the ocular surface pathologies, it was allergic conjunctivitis [Fig. 3]. A total of 30,840 (16.13%) patients had a refractive error and were prescribed spectacles, while 57,425 (30.03%) were prescribed medications for conditions such as allergic conjunctivitis, hordeolum externum, blepharitis, pingueculitis, and episcleritis and advised follow-up at the VC. A total of 17,798 (9.3%) patients had corneal pathologies such as foreign bodies, infectious keratitis, opacities, dystrophies, and corneal tear; 14,743 (7.71%) were diagnosed with posterior segment diseases like diabetic retinopathy (DR), macular degeneration, and retinal detachment; 9,732 (5.09%) were found to be glaucoma suspects; 5,277 (2.76%) patients had occuloplasty related conditions such as ptosis, chronic dacryocystitis, and lagophthalmos; and 1,107 (0.57%) had strabismus, all requiring advanced medical and surgical management and hence referred to the base hospital for further treatment. Among the 11,743 (6.14%) patients (23,486 eyes) diagnosed with varying severity of DR and referred to base hospitals, 8,996 eyes underwent retinal laser, 5,504 eyes were given intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) injections, and 4,838 eyes underwent pars plana vitrectomy surgery, while the remaining 4,148 eyes (mild–moderate NPDR) were advised observation and strict glycemic control. A total of 37,713 (19.72%) patients were advised cataract surgery, and 25,670 (36,235 eyes) came to the base hospital for the same. Among these, 23,623 (12.35%) were below poverty line patients and underwent the surgery free of cost, while 2,047 (1.07%) opted for cataract surgery under the paying section. Routine post-operative follow-up evaluations at 1 month and beyond were conducted via teleconsultation; the initial post-operative day 1 assessment was completed at the base hospital.

Discussion

A study by Bourne et al.[4] revealed that with a population of 1380 million, India had 270 million people living with vision loss, and 9.2 million were blind, with an all vision loss rate of 19.3%, making India one of the countries with the highest prevalence of vision impairment. In developed countries, the average ophthalmologist-to-population ratio is around 39 per million, while in India it is only about 15 per million. Within our country, urban areas have one ophthalmologist per 10,000 people, while rural areas have only one for 250,000.[5] Thus, teleconsultation can play a role in reaching out to people who do not have easy access to eyecare and help in bridging this gap.

In our study, more female patients (57.63%) were found to visit the VCs as compared to male patients (42.36%), which corresponds with findings from a study conducted by Sabherwal et al.[6] Inability to travel long distances to visit hospitals owing to lack of transport facilities, as well as catering to social responsibilities, probably make VCs a more favorable option for them. It was seen that among the people who visited the VCs, there was a peak in the older age group of >60 years (25.50%) and in those with severe visual impairment (<6/60 to 3/60, 25.19%).[3] Misra et al.[7] also reported similar findings. This could be due to the increase in longevity of the population and difficulty in travelling to far-off hospitals faced by ailing patients and those with severe visual impairment.

Misra et al.[7] found that 45% of the patients in their study had pathologies related to the ocular surface, cornea, and lid and adnexa-related conditions, followed by lens-related conditions (38.3%); however, our study showed that most patients had lens-related pathologies (33.61%). Out of these, 37,713 (19.75%) were found to have cataract and were advised surgery. A total of 23,623 patients were below the poverty line and underwent the surgery free of cost. Post-operative care of these patients via teleconsultation reduced the need for travelling long distance to far-off hospitals and thus ensured better compliance among the patients. A total of 57,425 patients were prescribed medications via teleconsultation and, on follow-up, were found to have resolution of symptoms, thus showing that teleconsultation can help reduce the burden on tertiary healthcare centers. However, as advised by Jayadev et al.,[8] it is advisable to avoid prescribing topical steroids via teleconsultation alone unless absolutely required, owing to their potential side effects and unmonitored usage.

Besides anterior segment conditions, teleophthalmology plays a very important role in the early diagnosis of posterior segment pathologies. Although these conditions may not be treatable via online consultation, their timely referral and treatment at higher centers prevents further disease progression.[9] A total of 11,743 patients (6.14%) in our study were found to have varying severities of DR and were treated accordingly. The Aravind Comprehensive Eye Survey Research Group Study in 2004 showed that in South India, the prevalence of DR was 10.5%.[10] Das and Pappuru[11] have shown that teleophthalmology is beneficial in providing DR care in rural areas. Other posterior segment conditions that can be diagnosed by teleophthalmology include hypertensive retinopathy, retinal vein occlusions, retinal detachments, and age-related macular degeneration. Hadziahmetovic et al.[12] found that remote diagnostic imaging (fundus photography and optical coherence tomography (OCT)) using an Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved noncontact retinal imaging device (iFusion; Optovue) versus standard examination by a retinal specialist were equivalent in detecting referable macular degeneration.

Another emerging cause of irreversible blindness is glaucoma. In our study, 9,732 patients (5.09%) were found to be glaucoma suspects (either found to have raised intraocular pressure, shallow anterior chamber, or glaucomatous disc changes) and were referred to the base hospital for further evaluation and treatment. Kiage et al.[13] compared cup-to-disc ratio by teleophthalmology with that of in-person examinations by glaucoma specialists and found a positive predictive value of 77.5% and negative predictive value of 82.2%. Kassam et al.[14] also found teleophthalmology to be a useful tool to evaluate a patient’s optic disc in remote areas of the world.

The cost-effectiveness of setting up teleconsultation units is a major concern. However, even though the initial cost of establishment of such centers may be excessive, eventually, as a greater number of patients are screened, there is significant future healthcare cost saving.[15] Gomez-Ulla et al.[16] found that teleophthalmology is more economical due to less expenditure for travel by the patient and lower lost income. Even though our study did not conduct any “cost-effectiveness” analysis, we assume that there was saving of both time and money in the form of lower duration and cost of travel and lesser (or no) daily wage loss for these patients. Poor network connectivity in rural areas is another limiting factor. Above all, diagnosis of certain conditions via teleophthalmology alone may be difficult, particularly macular diseases such as macular edema, as mentioned in similar studies.[1718]

The role of teleconsultation in India during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic has been well documented. Despite infrastructural challenges, it proved to be a cost-effectiveness means of bridging the rural–urban healthcare divide.[19] Integration with electronic medical records enabled timely remote care and continuity of follow-up, even during lockdowns.[20] These experiences highlighted the feasibility of managing follow-up visits through teleophthalmology.[20] A study from a tertiary government medical university found teleconsultation to be effective, with WhatsApp as the preferred medium, and nearly 60% of patients managed without physical examination.[21] Such findings underscore the relevance of teleconsultation not only during the pandemic but also in shaping future healthcare delivery models, particularly in underserved regions.

While 72% of India’s 1.2 billion people live in rural areas, over 70% of the doctors practice in urban areas.[22] This is a major cause for limited access to healthcare in rural India. Teleophthalmology expands healthcare by aiding ophthalmologists in screening, diagnosing, treating, and monitoring various conditions, while also identifying cases that need referral to and treatment at higher centers. This, in turn, helps relieve some pressure off the overburdened healthcare system while improving the quality of service. Our study and several others have found teleophthalmology to be beneficial to both patients as well as ophthalmologists, with most studies showing high satisfaction and acceptance levels.[23] The added advantage is that public awareness regarding eye diseases also increases, thereby improving the health-seeking behavior of the community.[24] In addition, postoperative care and follow-up can be done at the VCs. The strengths of the study include its substantial sample size and extended duration. While much of the literature focuses on the use of teleconsultation primarily during the COVID-19 pandemic, this highlights the continued significance and applicability of teleconsultation in the post-pandemic era.

Conclusion

Teleophthalmology has been in practice for many years, but it was the lockdown during the Covid-19 pandemic—during which it became a major source of access to healthcare—that made us realize its importance. However, even now, during the post-Covid-19 pandemic era, it continues to be a popular and beneficial mode of healthcare delivery among ophthalmologists and patients, proving that it is here to stay. It serves as a vital link between a beneficiary and an eyecare institution by creating an eyecare delivery channel that provides preventive, curative, and promotive eyecare services in a sustainable way closer to the community’s home.
