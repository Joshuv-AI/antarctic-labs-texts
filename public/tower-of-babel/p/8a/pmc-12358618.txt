Regulatory role of LncRNA FMR1-AS1 in the pathogenesis of alzheimer’s disease based on bioinformatics and in vitro experimental validation

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a major cause of dementia, characterized by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-amyloid (A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) plaque accumulation and Tau protein hyperphosphorylation. Although long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have been implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, their roles in AD remain unclear. This study analyzed RNA sequencing data from the brain tissues of 17 AD patients and 19 healthy controls (GEO: GSE138260) to construct a gene co-expression network and identified eight lncRNAs strongly associated with AD. Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is an irreversible neurodegenerative disorder and the leading cause of dementia, accounting for 50–80% of all cases1,2. Its clinical manifestations primarily include progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and behavioral abnormalities, which severely impair the health and quality of life of the elderly population. The hallmark pathological features of AD involve \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-amyloid (A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) plaque accumulation, abnormal Tau protein hyperphosphorylation, and neuroinflammation3–5. Recent projections indicate that by 2050, the prevalence of dementia in Europe will double, while the global number of cases is expected to triple. If the projection is based on biological rather than clinical criteria, this number may increase by as much as threefold6. Although AD has been extensively studied for over a century, its complex pathophysiological and molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood. Multiple pathological processes, including vascular dysfunction, mitochondrial impairment, oxidative stress, reduced cerebral glucose metabolism, and neuroinflammation, have been implicated in the onset and progression of AD7. However, no effective treatments are currently available based on these hypotheses. Therefore, there is an urgent need to further elucidate the molecular underpinnings of AD and to identify novel therapeutic targets that can offer new strategies for prevention and intervention.

In recent years, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), a representative class of non-coding RNA molecules, have attracted considerable attention as key regulators in the molecular networks underlying neurodegenerative diseases8–10. lncRNAs are a class of RNA transcripts typically ranging from 200 nucleotides to several kilobases in length, characterized by the absence of significant open reading frames (ORFs) and strong tissue specificity11. They are widely distributed across animals, plants, yeasts, and even viruses. Emerging evidence suggests that lncRNAs are involved in a variety of essential biological processes, including X-chromosome inactivation, genomic imprinting, chromatin remodeling, transcriptional activation and repression, and epigenetic regulation12. As critical regulators of transcription, lncRNAs have been increasingly implicated in the onset and progression of AD. A growing number of studies in humans13, animal models14, and various cell lines15 have revealed the functional significance of lncRNAs in the pathogenesis of AD. Through mechanisms such as epigenetic modification, transcriptional regulation, and post-transcriptional control, lncRNAs influence the expression of AD-related genes and are involved in key processes including neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and neuroinflammation16,17. However, systematic identification and experimental validation of AD-associated lncRNAs remain lacking. There is an urgent need to integrate high-throughput data analysis with experimental approaches to advance our understanding of lncRNAs in AD.

With the growing understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying AD, increasing attention has been directed toward gene–gene interactions and their regulatory networks in the onset and progression of the disease. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) provides a systematic approach that constructs co-expression modules based on gene expression profiles and correlates them with clinical phenotypes (such as disease status), thereby identifying modules significantly associated with the phenotype18. Further network analysis enables the identification of key genes within modules, known as hub genes, which may serve as potential therapeutic targets or biomarkers. In addition, WGCNA can evaluate the centrality and functional relevance of genes within the network using module membership values. Compared with traditional methods, WGCNA does not only detect gene expression changes but also reveals coordinated gene interactions and underlying regulatory mechanisms, offering critical insights into the systems biology of complex diseases. In recent years, several studies have applied WGCNA to transcriptomic data from AD patients to identify disease-related molecular modules and candidate biomarkers for diagnostic and therapeutic development19–21.

In this study, we applied WGCNA to a publicly available microarray dataset to construct a gene co-expression network. By calculating the correlation coefficients between modules and clinical phenotypes, we identified modules significantly associated with AD and performed Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses22. The identified module contained hub genes, including CDRT8, DSCR9, FAM138A, FLJ13224, FMR1-AS1, KU-MEL-3, TTTY16, and UBE2DNL. It is worth noting that FMR1-AS1 has not previously been reported to be directly associated with AD. Existing studies suggest that this lncRNA may serve as a biomarker for the early detection and treatment of Fragile X Syndrome (FXS) and Fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome (FXTAS)23, and that FMR1-AS1 regulates the dynamic balance of tumor stem-like cells in female esophageal cancer through the TLR7/NF\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}B/c-Myc signaling axis24. Based on these results, we further investigated the function of FMR1-AS1 in AD and its potential regulatory mechanisms through experimental approaches.

Materials and methods

This study was designed to investigate the regulatory mechanisms of lncRNAs in AD through a three-phase research framework consisting of data collection, bioinformatics analysis, and experimental validation. Based on transcriptomic data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GEO accession number: GSE13826025), we first performed data preprocessing and constructed a weighted gene co-expression network using WGCNA. Key modules were identified and subjected to GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to determine modules significantly associated with the AD phenotype. From the identified modules, we further screened core genes correlated with AD and, through literature review, selected lncRNA FMR1-AS1 as the primary candidate for subsequent investigation. Functional experiments were then conducted in an A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}1-42-induced neuronal injury model, in which FMR1-AS1 was either overexpressed or silenced. A comprehensive evaluation of its regulatory effects on neuronal viability, apoptosis, Tau protein phosphorylation, and oxidative stress was performed using Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT-qPCR), Western blotting, MTT assay, TUNEL staining, flow cytometry, and measurements of ROS, SOD, and MDA levels. Data collection

Data source

In this study, we obtained dataset GSE138260 from the GEO database25. This dataset contains brain tissue microarray data from 17 patients with AD and 19 healthy controls dying without any history of neurological or psychiatric disorder.

Data preprocessing

Gene expression and annotation data were extracted from the GSE138260 dataset and the GPL27556 platform, respectively. Probes with missing values or lacking gene symbol annotations were removed. For genes with multiple probes, the average expression values were calculated to obtain a unique value per gene, resulting in a matrix of 21,851 genes. All expression values were normalized using Z-score transformation to reduce measurement bias and improve comparability across samples.

To assess the presence of potential batch effects, we examined sample metadata, including tissue source, labeling method, platform ID, extraction and scanning protocols. All samples originated from the same brain tissue type and shared identical experimental conditions, indicating uniform sample processing. In addition, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize sample distribution in expression space. The results showed no apparent clustering by technical or non-biological factors (see Supplementary Fig. S1), supporting the absence of significant batch effects. Thus, no batch correction was applied in downstream analyses.

Bioinformatics analysis

Construction of co-expression network and module identification

After preprocessing, a weighted gene co-expression network was constructed using the R package WGCNA18. Pearson correlation coefficients were first calculated to assess pairwise gene similarity, and a soft-thresholding power of 4 was selected based on the scale-free topology criterion to transform the similarity matrix into a weighted adjacency matrix.

The adjacency matrix was then converted into a topological overlap matrix (TOM) to measure network connectivity among genes. Gene modules were identified using the dynamic tree-cutting algorithm implemented in the cutreeDynamic function from the R package DynamicTreeCut26, with a minimum module size of 100 genes and a deepSplit parameter set to 2. Subsequently, similar modules were merged based on eigengene dissimilarity using a merging threshold (cutHeight) of 0.25, which corresponds to a module eigengene correlation threshold of 0.75.

For each module, the module eigengene (ME) was calculated to represent the principal expression pattern. The dissimilarity between modules (MEDiss) was computed based on ME correlations, and modules with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MEDiss < 0.25$$\end{document} were merged to reduce redundancy and enhance the stability and biological interpretability of the network.

Module–trait relationship analysis and functional enrichment of significant modules (GO/KEGG)

After constructing co-expression modules, Pearson correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values were calculated between module eigengenes (MEs) and clinical traits to identify significantly associated modules (we set cutoff \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05$$\end{document}). These modules were then subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses using the R package clusterProfiler (version 4.17.0). The enrichGO function was applied with its recommended default parameters to explore the potential biological functions associated with each module. For KEGG analysis, the enrichKEGG function was used with pvalueCutoff = 0.1 and qvalueCutoff = 0.2. Only terms meeting both criteria were considered significantly enriched.

Modules associated with AD were further analyzed by calculating module membership (MM) and gene significance (GS), which quantify the correlation between each gene and its corresponding module eigengene, and the association with the AD phenotype, respectively. Hub genes were defined as those with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$MM > 0.85$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$GS > 0.5$$\end{document}, indicating strong intra-modular connectivity and high relevance to disease traits.

To assess oxidative stress levels, intracellular ROS were measured using the fluorescent probe DCFH-DA (D6470, Solarbio, China). Statistical analysis

All cellular experimental data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 7.0. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate differences among multiple groups, followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc multiple comparisons test. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as follows: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.05\ (^*)$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.01\ (^{**})$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p < 0.001\ (^{***})$$\end{document}. All experiments were performed with biological triplicates (n = 3) using three independent cell culture batches. Cells were processed and analyzed independently in each replicate to ensure that the results reflect true biological variability. Additionally, to enhance the stability of qRT-PCR data, three technical replicates were set up for each sample. The sample size was determined based on preliminary experimental performance and supported by relevant literature to ensure the reliability of the results28.

Results

Co-expression modules revealed by WGCNA

WGCNA was performed on the preprocessed gene expression data. The pickSoftThreshold function29 was used to evaluate the scale-free topology model fit index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}) and mean connectivity under different soft-thresholding powers to select the optimal power value, ensuring that the resulting network exhibited both scale-free properties and sufficient connectivity. The results are shown in Fig. 2a.

The selection of the soft-thresholding power (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}) was guided by the scale-free topology fit index (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}) and mean connectivity plots. A power of 4 was ultimately chosen as the optimal values, as this was the first point at which \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document} exceeded the threshold of 0.85, indicating that the network structure sufficiently approximated a scale-free topology. Further increases in the power value resulted in only marginal improvements in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^2$$\end{document}, suggesting that higher values were unnecessary. At the same time, the mean connectivity at power 4 was approximately 300, which falls within a reasonable range and helps to avoid excessive network sparsity that may occur at higher powers. Thus, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta = 4$$\end{document} was selected for constructing the weighted gene co-expression network, as it represents an appropriate balance between topological fit and network complexity. Based on the selected soft-thresholding power, an adjacency matrix was constructed and subsequently transformed into a TOM. Subsequently, co-expression modules were identified using the dynamic tree cutting algorithm with the deepSplit parameter set to 2 to allow for more refined module detection. To enhance module stability and biological interpretability, similar modules were merged based on the correlation between MEs. Specifically, the module dissimilarity was defined as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 - \text {correlation}$$\end{document} (Pearson), and a hierarchical clustering dendrogram of MEs was constructed. A merging threshold of cutHeight = 0.25 (equivalent to ME correlation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$> 0.75$$\end{document}) was applied to combine highly similar modules. The dynamic tree cut and module merging process are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S2.

Pearson correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values between MEs and clinical traits were calculated to identify significantly correlated modules, which were visualized as a heatmap (Fig. 2b). Among them, the MElight yellow, MEgrey60, and MEblue modules showed strong correlations with both AD status and age, suggesting their potential involvement in the pathogenesis of AD. Functional enrichment analysis of significant modules

We performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses on the three co-expression modules identified by WGCNA using the clusterProfiler package. The results showed that the MEgrey60 module did not have significantly enriched terms (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document}), while the MElight yellow module contained a few significant items, but with limited relevance to known AD pathological processes. In contrast, the MEblue module was significantly enriched (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document}) across all three GO categories—biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF)—as well as KEGG pathways, involving functions closely associated with neurodegeneration, such as cytoskeletal remodeling, olfactory perception, and hormone signaling (Fig. 2c).

Specifically, the enriched BP terms included detection of chemical stimulus involved in sensory perception and sensory perception of smell. The CC terms were primarily associated with the intermediate filament cytoskeleton, keratin filaments, and cornified envelope. In terms of MF, significant enrichment was observed in hormone activity and structural constituent of skin epidermis. These findings suggest that the MEblue module may be involved in the pathogenesis of AD by regulating cytoskeletal dynamics, neural sensory functions, and endocrine signaling pathways. Furthermore, KEGG analysis revealed that the MEblue module was significantly enriched in pathways related to olfactory transduction, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, metabolic processes, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction. These pathways are involved in neural signal transduction, metabolic regulation, immune responses, and structural integrity, suggesting that the MEblue module may contribute to AD development through multiple biological mechanisms. Therefore, the MEblue module was selected as the central focus for subsequent investigations.

Discussion

This study, through the integration of multi-omics data and functional validation, reveals for the first time the neuroprotective role of the lncRNA FMR1-AS1 in AD. The MEblue module, identified via WGCNA based on the GSE138260 dataset, was enriched in neurodegeneration-related pathways. FMR1-AS1, a hub gene within this module, was significantly downregulated in AD brain tissues and cell models, and exhibited neuroprotective effects by suppressing Tau phosphorylation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.01$$\end{document}), alleviating oxidative stress (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.05$$\end{document}), and reducing neuronal apoptosis (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P < 0.01$$\end{document}). The following sections discuss the functional characterization of the MEblue module and the biological significance of FMR1-AS1 based on cellular experiments.

Functional characteristics of the MEblue module and its role in the pathological network of alzheimer’s disease

Using the GSE138260 dataset, a co-expression network was constructed via WGCNA, identifying the MEblue module as highly correlated with AD. This module was significantly enriched in neurodegeneration-associated pathways, including “cytoskeletal remodeling,” “olfactory perception,” and the “autophagy–lysosomal system.” GO enrichment analysis indicated two major functional clusters: one associated with cell differentiation and morphogenesis, such as epidermis development and keratinocyte differentiation, which may influence neuron–glia interactions through extracellular matrix remodeling. For example, the gene NOTCH1 regulates mitochondrial dynamics to modulate oxidative stress in neurons, and aberrant Tau phosphorylation observed in fibroblasts from AD patients suggests that peripheral tissues might reflect central nervous system (CNS) pathology through evolutionarily conserved mechanisms36,37.

The second cluster involved neuronal sensory pathways, particularly olfactory perception, aligning with clinical observations of olfactory dysfunction in early-stage AD38,39. Disruption of olfactory receptor expression and signaling may be implicated in this phenotype. Glucocorticoid receptor signaling abnormalities are known to exacerbate A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} deposition40–43, and the specific expression of the olfactory receptor OR2K2 in human choroid plexus has been proposed as a potential biomarker for sporadic AD44, which resonates with the MEblue module enrichment results.

From a cytoskeletal perspective, the module is enriched in genes encoding intermediate filaments and keratin proteins, which contribute to neuronal morphological stability and axonal transport efficiency, thereby potentially participating in the propagation of Tau pathology. Abnormal Tau phosphorylation causes microtubule destabilization, a molecular hallmark of neurofibrillary tangle formation45,46. Thus, the MEblue module offers a multidimensional landscape of AD-related pathology and provides a theoretical framework for identifying key regulatory elements.

Selection criteria and neural expression profile of FMR1-AS1

Among the eight differentially expressed genes identified in the MEblue module, FMR1-AS1 was selected as the focus of this study based on multifaceted scientific rationale. From a molecular perspective, unlike other protein-coding genes within the same module—such as CDRT8 and DSCR9—FMR1-AS1 is a lncRNA located at the FMR1 gene locus on the X chromosome. It is highly expressed in the brain and kidneys and is capable of regulating FMR1 gene expression through complementary binding to FMR1 mRNA47,48. This antisense transcriptional feature endows it with unique gene regulatory potential. The FMR1 gene encodes the FMRP protein, a key regulator of local mRNA translation and synaptic development in neurons, whose dysfunction leads to FXS49,50. This well-established neurodevelopmental linkage provides a logical foundation for investigating FMR1-AS1 in the context of AD.

In terms of expression patterns, our study demonstrated significant downregulation of FMR1-AS1 in both AD patient brain tissues and A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}1–42-treated neuronal models. This consistent expression trend aligns closely with interaction predictions from the RNAInter and TRRUST databases, which suggest that FMR1-AS1 is functionally connected to synapse-associated AD pathway genes51. In contrast, the other seven differentially expressed genes in the module, while showing altered expression in AD, lack direct relevance to neurodegeneration. For instance, DSCR9 is located on chromosome 21 and is highly expressed in the placenta, but exhibits minimal expression in brain tissue and shows no clear involvement in key AD pathways52. TTTY16 is a Y-linked testis-specific gene unrelated to neurodegeneration53. UBE2DNL, a pseudogene involved in ubiquitination without catalytic activity, is also testis-expressed with no apparent AD relevance54. FAM138A, CDRT8, FLJ13224, and KU-MEL-3 have not been studied in the context of the nervous system or AD in any public database, and their functional annotations remain unclear.

The expression specificity and functional association of FMR1-AS1 in AD pathology distinguish it from other candidate genes in the module. It is noteworthy that most current studies focus on lncRNAs such as BACE1-AS and NEAT1, which are involved in A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} metabolism or neuroinflammation. In contrast, FMR1-AS1 appears to participate in AD pathology through regulation of synaptic activity and energy metabolism, presenting a mechanistically distinct profile51. Its tissue-specific expression and stress-responsive nature suggest that the downregulation of FMR1-AS1 in AD may be an active regulatory response to A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-induced stress. This dynamic expression pattern warrants further validation in in vivo animal models.

Multidimensional regulatory mechanisms and therapeutic potential of FMR1-AS1 in AD

FMR1-AS1 participates in AD pathology through multiple regulatory mechanisms, forming a stark contrast to the functional patterns of other lncRNAs reported in the literature. In the regulation of neuronal apoptosis, this study demonstrates that overexpression of FMR1-AS1 significantly reduces A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}1–42-induced neuronal apoptosis, consistent with previous findings that downregulation of FMR1 enhances apoptosis rates55. The underlying mechanism may involve modulation of TLR7-mediated signaling. In cancer models, FMR1-AS1 has been shown to activate downstream TLR7–NF-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\kappa$$\end{document}B signaling via direct interaction with TLR724. In neurons, however, this interaction may exert a protective role by attenuating TLR7 activation and suppressing the expression of pro-apoptotic factors56. Moreover, FMR1-AS1 helps maintain FMRP expression, indirectly regulating the translation of anti-apoptotic genes associated with the local synaptic mTOR pathway. This echoes the known role of FMRP in modulating axonal local mRNA translation in autism spectrum disorders, suggesting a cross-disease regulatory mechanism57.

Regarding Tau phosphorylation, our study confirms that FMR1-AS1 overexpression reduces the p-Tau/Tau ratio under A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} exposure. While existing studies have shown that lncRNA MAPT-AS1 regulates Tau expression via methylation of the MAPT gene promoter58, FMR1-AS1 provides an additional layer of regulation at the post-transcriptional level. Together, these mechanisms highlight the multilayered regulatory network of lncRNAs in Tau pathology.

In the context of oxidative stress, FMR1-AS1 overexpression markedly reduces intracellular ROS levels, increases SOD activity, and inhibits MDA production. This aligns functionally with reports that A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} impairs mitochondrial respiratory chain complex activity59, suggesting that FMR1-AS1 may participate in antioxidative responses by influencing mitochondrial dynamics or energy metabolism—an aspect not yet systematically described for other AD-related lncRNAs.

From a therapeutic perspective, FMR1-AS1 exhibits unique advantages over currently studied AD-associated lncRNAs. Most prior studies have focused on lncRNAs that exacerbate disease pathology, such as BACE1-AS, which stabilizes BACE1 mRNA and promotes A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} production60, and NEAT1, which enhances neuroinflammation via inflammasome activation61. In contrast, FMR1-AS1 simultaneously modulates key pathological features of AD, including Tau phosphorylation, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis. This multi-target regulatory capacity positions FMR1-AS1 as a promising therapeutic candidate.

Based on the present findings that FMR1-AS1 overexpression mitigates A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document}-induced neurodegeneration in cellular models, future translational studies may pursue the following avenues: (1) validation of the cognitive benefits of FMR1-AS1 overexpression in AD mouse models such as APP/PS1; (2) development of FMR1-AS1 mimetics or adeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene delivery systems for central nervous system–targeted interventions; and (3) optimization of FMR1-AS1 binding affinity to FMRP using RNA structural prediction tools to enhance its neuroprotective function. Collectively, these approaches may pave the way for multidimensional therapeutic strategies targeting protective lncRNAs in AD, potentially overcoming the limitations of current single-target treatments.

Conclusion

In summary, FMR1-AS1, as an emerging player in the molecular mechanisms of AD, exerts neuroprotective effects during AD progression by alleviating oxidative stress, inhibiting apoptosis, and regulating Tau protein phosphorylation. Although most current evidence is derived from correlative analyses and indirect inferences from other disease models, FMR1-AS1 provides a novel perspective for understanding the complex regulatory network of AD. Future studies employing animal models and clinical investigations to validate the specific role of FMR1-AS1 in AD pathology and to identify its upstream and downstream interacting molecules will be critical for evaluating its potential as a therapeutic target, paving the way for new strategies in AD intervention.
