Effects of low-level laser therapy on the aging process of human dermal fibroblast cells
Authors: Elham Mardanian Dehkordi, Mohammad Amin Fereidounian, Shahriar BourBour, Afshan Shirkavand, Qaem Yahoo, Shirin Farivar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSkin aging results from intrinsic factors and extrinsic stressors, particularly ultraviolet (UV) radiation, leading to structural and functional deterioration. Low-Level Laser Therapy (LLLT) has emerged as a potential non-invasive intervention. To evaluate the effectiveness of LLLT in mitigating UV-induced aging in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs). HDFs were exposed to UV-A (365 nm) and red light (650 nm), individually or sequentially. Cell viability and proliferation were assessed via (3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-Diphenyltetrazolium Bromide (MTT) assay and flow cytometry. Gene expression of collagen synthesis (Col1A1), apoptosis (Bax, Bcl2, Casp8), and DNA methylation (Dnmt3b) were analyzed by qPCR. UV irradiation reduced cell viability temporarily at 24 h, and subsequent treatment with red light after UV-A (UL group) resulted in partial recovery. The percentage of S-phase cells was higher in the UL group at 72 h (P < 0.05), suggesting an increased proliferative activity in this group. Expression of Col1A1 and Dnmt3b increased (P < 0.05, P < 0.01), while apoptotic markers were altered (P < 0.05, P < 0.01), suggesting enhanced collagen production, epigenetic regulation and reduced apoptosis. LLLT is a promising non-invasive approach to counteract UV-induced skin aging and support dermal regeneration. Further studies are required to optimize treatment protocols and validate clinical applications in dermatology and regenerative medicine.Keywords: Skin aging, Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), Photobiomodulation, UV-induced aging, Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs)

Introduction

Skin aging is an inevitable biological process that results in progressive structural and functional alterations of the skin. Clinically, it manifests as decreased elasticity, dryness, fine lines, wrinkles, and pigmentary changes. Skin aging can be broadly categorized into two forms: intrinsic (chronological) aging, which reflects genetically determined physiological changes, and extrinsic (premature) aging, which is primarily caused by environmental factors such as ultraviolet (UV) radiation, poor nutrition, smoking, psychological stress, and pollution [1].

Both intrinsic and extrinsic factors contribute to the degradation of dermal structure through reduced collagen and elastin synthesis, alterations in extracellular matrix organization, and diminished regenerative potential of stem cells. Among these, UV radiation is considered the predominant extrinsic factor responsible for photoaging, mainly through DNA damage, oxidative stress, and inflammation [2, 3]. One of the key biological mechanisms involved in photoaging is programmed cell death (apoptosis). Although multiple signaling pathways regulate this process, many remain incompletely elucidated. Dysregulation of apoptosis-related proteins—such as Bax, Bcl-xL, PARP, and caspases—initiates cellular degradation cascades [4]. Modulation of apoptotic genes including p53, Casp3, Casp8, Bax, and Bcl-2 has been shown to reduce UV-induced apoptosis and enhance cellular resistance to photoaging. Additionally, UV irradiation can activate MAPK and chemokine signaling pathways, promoting oxidative stress and necrotic cell death [5].

Epigenetic regulation, particularly DNA methylation, is another important mechanism implicated in the aging process. DNA methylation involves the addition of a methyl group to cytosine residues at CpG sites, influencing chromatin organization, gene transcription, and cellular renewal [6]. Methylation of promoter CpG islands is typically associated with transcriptional silencing, whereas hypomethylation is observed in actively transcribed genes. During aging, mammalian cells exhibit global DNA hypomethylation, particularly within repetitive elements, accompanied by localized hypermethylation at gene promoters [7, 8]. These alterations contribute to genomic instability, impaired gene regulation, and tissue degeneration. Previous studies have also identified age-associated CpG methylation signatures in white blood cells, linking global hypomethylation with frailty and loss of epigenetic control [9]. Unlike genetic mutations, DNA methylation patterns are reversible and modifiable by environmental and therapeutic interventions [10], suggesting potential avenues for the modulation of aging-related epigenetic changes.

Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), a form of photobiomodulation (PBM), has gained increasing attention as a non-invasive intervention for photoaged skin. PBM utilizes low-energy lasers or light-emitting diodes (LEDs) within the 400–1100 nm wavelength range to stimulate cellular activity and promote tissue repair [11]. LLLT exerts its effects through the activation of transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX), leading to enhanced mitochondrial activity and ATP production [12–14]. Furthermore, regulation of the intracellular redox state under LLLT promotes the expression of genes associated with tissue regeneration and cellular repair [15].

In this study, we investigated the effects of LLLT on the viability and proliferative potential of human dermal fibroblast (HDF) cells subjected to UV-A–induced photoaging. We also examined whether LLLT could reverse cellular aging by analyzing the expression of genes related to collagen synthesis (Col1A1), apoptosis (Bax, Bcl-2, and Casp8), and DNA methylation (Dnmt3b).

Materials and methods

Cell culture and experimental design

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.SBMU.REC.1400.00). Human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were obtained from the Stem Cell Bank of the Royan Institute (Tehran, Iran). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12; Bio-Idea, Iran) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Bio-Idea, Iran), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Pen/Strep; Bio-Idea, Iran), and 1% GlutaMAX 200 mM (Gibco, USA). Cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every two days, and cells were subcultured upon reaching 80–90% confluency.

Optical stimulation

HDFs (5 × 10⁴ cells per well) were seeded into 24-well plates containing basal culture medium and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment with 95% air and 5% CO₂. Optical stimulation was performed using an LED array (365–650 nm, continuous mode, 1 Hz) with an output power of 1 mW. The LED array illuminated the full surface of each well in the 24-well plate (inner diameter 15.6 mm; illuminated area 1.91 cm²). Based on the stated output power of 1 mW, the irradiance at the cell surface was 0.52 mW/cm² for both the 365 nm UV-A and the 650 nm red-light exposures. With a 60-second exposure duration, the energy fluence (dose) delivered per session was 0.031 J/cm² for each wavelength. To prevent cross-illumination between wells, cells were seeded in separate plates and divided into two main groups: treated (light-exposed) and control (dark).

The treated group was further divided into three subgroups:

UV-A group: exposure to a 365 nm LED array for 1 min.

UV-A group: exposure to a 365 nm LED array for 1 min.

Red light (LLLT) group: exposure to a 650 nm LED array for 1 min.

Red light (LLLT) group: exposure to a 650 nm LED array for 1 min.

Combined group: sequential exposure to 365 nm (1 min) followed immediately by 650 nm (1 min).

Combined group: sequential exposure to 365 nm (1 min) followed immediately by 650 nm (1 min).

During irradiation, the culture medium was replaced with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; DNABiotech, Iran) to prevent light interference from the medium. Following stimulation, PBS was replaced with fresh basal medium, and cells were incubated under standard conditions. The control group was kept in complete darkness throughout the stimulation procedure under identical environmental conditions (Fig. 1).

Two-dimensional cell surface area analysis

Cell surface area was quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA). A spatial calibration curve was generated using a reference scale bar, and the surface area (µm²) of individual cells was calculated from pixel dimensions. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually outlined around single cells to ensure accurate measurement.

Cell viability and proliferation assays

Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay. Briefly, HDFs were seeded in 24-well plates and evaluated 24 and 72 h after optical stimulation. The medium was replaced with 450 µL of DMEM/F12 and 50 µL of MTT stock solution (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), and cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Formazan crystals were solubilized with 500 µL dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Bio-Idea, Iran), and absorbance was measured at 570 and 630 nm using a microplate reader (PowerWave XS2; BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA).

For cell cycle analysis, cells were trypsinized, fixed in 70% ice-cold ethanol, and treated with RNase A (25 µg/mL) for 30 min at 37 °C to remove RNA. Samples were then stained with propidium iodide (PI; 50 µg/mL) for 16 h at room temperature and analyzed using a FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, UK). Data were acquired with CellQuest software (BD Pharmingen, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo v10.10.0 to determine the percentage of cells in G₀/G₁, S, and G₂/M phases.

Gene expression analysis by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

The expression of Col1A1, Bax, Bcl2, Casp8, and Dnmt3b was analyzed at 24 and 72 h after optical stimulation. Total RNA was extracted using Kiazol reagent (Kiazist, Iran), and cDNA was synthesized using the Easy cDNA Synthesis Kit (Parstous, Iran). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed with RealQ Plus 2× Master Mix Green Without ROX™ (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a Mic qPCR Cycler (BioMolecular Systems, Australia). Gene expression levels were normalized to GAPDH as an internal control, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad Software, USA). One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used to assess differences among treatment groups. Two-way ANOVA was applied to analyze the interaction between time and treatment on cell viability. Results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Results

Morphology of cells before and after optical stimulation

All experimental groups were evaluated 24 h before, and 24 and 72 h after optical stimulation. No morphological alterations were observed in either the control or treated groups before or after irradiation (Fig. 2A–F). Quantitative analysis of the mean two-dimensional surface area of cells (Fig. 2A–F) showed no significant differences in cell size among the groups at any of the evaluated time points (Fig. 2G).

The viability of HDFs in all four groups was assessed by MTT assay 24 and 72 h after optical stimulation. Flow cytometric analysis was used to evaluate cell cycle distribution after optical stimulation. Effect of optical stimulation on the expression of genes involved in collagen synthesis, apoptosis, and DNA methylation

The expression of genes related to collagen synthesis (Col1A1), apoptosis (Bax, Bcl2, and Casp8), and DNA methylation (Dnmt3b) was analyzed at 24 and 72 h after optical stimulation using real-time qPCR.

Col1A1

At 24 h after stimulation, no significant change was observed in Col1A1 expression across groups. However, at 72 h, Col1A1 expression was significantly downregulated in cells treated with low-level red light alone (L) (P < 0.05), whereas a sustained upregulation was detected in the group treated with low-level red light following UV irradiation (UL) (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5A).

Bax

Bax expression significantly decreased at 24 h in both the low-level red light (L) (P < 0.05) and UV + low-level red light (UL) (P < 0.05) groups. These changes were no longer significant at 72 h post-stimulation (Fig. 5B).

Bcl2

At 24 h post-stimulation, Bcl2 expression did not differ significantly among groups. However, at 72 h, a significant decrease was observed in the UV-irradiated (U) and UV + low-level red light (UL) groups (P < 0.05), while no significant changes were noted in the remaining groups (Fig. 5C).

Casp8

At 24 h, Casp8 expression was significantly upregulated in the UV-irradiated (U) (P < 0.01) and UV + low-level red light (UL) (P < 0.05) groups. At 72 h, Casp8 remained elevated in both the UV (P < 0.05) and UL (P < 0.01) groups compared with controls, while no significant changes were detected in other groups (Fig. 5D).

Dnmt3b

At 24 h post-stimulation, Dnmt3b expression was significantly increased in both the low-level red light (L) and UV + low-level red light (UL) groups (P < 0.01). By 72 h, Dnmt3b expression in treated groups showed a decreasing trend compared to the 24-hour levels, with significance at P < 0.05 and P < 0.01, respectively (Fig. 5E).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the effects of optical stimulation on reversing cellular aging processes in human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs). The skin is a complex organ composed of multiple interacting cell types that maintain its structural, protective, and aesthetic functions. Among these, keratinocytes, fibroblasts, and melanocytes play key roles in epidermal defense, dermal matrix synthesis, and photoprotection, respectively. Exposure to ultraviolet (UV) radiation remains one of the principal extrinsic factors driving premature skin aging, causing oxidative stress, collagen degradation, and DNA damage, which together result in dermal thinning, loss of elasticity, and pigmentation disorders [16–19].

A variety of therapeutic modalities have been developed to counteract photoaging, ranging from topical agents (retinoids, peptides, antioxidants) to minimally invasive techniques such as chemical peels, microdermabrasion, and injectable treatments. More advanced procedures, including laser and radiofrequency therapies, aim to remodel dermal collagen and improve elasticity, though many are associated with discomfort, downtime, or pigmentary complications [20–21].

Low-level laser therapy (LLLT), particularly using red light (600–650 nm), has emerged as a noninvasive, safe, and effective option for skin rejuvenation. By stimulating mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, LLLT enhances ATP production, promotes fibroblast proliferation, and increases collagen synthesis, ultimately improving dermal texture and elasticity [22–24]. Compared with more aggressive laser systems, LLLT causes minimal inflammation or pigment alteration, making it suitable for all skin types.

In the present study, we examined the effects of low-level red light on HDFs exposed to UV radiation. Morphological analysis revealed no structural alterations in any group, indicating that bio-optical stimulation did not cause cytotoxic or morphological damage. However, MTT assays showed a transient reduction in cell viability 24 h after UV exposure, consistent with UV-induced oxidative injury. Notably, fibroblasts treated with low-level red light following UV irradiation (UL group) demonstrated partial recovery in viability compared with the UV-only group, suggesting a reparative effect of red light.

Our flow cytometry results further supported this observation, showing an increased proportion of cells in the S phase in the UL group 72 h post-stimulation. This finding implies enhanced DNA synthesis and proliferation, corroborating previous reports where red light promoted fibroblast and mesenchymal stem cell proliferation [25–27]. Importantly, the absence of significant cytotoxicity following red light exposure suggests that the applied parameters represent a safe therapeutic window for skin cells.

At the molecular level, Col1A1 expression—a key marker of collagen synthesis—was downregulated in the UV-irradiated group but significantly upregulated in the UL group at 72 h. This pattern aligns with earlier studies showing that LLLT can restore collagen production suppressed by UV-induced matrix metalloproteinase activation through mitochondrial activation and TGF-β signaling [28–32]. The upregulation of Col1A1 after combined UV and red-light treatment indicates that LLLT may counteract photoaging by reactivating fibroblast collagen synthesis.

The expression pattern of Dnmt3b revealed an early increase at 24 h in the L and UL groups, potentially reflecting transient activation of DNA methylation mechanisms during early response to photostimulation. However, its subsequent downregulation at 72 h may suggest stabilization of epigenetic activity and reduced methylation-driven aging, consistent with previous reports linking hypermethylation to fibroblast senescence [33].

Regarding apoptotic markers, Bax expression decreased significantly within the first 24 h in both L and UL groups, while Bcl2 expression declined in UV-exposed groups after 72 h. Notably, this decrease in Bcl2 levels was a similarity between the UV-only (U) and combined (UL) groups, suggesting that the red-light treatment did not recover the anti-apoptotic Bcl2 signal inhibited by UV. This data is inconsistent with the hypothesis that LLLT provides broad anti-apoptotic protection. A more accurate interpretation of the data is that red light partially prevented early pro-apoptotic signalling (as seen by the transient decrease in Bax at 24 h) but did not completely reverse the downstream apoptotic cascade. Therefore, the claim of anti-apoptotic LLLT protection should be qualified: any protective effect appears to occur at an early, upstream level and cannot prevent the subsequent reduction of Bcl2 in UV-exposed cells [23].

Casp8 expression was elevated in both UV and UL groups, reflecting its role in early apoptotic signaling; however, in the context of the UL group, this may represent a regulated response contributing to controlled cell turnover rather than widespread cell death. Together, these gene expression findings suggest that LLLT may mitigate UV-induced apoptosis while promoting cell survival and matrix synthesis through modulation of key molecular cascades [34–35].

A key limitation of the present study is that the UV-A (365 nm, 1 min, 1 mW) exposure protocol is employed as a photoaging model without independent validation using established senescence markers. Canonical markers such as senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, p21𝐪𝐚𝐟𝐬/𝐞𝐢𝐩¹ and p16^INK4a protein levels, orγH2AX foci (a marker of DNA double-strand breaks) were not assessed. Without such validation, it cannot be confirmed that the UV-A protocol reliably induced a senescent or photoaged cellular state rather than a transient stress response. Future studies should include these markers to establish the photoaging model and thereby strengthen the interpretation that subsequent LLLT effects represent genuine reversal of aging-related changes.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrate that bio-photostimulation using low-power red laser light (650 nm) can modulate gene expression in human dermal fibroblasts, promoting cellular rejuvenation through the activation of reparative pathways and attenuation of apoptosis. These results suggest that low-level laser therapy (LLLT) may partially reverse photoaging-associated changes at the molecular level by enhancing collagen synthesis and maintaining fibroblast viability.

Given the limitations of current anti-aging and tissue repair modalities, LLLT represents a promising, noninvasive, and biologically safe approach for promoting skin regeneration. However, as this field remains in its early stages, further in vitro and in vivo studies are required to optimize treatment parameters, elucidate underlying molecular mechanisms, and confirm long-term efficacy and safety before clinical translation.
