Development and validation of the breastfeeding resilience scale (BRS) among employed mothers after returning to work
Authors: Yaqiong Guo, Xiaojing Chen, Caihong Zhang, Tong Li, Rong Zhou, Chen Yang, Sixing Liu, Lan Shang, Yan Chen, Honghua Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe increasing number of employed mothers in the workforce is an inevitable trend in China. Employed mothers may stop breastfeeding due to work-related stressors. However, many mothers overcome these challenges and continue breastfeeding after returning to work. This study aimed to develop and validate a tool to understand employed mothers’ experiences with continuing breastfeeding after returning to work, based on Kumpfer’s Resilience Framework, in China.MethodsThe study comprised three phases. Phase 1 involved item pool generation through a literature review, focus groups, and a Delphi survey, followed by pre-testing with 15 mothers to finalize the breastfeeding resilience scale (BRS) trial version. In Phase 2, the trial BRS was administered in a cross-sectional survey of employed mothers from 18 provincial-level regions of China who were recruited using purposive sampling, and valid responses from 172 participants were included in item analysis and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) to develop the provisional BRS. In Phase 3, the provisional BRS and the Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) were administered in a cross-sectional survey of employed mothers from 23 provincial-level regions of China who had returned to work, using purposive sampling. Valid responses from 200 participants were included to evaluate reliability and validity and develop the formal BRS. Reliability was examined using internal consistency and split-half coefficients. Validity was assessed through content, construct, convergent, discriminant, and criterion-related validity analyses.ResultsThe formal BRS contains 24 items across five dimensions. Cronbach’s α was 0.897 (95% CI: 0.875–0.916) for the overall scale, and Cronbach’s α coefficients for the five dimensions ranged from 0.750 (95% CI: 0.683–0.805) to 0.875 (95% CI: 0.847-0.900). Split-half reliability was 0.787. EFA yielded five factors explaining 63.139% of the variance, with all item loadings exceeding 0.50. Confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) showed that most fit indices met the recommended criteria, with χ²/df = 1.654, RMSEA = 0.057, CFI = 0.923, IFI = 0.924, TLI = 0.912, although GFI = 0.860 and NFI = 0.828 were below the 0.90 cutoff. Average variance extracted (AVE) values ranged from 0.441 to 0.570, with the social dimension having an AVE of 0.441, and composite reliability (CR) values ranged from 0.755 to 0.876. Correlation coefficients among the dimensions were below 0.50, and the square roots of the AVE values for each dimension ranged from 0.663 to 0.755. Correlations between BRS dimensions and the total score on the CD-RISC criterion scale ranged from 0.410 to 0.629, all statistically significant.ConclusionsThis study developed and validated a Breastfeeding Resilience Scale for employed mothers after returning to work. It may provide healthcare workers engaged in breastfeeding promotion with an assessment tool to identify employed mothers’ intrinsic motivation and psychological resilience throughout their breastfeeding journey.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13006-026-00870-5.

Background

Breast milk is the ideal natural food for infants. The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends exclusive breastfeeding in the first six months after birth and continued breastfeeding up to two years of age or beyond [1]. Although breastfeeding brings significant health benefits to both mothers and infants [2–3], the breastfeeding situation in China remains unsatisfactory. The 2019 Survey Report on Factors Influencing Breastfeeding in China indicated that the exclusive breastfeeding rate for infants under six months was only 29.2% [4]. While the Chinese government has promoted breastfeeding by implementing a one-hour daily breastfeeding break, constructing workplace maternal and infant facilities, and adopting flexible working systems [5], the Global Breastfeeding Scorecard 2024 released by the WHO showed that China’s exclusive breastfeeding rate for infants under five months was approximately 36%, still below the 50% recommended target [6]. China’s statutory basic maternity leave is 14 weeks at the national level, which does not fully cover the recommended six-month exclusive breastfeeding period. Coupled with inadequate workplace breastfeeding support—only 19% of workplaces have dedicated lactation rooms and 32.8% of employed mothers cannot enjoy the statutory daily breastfeeding leave—most mothers face a dilemma between returning to work and continuing breastfeeding after maternity leave [4]. Nevertheless, many employed mothers still persist in breastfeeding after resuming work. Exploring their psychological characteristics, inner motivations, and coping strategies may provide practical evidence for improving the overall breastfeeding rate in China.

Our previous qualitative study conducted semi-structured interviews with 13 employed mothers who continued breastfeeding after returning to work [7]. The results indicated that sustained breastfeeding among employed mothers returning to work is a dynamic process driven by the interaction between psychological resilience and environmental stressors, leading to three weaning outcomes: natural, voluntary, and involuntary weaning. Mothers with higher psychological resilience are better able to cope with breastfeeding challenges and more likely to achieve optimal feeding outcomes. Therefore, assessing breastfeeding resilience may help understand breastfeeding maintenance and inform the development of support interventions for this population.

For employed mothers, the lactation period after returning to work involves practical challenges. Their psychosocial perceptions and life experiences differ from those of the general population [7]. Although multiple psychological resilience scales have been developed [8, 9], they vary in applicable populations and scenarios, and none appears to be specifically designed for employed breastfeeding mothers. For instance, the Wagnild Resilience Scale mainly targets college students and patients with chronic diseases, focusing on internal and external resources for maintaining psychological balance [8, 10]. The Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) has a broader scope but is primarily applied to general community residents and patients [9]. These scales may not capture breastfeeding resilience characteristics specific to employed mothers after returning to work. Accordingly, this study aims to develop and validate a concise and applicable assessment tool to assess breastfeeding-related psychological resilience among Chinese employed mothers returning to work.

Theoretical framework

Kumpfer’s Resilience Framework posits that stressors and challenges act as stimuli for resilience, and that internal resiliency characteristics develop under pressure [11]. This framework emphasizes the dynamic interaction between environmental factors and individuals. According to this theoretical framework, environmental stimuli disrupt the existing balance and trigger interactions between the environment and the individual, strengthening resilience characteristics, which then dynamically interact with the environment. The resilience characteristics subsequently interact dynamically with the environment, leading to different outcomes [11, 12]. This study applied Kumpfer’s Resilience Framework to design a tool for understanding the resilience of employed mothers who continue breastfeeding after returning to work, even in the face of challenges.

Methods

Participants and analysis sample

This study was conducted in three phases (Fig. 1). The study participants were employed mothers who had experience of breastfeeding after returning to work. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) having returned to work; (2) engagement in paid socioeconomic activities excluding household duties; (3) having breastfed after returning to work, either currently or within the past two years [7]; (4) provision of informed consent and willingness to participate in the study; (5) ability to understand and speak Chinese. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals who left their jobs again for any reason after returning to work; (2) mothers or infants with a disease or condition that contraindicated continued breastfeeding. Phase 1 consisted of item generation. Phase 2 included 172 participants from 18 provincial-level regions of China for item analysis and development of the BRS. Phase 3 included 200 participants from 23 provincial-level regions of China for BRS validation. This study took place in China between January and December 2022 and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Hainan Medical University (Approval No. HYLL-2022-011) with written informed consent obtained from participants.

Phase 1: Item generation

In the first phase, item generation was guided by Kumpfer’s Resilience Framework and conducted in accordance with the PCC (Population, Concept, Context) framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute Evidence-Based Nursing Collaboration Centre at Fudan University. We defined the target population as employed mothers who had returned to work, the core concept as resilience, and the contextual focus as breastfeeding. A comprehensive search was conducted across seven databases—Web of Science, PubMed, Embase, CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, and CBM—from their inception through December 2021. Following deduplication and rigorous eligibility screening, 46 empirical studies were included in the analysis. Relevant factors were categorized into external contextual influences (family, workplace, and sociocultural environment) and individual characteristics (psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical attributes). Building on the theoretical framework and systematic review methods, we referenced established resilience scales, incorporated a positive psychology perspective, and formulated all items using positively worded statements aligned with Chinese cultural values. This process yielded an initial item pool comprising 76 items distributed across five domains. This number was approximately three to four times the intended final length, to which was intended to facilitate subsequent psychometric refinement [13]. Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater resilience.

From March to April 2022, we established a seven-member panel with expertise in obstetric nursing, nursing education, and management to conduct a preliminary item screening. Through three rounds of expert panel discussions, we evaluated item clarity, comprehensibility, and alignment with the study objectives. Ambiguous items, those deviating from the original construct, or those duplicating content were removed; disputed wording was revised; brief explanatory notes were added where interpretation was difficult. From May to July 2022, a panel of 17 experts with at least 10 years of experience in obstetrics, psychology, or nursing education was invited to participate in three rounds of Delphi consultation. The consultation process employed a hybrid approach, integrating both online and face-to-face meetings. Experts evaluated the importance of each dimension and item using a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very unimportant) to 5 (very important). An authority coefficient (Cr = (Ca + Cs) / 2), Kendall’s W, and the coefficient of variation (CV) were calculated to assess consensus, with statistical significance set at P ≤ 0.05 [14]. Item-retention criteria followed published guidelines: mean importance > 4.0, full-score ratio > 0.2, and CV < 0.30 [14]. Items meeting all three thresholds were retained; those satisfying one or two were discussed by the research team. After integrating expert feedback and panel discussions, the scale was refined into the BRS trial version, a 29-item instrument covering five domains—psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical.

Pilot study

In August 2022, the trial version of the BRS was pretested in Hainan among 15 employed breastfeeding mothers who had returned to work. The pilot study aimed to assess item clarity and comprehensibility and to further refine the scale.

Phase 2: Item analysis of the BRS

From September to October 2022, a cross-sectional online survey was conducted among employed mothers from 18 provincial-level regions of China who had returned to work and had breastfeeding experience. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire and the BRS trial version. Through item analysis and refinement, the provisional BRS was developed. Based on the principle that the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of items [15] and considering a 10% invalid response rate, the minimum required sample size was determined to be 162 participants. We contacted heads of obstetrics departments in hospitals from the sampled regions through referrals from project team members, instructors at internship hospitals, colleagues, and friends. After providing a concise explanation of the research purpose, we obtained permission to join breastfeeding support WeChat groups established by these hospitals. Additionally, we identified online shopping groups targeting mothers through internet platforms. Data were collected via the Questionnaire Star online survey platform. The questionnaire link or QR code was distributed through these two types of WeChat groups. Prior to each distribution, the study purpose, target population, and researcher contact information were introduced, and one-on-one guidance via phone or WeChat was made available. The questionnaire itself featured an introductory statement ensuring anonymity and data privacy, along with section-specific instructions. Throughout data collection, respondent numbers across different breastfeeding stages were monitored to describe the sample distribution (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Item analysis

Item analysis was used to screen items. The items of the provisional BRS were screened by frequency analysis, dispersion tendency analysis, the critical ratio method, the correlation coefficient method, Cronbach’s α coefficient method, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA). The screening criteria were as follows: (a) Frequency analysis: if the selection rate for an item option was ≥ 80%, it indicated that the item had poor discrimination and low sensitivity, and thus warranted revision or deletion [16]; (b) Dispersion tendency analysis: discriminatory power was evaluated based on the standard deviation (SD) of responses to each item. Items with an SD below 0.75 were considered to have poor discrimination and sensitivity and were therefore modified or removed [17]; (c) Critical ratio method: total scale scores were ranked, with the top 27.0% defined as the high-score group and the bottom 27.0% as the low-score group. An independent samples t-test was used to compare the mean score of each item between the two groups. Items with a critical ratio value < 3 and p > 0.05 were deleted [18]; (d) The correlation coefficient method: the item score was significantly correlated with the total score of the scale or the items with a correlation coefficient of 0.4 and above were retained [19]; (e) Cronbach’s α coefficient method: an item was considered to affect the scale’s internal consistency if its deletion resulted in a higher Cronbach’s α coefficient; such items were consequently modified or removed [20]; (f) EFA: Bartlett’s test of sphericity yielded a statistically significant result (p < 0.05), and the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was > 0.6 [21]. Principal component analysis (PCA) was used to extract factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 [21]. The cumulative variance explained by the extracted factors was required to reach 50%–60% [22]. The maximum variance orthogonal rotation method was then applied. An item was subject to revision or deletion if it met any of the following criteria: (1) the item’s factor loading was < 0.5; (2) the item loaded onto two or more factors, with a difference between the factor loadings < 0.1 [21, 22]. The decision on the factors extracted was based on the scree plot and the eigenvalues above 1 [23]. All items were ultimately modified, retained, or deleted based on a combination of theoretical relevance and semantic considerations [24].

Phase 3-validation of tool

From November to December 2022, a cross-sectional online survey was conducted among employed mothers from 23 provincial-level regions of China who had returned to work and had breastfeeding experience. Data were collected using a general information questionnaire, the provisional BRS, and the CD-RISC. The CD-RISC was used as the criterion measure to assess criterion-related validity. It consists of 25 items across three dimensions: optimism, tenacity, and strength. Each item is rated on a 5-point scale, with higher total scores indicating greater psychological resilience. It has been widely used in both general and clinical populations worldwide and has demonstrated criterion-related validity with personality traits and self-esteem [9]. Considering that the CD-RISC measures general psychological resilience, whereas the BRS assesses breastfeeding-specific resilience among employed mothers after returning to work, the two constructs were conceptually related but not equivalent. Therefore, the CD-RISC was selected as the criterion measure in this study. The collected data were used to evaluate reliability and validity and to finalize the formal BRS. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were consistent with those described above. The sample size requirements for CFA were as follows: a total sample size of at least 200 participants [25], and a sample size 5–10 times the number of scale items [26]. The provisional BRS composed 24 items. Considering these requirements and an estimated 10% invalid response rate, the minimum required valid analytic sample size was set at 200 participants. The data collection methods were consistent with those used in the item analysis stage.

Reliability and validity analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 27.0 and AMOS 28.0. General information was described using frequencies and percentages. Cronbach’s α coefficient and split-half reliability were used to assess the reliability of the BRS. For the total scale and each dimension, Cronbach’s α coefficient ≥ 0.7 and Spearman-Brown split-half reliability ≥ 0.7 were considered acceptable [20, 27]. The validity of the scale was assessed in terms of content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and criterion-related validity. Content validity was primarily assessed by calculating the full score ratio and coefficient of variation based on experts’ ratings of the importance of each scale item [28]. Construct validity refers to the extent to which a scale reflects the construct it is intended to measure. It was assessed primarily through EFA for factor extraction, CFA, and correlation analysis [29]. CFA was performed to assess model fit. Model fit was evaluated using several fit indices, including the chi-square/degrees-of-freedom ratio (χ²/df), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Goodness of Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), and Incremental Fit Index (IFI). Values of χ²/df ≤ 3.0, GFI, CFI, TLI, NFI and IFI ≥ 0.90, and RMSEA ≤ 0.08 were considered to indicate acceptable model fit [30, 31]. Convergent validity was evaluated using the average variance extracted (AVE) and composite reliability (CR). It is generally accepted that AVE > 0.50 and CR > 0.70 indicate adequate convergent validity [32]. Discriminant validity was primarily assessed by determining whether the square root of the AVE for a given construct was greater than its correlations with other constructs. If this condition was met, discriminant validity was considered satisfactory [33]. Criterion-related validity was assessed by examining the correlation between the newly developed BRS and the CD-RISC total score. A correlation coefficient greater than 0.30 was considered indicative of acceptable criterion-related validity [34].

Results

Phase 1: Item generation

Guided by Kumpfer’s resilience theory of individual-environment interactions, we developed the initial item pool through a systematic literature review and a review of existing resilience scales. This process yielded an initial item pool comprising 76 items distributed across five domains: psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical. The initial pool was designed to be approximately three to four times the intended final length, which facilitated subsequent psychometric refinement. The item pool was refined through three rounds of panel discussions followed by three rounds of Delphi expert consultations. In terms of content, some items were redundant. For instance, although worded differently, the items “I always want to see one thing through” and “No matter what happens, once I decide to do something, I will definitely do it” both reflect the same underlying construct of perseverance. To ensure item independence, items measuring the same latent construct were either merged or removed. Regarding specificity, all items were developed based on the breastfeeding experiences of employed mothers, thereby explicitly embedding the breastfeeding context. This approach highlights resilience characteristics, such as cognitive appraisal and self-management, that are specific to employed mothers during breastfeeding after returning to work. A total of 17 experts participated in all three rounds of Delphi consultations and provided suggestions. All 17 questionnaires were returned in each round, yielding a 100% response rate. The overall expert authority coefficient was 0.87. Kendall’s coefficient of concordance was statistically significant in all three rounds (p < 0.001), indicating a high level of expert agreement. The item-level coefficient of variation decreased gradually across rounds. In the third round, all items had a coefficient of variation below 0.30. After three rounds of consultation, consensus was achieved regarding the dimensions and items of the scale. Based on expert feedback and panel discussions, items were deleted, added, or revised, and the scale was refined into the BRS trial version.

Pilot study

Based on feedback from 15 participants, the wording of five items was modified. For example, the item “I can maintain a balanced and healthy diet to facilitate breastfeeding” was revised to “I pay attention to my diet during the breastfeeding period to facilitate breastfeeding.” Following these revisions, the finalized BRS trial version was developed, consisting of 29 items across five domains: psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical.

Item analysis

Frequency analysis showed that the selection rate of each response option for each item was below 80%, meeting the criterion for item retention [16]. In the dispersion tendency analysis, the standard deviations of all scale items were greater than 0.75; therefore, all items were retained. Using the critical ratio method [35], the item t-test showed statistically significant differences in item scores between the high- and low-scoring groups (p < 0.05), and the absolute values of the t-statistics were all greater than 3.0. Correlation analysis showed that all items met the retention criteria, with item-total correlation coefficients greater than 0.40 and all p values < 0.05, suggesting acceptable item-total correlations [16]. When item A3 (“I am full of hope for breastfeeding my baby until the age of 2”) was deleted, Cronbach’s α coefficient increased from 0.968 to 0.969. Based on the statistical results and theoretical considerations, item A3 was deleted, and all other items were retained.

Exploratory factor analysis

EFA was performed to evaluate the validity of the instrument structure. KMO and Bartlett’s tests were conducted before the EFA. The KMO was 0.944, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (chi-square = 4323.348, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. We conducted the first EFA on the remaining 28 items after item A3 was deleted, using principal component analysis with varimax orthogonal rotation. Without limiting the number of factors, five common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, which together explained 74.450% of the total variance. After analyzing the items within the five common factors, item C2 (“I understand that breast milk substitutes are harmful to infant growth and development, so I prioritize breastfeeding”) exhibited a factor loading below 0.50. Item C5 (“I am fully aware that breastfeeding strengthens the emotional bond between my baby and me”) showed cross-loadings on two factors; however, the difference between the two loadings exceeded 0.1, suggesting that C5 could be retained—though its domain attribution remains ambiguous. Based on theoretical considerations, C5 was assigned to the cognitive dimension. Ultimately, we retained 27 items.

Five common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted both in the second and the third EFA, together explaining 74.984% and 76.221% of the total variance, respectively. In the second EFA, item C1 (“I can use different channels to obtain knowledge that supports continued breastfeeding after returning to work”) and item D4 (“I will communicate with my supervisor to negotiate flexible working hours to facilitate breastfeeding”) exhibited factor loadings below 0.50. In the third exploratory factor analysis, item D6 (“Employed mothers around me who continue breastfeeding after returning to work give me a sense of motivation”) exhibited a factor loading below 0.50. The scale was reduced to 24 items.

We conducted a fourth EFA on the remaining 24 items. The scree plot showed five factors had eigenvalues greater than 1.0, indicating that five dimensions were captured by the 24 items of the scale (Fig. 2). The first eigenvalue was 13.417, the second was 1.475, the third was 1.317, the fourth was 1.171, and the fifth was 1.001, which together explained 76.590% of the total variance. There were no items with cross-loadings or low factor loadings. The five-factor structure of the BRS was generally consistent with the hypothesized ones. The final 24-item BRS comprised five dimensions: Psychological (5 items), Emotional (5 items), Cognitive (7 items), Social (4 items), and Physical (3 items) (Table 2).

Phase 3: validation of tool

Participants

A total of 216 questionnaires were distributed, and 200 valid responses were obtained, resulting in a valid response rate of 92.59%. The average age of the participants was 31.67 ± 3.45 years. More than 90% of the mothers reported receiving breastfeeding support from family members, supervisors, or colleagues (Table 3).

Reliability

The Cronbach’s α coefficient for the provisional BRS was 0.897 (95% CI: 0.875–0.916), and the coefficients for the five factors were 0.863 (95% CI: 0.830–0.891), 0.867 (95% CI: 0.836–0.894), 0.875 (95% CI: 0.847-0.900), 0.756 (95% CI: 0.696–0.807), and 0.750 (95% CI: 0.683–0.805). The provisional BRS was sequentially divided into two parts, yielding a Spearman-Brown split-half reliability coefficient of 0.787. The Cronbach’s α coefficients of the two parts were 0.847 and 0.827. These results indicated that the formal BRS demonstrated acceptable reliability.

Validity

In this study, we examined content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and criterion-related validity.

Content validity

The scale was developed following established procedures for nursing assessment tool development. Based on the theoretical framework, the initial item pool was constructed through an evidence-based literature review and further refined through group discussions, Delphi expert consultations, and a pilot test. According to the Delphi consultation results, all retained items met the predefined criteria, with an importance score > 4.0, coefficient of variation < 0.30, and full-score ratio > 0.20. These findings indicated a degree of expert consensus and supported the content validity of the BRS.

Construct validity

Construct validity was assessed primarily through EFA for factor extraction, CFA, and correlation analysis [29]. AMOS 28.0 was used to test whether the scale structure corresponded to the EFA results. The five-factor model identified in the factor structure was tested using CFA in the validation sample (N = 200) to determine its validity and appropriateness. KMO and Bartlett’s tests were conducted before the EFA. The KMO was 0.857, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (chi-square = 2223.270, p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis. We conducted EFA on the 24 items of the provisional BRS using principal component analysis with varimax orthogonal rotation. Without limiting the number of factors, five common factors with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, which together explained 63.139% of the total variance. (Supplementary Fig. 1).

As shown in Fig. 3, the χ²/df value was 1.654, indicating acceptable model fit. The RMSEA value was 0.057, which was within the acceptable range (Table 4). The CFI, IFI, and TLI values were all > 0.9. Some indices (GFI = 0.860, NFI = 0.828) did not align with the expected study results, but they were close to the cut-off values, meaning that they were acceptable [30, 31]. The standardized regression coefficients of 24 items ranged from 0.60 to 0.81, above 0.5. In conclusion, the CFA results generally supported the structure of the five-factor model. (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Correlation

The survey results indicated that the mean total score of the scale for this group of participants was 96.34 ± 13.250. The total score of the scale was correlated with each dimension score, with r values ranging from 0.60 to 0.76 (p < 0.05). The correlation coefficients between dimensions ranged from 0.27 to 0.49. This indicated that the subscales were correlated with each other but remained relatively distinct, suggesting internal consistency of the scale (Table 6).

Criterion-related validity

The Cronbach’s α coefficient of the CD-RISC in this study was 0.965. The correlation coefficients between the BRS subscale and total scores and the CD-RISC total score ranged from 0.410 to 0.629 (p < 0.01), indicating acceptable criterion-related validity.

The mean scores for the psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical dimensions were 20.52 ± 3.958, 20.73 ± 3.908, 26.89 ± 5.906, 16.51 ± 2.791, and 11.69 ± 2.489, respectively. Discussion

This study developed and validated the Breastfeeding Resilience Scale (BRS) for employed mothers returning to work. Grounded in Kumpfer’s resilience theory [11], which emphasizes individual-environment interaction, the BRS integrates the practical challenges faced by employed mothers after returning to work, such as balancing work and breastfeeding, thereby supporting its theoretical basis and practical applicability [36]. The results indicate that the BRS shows an acceptable factor structure, reliability, and validity based on conventional psychometric standards. It may be useful for assessing breastfeeding resilience among employed mothers after their return to work. The BRS exhibited a five-factor structure comprising psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical dimensions. The innovation of this study lies in two aspects. To our knowledge, few instruments specifically assess breastfeeding resilience in employed mothers after returning to work; the BRS was developed to address this measurement gap. Unlike general resilience scales that assess traits such as optimism and perseverance, the BRS focuses on the challenges of combining paid work with breastfeeding. These challenges include, for instance, managing pumping during work hours, negotiating workplace support, and dealing with role conflict. In addition, Kumpfer’s resilience framework describes how individuals interact with their environment to overcome adversity [11]. The BRS operationalizes this framework by including items that assess how employed mothers use workplace supports and personal strategies to cope with breastfeeding-related challenges. This enhances the contextual relevance of the BRS [36]. Therefore, future research can formulate intervention measures for breastfeeding resilience based on the BRS.

All items of the BRS were developed based on a literature review and interviews with mothers who returned to work and continued breastfeeding after childbirth. The item pool was designed to reflect the real-life breastfeeding difficulties, dual-role conflicts between maternal and professional roles, and internal psychological experiences of employed mothers after returning to work. Although only Item 16 explicitly links work and breastfeeding, other items also reflect the work-related context of breastfeeding after returning to work. For instance, Items 15 and 17 capture employed mothers’ need to balance work schedules, commuting time, and routine work arrangements with milk expression and breastfeeding needs. Such time management and behavioral adjustment under multiple role demands are important challenges for employed breastfeeding mothers and represent a core feature of this scale. Items 1, 4, 5 and 9 reflect mothers’ inner motivation to sustain breastfeeding amid workplace-specific stressors, including inadequate lactation facilities, limited supervisory support, fatigue from long-term back-to-work milk expression, and social prejudice. Items 6 and 7, concerning emotional regulation and positive coping, may address breastfeeding difficulties and negative emotions arising from work burden, role burnout, and workplace marginalization among returning mothers. In addition, Items 18, 19 and 21 may reflect social support and peer assistance resources. Overall, despite most items not explicitly referring to work scenarios, their implied stressors, life situations, role conflicts, and support systems are consistent with the real-life conditions and psychological experiences of postpartum mothers returning to work. Therefore, the BRS appears to have population specificity and practical applicability for assessing breastfeeding resilience among employed mothers after returning to work.

In terms of reliability, the Cronbach’s α coefficient of the overall scale was 0.897, and the Cronbach’s α coefficients for the five dimensions were all greater than 0.75. The split-half reliability of the total scale was 0.787. These results indicate that the internal consistency and split-half reliability met the recommended psychometric standards [28]. Compared with the CD-RISC [9], which measures general resilience, the BRS targets the specific challenges of returning to work while breastfeeding. Because its items are closely related to the daily experiences of the target population, the BRS may provide a more context-specific assessment of breastfeeding resilience. The results of this study suggest that the BRS reflects the psychological resilience and behavioral characteristics of employed mothers facing breastfeeding challenges after returning to work. In addition, content validity was supported by three rounds of Delphi expert consultations involving experts in psychology, nursing, and sociology. The expert authority coefficient was 0.87 and the content validity index (CVI) met the recommended standards. These values are similar to those reported in previous studies [37]. As Berman et al. [38] pointed out, expert involvement improves the cultural adaptability and relevance of a scale. In this study, expert feedback confirmed that the items align with the real-life experiences of the target group (e.g., “getting breastfeeding advice from other mothers” reflects their social support needs after returning to work).

In terms of validity testing, first, the KMO value for the EFA was 0.857, and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.05). A total of five common factors were extracted, with a cumulative variance contribution rate of 63.139%. Second, CFA indicated that the five-factor model of the scale was generally supported by the fit indices: χ2/df = 1.654 and RMSEA = 0.057 were within the acceptable range, and CFI = 0.923, IFI = 0.924, and TLI = 0.912 met the recommended criteria. However, GFI = 0.860 and NFI = 0.828 were below the 0.90 cutoff [30, 31]. This result is consistent with the validation by Yu Xingli et al. [39] regarding the rationality of a multidimensional resilience structure, suggesting that the psychological, emotional, cognitive, social, and physical dimensions may capture different aspects of the resilience mechanism of the target group. The total score of the scale was correlated with each dimension score, with r values ranging from 0.60 to 0.76 (p < 0.05). The correlation coefficients between dimensions ranged from 0.27 to 0.49, indicating that the subscales were correlated with each other but remained relatively distinct. The BRS demonstrates a reasonable factor structure and dimension correlations, which may support its use as a potentially useful instrument for assessing resilience in the specific context of returning to work while breastfeeding. In future studies, the number of participants should be expanded to evaluate the validity of the BRS.

In this study, the AVE values of the five dimensions ranged from 0.441 to 0.570, and the CR values ranged from 0.755 to 0.876. The social dimension showed an AVE of 0.441, slightly below the 0.50 threshold, whereas its CR was 0.759. Previous research has indicated that when the AVE is slightly below 0.50, convergent validity can still be considered generally acceptable if the CR is greater than 0.70 [32]. The correlation coefficients among the dimensions were lower than the square roots of the corresponding AVE values, suggesting that the scale had acceptable discriminant validity. This finding is consistent with the study by Wu Jingling et al. [40], who also used the AVE method and the correlation coefficient comparison method to evaluate discriminant validity when developing the Mothers’ Breastfeeding Behavior Scale within 6 Weeks Postpartum (MBBC-6 W). In their study, although the correlations among some dimensions (such as self-decision-making behavior, self-coping behavior, and self-control behavior) were relatively high, the independence among the dimensions was confirmed through the chi-square difference test [40, 41]. Similarly, the multidimensional structure of the BRS may help distinguish different aspects of breastfeeding resilience among employed mothers after returning to work.

The survey results indicated that the mean total score of the scale for this group of participants was 96.34 ± 13.250. This finding provides preliminary evidence that the breastfeeding resilience of employed mothers after returning to work may be relatively favorable. Moreover, this result is similar to findings from Daniels et al.’s study, in which employed mothers who continued breastfeeding while working also expressed strong beliefs and inner strength regarding breastfeeding [42]. The BRS was informed by positive psychology and was designed to assess individuals’ psychological beliefs, positive emotions, cognitive strategies, social behaviors, and physical adjustment abilities as demonstrated during the process of breastfeeding after returning to work. Stronger abilities in these areas may help individuals identify and use internal and external protective factors and cope with risk factors. As a result, they are more likely to achieve sustained breastfeeding after returning to work [43, 44]. Furthermore, this scale adopted a positive scoring scheme, which was consistent with the study by Connor and Davidson [9], in which higher scores indicated greater psychological resilience. Therefore, a higher score on this scale indicates a higher level of breastfeeding resilience among employed mothers after they return to work; conversely, a lower score indicates a lower level of resilience.

In this study, the proportions of participants who had ceased breastfeeding were 69.2% (119/172) in Phase 2 (item analysis) and 67.5% (135/200) in Phase 3 (reliability and validity testing). Further analysis revealed that among the 119 employed mothers who had stopped breastfeeding in Phase 2, only 4 (3.4%) stopped when their infants were < 6 months of age, 20 (16.8%) stopped at 6–8 months, 43 (36.1%) stopped at 9–12 months, and 52 (43.7%) stopped at > 12 months. Among the 135 employed mothers who had stopped breastfeeding in Phase 3, 7 (5.2%) stopped at < 6 months, 13 (9.6%) stopped at 6–8 months, 39 (28.9%) stopped at 9–12 months, and 76 (56.3%) stopped at > 12 months. Across both phases combined, 95.7% (243/254) of those who had ceased breastfeeding continued beyond 6 months, 82.7% (210/254) continued beyond 9 months, and 50.4% (128/254) continued beyond 12 months. Research has suggested that resilience, as a multidimensional trait, operates across the lifespan [45]. Therefore, breastfeeding resilience after returning to work may remain meaningful even after breastfeeding has ceased. According to previous studies, resilience is defined as a relatively stable psychological trait and adaptive capacity that individuals demonstrate when facing stress, challenges, or adversity [11, 46]. Unlike transient emotions or attitudes, resilience exhibits consistency across time and situations, representing a relatively enduring psychological resource [36]. Thus, although the employed mothers had stopped breastfeeding by the time they completed the scale, their retrospective evaluations of their psychological beliefs, emotion regulation, cognitive strategies, social support utilization, and physical adjustment during the breastfeeding period after returning to work may still reflect their resilience level during that period. These data indicate that the vast majority of employed mothers who had stopped breastfeeding did not discontinue early; rather, they stopped for objective and reasonable reasons such as infant age and natural weaning after completing breastfeeding durations that met or approached WHO recommendations [1]. Although many participants had already ceased breastfeeding, most had continued breastfeeding beyond six months, suggesting that they had substantial experience of breastfeeding after returning to work. Their responses may therefore provide useful retrospective information about breastfeeding resilience in this context. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that retrospective assessment may introduce recall bias, and future prospective studies should validate the BRS among currently breastfeeding employed mothers.

Limitations

First, the geographical coverage of the sample was limited, with participants predominantly from eastern China, which may reduce the generalizability of the scale to women in central and western China. Future research should include participants with greater geographical diversity. Second, this study adopted a cross-sectional design, which limited its ability to capture dynamic changes in breastfeeding resilience over time. However, we assessed criterion-related validity using the CD-RISC, which showed correlations with the BRS. This finding provides evidence of criterion-related validity, but it does not replace predictive validation, which remains a limitation of the current study. Thus, future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the predictive validity of the BRS. While Cronbach’s alpha remains the conventional measure of internal consistency in nursing scale development studies, McDonald’s omega is now recommended as a more robust alternative, particularly for multidimensional scales. Future research should report McDonald’s omega alongside Cronbach’s alpha to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of the BRS’s reliability.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the Breastfeeding Resilience Scale for employed mothers after returning to work. It may provide healthcare workers engaged in breastfeeding promotion with an assessment tool to identify employed mothers’ intrinsic motivation and psychological resilience throughout their breastfeeding journey. Consequently, the application of this scale may support the design of tailored interventions to improve breastfeeding adaptation and persistence among employed mothers after their return to work, which may have positive public health implications for maternal-infant health and women’s occupational well-being. Furthermore, as the scale was developed within a Chinese cultural context, cross-cultural adaptation and validation will be required before its use in other countries or regions.
