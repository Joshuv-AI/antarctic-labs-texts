Pathophysiological dynamics of acute myocardial infarction rats under chronic psychological stress at different time points
Authors: Luying Chen, Jiawei Xu, Jiangang Liu, Yuerong Jiang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThere is a lack of in-depth research on the impacts and changes in chronic psychological stress (CPS) on the cardiovascular system after acute myocardial infarction (AMI). This study aims to explore the comorbid mechanism and dynamic evolution of AMI exposed to CPS. 120 Wistar rats were randomly divided into Sham Operation group, Sham Operation + Chronic Unpredictable Mild Stress (CUMS) group, AMI group and AMI + CUMS group, with each group further divided into subgroups at days 7, 14, and 28. The AMI model was created by ligating the left anterior descending coronary artery, and CUMS model was used to induce CPS in rats. Behavioral changes were assessed through open field tests and sucrose preference tests. Cardiac function and structure were evaluated via echocardiography. The serum levels of TNFα, IL-6, NO, ET, CK-MB, cTNT, and ANP were measured using assay kits. Pathological changes in cardiac and brain tissues were observed under an optical microscope. Comparative analysis across different models revealed that CUMS significantly reduced behavioral activities in rats, with an interaction between CUMS and AMI affecting total distance (P < 0.05). Both CUMS and AMI significantly reduced cardiac function indicators, with their interaction effects on LVEF, LVFS, and CO (P < 0.05). AMI significantly altered cardiac structural parameters, particularly on day 28 (P < 0.05); while the impact of CUMS on cardiac structure was not significant, except for a notable reduction in LVAW/s on day 7 in AMI + CUMS group (P < 0.05). AMI caused significant changes in the serum biomarkers, while CUMS only significantly increased cTnT on day 7, ANP, TNFα, and IL-6 on day 14, and CK-MB on day 28, with their interaction effects on the three myocardial injury markers and TNFα (P < 0.05). Comparative analysis across different time points demonstrated that behavioral activity, cardiac function, CK-MB, cTnT, ANP, TNFα, and ET levels decreased significantly over time in the AMI model rats, while the left ventricular mass increased significantly (P < 0.05). Pathologically, compared with stress or AMI alone, the AMI + CUMS group exhibited more severe myocardia cellular degeneration and inflammatory infiltration, causing larger infract areas in myocardial tissue, as well as cell number decreases and morphological changes in hippocampal tissue. AMI with CPS exacerbates myocardial injury through sustained inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, leading to heart-brain pathology manifestations characterized by decreased cardiac function and hippocampal tissue damage.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Chronic unpredictable mild stress, Inflammatory response, Endothelial dysfunction, Cardiac functionSubject terms: Myocardial infarction, Anxiety, Depression, Inflammation, Risk factors

Introduction

Ischemic heart disease (IHD) is the leading cause of death and disability globally, with its incidence increasing year by year1,2. Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is the leading cause of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest caused by IHD, accounting for approximately 50% of cases3. The INTERHEART study has revealed that psycho-emotional factor is one of the risk factors for AMI4,5. AMI as an acute stressful event also exacerbates psychological burden in patients, leading to chronic psychological stress (CPS), such as stress, anxiety, fear, and depression. According to statistics, the prevalence of depression among Chinese patients with AMI is as high as 21.66%, significantly higher than that among individuals without a history of cardiovascular disease (10.36%)6. This indicates that negative emotions, such as anxiety and depression, are not only important precipitating factors for AMI but also common comorbidities throughout the prolonged course of cardiovascular disease7. A prospective cohort study has shown an association between depression and increased risk of cardiovascular disease8. The long-term mortality rate is also increased in AMI patients with comorbid depression9,10. Similarly, anxiety is associated with a poor prognosis for AMI patients11. Therefore, it can be considered that there is a high comorbidity between AMI and psychological disorders. The concept of “Mind-Heart-Body” has also been proposed and widely accepted in this context12.

Currently, the comorbid mechanisms of AMI and psychological stress are often explored through various factors such as neuroendocrine theory, immune inflammatory response, endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation, and genetics13. Although psychological stress serves as an independent risk factor for coronary heart disease (CHD), its mechanisms in increasing the risk of cardiovascular diseases are varied and debated. Notably, cardiovascular disease risks in patients with CHD is affected by multiple factors, such as anxiety, depression, smoking, obesity, etc. Therefore, the direct effects of CPS on the heart are difficult to observe and assess in clinical human trials. However, the animal models of chronic stress can provide direct experimental evidence of cardiac changes after AMI, with little to no confounders.

The chronic unpredictable mild stress (CUMS) model is currently one of the most used, reliable, and representative stress modeling methods for mimicking human stress conditions in the real world14. The CUMS model can also effectively evaluate the impact of psychological stress on cardiovascular activities following AMI15–17. In our earlier research, rats with AMI exposed to CUMS model showed abnormal elevations in the levels of vascular active substance nitric oxide (NO) and inflammatory factor high-sensitivity C-reactive protein. This exacerbated the hypercoagulable state of blood rheology and promoted platelet activation and thrombus formation18,19. However, there is a lack of in-depth research on the effects of CPS on the pathophysiological changes in AMI rat at different time points.

Therefore, this study aimed to observe the changes in behavior, serum markers, cardiac function and structure, as well as histopathology of the heart and brain in AMI rats under CPS at different time points. These findings clarify the mechanisms by which CPS increases cardiovascular risk after AMI and provide new perspectives and mechanisms for the treatment of AMI comorbid with anxiety and depression.

Methods

Experimental materials

Forty Wistar rats with an equal distribution of males and females were provided by Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. All rats were specific-pathogen-free animals and were reared in a barrier facility. The full-nutrient feed administered to the rats on a regular schedule was provided by Beijing Ke’aoXieli Feed Co., Ltd., production certificate number: Jing Feed Certificate (2014) 06054. The rearing environment was maintained at a temperature of 22–25 °C, humidity ranging from 50 to 70%, and a light-dark cycle of 12 h each. The experiments were conducted after one week of acclimatization.

The reagents used in the experiment included Evans Blue (Sigma-Aldrich Co., Ltd., USA), and 1% solution of pentobarbital sodium. The equipment used included a small animal ventilator (1680 model, Harvard Apparatus Co., Ltd., USA), an electrocardiograph (ML870Br model, ADInstruments Co., Ltd., Australia), a digital camera 6.0 viewer (Beijing Ruizhi Aoheng Technology Co., Ltd., China), and surgical instruments such as surgical scissors, hemostatic forceps, fine forceps, and medical sutures.

Model preparation

A long-term survival AMI model in rats was established using the in vivo method of ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery20. First, the rats were anesthetized intraperitoneally with 1% pentobarbital sodium solution. Then, we incised the rats’ skin beside the left midline of the chest, and sequentially separated the subcutaneous tissue and muscles to squeeze out the heart. Subsequently, we ligated the LAD artery between the left atrial appendage and the pulmonary artery cone using an ophthalmic suture needle, and then quickly returned the heart to its original position, squeezed out air, and closed the chest. The above operations were performed under strict aseptic conditions. Finally, the successful preparation of the AMI model was confirmed by ST segment elevation in standard limb lead II on the electrocardiogram. Except for the non-ligation of LAD artery, the operations in the sham operation (SO) groups were the same as those in the AMI model groups. Postoperatively, penicillin (40,000 units) was administered intramuscularly for three consecutive days. Additionally, local infiltration with lidocaine was provided as needed for pain relief based on the condition of the rats.

The CUMS modeling method referred to the recent literature14, and considering the laboratory environment and the tolerance conditions of AMI rats, the stressors of the CUMS model were improved as follows: water deprivation for 24 h, fasting for 24 h, shaking the rat cage for 15 min (until the rat cannot stand steadily), tail clamping for 5 min, swimming in ice water (4 °C) for 5 min, 24-hour reversal of light-dark cycle (a cardboard box was placed upside down over the cage at 6 a.m. to create darkness, removed at 6 p.m., and the cage was placed back in the light with a 12-hour light cycle, repeated the next day), exposure to humid environment for 24 h, sprinkling pepper powder in the cage for olfactory stimulation for 24 h, and sleep deprivation for 24 h. One of the nine stimulus sources mentioned above was randomly selected each day from 9:00 to 17:00 to expose the rats to unpredictable stimuli, continuously for 4 weeks. The success of the CUMS modeling was assessed through the sucrose preference test and open field test.

Rats grouping

According to a random number table, 120 Wistar rats were divided into the following groups according to: 7-day SO group, 7-day SO + CUMS group, 7-day AMI group, 7-day AMI + CUMS group, 14-day SO group, 14-day SO + CUMS group, 14-day AMI group, 14-day AMI + CUMS group, 28-day SO group, 28-day SO + CUMS group, 28-day AMI group, and 28-day AMI + CUMS group, with ten rats in each group.

After one week of acclimatization, total of 120 rats were subjected to a sham operation or AMI model according to the groups, and 19 died after the surgery, resulting in a postoperative mortality rate of 15.83%. Twenty-four hours after successful modeling, rats in the SO + CUMS and AMI + CUMS groups were subjected to chronic mild and unpredictable stimulation for 7, 14, or 28 days respectively, depending on their groups. During the CUMS modeling process, 2 additional rats died, leaving a total of 99 rats that survived.

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiyuan Hospital, China Academy of Chinese Medical Sciences (Approval No.: 2021XLC039-2). All handling of animals during the experiment complied with the ethical requirements of the “Regulations on the Management of Experimental Animals” issued by the National Science and Technology Commission and the “Guidelines for Ethical Review of Experimental Animal Welfare in Beijing”. This study was also performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

Indicators and detection methods

Behavioral tests

Open field test: After 15 min of environmental adaptation, each rat was placed into the testing chamber for a 3-minute observation and recording period. The animals were placed in the center of the chamber in the same position and direction for each time. Test indicators included total distance, central area distance and central area latency time within three minutes.

Open field test: After 15 min of environmental adaptation, each rat was placed into the testing chamber for a 3-minute observation and recording period. The animals were placed in the center of the chamber in the same position and direction for each time. Test indicators included total distance, central area distance and central area latency time within three minutes.

Sucrose preference test: Forty-eight hours before the experiment, rats were acclimated to sucrose water by providing two bottles in the cage, one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other containing pure water, with the positions of the two bottles swapped midway. Then, food and water were restricted for 24 h. The consumption of sucrose water and pure water within 1 h was measured, and the sucrose preference level was calculated as sucrose consumption / (sucrose consumption + pure water consumption) × 100%.

Sucrose preference test: Forty-eight hours before the experiment, rats were acclimated to sucrose water by providing two bottles in the cage, one containing 1% sucrose solution and the other containing pure water, with the positions of the two bottles swapped midway. Then, food and water were restricted for 24 h. The consumption of sucrose water and pure water within 1 h was measured, and the sucrose preference level was calculated as sucrose consumption / (sucrose consumption + pure water consumption) × 100%.

Cardiac ultrasound

After the duration of stress, the rats were anesthetized and placed in a supine position on a surgical board for 2D M-mode echocardiography. Initially, a 7–15 MHz linear array transducer (CL15-7 model, Philips Investment Co., Ltd., China) was placed on the left chest to obtain satisfactory parasternal short-axis two-dimensional images of the left ventricle. Subsequently, a high-frequency linear array transducer (HDI 5000 model, Philips Investment Co., Ltd., China) was used to position the M-mode sampling line perpendicular to the interventricular septum and the atrium and left ventricle at the level of the papillary muscle to obtain M-mode echocardiography.

Cardiac ultrasound parameters for the same cardiac cycle included left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left ventricular fractional shortening (LVFS), cardiac output (CO), stroke volume (SV), heart rate (HR), left ventricular mass corrected (LV Mass cor), end-systolic and end-diastolic left ventricular anterior wall (LVAW/s, LVAW/d) thickness, and end-systolic and end-diastolic left ventricular posterior wall (LVPW/s, LVPW/d) thickness. Measurements were obtained as the average of three consecutive cardiac cycles. The ultrasound examination was performed by two experienced ultrasound physicians who were blinded to the rats grouping.

Serum biomarkers

Abdominal aortic blood was drawn from the anesthetized rats, and the serum was centrifuged for detection. Creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) detection was performed using the colorimetric immunosuppression method. Cardiac troponin T (cTnT), atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin 6 (IL-6) and endothelin (ET) were detected using the ELISA competitive immunoassay. NO was detected via the nitrate reductase method. All the detection procedures strictly followed the instructions provided with the assay kits.

Myocardial and brain tissue observation

After the above detections, all the rats were euthanized using carbon dioxide inhalation. The brain and infarcted myocardial tissues were removed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. After 24 h of fixation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin, sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm, deparaffinized, mounted on slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE). The stained sections were observed under an optical microscope, and images were scanned using Digital Camera 6.0 Viewer software. The myocardial infarct area was calculated using Image J software. All the histological analysis during the experiment was carried out by a professional pathologist.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to test the normality of the data. Data that did not conform to a normal distribution were transformed before conducting statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences in different stress duration under the same model. Two-way ANOVA was employed to compare differences among different models at the same time point, specifically examining the interaction between the presence of AMI and stress [i.e., (SO vs. AMI) * (non-CUMS vs. CUMS)]. If there were significantly statistical differences, LSD test was used for further pairwise comparisons. SPSS software (version 13.0) was used for the data analysis, with P<0.05 indicating statistical significance.

Results

Behavioral changes

We employed open field tests and sucrose preference tests to assess the success of the CUMS model and evaluate the psychological state of the rats (Figs. 1, 2, 3 and 4). On day 7, stress alone significantly decreased the three open field test indicators in rats (i.e., SO vs. SO + CUMS, all P < 0.05). On day 14, stress led to significant reductions in total distance (SO vs. SO + CUMS, P < 0.001; AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P = 0.019), center area distance (AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P = 0.028), center area time (SO vs. SO + CUMS, P = 0.024), and sucrose preference levels (AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P = 0.018). Meanwhile, AMI significantly reduced the total distance on days 7 and 14, regardless of stress (i.e., SO vs. AMI, and SO + CUMS vs. AMI + CUMS, all P < 0.05). Until day 28, regardless of AMI, all four behavioral indicators of the CUMS model rats were significantly lower than those of the non-CUMS model rats (i.e., SO vs. SO + CUMS, and AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, all P < 0.05). AMI also significantly reduced the open field test indicators in rats (P < 0.05); specific intergroup comparisons are shown in the Figs. 1, 2 and 3. Moreover, there was an interaction effect between AMI and stress on the total distance at days 7 and 28 (P = 0.011, P = 0.001), while no interaction effect on day 14 (P = 0.149). Observing the behavioral changes under the same model but different stress durations, the total distance in the SO + CUMS group, the central area distance in the AMI group, and all behavioral indicators in the AMI + CUMS group significantly decreased over time (P < 0.05).

We used the echocardiographic parameters LVEF, LVFS, CO, and SV to assess cardiac function (Figs. 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9). At all three time points, AMI significantly reduced all four cardiac function indicators in rats, regardless of stress (i.e., SO vs. AMI, and SO + CUMS vs. AMI + CUMS, all P < 0.05). On day 7, stress significantly decreased LVEF (AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P = 0.033) and LVFS (SO vs. SO + CUMS, P = 0.019) in rats, but no interaction effect was observed. On day 14, stress led to significant reductions in LVEF (AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P < 0.001), CO (AMI vs. AMI + CUMS, P < 0.001), and LVFS (SO vs. SO + CUMS, P = 0.020), with an interaction effect between AMI and stress on LVEF and CO (P = 0.043, P = 0.043). Moreover, by day 28, the three indicators in both groups were significantly lower than those on day 7 (P < 0.05). Pathological changes in myocardial tissues

Observing the entire ventricular sections, no obvious infarct area was found in the sham-operated rats, whereas clear infarct areas were evident in the AMI model rats. The average percentage of the infarct size (i.e., infarct size/ventricular size) in AMI rats was measured, as shown in the Fig. 22. It indicated that over time, the infarct size gradually decreased due to tissue repair and ventricular remodeling; however, concurrent stress led to an increase in the infarct size.

Percentages of myocardial infarct size in AMI model rats.

Observing myocardial tissues at 40x magnification, the sham operation models showed orderly arranged cardiomyocytes with intact contours, uniform staining, and no apparent cellular degeneration, necrosis, or inflammatory infiltrates. In rats with AMI models, the infarcted myocardial tissues showed myocardial cell rupture or disappearance, accompanied by extensive infiltration of neutrophils and macrophages at day 7. By day 14, the necrotic myocardial fibers in the infarcted area were cleared by macrophages and replaced by collagen fiber, forming loose connective tissue. By day 28, dense fibrous tissue was largely observed, with little inflammatory cells. Compared with the AMI group, the rats in the AMI + CUMS group exhibited a larger infarct area on day 7, with more inflammatory cells infiltration and a disorganized texture. On day 14, the infarct area still contained a significant number of inflammatory cells, with a lower degree of fibrosis and unevenly arranged fibrous tissue. By day 28, persistent chronic inflammation resulted delayed myocardial fibrosis in the AMI + CUMS group, so residual inflammatory cells and loose collagen fibers were still visible, and the arrangement of the scar tissue was disorganized. Under an optical microscope, the hippocampal brain tissues of rats in the SO group exhibited intact structures, orderly cell arrangement with abundant numbers and high density, clear layers, and normal morphology of neuronal nuclei. The SO + CUMS and AMI groups showed similar hippocampal tissue structures, with relatively normal cell shapes but a reduced density. By day 28, some cells exhibited morphological changes. In the AMI + CUMS group, hippocampal region tissues showed disrupted structures, with disorganized cell arrangement and decreased density. Discussion

This study described the dynamic changes in behavior, serum markers, cardiac function and structure, as well as histopathology of the heart and brain in AMI rats under the CUMS model over a period of 28 days. The experimental duration for the CUMS model typically ranges from 3 to 12 weeks, with recent studies often lasting for 4 weeks14. The pathological evolution after AMI is bounded by 7 days, with the acute injury occurring within the initial 7 days, followed by myocardial repair, reconstruction, and gradual stabilization over several weeks. Therefore, to better observe the dynamic changes in rats, we selected days 7, 14, and 28 as the three different stress durations. The results indicate that with prolonged CPS, the myocardial damage, inflammatory response, and endothelial dysfunction after AMI become more severe, thus accelerating the decline in cardiac function.

In this study, AMI and CUMS models led to depressive and anxious behaviors in rats, as indicated by significant decreases in the results of open field test and sucrose preference test. Depressed rats typically exhibit reduced sucrose intake due to anhedonia, while anxious rats exhibit decreased exploratory behavior21. In this study, the behavioral indicators in the AMI group were significantly lower than those in the SO group, suggesting that AMI is also an acute stressor. However, compared to those with AMI alone, rats in the AMI + CUMS group exhibited lower levels of behavioral performance, and the differences became increasingly significant over time. This suggests the psychological harm of continuous CPS after AMI and the necessity of early clinical intervention.

Cardiac ultrasound is an effective method for assessing changes in cardiac structure and function. The results of this study demonstrated that the cardiac changes in rats under AMI models induced by LAD coronary artery ligation manifested as decreased cardiac function and thinning of the LVAW, compensatory thickening of the non-infarcted area of the LVPW and increased LV Mass cor. Specifically, compared to the AMI group, rats in the AMI + CUMS group exhibited more statistically pronounced decreases in cardiac function parameters over time. However, no significant compensatory changes in structural indicators, such as LV Mass cor or LVPW thickness, were observed between the two groups, except for a significant reduction of LVAW/s observed on day 7 in the AMI + CUMS group. Subsequent myocardial tissue imaging analysis, revealing a larger infarct area and delayed myocardial fibrosis in the AMI + CUMS group than in the AMI group, was consistent with the changes in ultrasound indicators. This suggests that CPS affects the process of ventricular remodeling, primarily manifesting as cellular-level changes and a significant decline in cardiac function, but without noticeable structural alterations within the study period. These may be due to the delayed myocardial fibrosis or the in sufficient sensitivity of ultrasound testing.

Exploring its mechanistic connections, current literature often links psychological stress with acute cardiac events through a novel perspective known as the psycho-neuro-endocrine-immune system axis22–24. On the one hand, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activation and sympathetic system overexcitation mediate the occurrence of AMI and post-psychological stress. Then, increased secretion of cortisol and catecholamines under stress induces endothelial dysfunction, thereby affecting the adverse prognosis of AMI25. Ghasemi et al. found abnormally elevated levels of NO in patients with severe depression26. However, unlike previous studies18, CPS did not lead to an increase of NO levels in this study. This may be due to endothelial dysfunction caused by oxidative and nitrosative stress resulting from activation of the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system27, which inhibits endothelial nitric oxide synthase (NOS) activity and NO production. Therefore, under different experimental conditions and with varying types and durations of stress, the dominant NOS type may differ, resulting in variations in NO production rates. Furthermore, NO levels in the AMI + CUMS group gradually increased over time. This increase may be due to enhanced immune-inflammatory responses inducing inducible NOS expression and NO production28. In addition, the ET level in the AMI + CUMS group remained higher than that in the AMI group throughout the study, but it significantly decreased over time. This may be attributed to the alleviation of acute stress and neuroregulatory effects. A previous study indicated that acute stress responses following AMI led to a significant increase in ET-1 levels and subsequent activation of ETA receptors29. ETA receptors in the brain can regulate the activity of sympathetic and vagus nerves and reverse the occurrence of arrhythmias during myocardial necrosis30. Considering the changes in ET levels observed in this study, further research is warranted to elucidate the regulatory role and mechanisms of ET in stress-induced AMI.

On the other hand, the immune inflammatory response is also a coupled mechanism of AMI combined with CPS, which has been supported by clinical studies31–33. The immune cells residing in infarcted tissues can synthesize and release cytokines, which respond to acute tissue damage and myocardial remodeling through complex immune-inflammatory responses34. CPS is also associated with neuroinflammation, and reducing the release of pro-inflammatory factors can improve mood disorders35. In this study, serum inflammatory factors levels in post-AMI rats significantly increased and peaked at day 7, which is consistent with previous research findings indicating early inflammatory activation and the release of a large number of inflammatory factors after AMI36,37. However, there was no significant increase in IL-6 levels in rats under CUMS model groups, while an increase in TNFα was more significant. A previous animal study showed that TNFα is associated with endothelial cell activation and pro-endothelial inflammation38. Therefore, the elevated levels of TNFα in this study suggests that stress is more related to inflammation and its mediation of endothelial damage. This finding is also consistent with previous clinical findings, where only elevated TNFα is associated with an increased incidence of adverse cardiovascular events caused by anxiety and depression39. Further analysis of TNFα changes revealed no significant difference between the SO and SO + CUMS groups, but a significant difference observed between the AMI and AMI + CUMS groups, which may be due to higher baseline inflammation levels and stronger stimulation experienced by AMI + CUMS rats. AMI, as an acute stressor, like chronic stress, also trigger the release of pro-inflammatory factors through the activation of the HPA axis and sympathetic nervous system40. The combined effect of both stressors led to a marked increase in TNFα levels. The two-way ANOVA analysis confirming an interaction between AMI and CUMS also supported this conclusion. Furthermore, the trends of TNFα levels differed between the AMI and AMI + CUMS groups. Both groups exhibited excessive inflammation due to AMI23, which the body attempted to regulate through neurohormonal negative feedback mechanisms and adaptive transformation of immune cells22,40. So, reductions of TNFα levels were observed. However, the TNFα levels in the AMI + CUMS group was slightly decreased on day 14 due to the chronic low-grade inflammation induced by CUMS22, and significant suppression of pro-inflammatory factor release only occurred by day 28. In contrast, the AMI group showed a marked decrease in TNFα on day 14, as anti-inflammatory mechanisms became predominant. By day 28, the AMI group had entered the tissue remodeling phase, necessitating further involvement of pro-inflammatory factors to promote new tissue formation, which led to a resurgence in TNFα levels. However, due to the delayed fibrosis process, the AMI + CUMS group did not exhibit an increase in TNFα levels within the study period. In summary, it is believed that CPS promotes the inflammatory response in the context of AMI. However, due to the body’s negative feedback regulation and immune adaptation, pro-inflammatory cytokines do not continuously increase but instead fluctuate. A recent review also highlighted the dynamic nature of pro-inflammatory cytokines in various cardiac diseases41. Further research is also needed to explore the dynamic regulation of pro- and anti-inflammatory factors and the involvement of other inflammatory mediators and cell types.

In terms of histological changes, the infarct size in the AMI + CUMS group was consistently larger than in the AMI group. Furthermore, even 14 days post-AMI, significant inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the infarcted area of the AMI + CUMS group, and the quality of scar tissue formation was reduced. This indicated that CPS may exacerbate myocardial damage and impair tissue repair through persistent inflammation. Observations of the hippocampal tissue revealed while the density of hippocampal cells only slightly decreased after AMI, the AMI + CUMS groups exhibited obvious cellular degeneration and disorganization in the hippocampal region. Previous studies have shown that chronic stress can lead to neuroinflammation in the medial prefrontal cortex microglia of mice42. This indicates that while AMI is an acute stressor, the inflammatory response and its adverse effects induced by CPS after AMI warrant more attention.

There are some limitations to the results of this study. Firstly, the employment of lidocaine for postoperative analgesia avoided affecting the central nervous system of the rats. However, it has limitations in managing complex and persistent pain following infarction surgery. Uncontrolled postoperative pain may exacerbate the stress in rats. Using a multimodal analgesia approach in the CUMS model could provide more rigorous experimental results. Moreover, by establishing a stable myocardial ischemia model through permanent ligation of the LAD artery in rats, the study controlled for other confounding factors and reflected the direct and long-term effects of ischemia combined with CPS on rat pathophysiology. However, myocardial reperfusion after AMI is a common clinical intervention. Whether myocardial ischemia-reperfusion and CPS interact is an important question for future research.

Conclusion

The AMI + CUMS model rats exhibited obvious behavioral changes, significantly decreased cardiac function, dynamic alterations in myocardial injury markers, persistent endothelial dysfunction and evident inflammatory responses. Abnormal pathological changes in myocardial and hippocampal tissues were also observed under stress. These changes suggest that CPS may exacerbate and accelerate the decrease in cardiac function after AMI through sustained inflammatory responses and endothelial damage, thereby increasing the risk of adverse outcomes.
