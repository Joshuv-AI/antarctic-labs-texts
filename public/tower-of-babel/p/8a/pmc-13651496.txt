Adverse Childhood Experiences and Unintended Pregnancy: Evidence From North Dakota PRAMS, 2017–2020
Authors: Omobosinuola F. Shyllon, Rick J. Jansen, Grace N. Njau, Ramona A. Danielson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
HIGHLIGHTS•Study used 4 years of Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System data from North Dakota (2017–2020).•Higher cumulative adverse childhood experience (ACE) exposure showed a dose–response relationship with unintended pregnancy.•Women with ≥4 ACEs had ∼2 times higher odds of unintended pregnancy.•Cumulative ACE score outperformed factor-based and individual-item ACE models.•Two specific ACEs (household substance use and parental separation/divorce) showed independent associations.

INTRODUCTION

Unintended pregnancy, defined by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) as a pregnancy that is mistimed or unwanted, has declined nationally from 43.3% in 2010 to 41.6% in 2019.1,2 Rates have dropped most among women aged 15–19 and 20–24 years.2 Despite the reduction in the prevalence rates of unintended pregnancies, the issue remains a public health concern owing to its impact on maternal outcomes, including psychosocial well-being, and its behavioral and economic implications.3 Disparities in pregnancy intendedness exist. Women aged 15–44 years living below the federal poverty level experience 60 unintended pregnancies per 1,000 women, compared with 30 per 1,000 women at twice the federal poverty level.3 In addition, rates are higher among unmarried and young adult women.3

Additional risk factors include cohabiting, being non-Hispanic Black or African American, having less than a high-school education, having limited knowledge and use of contraception, and partner intentions.1,4, 5, 6, 7 Behaviors such as smoking, substance abuse, and having multiple sexual partners also increase the risk.8, 9, 10, 11 For example, women who consumed alcohol had 3.4 times higher odds of unintended pregnancy, and combined tobacco and alcohol use increased the odds to 5.1.11 Risky sexual behavior (e.g., unprotected sex, having multiple sexual partners, early sexual initiation), which is more common among those with adverse childhood experiences (ACEs), also increases the risk of unintended pregnancy.1,5,10,12, 13, 14

ACEs are traumatic events during childhood that impact physical, mental, and emotional well-being across the lifespan.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 In the U.S., 64% of adults report at least 1 ACE, and 17% (nearly 1 in 6) report ≥4 ACEs.15 Examples include abuse, neglect, witnessing violence in the home, household substance abuse, parental separation or divorce, or incarceration of a household member.15 ACEs are associated with an increased risk of physical and mental health challenges and chronic conditions, including cancer, diabetes, heart disease, and suicide.16,18,21, 22, 23

Prior research has shown that higher ACE scores are associated with an increased risk of unintended pregnancy.5,14,24 A dose–response relationship has been observed between ACEs and unintended pregnancy.5,6,25,26 A study carried out by Dietz et al.24 (1999) found that women who experienced ≥4 ACEs were 1.5 times more likely to have an unintended first pregnancy during adulthood than women with 0 ACEs. Another study observed that there was a higher proportion of early sexual initiation by age 15 years among women who had any ACEs than among women with no ACEs.14

Limited research has explored ACEs and unintended pregnancy in rural states such as North Dakota (ND). This analysis aggregated 4 years of ND Pregnancy Risk Assessment Monitoring System (PRAMS) data to examine this relationship more robustly. In addition to evaluating the association between ACEs and pregnancy intendedness, factor analysis was used to compare the predictive value of alternative approaches to measuring ACE exposure, including cumulative ACE scores, factor-based groupings (abuse/neglect, household dynamics), and individual ACE items. These analyses help to inform best practices for modeling childhood adversity in reproductive health research, especially in rural populations. The findings can inform evidence-based public health strategies.

METHODS

Study Sample

The ND PRAMS is a collaborative surveillance project of the CDC and the ND HHS. PRAMS provides data not available from other sources, which can be used to identify groups of women and infants at high risk for health problems, monitor changes in health status, and measure progress toward the goals of improving the health of women and infants. The PRAMS protocol was approved by the CDC’s IRB.27 This study was reviewed by the ND HHS IRB Chair and was determined not to need ND HHS IRB review. The ND PRAMS aggregated data set obtained from ND HHS contained no identifying information, and access to the data was limited to coauthors with a signed data-sharing agreement with ND HHS.

This study included data from 3,016 women who completed the ND PRAMS survey from 2017 to 2020; 1.4% of respondents who did not answer the question about pregnancy intendedness were excluded, resulting in a final N of 2,973. ND PRAMS response rates exceeded the CDC’s required threshold annually (i.e., 50%) for creating a weighted statewide sample for each of the 4 years (70.2% in 2017, 59.9% in 2018, 59.1% in 2019, and 60.5% in 2020). The majority of respondents completed their survey questionnaire by mail (79.0%), whereas 21.0% completed it after being moved to the phone phase of data collection.

Measures

Predictor variables were drawn from questions measuring ACEs. The ACEs module included 10 binary (yes/no) questions answered retrospectively about experiences before age 18 years, including abuse/neglect and household dynamics. A cumulative ACE score (0–10) was calculated on the basis of the number of yes responses. Respondents missing responses to any ACE question (4.9%) were not assigned a cumulative ACE score but were included in item-specific ACE analyses. Cronbach’s alpha showed good reliability of the cumulative score (alpha=0.83). ACE scores were categorized as 0, 1, 2–3, and ≥4. The cumulative category of ≥4 is consistent with other ACEs research examining dose–response relationships.17,28, 29, 30, 31, 32 In addition, we combined responses of 2 and 3 ACEs experienced to retain a large enough number for analysis purposes.

Factor analysis of the 10 ACE items identified two factors. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to test each factor’s reliability; both factors demonstrated adequate reliability. Factor 1 (alpha=0.78) included abuse/neglect items (i.e., physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect). Factor 2 (alpha=0.70) included items related to household dynamics (i.e., domestic violence, mental illness, separation/divorce, incarcerated household members, and substance abuse).

The outcome variable was pregnancy intendedness, which was assessed by asking how women felt just before becoming pregnant. Responses were dichotomized into intended and unintended per CDC PRAMS questions, aligning with prior research.31,33, 34, 35 Unintended pregnancies comprised responses of I wanted to be pregnant later, I didn’t want to be pregnant then or at any time in the future, and I wasn’t sure what I wanted, and intended pregnancies comprised responses of I wanted to be pregnant sooner and I wanted to be pregnant then. Secondary sensitivity analyses were conducted by disaggregating the unintended category into 2 subcategories (later/never, not sure), with intended pregnancy serving as the reference group. Results from the disaggregated sensitivity analyses are presented in Table A.1 (available online).

For categorical predictor variables with more than 2 levels, p-values represent the overall survey-adjusted associations across categories. Posthoc pairwise comparisons were not conducted because these analyses were intended to describe population-level distributions rather than testing all possible subgroup contrasts. To reduce the risk of Type 1 error from multiple comparisons, statistical interpretation focused on overall associations in descriptive tables and on adjusted comparisons from the multivariable regression models using the reference groups.

Several demographic variables collected on the survey were used as covariates. The variables used were age (≤24, 25–29, 30–34, and ≥35 years), race (White; Black; American Indian; and other races, including multiple races); ethnicity (Hispanic/not Hispanic), educational level (high-school diploma/GED or less, some college/Associate’s degree, college graduate), and geographic residence (urban county/rural county). Marital status was defined as married at the time of birth or other. Prior live births were categorized as 0, 1, 2, and ≥3. ACE items included exposure before the age of 18 years to emotional abuse, physical abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect as well as witnessing domestic abuse, mental illness in the home, separation/divorce in the home, a household member incarcerated, and substance abuse in the home.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 29) Complex Samples module and R (Version 2026.01.0+392). CDC’s complex weighting design ensured a representative statewide sample.27 Chi-square tests assessed the associations between pregnancy intentions and predictor variables. A backward stepwise logistic regression removed variables with p>0.1 to generate the best-fitting model of variables associated with pregnancy intendedness. Weighted proportions and CIs were calculated using survey-adjusted methods. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Rao-Scott adjusted chi-square test with statistical significance set at p<0.05.

Multiple modeling approaches were explored, including individual ACE items alone, ACE factor groupings, and cumulative ACE score. Because the ACE factor groupings were derived from the same underlying ACE items as the cumulative score, these measures were evaluated in separate exploratory models to avoid multicollinearity. The final model prioritized the cumulative ACE score because it reflects the dose–response relationship between childhood adversity and pregnancy intendedness, and it demonstrated a stronger and more consistent association than the factor-based or individual item models. Exploratory models comparing ACE operationalization strategies are presented in Table A.4 (available online).

RESULTS

The sample analyzed in this study (weighted n=39,825; raw n=2,973) was 74.9% White, 6.5% Black, and 8.3% American Indian; 94.4% were not Hispanic (Table 1). The age distribution was 21.7% for ages ≤24 years, 34.7% for ages 25–29 years, 30.3% for ages 30–34 years, and 13.3% for ages ≥35 years. Two thirds of the women were married at the time of birth (66.8%). Education was nearly evenly split among women having a high-school diploma/GED or less (29.7%), some college/Associate’s degree (32.6%), and a college degree (37.7%). In the PRAMS questionnaire, women were asked how they felt about becoming pregnant. The 15.8% who said they wanted to be pregnant sooner and the 44.4% who said then comprised intended pregnancies (60.2%). Univariable Associations with Select Characteristics of Women by Pregnancy Intendedness Status

Univariable associations with pregnancy intendedness by select characteristics are shown in Table 1. Women aged ≤24 years (60.0%) were significantly more likely than women in other age groups to report an unintended pregnancy. Women with a high-school diploma/GED or less (55.5%) were significantly more likely to report that their pregnancy was unintended than women with some college or an Associate’s degree (43.3%) and college graduates (24.5%). Women with ≥3 prior live births were more likely to have unintended pregnancies (55.3%) than women with fewer prior births. Women who reported using marijuana (71.4%), who used Adderall/stimulants (61.1%), and/or who used amphetamines/meth (77.5%) in the 12 months before pregnancy were more likely to indicate that their pregnancy was unintended than women who did not use these drugs. Table A.2 (available online) further describes the distribution of ACE score categories across sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics.

Univariable associations with pregnancy intendedness by individual ACEs, ACE score, and ACE factors are shown in Table 2. The rate of unintended pregnancy was highest among women with ≥4 ACEs (51.3%) and lowest among women with 0 ACEs (32.4%). Analysis of ACEs related to abuse and neglect revealed the highest rate of unintended pregnancy among women who reported emotional neglect (52.7%), followed by women who reported physical abuse (50.4%), sexual abuse (49.2%), emotional abuse (48.0%), and physical neglect (47.2%). The study examined multiple approaches to assessing ACE exposure, including the cumulative ACE score, ACE factor groupings, and individual ACE items. Table 3 shows the final multivariable model using the cumulative ACE score as the primary ACE exposure measure. Exploratory regression models comparing alternative ACE measure approaches are presented in Table A.4 (available online). In the exploratory analyses, models including ACE Factor 1 (abuse/neglect) and ACE Factor 2 (household dynamics) demonstrated weaker and less consistent associations with unintended pregnancy than the cumulative ACE score model. Similarly, models that included only the individual ACE items did not demonstrate the same predictive strength as the cumulative ACE score model. These findings provide support for the cumulative ACE score as a more stable and predictive measure for evaluating the association between childhood adversity and pregnancy intendedness.

In the final model, women aged ≤24 years had a higher likelihood of unintended pregnancy (OR=1.80) than women aged ≥35 years (Table 3). Women who were not married at the time of birth were more than 3 times as likely to have an unintended pregnancy (OR=3.41) than women who were married. Women who had ≥3 prior live births had the highest odds of unintended pregnancy (OR=2.70) than women who had no prior births before this pregnancy. Marijuana use by women in the 12 months before pregnancy was associated with nearly 2 times the odds of having an unintended pregnancy (OR=1.95).

A dose–response relationship was observed between ACE exposure and unintended pregnancy. Women with ≥4 ACEs had nearly twice the odds of unintended pregnancy (OR=1.99) compared with women with 0 ACEs. In addition, 2 individual ACE items remained independently associated with unintended pregnancy in the final model beyond the cumulative ACE score. Secondary sensitivity analyses revealed variability in pregnancy intention across ACE scores. Results of the disaggregated sensitivity analyses are presented in Table A.1 (available online). Women who had 0 ACEs reported the highest proportion of intended pregnancies (67.6%) than later/never (19.7%) and the not sure (12.6%) groups. An inverse relationship was observed between ACE score and intended pregnancy. As ACE exposure increased, the proportion of intended pregnancies decreased; 48.7% of women who reported that their pregnancy was intended had an ACE score of ≥4. Conversely, among the later/never and not sure groups, there was a direct relationship between ACE score and pregnancy intentions. Women with an ACE score of ≥4 had a higher proportion of later/never (26.9%) or not sure (24.4%) pregnancy intentions than those with 0 ACEs.

DISCUSSION

The findings show a clear dose–response pattern in a rural, population-based sample of live births in ND. The analysis found that unintended pregnancy is prevalent among women with a live birth in ND; 2 in 5 women reported that their pregnancy was unintended (i.e., they wanted to be pregnant later, they did not want to be pregnant then or at any time in the future, or they were not sure what they wanted). In univariable analyses, several sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics were significantly associated with pregnancy intendedness, including age, marital status, education, race, ethnicity, prior live births, marijuana use, Adderall/stimulant use, and amphetamines/meth use. Geographic residence, drinking 3 months before becoming pregnant, and use of prescription pain medications in the 3 months before pregnancy were not significantly associated. This is consistent with prior research that showed an association between ACEs and unintended pregnancy across diverse populations.24,26,36

Women with ≥4 ACEs had a higher proportion of unintended pregnancies than women without ACEs, consistent with recent research.19,26,31,36 In univariable analyses, nearly all forms of abuse/neglect and household dynamics were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of having an unintended pregnancy, except for physical neglect, which was not significant. As highlighted in Figure 1, the characteristics showing the strongest dose–response relationship with unintended pregnancy included not being married at the time of birth, having ≥3 prior births, having ≥4 ACEs, having used marijuana in the 12 months before pregnancy, and being aged ≤24 years.18,37

In the multivariable analyses, the cumulative ACE score revealed stronger and more consistent associations with unintended pregnancy than the other approaches, including ACE factors and individual ACE items separately. This is highlighted in the exploratory model presented in Table A.4 (available online). Although the ACE factor groupings and the individual ACE items contributed relevant contextual information, the cumulative ACE score effectively reflects the dose–response relationship between increasing exposure to childhood adversity and unintended pregnancy. Notably, separation/divorce in the home and household substance abuse remained independently associated with unintended pregnancy even after adjustment for the cumulative ACE score. This suggests that some forms of household instability may contribute additional risk beyond the overall burden of adversity.17,24

Although there has been growing literature on the association between ACE score and unintended pregnancy, there have also been findings that show no significant association, which could result from differences in sample size, demographics, and approach to analysis.38 In addition, some studies suggest that the association is more specific to unwanted pregnancy rather than to mistimed pregnancy.26 The findings from the disaggregated sensitivity analysis (Table A.1, available online) reinforce that combining later/never with not sure pregnancy intendedness in this study did not obscure the relationship with ACEs. Women who reported later/never and not sure pregnancy intentions had a substantially higher proportion of ≥4 ACEs than women who reported that their pregnancies were intended.

Table A.2 (available online) provides further context regarding the distribution of ACE exposure across sociodemographic characteristics. The study observed that higher ACE exposure is not distributed randomly across the population but is concentrated among women with known risk factors for unintended pregnancy, including younger age, being unmarried, lower educational attainment, and substance use. This suggests that ACEs may work in tandem with sociobehavioral factors that reinforce behaviors that increase the risk of unintended pregnancy.

These findings reveal important implications for clinical practice, supporting the value of screening for trauma in discussions of reproductive health.39 Although there is ongoing discussion regarding standardized ACE screening in clinical settings, growing evidence supports its utility in identifying patients who may benefit from additional trauma-informed assessment and support.40 ACEs may influence reproductive decision making through various pathways, including psychosocial stress, disruptions in relationship stability, decreased access to supportive resources, and engagement in risky behaviors.18,37 Together, these findings support the integration of trauma-informed care approaches into reproductive health counseling to better address the needs of women with a history of childhood adversity.

Fostering and building resilience is an important aspect of empowering women who have experienced some form of ACEs. Utilizing a systems-thinking approach to address the structural and systemic factors that cause stress and promote adversity is beneficial in fostering resilience.41 Collaboration between healthcare and public health professionals in providing accessible and quality mental healthcare services can help equip individuals with the right tools to deal with adversity. In addition, where possible, eradicating the root causes of childhood adversity in society, such as addressing social determinants of health and promoting self-healing communities, will help provide environments that reduce stress and promote positive outcomes.42

Limitations

This study has some important limitations. As a cross-sectional survey, PRAMS data cannot determine causation. In addition, the data in this study are from ND, where about half of births are to women living in rural counties. The state-specific nature of the results should be considered when extrapolating the results to other contexts. In addition, PRAMS is a retrospective survey, in which women’s recollection of their experiences as children may be susceptible to recall bias. However, because a key intent of the ACE questions is to create a composite score and because ACEs are known to co-occur, analyzing the data cumulatively limits the impact of recall bias on individual questions. Not every woman chose to answer every ACE question; 4.9% of respondents on this survey did not answer all 10. Social desirability bias may influence respondents to respond to sensitive questions (e.g., ACEs or illicit drug use) in a way that they think will be more acceptable than their truthful response, which would result in underestimates of prevalence. However, 4 of 5 of the completed surveys were completed by mail, and research shows less impact of this type of bias using self-reported modes of data collection such as mail surveys.43 The aggregated data set includes data from 2020, the first year of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic. Survey response rate remained stable between 2019 (59.1%) and 2020 (60.5%). In addition, although COVID-19 contributed to higher rates of depression and anxiety and poorer self-reported physical health, these were not key variables in the analysis.44, 45, 46 Anyone who completed the survey by December 2020 (i.e., the end of the aggregated data timeframe) would have become pregnant before the beginning of the pandemic. Regarding the dichotomous grouping of pregnancy intendedness, prior research36 suggests that respondents reporting that they never wanted to be pregnant may differ meaningfully from those reporting a mistimed pregnancy. In this analysis, even with 4 years of aggregated data, the sample size was not large enough to provide model stability, limiting the examination of these groups separately.

This study also has several strengths. Aggregating 4 years of data provided narrower CIs, and a large number of rural births provided an opportunity for robust analyses of potential rural/urban differences. Furthermore, births to American Indian women represent more than 8% of total births in the state. ND PRAMS oversampled American Indian birth records to ensure a sufficient sample size so that these births were not relegated to the other race category, allowing for important insights into the experiences of American Indian women in the state. In addition, the supplementary analyses of factor-based ACE groupings helped reinforce the value of the cumulative ACE score, in combination with individual ACE items in the regression analyses, for exploring the impact of ACEs on pregnancy intendedness.

CONCLUSIONS

This study confirms a dose–response relationship between cumulative ACE exposure and pregnancy intendedness. The data set of 4 years of statewide, population-based PRAMS data includes a high representation of births from rural areas as well as from American Indian women. The study extends prior research by clarifying how childhood adversity may best be measured and modeled in population-based analyses of pregnancy intendedness. In addition, the study addresses an unresolved methodologic question through a sensitivity analysis, which shows that combining later/never with not sure pregnancy intendedness does not obscure the relationship with ACEs. In addition, a systematic model comparison is conducted, which demonstrates that the cumulative ACE score outperforms the factor-only and item-only models in its association with unintended pregnancy. In this analysis, specific ACEs related to household dynamics (i.e., separation/divorce and household substance use) have independent predictive value beyond the cumulative experience represented by the ACE score.

This research has implications for clinical practice. The findings support the use of screening for trauma in discussions of reproductive health. Acknowledging childhood adversity when discussing reproductive intentions as well as specific household dynamics may enhance the understanding of the broader context in which women make such decisions. In a rural state such as ND, incorporating trauma-informed and strengths-based approaches to reproductive health could help identify women at increased risk of an unintended pregnancy and inform prevention and counseling strategies. The study also has epidemiologic implications. Future research could explore whether these patterns hold across other rural states and whether interventions that specifically strengthen household stability impact reproductive outcomes among women with high cumulative childhood adversity.
