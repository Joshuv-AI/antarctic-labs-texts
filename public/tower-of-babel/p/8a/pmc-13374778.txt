Setting expectations for current and future directions of native mass spectrometry in the biopharmaceutical industry
Authors: Elizabeth Hecht, Esther Martin, Idlir Liko, Andrew Mahan, Aniruddha Sahasrabuddhe, Nastaran N. Tajoddin, Iain D. G. Campuzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile native mass spectrometry (native-MS) has been widely explored in academic laboratories, its practical role within biopharmaceutical research remains less clearly defined. In this perspective, we present an industry-driven view of how native-MS is currently applied, where it offers unique advantages over established analytical technologies, and where alternative methods remain more practical for routine characterization. Within biopharma workflows, characterization strategies traditionally rely on orthogonal techniques such as size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), ion-exchange chromatography (IEX), electrophoresis, light scattering, calorimetry, and denaturing liquid chromatography (LC) MS. Native-MS complements these methods by enabling direct assessment of intact molecular assemblies, including monoclonal antibodies (mAbs), multispecific antibodies, antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs), glycoproteins, and protein complexes. Applications include evaluation of higher-order assembly, ligand or cofactor binding, stoichiometry of target complexes, and heterogeneity that may be obscured under denaturing conditions. However, challenges related to throughput, sensitivity, automation, and accessibility have limited widespread adoption in industrial laboratories. Emerging developments, including chromatographic hyphenation, online buffer exchange (OBE), improved automation, and charge-detection MS (CDMS), are beginning to address these constraints. We argue that the future impact of native-MS in biopharma will depend on integrating these technological advances with platformed analytical workflows and software capable of supporting high-throughput characterization across therapeutic pipelines. We hope that the ideas raised in this article spur debate on when, how, and if native-MS would or should see increased adoption.

Introduction

Native mass spectrometry is a gas‑phase-based analytical technique commonly used to provide insights into predominantly high m/z ion transmission, the preservation of noncovalently assembled protein complexes, and ultimately inferences to solution-phase structural biology. Yet we believe there is a gap in understanding as to the primary applications that drive biopharmaceutical scientists to adopt and routinely utilize this technology. Anecdotally, this was reflected in audience-raised questions during recent American Chemical Society and the American Society for Mass Spectrometry conferences. It is also reflected in reviews of native-MS in drug discovery that may reflect a more academic viewpoint because they do not include an industry coauthor to help guide the perspective, nor cite a substantial body of industry-authored/coauthored work.1–5 While there is value in the demonstration of native-MS technologies on industry-derived biotherapeutics, these reviews can lack context about which state these molecules occupy in current therapeutic pipelines or biopharma-specific analytical needs. The present article is intended to complement that body of literature by providing an industry perspective.

This perspective is therefore written by a consortium of native-MS, industry-based experts with the goal of highlighting biopharmaceutical application areas where native-MS and associated analytical methods are fully utilized or emerging and where they should be utilized. We then offer insight as to where native-MS would or would not outperform alternative established technologies to justify the resource (time and capital) investment. Here, we have assumed that readers understand the basic principles of native-MS, which are discussed in reviews of the technology6,7 and also refer readers to a subset of more in‑depth native-MS application reviews that do include industry authors.8–14

Which analytical techniques currently compete with native-MS in biopharma research?

Throughout the progress of a biotherapeutic’s lifespan—from research to development—multiple analytical techniques are required for full and deep characterization. Such analytical techniques include, but are not limited to, size-exclusion chromatography (SEC), hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC), ion-exchange chromatography (IEX), gel- and capillary-electrophoresis, differential scanning calorimetry and fluorimetry, surface plasmon resonance, Fourier transform infrared and Raman spectroscopy, circular dichroism, multiangle and dynamic light scattering, rheology, and MS.

Depending on the specific stage of the biotherapeutic in the research pipeline, in-depth MS characterization may not be required. While MS‑based proteomics is routinely used for target and biomarker discovery in early research, MS instrumentation may not be as common within protein‑purification laboratories as other analytical methods. For small start-up biotherapeutic companies, MS instrumentation is expensive, and large pharma companies have historically used in-house MS core labs that may not always be able to offer the required rapid turnaround times. Therefore, we highlight the enabling developments of walk‑up open‑access reversed-phase liquid chromatographic (RPLC) MS solutions for in-process samples and routine molecular weight (MW) characterization (primary sequence verification)15 that can also afford intact protein MW determination, resulting in MS democratization for non-MS users and real-time results. Native-MS analysis has not yet achieved this level of democratization, leaving a gap in walk‑up open‑access proteins and protein complexes analysis; with the help of instrument vendors, it can be addressed, and we are excited for such a future.

Unique challenges of native-MS technology in biopharma

Although the first native-MS spectrum was published in 199116,17, and high m/z noncovalent multimeric protein gas-phase research was subsequently explored18,19, the biopharma industry did not adopt native-MS as a supporting analytical technique (albeit in a niche capacity) until the mid-to-late 2000s, with the first biopharma native-MS publication emerging in 2009 (Figure 1). Native‑MS remained firmly grounded as an academic analytical technique, as reflected in the fact that the number of publications per year remained roughly at one until 2018 and thereafter, under 10 per year. This timing roughly correlated with commercially available instrumentation21,22 optimized for native-MS. Native-MS in biopharma is not about only analyzing high m/z species that represent non-covalently assembled protein complexes. The applications of biopharma native-MS can be diverse, encompassing analysis of fully covalently assembled mAbs and multispecific antibodies,23 antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs),24,25 heterogeneous proteins,26 and glycosylated drugs,27 as well as the stoichiometry of disease targets28, affinity maturation of small molecule binders,29 or the assessment of cofactors,30 molecular‑glue‑induced ternary complexes31 and intra-chain disulfide conjugation.25 Native-MS drug-antibody-ratio quantification and characterization of ADCs is one of the most mature workflows32–37, although even to industry veterans, it is unknown (or confidential) whether this data has been part of the IND filings leading to at least 18 Food and Drug Administration or European Medical Agency‑approved plus 1 breakthrough‑therapy‑designated medicines.38 It’s certainly possible that native-MS is used to build spectrophotometric lot release assays, for example, noting that intact RPLC-MS characterization is more than adequate for smaller or less complex conjugates.39–41 While a full exploration of biopharma molecule characterization by native-MS is out of scope, we’ll generally comment that the number of requests for this analysis in the research and optimization phase is highly variable across companies and somewhat driven by the complexity of the ADC, such as the disulfide linkages.

In principle, there is a direct path to small molecule screening by native-MS, and this application could most benefit membrane protein targets, where screening for binding is difficult by conventional approaches. However, we would argue that affinity selection MS42 will remain a first-in-class method because it is not subject to gas‑phase dissociation, is sample agnostic, may be carried out in nonvolatile buffer systems (prior to MS analysis), and it is compatible with lower affinity molecules. This highlights the current sensitivity issue with native-MS instrumentation and explains why low‑affinity binders (µM range) are hard to screen.

We’d like to set the expectation that, even though nano-electrospray ionization (nESI) single‑shot needles have unequivocally been one of the most enabling developments for native-MS, they do not meet the current needs of biopharma, where sample numbers in early research can reach hundreds, if not thousands, per project per week. This is ironic, given that many industry-based reports of successful native-MS use single-shot nESI in biopharma.43,44 Fortunately, the required level of automation and robustness for native-MS can now mostly be achieved through hyphenation with chromatographic methods, such as SEC, IEX, and HIC chromatography.45 The immediate and realized benefits of the aforementioned platformed chromatographic hyphenations are reliability (no clogging issues) and automation. We believe there will be immense value in coupling ubiquitous SEC-UV and IEX-UV analytical methods to MS for global high MW and assessment of acidic and basic species as a function of forced degradation. Why rely on UV only, when one can generate both UV and MS data and identify chromatographic peak(s) based on MW, which were previously mis- or unassigned and gain confidence in the identification?

Interestingly, we can envision a not-too-distant future in which all protein quality controls of concentration-abundant proteins are performed by online buffer exchange-mass spectrometry (OBE-MS).46 The increased charge state spacing afforded under native-MS conditions, coupled with the speed of OBE (<3 min) could make this a compatible and highly attractive strategy for all recombinant proteins, both heterogeneous and monodisperse. Furthermore, the approach is compatible with the growing field of multimeric membrane protein analysis. While the signal-to-noise ratio by native-MS (Figure 2a,c) is usually significantly lower than by denaturing RPLC-MS (Figure 2b,d), this may in certain cases be compensated for by injecting higher amounts or by infusing organic solvents post-column to boost the signal.23 Nevertheless, for these reasons, native-MS may remain niche until routinely enabled with automated column switching on a single RPLC/SEC-MS system. Chromatographic columns with universal SEC, RP, and hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography stationary phases are also intriguing and may be the way forward. Until then, RPLC-MS will remain the de‑facto method for primary sequence verification,47 isoform48 clip characterization, and multispecific antibody chain pair variants.49 Finally, the deconvolved (data not shown) intensity of the RPLC-MS data is two orders of magnitude higher than the native-MS data, demonstrating its suitability for lower concentration material. Emerging roles of native-MS and native-CDMS in biopharma

The rise of cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) has also increased demand for native-MS as a complementary and orthogonal analytics support technique. However, we largely disagree with the prevailing opinions that native-MS should be used to quality‑control every protein or purification condition (e.g., buffers and detergents). In biopharma, we instead rely on the biochemists’ scientific understanding of the system to optimize yield/purity and activity assays to select the solution components. Currently, the most common native-MS support request from structural biology groups is to troubleshoot misbehaving proteins, usually multimeric complexes such as induced proximity ternary complexes, on EM-grids or to provide orthogonal information such as heterogeneity in stoichiometry, lipid or metal cofactors, and confirmation of post‑translational modifications (PTMs) to complement a lower resolution cryo-EM structure. In particular, membrane protein complexes are newer targets and growing systems that require enhanced and bespoke analytical MS support. When challenged primarily with the non-MS friendly detergents preferred by protein biochemists on sub-optimal MS instrumentation, by our estimates, 25–50% of these projects are not successful in producing useable spectra by native-MS.

For membrane proteins, glycoproteins, and large non-monodisperse particles such as liposomes or adenoviruses (>1MDa), we believe that native CDMS could offer a paradigm shift in characterization. However, for monodisperse proteins and complexes (<1MDa) well-resolved charge states are still realized when analyzed on current m/z-based detection MS instrumentation. The advantages of CDMS for particle characterization are well published50 and include lot-to-lot variability and cargo loading. While CDMS is a natural fit for particle characterization in research and optimization, light scattering techniques will likely remain the dominant analytical technique due to their entry-level pricing, lower expertise requirements, and role in historic QC. Most CDMS instrumentation remains too expensive and niche to be widely adopted, and CDMS has only recently become partially compatible with autosampler‑based sample handling.51,52 Even with these elements were in place, and with CDMS measurements recently demonstrated to be preferred or similar to classical techniques such as analytical ultracentrifugation or SEC-UV, for example, in calculating percent aggregation, regardless of accuracy,53 industry adoption and acceptance may come slowly for cost, expertise, and acceptance reasons. However, CDMS industry adoption trends are a subject of active debate, and it remains to be seen whether the quality of CDMS data will accelerate adoption beyond what these authors anticipate. While > 1MDa particles represent an important modality, when considered in the context of the modality market, they represent a small fraction of therapeutic development. In 2025, nucleic acids as a fraction of the preclinical, clinical, and marketed markets represented ~9% of products, and this does not delineate nucleic acids in non-particle, conjugate formats, for example.54 Even with projected growth, nucleic acid therapeutics will not surpass 10% of products by 2030.54 Consequently, this application may not be large enough to singularly drive adoption across all of biopharma. Demonstrating the applicability of CD-MS to heterogeneous proteins in the 100–500 kDa range, such as glycosylated target antigens, for which conventional LC-MS techniques may be unsuccessful, may also accelerate adoption.

We ask the following provocative questions: For CDMS to gain traction in biopharma, would it need to come at the cost of replacing native-MS? Without MS/MS, will CDMS remain a hypothesis‑confirmatory tool rather than a discovery technique? Is there room in biopharma for both techniques? Is this only feasible if the same instrumentation seamlessly switches between m/z measurement (native/intact) and z measurement (CDMS mode)? Unless a question requiring high resolution emerges (e.g., ADC clipping or PTM characterization) is native-MS not required for any protein complex or heterogeneous molecule measurements? Could native CDMS, with just some modest resolution improvement, prove a more reliable and universal industrial analytical MW characterization technique?

How the biopharma industry is and will be driving for native-MS

In the sample handling space, we strive for chromatographic miniaturization (including, but not limited to, SEC, IEX, and HIC), thereby affording increased sensitivity, lower material consumption, and speed. Lower flow rates (and reduced internal diameter columns) also reduce the risk of salt deposition within the MS source. Capillary electrophoresis operates at a low flow rate and requires minimal sample material, but it is not commonly used for native-MS analytical support.55 Approaches that enable dual infusion for microdroplet reaction studies while remaining compatible with existing LCs—without the purchase of an additional pump/autosampler—are also desired.

Another emerging area exists in the field of soft landing as a grid preparation device for cryo-EM or as a direct imaging system, such as holography. In either case, we see value in reducing cycle times to obtain structures, even if those resulting structures are lower resolution. In biopharma, a lot can be done with a 5Å structure if acquired with trivial optimization at scale, coupled to automation and artificial intelligence models. SEC-MS can and should replace negative stain pipelines, if they are in place for sample quality control prior to EM. As such, we predict that industry groups will become immediately supportive of any soft-landing technique, yet an open question remains: is the future of soft landing to be operated by a microscopist or a mass spectrometrist.

An area where biopharma is blazing a path forward, driven by the need for high-throughput and full automation, is the Lab of the Future,56 where MS and MS/MS are hyphenated directly to the early project stage of antibody purification. One can envisage some simple modifications that will enable chromatographically hyphenated native-MS.

From a software perspective, an area of development is on-the-fly deconvolution. Software that auto-tunes MS collection to optimize data quality would enable native-MS to be accessible to non-expert users. Post‑data collection, there is also room for software improvement. Currently, it is up to the user to set MS resolution and advanced commands in complex native-MS spectra, such as using a comb filter to improve data deconvolution. It would be a paradigm shift if the software could independently recognize repeating units, or if an algorithm could shift to a low‑mass interpolation when the heterogeneity is too high. Ironically, the early iterations of MaxEnt (within MassLynx, MassHunter software platforms) have minimal user‑changeable values and still work for most biopharma native-MS samples.

Concluding remarks and industry dreams for native‑MS

Here, our aim is to provide context for when, where, and how native-MS is applied within research as an analytical method supporting pipeline progression. The impact of native-MS within biopharma research is currently limited when compared to other analytical technologies, but it is growing, and likely looks very different than what is currently envisioned by non-biopharma research groups. Engaging management, laboratory scientists, and vendors is critical to creating awareness and understanding of the true utility of native-MS technology within biopharmaceutical research and development.

Native-MS technologies exist today in such a way that, for many applications, they may be platformed, allowing industry to adopt them if they choose. Perhaps the field will come full circle, and instrument vendors will roll out updated historical mass spectrometers with smaller‑footprint instrument geometries that were proven to be capable of high m/z transmission, such as the Micromass LCT.57 This was a true, affordable, native-MS capable benchtop instrument. Budget and lab space remain some of the highest barriers to venturing into the native-MS world in industry.

Interestingly, native-MS remains one of the most difficult areas to outsource to commercial laboratories, with most contract research organizations refusing to accept the workflows. Academic partners in this area are open and willing to collaborate with industrial scientists, but constraints on academia-industry contracting may hinder the throughput and development of these collaborations.

Finally, we would like to recognize and acknowledge that the ability to have commercial native-MS capable MS instruments is thanks to the groundbreaking research performed by both academic groups and MS vendors. Through that work, there are a new generation of native-MS scientists excited to shape the field, see what it brings, and adopt the pieces that impact the pharma trajectory.
