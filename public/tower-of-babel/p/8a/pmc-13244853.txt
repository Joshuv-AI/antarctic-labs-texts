Prophylactic anti-seizure medication for post-traumatic seizures in Chinese PICUs: current practices and knowledge gaps
Authors: Jian Ji, Quan Wang, Zheng Li, Boliang Fang, Lijuan Wang, Suyun Qian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate current practices and asses knowledge gaps in the use of prophylactic anti-seizure medication (ASM) for managing pediatric post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE) in Mainland China, with a particular focus on clinical decision-making of pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) physicians.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey of 271 physicians across 30 tertiary hospital PICUs in 23 provinces was conducted between December 2023 and March 2024. The questionnaire assessed ASM utilization, treatment selection and duration, management of adverse events, and awareness of PTE risk factors. Descriptive statistics were used for data analysis.ResultsAmong the surveyed physicians, 72.7% reported prescribing prophylactic ASM, with levetiracetam (52.3%), phenobarbital (23.4%), and sodium valproate (22.3%) being the most common. The duration of treatment varied considerably: 29.4% of physicians continued prophylactic ASM up to 3 months following traumatic brain injury, whereas 22.3% of them limited the course to ≤ 7 days. Routine electroencephalogram monitoring was conducted by 78.7% of physicians. Common adverse events, including hepatotoxicity, rashes, and cognitive effects, were reported by 40.6% of the physicians. Notably, 85% of physicians continued ASM despite observing these complications. Hence, substantial knowledge gaps exist, particularly regarding the criteria for discontinuing ASM.SignificanceSignificant variability exists in prophylactic ASM practices across PICUs in China, influenced by inconsistent adherence to guidelines, physician experience, and interdisciplinary collaborations. These findings highlight the urgent need to develop standardized protocols and conduct evidence-based training to optimize PTE prevention. Further research is warranted to device individualized ASM regimens tailored to the severity of the injury, age of the patients, and neurodevelopmental outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04974-7.Keywords: Anti-seizure medication, China, Pediatric intensive care unit, Post-traumatic epilepsy, Prophylaxis, Traumatic brain injury

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a major global pediatric health issue, contributing to over 28% of injury-related disability-adjusted life years worldwide [1]. With rapid urbanization, China has witnessed a 67% increase in pediatric TBI incidence, mainly attributed to road accidents (48.2%) and falls (31.7%) [2, 3]. Even mild TBI can lead to long-term complications such as post-traumatic epilepsy (PTE), which occurs in approximately 3%–5% of cases [4, 5] and accounts for 10%–20% of childhood epilepsy cases [6].

TBI triggers excitotoxicity, neuroinflammation, and disruption of synaptic plasticity, leading to neuronal damage and abnormal neuroplasticity observed in PTE [7, 8]. The epileptogenic process is driven by maladaptive neuroplasticity, primarily caused by glutamate excitotoxicity and impaired blood–brain barrier integrity. This process increases the risk of developmental delay by 3.2 times in children with epilepsy compared with nonepileptic TBI cases [9].

Selecting an effective anti-seizure medication (ASM) is crucial for mitigating secondary brain injury and enhancing neuroprotection by leveraging antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neurotrophic pathways, thereby improving clinical outcomes in TBI cases [10, 11]. The selection of ASM is increasingly based on its ability to regulate neurobiological pathways, with evidence suggesting that some ASMs provide neuroprotective effects beyond seizure control [12, 13].

Although prophylactic ASM may reduce the risk of epilepsy in children with TBI, the efficacy of early ASM administration remains debated. Conflicting results regarding its role in preventing seizures, promoting recovery, and supporting cognitive development have been reported [14, 15]. High-quality clinical data comparing the effects of ASMs in children with TBI-related PTE are lacking. Consequently, treatment guidelines for pediatric PTE remain unclear, and evidence to guide clinical decisions on optimal medication strategies is insufficient. To address this gap, the Chinese Expert Consensus on the Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Traumatic Brain Injury was published in 2021 to provide guidance for standardized clinical management in China [16].

In China, pediatric intensive care units (PICUs) provide the primary setting for managing TBI in children, with PICU physicians playing a key role in initial care [17]. However, research has predominantly focused on acute TBI treatment and prognosis evaluation, leaving a gap in studies on prophylactic ASM. A systematic assessment of how PICU physicians in China understand, perceive, and implement prophylactic ASM for pediatric TBI is essential, considering the unique characteristics of the Chinese healthcare system and advancements in pediatric intensive care.

In this context, this study was conducted to gain insights into PTE management through a cross-sectional survey of multiple PICUs in Mainland China. The findings are expected to help improve clinical strategies for managing PTE.

Methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between December 2023 and March 2024. The study was carried out across 30 randomly selected research units at 69 hospitals affiliated with the China Futang Children’s Medical Development Center. The study workflow is presented in Fig. 1.

Study workflow. Flow diagram outlining the design, sampling, data collection, and analysis for the nationwide cross-sectional survey conducted to evaluate prophylactic anti-seizure medication (ASM) practices among pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) physicians in China. EEG, electroencephalogram; PTE, post-traumatic epilepsy

Sampling and participants

Stratified sampling was employed to ensure representation of physicians at different professional levels, including chief, deputy chief, attending, and resident physicians. Although the sample represented major pediatric PICUs in China, the generalizability of the findings was enhanced by including hospitals from rural and remote areas.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using a formula based on previous literature concerning prophylactic ASM usage in the TBI population. The calculation considered effect size, significance level (α = 0.05), and statistical power (1−β = 0.80), derived from prior studies. An initial sample size was calculated and then adjusted to account for an expected follow-up rate of 80%–90% and an additional 10%–20% to compensate for potential missing data. A final target sample size range of 252 to 280 cases was determined. This estimation is currently under review by the SingHealth Centralized Institutional Review Board (CIRB 2018/2457 and CIRB 2018/2076).

Data collection

Data were collected using a questionnaire designed to assess physicians’ awareness, current practices, medication protocols, and perceptions regarding the effectiveness of prophylactic ASM in pediatric TBI patients. The questionnaire specifically evaluated the knowledge of PICU physicians in Mainland China (see supplementary Table 1), covering key aspects such as ASM use, drug selection, timing of administration, adverse effects, risk factors for PTE, and monitoring practices. To ensure anonymity and enhance response rates, participants were required to complete the questionnaire online via a WeChat QR code provided to them. No personal information was collected.

Data management and statistical analyses

Data entry was performed by two researchers using a double-entry method in Microsoft Excel to ensure accuracy. The SAS software was used to validate the data entries. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 20.0). Quantitative data were summarized using descriptive statistics, including mean and standard deviation. Qualitative data were analyzed for distribution characteristics and are presented as frequency, composition ratio, and percentage.

Results

Thirty tertiary hospital PICUs across 23 provinces and cities in Mainland China, comprising 24 children’s specialty hospitals (80.0%) and 6 general hospitals (20.0%), were included in the study. Among them, 17 (56.7%) hospitals were certified by the China Trauma Care Alliance. A total of 271 questionnaires were collected, capturing responses from physicians with varying levels of experience: 41.0% (111/271) attending physicians, 10.0% (27/271) chief physicians, 18.8% (51/271) deputy chief physicians, and 30.2% (82/271) resident physicians.

ASM cognition and practice

Of the 271 PICU physicians surveyed, the vast majority (94.8%; 95% confidence interval (CI) [92.2%, 97.4%]) reported referring to the Chinese Expert Consensus for managing pediatric TBI (Fig. 2 A, B). A majority (72.7%; 95% CI [67.5%, 77.9%]) reported using prophylactic ASM (Fig. 2 A, B). The primary decision factors among ASM users were guidelines (46.9%), clinical expertise (27.3%), and neurosurgeon consultation (18.2%) (Fig. 2 C). Subgroup analysis by years of clinical practice and professional title revealed some variation in prophylactic ASM use. As shown in Table S2, the proportion of physicians who reported using prophylactic ASM ranged from 64.9% among those with 5–10 years of experience to 81.0% among those with less than 3 years of experience. By professional title, associate chief physicians had the highest rate of prophylactic use (84.3%), whereas attending physicians had the lowest (65.8%).

The duration of prophylactic ASM treatment varied considerably: 22.3% treated with ASM for up to 7 days, 18.9% continued until discharge, and 29.4% treated for 3 months post-TBI (Fig. 2D). A statistical comparison of adherence to guidelines between groups revealed a significant difference (χ²(1) = 6.694, p = 0.0097). The proportion of physicians referring to the consensus was significantly higher than that of physicians using ASM (z = 6.988, p < 0.0001), with a small effect size (Cohen’s h = 0.381).

These results indicate a high level of awareness of consensus guidelines among physicians but variable implementation in clinical practice, particularly regarding the use and duration of prophylactic ASM.

Selection of ASM

Among the 197 physicians surveyed regarding ASM prescription for TBI management, levetiracetam was the most commonly prescribed medication (103/197, 52.3%), followed by phenobarbital (46/197, 23.4%), sodium valproate (44/197, 22.3%), and other drugs (included midazolam (n = 2) and clonazepam (n = 2)) (4/197, 2.0%) (Fig. 3A). The distribution of ASM preference differed significantly from an equal distribution (χ²(3) = 57.834, p < 0.001), indicating a clear preference for levetiracetam.

Multi-panel analysis of practices for selecting the anti-seizure medication (ASM). A ASM prescription preferences. B Comparison of therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) practices. C Factors influencing the combination therapy choice. D Two-drug combination therapies preferred by physicians using multidrug regimens

Among levetiracetam prescribers, the dosage range was 20–60 mg/kg/day, with 73.8% (76/103) prescribing 20 mg/kg/day, 12.6% (13/103) prescribing 40 mg/kg/day, and 13.6% (14/103) prescribing 60 mg/kg/day (Fig. 2B). The distribution of dosage preferences did not differ significantly from an equal distribution (χ²(2) = 0.000, p = 1.000).

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) varied substantially between medications: 34.8% (16/46) of physicians monitored phenobarbital levels, whereas 95.5% (42/44) monitored sodium valproate (z =−6.011, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3B). Notably, phenobarbital was exclusively administered via intramuscular injection for prophylactic treatment.

Combination therapy was used by 30% (59/197) of physicians. The primary factors influencing this decision were TBI severity (42/59, 71.2%) and clinical experience (14/59, 23.7%), whereas neurosurgeon recommendations (1/59, 1.7%) and personal habits (2/59, 3.4%) had minimal impact (Fig. 3C). The most common combinations were levetiracetam plus sodium valproate (27/59, 45.7%), levetiracetam plus phenobarbital (25/59, 42.4%), and phenobarbital plus sodium valproate (7/59, 11.9%) (Fig. 3D).

The distribution of factors influencing combination therapy did not differ significantly from an equal distribution (χ²(3) = 0.000, p = 1.000). Overall, these results highlight a clear preference for levetiracetam, marked disparities in TDM practices, particularly for phenobarbital, and combination therapy decisions guided primarily by patient-specific factors, especially TBI severity.

Occurrence and treatment of ASM side effects

Among the 197 physicians administering prophylactic ASM, 95.4% (188/197) reported monitoring for adverse drug reactions (ADRs; Fig. 4A), and 40.6% (80/197) encountered ADRs (Fig. 4B), including liver dysfunction, rash, pharmacokinetic interactions, renal impairment, and cognitive effects.

Multi-panel analysis of the occurrence and management of the side effects of anti-seizure medication (ASM). A Physicians’ intention to monitor adverse drug reactions (ADRs). B Occurrence of drug-related adverse reactions. C Management approach when ADRs occurred. D Actions taken by physicians who continued treatment despite ADRs

When ADRs occurred, 15.0% (12/80) of physicians discontinued ASM, whereas 85.0% (68/80) continued treatment (Fig. 4C). Among those who continued treatment, 55.9% (38/68) adjusted the ASM regimen, 11.8% (8/68) reduced the dosage, and 32.4% (22/68) continued the ongoing treatment pending specialist evaluation (Fig. 4D).

Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference between discontinuation and continuation rates (χ²(1) = 20.769, p < 0.001), whereas the distribution of actions among physicians who continued treatment did not differ significantly from an equal distribution (χ²(2) = 0.000, p = 1.000).

These findings reveal a high level of awareness regarding ADR monitoring and indicate that most physicians continued ASM therapy with flexible management strategies, primarily by adjusting the regimen.

Knowledge of PTE risk factors

Among the 271 physicians surveyed, 64.2% identified age as a significant risk factor for PTE, with 57.5% of these respondents associating younger age with higher risk (Fig. 5A). Recognition of injury site as a determinant of PTE was reported by 63.1% of participants, with the temporal lobe most frequently (57.9%) cited as the most susceptible location (Fig. 5C).

C Identification of injury site as a risk factor. D Trauma type risk beliefs. E High-risk intracranial lesion types. F Identification of severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) as a risk factor

A strong consensus was observed regarding trauma type, with 86.5% of physicians considering open injuries to pose a greater risk than closed injuries (Fig. 5D). Among intracranial lesion types, diffuse axonal injury and intracerebral hemorrhage were equally identified as top risk factors (33.7% each) (Fig. 5E). Additionally, 53.3% of respondents considered severe TBI, as reflected by low Glasgow Coma Scale scores (supplementary Table 2), to be a primary risk factor for PTE (Fig. 5F).

Overall, physicians demonstrated good recognition of established PTE risk factors, with particularly strong consensus on the risks associated with open injuries and temporal lobe involvement. However, recognition of the contribution of severe TBI was moderate, highlighting a potential area for educational reinforcement.

As shown in Tables 1 and 57.2% of physicians reported routinely performing bedside EEG monitoring, while 11.4% ordered EEG only when clinically indicated, and 4.1% did not perform EEG due to equipment unavailability. Among those who ordered EEG selectively, the most common indications were seizure-related conditions (5.2% of all respondents) and severe neurological injury (5.2%), with other reasons accounting for 1.1%.

Discussion

PTE in children after TBI is a major clinical concern. Significant variability in the choice and duration of ASM for seizure prophylaxis has been reported among PICUs in both the United States and francophone regions, despite general acceptance of its value [18, 19]. Differences among practices may arise from physician experience, resource availability, and familiarity with clinical guidelines, emphasizing the importance of developing new strategies to prevent seizures in pediatric TBI.

Our survey yielded a response rate of 41.0% from attending physicians, reflecting current clinical practices in Mainland China. Significant variability in PTE prevention practices was identified across PICUs, with inconsistent patterns related to ASM selection, timing, initiation protocols, side effect management, and risk determination. These differences are likely influenced by sociodemographic factors, healthcare disparities, and regional practice variations [20, 21].

Temkin demonstrated in the 1990 s that prophylactic phenytoin reduced the rate of early post-traumatic seizures (EPTS) from 14.2% to 3.6% [20]. Subsequent studies confirmed that prophylactic ASM can reduce the risk of EPTS [15, 22]. Our survey was largely consistent with these reports, showing that many clinicians practice ASM prophylaxis in TBI patients. However, no clinical trial has yet explored the hypothesis that ASM can prevent EPTS in children. Data from the latest Cochrane review indicate that although ASM use may reduce the risk of EPTS, there is currently no strong evidence that it prevents epilepsy at two years or improves survival [22]. This underscores the need for long-term studies to determine whether ASM can reduce both early and late seizures and improve neurodevelopmental outcomes, quality of life, and safety in children. In this context, longitudinal studies are especially relevant to define the long-term effects of ASM and provide stronger evidence for clinical decision-making.

Our survey also demonstrated that while most PICU clinicians actively use prophylactic ASM, significant variability exists regarding the optimal duration and discontinuation criteria for therapy. In the United Kingdom and Ireland in 2017, 60% of participants were uncertain about the efficacy of ASM prophylaxis, and 53% did not use it consistently. Furthermore, 90% of physicians were unsure about the appropriate duration of therapy, with practices ranging from one week (38%) to three months (42%) [23, 24]. In agreement with our results, more than half of the physicians preferred prolonged ASM treatment due to concerns about seizure recurrence and epilepsy development. Interestingly, fewer than 25% discontinued treatment after 7 seizure-free days, suggesting a pragmatic treatment approach. These data highlight the challenges associated with TBI-related epilepsy management and emphasize the need for clinicians to be better informed through clear guidelines, professional support, and evidence-based recommendations.

Additionally, there is no consensus on the most effective ASM for seizure prevention after TBI. Although phenytoin has been traditionally used, levetiracetam is increasingly employed because of its better pharmacokinetic profile, minimal drug–drug interactions, and lower hepatotoxicity compared with phenobarbital [25, 26]. Our survey results align with reports from other countries showing declining use of phenytoin and increasing use of levetiracetam in North America and Europe. This shift may be due to improved understanding of brain injury mechanisms and neuroplasticity. Because levetiracetam acts on the SV2A protein, it not only modifies synaptic transmission but also provides neuroprotective effects against excitotoxicity [27–29]. Recent data even suggest a beneficial effect on neuroinflammation and neuronal recovery after TBI, making it an excellent candidate for seizure prophylaxis in children [30].

However, phenytoin and other older antiepileptic drugs (AEDs) are still more frequently used in developing countries. An international multicenter study involving 28 pediatric PICUs demonstrated this to be true, at least for South America (under review by CIRB 2018/2457 and CIRB 2018/2076). This inequality is primarily due to issues related to drug supply, cost, national infrastructure, and physician familiarity. Financial considerations and resource constraints play a central role in treatment decisions. To minimize these disparities, interventions such as subsidies, international collaborations to reduce costs, and regionally adapted guidelines should be encouraged to standardize care across all areas. Specific to phenytoin, its limited use in our survey may reflect several factors: complex pharmacokinetics and drug–drug interactions [31], concerns about adverse effects (e.g., hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias) [32], and the increasing availability of newer ASMs with favorable safety profiles [33]. Institutional protocols and regional prescribing habits may also contribute.

It is worth noting that in our survey, phenobarbital was reported to be administered exclusively via the intramuscular route. This practice may reflect limited availability of intravenous phenobarbital formulations in some Chinese pediatric centers, particularly in resource-limited settings [34]. Pharmacokinetic studies have demonstrated that intramuscular phenobarbital achieves rapid and complete absorption in children [35]. Additionally, oral administration is often not feasible during the acute phase of pediatric TBI due to altered mental status, intubation, or nil-per-os orders. Under such circumstances, intramuscular injection may serve as a practical alternative when intravenous access is unavailable. These observations further illustrate the impact of drug formulation availability and clinical context on ASM selection and administration in real-world practice.

Our study also revealed significant variation in maintenance ASM dosages. Although most physicians preferred to use 20 mg/kg/day of levetiracetam as a standard dose, approximately half prescribed higher doses (40–60 mg/kg/day) for more effective control. This variation reflects individualized clinical judgment based on physician experience rather than standardized dosing practices from evidence-based guidelines.

Adverse reactions to ASM are another important consideration. Adverse reactions were monitored by 95.4% of physicians, and approximately one-third reported experiencing them in clinical settings. The most frequent adverse events included hepatotoxicity, skin rash, pharmacokinetic drug–drug interactions (DDIs), renal dysfunction, and cognitive decline. In this context, valproate warrants particular attention due to its association with dose-related thrombocytopenia, with reported incidence rates of 5%–40% in pediatric populations [36]. This is especially concerning in TBI patients, who may have coexisting coagulation abnormalities or intracranial hemorrhage. However, valproate may still be selected in certain scenarios due to its broad-spectrum efficacy and low cost. The decision likely involves an individualized risk–benefit assessment, weighing thrombocytopenia risk against the consequences of uncontrolled seizures. Close platelet monitoring is essential when valproate is used. In addition, the lack of standardized approaches for managing these complications contributes to differences in clinical responses, diagnostic delays, and inconsistent treatment strategies.

The occurrence of PTE after TBI correlates with years of neurological disability. Several risk factors—such as subdural hematoma, cerebral contusion, and severe TBI—contribute significantly to the incidence of PTE [30]. Notably, our survey revealed good physician awareness of these risks, with most respondents considering age, injury type, lesion location, and injury severity when developing seizure prevention strategies. These findings indicate that frontline clinicians have a sound understanding of PTE prevention and are willing to implement appropriate interventions, even if limited by the lack of evidence-based protocols.

Finally, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the number of valid responses from voluntary participants and the self-selected nature of the sample may introduce selection bias. Second, treatment choices largely reflect the practices of attending physicians in participating institutions, which could limit generalizability. Third, the lack of long-term follow-up data is another major limitation, as it prevents evaluation of the sustained efficacy and safety of ASM. Fourth, although our survey defined combination ASM use as prophylactic combination therapy, it did not distinguish between simultaneous initiation of two agents and sequential addition of a second agent during the prophylactic period. While both scenarios represent combination prophylaxis rather than treatment escalation, they may reflect different clinical rationales and risk perceptions. Future studies with more granular data collection are needed to explore these nuances and to establish correlations between specific ASMs and their associated adverse drug reactions. Additionally, prospective studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to investigate late-onset seizures, cognitive and neurodevelopmental outcomes, and potential long-term side effects of prophylactic ASM treatment in pediatric TBI patients.

Conclusion

Given the considerable variability in current practices, we recommend the national development of evidence-based guidelines for the standardized use of prophylactic anti-seizure medication in pediatric TBI. Such guidelines should be formulated and periodically revised by a multidisciplinary task force and complemented with training programs to ensure their universal implementation across China’s PICUs. Furthermore, the effectiveness of these protocols should be validated through the collection of robust data and continuous evaluation to guide evidence-based clinical decision-making.
