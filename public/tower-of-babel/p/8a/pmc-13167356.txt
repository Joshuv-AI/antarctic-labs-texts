Leadership and healthcare professionals’ role in hand hygiene compliance: Identifying risk factors at surgical clinics of the university clinical center NIS, Serbia
Authors: Mirjana Marinkovic, Miodrag Djordjevic, Vukica Djordjevic, Vesna Karanikolic, Sonja Novak, Jelena Perisic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHospital-associated infections (HAIs) caused by multidrug-resistant organisms represent a global threat to public health. Despite various strategies, compliance with effective hand hygiene (HH) remains inconsistent. This study examines risk factors influencing hand hygiene compliance (HHC) in surgical clinics of the University Clinical Center Nis, Serbia.MethodsA cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study was conducted from August 16 to September 16, 2023, among healthcare professionals in surgical, urgent care, and anesthesia units at UCC Nis. The representative sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1. Effect sizes were assessed with Hedges’ g, Glass’s Δ, η2, and R2 for correlations. Regression analyses were performed to model HHC scores.ResultsParticipants demonstrated relatively good HH knowledge. HHC scores varied most across organizational units, with large effect sizes for subscales (η2 = 0.13-0.2) and intermediate effect for the total score. Total HHC did not significantly correlate with job satisfaction (r = -0.023) or Leadership trait score (r = 0.041), though some subscales showed low but significant correlations. Job satisfaction and Leadership traits were moderately correlated (R = -0.579, p < 0.001). Of seven regression models, only the model predicting “transmission of microorganisms to healthcare workers” achieved R2 > 0.3 (intermediate effect).ConclusionsHH knowledge and practices in surgical settings appear to depend more on the work environment, particularly leadership, than on individual characteristics. Sustained improvements in hand hygiene behavior require comprehensive, tailored educational programs and robust promotional campaigns, while strengthening managerial leadership skills emerges as a critical, evidence-based strategy. Job satisfaction showed only limited influence.Keywords: hand hygiene, hospital-acquired infections, surgical clinics

Introduction

Hospital-associated infections (HAIs) caused by multidrug-resistant organisms represents a global threat to human health and well-being. Because transmission of pathogens to patients often occurs via contaminated hands of healthcare personnel during health care, hand hygiene is considered the single most important measure for preventing HAIs. Although various strategies have been deployed to combat this problem in many countries, compliance with regular and effective hand hygiene practices remains inconsistent.1–4 Also, the economic implications of HAIs leading to substantial healthcare costs are significant.5,6

Research indicates a disparity in the exposure to HAIs between patients in lower-income versus higher-income countries. 7 Effective control of hospital infections is one of the priorities of the hospital’s quality management system in Serbia, Europe. All hospitals should implement a Quality Management System that refers to the revised international standard ISO 9001:2015 and HAIs are included on the list of possible adverse events for which appropriate corrective and preventive actions are designed. As the only non-European Union (EU) country Serbia - participated in a second point prevalence survey of HAIs and antimicrobial use organized by the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control in 2016-2017. 8 Patients undergoing surgical procedures are at increased risk of acquiring HAIs and the prevalence of HAI in the group of adult surgical patients from 61 Serbian acute-care hospitals was almost two and half times higher than in non-operated patients. 9 HAIs are often seen as a direct consequence of low hand hygiene compliance, as can be seen in many healthcare settings.10,11

By launching national campaign for hand-hygiene in healthcare settings entitled “Clean Hands - Safe Hands“ Serbia became a member of the Alliance for Patient Safety of the World Health Organization, but still Serbian healthcare workers showed only basic knowledge about hand hygiene. 12 Study of hygiene practices in surgical wards 13 in 9 European countries (among which was also Serbia) was marked interhospital variation in hand hygiene compliance (overall compliance 40%). Physicians’ hand hygiene practices were poor compared to nurses’, as well as behavioral factors, surgical subspecialty, professional category, type of care activity, and workload were independently associated with compliance. Thus improving hand hygiene compliance is still a major challenge for health care institution in Serbia and innovative approaches are needed to improve practice. As in many other countries, hand hygiene improvement strategies in Serbian health care institution have been concentrated on the health care professional or focused on the introduction of new products and facilities (organisational level). 14 Specific team-oriented activities, which include the responsibilities of the managers, are rarely applied within these strategies. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study in Serbia assessing the role of leadership and healthcare professionals in hand hygiene compliance.

The University Clinical Center Nis (UCC Nis) is a tertiary-level, regionally referent healthcare institution serving a catchment population of approximately two million inhabitants. Within its organizational structure, UCC Nis includes the Department for Sanitary and Epidemiological Surveillance, staffed by 15 healthcare professionals (five epidemiologists, one nurse, eight sanitary-environmental engineers, and one environmental technician). Surveillance of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) is regulated by an institutional protocol and is carried out by the Department in cooperation with the designated physician and nurse from each organizational unit. The implementation of prescribed preventive measures is the responsibility of the head of each clinic (physician) and the clinic’s chief nursing officer. The protocol defines the scope of surveillance activities, communication pathways, and reporting procedures. Identified challenges are presented to, and subsequently addressed by, the institutional HAI Commission. In accordance with the protocol, both routine and extraordinary surveillance activities are conducted by an epidemiologist in collaboration with sanitary-environmental engineers. For this study, hand-hygiene data were collected using a validated WHO hand-hygiene questionnaire and complementary leadership and job-satisfaction surveys; no direct observational audits were performed as part of the study protocol.

As in many other countries, hand hygiene improvement strategies in Serbian health care institution have been concentrated on the health care professional or focused on the introduction of new products and facilities (organizational level). 14 Specific team-oriented activities, which include the responsibilities of the managers, are rarely applied within these strategies.

The study aims to explore HHC risk factors in Serbia, in particular HHC knowledge and organizational aspects, and to provide recommendations for guidelines to enhance HHC among healthcare workers, thereby addressing a significant research gap and contributing to global efforts in HAI prevention.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This study was a cross-sectional questionnaire-based study carried out in a tertiary care teaching hospital of the University Clinical Center Nis, Serbia, from August 16 to September 16, 2023. The study population consisted of healthcare professionals from surgical departments, including units specializing in urgent care and anesthesia. Hand-hygiene data were self-reported using validated questionnaires; no direct observational audits were performed in this study, therefore results may be affected by social-desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect.

Material

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted on a stratified random sample of healthcare workers employed at the University Clinical Center (UCC) Nis. Stratification was performed proportionally according to Organizational Units (OUs), i.e., clinical departments, to ensure adequate representation across all major units of the institution.

Baseline demographic and professional characteristics of participants (gender, profession, age, years of service, and place of living) are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Participant flow is shown in Figure 1.

Participant flow diagram.

Inclusion criteria were: over 18 years of age, doctor, nurse/technician, employed at the Clinical Center Nis (UCC Nis) for more than 6 months, employed in surgical clinics, anesthesia, and emergency center, no interruption in continuous work for one year or more, voluntary participation. No formal exclusion criteria were applied. During recruitment, however, 150 invited individuals were not included in the final sample because they were not present at work during the study period or they declined to participate.

Sample size and power calculation

The sample size was determined a priori based on the most statistically demanding planned analysis, namely a two-tailed t test of a regression coefficient (slope) within a linear regression framework. The anticipated effect size was defined as a standardized regression coefficient (β) of 0.15, corresponding to a small-to-moderate effect size.

The significance level (α error probability) was set at 0.05, and the desired statistical power (1 - β error probability) at 0.90. Based on these parameters, the minimum required sample size was calculated using G*Power software version 3.1 (Franz Faul, Germany), 15 yielding an estimated requirement of 459 participants.

The expected response rate of approximately 75% required an increase of 150 invited participants. An additional 41 healthcare workers were invited to further safeguard against potential non-response and incomplete data, resulting in a target sample size of 650 individuals.

Final sample and response rate

The final realized sample consisted of 500 participants. Based on the originally specified parameters (β = 0.15, α = 0.05, two-tailed test), the achieved statistical power for this sample size was approximately 0.94. The overall response rate was 77%. Response rates varied across organizational units; however, proportional stratification was preserved, and this imbalance is unlikely to have introduced systematic bias or to have materially affected the validity or generalizability of the study findings.

Data collection tools

Data were collected via three self-administered questionnaires:

Hand hygiene knowledge questionnaire for health-care workers

To assess knowledge on the essential aspects of hand hygiene, an anonymous survey was conducted based on the WHO’s “Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire for Health-Care Workers” (revised August 2009). 16 The questionnaire, consisting of a total of 25 items, includes a first section that collects socio-demographic data (11 questions), a question on staff training, and 9 questions that assess theoretical knowledge and, indirectly, hand hygiene practices related to the topic. The HHC scale contains eight questions. Some questions contain sub-questions, so the entire scale can be divided into five subscales: (1) knowledge about the transmission of microorganisms (MO) to patients, (2) knowledge about the transmission of MO to healthcare workers, (3) knowledge about the effectiveness of hand disinfection and washing procedures, (4) knowledge of situations where HH is required or not required, and (5) knowledge of what should be avoided in patient contact to prevent the transmission of MO. The three questions that are not part of the subscales relate to the source of microorganisms, the main route of transmission of MO from a patient to another patient or healthcare worker, and the time required for alcohol-based hand sanitizers to kill most microorganisms on the hands. The entire HHC scale consists of 25 items in total. For each correct answer, the respondent receives one point, so the theoretical maximum score is 25. The questionnaires were distributed by the head nurses of the departments, and after completion, they were returned in sealed envelopes to ensure respondent anonymity.

HHC instrument description: total HHC score range 0-25; subscale score ranges for HHC Situations in which disinfection is required (0-6), while for the other subscales score ranges (0-4).

The leadership trait questionnaire (LTQ)

To assess leadership perceptions the self-administered, previously validated and modified, the Leadership Trait Questionnaire (LTQ) 17 was implemented. The purpose of this questionnaire is to measure personal characteristics of leadership (Articulate, Perceptive, Self-confident, Self-assured, Persistent, Determined, Trustworthy, Dependable, Friendly, Outgoing, Conscientious, Diligent, Sensitive, Empathic) and it was completed by participants. Using the following scale, each individual indicate the degree to which he or she agrees or disagrees with each of the 14 statements below. Key: 1 = Strongly disagree, 2 = Disagree, 3 = Neutral, 4 = Agree, 5 = Strongly agree.

The job satisfaction survey (JSS)

With a 36 item, 18 nine facet scale to assess employee attitudes about the job and aspects of the job was also used. Each facet is assessed with four items, and a total score is computed from all items. A summated rating scale format is used, with six choices per item ranging from: 1= Disagree very much, 2=Disagree moderately, 3=Disagree slightly, 4=Agree slightly, 5=Agree moderately, 6=Agree very much. Items are written in both directions, so about half must be reverse scored. The nine facets are Pay, Promotion, Supervision, Fringe Benefits, Contingent Rewards (performance based rewards), Operating Procedures (required rules and procedures), Coworkers, Nature of Work, and Communication. Although the JSS was originally developed for use in human service organizations, it is applicable to all organizations.

Data collection procedure

Clinics’ Head nurses distributed questionnaires in empty envelopes, in which the completed questionnaires were returned as sealed envelopes, with the aim of ensuring the anonymity of the respondents. The random selection of respondents was intended to neutralize any selection bias; the anonymity of the respondents was meant to eliminate socially desirable responding.

Reporting

This manuscript was prepared following the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional studies. The completed STROBE checklist is provided as Supplementary File.

Questionnaires were distributed and returned in sealed envelopes and responses were anonymous to reduce socially desirable responding; nevertheless, self-reporting may still introduce social-desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect.

Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis.

Model assumptions (normality of residuals, multicollinearity, and heteroskedasticity) were checked prior to interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Effect size estimates were additionally calculated using the online effect size calculator available on the Psychometric website. 19

The results of the HHC knowledge test are presented as means and standard deviations (SD) for the total HHC score and its subscales. Since the scores are not directly comparable due to the different maximum possible scores of the subscales, the percentage of correct answers on each subscale is additionally reported to allow comparability.

The relationship between HHC knowledge and socio-demographic characteristics was tested using the independent-samples t test (for two groups), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for multiple groups), and Pearson’s correlation coefficient to assess associations between continuous variables.

Effect size was determined using Hedges’ g (for t tests with unequal groups), or Glass’s Δ (for t tests with unequal variances), and η2 for ANOVA, and the coefficient of determination (R2) for correlation analyses. 19

Incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the analysis; no systematic pattern of missing data was observed.

The combined relationship between the set of socio-demographic and other characteristics (which are not personal traits of the participants) and HHC scores was examined using multivariable regression analysis. Regression diagnostics indicated that model assumptions were met, with variance inflation factors below 3 and residuals showing no significant deviation from normality.

Model assumptions were assessed prior to interpretation of results, and statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

This study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Clinical Center Nis (Approval No. 30414/7; 4 October 2023).

All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and national legislation governing research on human subjects.

Prior to data collection, each participant provided written informed consent, and the confidentiality of all responses was strictly maintained.

All participants received oral and written information about the study objectives, methods, and confidentiality safeguards. Written informed consent was obtained from every healthcare professional prior to completing the survey.

Participation was voluntary, and no financial or other incentives were offered.

The questionnaires were completed by 170 males and 330 females. The fewest completed questionnaires were in anesthesia (10%), and the largest number of questionnaires in orthopedics (100%), endocrine surgery (86%) and ENT (75%).

For the other subscales, the effect is small (r = 0.10-0.29).

HHC score and HHC subscores by ‘Organizational Unit’.

When the scores on the HHC and scores of its subscales are examined by organizational units (clinics), it is observed that the scores on all subscales differ in at least one of the clinics. All differences are statistically significant at the p=0.001 level. The F-statistics (which describe how many times the explained variance is greater than the remaining variance) are relatively high, ranging from 4.4 to 11.6. The effect size measure (η2) falls into the ‘large’ effect category (0.13 - 0.2) for each of the six subscales. Only for the HHC total score is the effect slightly weaker, falling into the ‘intermediate’ effect category, Pearson’s r, (r = 0.30 - 0.49). However, what causes confusion is the fact that the means by clinic do not show any recognizable pattern. Clinics that have extremely high relative scores on one subscale show average or even low relative scores on other subscales. What further complicates the issue is that the scores by clinic are not consistent, so clinics, despite having relatively high or low scores, sometimes behave as poor predictors of the scores on the subscales.

Correlations of the HHC scale and its subscales with job satisfaction and leadership traits

For the variable ‘job satisfaction’, the average score is 106.22 (SD 17.41), with a minimum score of 67.75 (orthopedics) and a maximum score of 129.12 (eye clinic). The variable ‘leadership trait’ has an average score of 4.11 (SD 0.49), with a minimum of 2.80 (urology) and a maximum of 4.87 (orthopedics). The total score of the HHC scale does not correlate significantly with job satisfaction (r = -0.023) or with the leadership trait score (r = 0.041). However, some subscales show low but statistically significant correlation coefficients with these two variables. The leadership trait score correlates with the subscale ‘Transmission of Microorganisms to the Patient’ R = -0.215 (p < 0.001), with the subscale ‘Transmission of Microorganisms to Healthcare Workers’ R = 0.171 (p < 0.01), with the subscale ‘What Should Be Avoided’ R = -0.166 (p < 0.001), with the subscale’ Effectiveness of Disinfection and Handwashing’ R = 0.125 (p < 0.001), and with the subscale ‘Situations Requiring Disinfection’ R = 0.092 (p < 0.05). The only subscale that does not significantly correlate with the leadership trait score is the ‘Source and Route’ subscale.

The job satisfaction scale score correlates with the subscale ‘Transmission of Microorganisms to the Patient’ (r = 0.215, p < 0.001), ‘Transmission of Microorganisms to Healthcare Workers’ (r = 0.301, p < 0.001), with the subscale ‘Effectiveness of Disinfection and Handwashing’ (r = -0.223, p < 0.001), and with the subscale ‘Source and Route’ (r = 0.132, p = 0.003). The job satisfaction scale score and leadership trait score correlate with each other (r = -0.579, p < 0.001).

To explore the combined effects of various factors and their overall impact on the HHC score, seven regression analyses were conducted-one where the criterion variable was the total HHC score, and six more analyses for each HHC subscale separately. Different sets of variables were used as predictors to arrive at the most efficient model. Numerical variables from the questionnaire and several ‘dummy’ variables created from different categories of the nominal variable ‘Organizational Unit’, as well as the variables Leadership trait and Job satisfaction, were used. Since these two latter variables are highly correlated with each other, they cannot have significant coefficients within the same model. If each variable is a significant predictor in the model, both variants are presented.

Regression models for the HHC scale score and its subscale scores.

For each of the HHC subscales (Table 7), a predictive model was created to link the scale score with a set of predictor variables. In the regression analysis, variables were entered into the model using the enter method. In the presented regression models, only variables with statistically significant regression coefficients (standardized β) were retained. Models in which the dependent variables were the HHC total score, “what should be avoided to prevent infection,” and “source and route of infection” produced a very low R2 (less than 0.1) and are treated as having no effect on the dependent variable, despite their statistical significance.

Models predicting scores on the scales “transmission of microorganisms to the patient”, “Effectiveness of disinfection and handwashing” and “situations requiring disinfection and handwashing” have somewhat higher R2 values (0.135, 0.122, and 0.216, respectively), which suggest the presence of a small effect. The model predicting the score on the scale “transmission of microorganisms to the healthcare worker” has an R2 of 0.303, which falls within the range of an intermediate effect size, indicating that this score can be predicted more effectively-based on the chosen set of predictor variables-than the scores on the other subscales.

Predictor variables in the most successful model (midwifery, orthopedics, vascular surgery) do not appear in the next most successful model (years of work experience, digestive surgery, age, anesthesiology, thoracic surgery, formal HH training), except for vascular surgery. Vascular surgery appears as a predictor in three of the four successful regression models, but in each model it has the smallest regression coefficient. Digestive surgery is an important predictor in three successful models, but it is absent from the most successful one. Several variables appear as predictors in at least two successful models: midwife (a proxy for the gynecology and obstetrics clinic), orthopedics, and anesthesiology. However, both midwife and anesthesiology appear with positive coefficients in one model (predicting a higher score) and negative coefficients in another (predicting a lower score).

The job satisfaction variable is a significant predictor in the models where the dependent variables are: “Transmission of microorganisms to the patient”, “Statements about disinfection”, “What should be avoided” and “Source and route of transmission”.

The leadership trait variable appears as a statistically significant predictor in two regression models: the model where the dependent variable is the subscale “Transmission of microorganisms to the patient” and the model where the dependent variable is “What should be avoided”. Both variables are predictors in the models with “Transmission of microorganisms to the patient“ and “What should be avoided” as dependent variables, but only when one is included at a time. Including one automatically excludes the other. Including job satisfaction slightly improves the model with the dependent variable “Transmission of microorganisms to the patient” and slightly weakens the model with “What should be avoided”. However, its overall explanatory power remains limited compared to leadership traits, which retain greater importance in line with the main findings.

All our results suggest that organizational units are an important factor in creating and maintaining knowledge about hand hygiene (statistically significant differences in HHC scores, regression equation coefficients), but also that their impact often affects different aspects of knowledge (measured by different subscales) and operates in opposing directions (sometimes promoting certain types of knowledge, and other times suppressing them). Therefore, more research attention should be given to organizational culture and the work atmosphere within organizational units to better understand the differences in knowledge and hand hygiene practices.

Discussion

The analyzed sample is representative of the surgical clinics at the University Clinical Center Nis, based on its characteristics. The sample reflects the broader healthcare system, with a predominance of nurses, female respondents, aged between 32-41, and with an average work experience of 15 years. In comparison, other studies show that nurses tend to be older, with more experience and formal training, thus demonstrating better compliance.3,4,13 The majority of respondents live in the city, and two-thirds of them have received formal hand hygiene training.

Respondents demonstrated good knowledge of hand hygiene, which is consistent with findings reported in the literature. 20 In our sample, a significant majority (92%) reported regular use of alcohol-based hand hygiene products. However, there were no statistically significant differences in knowledge, attitudes, or adherence to protocols by seniority, age, or gender; previous training did not show significant effects across all HHC subscales (results from the present study). Good knowledge does not always translate into practice: leadership, availability of supplies, and workload are important mediating factors, 20 as it was indicated in the literature. The study describes these mechanisms and therefore provides conceptual (thematic) alignment with our quantitative findings.

This indicates that education should not be viewed as a one-time transfer of information but as a continuous behavioral intervention equally necessary for all healthcare workers in surgical clinics, regardless of their seniority or previous training.20,21 The correlation of responses across different clinics provides strategically important insights. Based on the results, special attention should be given to specific clinics, while others could serve as role models. Similar results were found in a study by Japanese authors. 22 Statistically significant differences were found between clinics and professions, with an unexpected finding being the low level of knowledge among midwives. This contrasts with the literature, which indicates that anesthesiologists typically exhibit the lowest adherence to hand hygiene practices. 23

The absence of such disparities in our study suggests a more uniform pattern of practice within our setting.

The fact that previous training did not produce significant improvements highlights a systemic need to evolve education beyond basic protocols.

Future programs should emphasize the specific pathways of microorganism transmission and the critical role of each healthcare worker, ensuring that high knowledge consistently translates into high compliance across all specialties.20–23

Only 40% of respondents were aware of the most common sources of microorganisms responsible for healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) in healthcare facilities. Many were also unclear about key moments for handwashing and the fact that hand hygiene protects both the patient and the healthcare worker. Furthermore, it is essential to stress the importance of both alcohol-based hand sanitizers and handwashing with soap and water. While the disinfectant is faster, it may not always be a more effective alternative. Both methods should be used appropriately depending on the context.

A particularly concerning finding was that some employees believed no hand hygiene action was needed when working with patients. It is especially troubling that the importance of washing hands after glove removal was not fully recognized. Additionally, some studies 20 found no significant difference between beliefs and knowledge about hand hygiene, suggesting that knowledge alone is not enough to change employees’ beliefs. It is crucial to focus on other factors and strengthen the practical application of this knowledge in training curricula. 11 The hand hygiene of healthcare workers can be improved with the support and full commitment of managers at all levels,17,18 and our study findings align with these results. Specifically, our results highlight the need to implement WHO’s “Five Moments” training protocol, marketing and promotional strategies, role models, and systems of rewards and punishments. Hand hygiene remains the leading preventive measure against HAI,3,16 and various authors have investigated different aspects of this phenomenon. 2 One important intervention is the “nurse link” 11 and a focus on training. 6 Other studies emphasize that handwashing rates are low among healthcare workers, 12 and that re-education and proper handwashing equipment are crucial, as shown by authors from Oman in their national project, 24 which also emphasizes the success of role models.

Our results did not show a significant correlation between knowledge and professions, which contrasts with findings from other authors12,22 who reported lower compliance among doctors, and with studies from Oman 24 indicating weaker adherence among nurses and non-medical staff. In relation to leadership, our data revealed only limited correlations with specific subscales of hand hygiene knowledge, suggesting that leadership traits may influence certain aspects of practice rather than overall compliance. Nevertheless, the literature consistently highlights the importance of leadership in shaping hand hygiene behavior,20,25,26 and our findings conceptually align with this evidence. Strengthening managerial leadership skills therefore remains a strategically important recommendation for improving hand hygiene and preventing HAIs.

Based on the findings of our study, organizational conditions and leadership quality play a major role in sustaining hand-hygiene practice. Targeted organizational actions and stronger leadership consistently improve adherence to hand-hygiene measures.

These results have direct implications for Serbia’s regulatory framework. The Regulation on Prevention, Early Detection and Control of Hospital Infections 27 should include a section on management of hand-hygiene programmes, with systematic training and ward-level evaluations. The Regulation on Accreditation of Health Care Institutions 28 should add quality indicators for hand hygiene as an accreditation criterion, and the Law on the Protection of the Population from Infectious Diseases 29 could provide a legal basis for mandatory national hand-hygiene programmes.

The Decree on the National Programme for the Control of Antibacterial Resistance 30 would be strengthened if hand hygiene were explicitly recognised as a primary preventive measure. At the institutional level, the University Clinical Center Nis Programme on HAI prevention (2023) 31 can serve as a pilot model for combining leadership interventions with structured training.

These changes would translate our findings into sustainable policies that connect individual practice with system-level measures, creating a foundation for long-term improvements in patient safety and fewer hospital-acquired infections.

Relation to prior work

The majority of published research has focused on general hospital wards, intensive care units, or large tertiary-care centers, identifying factors such as workload, availability of supplies, staff perceptions, and organizational climate as contributors to compliance variability. However, evidence specifically addressing surgical clinics-particularly in the context of healthcare systems in Southeast Europe-is limited. Existing studies rarely combine leadership behaviors, individual healthcare professional practices, and structural risk factors within a single analytical framework.

The present study provides several novel contributions. First, it focuses exclusively on surgical clinics, a setting where workflow intensity and specific procedural risks may influence compliance differently than in other hospital departments. Second, the study integrates leadership-related indicators with behavioral and environmental factors, offering a more comprehensive assessment than previously reported. Third, this research is, to our knowledge, the first to examine hand hygiene compliance at the University Clinical Center Nis, Serbia, and the region, providing context-specific data for a large regional healthcare institution in Serbia. Finally, the identification of distinct risk factors within this setting adds new evidence that may support the development of targeted interventions for improving compliance in surgical environments.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths of this study include a large sample size (n=500) with a high response rate (77%), the approach combining a validated WHO hand-hygiene questionnaire with leadership and job-satisfaction surveys, and the focus on surgical clinics at a major Serbian tertiary center-addressing an important regional research gap. The study did not include direct observational audits; compliance estimates are based on validated self-report instruments.

Limitations include the single-center, cross-sectional design that precludes causal inference.

This study relied on self-reported questionnaire data and did not include direct observational audits; consequently, results may be affected by social-desirability bias and the Hawthorne effect. Participants may overreport compliance or knowledge due to perceived expectations, and therefore the reported HHC scores could overestimate actual hand-hygiene behaviour in clinical practice. These limitations restrict causal inference and the generalizability of findings; future studies should incorporate direct observational audits or objective measures (e.g., product consumption, electronic monitoring) to validate self-reported compliance.

The results cannot be generalized beyond the Clinical Center of Nis (UCC Nis). They are only applicable to healthcare workers at UCC who meet the eligibility criteria outlined in the “Materials and Methods” section.

Conclusion

Based on our results, factors such as gender, age, professional experience, and whether an individual lives in an urban or rural area do not significantly influence hand hygiene knowledge and practices. The individual characteristics of the respondents were not significantly related to differences in HHC scale scores or any of its subscales. The majority of correlations appeared only when respondents were grouped according to the organizational units in which they work. Our findings indicate that hand hygiene (HH) knowledge and practices are shaped more by the organizational environment (particularly leadership) than by individual sociodemographic characteristics.

However, leadership characteristics within the healthcare setting emerged as a crucial factor, demonstrating a significant relationship with hand hygiene compliance, particularly with regard to the use of alcohol-based disinfectants and the average hospital stay duration. Job satisfaction was observed to have a limited influence on hand hygiene behaviors, notably impacting specific aspects as captured in certain survey questions. The study also identified notable variations in hand hygiene awareness and adherence across different clinics, professional roles, and departments. This nuanced understanding allows for the development of targeted strategies aimed at enhancing hand hygiene compliance where it is most needed, leveraging departments and individuals with exemplary practices as benchmarks and role models. Based on associations between leadership characteristics and hand-hygiene compliance-particularly routine use of alcohol-based disinfectants-we recommend targeted leadership development and department-level interventions to enhance and sustain hand-hygiene compliance.

Key recommendations include the formulation of a comprehensive educational initiative tailored to promote hand hygiene, supported by a robust promotional campaign. Additionally, there is a call to strengthen the leadership qualities of all managerial staff, recognizing this as a pivotal, evidence-backed factor in improving hand hygiene practices.
