Effect of phosphate availability on the dynamics of polyphosphate accumulation in microalgae
Authors: Subhisha Raj, Arathi Sreenikethanam, M. Gobi, Deepak Sakate, Tharunkumar Jayakumar, Suchitra Rakesh, J. Rajesh Banu, Amit K. Bajhaiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phosphorus is one of the crucial elements required for the proper functioning of metabolic processes in microalgae. Despite the crucial role of phosphate (P), the dynamics of polyphosphate accumulation with respect to nutrient availability remain unknown in freshwater microalgae. We have investigated three freshwater microalgal strains – Chlorella pyrenoidosa, Scenedesmus obliquus, and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii under varied phosphate treatments to understand the phosphate metabolic responses and polyphosphate dynamics. Our results show that the accumulation of polyP in microalgae is very dynamic and mainly depends on the availability of extracellular phosphate. Reduced P availability showed algal species-specific reduction in the polyphosphate storage with a decline in growth and total chlorophyll content. Further, an increase in lipid and carbohydrate content with a substantial decrease in protein was observed under P stress, suggesting preferential utilization of stored polyP to support cell survival. Species-specific differences in the fatty acid profiles were also observed in the GC analysis among all three algal strains, indicating varied mechanisms happening among the species to adapt and protect themselves against cellular damage under P stress. Our results suggest the existence of natural variability among the selected algal strains in their ability to accumulate polyP and metabolites with respect to P-varied conditions. Among the three microalgal species, Scenedesmus obliquus showed notably enhanced accumulation of polyphosphate, highlighting its potential application as P-rich biofertilizer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-24985-7.

Introduction

Phosphorus is one of the most important macronutrients which is required for the proper growth and development in almost all organisms, including microalgae. This inevitable nutrient plays a crucial role in carrying out several metabolic processes in microalgae, like the synthesis of nucleic acids, phospholipids, proteins, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP)1. Additionally, P helps in maintaining the cell membrane integrity and cellular homeostasis in microalgae, even while regulating their gene expression and energy utilization2,3. Thus, variations in the external P supplementation can have a great impact on both the growth and metabolic processes in microalgae.

One of the notable adaptive features of microalgae is their ability to store P in the form of polyphosphates (polyP) internally within their cells. PolyP can be defined as linear polymers of orthophosphate units linked via high-energy phosphoanhydride bonds, and they are normally stored within acidic vacuoles called acidocalcisomes4. These polyP reserves inside the algal cells will help in temporarily uncoupling the phosphate uptake and their immediate metabolic demands, helping their survival during P scarcity. Apart from P storage, polyP also helps in stress tolerance by participating in metal ion sequestration, redox homeostasis, and osmotic regulation in microalgae5.

Recent studies, particularly in cyanobacteria, have highlighted the dynamic nature of polyP metabolism. They found out that the polyP accumulation is not only influenced by phosphorus concentration in their medium but also by the growth stage of those particular cyanobacteria. For instance, the cells may exhibit “luxury uptake,” in which they store excess phosphorus under high-P conditions, or they may also show “overplus response,” in which they rapidly accumulate a high amount of polyP upon P repletion (re-supplementation of P following prior starvation)6. However, when the P limitation extends for a longer period, these stored polyP reserves will be gradually used by them to support their essential cellular functions, which in turn can result in a reduction of their growth and photosynthetic pigments7. Despite such observations in cyanobacteria, polyphosphate dynamics in freshwater microalgae remain largely underexplored, especially under different phosphate concentrations.

In addition to phosphate storage, microalgal cells show great differences in their ability to accumulate primary metabolites like proteins, carbohydrates and lipids in response to P fluctuations. These metabolites are important components of microalgal structural and metabolic needs, and they also act as their critical energy reserves under various stress conditions8–11. Under P-limited conditions, it is observed that the cells will redirect the metabolic flow towards carbohydrate and lipid production, simultaneously limiting their growth as well as protein and pigment production12–14. These shifts in their metabolism are an essential part of their survival strategy under nutrient stress, which will help them to adapt and adjust to the challenging environments. However, studies on the integrated relationships between these metabolic adjustments and polyP dynamics in freshwater microalgae are very limited.

Besides, compared to cyanobacteria, polyP dynamics and their influence on the growth and metabolite profiles are poorly studied in freshwater microalgae. Furthermore, research based on this in a species-comparative framework is very limited. It is very important to understand these differences as the species-specific strategies might help in determining their ecological roles, phosphate recovery potential, as well as in the development of natural phosphate biofertilizers.

In the present study, the growth, biochemical responses and polyP dynamics of three common freshwater microalgal strains—Chlorella pyrenoidosa, Scenedesmus obliquus, and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii—were examined under varied phosphate concentrations. We have done a detailed time-course experiment in which the cultures were monitored every alternate day until they reached their stationary phase under gradually decreasing P concentrations and under phosphate-replete conditions. We have used a combination of techniques in order to get a detailed overview of their growth characteristics, total chlorophyll content, and metabolite accumulation patterns. The quantitative analysis of polyP was done via biochemical extraction, and they were visualized microscopically. We observed species-specific variations in the overall polyP dynamics in all three species that we analyzed. They exhibited variations in their phosphate uptake and polyP accumulation patterns. Among the three strains, Scenedesmus obliquus was able to accumulate the highest amount of polyP inside its cells both under P-standard and replete conditions, highlighting its potential for application in phosphate recovery and biofertilizer development.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

Single isolates of freshwater strains Chlorella pyrenoidosa and Scenedesmus obliquus were purchased from the National Collection of Industrial Microorganisms (NCIM), CSIR-National Chemical Laboratory (NCL), Pune, India and the model green microalgae Chlamydomonas reinhardtii CC125 was purchased from the Chlamydomonas Resource Centre, USA. All the strains were cultured in Tris Acetate Phosphate (TAP) medium under continuous illumination of cool white fluorescence lamps (≈ 100 µmol photons/m2 s) at 22 ± 1 °C in Being scientific plant growth chamber (MGC-450HP-2) with continuous shaking at 100 rpm. The axenic cultures were maintained on multiple plates using sterilized TAP solid medium containing 1.5% W/V agar. Cultures were routinely checked (once every two weeks) for contamination and were discarded if found contaminated.

Cultivation of microalgae in phosphate-deplete and replete conditions

The standard TAP media (1 L) was prepared by adding 1 ml phosphate buffer stock solution, which contains KH2PO4/K2HPO4 (0.4 M/0.6 M) along with other media constituents as described by Anderson et al., 201515. The phosphate-depleted media with total phosphate concentrations of 0.1mM, 0.01mM, and 0.001mM were prepared by adding 100 µl, 10 µl, and 1 µl of phosphate buffer, respectively, to the remaining TAP media constituents. The phosphate-depleted media were supplemented with the respective amount of 1 M KCl solution to maintain the potassium concentration. In the P-replete experiments, on the 5th day of their growth, the P-deplete (0.01 Mm P) cultures were supplemented with the phosphate buffer solution to get a final P concentration of 1mM (Standard TAP concentration) in the media. For all the experiments, the initial inoculum was grown for 4–5 days, and after normalizing the optical density (OD) at 700 nm to 0.5 OD, the cells were inoculated into 300 ml TAP media in the above-mentioned phosphate concentrations in triplicates. The inoculum size was kept at 1% v/v. Before inoculation, the cells were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for two minutes, and the pellet was washed twice using phosphate-free de-ionized water. OD of the cultures were recorded every alternate day till day 8 to monitor their growth pattern. On day 8 (stationary phase), the microalgal biomass was harvested via centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, cryopreserved and stored at −80 °C for metabolite analysis. Fresh algal cultures were used for both light and fluorescent microscopy.

Biochemical analysis of primary metabolites

Protein extraction and estimation

The protein content was estimated using Lowry’s method, and a protein standard curve was plotted using BSA (0 to 100 µg/ml). The collected algal cells were disrupted using alkaline solution-assisted ultrasonication at 80% amplitude for 2 min. After sonication, the biomass was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and the obtained supernatant was used for protein estimation. 1 ml of already prepared Lowry’s reagent was added to the collected supernatant (200 µl), and the resulting mixture was incubated for 10 min at room temperature (RT) after mixing thoroughly. After incubation, 100 µl of Folin–Ciocalteu Reagent (1n) was added, and samples were vortexed and incubated for 30 min at RT. After incubation, the absorbance of the resulting blue-colored solution was measured at 750 nm using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf AG 22331, Hamburg)16,17. The protein content of the sample was calculated using the equation given by Gonzalez et al., 201018.

Carbohydrate extraction and estimation

The phenol-sulfuric acid method was used to determine the carbohydrate content in algal samples19. In 1 mg algal biomass, 1 ml of 2.5 N HCl solution was added, and samples were incubated for 3 h in a boiling water bath. After incubation, the samples were neutralized by adding 1 ml 2.5 N NaOH. In 200 µl of the hydrolyzed samples, 200 µl of 5% phenol (w/v) and 1 ml conc. sulfuric acid (96%) was added. Finally, the samples were incubated at 35 °C for 30 min before recording their absorbance at 492 nm using a microplate reader (Analytical AT2960R, India). The carbohydrate concentration was estimated against the glucose standard curve (0 to 100 µg/ml)20,21.

Lipid estimation and visualization of lipid bodies

The total lipid content in the dry algal biomass was determined using the Bligh and Dyer method22. Using 50 ml of algal culture, the biomass pellet was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, and 3.75 ml of methanol: chloroform (2:1, v/v) mixture was added to dissolve the pellet. The samples were sonicated for 20 min in a water bath sonicator, followed by overnight incubation at RT. After incubation, chloroform and water were added to make the final concentration of methanol: chloroform: water to 1:1:0.5 (v/v/v). The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 3000 rpm at RT for 5 min to separate the organic (bottom) and aqueous (top) layers. The organic layer was carefully transferred to pre- weighed tubes, and incubated at 60 °C to evaporate the chloroform. After evaporation, the tubes were weighed in order to calculate the lipid % per biomass. Visualization of neutral lipid bodies in microalgal cells was performed using Nile red fluorescence microscopy as described by23. Nile Red staining solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg Nile Red in 1 ml DMSO (Dimethyl Sulphoxide) and diluting it to a working solution of 100 µg/ml. The microalgal cells were harvested and resuspended in distilled water to a desired density. The Nile Red solution was added to the cell suspension in a way that the final concentration becomes 1 µg/ml and it is then incubated in the dark for 10–15 min. The stained cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope (Radical RXLr-5) with excitation at 488 nm and emission around 570 nm. Cell sizes were calculated from the microscopy images using ImageJ software.

Total pigment estimation

The total chlorophyll and carotenoid content was determined spectrophotometrically24,25. 1 ml of day 8 culture was pelleted by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 5 min, to which 2 ml 100% methanol was added. The mixture was incubated in a water bath sonicator (50 kHz) for 20 min in order to break open the cells. Then the cell suspension was incubated at 60 °C for 30 min in a water bath, followed by cooling on ice for 10 min. Further, the samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant’s OD was measured at 470, 652, and 665 nm using a spectrophotometer (Eppendorf AG 22331, Hamburg). The total chlorophyll and carotenoid content was determined using Arnon’s equation26.

Phosphate and polyphosphate estimation in algal cultures

Polyphosphate quantification and visualization in cells

We used the polyphosphate estimation protocol by Mukherjee and Ray (2015)27 to quantify the polyphosphate content in the algal cells grown in P-deplete and P-replete conditions. According to this protocol, firstly, 10 ml of the collected algal cultures were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 min once they reached their stationary phase. After washing the pellet using autoclaved deionized water, they were resuspended in 600 µL deionized water. Then the samples were sonicated at 30–40 Hz for 10 min, and they were incubated in a boiling water bath for 2 h. These samples were added with 600 µL of chloroform: isoamyl alcohol (24:1, v/v) after cooling them to room temperature. The mixture was vortexed and centrifuged at 13,500 × g for 15 min, and the upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, to which 3 ml toluidine blue solution (30 mg/L) and 1 ml 0.2 N acetic acid solution was added. The absorbance of the resulting bluish- violet colored solution was measured at 663 nm using a microplate reader (Analytical AT2960R, India). The polyP content in each sample was estimated using a sodium polyphosphate glass type 45 calibration curve. The same protocol was followed in the P-replete experiment. However, in this experiment, the samples were collected on day 4, day 6 and day 8 for polyP quantification in order to investigate the polyP dynamics when cells are replenished with phosphate after phosphate starvation.

Further, the polyP visualization inside the algal cells was performed using the lead sulfide method28,29. 100 µL of algal culture was dried on a glass slide, and treated with lead nitrate solution (2.5 g Pb(NO₃)₂ in 100 ml 5% HNO₃) for 5 min. Slides were rinsed with deionized water, and treated with 18% ammonium sulfide for 15 s. Then the slides were again rinsed with deionized water and dried before examining under the light microscope (Olympus CX23, Japan) at 100x magnification.

Soluble phosphate estimation in culture media

In the phosphate-replete experiments, the soluble phosphate concentration in the culture medium was quantified using the molybdenum blue method30. Supernatants from algal cultures were collected by centrifugation, every alternate day till the 10th day of culture. To the supernatant, a working solution consisting of 2.5% (w/v) ammonium molybdate solution, 1% (w/v) ascorbic acid solution, and 5 N sulfuric acid in the 1:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated for 20 min for color development. Finally, the absorbance of the samples was measured at 880 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, Molecular Devices). The final phosphate concentration was calculated using KH₂PO₄ standard curve.

FTIR analysis

The sample preparation for FTIR was done according to the protocol proposed by Pejcic et al., 200931,32. One part of the completely dried algal biomass was mixed with 150 parts of potassium bromide (KBr), and the mixture was ground into fine powder using a mortar and pestle. A 135 mg mixture of the powder was compressed for 5 min under high pressure (5 to 6 bar) to obtain 1 mm-thick pellets. The transmission spectra of the algal-KBr pellets from 4400 to 400 cm−1 were measured using an FTIR spectrophotometer (SHIMADZU, IRTRACER 100).

The obtained original spectroscopic data was pre-processed for continuum removal using the prospectr package in R program. The cut-down spectra were plotted from 1760 to 950 cm-1. The Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using NIPALS algorithm was performed over the cut-down spectral range using ‘R’.

Gas chromatography (GC) analysis

Fatty acid composition in the samples was analyzed using ThermoFisher Scientific gas chromatograph model Trace 1110, equipped with a flame ionization detector (FID). The GC column, TG-5MS, measured 30 m in length, with a diameter of 0.25 millimeters and a film thickness of 0.25 micrometers, column allowed for on-column injection. The FID operated at 250 °C, with hydrogen flowing at a rate of 25 milliliters per minute, air at 100 milliliters per minute, and nitrogen as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 27 milliliters per minute. The initial oven temperature of the GC was set at 50 °C and then ramped up at a rate of 7 °C per minute until reaching 100 °C. Trichloromethane was used as the blank. A syringe was used to inject two microliters of the sample into the injector for fatty acid detection. Fatty acid peaks were recognized by comparing their retention times to those of external standards. Quantified fatty acids (expressed as % of total fatty acids) from biological replicates across four phosphate treatments (Standard, 0.1 mM_P, 0.01 mM_P, and 0.001 mM_P) were compiled into a data matrix.

From GC results, samples were grouped according to treatment and species, after which a hierarchical clustering heatmap was generated using ‘R’ software. The compiled data was first normalized by z-score transformation/autoscaling, which helped in addressing the differences in scale across features. During the heatmap generation, the distance matrix was computed using Euclidean distance, and clustering was performed using the ‘ward linkage’ method. This will help in grouping the samples and features with similar expression patterns.

Results

Culture growth and pigment content in P-deplete conditions

Firstly, we analyzed the effect of depleting phosphate concentrations on the growth of Chlorella pyrenoidosa, Scenedesmus obliquus and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, which were recorded by measuring their optical density at 700 nm every alternate day till they reached stationary phase (Fig. 1). The availability of phosphorus in the growth medium has a prominent effect on the growth and metabolism of microalgae, which can broadly impact their accumulation of metabolites. Thus, we observed that in all three species, there was a progressive decline in their growth with the decreasing phosphate availability in their media. All three strains exhibited a typical sigmoidal growth kinetics pattern under standard (1 Mm) P conditions. However, the inhibition in their growth became more evident with an increase in P limitation, i.e., growth was restricted in 0.01mM P and further in 0.001mM P treatment. Scenedesmus obliquus and Chlamydomonas reinhardtii showed strong dependency on the phosphate availability in media as a significant reduction in their OD values was observed under low P treatments. In contrast, Chlorella pyrenoidosa showed relatively better tolerance, maintaining moderate growth at 0.01 mM P with significant decline in growth under 0.001 mM P treatment. We observed similar pattern for total chlorophyll and carotenoid content (Fig. 2). The total pigment content was highest in Chlorella sp. (~ 35 mg/L of biomass), followed by Scenedesmus (~ 20 mg/L of biomass) and Chlamydomonas (~ 12 mg/L of biomass) species. However, from Fig. 2, it is very clear that the pigment content in Scenedesmus was the most affected by phosphorus stress as compared to other species.

Metabolic shifts under P-deplete conditions

Metabolite analysis using biochemical methods showed a decline in total protein content in all three species under phosphate-limited treatments (Fig. 3a). However, as compared to Chlamydomonas and Scenedesmus, the total protein content was observed to be higher in Chlorella sp. In total, a 9–15% decrease in protein was observed in Chlorella and Chlamydomonas, whereas a 20% decline was observed in Scenedesmus sp. under 0.001 mM P-limited treatment as compared to their respective controls.

In contrast, an increase in carbohydrate and lipid content was observed in all three algal species. (Figure 3b and c). The carbohydrate content was high in Chlorella (~ 8%) and Scenedesmus (~ 12%) compared to Chlamydomonas (~ 5%) under control treatment. However, in 0.001mM P-limited treatment, the carbohydrate content increased in all three species. Their total carbohydrate content per biomass increased by nearly 40%, 47% and 20% in Chlorella, Scenedesmus and Chlamydomonas, respectively. Similarly, there was a prominent increase (~ 2 fold) in lipid content under P limitation in all three species. Under control treatment, Scenedesmus exhibited a high lipid content per unit of dry cell weight (~ 55%), while Chlorella and Chlamydomonas had approximately 40% lipid content per unit dry weight. However, when they were exposed to phosphate limitation, their lipid content rose to ~ 60–70%, which is approximately a 1.5 to 2-fold increase per unit dry weight. To further confirm our results on the lipid content, we performed Nile red microscopy and calculated their cell size from the obtained images (Fig. 4). The microscopy images indicated an increase in neutral lipid accumulation (represented by bright yellow spots) and an increase in their cell size under phosphate-limited conditions in all three species (Fig. 4).

Polyphosphate dynamics under P-deplete conditions

Polyphosphate quantification and visualization via biochemical means27 and lead sulfide microscopy28 (Fig. 5) showed that Scenedesmus sp. accumulated higher amount of intracellular polyP (approx. 250 µg/g) compared to Chlorella sp. (110 µg/g) and Chlamydomonas sp. (60 µg/g), suggesting polyP accumulation capability vary from species to species.

Further, under P-deplete treatments we observed an evident decrease in polyP storage. Specifically, the polyP content was very low in the cultures grown under the lowest phosphate concentration (0.001 mM P) in all three species. The polyP content in Scenedesmus cells declined by 15-fold under the low P treatment, whereas both Chlorella and Chlamydomonas showed nearly a 5-fold decrease (Fig. 5-ii). We confirmed our findings by performing lead sulfide staining on fresh algal cells grown under both control and P- limited treatment (Fig. 5-i). In this, we were able to observe polyP molecules in the form of black-brown spots due to the formation of lead sulfide molecules. The number of these black-brown spots were more in high P-treated cells compared to the P-limited cells.

FTIR analysis

The FTIR (Fourier transform infrared) spectroscopy was used to analyze the variations in carbon allocation of selected three species under control and P-limited treatments. The bands of the spectra were marked according to Dean et al., 200833. The FTIR spectra of all three species (Fig. 6) exhibited several distinct absorption bands over a wavenumber range of 1760–950 cm−1, out of which four of them were considered important in our analysis. These bands include the ν(C = O) stretching (amide I, ~ 1652 cm−1) and δ(N-H) bending (amide II, ~ 1540 cm−1) of amides from proteins. Further, the other two principal bands are those associated with the ν(C = O) stretching of ester groups (~ 1730 cm−1) and with the ν(C-O-C) stretching of polysaccharides (1180–950 cm−1). The bands associated with the ester groups mainly represent lipids and fatty acids, and those associated with polysaccharides represent carbohydrates. Figure 6, shows that the FTIR absorption spectra from phosphate-limited cultures (0.01mM and 0.001mM) had more prominent lipid and carbohydrate bands in all three strains. Meanwhile, the amide bands, which represents proteins were more prominent in control and 0.1mM phosphate cultures.

Gas chromatography analysis

We performed gas chromatography in order to detect the variations in the fatty acid profiles of Chlamydomonas reinhardtii, Chlorella pyrenoidosa, and Scenedesmus obliquus under four phosphate concentrations (Standard, 0.1, 0.01, and 0.001 mM_P). The heatmap, which was created via hierarchical clustering of z-score normalized data, revealed a strong species- and treatment-specific response (Fig. 8). Under phosphate-deplete conditions, Chlamydomonas showed an increase in the amount of unsaturated fatty acids such as C18:1 and C18:3, which are two of the main constituents of photosynthetic membranes34. However, with increasing phosphate stress, a progressive decline in polyunsaturated fatty acids (e.g., C22:2, C20:5(n-3)) was observed with an increase in saturated fatty acids like C18:0. However, compared to the other two species, Chlorella was able to maintain a relatively stable lipid profile across all the treatments, particularly preserving high levels of α-linolenic acid (C18:3(n-6)) under all P conditions. Further, in Scenedesmus, there was a marked increase in both saturated fatty acids (C20:0, C23:0) and long-chain PUFAs (C20:5(n-3), C20:4(n-6)) under P-limited conditions. These results highlight the occurrence of diverse fatty acid profiles in different microalgal species induced by P stress in accordance with their metabolic adaptations.

P-replete experiments

Culture growth and metabolic shifts under P-replete conditions

For the replete experiment, cultures were initially grown in 0.01mM P media and replenished to 1mM P on day 5. Comparative growth analysis of all 3 strains was performed with standard and deplete (0.01 mM phosphate) treatments (Fig. 9-i). As in P-deplete treatments, a significant decline in growth was observed in all 3 strains; however, a notable improvement in growth was observed in C. reinhardtii and C. pyrenoidosa, on P-replete treatment. These strains were able to restore their growth and biomass after repletion, almost similar to that of the standard treatment. However, gradual growth recovery was observed in S. obliquus, indicating phosphate reabsorption and reutilization capacity differences in species.

Similar trends were observed in the case of phosphate concentrations of the culture media (Fig. 9-iii). In case of P-replete conditions, rapid phosphate depletion occurred within the first 4 days, similar to that observed in P-deplete cultures. However, there was a significant increase in the media phosphate levels on day 5 after repletion, which confirmed the effective replenishment of phosphate in their growing media. However, a steady decline in the media phosphate level was observed afterwards, indicating a consistent enhancement in the P uptake by recovering algal populations.

Furthermore, we analyzed the metabolite accumulation patterns in all three species under P-replete conditions (Fig. 10). Among the metabolites, chlorophyll levels were restored in C. pyrenoidosa, exhibiting a significant increase after repletion (Fig. 10-a). However, S. obliquus and C. reinhardtii showed moderate recovery in their chlorophyll content. The protein accumulation also followed a similar trend, in which all three species consistently improved their protein content after P repletion (Fig. 10-b). In contrast, the carbohydrates, which were strongly induced in S. obliquus under P depletion, decreased in all three species under P repletion, reverting closer to the standard levels (Fig. 10-c). Similarly, the lipid levels, which peaked under P depletion, also declined after P re-supplementation (Fig. 10-d). However, lipid levels in all three species were comparable to those of their standard conditions. These findings suggest that, although P repletion improves chlorophyll and protein accumulation in microalgae, it can simultaneously suppress the stress-induced synthesis of storage metabolites, such as lipids and carbohydrates, which are typically accumulated under P stress in microalgae.

Polyphosphate dynamics under P-Replete conditions

The intracellular polyphosphate (polyP) content was estimated to determine the patterns of cellular phosphate storage in response to external phosphate availability. As shown in Fig. 11-i, polyP levels in all three species were low under continuous P-depletion. In contrast, polyP accumulation increased prominently in the P-replete treatment on day 5. C. pyrenoidosa and C. reinhardtii showed a significant increase in their polyP content from day 6 to 8, with their polyP content approaching that of standard cultures. However, S. obliquus showed a comparatively slower and more regulated polyphosphate accumulation mechanism.

These quantitative observations were further supported by lead sulfide-based light microscopy (Fig. 11-ii). Initially, on day 4, P-deplete and P-replete samples showed similar P accumulation patterns. However, on day 6, one day after repletion, the number of polyP granules increased rapidly, becoming similar to standard treatment, suggesting that phosphate repletion can restore the intracellular polyphosphate levels and growth in freshwater microalgae (Figs. 9 and 11).

Discussion

Polyphosphate metabolism under phosphate fluctuations

In our study, we were able to observe the innate differences in polyP dynamics among three different freshwater microalgal strains grown under varying P environments. The microalgae - S. obliquus, C. pyrenoidosa, and C. reinhardtii - were able to demonstrate distinct polyP uptake and storage patterns according to their external P availability. The polyP content observed in these strains (ranging from 50 to 250 µg/g of biomass under standard conditions) falls within the broad spectrum observed in other cyanobacteria and microalgae. For instance, Ray and Mukherjee in 2015 quantified and reported the polyP content in some microalgal and cyanobacterial species, such as Synechocystis sp., Anabaena sp., Lyngbya sp. and Lymnothrix sp., in which all the species accumulated around 50–200 µg/g, which approximately falls within our observed range27.

S. obliquus demonstrated the ability to accumulate the highest amount of basal polyP content (~ 250 µg/g of biomass), which indicates its strong innate capacity for polyP storage. However, under severe P deprivation (0.001 mM), the same strain showed a more prominent decline (~ 20 µg/g of biomass) in polyP storage. This suggests that S. obliquus depends on the intracellular polyP reserves once they are exposed to P-starved conditions, which is similar to the ‘polyP overplus’ phenomenon reported in different cyanobacterial species6,35. However, in the case of C. pyrenoidosa and C. reinhardtii, moderate levels of polyP accumulation were observed compared to S. obliquus, which implies that these two strains may not be highly dependent on the polyP-based buffering of intracellular Pi, and instead they may have their own strategy to conserve and stabilize their metabolic energy.

Interestingly, under P-replete treatment, there was a rapid re-accumulation of polyP granules in C. pyrenoidosa and C. reinhardtii, suggesting that P replenishment may lead to the reactivation of polyP biosynthesis pathways in microalgae. The study suggests the occurrence of a phenomenon called ‘luxury uptake’, in which phosphate is stored in excess amounts, more than what is actually needed for the immediate cellular needs36,37. Additionally, the microscopy-based lead sulfide staining results indicated the dynamic redistribution of polyP granules in response to external phosphate, indicating the reversible nature of P starvation responses in freshwater microalgal strains.

Further, the observed variations in polyP dynamics among these three microalgal species may be partially regulated by the differential expression of polyP-associated genes and enzyme, thereby determining the polyP biosynthesis patterns in microalgae. Generally, in microbes like bacteria and archaea, polymerization of inorganic phosphate into polyP is catalysed by the enzyme polyphosphate kinases (PPK) and the hydrolysation of polyP to orthophosphate is catalysed by exopolyphosphatases (PPX)38,39. This, in turn, will enable the mobilisation of phosphate molecules during stress. In eukaryotic microalgae, it was found that a gene complex called vacuolar transporter chaperone (VTC) complex is responsible for the regulation of polyP synthesis and storage40,41. Thus, the differences in polyP synthesis, storage, recovery, and utilisation among microalgal species might imply variations in the expression of polyP-related genes among these species. Hence, further transcriptomic and genetic studies are needed to identify the specific molecular strategies employed by these freshwater microalgal species in regulating their phosphate homeostasis.

Metabolic shifts and polyphosphate dynamics under phosphate starvation and re-supplementation

When the algal cells experience P scarcity, they will start to reduce their growth and pigment production in order to save their energy for survival, which is evident from our results in Figs. 1 and 242. Additionally, phosphate limitation in the growing media can lead to various metabolic shifts in microalgae. PolyP can be a central player in these shifts by regulating stress responses in them, aiding the phosphate and energy storage inside their cells. In all three strains—C. pyrenoidosa, S. obliquus, and C. reinhardtii—there was a notable decline in their protein content under P-depletion. This suggests a remarkable downregulation of protein biosynthesis in these species, which might be due to very low P availability for their nucleotide and ribosome assembly33,43.

However, both carbohydrate and lipid contents increased in all three species under P-limited conditions (Fig. 3). These increase in lipids and carbohydrates indicates a carbon overflow response among these species, which is reported in similar microalgae like Scenedesmus quadricauda, Scenedesmus rubescens, Chlorella sp. under nutrient stress, in which the carbon flux is redirected towards the production of growth-related macromolecules like protein and pigments, to energy-related macromolecules such as neutral lipids and carbohydrates/starch (Fig. 3)44,45. Interestingly, a recent proteomic analysis study in Chlorella PY-ZU1 showed that phosphorus supplementation under nitrogen starvation upregulated the UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase enzyme, which suggests that starch and lipid biosynthesis are not strictly competitive processes46. This indicates that under certain nutrient stress conditions, if an adequate amount of phosphorus is supplemented, it might allow simultaneous allocation of carbon into both pathways, which helps in enhancing the microalgae’s cellular flexibility in resource management. The FTIR spectra support these observations, as enhanced absorption bands corresponding to ester (lipid) and polysaccharide (carbohydrate) groups were observed in low-P treatments (Fig. 6). This metabolic adjustment also indicates a broader strategy implemented by the microalgal cells in order to balance the intracellular C: N: P ratio under stress conditions. Similar mechanisms were already observed in cyanobacteria and higher plants, in which the phosphorus limitation leads to downregulation of P-intensive processes such as RNA and phospholipid synthesis, membrane lipid remodelling, and reallocation of carbon and nitrogen resources toward storage compounds or essential functions47–49. The microalgal cells will try to optimize the distribution of resources under the stress conditions by reducing phosphorus-required metabolic activities and by preserving the carbon and nitrogen pools within the cells. In this situation, polyP may serve dual functions- it may act as a phosphorus reservoir and participate in the cell’s metabolic regulations. The dynamic turnover of these polyP molecules might be associated with phosphate recycling from the already degraded nucleic acids and proteins, which can contribute to cellular homeostasis. Thus, polyP can act both as a biochemical buffer for the cells, balancing the Pi ionic flux inside the cells and also as a signaling molecule regulating the metabolic shifts under nutrient stresses (Figs. 3, 5), assisting in microalgal stress adaptation strategies38. Furthermore, under P-repletion, the observed recovery of chlorophyll and protein content in all three species (Fig. 10) indicates that re-supplementation of phosphate alleviates the metabolic constraints that occurred in the algal species under P-depletion, thereby helping to restore the core photosynthetic and biosynthetic processes. As phosphorus is important for energy transfer and in biosynthesis of nucleic acids, supplementing phosphate to the P-deplete algal media leads to the reallocation of resources from stress reserves to growth functions in microalgae50. Specifically, a study conducted in Chlorella sp. showed that the accumulation of a high amount of lipid that happened under P-limited conditions, reversed once they were supplemented with phosphate, simultaneously restoring the protein content51. Moreover, the difference in their recovery pattern, may be due to their unique regulatory capabilities for polyP mobilization and carbon partitioning52,53.

Fatty acid remodeling and phosphate sensitivity

Our GC-FAME analysis further revealed that phosphate stress induces significant remodeling of the fatty acid (FA) profiles in microalgae. In Chlamydomonas under low P conditions, it was observed that there was a noticeable drop in the amount of PUFAs (C22:2 and C20:5(n-3)), while there was an increase in saturated fatty acids (C18:0 and C20:0) compared to that of the standard P condition (Fig. 8). This might be due to the downregulation of desaturase enzymes required for PUFA biosynthesis under P starvation and due to the activation of ‘fallback’ mode by the cells, where they will accumulate more stable, energetically inexpensive fatty acids under stress54. However, Chlorella pyrenoidosa demonstrated a comparatively stable PUFA accumulation in all P concentrations, indicating the presence of a robust lipid regulatory mechanism in them and the ability to maintain its nutritional value even under stress, as shown in Fig. 8. Even under low P conditions, Chlorella maintained moderate levels of C18:3(n-3) (α-linolenic acid), which is an omega-3 PUFA. This increases its potential in nutritional industries, as α-linolenic acid is a precursor for other omega-3 fatty acids like EPA and DHA55. Further, Scenedesmus showed strong variations in its lipid profiles under varying P concentrations. Under low P conditions, the amount of long-chain polyunsaturated FAs, such as C20:4(n-6) and C20:5(n-3), increased in these species. These lipids are reported to be associated with cellular functions like membrane signaling, stress response, and oxidative protection56,57. At the same time, they also accumulated saturated FAs like C20:0 and C23:0 under low P conditions. This dual trend may indicate the coexistence of storage lipid biosynthesis for energy and, at the same time, stress-induced membrane lipid modifications, which protect them from oxidative stress. Similar co-occurrence of both TAG accumulation and membrane lipid remodelling has been reported in other microalgae under different stress conditions, such as under nitrogen starvation and salinity stress58,59 highlighting a conserved lipid-based strategy to cope with the oxidative and osmotic stress while maintaining cellular homeostasis. This shows that Scenedesmus is more metabolically flexible and has high potential in producing high-value lipids under stress.

These findings indicate that membrane remodeling is an integrated part of the P starvation response. The organized lipid and polyP dynamics in microalgae suggest that when they are exposed to nutrient starvation, the cells will invest in a storable yet recyclable form of carbon and phosphorus as a survival strategy60. Overall, these findings show distinct phosphate stress responses among the three strains – Chlamydomonas focuses more on the storage of simple lipids for its survival, Chlorella maintains its stability, and Scenedesmus implements an integrated strategy of stress tolerance as well as energy storage. These traits can be utilized in the biofuel and nutraceutical industries in future.

Potential applications in sustainable biofertilizers

The dynamical nature of microalgae to accumulate and degrade polyP under controlled conditions opens a wide variety of possibilities to use them in biofertilizer formulations. Microalgal biomass-enriched polyP will act just like the naturally occurring microbial phosphorus pools. As S. obliquus is able to accumulate high levels of polyP inside its cells and also due to its stress-resilient nature, it is a potential candidate for biofertilizer production. Moreover, the reversible nature of P starvation responses and the ‘overplus’ uptake upon P repletion observed in Chlorella and Chlamydomonas strains suggests that these strains can be used in wastewater treatment systems for P removal from wastewater. The resulting biomass after wastewater treatment can be utilized in biofertilizer industries after necessary modifications. This can help in achieving our future bioeconomy goals61,62.

Broadly, our study evaluates the dynamics of polyP in freshwater microalgae under different phosphorus concentrations in their growing environment. These findings highlight the importance of species selection during algal biofertilizer development, which pave the way for further biotechnological studies on polyP-associated genes. This helps in the development of P-enriched biomass for biofertilizer development in future.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the specific differences in growth and polyP dynamics of three common freshwater microalgal strains under varying phosphorus conditions. Out of the three strains that we screened, we found that Scenedesmus obliquus was able to accumulate the highest amount of intracellular polyP. This indicates its potential in developing sustainable polyphosphate-enriched biofertilizers. Our findings provide a foundation for further studies on nutrient recycling and biofertilizers in future.
