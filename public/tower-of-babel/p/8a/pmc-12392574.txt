Growth differentiation factor 11 attenuates sepsis-associated acute kidney injury by reducing inflammation and coagulation via PGC-1α/Nrf2 activation
Authors: Hong-Wei Wang, Min-Min Wu, Mian-Mian Zhu, Yu-Ying Qin, Ke-Qi Wang, Chen-Yu Wu, Rong-Rong Zhang, Yin Wang, Chen Zhou, Shuang Luo, Chao-Sheng Lu, Jing-Ye Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with sepsis commonly endure severe renal dysfunction and damage, hastening to end-stage renal failure with high mortality, and effective treatment options are currently lacking. Growth differentiation factor 11 (GDF11), belonging to the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily, has shown therapeutic potential for numerous acute and chronic inflammatory conditions. Nevertheless, its function in sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SAKI) remains unclear.PurposeThis study sought to explore GDF11’s role in SAKI and determine the signaling pathways it modulates.MethodsAlterations in GDF11 expression in the kidneys of mice with SAKI were analyzed. The influence of GDF11 knockdown and recombinant GDF11 (rGDF11) supplementation on cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)-induced SAKI in mice was determined. RNA sequencing, Western blot, real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), and kit assays were performed to explore the underlying mechanisms.ResultsTubular epithelial cells and macrophages in the kidneys of CLP-induced SAKI mice exhibited high levels of GDF11 expression. Moreover, gene silencing of GDF11 using adeno-associated virus (AAV) aggravated renal dysfunction, increased tubular damage, and augmented renal apoptosis in CLP-induced SAKI mice. In contrast, replenishment of rGDF11 significantly mitigated these adverse effects. Further studies indicated that GDF11 stimulated the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2)-regulated antioxidative pathways, primarily by inducing the expression of Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), which subsequently decreased excessive inflammation and coagulation. Additionally, these beneficial effects of GDF11 were largely diminished by AAV-mediated PGC-1α knockdown and depletion of Nrf2 in CLP-induced SAKI mice.ConclusionsIn summary, these findings indicate that GDF11 is a potential therapeutic approach for SAKI and highlight the crucial role of PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling in GDF11-mediated renal protection during SAKI.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-025-00762-2.Keywords: Sepsis-associated acute kidney injury, Inflammation, Coagulation, Antioxidation, Nrf2

Background

Sepsis is a severe medical condition that occurs when the body’s response to an infection becomes dysregulated, leading to multiple organ dysfunction [1]. It is the leading cause of death in intensive care units, impacting over 48 million individuals annually and carrying a mortality rate of 20–30% [2]. The kidneys play a vital role in maintaining the body’s homeostasis, thus sepsis-associated acute kidney injury (SAKI) is commonly connected to poor outcomes in sepsis [3]. Acute kidney injury affects up to 60% of patients with sepsis, with approximately 35% progressing to renal failure [4]. Despite the use of renal replacement therapy, SAKI development remains associated with high mortality and significant clinical challenges, highlighting the need for alternative treatment strategies [5].

Growth differentiation factor 11 (GDF11), also known as bone morphogenetic protein 11 (BMP11), belongs to the transforming growth factor beta (TGF-β) superfamily and shares a high degree of similarity with growth differentiation factor 8 (GDF8) [6]. However, it performs a distinct physiological function compared with GDF8 [7]. GDF11 is broadly expressed in organs such as the heart, liver, skeletal muscles, and kidneys and regulates metabolism through the endocrine or paracrine mechanism [8]. Its classical signaling pathway involves binding to activin receptor type IIA or IIB (ActRIIA and ActRIIB), resulting in the recruitment and phosphorylation of three type I serine/threonine kinase receptors, including activin receptor-like kinase (ALK) 4, ALK5, and ALK7 [9]. Subsequently, the activated ALK receptor phosphorylates and activates SMAD2 and SMAD3, leading to their translocation into the nucleus to regulate gene expression [9, 10]. However, this is not the only signaling pathway through which GDF11 exerts its effects. In its nonclassical pathway, the GDF11 receptor complex activates signaling molecules, such as PI3K/AKT, AMPK, MAPK, and Foxo1, influencing cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis [11–13]. Recently, the therapeutic potential of GDF11 has been demonstrated in various animal models of acute and chronic inflammatory diseases. Available evidence indicates that GDF11 inhibits the formation of atherosclerosis plaques in mice by regulating the c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) and nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) inflammatory pathways [14]. In a mouse model of acute pancreatitis, GDF11 mitigates inflammation by activating the TGFβR1/SMAD2 pathway [15]. Notably, a previous study demonstrated that GDF11 promotes tubular regeneration in aged mice after acute kidney injury [16]. More recent finding reports that GDF11 alleviates acute kidney injury caused by ischemia–reperfusion by promoting the polarization of M1 macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [17]. However, the protective effect of GDF11 in SAKI and its underlying molecular mechanism remains unexplored.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α), a transcriptional coactivator, was first identified as associating with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ in response to reduced temperatures [18, 19]. Recently, the significance of PGC-1α in regulating inflammation, thrombosis, and oxidative stress in various inflammatory diseases has gained increased recognition [20–22]. Notably, research has revealed that GDF11 regulates thermogenesis in both white and brown adipocytes by upregulating the expression of PGC-1α [23]. There is growing evidence suggesting that PGC-1α is closely related to promoting renal regeneration and maintaining kidney function [24]. Studies have demonstrated that increasing PGC-1α expression through genetic manipulation or the use of PGC-1α agonists can help reduce kidney damage in rodent models of SAKI [25, 26].

Previous research has revealed that PGC-1α modulates redox balance by directly elevating nuclear levels of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) [27]. Upon oxidant activation in SAKI, Nrf2 is phosphorylated and moves from the cytosol to the nucleus, where it binds to the antioxidant response element (ARE) in the regulatory regions of its target genes, such as NADPH quinone oxidoreductase-1 (NQO-1) and hemoglobin oxygenase-1 (HO-1), offering renal protection [28, 29]. However, this defense mechanism is inadequate against the excessive oxidants generated during SAKI [30, 31]. Research indicates that Nrf2-deficient mice exhibit increased susceptibility to SAKI relative to their wild-type (WT) counterparts [31].

This study explored the impact of GDF11 deficiency and supplementation on the pathogenesis of SAKI and identified GDF11-regulated signaling pathways. We aimed to investigate whether GDF11 could alleviate SAKI by regulating PGC-1α-mediated Nrf2 activation. This study elucidated GDF11’s role in SAKI and proposes a promising therapeutic approach for its future treatments.

Methods

Main reagents and antibodies

Recombinant GDF11 (rGDF11, 1958-GD/CF) was procured from PeproTech (New Jersey, USA). Lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Escherichia coli O111:B4, L2630) and small interfering RNAs (PGC-1α-siRNA-1, SASI_Mm01_00082035; Si-PGC-1α−2, SASI_Mm01_00082036) were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (Missouri, USA). Assay kits for albumin (ALB, A028-2-1), creatinine (CRE, C011-2-1), blood urea nitrogen (BUN, C013-2-1), alanine aminotransferase (ALT, C009-2-1), aspartate aminotransferase (AST, C010-2-1), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px, A005-1), superoxide dismutase (SOD, A001-3), malondialdehyde (MDA, A003-1), and catalase (CAT, A007-1) were supplied by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay kit (E-CK-A320) was sourced from Elabscience (Wuhan, China). The hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (C0105S), dihydroethidium (DHE) solution (S0063), and 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) solution (S0035) were supplied by Beyotime (Shanghai, China). The enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for thrombin antithrombin (TAT; ab137994), interleukin-6 (IL-6) (ab222503), plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1; ab197752), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β; ab197742), fibrinogen (ab108844), tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α; ab208348), and tissue factor (TF; ab214091) was obtained from Abcam (Massachusetts, USA). d-dimer ELISA kit (F10354) was sourced from Westang Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Antibodies against GDF11 (AF1958-SP) and Wilms tumor-1 (WT-1; NBP2-44607) were obtained from Novusbio (Colorado, USA). Antibodies against neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL; ab318210), monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1; ab315478), TF (ab318995), thrombin (ab314480), PAI-1 (ab317604), NF-κB p65 (ab32536), NQO-1 (ab314480), PGC-1α (ab313559), iNOS (ab178945), Lamin B (ab16048), and GAPDH (ab245355) were supplied by Abcam (Massachusetts, USA). Antibodies against cleaved caspase-3 (C-CAS-3; 9664S), Bcl-2-associated X protein (Bax; 14796S), B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2; 3498S), Nrf2 (20733S), HO-1 (43966S), CD206 (24595S) were supplied by Cell Signaling Technology (Massachusetts, USA). Antibodies against TNF-α (BS6000), IL-6 (BS6419), and IL-1β (BS3506) were supplied by Bioworld Technology (Minnesota, USA). Antibodies against aquaporin-1 (AQP-1; sc-32737) and F4/80 (sc-377009) were obtained from Santa Cruz Biotech (Texas, USA). Antibodies against CD86 (13395-1-AP) and fibrinogen beta chain (16747-1-AP) were supplied by Proteintech (Wuhan, China).

Animal experiments

C57BL/6J WT mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center at Wenzhou Medical University (Wenzhou, China). Guangzhou Cyagen Technology Co., Ltd provided the Nrf2 knockout (KO) mice with a background of C57BL/6J. Homozygous Nrf2 KO mice were bred with WT mice to produce heterozygous offspring, which were then interbred to obtain Nrf2 KO and WT littermates. Genotypes of Nrf2 KO mice were confirmed by Western blot and PCR before the experiment. The genotyping primers used for Nrf2 KO mice were as follows: Nrf2-F1, 5′-GAAGCATTATCATTTGGTGGGAAC-3′, Nrf2-R1, 5′-TGCACCAGAGTTCAAAGGTAGC-3′. Eight-week-old male mice weighing 20–25 g were used for data collection. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free environment under controlled conditions (12-h light or dark cycle, temperature 24 ± 2 °C, and humidity 40–60%). The SAKI mouse model was developed using cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) surgery, as previously described [32]. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) was delivered into the mice through tail vein injection. rGDF11 was dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and administered intraperitoneally to mice post-CLP at doses of 250 μg/kg or 500 μg/kg. The rGDF11 dosages used in this study were adapted from existing literature with minor adjustments [15, 33]. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane (RWD Life Science, PRC) and euthanized 24 h after the CLP procedure. To eliminate the intergroup differences in immune cell function of mice affected by isoflurane, we strictly controlled the concentration and exposure time of isoflurane for each group of mice. Blood samples and major organs, including the kidneys, were collected for further analysis. Notably, major organs were collected after perfusion with 0.9% normal saline containing 6.25 IU/mL heparin.

CLP operation

The sepsis mouse model was established through CLP surgery [34]. Before the procedure, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane. Following an abdominal incision, the cecum was identified and a 4–0 monofilament nylon suture was used to permanently ligate 75% of the cecum to induce high-grade sepsis. Then, a 21-gauge needle was used to puncture the cecum twice, releasing a droplet of feces into the abdominal cavity. Subsequently, the cecum was repositioned, and the incision was sutured closed. A sham group was also included, in which the same procedure was performed without ligating or puncturing the cecum. Following the surgery, all mice received a subcutaneous injection of 1 mL of warm 0.9% saline solution for fluid resuscitation.

Construction of AAV vectors for silencing GDF11 and PGC-1α in the kidneys of mice

AAV vectors targeting GDF11 and PGC-1α were developed and manufactured by OBiO Technology (Shanghai, China) Corp., Ltd. AAV2 is known for its strong expression in renal tissue [35]. Briefly, the AAV2 vector was used to express short hairpin RNA (shRNA) specifically targets GDF11 and PGC-1α: AAV2-U6-shRNA (GDF11)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE and AAV2-U6-shRNA (Ppargc1a)-CMV-EGFP-WPRE. The shRNA sequences for GDF11 and PGC-1α were cloned into the AAV2 vector, and the recombinant plasmid was co-transfected with PEI into HEK293T cells. Cells were collected at 72 h post-transfection. The cell pellet was vortexed in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris, and pH 8.0) and incubated with a concentration reagent overnight at 4 °C. The AAV2 vector concentration was quantified, and the viral stock was prepared for injection by combining it with HN buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 0.15 M NaCl). The shRNA sequences utilized in this study were as follows: GDF11: 5′-GCAGATCTTACGACTGAAA-3′ and Ppargc1a: 5′-GGTGGATTGAAGTGGGT GTAGA-3′. To knock down GDF11 and PGC-1α in the mouse kidney, AAV2 carrying GDF11 and PGC-1α shRNA were administered through tail vein injection 3 weeks before the CLP operation (AA2-sh-GDF11 and AA2-sh-PGC-1α). The control groups received an equivalent quantity of AAV2 vehicles with a negative control sequence (AAV2-Scramble and AA2-sh-NC).

Cell culture, treatment, and transfection

The TCMK-1 mouse kidney tubular epithelial cells and Raw264.7 mouse macrophages were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). TCMK-1s were maintained in minimum essential medium (MEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Sigma-Aldrich) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Solarbio) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. Raw264.7s were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin under the same incubation conditions. Cells cultured for two to four generations were utilized in the experiments. To treat TCMK-1 cells with LPS, cells at 50–60% confluence were first starved in MEM medium containing 0.5% FBS for 24 h. They were then exposed to LPS (100 μg/mL) for 12 h as previously described [36]. Before LPS administration, rGDF11 (200 ng/mL, dissolved in sterile water) was applied to TCMK-1 cells for 30 min. An in vitro LPS-induced injury model in Raw264.7 macrophages was established as previously described [15]. Raw264.7 macrophages were pretreated with rGDF11 (200 ng/mL) for 30 min, followed by stimulation with LPS (1 μg/mL) for 12 h. PGC-1α gene silencing was performed using Lipofectamine™ 2000 (Thermo Fisher, USA) following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Mouse bone marrow neutrophils isolation, culture, and treatment

Mouse bone marrow neutrophils were isolated following the manufacturer’s protocol using mice neutrophils separation medium (TBD70907, TBD, Tianjin, China) as previously described [37]. Briefly, the femurs and tibias were flushed with 6 mL PBS to isolate the bone marrow cells. The obtained cell suspension was subjected to red blood cell lysis and filtered through a 70 µm cell strainer, and centrifuged at 200g for 10 min. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in sorting buffer, and centrifuged at 200g for 10 min. The pellet was resuspended again in sorting buffer and the cell density was adjusted to 1 × 108 cells/mL. Combine 100 µL of the cell suspension with 2 µL of Biotin-Antibody Mix and let it incubate at 4 °C for 10 min. Following the incubation period, the cells underwent washing with 1.5 mL of sorting buffer and were then centrifuged at 200g for 10 min. The resulting pellet was resuspended in 100 µL of sorting buffer, and 20 µL of washed TBD streptavidin was added, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at 4 °C for 10 min. After incubation, the volume was adjusted to 2.5 mL with sorting buffer, mixed well, placed on a magnetic stand, and left to stand for 5 min. The cell suspension was then gently transferred to another sterile centrifuge tube, and centrifuged at 200g for 10 min to obtain purified neutrophils. Finally, the purified neutrophils were washed twice with PBS (200g, 10 min). Neutrophils were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 0.5% FBS at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2. After a 30 min pretreatment with rGDF11 (200 ng/mL), the cells were stimulated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 4 h as described previously [38].

Evaluation of purity and viability of murine neutrophils

To determine murine neutrophils purity and viability, isolated cells were exposed to antibodies targeting CD11b (101215, Bioledgend) and Ly6G (127621, Bioledgend) or viability dye 7-AAD (420403, Bioledgend) for 30 min at 4 °C and analyzed via flow cytometry (BD, FACSCantoTM II). For viability evaluation, isolated cells incubated at 65 °C for 10 min were utilized as positive controls.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from fresh mouse kidney tissues using TRIzol reagent. cDNA libraries were subsequently prepared following the manufacturer’s instructions using the NEBNext UltraTM RNA-seq Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA). RNA sequencing was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed with fastp (version 0.22.0) to eliminate low-quality reads and adapters. The resulting clean reads were aligned to the Ensembl mouse reference genome (mm10/grcm38) using HISAT2. StringTie was used to calculate the fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). DESeq2 was employed to normalize gene expression levels and identify significantly differentially expressed genes (p < 0.05, |log2 fold change|> 0). Differentially expressed genes were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler R package, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed in R using the hypergeometric distribution. Transcriptome sequencing and analysis were conducted by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. The transcriptomic data have been submitted to the National Center for Biotechnology Information Gene Expression Omnibus under accession number GSE297658.

Blood cell counts and blood biochemical assay

A BC-5000 Vet automated hematology analyzer was employed to measure platelet (PLT), white blood cell (WBC), and red blood cell (RBC) counts in whole blood samples. Serum was obtained by centrifuging the blood 3000×g for 15 min at room temperature. Serum levels of CRE, BUN, ALB, ALT, and AST were assessed using commercial assay kits, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

H&E staining

Kidney or other tissue samples were preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, then dehydrated through a graded alcohol series and embedded in paraffin. The tissues were then sectioned into 4-μm slices, deparaffinized twice for 15 min using xylene, and rehydrated in a sequential ethanol series (100%, 95%, 90%, 80%, and 75%) followed by ultrapure water. Subsequently, the sections were stained with H&E assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Microscopic images were captured using a Leica DM750 microscope. The technician who acquired and analyzed the images was blinded to the treatment group.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Renal tissue sections were deparaffinized with xylene and rehydrated through a graded alcohol series. Endogenous peroxidase was reduced by treating the sections with 3% H2O2 for 30 min. Antigen retrieval was achieved by microwaving the sections in citrate-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution for 20 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 30 min, the sections were incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibodies at 1:200 dilution. Following washes, the sections were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. The signals were visualized using diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution. The IHC images were captured using a optical microscope. The technician who acquired and analyzed the images was blinded to the treatment group.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

For in vivo studies, fresh kidney specimens were frozen in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound using liquid nitrogen and sectioned into 10-μm sections. The sections were permeabilized using 0.5% Triton X-100 for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 40 min at room temperature. The samples were then incubated overnight with the corresponding primary antibodies at a 1:200 dilution. After washing, the sections were incubated with the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibodies. Cell nuclei were stained using a 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) solution. In vitro, Raw264.7 s were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 40 min at room temperature. The cells were then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against TF (1:200 dilution), followed by incubation with fluorescent secondary antibodies. Finally, the cell nuclei were stained with DAPI solution. IF images were captured using a confocal microscope and the technician who acquired and analyzed the images was blinded to the treatment group.

Immunoblotting

The renal tissues or cell samples were lysed using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (P0013C, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) supplemented with phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (ST506, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) to extract total protein. Nuclear protein was extracted using the Nuclear Protein Extraction Kit (P0028, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The BCA protein analysis kit (P0012S, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was employed to determine the protein concentrations. Equal amounts of total protein were separated by SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat skimmed milk for 1 h at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific primary antibodies diluted at 1:1000, except for GAPDH, which was diluted at 1:10,000. After three washes with TBS-T, the membranes were incubated with an horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (K-12045-D50, Advansta, USA).

Real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent. cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Kit (KR107, Tiangen, Beijing, China). RT-qPCR was performed on a 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, USA) utilizing the SYBR Green PCR master Mix (FP205, Tiangen, Beijing, China). Gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method, with β-actin serving as the normalization control. The specific primer sequences used in the study are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

TUNEL staining

Renal apoptosis in paraffin-embedded kidney sections was evaluated in situ using a TUNEL assay kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were counterstained with DAPI to visualize and quantify total cell nuclei. TUNEL images were captured using a confocal microscope and the technician who acquired and analyzed the images was blinded to the treatment group.

Determination of GSH-Px, SOD, CAT, and MDA levels

Kidney tissues or cell samples were lysed and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected to measure protein concentration using a BCA assay kit (P0010S, Beyotime, China). The levels of GSH-Px, SOD, CAT, and MDA were evaluated with specific commercial kits, following the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Evaluation of sperm parameters

Sperm parameters were measured as previously described [39]. The epididymis that had been freshly excised was chopped up and placed in 0.9% normal saline at 37 °C. Once the spermatozoa were completely dissociated, they were passed through a 200-mesh cell sieve. The resultant spermatozoa suspension was then thoroughly mixed with the sperm fixation agent at a 1:20 ratio. The quantity of spermatozoa in each epididymis was determined using a hemocytometer. A separate portion of the spermatozoa suspension was combined with an equal amount of 0.1% eosin-Y. Following a 2-min incubation at room temperature, the morphology of the spermatozoa was examined under a optical microscope.

Assessment of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

For in vivo studies, ROS levels in the kidneys were assessed by DHE staining. Kidney tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound, sectioned to a thickness of 10 μm, and incubated with 10 μM DHE solution for 30 min at 37 °C. The sections were then washed with PBS and examined using a confocal microscope. For in vitro studies, intracellular ROS production in TCMK-1s, Raw264.7s and purified murine neutrophils was monitored using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. Cells were washed with PBS, stained with 5 μM DCFH-DA, and visualized using a confocal microscope.

ELISA quantification

Blood was drawn from the inferior vena cava of anesthetized mice using plastic syringes containing 3.8% sodium citrate. Plasma was isolated by centrifuging at 3000×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The plasma concentrations of TAT, PAI-1, fibrinogen, d-dimer, IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were quantified using the corresponding ELISA kits, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Similarly, the concentrations of TF, TNF-α, and IL-6 in the supernatant of cultured cells were measured using their respective ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s directions.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). All datasets followed a normal distribution and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Student’s unpaired t-test was used to compare two groups, while a one-way analysis of variance was employed for comparisons involving multiple groups. Tukey’s post hoc test was performed when the F value was statistically significant (p < 0.05). p < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

GDF11 expression is significantly upregulated in the SAKI mouse model

The establishment of a mouse model for SAKI using the CLP operation was preliminarily evaluated through histopathological analysis, TUNEL staining of the kidney, and measurement of serum CRE and BUN levels. H&E staining revealed a notable rise in renal tubular lesions in CLP-operated mice compared with the sham group (Fig. 1A, B). TUNEL staining consistently revealed a marked increase in apoptotic cell numbers in the kidneys of CLP mice (Fig. 1A–C). Additionally, compared with sham mice, CLP treatment led to significantly elevated serum CRE and BUN levels (Fig. 1D, E). These findings confirm the successful establishment of the in vivo SAKI model.

To explore the relationship between GDF11 and SAKI, we analyzed changes in GDF11 expression in renal tissue 24 h after CLP treatment in mice. Notably, RT-qPCR and Western blot analysis demonstrated notable elevations in both mRNA and protein levels of GDF11 in the kidneys of CLP-treated mice (Fig. 1F, G). IHC and IF imaging of renal tissue from CLP-treated mice revealed elevated GDF11 expression, primarily in the renal tubules, compared with sham mice (Fig. 1H–J). We then performed IF double-staining with an antibody against GDF11 and cell-type specific markers to identify the renal cell types expressing GDF11 in CLP-treated mice. GDF11 expression was identified in two primary renal cell types: AQP-1-positive tubular cells and WT-1-positive podocytes. Compared with sham mice, CLP-operated septic mice revealed increased GDF11 expression in tubular cells, but no expression was observed in podocytes (Supplementary Fig. 1 A, B). We also found that GDF11 expression was upregulated in the infiltrating F4/80+ macrophages within the renal tissue of CLP-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 1 C). These in vivo findings were further validated in vitro by exposing TCMK-1 mouse kidney tubular epithelium cells and Raw264.7 murine macrophage cell line to LPS stimulation. A 12-h incubation markedly increased GDF11 expression in both cell lines, consistent with our in vivo findings, as illustrated in Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. 2A. These results suggest that elevated renal GDF11 expression is linked to CLP-induced septic renal damage, indicating a potential role for GDF11 in the SAKI pathogenesis.

GDF11 knockdown worsens CLP-induced SAKI

To explore the role of GDF11 in SAKI, we employed an AAV2-mediated loss-of-function approach [35], which successfully knocked down GDF11 expression in the kidney following tail vein injection after 3 weeks (Fig. 2A). Twenty-four hours after CLP surgery, the control group exhibited significant increases in serum CRE and BUN levels, along with notable renal histopathological alterations, such as renal tubular epithelium dilation, watery degeneration, lumen narrowing, and interstitial capillary dilation, as illustrated by H&E staining (Fig. 2B–E). Interestingly, these serological and histological abnormalities were significantly worsened in GDF11 knockdown mice (Fig. 2B–E). Furthermore, GDF11 knockdown mice exhibited heightened levels of kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) and NGAL, markers of tubular injury, following CLP induction (Fig. 2F–H). Given that cellular apoptosis plays a key role in SAKI pathogenesis [40], we evaluated whether GDF11 deficiency exacerbates renal apoptosis in this context. TUNEL analysis indicated an elevated percentage of TUNEL-positive cells in the kidneys of GDF11 knockdown mice (Figs. 2I, J). Consistently, GDF11 deficiency resulted in elevated renal expression of C-CAS-3 and Bax, both markers of apoptosis (Fig. 2I, J and Supplementary Fig. 3 A). In contrast, expression of the antiapoptotic factor Bcl-2 were reduced in GDF11 knockdown mice (Supplementary Fig. 3 A). Overall, these findings suggest that the lack of GDF11 aggravates CLP-induced renal dysfunction and kidney damage in mice.

Replenishment of rGDF11 attenuates CLP-induced SAKI

Considering the exacerbation of renal dysfunction and damage induced by CLP in GDF11 knockdown mice, we proceeded to assess whether replenishing rGDF11 could alleviate CLP-induced SAKI, following the experimental protocol revealed in Fig. 3A. Intraperitoneal injection of rGDF11 significantly reduced serum CRE and BUN levels 24 h after CLP surgery (Fig. 3B, C). Consistently, H&E staining analysis indicated that tubulointerstitial injury was considerably ameliorated in the kidneys of rGDF11-treated mice (Fig. 3D, E). Moreover, the reduced levels of KIM-1 and NGAL further demonstrated that rGDF11 supplementation protected mice from CLP-induced tubular injury (Fig. 3F–I). Additionally, rGDF11 significantly alleviated renal apoptosis, as indicated by TUNEL and C-CAS-3 IHC staining analysis (Fig. 3J–L). Data from Fig. 3B–G also revealed that a 500 μg/kg dose of rGDF11 provided greater benefits than the 250 μg/kg dose, suggesting that rGDF11 mitigates CLP-induced renal dysfunction and damage in a dose-dependent manner.

To evaluate the biosafety of rGDF11 after in vivo pharmacological validation, C57BL/6 mice received a daily intraperitoneal dose of rGDF11 (500 μg/kg) for 7 days (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). Body weight monitoring revealed no significant differences in weight changes between the rGDF11-treated group and the vehicle group (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Moreover, routine blood tests and biochemical analysis were performed to assess the hemogram, liver function, and renal function in the mice. The results revealed that rGDF11 had minimal impact on the peripheral blood circulation in mice, as reflected by the RBC count, WBC count, and PLT count (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). Additionally, the serum levels of ALB, AST, and ALT remained unchanged, suggesting that rGDF11 does not induce liver toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). Furthermore, serum levels of CRE and BUN did not increase, suggesting no adverse effects of rGDF11 on kidney function (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). Histological analysis using H&E staining revealed no notable morphological or pathological alterations in major organs (lung, heart, duodenum, liver, kidney, and testis) of rGDF11-treated mice compared with the vehicle group, indicating the absence of toxicity associated with rGDF11 administration (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Interestingly, evaluation of sperm deformity rate and sperm count in the epididymis revealed that rGDF11 treatment did not affect sperm deformity rate but slightly increased the number of sperm in the epididymis (Supplementary Fig. 4E). These data suggest that organ toxicity of rGDF11 at the dose of 500 μg/kg is negligible, at least within 7 days of in vivo use.

rGDF11 ameliorates renal inflammation and coagulation in CLP-induced SAKI mice

The pathophysiological progression of SAKI is strongly influenced by renal inflammation and coagulation abnormalities [4, 41]. We hypothesized that the protective effects of rGDF11 against SAKI may stem from its anti-inflammatory and anticoagulant properties. Previous studies have indicated that a hallmark of the inflammatory response in SAKI is the infiltration of immune cells, particularly macrophages, into the renal interstitium [42]. In CLP mice, IF staining indicated a significant increase in F4/80+ macrophage infiltration in the kidneys, whereas rGDF11 supplementation significantly decreased the number of these infiltrating cells (Fig. 4A, B). MCP-1 is a crucial regulator of macrophage recruitment during inflammatory responses, and its expression tightly controls macrophage infiltration [43]. In line with this, the renal tissue of CLP mice exhibited a marked increase in MCP-1 protein expression, which was significantly reduced by rGDF11 supplementation (Fig. 4A, B). Moreover, rGDF11 treatment reduced the renal mRNA expression of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in CLP mice (Fig. 4C, D).

We next investigated the role of rGDF11 in coagulation activation in CLP-induced SAKI mice. As expected, CLP mice exhibited abnormal coagulation responses, including elevated fibrin deposition, decreased PLT count, and elevated levels of thrombin, TF, and PAI-1. Notably, rGDF11 treatment effectively reversed these pathological changes (Fig. 4E–G and Supplementary Fig. 5 A, B). Furthermore, overactivation of the coagulation cascades results in excessive consumption of fibrinogen and increased production of d-dimer, TAT complexes, and PAI-1 in the bloodstream [44]. To assess this, we measured plasma levels of these indicators using ELISA. We found that rGDF11 treatment inhibited the decrease of fibrinogen levels and suppressed the elevation of d-dimer, TAT complexes, and PAI-1 levels in the plasma of CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 6A–D). Moreover, heat map analysis of RNA sequencing data from kidney tissue of CLP mice and rGDF11-treated mice further confirmed that rGDF11 inhibited renal inflammatory response and complement-coagulation cascade activation compared with the CLP group (Fig. 4H). Conversely, GDF11 knockdown in CLP mice led to increased levels of proinflammatory cytokines in both plasma and renal tissue, decreased PLT count in whole blood, elevated thrombin, TF, and PAI-1 production, and enhanced fibrin deposition in the kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 7). Collectively, these results highlight the critical anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation roles of GDF11 in CLP-induced SAKI.

rGDF11 prevents CLP-induced renal oxidative stress, possibly by activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathway

To further investigate the mechanism by which GDF11 protects against CLP-induced SAKI, we conducted additional analysis of RNA sequencing data obtained from the kidneys of CLP-operated mice and rGDF11-treated mice. We identified 152 upregulated and 288 downregulated genes, defined by |log2 fold change| > 0 and p < 0.05 (Fig. 5A). GO and KEGG analyses revealed that these differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched for the regulation of response to oxidative stress and xenobiotic metabolic process, suggesting that the redox pathway may play a vital role in GDF11’s protective effect against SAKI (Fig. 5B). During SAKI, ROS accumulate early in response to sepsis, contributing to several pathological processes that exacerbate kidney damage, including renal inflammation and coagulation [45]. Consequently, we explored the potential of rGDF11 to mitigate oxidative stress in the kidneys of CLP mice. DHE staining analysis revealed a marked increase in ROS production in the renal tissue of CLP mice compared with sham mice (Fig. 5C). However, rGDF11 treatment significantly reduced this increase (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, the concentrations of CAT, GSH-Px, SOD, and MDA, key internal markers of redox balance, were evaluated in the renal tissue of mice. Following CLP induction, the levels of antioxidant enzymes, CAT, GSH-Px, and SOD, were noticeably reduced (Fig. 5D). However, treatment with rGDF11 restored these antioxidant markers (Fig. 5D). In contrast, MDA levels, which indicate lipid peroxidation, were increased in the kidneys of CLP-induced septic mice but were significantly decreased with rGDF11 supplementation (Fig. 5D). Conversely, knockdown of GDF11 resulted in higher ROS production, lower SOD concentrations, and increased MDA levels in the kidneys of CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 8).

An imbalance between ROS generation and the scavenging systems leads to ROS overproduction [46]. To investigate the effect of rGDF11 on this balance, we analyzed the renal expression of key genes involved in prooxidant and antioxidant processes using RT-qPCR. The results revealed a notable increase in mRNA expression of both prooxidant genes (CYP2E1, Aco, CYP4A10, and Nox2) and antioxidant genes (G6PDH, GCL-c, Gpx-1, and SOD2) in the kidneys of CLP mice (Fig. 5E and Supplementary Fig. 9). Moreover, rGDF11 treatment further increased the mRNA expressions of antioxidant genes (Fig. 5E) while having no significant effect on the expression of prooxidant genes (Supplementary Fig. 9). These data imply that rGDF11 treatment inhibits CLP-induced renal oxidative stress by enhancing the kidney’s antioxidant capacity.

Considering that Nrf2 is an essential transcription factor responsible for activating the antioxidant defense system [28–31], we determined whether rGDF11 modulates Nrf2 activity in the kidneys of CLP mice. The results revealed increased levels of nuclear Nrf2 and its downstream targets, HO-1 and NQO-1, in the renal tissue of CLP mice (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 10). After rGDF11 treatment, the expression of these proteins was further elevation, suggesting activation of the Nrf2 pathway (Fig. 5F and Supplementary Fig. 10). These data indicate that rGDF11 likely prevents CLP-induced renal oxidative stress by activating Nrf2-mediated antioxidant activity.

Nrf2 is essential for rGDF11-mediated renal protection in CLP-induced SAKI

To determine whether the positive impact of rGDF11 on SAKI relied on Nrf2 activation, we employed Nrf2 KO mice to create a CLP-induced SAKI model with rGDF11 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 11 A). Nrf2 KO was validated through Western blot and PCR analyses (Supplementary Figs. 11B, C). Following CLP operation, Nrf2 KO mice exhibited significant renal dysfunction and damage, as evidenced by markedly increased tubulointerstitial injury, elevated renal expression of KIM-1 and NGAL, and enhanced serum levels of CRE and BUN (Fig. 6A–C and Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). However, rGDF11 treatment did not ameliorate these elevated markers in Nrf2 KO mice (Fig. 6A–C and Supplementary Fig. 12A–C). In addition, the antiapoptotic impact of rGDF11 was eliminated in Nrf2 KO mice, as reflected by TUNEL and C-CAS-3 IHC staining analysis (Fig. 6D–F). Moreover, CLP-operated Nrf2 KO mice revealed equivalent levels of GSH-Px, CAT, SOD, MDA, and ROS accumulation in the kidneys regardless of rGDF11 treatment (Fig. 6G, H). These findings highlight the essential role of Nrf2 activation in mediating the protective effects of rGDF11 against CLP-induced SAKI in mice.

Nrf2 deficiency diminishes rGDF11’s anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation benefits in CLP-induced SAKI mice

Subsequently, we examined whether Nrf2 activation mediates the anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation effects of rGDF11. Nrf2 KO mice subjected to CLP surgery displayed similar levels of F4/80+ macrophage infiltration and MCP-1 protein expression in the kidneys, regardless of rGDF11 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 13 A). Moreover, RT-qPCR analysis indicated that rGDF11 treatment did not significantly reduce the elevated mRNA levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in CLP-operated Nrf2 KO mice (Supplementary Figs. 13B, C). Regarding coagulation, the anticoagulation effects of rGDF11 were also diminished in Nrf2 KO mice, as reflected by persistent fibrin deposition and unaltered protein levels of thrombin and TF (Supplementary Figs. 13D, E). Moreover, ELISA analysis indicated that rGDF11 supplementation did not reduce fibrinogen levels or affect the elevation in d-dimer, TAT complexes, and PAI-1 levels in the plasma of CLP-operated Nrf2 KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 14). These data indicate that the absence of Nrf2 impairs rGDF11’s protective effect against both renal inflammation and coagulation of CLP-induced SAKI.

rGDF11-mediated Nrf2 activation is undercontrolled by PGC-1α

Notably, Nrf2 is regulated by PGC-1α, a transcriptional coactivator that is induced by GDF11 through its nonclassical signaling pathway [23, 47, 48]. To investigate this relationship, we performed Western blot to assess the impact of rGDF11 on PGC-1α expression in the kidneys of CLP mice. Our findings indicated that rGDF11 treatment further enhanced CLP-induced renal expression of PGC-1α (Fig. 7A). In contrast, GDF11 knockdown led to a reduction in PGC-1α and Nrf2 expression in the renal tissue of CLP mice (Fig. 7B). Given the frequent use of kidney tubular epithelium cells in SAKI research and their high GDF11 expression following CLP operation, we initially employed TCMK-1s to assess the impact of rGDF11 on PGC-1α expression. Our findings indicated that rGDF11 enhances the expression of both PGC-1α and Nrf2 in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 7C, D). These findings suggest that rGDF11 may activate Nrf2 by upregulating PGC-1α expression. To test this hypothesis, we used a loss-of-function approach by knocking down PGC-1α expression in TCMK-1s. The knockdown efficiency of siPGC-1α was confirmed by Western blot, and the siRNA with the strongest silencing effect was selected for further experiments (Fig. 7E). As expected, PGC-1α was successfully silenced by siRNA, and its knockdown significantly inhibited rGDF11-induced Nrf2 nuclear translocation in LPS-challenged TCMK-1s (Fig. 7F). We next determined whether rGDF11 affects cell oxidative stress and inflammation through PGC-1α. DCFH-DA staining revealed that PGC-1α silencing markedly abrogated the inhibitory effect of rGDF11 on ROS generation in TCMK-1s (Fig. 7G, H). Moreover, in the presence of rGDF11, siPGC-1α treatment induced notable reductions of CAT and SOD levels and significant elevations of MDA contents in TCMK-1 cells (Fig. 7I). Furthermore, rGDF11-mediated upregulation of antioxidant genes mRNA expression was suppressed in siPGC-1α-treated TCMK-1s (Supplementary Fig. 15 A). Similarly, siRNA knockdown of PGC-1α significantly counteracted the anti-inflammatory effects of rGDF11 in LPS-challenged TCMK-1s, as indicated by increased NF-κB signaling, elevated mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and Ccl2, as well as higher supernatant levels of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. 7F and Supplementary Figs. 15B–D). These data suggest that Nrf2 activation occurs downstream of PGC-1α after rGDF11 treatment and highlight the crucial role of PGC-1α in mediating the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of rGDF11 in LPS-challenged TCMK-1s.

rGDF11 mitigates the increase in proinflammatory mediators and coagulation factors in LPS-stimulated macrophages by enhancing PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling activation

Besides the tubular epithelium cells, macrophages also exhibited high levels of GDF11 post-CLP operation. As the primary immune cells responsible for secreting proinflammatory mediators and coagulation factors [49], macrophages were further studied using Raw264.7s to investigate whether the anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation benefits of rGDF11 were mediated through activation of the PGC-1α/Nrf2 pathway. To this end, PGC-1α expression was silenced using siRNA in Raw264.7 macrophages, and the knockdown efficiency was verified through immunoblotting assays (Fig. 8A). As expected, siPGC-1α treatment significantly attenuated rGDF11-induced activation of the PGC-1α/Nrf2 pathway, as evidenced by lower PGC-1α protein levels and decreased Nrf2 nuclear accumulation in Raw264.7 cells (Fig. 8B–D). Moreover, silencing PGC-1α abolished rGDF11’s antioxidative effects in Raw264.7 macrophages, as indicated by increased MDA levels and ROS production, reduced CAT and SOD concentrations, and diminished mRNA expression of antioxidant genes (Figs. 8E–H and Supplementary Fig. 16 A).

We then evaluated the levels of proinflammatory mediators in Raw264.7 macrophages following PGC-1α knockdown. Our results indicated that the positive effects of rGDF11 in reducing LPS-induced inflammation were significantly reduced in siPGC-1α-treated Raw264.7 cells. This was indicated by enhanced nuclear levels of NF-κB p65, elevated supernatant concentrations of TNF-α and IL-6, and upregulated mRNA expression of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and CXCL1 (Figs. 8C, D, I, and Supplementary Figs. 16B, C). Subsequently, we assessed the impact of rGDF11 on macrophage polarization, a crucial mechanism that modulates macrophages to produce proinflammatory mediators in SAKI [50, 51]. Our results demonstrated that rGDF11 attenuated the elevated expression of iNOS and CD86 in the kidneys of SAKI mice, which are markers of M1 macrophages (Supplementary Fig. 17 A, B). Conversely, rGDF11 treatment increased the reduced expression of CD206, a marker of M2 macrophages, in the renal tissue of SAKI mice (Supplementary Fig. 17 A, B). Additionally, in LPS-stimulated Raw264.7 cells, the addition of rGDF11 effectively suppressed the elevation of iNOS and CD86 expression while reversing the reduction of CD206 expression (Supplementary Fig. 17 C, D). Nevertheless, these effects were reversed with PGC-1α knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 17 C, D). It appears from these findings that rGDF11 is involved in modulating the transition of M1 macrophages to M2 phenotype probably via the PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling pathway. Besides, the inhibitory effects of rGDF11 on the production of coagulation factors such as TF and PAI-1 were also reduced by PGC-1α knockdown (Fig. 8J–M). Collectively, these data illustrated that rGDF11 mitigates the increase of proinflammatory mediators and coagulation factors in LPS-stimulated macrophages, at least in part, through the activation of PGC-1α/Nrf2 pathway-mediated antioxidant effect.

rGDF11 exerts anti-oxidative stress and anti-inflammatory effects in LPS-stimulated murine neutrophils

Notably, neutrophils are also potent effector immune cells that produce ROS and proinflammatory mediators and play a key role in the pathophysiology of SAKI [52–54]. Therefore, we isolated highly pure and viable murine neutrophils to clarify the effect of rGDF11 on neutrophil function (Supplementary Figs. 18 A, B). Our results showed that treatment with rGDF11 significantly reduced ROS production and MDA levels while largely restoring decreased SOD levels in LPS-stimulated neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 18 C, D). Additionally, the presence of rGDF11 inhibited the release of proinflammatory cytokines from mouse neutrophils upon LPS stimulation, as reflected by reduced levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in the supernatant (Supplementary Fig. 18E). These results suggest that rGDF11 has anti-oxidative stress and anti-inflammatory properties in LPS-stimulated murine neutrophils.

PGC-1α mediates the protective role of rGDF11 in CLP-induced SAKI

To explore whether the renoprotective effects of rGDF11 depend on PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling activation in vivo, we utilized an AAV2-mediated strategy to reduce PGC-1α expression in the kidney through tail vein injection. The experimental design is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 19 A, and immunoblotting confirmed the effective knockdown of PGC-1α in the kidneys 3 weeks after AA2-sh-PGC-1α injection (Supplementary Fig. 19B). As expected, PGC-1α knockdown significantly reduced rGDF11-induced upregulation of Nrf2 and its downstream proteins, HO-1 and NQO-1, in the kidneys of CLP-operated mice (Fig. 9A). Furthermore, the beneficial effects of rGDF11 against CLP-induced renal dysfunction and damage were significantly diminished by AA2-sh-PGC-1α injection, as evidenced by increased tubulointerstitial injury, elevated renal KIM-1 expression, and higher serum CRE and BUN levels (Fig. 9B, C and Supplementary Figs. 20 A, B). TUNEL and C-CAS-3 IHC analysis indicated that the antiapoptotic effects of rGDF11 were attenuated after AA2-sh-PGC-1α intervention (Fig. 9D). Moreover, the antioxidant effects of rGDF11 in the kidneys of CLP mice were found to be dependent on PGC-1α, as AA2-sh-PGC-1α injection resulted in decreased levels of CAT, GSH-Px, and SOD, along with increased MDA content, and elevated ROS production as measured by DHE staining (Fig. 9E, F).

We then examined whether the anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation effects of rGDF11 in CLP-induced septic kidneys rely on PGC-1α/Nrf2 activation-mediated antioxidant activity. AA2-sh-PGC-1α intervention notably increased F4/80+ macrophage infiltration and elevated MCP-1 protein expression in the renal tissue of rGDF11-treated CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 21 A, B). RT-qPCR analysis also demonstrated that AA2-sh-PGC-1α treatment diminished the anti-inflammatory effects of rGDF11, leading to elevated mRNA expressions of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in the kidneys (Supplementary Fig. 21 C). Furthermore, PGC-1α knockdown significantly impaired the ability of rGDF11 to decrease fibrin deposition and the protein expression levels of thrombin, PAI-1, and TF in the kidneys of CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 21D–F). Additionally, our data demonstrated that AAV2-sh-PGC-1α treatment abolished the rGDF11-mediated restoration of plasma fibrinogen and whole blood platelet levels, as well as its inhibitory effects on plasma d-dimer, TAT complexes, and PAI-1 levels in CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 21G and Supplementary Fig. 22A–D). Overall, these findings imply that activation of PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling is crucial for the protective effects of rGDF11 against CLP-induced SAKI.

Discussion

Despite significant research into the pathophysiology of SAKI, current treatment options remain limited, and no specific agents have been conclusively proven effective [5]. Therefore, the main objective of this study was to investigate the pharmacological potential of rGDF11 as a new treatment strategy for SAKI. We demonstrated that rGDF11 supplementation effectively protects against renal dysfunction and damage in mice with SAKI, as evidenced by decreased serum CRE and BUN levels, alleviated kidney pathology, reduced renal expression of KIM-1 and NGAL, and improved renal apoptosis. Furthermore, our research indicates that rGDF11 exhibits a favorable drug safety profile, with no significant toxicity to major organs observed during 7-day in vivo testing. Given the lethal and rapidly progressive nature of SAKI [55, 56], we believe that rGDF11 holds great therapeutic potential as a secure and efficient agent for halting the development of this disease.

We have demonstrated that subjecting mice to CLP operation for 24 h successfully established a mouse model of SAKI, accompanied by significantly increased serum CRE and BUN levels, renal apoptosis, and severe renal tubular damage. Furthermore, CLP surgery significantly elevated renal GDF11 expression, suggesting its potential involvement in SAKI pathogenesis. Notably, we observed high levels of GDF11 expression in SAKI-related cell types, including tubular epithelial cells and macrophages, following CLP operation, while no expression was detected in the podocytes. On the basis of these findings, we suggest that tubular epithelial cells and macrophages are primarily responsible for the intrinsic impact of GDF11 in CLP-induced SAKI. Previous research has indicated that increased GDF11 expression protects pancreatic acini from severe injury in mice with pancreatitis [15]. Additionally, studies have revealed that myocardial overexpression of GDF11 attenuates cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury and diabetic cardiomyopathy in mice [48, 57]. Consequently, we hypothesized that elevated renal GDF11 following CLP surgery may provide a protective effect, safeguarding the kidneys from CLP-induced renal dysfunction and damage. To support this hypothesis, we conducted two lines of investigation. First, we observed that GDF11 knockdown exacerbated renal dysfunction and increased renal injury compared with control mice after CLP treatment. Second, replenishing GDF11 significantly reversed these adverse effects. Overall, our findings suggest that GDF11 is a key regulator in CLP-induced SAKI, with its elevations in the kidneys serving as a compensatory response to CLP stress.

Among the numerous pathogenetic mechanisms contributing to SAKI progression, inflammation and intravascular coagulation are pivotal factors driving kidney damage [41, 58]. When SAKI occurs, damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) attract immune cells to infiltrate the renal tissue, which secrete cytokines and chemokines, triggering an uncontrolled inflammatory response that directly injures the kidney tubules and leads to cell death [59, 60]. Besides the inflammatory response, intravascular coagulation is another key driver of SAKI progression. During sepsis, the renal microvasculature endothelium is disrupted by stimuli such as microbial toxins and endogenous danger signals. In response, activated monocytes adhere to the injured endothelium and secrete high levels of TF, which enters the renal circulation and activates coagulation-related pathways [3, 60, 61]. Meanwhile, the kidney’s anticoagulant and fibrinolytic systems are activated to counteract intravascular coagulation. However, as the disease progresses, anticoagulant factors become gradually depleted, and the fibrinolytic system is hindered by increased levels of fibrinolysis inhibitors. This disruption of the dynamic balance among coagulation, anticoagulation, and fibrinolysis in the kidney leads to hypercoagulable state, fibrin deposition, and extensive microthrombus formation in the renal microcirculation, ultimately impairing renal perfusion and exacerbating kidney injury [41, 62–64]. Additionally, the interplay between inflammation and coagulation, known as thromboinflammation, further complicates the pathogenesis of SAKI and presents additional challenges for therapeutic interventions [65]. Accordingly, decreasing renal inflammation and coagulation represents a promising strategy to delay the onset of SAKI. Our study demonstrated that rGDF11 supplementation effectively inhibited macrophage infiltration, decreased MCP-1 expression, and reduced the levels of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines in CLP-induced septic kidneys, whereas GDF11 knockdown had the opposite effects. Moreover, we discovered that rGDF11 replenishment exerted a clear anticoagulation effect in CLP-induced SAKI mice, whereas GDF11 reduction exacerbated renal coagulation after CLP operation. Collectively, the protective effects of GDF11 on CLP-induced renal inflammation and coagulation highlight its potential as a promising therapeutic target for SAKI.

Subsequently, we investigated the specific mechanism through which rGDF11 exerts its anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation effects in the kidneys of CLP mice. RNA-sequencing analysis following rGDF11 treatment indicated significant enrichment of differentially expressed genes involved in the regulation of oxidative stress response and xenobiotic metabolism, highlighting the redox pathway’s potential role in GDF11’s protective effects against SAKI. During SAKI, DAMPs and PAMPs have a direct impact on metabolism and result in massive ROS production [66]. These ROS can function as signaling molecules that activate inflammation-related pathways, such as JNK, P38, and NF-κB, thereby worsening renal inflammation [67, 68]. Moreover, multiple studies have demonstrated that ROS significantly influences various aspects of the coagulation process. For example, ROS can induce TF expression, a crucial initiator of the extrinsic coagulation pathway, in endothelial cells and monocytes [69]. Additionally, ROS can oxidize fibrinogen, making it more susceptible to cleavage by thrombin [70]. ROS can also directly activate PLT and enhance their aggregation, potentially promoting thrombus formation [71]. In addition, the inflammation and coagulation process can further stimulate ROS generation, exacerbating oxidative stress and establishing a vicious cycle [72, 73]. In this study, rGDF11 supplementation in CLP-induced SAKI mice decreased renal ROS and MDA levels while increasing antioxidant enzyme activity, indicating that rGDF11 suppresses oxidative stress in SAKI. Furthermore, the improvement in renal oxidative stress by rGDF11 is primarily linked to a significant upregulation of antioxidant capacity rather than the inhibition of pro-oxidative systems in the kidneys. These findings suggest that the antioxidant properties of GDF11 may contribute to its anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation effects in CLP-induced SAKI mice.

Cells initiate the antioxidant defense system in response to oxidative stress to prevent damage and maintain redox balance [27]. Given the crucial role of Nrf2 in counteracting excessive ROS in CLP-induced SAKI mice [31], we investigated the impact of rGDF11 on Nrf2 activity in the kidneys. Our results revealed that rGDF11 treatment increased nuclear Nrf2 levels, along with upregulation of its downstream targets, HO-1 and NQO-1, in the kidneys of CLP mice. However, treatment with rGDF11 further increased these protein expressions, indicating activation of the Nrf2 signaling pathway. This suggests that rGDF11 prevents CLP-induced renal oxidative stress, at least in part, by enhancing Nrf2-mediated antioxidant activity. To determine whether this Nrf2-mediated antioxidant effect contributes to rGDF11’s protective role against CLP-induced renal inflammation and coagulation, we conducted a comparative study using Nrf2 KO mice. Our study indicated that Nrf2 deletion impaired rGDF11’s ability to alleviate renal damage and oxidative stress in CLP mice. Additionally, rGDF11 treatment failed to reduce renal inflammation and coagulation in Nrf2 KO mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that the protective effect of rGDF11 in SAKI is mediated through Nrf2-dependent antioxidant effects, which play a crucial role in inhibiting renal inflammation and coagulation.

PGC-1α, a transcriptional coactivator, regulates gene expression by interacting with transcription factors and is modulated by GDF11 through its nonclassical signaling pathway [23, 48, 74]. Furthermore, PGC-1α can modulate various antioxidant genes expression, possibly by inducing Nrf2 expression or by coactivating with Nrf2 to transcriptionally activate its target genes [75]. This study provided several lines of evidence indicating that PGC-1α regulates the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response after rGDF11 treatment, which in turn underlies the anti-inflammatory and anticoagulation effects of rGDF11 in CLP-induced SAKI. First, silencing GDF11 expression with AA2-sh-GDF11 lowered renal PGC-1α and Nrf2 levels in CLP mice, whereas rGDF11 treatment restored their expression. Second, rGDF11 supplementation in TCMK-1s increased the expression of both PGC-1α and Nrf2 in a concentration-dependent manner. Third, the benefits of rGDF11 on activating the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant effect, and consequent reduction of renal inflammation and coagulation, were diminished by AA2-sh-PGC-1α treatment in the kidneys of CLP mice. Finally, siRNA-mediated knockdown of PGC-1α in LPS-challenged TCMK-1s and Raw264.7 macrophages further supported our hypothesis. However, it remains difficult to attribute rGDF11’s protective effects against SAKI solely to the PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling pathway, as previous research has revealed that GDF11 can regulate cell survival through multiple pathways, such as PI3K/AKT, Foxo1, AMPK, and the NLRP3 inflammasome [11–13, 76]. In this study, GDF11-mediated activation of PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling may interact with these pathways, warranting further investigation. In addition, the renal damage seen in PGC-1α knockdown and Nrf2 KO mice might be too severe for rGDF11 to have an effect on other GDF11-regulated targets. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the vital role of the PGC-1α/Nrf2 signaling pathway in mediating the protective effect of GDF11 in SAKI treatment.

Despite the novel insights gained from our study, several limitations remain. First, all experiments were conducted solely in rodent models. Given the differences in metabolic profiles and kidney structure between rodents and humans, the relevance of GDF11 in SAKI still needs to be confirmed through studies in large animal models and clinical trials. Second, further research is required to identify the endogenous factors responsible for GDF11 upregulation in SAKI. Third, male mice were exclusively studied in this research. To obtain a more comprehensive insight into the role of GDF11 in SAKI, the involvement of female mice should be considered and explored in more detail. Fourth, in our study design, we administered rGDF11 directly after sepsis induction in mice. Clinically, septic patients usually do not receive treatment immediately after the onset of sepsis. Therefore, future studies should explore the therapeutic effect of rGDF11 on mouse sepsis several hours after its onset.

Fifth, RNA sequencing was carried out on kidney tissue without any cell sorting. Hence, the sequencing results cannot be attributed to a specific cell line. Subsequently, single-cell sequencing analysis is required to specifically explore the main cell types and signal regulatory networks in which rGDF11 functions.

Conclusions

This study revealed the protective effects of GDF11 in mitigating renal dysfunction and damage in CLP-induced SAKI by alleviating renal inflammation and coagulation. Importantly, this study enhanced our understanding of GDF11’s role in SAKI prevention. GDF11 promotes PGC-1α expression, thereby activating Nrf2-regulated antioxidative pathways to combat excessive inflammation and coagulation in the kidney, which plays a vital role in protecting against SAKI in CLP mice (Supplementary Fig. 21). These findings suggest that GDF11 may represent a promising therapeutic target for SAKI treatment.
