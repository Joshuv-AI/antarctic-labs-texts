Socioeconomic determinants of COVID-19 vaccination and mortality: a cross-national path analysis
Authors: Fulya Ipek-Erdem, Esra Erpek, Volkan Genç, Aybars Bars, Sabri Erdem, Servet Akar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNumerous clinical and epidemiological studies demonstrated that COVID-19 vaccines and boosters reduce infection and mortality; however, the extent to which these effects vary across socioeconomic and political contexts remains insufficiently explained at the population level. Cross-national evidence remains fragmented, with many studies examining vaccination coverage and macro-structural determinants separately rather than evaluating how structural readiness, vaccination and booster coverage interact simultaneously to influence mortality outcomes, particularly during the post-Omicron phase. Consequently, it remains unclear how structural development and policy capacity are associated with differences in the population-level mortality impact of vaccination strategies.ObjectivesThis study tested an integrated cross-national structural model examining the interplay between COVID-19 vaccination strategies and country-level macro-structural indicators —the Human Development Index (HDI) and the Containment and Health Index (CHI)— and assessed whether these factors shape vaccination and booster uptake and subsequent COVID-19 mortality change across countries.MethodsUsing country-level data from 195 countries between March 11, 2020, and August 1, 2022, structural path modeling evaluated hypothesized directional pathways among HDI, CHI, vaccination and booster coverage, and COVID-19 mortality change. Variables were standardized and annualized, and before/after ratios captured mortality changes associated with vaccination rollout. Vaccination and booster coverage were measured at a harmonized late-2021 reference point to ensure cross-national comparability prior to outcome assessment.ResultsThe model demonstrated acceptable fit and statistically significant associations within the modeled pathways (p < 0.05). Higher HDI was associated with greater CHI, higher vaccination and booster coverage, and lower mortality change. Booster uptake showed an additional negative association with mortality change, consistent with an implementation pathway linking structural capacity and mortality outcomes.ConclusionsStructural development and policy context were associated with implementation and mortality dynamics at the population level. The integrated global analytical framework suggests that observed mortality dynamics vary according to structural capacity. These findings provide context for global public health decision-making, emphasizing structural readiness alongside biomedical strategies and highlighting the role of health system capacity and governance in shaping differences in the real-world effectiveness of vaccination programs during large-scale infectious threats. Sustained booster uptake emerged as a key operational pathway linking structural capacity to mortality change across countries.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic, declared a global public health emergency by the World Health Organization (WHO) on March 11, 2020 [1], has fundamentally transformed global healthcare systems, economic stability, and institutional governance worldwide [2–4]. By August 1, 2022, more than 572 million confirmed cases and over 6.3 million deaths have been reported [5, 6], reflecting its sweeping impact on public health and socio-economic resilience [3].

The causative agent, SARS-CoV-2, has continually evolved, producing successive variants with distinct transmissibility and immune escape characteristics [7–9]. Variants such as Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), and Delta (B.1.617.2) defined earlier phases of the pandemic between late 2020 and mid-2021, while the emergence of Omicron (B.1.1.529) in late 2021 marked a pivotal shift in pandemic dynamics. Its sub-lineages —including BA.2.12.1 and BA.4/5— amplified global transmission and reduced vaccine-derived immunity [10]. The emergence of these variants further illustrates the virus’s adaptive potential, reinforcing the importance of adaptable and data-driven immunization strategies [9, 11].

In response, governments implemented a range of non-pharmaceutical interventions, from personal preventive measures to nationwide mobility restrictions and institutional closures [3, 12]. As these measures became increasingly difficult to sustain amid repeated variant-driven surges, vaccination emerged as the primary strategy for public health protection and socio-economic recovery [12–14]. By the end of 2020, multiple vaccines had received emergency use authorization globally, including mRNA (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna), viral vector (AstraZeneca, Sputnik V), and inactivated virus platforms (Sinovac) [14–16]. These vaccines stimulate the production of neutralizing antibodies, thereby reducing infection risk and limiting community transmission [17].

Initial evidence demonstrated 50–90% efficacy against symptomatic infection and near-complete protection against severe disease [18–21]. However, protection declined by 20–30% within four to six months due to waning immunity and the emergence of new variants [22–25]. In response, global booster campaigns were initiated in late 2021 and expanded throughout 2022, with additional doses restoring protection up to 97% against severe outcomes [26–28]. Nonetheless, much of the existing literature continues to prioritize clinical efficacy while overlooking the broader institutional, political, and structural determinants that shape vaccine performance across diverse national contexts [29–31].

Structural and institutional determinants of vaccination outcomes

While clinical trials and individual-level studies have robustly documented the biomedical efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines, there remains limited understanding of how macro-level conditions are associated with differences in vaccine performance. A growing body of research indicates that countries with higher Human Development Index (HDI), Gross Domestic Product (GDP), and stronger institutional capacity tend to exhibit superior health outcomes and more rapid vaccine deployment [32–36]. These macro-level indicators reflect the broader infrastructural and governance systems within which public health interventions are implemented.

Building on this line of inquiry, Giovanetti et al. [8] conducted a multi-country regression analysis and emphasized the roles of intensive care availability, public health expenditure, and population aging in explaining early pandemic lethality. However, their analysis was restricted to the pre-booster period and therefore did not incorporate later epidemiological dynamics, such as waning immunity, booster implementation, and structural disparities that became more pronounced across regions during subsequent waves.

Subsequent empirical studies have broadened this perspective by integrating institutional, socio-political, and behavioral dimensions into analyses of vaccine impact. Structural attributes such as governance quality, healthcare system robustness, political trust, and income equality significantly influence both vaccine uptake and COVID-19-related mortality [37]. Countries characterized by higher institutional trust and coordinated regulatory systems achieved broader immunization coverage and experienced lower excess mortality [38–40], whereas regions marked by fragmented governance and pervasive vaccine hesitancy such as parts of Eastern Europe faced disproportionately high death rates [41, 42]. Other researchers have highlighted the influence of political regimes, fiscal constraints, and demographic vulnerabilities on pandemic trajectories [43, 44]. Wu and Huang [45] demonstrated that population aging combined with healthcare deficiencies further intensified mortality risks. Moreover, Jeon et al. [46], Sun and Bisesti [47], and Albrecht [48] have shown how misinformation and socio-demographic polarization fuel vaccine hesitancy and undermine the effectiveness of public health interventions, particularly in politically polarized societies like the United States.

The integration of booster doses into national immunization strategies introduced additional complexity in evaluating vaccine performance across diverse settings, particularly during the Omicron-dominant phase characterized by increased transmissibility and partial immune escape. Current evidence indicates that the timing, uptake, and equitable distribution of booster campaigns played a decisive role in shaping case fatality rates and excess mortality, especially during this period [21, 49, 50]. Supporting evidence from Rzymski et al. [51] and Jain et al. [52] further confirms the critical role of booster campaigns in mitigating hospitalizations and fatalities during later pandemic stages. Nonetheless, many of these analyses remain geographically limited in scope or focus on specific dimensions of vaccine rollout without systematically incorporating broader structural determinants, such as institutional capacity, regulatory effectiveness, and healthcare equity [53]. These evolving dynamics underscore the need for analytical models capable of simultaneously capturing temporal immunological changes and structural system-level determinants.

A growing body of large-scale cross-national research reveals substantial disparities in vaccination outcomes, even among countries with access to comparable biomedical tools. These differences reflect implementation variability embedded within pandemic response systems, including income inequality [49], urban density, vaccine inequity [54], health policy infrastructure [55], and population aging [56]. In addition, trust in public institutions and exposure to misinformation —particularly through social media— play decisive roles in shaping vaccine uptake and excess mortality [40, 57].

Several global studies substantiate these findings. For instance, Mendoza-Cano et al. [58] conducted a regression analysis covering 178 countries and reported that higher vaccination rates significantly reduced COVID-19 mortality. Similarly, Allen [39] explained up to 79% of excess death variation across OECD countries through differences in booster coverage. Other multidimensional studies confirm that even modest increases in vaccination rates can yield substantial mortality reductions, particularly in high-income settings [20, 59–61]. In contrast, Coccia [36] and Carta et al. [62] caution that vaccination alone may be insufficient to mitigate mortality risk in the absence of broader structural reforms and sustained policy interventions.

Despite these advances, much of the current global literature remains analytically segmented, frequently examining structural determinants, vaccination dynamics, or mortality outcomes in isolation rather than within a unified framework capable of modeling their interrelationships. This separation makes it difficult to disentangle whether observed cross-national differences in mortality reflect differences in vaccination coverage alone or the structural environments within which vaccination programs are implemented. Consequently, vaccine impact cannot be fully understood through biological efficacy alone but must also be assessed within the socio-political and economic systems that facilitate or constrain its implementation [63, 64]. From a systems-based public health perspective, health outcomes during a pandemic emerge from the interaction of structural capacity, institutional governance, and biomedical interventions, shaping differences in the real-world effectiveness of vaccination strategies across diverse national contexts. Therefore, vaccination effectiveness at the population level should be conceptualized not only as a biological phenomenon but also as a function of broader socio-economic and policy environments.

Study rationale and objectives

Although existing research has substantially advanced understanding of COVID-19 mortality patterns and vaccine effectiveness, important conceptual and empirical gaps remain. As outlined above, much of current literature can be grouped into three partially overlapping yet analytically separated domains: clinical and population-level vaccine effectiveness studies; macro-level socio-economic and governance determinant analyses; and cross-national vaccination coverage and outcome studies [20, 34, 36, 59]. However, these domains have seldom been integrated within a unified analytical framework capable of simultaneously modeling structural readiness, vaccination implementation dynamics, booster uptake and mortality outcomes.

In addition, a considerable portion of cross-national research was conducted prior to the Omicron-dominant phase and therefore does not fully account for the epidemiological and policy complexity introduced by booster strategies, waning immunity, and variant-driven transmission dynamics [21, 35, 49]. Importantly, these later-phase dynamics did not merely alter biological protection levels; they also reshaped the structural and policy contexts within which vaccination programs were implemented. As booster uptake became increasingly uneven across countries and immunity waned over time, mortality trajectories more clearly reflected the interaction between structural capacity, vaccination implementation, and adaptive policy responses. Yet existing global analyses have rarely modeled these structural, immunological, and implementation pathways simultaneously. This omission limits the ability to model how structural disparities shape and condition vaccination effectiveness during later pandemic phases.

To address this gap, the present study develops a globally scaled path-analytic framework that jointly models structural macro-indicators —capturing long-term socio-economic development (HDI) and policy stringency and adaptive response capacity (CHI)— alongside vaccination coverage, booster uptake, and COVID-19 mortality change. By estimating both direct and mediated pathways linking structural readiness and vaccination dynamics to mortality outcomes, this study provides an integrated analytical assessment of how socio-economic and governance environments condition the population-level impact of vaccination across countries.

Through its multidimensional and statistically robust design, this study contributes methodologically by integrating previously fragmented analytical streams into a single structural path-analytic framework capable of estimating both direct and mediated effects between structural readiness, vaccination dynamics, and mortality outcomes. Beyond the immediate COVID-19 context, the findings offer evidence relevant to strengthening health system resilience and informing preparedness strategies for future large-scale infectious disease threats [61, 63].

Methods

Data sources and preliminary analytical procedures

This study utilized publicly available country-level data from 195 countries, including cumulative COVID-19 cases and deaths, complete vaccination rates, and booster vaccination rates between March 11, 2020, and August 1, 2022. In addition, country-level HDI and Containment and Health Index (CHI) values were incorporated to assess the role of socio-economic development and policy stringency in shaping vaccination dynamics and post-vaccination changes in epidemiological outcomes. Data were obtained from Our World in Data, an open-source platform compiling standardized global COVID-19 statistics [5, 6, 65–67].

COVID-19 related outcomes and ratio construction

The analysis period spanned from March 11, 2020 (the official WHO pandemic declaration), to August 1, 2022, corresponding to the Omicron-dominant phase during which large-scale vaccination programs had been widely implemented [1]. April 1, 2021 reflects the global vaccine deployment phase across countries, whereas August 1, 2022 was selected to represent a later phase when vaccination and booster programs had been widely implemented across countries.

COVID-19 cases and deaths were calculated separately for two predefined time windows:

March 11, 2020, to April 1, 2021 (55 weeks), defined as the pre-vaccination period;April 1, 2021, to August 1, 2022 (68 weeks), defined as the post-vaccination period.

Because the intervals differed in duration, cases and deaths were standardized into annualized rates per million population. To ensure temporal consistency and minimize distortion due to population growth or demographic shifts, country-specific mid-year population estimates for 2020, 2021, and 2022 were applied to the respective time windows. Population data were obtained from Worldometer [68], which compiles demographic estimates from international sources.

Given the unequal duration of the pre- and post-vaccination periods (55 and 68 weeks, respectively), annualization was performed to ensure comparability across time windows. The adjustment factor (52/TimeWindow) rescales observed cumulative counts to a standardized 52-week equivalent, thereby allowing direct comparison of annualized incidence and mortality rates across periods of unequal length. This transformation reduces bias arising from unequal exposure duration across analytic periods.

A ratio below 1 indicates a relatively lower annualized death rate during the post-vaccination period compared to the pre-vaccination period. The same procedure was applied to compute change ratios for COVID-19 cases. Summary statistics for both change ratios are presented in Table 2.

Ratios were preferred over absolute differences to enable proportional comparison across countries with substantially different baseline epidemiological burdens. Whereas absolute differences may disproportionately reflect initial case or death magnitudes, after/before ratios provide a scale-independent measure of relative change, thereby enhancing cross-national comparability and reducing bias related to heterogeneous baseline incidence levels. Countries with near-zero pre-vaccination denominators were examined during distributional screening to prevent artificial ratio inflation, and countries with zero pre-vaccination rates were excluded from ratio computation to avoid undefined values. In addition, ratio measures align with the multiplicative growth dynamics characteristic of infectious disease transmission, offering a conceptually appropriate metric for evaluating relative post-vaccination shifts in epidemiological outcomes.

Vaccination coverage measures

The primary independent variables captured country-level COVID-19 vaccination coverage. Full vaccination coverage was defined as the proportion of the total population that completed the initial COVID-19 vaccination protocol (i.e., received all required doses of the primary vaccine series), expressed as a percentage.

Vaccination rates were extracted for each country using a common reference point (late 2021) to ensure temporal comparability across countries. By the end of 2021, substantial cross-national variation in vaccination coverage was observed, ranging from single-digit levels to over 80% of the population fully vaccinated (Table 1).

Booster coverage was operationalized as the proportion of the total population receiving at least one booster dose beyond the initial primary vaccination protocol [67]. Both complete vaccination and booster rates were therefore calculated relative to the total country population. This temporal ordering ensured that vaccination exposure preceded outcome assessment within the analytic framework.

In supplementary analyses, the proportion of individuals receiving at least one vaccine dose was also examined. However, due to its high correlation with full vaccination coverage, the completed vaccination rate was retained as the primary vaccination indicator in the final structural models.

Social and economic indicators

Two structural country-level covariates were incorporated to account for non-vaccine determinants of epidemiological outcomes: the HDI and the CHI. HDI is a composite indicator of general development, ranging from 0 to 1, that combines key dimensions of human progress – a long healthy life, educational attainment, and a decent standard of living. We used the most recent pre-pandemic HDI value for each country (2019/2020 UNDP Human Development Report) as a summary measure of socio-economic development [65, 69]. For modeling consistency and interpretative clarity, HDI values were rescaled from the original 0–1 metric to a 0-1000 scale by multiplying UNDP values by 1000. This index captures baseline cross-national differences in development-related resources that may shape vulnerability, healthcare system capacity, and pandemic resilience.

CHI reflects the stringency of pandemic response policies. It is one of the aggregate indices reported by the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT) and is calculated from 11 containment and health policy indicators (e.g., school closures, gathering bans, travel restrictions, public information campaigns, testing policies, and contact tracing) recorded on ordinal scales [70]. The index ranges from 0 to 100, with higher values indicating more stringent interventions. For each country, we calculated the average CHI during the study period to represent the overall intensity of non-pharmaceutical interventions. Inclusion of CHI allows adjustment for cross-national variation in government-enforced containment measures, enabling vaccination-related associations to be interpreted net of policy stringency effects [66, 71]. Accordingly, CHI functions as a contextual structural indicator of national policy response intensity throughout the analytic period. Within the modeling framework, CHI was interpreted as a contextual indicator of policy responsiveness across the study period rather than a purely preventive measure.

Both the HDI and CHI data were obtained from UNDP and OxCGRT, respectively, and their summary statistics are presented in Table 1.

Structural equation modeling (SEM) and path analysis

All variables were modeled as observed indicators within a path-analytic SEM framework. Missing country-level observations were handled using listwise deletion within SEM estimation. This procedure contributed to the reduction of the analytic sample following ratio screening and truncation. Subsequently, SEM was employed using a path analytic framework to examine the multidimensional relationships among study variables. A path model was specified to assess the interrelationship between HDI, CHI, and vaccination indicators as predictors of changes in death and case rates (target variables). Complete vaccination and booster coverage were modeled as primary vaccination indicators directly associated with the target variables, while HDI and CHI were specified as structural determinants operating both directly and indirectly within the model.

The model estimated a system of simultaneous structural equations linking predictors and outcome variables to obtain parameter estimates and evaluate overall model fit. This approach enabled examination of the associations between vaccination and booster policies and the target variables, while accounting for social, economic, and political indicators [72]. SEM was selected due to its capacity to simultaneously estimate direct and indirect effects among multiple dependent and independent variables within a single integrated framework, accommodating complex pathway relationships. The specified model enabled simultaneous estimation of structural and implementation pathways linking macro-level context to epidemiological outcomes. This approach allows interpretation of vaccination effects within broader structural system dynamics rather than as isolated associations [73].

Model fit was evaluated using widely accepted statistical criteria, including the Normed Fit Index (NFI) and Comparative Fit Index (CFI), with values greater than 0.95 indicating good fit, and the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA) and Chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio (χ²/df), with values lower than 0.08 and 2, respectively, indicating acceptable model fit. The Hoelter index was also calculated to assess sample adequacy. Standardized regression coefficients (r) were interpreted as weak (0.10–0.30), moderate (0.30–0.50), or strong (> 0.50). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 [74, 75].

All statistical analyses, including descriptive procedures and SEM estimation, were conducted using IBM AMOS version 23.

Structural determinants of mortality change

The model predicting change in death rate after vaccination explained 33% of the cross-national variance (R² = 0.33; Table 6).

Higher HDI was negatively associated with change in death rate through both a statistically significant direct effect (r = − 0.27, p < 0.05) and a statistically significant indirect effect via booster uptake (r = − 0.272, p < 0.05), resulting in a total effect of r = − 0.54 (p < 0.05) (Table 5). The presence of both significant direct and indirect effects via booster uptake was consistent with a partial mediating pathway specifically linking HDI and mortality change through booster implementation.

Higher booster coverage was negatively associated with change in death rate (r = − 0.40, p < 0.01). In contrast, higher CHI was positively associated with change in death rate within the modeled framework (r = 0.33, p < 0.01), and higher change in case rate was also positively associated with change in death rate (r = 0.43, p < 0.01) (Table 4; Fig. 5).

Structural determinants of case change

The model predicting change in case rate after vaccination explained 20% of the cross-national variance (R² = 0.20; Table 6).

Higher HDI was positively associated with change in case rate after vaccination (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) (Table 4; Fig. 5).

Determinants of vaccination and booster uptake

The model predicting complete vaccination coverage explained 53% of the cross-national variance (R² = 0.53), and the model predicting booster coverage explained 67% of the variance (R² = 0.67) (Table 6).

Higher HDI was positively associated with complete vaccination coverage (r = 0.73, p < 0.01) and with booster coverage (r = 0.68, p < 0.01). Complete vaccination coverage was positively associated with booster uptake (r = 0.61, p < 0.05), indicating closely related vaccination implementation dynamics (Table 4; Fig. 5).

Structural interrelations among contextual indicators

The model predicting CHI explained 37% of the cross-national variance (R² = 0.37; Table 6).

Higher HDI was positively associated with CHI (r = 0.61, p < 0.01) (Table 4; Fig. 5).

Cross-national pattern illustration

The structural associations identified in the path model were reflected in observable cross-national variation.

Countries with higher HDI and higher booster uptake, including Portugal, Denmark, Ireland, Spain, Norway, and Chile, were more frequently observed among lower change in death rates after vaccination. In contrast, countries including Nigeria, Ethiopia, Yemen, Uganda, Bangladesh, and Pakistan, characterized by lower HDI and limited booster coverage, were more frequently observed among higher mortality change ratios.

Heterogeneity was also observed within these structural groupings. For example, the United Kingdom, the United States, and Denmark, despite relatively high HDI, showed comparatively elevated case change ratios. Conversely, Uruguay, Costa Rica, the United Arab Emirates, and Chile were observed among comparatively lower mortality change alongside moderate HDI and relatively stronger booster uptake.

These country-level distributions were broadly consistent with the structural associations estimated in the model.

Discussion

This study applied SEM through path analysis to examine the global influence of vaccination on changes in COVID-19 case and death rates following the implementation of vaccination policies. The model demonstrated satisfactory fit and provided an integrated perspective on the relationships among socio-economic development, public health capacity, policy responses, and vaccination performance.

The findings indicate that HDI exerted both direct and indirect effects on changes in COVID-19-related mortality following vaccination. Booster uptake emerged as a central mediating pathway, suggesting that structurally advantaged countries were more likely to achieve sustained booster coverage, which in turn contributed to mortality reduction. The total effect of HDI on the death rate change (r = -0.54, p < 0.05) underscores its pivotal role as a macrostructural determinant in pandemic mitigation. This pattern aligns with previous research linking socio-economic development to health system responsiveness during crises [44–48].

The structural pathways identified in the model were reflected in observable country-level variation. Higher HDI was associated with stronger containment policies and greater vaccination uptake, alongside increased case detection and reduced mortality growth. At the same time, the association between containment intensity, case dynamics, and mortality highlighted the complexity of post-vaccination epidemiology during the Omicron and global booster administration period. Within this context, booster implementation appeared to be associated with more favorable mortality trajectories, although this relationship varied across settings. Some structurally similar settings exhibited divergent trajectories, suggesting that vaccination coverage alone did not fully determine outcomes. These patterns are consistent with the interaction of structural capacity, implementation timing, and transmission dynamics.

HDI functioned as a foundational determinant within the model by capturing differences in health, education, and socio-economic conditions [37]. Existing literature attributes cross-national variation in vaccination implementation to inequitable vaccine distribution, differences in public trust, and vaccine hesitancy [14, 15, 80]. Frequently reported drivers of vaccine hesitancy include limited access to reliable information, lower health literacy linked to rural residence and socio-economic disadvantage, and institutional mistrust toward governments and health systems, all of which may weaken public confidence in COVID-19 vaccination [2, 14, 15, 29, 80]. These factors may slow vaccination uptake and weaken population-level protection, particularly in the context of highly transmissible diseases such as COVID-19 [2, 11, 15]. Cross-country research further indicates that governance quality and economic capacity facilitate vaccine procurement, distribution, and early campaign implementation [11, 37]. The present findings support this perspective, suggesting that countries with higher HDI —often associated with stronger governance and economic capacity— achieved greater primary and booster vaccination coverage and were better positioned to invest in public health infrastructure, coordinated delivery systems, and institutional capacity for large-scale rollout [30, 31, 81, 82]. However, the extent to which structural advantages translated into vaccination performance depended on institutional trust, implementation speed, and policy coordination.

Macro-socioeconomic conditions shape both COVID-19 transmission and mortality patterns, with lower-income settings generally experiencing greater disease burden and lower vaccination coverage [37, 83]. In the present study, higher HDI was associated with increased case detection alongside reduced mortality growth, suggesting a decoupling between transmission intensity and severe outcomes. This pattern may partly reflect structural characteristics such as urbanization, population mobility, greater population density or size, and older demographic profiles in more developed countries, which can facilitate viral transmission and increase detected case rates [84–86]. Improvements in health system capacity, including expanded hospital and intensive care availability, may reduce mortality risk despite rising case counts [37, 87]. Consistent with prior macro-level findings linking HDI and urbanization more strongly to mortality patterns than case incidence [86, 88], this decoupling may also reflect declining disease severity during later pandemic phases and policy responses that balanced transmission control with protection of vulnerable populations [2, 7, 32]. Together, these structural and epidemiological factors may explain why some countries experienced faster increases in detected cases alongside slower growth in mortality.

In addition to mortality outcomes, HDI showed a strong positive relationship with both complete vaccination (r = 0.73, p < 0.01) and booster coverage (r = 0.68, p < 0.01), indicating substantial explanatory power for vaccination capacity (R² = 0.53 and R² = 0.67, respectively). CHI operated as a contextual pathway indicator linking structural capacity and mortality outcomes, with HDI strongly associated with containment intensity (r = 0.61, p < 0.01), and CHI demonstrating a moderate relationship with mortality outcomes (r = 0.33, p < 0.01). These findings suggest that while structural development provides the foundation, effective containment strategies and policy implementation are essential for translating resources into population-level protection.

The literature on non-pharmaceutical interventions presents mixed findings regarding the relationship between containment policies and COVID-19 outcomes across different governance contexts. While some studies report that higher containment intensity is associated with reduced incidence and mortality [89, 90, 91], others find limited or context-dependent effects, particularly during later pandemic phases and variant transitions [11, 92, 93]. Evidence from early pandemic stages also suggests that stricter enforcement strategies in some political systems were associated with short-term epidemiological advantages, although concerns regarding reporting accuracy have been raised [94–96]. Consistent with this complexity, the present study observed that higher CHI scores were sometimes associated with higher mortality change, reflecting policy responsiveness rather than reduced effectiveness. Countries facing worsening epidemiological conditions were more likely to intensify containment measures, producing heterogeneity in this pathway [2, 32]. These findings indicate that containment intensity largely reflected policy responsiveness to epidemiological conditions rather than a consistent protective effect on mortality.

Interestingly, the model indicated that higher transmission intensity was associated with reduced improvement in mortality outcomes (r = 0.43, p < 0.01), suggesting that sustained case growth may attenuate the population-level benefits of vaccination, potentially through healthcare system strain or viral evolution. This observation supports the continued relevance of non-pharmaceutical interventions alongside vaccination, particularly in settings with persistently elevated case rates. The magnitude of this attenuation varied across countries, suggesting interactions between transmission intensity, healthcare capacity, and implementation timing in shaping mortality outcomes.

Clinical and immunological evidence consistently demonstrates that primary and booster vaccination protects against symptomatic disease, severe infection, and mortality, with reported effectiveness ranging between 50% and 100% [9, 18, 19, 31, 97–99]. Booster doses further reduce the risk of infection, hospitalization, and death compared with the primary series, although effectiveness may decline over time, with reductions of roughly 5% for severe disease and up to 20% for infection within 5–6 months after full vaccination [28, 94, 100, 101]. Protection also varies across variants, with estimated effectiveness against Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta reported at approximately 85%, 75%, 54%, and 74%, respectively. Evidence indicates that booster administration generates stronger neutralizing responses against immune-evasive variants such as Omicron; however, protection against infection is lower than for Delta and wanes within 4–6 months during Omicron dominance [28, 94, 100, 101]. In the present global analysis of 136 countries, primary and booster coverage were not strongly associated with incidence dynamics, whereas broader booster implementation was linked to smaller increases in mortality. These findings are consistent with clinical evidence, suggesting that booster doses enhance population protection despite persistent transmission and variant evolution. Considering the global spread of Omicron and the potential emergence of new variants, the results may support consideration of longer-term strategies focused on next-generation vaccines rather than reliance on repeated administration of existing formulations [100].

Across the analyses, cross-national variability emerged as an intrinsic feature of post-vaccination pandemic dynamics, reflecting the interaction of structural capacity, policy timing, booster implementation, and transmission conditions rather than a methodological limitation. These mechanisms were reflected in country-level trajectories, where several high-HDI settings experienced substantial increases in reported cases during the Omicron period while maintaining relatively stable mortality trends, suggesting a decoupling between transmission intensity and severe outcomes. Conversely, some moderately developed countries that implemented timely booster campaigns showed greater mortality improvement than expected based on structural indicators alone. Taken together, these illustrative country-level patterns reinforce the interpretation that structural capacity and implementation pathways jointly shape mortality trajectories rather than reflecting uniform vaccination effects across settings.

Modeling and observational research consistently demonstrate the life-saving impact of vaccination programs [12, 17, 32]. For example, global estimates suggest that vaccines prevented approximately 41% of deaths during the first year of rollout [32], while analyses conducted during Delta dominance reported that a 10% increase in vaccination coverage was associated with reductions of 8% in mortality and 7% in incidence [12]. Country-level studies, including analyses from the United States and Japan, similarly identified inverse relationships between vaccination coverage and case burden [17]. Consistent with this literature, the present findings —including the Omicron period— showed that high-HDI countries with stronger booster delivery experienced smaller increases in mortality. Together, these results provide global evidence supporting vaccination as a key determinant of mortality reduction.

Although the coefficient of determination for case-rate change was statistically significant (R² = 0.20), it indicates that substantial variation in transmission remained unexplained. Behavioral compliance, vaccine hesitancy, and viral evolution likely contributed to these dynamics and were not fully captured in the model. Future research should therefore incorporate behavioral and epidemiological variables to refine predictive accuracy.

Overall, these findings suggest that national development and policy infrastructure play a substantial role in shaping pandemic outcomes. The evidence supports a multi-level strategy that combines vaccination rollout with sustained investments in health governance, healthcare capacity, and containment preparedness. Such integrated approaches are particularly critical in low- and middle-income countries, where structural and institutional constraints may otherwise limit the population-level impact of vaccination efforts.

Limitations

This ecological country-level analysis using aggregated indicators does not allow causal inference, and the findings should be interpreted as population-level associations. The lack of macro-level data on reinfection rates, the number and homologous or heterologous nature of booster doses may have affected cross-national comparability of vaccine effectiveness estimates. The absence of longitudinal information limited the ability to examine changes in vaccine protection over time and effectiveness across virus variants. In addition, confounding factors, including the proportion of vaccine types administered and vaccination among vulnerable populations (pregnant individuals, older adults, and those with underlying comorbidities), may have influenced global vaccination outcomes. These data limitations may affect cross-national comparability due to differences in reporting and surveillance intensity. Differences in booster reporting completeness across countries may also have influenced the estimated strength of the indirect pathway via booster uptake.

Recommendations for further study

By considering the moderate explanatory power of HDI on case rate outcomes, future research should incorporate behavioral, epidemiological, and contextual variables to refine the model and enhance predictive accuracy. Nevertheless, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the view that global health equity and effective public policy are both vital to improving pandemic outcomes, especially in the context of emerging threats.

Given the evolving nature of the COVID-19 pandemic, characterized by successive viral variants and policy shifts, future studies should investigate the presence and timing of potential structural breaks in the relationships among vaccination rates, socio-economic indicators, and COVID-19 outcomes. Testing for structural breaks could identify critical junctures —such as the emergence of new variants or shifts in public health policy— that significantly alter the dynamics explored in this analysis. Such an approach would enhance understanding of the stability and robustness of observed associations over different pandemic phases, improving predictive accuracy and informing adaptive policy-making. Examining country-level implementation pathways —including timing of booster rollout and reporting practices— may help clarify divergent epidemiological trajectories across structurally similar settings.

Policy implications

Governments may benefit from recognizing that successful vaccination programs require integrated approaches combining vaccine administration with effective governance and robust public health infrastructure. CHI appears to capture policy responsiveness to epidemiological pressure, indicating that containment intensity reflects how countries react to worsening conditions rather than a direct protective mechanism.

Given the strong predictive influence of HDI on vaccination outcomes, international health policies may consider prioritizing equitable vaccine distribution, particularly targeting low-HDI countries to enhance global health resilience and reduce disparities in pandemic outcomes.

Findings are consistent with the interpretation that higher booster uptake is associated with more favorable mortality dynamics. Maintaining robust booster campaigns may therefore strengthen the real-world impact of vaccination programs, particularly in the context of ongoing viral evolution and waning immunity.

Policymakers should not rely solely on vaccination; continued emphasis on non-pharmaceutical interventions and effective risk communication remains important to minimize transmission and protect healthcare systems. Policy effectiveness is likely to vary across countries, supporting context-adapted vaccination and containment strategies.

Conclusion

This study highlights the role of structural and institutional factors in shaping national COVID-19 vaccination outcomes. The findings indicate that socio-economic development, as captured by HDI, was associated with mortality dynamics both directly and indirectly through booster uptake, while CHI contributed additional explanatory value as a contextual indicator of policy responsiveness and health-system constraints.

Overall, the results underscore the interrelationship between socio-economic development, policy responses, and vaccination implementation. Countries with higher HDI achieved greater vaccination coverage, and sustained booster uptake emerged as a key pathway through which structural capacity translated into mortality trajectories following vaccine rollout. However, developmental capacity appeared insufficient without effective health governance and coordinated policy implementation.

Although COVID-19-related hospitalizations and deaths declined globally, pandemic dynamics continued to vary across settings [102]. Given the highly contagious nature of COVID-19 and declining vaccination coverage in some regions, sustaining high vaccination coverage —including timely booster administration— may remain important for population-level protection, societal recovery, and broader economic stability [15, 70]. The present findings are consistent with the interpretation that structural domains such as vaccine supply capacity, equitable distribution, booster implementation infrastructure, and population health literacy may contribute to differences in vaccination performance across countries [4, 29, 31].

The model’s satisfactory fit and structural pathways support interpretation of vaccination outcomes within broader health-system and governance environments. Together, the interaction between HDI and CHI suggests that structural readiness operates alongside responsive policy measures, supporting context-aware interpretation of vaccination implementation that account for differences in structural capacity, epidemiological timing, booster implementation, and policy coordination across countries.
