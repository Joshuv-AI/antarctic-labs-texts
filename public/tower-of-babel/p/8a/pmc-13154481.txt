FOXA2 promotes the occurrence and development of osteosarcoma cells by regulating ferroptosis through the Rheb/mTOR axis
Authors: Jiahao Xia, Jingbin Wu, Yuan Tao, Zhongxing Wan, Weihao Duan, Yiping Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is a prevalent and aggressive primary carcinoma of the bone that predominantly influences children and adolescents. Inadequate therapeutic strategies have hindered improvements in patient survival. The forkhead box transcription factor FOXA2 plays a role in metabolic homeostasis and tumor progression. However, its association with OS remains underexplored.MethodsFirstly, we investigated the expression of FOXA2 in OS cells and in OS tissue samples from patients at different clinical stages using western blot and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Analysis of the R2 database revealed the association between FOXA2 and the prognosis of OS patients. Subsequently, a series of functional experiments confirmed the biological behaviors of OS caused by FOXA2. Furthermore, through western blot, chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP), dual-luciferase reporter assays, and other experiments, we identified a key underlying molecular mechanisms.ResultsThe present study determined that FOXA2 is overexpressed in OS cells and tissues, affecting OS patient prognosis. FOXA2 knockdownin functional experiments on OS cells attenuated their proliferation and migration while promoting ferroptosis, thereby offering an alternative intervention for OS as a form of programmed cell death. Upregulated Rheb restored the proliferation and migration in FOXA2 knockdown OS cells by facilitating ferroptosis induced by the mTOR pathway.ConclusionsThe study results corroborated that FOXA2 suppresses ferroptosis via the Rheb/mTOR axis, stimulating OS cell proliferation and migration. Targeting the FOXA2/Rheb/mTOR axis and clarifying the fundamental mechanisms are thus crucial for the clinical OS treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04308-4.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) refers to the primary malignancy of the bone. It predominantly impacts adolescents at the average age of 16 years and represents a considerable mortality risk [1]. Highly aggressive OS rapidly invades nearby tissues, often resulting in distant metastasis and primarily affecting the lungs [2]. Personalized immunotherapies are the current standard treatment for OS that has been highly successful clinically [3–5]. However, additional treatment options for OS remain limited. Advancing targeted OS therapies requires a deeper insight into its molecular progression mechanisms [6].

The forkhead box (FOX) family of transcription factors, comprising FOXA1, FOXA2, and FOXA3, are essential regulators of early mammalian organogenesis. They participate in a variety of biological processes, ranging from differentiation, glycolipid metabolism, and drug resistance to proliferation [7]. FOXA2 consists of three exons, two introns, and a conserved DNA binding domain [8]. FOXA2 interacts with promoter and enhancer regions of target genes to regulate their transcription. Numerous studies have highlighted the role of FOXA2 in tumor growth [9–11]. Aberrant FOXA2 expression is linked to proliferation, metastasis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition in hepatocellular carcinoma, prostate cancer, small cell cancer, breast cancer and various other tumors [12–15]. Recent evidence suggests a potential link between FOXA2 and OS prognosis [12]. Nevertheless, FOXA2 remains underexplored, particularly in terms of its functions and molecular mechanisms in relation to ferroptosis. FOXA2 holds significant potential as a hallmark and drug target in OS development [16].

Ferroptosis is a newly recognized mode of regulated cell death (RCD) that differs distinctly from apoptosis, autophagy, and necroptosis [17]. It is characterized by iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation and decreased glutathione (GSH)/glutathione oxidized (GSSG) ratio levels [18]. Research increasingly shows that inhibiting the cystine/glutamate transporter xCT and GPX4 can induce ferroptosis [19–21]. Current evidence suggests that ferroptosis participates in the progression of various malignant tumors, including OS [22–25]. A prior study found that the promotion of ferroptosis can inhibit tumor progression in OS [26]. The relationship between FOXA2 and ferroptosis in OS is not fully understood, despite the close association of these factors with OS [27, 28]. The FOXA2-ferroptosis interaction in OS represents a promising therapeutic target and warrants a further investigation. The mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) is a protein kinase that plays a crucial role in regulating metabolism, cell growth and survival, immunity, and aging in response to various stimuli such as hormones, growth factors, nutrients, and stress signals [29, 30]. It is well known that dysregulation of the mTOR pathway is also implicated in numerous cancers, where uncontrolled cell growth and division directly promote tumorigenesis and progression [31]. Recent studies reveal that the mTOR pathway also plays a critical role in ferroptosis [32, 33]. OTU deubiquitinase 4 (OTUD4) was found to block GPX4 degradation through Rheb-mediated autophagy, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis and promoting tumor progression [34]. This potential link between the Rheb, mTOR pathway and ferroptosis provides a new avenue for exploring the molecular mechanisms by which FOXA2 and ferroptosis function in osteosarcoma.

The present study demonstrated that FOXA2 is upregulated in OS and promotes tumor progression by inhibiting ferroptosis. For the first time, we found that FOXA2 interacts with Rheb, which is a crucial element of the mTOR signaling pathway, leading to increased Rheb expression, mTOR pathway activation, and ferroptosis inhibition in OS. Our research on the mechanism of the FOXA2/Rheb/mTOR pathway may serve as a new insight in the OS treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and reagents

The Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences supplied the human osteoblast cell line hFOB1.19 and human OS cells lines U‐2OS, MNNG‐HOS, 143B and Saos‐2. Genus-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR) assays were performed to assess mycoplasma contamination. Cell lines were cultured following the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) guidelines. Mycoplasma contamination testing was performed using Mycoplasma Genus PCR. Human OS cells were cultured under standard conditions of 37 °C with 5% CO2, while hFOB1.19 cellswere culturedat 34.5 °C with 5% CO2. All cells underwent 7–10 passages. The utilized antibodies included FOXA2 (1:500,Proteintech, Wuhan, China), GPX4 (1:1,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), xCT (1:1,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) (1:1000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), mTOR (1:2,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), p-mTOR (1:2,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Rheb (1:1,000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), β-actin (1:10,000; Abclonal, Wuhan, China), HRP goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (1:5,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (1:5,000; Proteintech, Wuhan, China). Ferrostatin-1 and necrostatin-1 were obtained from Meilunbio (Dalian, China). Bafilomycin A1 and Z-VAD were from TargetMol (Shanghai, China).

Cell transfection

First, sh‐1, 5′-GAACAUGUCGUCGUACGUG‐3′ and sh‐2,5′-GCAGAUACCUCCUAACC‐3′ were selected as the FOXA2-targeting shRNA sequences. FOXA2 shRNA plasmids together with their negative controls were sourced from GeneChem, China. OBiO Technology provided plasmids, including the negative control and Rheb‐HA. HEK 293T cells were utilized for plasmid packaging into virus particles, followed by viral titer measurements. Six-well plates were employed to inoculate target cells for co-infection with 1 × 108 lentivirus-transducing units in the presence of polybrene (Sigma) to establishcell lines with stable FOXA2 knockdown or Rheb overexpression. Puromycin (2.5 μg/mL) screening was conducted for the infected cells after 72 h. Western blotting was executed to confirm the effectiveness of interference.

Western blot

Total protein was extracted using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined with a BCA assay kit. Proteins were electrophoresed in equal amounts on 8% and 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After blocking with 5% skim milk for one hour, the membrane was incubated with primary and secondary antibodies. Finally, the membrane was washed with TBST and the immunoreactive bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit. MHY1485 was obtained from TargetMol (Shanghai, China).

CCK-8 and colony formation tests

The CCK-8 and colony formation assays were carried out according to the previously established methods [35].

RNA extraction followed by qRT-PCR

Cells were used for total RNA extraction employing the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). The qRT‐PCR was conducted utilizing the Quantscript RT kit (Vazyme, China) and SYBR RT PCR kit (Biomake, USA) according to the previously described protocols [35]. Primer sequences are represented in Supplementary Table 1.

Migration and invasion assays

For the cell migration, total of 2 × 104 143B or MNNG-HOS cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert in 200 µL of serum-free medium. The lower chamber was filled with 500 µL of medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum as a chemoattractant. The cells were then incubated for 48 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. After washing the Transwell membrane in phosphate-buffered saline, a cotton swab was used to carefully wipe off the non-migrated cells from the upper chamber surface. Next, 4% paraformaldehyde was added to fix the migrated cells on the lower surface of the polyethylene terephthalate membrane prior to crystal violet staining. The procedure was identical for the cell invasion assay, except that the cells were seeded into the Transwell inserts pre-coated with Matrigel substrate (Corning, USA). An inverted fluorescence microscope was utilized for visualization and imaging of the stained cells.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM) analysis

The TEM was used to examine and record mitochondrial morphological changes in MNNG-HOS and 143B cells at scale bars of 5 μm and1μm, respectively.

GSH/GSSG assay

Total glutathione/oxidized glutathione assay kit (DOJINDO, China) was utilized to perform the GSH and GSSG assays. GSH/GSSG standard sample and related reagents were prepared in accordance with the manufacturer instructions. The cells were centrifuged after blending with the homogenization medium. The supernatant was then aspirated and mixed with the aforementioned reagents and standard sample. A microplate reader was utilized to determine the difference in absorbance at 412 nm in the obtained mixture at 30 s and 10 min 30 s.The manufacturer formula was adopted to calculate the GSSG and GSH levels to yield the GSH/GSSG ratio.

Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay

The MDA assay kit (DOJINDO, China) was used to measure the MDA level, which is a quantitative indicator for the pathogenesis of ferroptosis. The transfected cell mixture was prepared using the reagent supplied in the kit following the manufacturer instructions. Each sample was subsequently submerged in a water bath, cooled, centrifuged at 3,500 rpm, and examined via a microplate reader at 532 nm to obtain the absorbance value.

Lipid peroxide detection

C11-BODIPY 581/591 (BODIPY) (DOJINDO, China) staining was performed to detect lipid peroxides. After treatment, the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 µM BODIPY for 30 min under protection from light. The cells were then washed and examined using a confocal microscope to assess green fluorescence intensity, which directly correlated with lipid peroxide levels.

Database analysis

Data from CHIP-ATLAS (http://chip-atlas.org), ENCODE (http://genome.ucsc.edu/ENCODE/), GTRD (gtrd20-06.biouml.org), hTFtarget (http://guolab.wchscu.cn/hTF/) and GSEA (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/index.jsp) were applied to analyze potential interacting genes of FOXA2 or Rheb.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay

143B and MNNG-HOS cells underwent 10 min of cross-linking at 37 °C with 1% formaldehyde. The Pierce™ Agarose ChIP kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was utilized to carry out the ChIP assay. The input and immunoprecipitated DNA samples were subjected to PCR analysis following an overnight incubation with a specific antibody. The primer sequences for detecting specific binding sites through ChIP and qPCR assays are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Luciferase reporter assay

The CCK-8 and colony formation assays were carried out according to the previously established methods [35].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

A microarray with tissue samples from 40 OS patients was obtained from Alena Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Xi’an, China. The IHC assay was conducted following the established protocols [35]. FOXA2 was identified using specific primary antibodies diluted at a ratio of 1:2,000. Sections were imaged with a Carl Zeiss fluorescence microscope (Oberkochen, Germany). The immunoreactive score (IRS) was applied to assess staining results. The IRS was generated by multiplying the score for the proportion of positive cells (0, < 5%; 1, 5–25%; 2, 26–50%; 3, 51–75%; 4, > 75%) by the score for staining intensity (0, negative; 1, weak; 2, moderate; 3, strong). Cell proliferation in the xenograft tumor tissue was evaluated using the corresponding Ki67 primary antibody (GB13030; Servicebio, Wuhan, China) at a dilution of 1:200.

Xenograft mouse model

First, 1.5 × 106 target cells were subcutaneously injected into male BALB/C nude mice five weeks of age that were assigned into groups in a random manner. Tumor size measurements were taken at five-day intervals. The mice were euthanized after 20 days. The xenograft tumors were then harvested and their volumes and weights were recorded. Histological analysis of the tissue samples was carried out after their fixation. All animal experiments were implemented in accordance with protocols approved by the Nanjing Medical University Animal Care Commission.

Results

FOXA2 shows upregulation in OS and a close association with survival rates

To examine the expression pattern of FOXA2 in OS, western blot and qRT-PCR were used to compare FOXA2 expression between hFOB1.19 cells (normal human osteoblasts) and OS cell lines, including MNNG-HOS, 143B, U-2OS, and Saos-2. FOXA2 levels rose significantly in MNNG-HOS and 143B cells relative to those in the osteoblast cell line. FOXA2 expression was significantly elevated in all four OS cell lines compared to that in the normal human osteoblast cell line (Fig. 1a, b). FOXA2 expression in tissue samples was identified using IHC staining. A strong correlation was noted between elevated FOXA2 expression and advanced pathological stages in OS (Fig. 1c,d). The significance of FOXA2 in OS patient prognosis was evaluated using an online database. R2 analysis of OS patients manifesting an increase in FOXA2 expression showed reduced overall (p = 0.033) and metastasis-free (p = 0.024) survival rates (Fig. 1e). These findings indicate that FOXA2 expression is increased in OS. FOXA2 inhibits ferroptosis in OS cells

To determine the role of FOXA2 in OS, it was first demonstrated which mode of death was involved in OS cell death. CCK-8 assays showed that the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 reduced cell death caused by low FOXA2 levels by approximately 50% in OS. In contrast, necroptosis inhibitor necrostatin-1, autophagy inhibitor bafilomycin A1, and apoptosis inhibitor Z-VAD showed no significant impact on cell death in FOXA2 knockdown cells (Supplementary Fig. 1b). This implies that ferroptosis may serve as the primary cell death mode in FOXA2 knockdown cells, indicating that low FOXA2 levels induce ferroptosis rather than apoptosis, necrosis or autophagy in OS. TEM was used to observe intracellular morphological changes in order to explore whether FOXA2 influences OS via ferroptosis modulation (Fig. 3a). Compared to the non-targeting shRNA control groups, the OS cells with reduced FOXA2 levels exhibited pronounced ferroptosis-related mitochondrial abnormalities, such as atrophy, cristae reduction, and outer membrane rupture. Additionally, a GSH/GSSG assay revealed a decreased GSH/GSSG ratio in these cells relative to that in the non-targeting shRNA control groups (Fig. 3b). In a follow-up MDA assay, OS cells with reduced FOXA2 levels exhibited a higher relative MDA level compared to that in the non-targeting shRNA control groups (Fig. 3c). BODIPY staining indicated enhanced green fluorescence intensity, suggesting that low FOXA2 level triggeredlipid peroxide production (Fig. 3d). Western blot demonstrated that anti-ferroptosis molecules xCT and GPX4 were positively correlated with FOXA2 at the expression level (Fig. 3e; Supplementary Fig. 1c). FOXA2 activates mTOR pathway by binding to Rheb

Given the relationship between the mTOR pathway and ferroptosis, the present study aimed to determine whether FOXA2 modulates ferroptosis in OS by affecting the mTOR pathway [32, 33]. The correlation between FOXA2 and the mTOR pathway was subsequently explored using western blot. No significant change in mTOR expression was observed in FOXA2 knockdown OS cells. However, the phospho-mTOR levels decreased (Supplementary Fig. 1 d). It was then hypothesized that FOXA2 may regulate ferroptosis via the mTOR pathway. To validate the above hypothesis, ferroptosis-related experiments were repeated on FOXA2-knockdown OS cells after treatment with 1 μM/ml of the mTOR agonist MHY1485. The results indicated that MHY1485-treated FOXA2-knockdown OS cells partially reversed the enhanced ferroptosis caused by FOXA2 knockdown, which means FOXA2 regulates ferroptosis by activating the mTOR pathway (Supplementary Fig. 2a-e). Four distinct FOXA2 target gene datasets were identified from four independent databases (CHIP-ATLAS, ENCODE, GTRD and hTFtarget databases). An mTOR pathway-related gene dataset was obtained from the GSEA database. Nine common target genes were identified by overlapping these datasets (Fig. 4a). In OS cells with reduced FOXA2 levels, qRT-PCR revealed significant downregulation of four target genes (EIF4EBP1, mTOR, Rheb, and RPS6KB12) in MNNG-HOS cells and three target genes (PI3KCA, Rheb, and TsC1) in 143B cells. Among these, Rheb showed the lowest expression in both cell types and was significantly reduced compared to the level in the non-targeting shRNA control groups (Fig. 4b). Western blot demonstrated that FOXA2 knockdown leads to reduced Rheb expression(Supplementary Fig. 3a). There were binding sites between transcription factor FOXA2 and Rheb promoter, as predicted by the JASPAR database (Fig. 4c). A ChIP assay was conducted to investigate FOXA2’s potential regulation of Rheb expression. FOXA2 had one common binding site in the RHEN promoter in both MNNG-HOS and 143B cells. Subsequent qPCR analysis assessed the binding potential (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 3b). Luciferase reporter plasmids for both wild-type and mutant Rheb promoters were constructed. Dual-luciferase reporter assays revealed that the Rheb promoter in 143B and MNNG-HOS cells displayed transcriptional activity significantly enhanced by FOXA2, while a notable decrease was observed in cells with the mutated Rheb promoter construct (Fig. 4e). Cell lines with both wild-type and stable FOXA2 knockdown were chosen for Rheb overexpression. Western blot confirmed reduced Rheb expression in FOXA2 knockdown cell lines, validated successful Rheb overexpression, and revealed that Rheb overexpression reversed the mTOR pathway inactivation and ferroptosis suppression-related protein downregulation induced by FOXA2 knockdown (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. 3c-g). Discussion

OS is a primary malignancy of the bone characterized by high aggressiveness. However, OS treatment outcomes remain inadequate due to the limited understanding of its molecular pathogenesis. FOXA2 acts as a crucial marker in the development of various tumors. The present study showed a significant FOXA2 upregulation in the OS cells. R2 analysis revealed that its high expression predicted low overall survival rate in patients with OS. When exploring the role of FOXA2 in OS progression, FOXA2 knockdown reduced OS cell viability and motility in vitro and significantly inhibited tumor growth in vivo. These results suggest that FOXA2 is essential for OS progression. Research on FOXA2 upstream elements in osteosarcoma remains limited, but in prostate cancer, Lysine-Specific Demethylase 1 (LSD1) has been found to stabilize FOXA2 chromatin binding through K265 demethylation [36]. In colorectal cancer, Tripartite Motif-Containing 36 (TRIM36) binds directly to FOXA2, inducing K48-associated polyubiquitination that promotes FOXA2 degradation [37]. These findings provide direction for future investigations into the specific mechanisms driving FOXA2 overexpression in osteosarcoma patients.

Inducing autophagic cell death, ferroptosis, apoptosis, necroptosis and various other RCD processes has become an effective strategy for treating malignancies. All RCD processes operate by virtue of an individual subroutine, which are under the modulation of distinct signal transduction pathways. Understanding the molecular mechanisms of their regulatory networks is crucial for identifying therapeutic targets and developing new cancer treatments. Notably, FOXA2 knockdown cell death in OS is primarily caused by ferroptosis instead of apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy. Lipid metabolism disorder and GSH imbalance are the main mechanisms of ferroptosis. The present study demonstrated that FOXA2 inhibition promotes ferroptotic events by exploring the intracellular morphological alterations, GSH/MDA ratio, MDA level, lipid peroxidation accumulation, and protein levels associated with ferroptosis [38]. It has been demonstrated that xCT plays a role in maintaining redox balance [39]. The present study findings indicate that FOXA2 inhibits ferroptosis in OS.

The mTOR signaling pathway plays a critical role in the development and progression of carcinomas and is a significant regulator of ferroptosis [40, 41]. The present study findings indicate that FOXA2 down-regulation can inactivate the mTOR pathway, which leads to ferroptosis. Rheb was identified as a FOXA2 target gene that regulates the mTOR pathway. The binding site between FOXA2 and Rheb was validated. Rheb overexpression in FOXA2 knockdown OS cells reactivated the mTOR pathway previously inactivated by FOXA2 knockdown and partially restored ferroptosis levels and tumor cell proliferation and viability. These results demonstrate that a direct interaction between FOXA2 and Rheb stimulates the mTOR pathway and inhibits ferroptosis. Thus, FOXA2 promotes the OS progression. Peroxisome gamma coactivator-1β (PGC-1β) downregulation and FOXA2 overexpression exert their biological functions in breast cancer by regulating the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway. The potential overlapping mechanisms represent a basis for future studies aimed at understanding how FOXA2 regulates the mTOR pathway [42]. A limitation of this study is that we did not delve into potential upstream regulatory mechanisms of FOXA2 or other possible mechanisms by which FOXA2 modulates the mTOR pathway, or elucidate the specific mechanisms by which the mTOR pathway affects ferroptosis, which provides a clear direction for our subsequent research. Future studies focusing on mechanism and inhibitors or agonists of this pathway may lead to the development of innovative therapeutic strategies to improve the prognosis of osteosarcoma patients.

Conclusions

The present study confirmed that FOXA2 up-regulation contributes to the progression of OS. FOXA2 deletion suppresses tumors in OS by inducing ferroptosis via Rheb/mTOR signaling inhibition. The study data offer novel insights into the molecular mechanism by which FOXA2 influences ferroptosis in OS. The present study consolidates the knowledge about OS tumorigenesis and identifies FOXA2 as an underlying target in OS treatment by inducing ferroptosis.
