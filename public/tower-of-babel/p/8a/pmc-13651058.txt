PfnsLTPd-5 interacts with PfGELP24 to enhance drought and salt stress tolerance in Paulownia fortunei
Authors: Xibing Cao, Haibo Wang, Jiayi Zhang, Na Guo, Zhenli Zhao, Guoqiang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNon-specific lipid transfer proteins (nsLTPs) are small, basic proteins widely distributed in plants and have been implicated in various stress responses. However, systematic characterization of the nsLTP gene family in Paulownia fortunei and its potential roles in drought and salt stress tolerance remain limited.ResultsIn this study, a genome-wide identification of the nsLTP gene family in P. fortunei was performed using bioinformatics approaches. A total of 78 PfnsLTP members were identified and classified into six subfamilies, which were unevenly distributed across 19 chromosomes. Promoter cis-element analysis revealed that 68 PfnsLTP members contained stress-responsive elements. Transcriptomic profiling combined with qRT-PCR validation identified eight PfnsLTPs that were significantly differentially expressed under both drought and salt stress. Protein–protein interaction and yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) analyses demonstrated that PfnsLTPd-5 interacted with PfGELP24, which was subsequently validated through bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and Glutathione-S-transferase pull-down (GST pull-down) assays. Subcellular localization assays showed that PfnsLTPd-5 is targeted to the plasma membrane.ConclusionThis comprehensive analysis provides insights into the evolution, structural features, and stress-responsive expression of the PfnsLTP gene family in P. fortunei. The candidate genes, particularly PfnsLTPd-5, are confirmed to physically interact with PfGELP24 to regulate drought and salt stress responses. lays a foundation for further functional characterization and provide new insights for future molecular breeding of stress-tolerant P. fortunei varieties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09458-x.

Introduction

Drought and salinity are the two most critical abiotic stressors that drastically disrupt the balance of plant physiological metabolism, inhibit plant growth and development, and pose a severe threat to global food security and the sustainability of agricultural systems [1, 2]. Therefore, mining critical genes that confer drought and salt tolerance lays a foundation for developing stress-resistant varieties, and this strategy is an efficient solution to the aforementioned problems.

Plant nsLTPs are small, cysteine-rich proteins widely distributed in terrestrial plants. They were first isolated from potato tubers by Kader in 1975 [3].There are distinct differences in nsLTP types among different plants [4–7]. Its core structure is the conserved motif (C-Xₙ-C-Xₙ-CC-Xₙ- CXC-Xₙ-C-Xₙ-C)(8CM) containing a hydrophobic cavity capable of transporting various lipid small molecules, which play vital roles in membrane lipid stabilization, signal transduction, stress response, and other biological processes.

Numerous studies have investigated the functions of nsLTPs in plant stress resistance. A primary function attributed to nsLTPs is their role in cuticular wax formation, which contributes to stress tolerance by reducing non-stomatal water loss [8]. Moreover, nsLTPs are indispensable for enhancing antioxidant defense systems and scavenging excessive ROS. For example, NtLTP25 overexpression activated genes associated with ROS scavenging and mitigated the accumulation of ROS during the phases of pathogenesis in Nicotiana tabacum [9]. Song et al. [10] indicated that overexpression of NtLTP1.38 enhanced heat tolerance in tobacco by improving osmoregulation and antioxidant capacity. Besides these, nsLTPs have been demonstrated to be involved in hormone signalling pathways. Zhang et al. [11] found that overexpression of GhLTP4 in cotton improves drought tolerance by remodeling lipid profiles, regulating ABA homeostasis, and modulating the tricarboxylic acid cycle. Yang et al. [12]. confirmed that tobacco NtLTP1.38 enhanced salt tolerance by regulating lipid biosynthesis, antioxidant enzyme activities, ion homeostasis, and ABA signaling pathways. Recent advances have further uncovered that nsLTPs exert biological functions via direct protein–protein interactions: kenaf (Hibiscus cannabinus L.) HcnsLTP111 interacts with the stress-responsive protein HcIPCS1 to modulate ROS homeostasis under drought and salt stress [13]. These studies highlight the multifaceted involvement of nsLTPs in stress response pathways, while their specific biological roles and regulatory mechanisms in P. fortunei remain largely uncharacterized.

P fortunei is a fast-growing tree species with remarkable adaptability to diverse soil conditions. Its high photosynthetic efficiency and soil-improving root system make it a typical pioneer species for ecological functions in China [14]. Beyond this, it is valued for its timber, ornamental flowers, and medicinal fruits, leading to its widespread introduction and cultivation globally [15–17]. Previous studies have demonstrated that drought and salt stress significantly impair the physiology of P. fortunei, inducing stomatal closure, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and membrane disruption characterized by increasing lipid peroxidation, relative permeability, and malondialdehyde content [18]. Notably, these stress conditions were accompanied by pronounced changes in nsLTPs expression levels [19]. However, the function of the nsLTPs gene family in P. fortunei has not been reported. In this study, we performed a comprehensive genome-wide identification and bioinformatic analysis of the nsLTPs gene family in P. fortunei. The gene structure, conserved motifs, and phylogenetic relationships were examined. This study also predicted the interaction between PfnsLTPs and their target proteins to explore how these proteins respond to drought and salt stresses. Specifically,The interaction between PfnsLTPs and their target protein was confirmed through BiFC and Pull-down assays. These findings provide insights into the molecular mechanisms of PfnsLTPs-mediated stress responses, which lay a theoretical foundation for the molecular breeding of Paulownia and other related tree species.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatment

Tissue-cultured P. fortunei seedlings (PF) were obtained from the Paulownia Institution of Paulownia, Henan Agricultural University. Apical buds 1.5 cm long from 30-day-old seedlings were cultured on 1/2MS medium at 25 ± 2 °C under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod.(130 μmol·m−2·s−1). Seedlings (5–7 cm tall) were transferred to pots (15 cm in diameter) containing standard potting soil and were acclimatized in a greenhouse for 10 d. Then the roots of uniformly PF seedlings were carefully separated from the soil. Afterwards, the entire root systems were fully immersed separately in 15% polyethylene glycol 6000 (PEG 6000) as moderate drought stress, and those were immersed in 100 mM NaCl solution as moderate salt stress, and PF control seedlings were immersed in nutrient solution without stress agents. PF seedlings subjected to drought treatments were named PFD, and PF seedlings subjected to salt treatments were named PFS. After 24 h, the second pair of leaves at the shoot apex were collected from aboved seedlings. Leaves from ten individual plants were pooled as one sample, and three biological replicates were prepared for each treatment. immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for RNA extraction. Nicotiana benthamiana seeds were sown in July 2025; seedlings were transplanted at the four-true-leaf stage and used for transient transformation 20 days later. Growth conditions were the same as those for P. fortunei.

Identification of the PfnsLTPs gene family elements

To comprehensively identify members of the nsLTP gene family in P. fortunei, a two-step search strategy was employed based on the P. fortunei genome database (version V1.0). Firstly, based on the AtnsLTP gene dataset of A.thaliana characterized by Edstam et al. [7], download the proteome data of the nsLTP family from the TAIR database (https://www.arabidopsis.org/index. jsp), then a BLASTP search (E-value < 1e-5) was performed against the P. fortunei proteome using A. thaliana nsLTP protein sequences as queries. Secondly, Hidden Markov Model (HMM) profiles corresponding to the nsLTP conserved domains (PF00234 and PF14368) were retrieved from the InterPro database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/interpro), and the Hmmer search (version 3.0) [20] was used to screen the P. fortunei proteome with an E-value threshold of 1e-5.Candidate sequences obtained from both methods were pooled. We eliminated proteins that lacked a canonical nsLTP signal peptide, had an abnormal protein length outside the typical nsLTP range, had an incomplete 8CM core motif, and were closely homologous cysteine—rich secretory proteins annotated as non—nsLTP family through Pfam and phylogenetic analysis.These filters were applied to avoid false positives, preserve the conserved structural characteristics of nsLTP members, and prevent biased results in subsequent subfamily classification and functional analysis. All candidate proteins were subsequently validated using the SMART database (http://smart.embl-heidelberg. de/), and the NCBI Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi. nlm.nih.gov/cdd/). Finally, the high—confidence, non—redundant sequences were named as P. fortunei PfnsLTP family members, and their intersection was visualized using a Venn diagram.

Chromosomal localization and physicochemical property analysis of PfnsLTPs

Chromosomal positions of PfnsLTP genes were mapped using TBtools—Ⅱ (version 2.458) [21] based on genome annotation data. Gene collinearity analysis of PfnsLTP was also performed. The MCScanX [22] algorithm was used to align the PfnsLTP protein sequences, and TBtools-II (version 2.458) [21] was employed to construct a Circos plot to visualize the results. Gene nomenclature was assigned according to phylogenetic clustering with A. thaliana nsLTPs and the conservation of the characteristic cysteine motif topology 8 CM, ensuring consistent subfamily classification. The physicochemical properties of PfnsLTP proteins, including molecular weight (MW), theoretical isoelectric point (pI), grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY), and instability index, were analyzed using the ExPASy ProtParam online tool (https://web.expasy.org/protparam). Subcellular localization was predicted using the WoLF PSORT platform (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Phylogenetic analysis and subfamily classification of PfnsLTPs

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships among PfnsLTP proteins, multiple sequence alignments were carried out using the ClustalW algorithm implemented in MEGA (version 11.0) [23], which incorporated both PfnsLTP and AtnsLTP protein sequences. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the neighbor-joining (NJ) method [24, 25] with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate node support. Other parameters were set to default values. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated using the iTOL online tool (https://itol.embl.de/). Based on phylogenetic clustering with AtnsLTP subfamilies, PfnsLTP members were classified into distinct subfamilies.

Gene structure and conserved motif analysis of PfnsLTPs

The exon–intron organization of PfnsLTP genes was determined based on the P. fortunei genome annotation file and visualized using the GSDS 2.0 online platform (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/). To identify conserved motifs within PfnsLTP proteins, the MEME suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/) was employed with the following parameters: the maximum number of motifs to identify was set to 10, and the motif width ranged from 6 to 50 amino acids. The identified motif patterns were visualized using TBtools-Ⅱ (version 2.458) [21].

Analysis of cis-acting elements in PfnsLTPs

The promoter regions upstream of the CDS of PfnsLTP family members, spanning 2000 bp, were analyzed for cis regulatory elements using TBtools-Ⅱ (version 2.458) [21] and the Plantcare website to identify the cis-regulatory elements present in the promoter regions of the PfnsLTP family members.

Transcriptome analysis and validation of PfnsLTPs under drought and salt stresses

Absolute transcript abundance of PfnsLTP genes under drought and salt stress was quantified using raw FPKM values. Comparing drought-treated samples to controls (PF vs. PFH) and salt-treated samples to controls (PF vs. PF044). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on (log₂FC) calculated from averaged FPKM values of stress-treated and control groups. Genes with (|log2FC|≥ 1) and adjusted P-value < 0.05 were regarded as DEGs. Genes responsive to both stresses were identified by intersecting the DEGs sets from each treatment. Expression patterns were visualized as heatmaps using the TBtools-Ⅱ (version 2.458) [21] software.

Total RNA was extracted from Paulownia seedlings using the FastPure Universal Plant Total RNA Extraction Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China), and cDNA synthesis was performed with the 2 × RealStar Fast SYBR qPCR Mix Kit (GeneStar, Beijing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted on an Applied Biosystems™ 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with the β-Actin gene as the internal reference,the reliability of β-Actin as a reference gene as reported in previous studies [26, 27], PCR was performed using a Vazyme kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) with a 20 μL volume and 200 ng cDNA per reaction. Relative expression levels were calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. Each experiment was performed in triplicate. Statistical analysis and data visualization were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 10.1.2) (San Diego, CA, USA). Gene-specific primers are listed in Table S1.

Protein interaction prediction

Protein interaction predictions were made using the String website (https://cn.string-db.org/). Additionally, the AlphaFold3 protein interaction prediction website (https://golgi.sandbox.google. com/) was employed to predict interactions among the candidate proteins identified. Pair with an interface predicted template modeling score (ipTM) > 0.7 were regarded as high confidence in the predicted interaction interface.

Verification of protein–protein interacting by yeast two-hybrid

Yeast Two-Hybrid (Y2H) was used to assess the protein interaction analysis, pGBKT7-PfnsLTPd-5 and pGADT7-PfGELP24, pGBKT7-PfnsLTPg-3 and pGADT7-PfGELP24 were separately co-transformed into AH109 cells. Transformed yeast was first cultured on SD/-Trp/-Leu medium at 30 °C for 3 days, and colonies were subsequently screened on SD/-Trp/-Leu/-Ade/-His medium. Protein interactions were determined based on reporter gene activation.

Verification of protein–protein interacting in vivo and vitro

The BiFC vectors were constructed using the pNC-BiFC system (pNC-BiFC-Ecc/Enc) following the manufacturer’s protocol (pNC Plant BiFC Vector Construction Kit; http://ncloding.com/productinfo/827181.html). Specifically, we generated PfGELP24-Ecc and PfnsLTPd-5-Enc fusion constructs. These constructs were subsequently transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101-pSoup-P19. For agroinfiltration, bacterial cultures carrying the BiFC constructs were resuspended to OD600 = 0.6 in infiltration buffer (10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM MES, 150 µM acetosyringone, pH 5.6). Equal volumes of PfGELP-Ecc and PfnsLTPd-5-Enc suspensions were mixed, incubated for 2 h at room temperature, and then co-infiltrated into leaves of 5-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana. Fluorescence signals were observed 72 h after infiltration using a high-resolution confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1 HD25) with appropriate filter sets for eGFP detection. Image acquisition parameters were kept consistent across all samples to enable comparative analysis.

A GST pull-down assay was conducted to further substantiate the interaction between PfnsLTPd-5 and PfGELP24. Briefly, the coding sequence of PfnsLTPd-5 was cloned into the pMAL-c5x vector to generate MBP-PfnsLTPd-5, while PfGELP24 was cloned into the pGEX-6P-1 vector to generate GST-PfGELP24. Escherichia coli cells transformed with the empty pGEX-6P-1 vector were used as a negative control. The recombinant plasmids expressing MBP-PfnsLTPd-5 and GST-PfGELP24 were separately transformed into Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) competent cells, expressed, and purified. Protein expression was induced using isopropyl-β-D-thiogalactoside (IPTG) with a final concentration of 0.5 mM at 16 °C for 16 h. The soluble GST fusion proteins were extracted and immobilized on glutathione agarose beads (Solarbio, Beijing, China) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. GST-PfGELP24 was used as the bait protein immobilized on glutathione agarose beads, while MBP-PfnsLTPd-5 served as the prey protein. The immobilized GST or GST-PfGELP24 was then incubated with PfnsLTPd-5-MBP protein. Following SDS-PAGE fractionation, the proteins were detected with an anti-GST (Yeasen, 30901ES50) and anti-MBP (Proteintech, 66,003–1-Ig) antibodies.

Subcellular localization detection

To investigate the subcellular localization of the PfnsLTPd-5 protein, we constructed an overexpression vector using 35S::PfnsLTPd-5-eGFP, with 35S::PRI101-eGFP (empty vector) serving as the negative control. The recombinant plasmids were introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101-pSoup-P19 via the freeze–thaw transformation method. Transformed Agrobacterium cultures carrying 35S::PfnsLTPd-5-eGFP or 35S::PRI101-eGFP were cultured in LB liquid medium supplemented with rifampicin (50 mg·L−1) and kanamycin (50 mg·L−1) until the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation at 3,500 rpm and resuspended in infiltration buffer (10 mM MES, 10 mM MgCl₂, 100 μM acetosyringone, pH 7.5). After incubation at room temperature for 2 h, the bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into the leaves of 5-week-old Nicotiana benthamiana plants using the agroinfiltration method. Following infiltration, the plants were incubated in the dark for 12 h to facilitate T-DNA transfer. At 48 h post-infiltration (hpi), the infiltrated leaf tissues were excised and observed under a high-resolution confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon A1HD25) for subcellular localization analysis. The scale bar is indicated in the figures.

Results

Genome-wide identification and chromosomal distribution of the PfnsLTPs

A total of 124 potential members were obtained through initial BLASTP and HMM searches. After removing 46 unqualified nsLTP candidates. Finally, a total of 78 PfnsLTP members were identified based on the 8CM conserved domain through CDD search. Chromosomal localization analysis revealed a heterogeneous distribution of the 78 PfnsLTP members across the genome (Fig. 1). Of these, 60 members were mapped to 19 distinct chromosomes, while the remaining 18 members were located on unanchored scaffolds. In which, chromosome 19 harbored the highest gene density with nine members, followed by chromosome 9 with seven members. Notably, no PfnsLTPs were detected on chromosome 18. Furthermore, we observed several gene clusters on chromosomes, such as ch9 and ch19, and a total of 12 collinear gene pairs were screened out (Fig.S1). The PfnsLTPs member names and physicochemical properties information were shown in Table S2. Phylogenetic analysis of the PfnsLTPs

A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the 78 PfnsLTP proteins together with A. thaliana AtnsLTPs. Gene subfamilies were classified by their sequence similarity to A. thaliana homologs. The phylogenetic tree resolved a total of seven subfamilies (Type 1–7) (Fig. 2). Notably, the Type C clade exclusively contains A. thaliana AtnsLTPs, with no orthologous genes identified in P. fortunei. Accordingly, PfnsLTP members were distributed across only six subfamilies: including 4 major categories (Type 1, Type 2, Type d and Type g) and 2 auxiliary subtypes (Type e, Type x). Among these, Type 1 subfamily is the largest one with 26 members, followed by the Type g subfamily with 19 members, while type e is the smallest subfamily, containing only five PfnsLTPs. Notably, the Type c subfamily has no PfnsLTP homologues and only includes several AtnsLTPs members. The loss of Type C homologs in P. fortunei points to lineage-specific evolutionary divergence of nsLTPs [6, 7, 28, 29]. Conserved motif and gene structure analysis of the PfnsLTPs

A total of 10 conserved motifs were identified among the 78 PfnsLTP members (Fig. S2). Motif 1, motif 5, and motif 4 frequently occurred in tandem, with motif 1 being the most prevalent, present in 91.03% of PfnsLTPs. In contrast, motif 9 was detected in only three proteins, all belonging to the Type 2 subfamily. Type 1 subfamily generally harbored motifs 1, 2, 3, and 6. The Type d and Type g subfamilies mostly contained motifs 1, 4, and 5. The Type x subfamily exhibited a motif composition similar to Type 1, generally containing motifs 2, 3, and 6. These observations suggest that PfnsLTP members within the same subfamily share conserved structural features, which supports the robustness of the phylogenetic classification.

Analysis of gene structure revealed considerable structural variation among PfnsLTPs. The coding sequences (CDSs) of PfnsLTPs accounted for > 80% of the total gene length, and the majority of these CDS fragments were less than 500 bp (Fig. S2). In terms of intron organization, genes within the same phylogenetic clade presented highly conserved distribution patterns, whereas obvious structural differences existed between different clades. Collectively, All these motif-based classification evidences supports the results of phylogenetic clustering.

Promoter cis-regulatory element analysis of PfnsLTPs

A total of 24 distinct regulatory elements were identified, which could be categorized into four functional classes: light-responsive elements, hormone-responsive elements, stress-responsive elements, and physiological/metabolic regulatory elements (Fig. 3). Light- responsive elements were ubiquitously distributed across the PfnsLTP family. Hormone- responsive elements were detected in 77 members, with PfnsLTP2-6 being the sole exception. Stress-responsive elements were identified in 68 PfnsLTPs, while physiological and metabolic regulatory elements were present in only 44 members. Further examination of stress-responsive elements revealed that 49 PfnsLTPs (63%) contained MYB-binding sites (MBS)(Table S3) associated with drought response. Notably, this element was particularly enriched in the Type d subfamily, where 11 of 13 members (85%) harbored MYB-binding sites (MBS). Additionally, 59 PfnsLTPs (76%) contained ABA-responsive elements (ABREs), which are known to mediate abscisic acid signaling during stress adaptation. Within the Type g subfamily, 16 of 19 members (84%) carried ABREs, indicating a preferential association of this subfamily with ABA-dependent stress signaling pathways. Verification of PfnsLTPd-5 and PfGELP24 interaction by BiFC and GST pull-down assays

Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays were subsequently performed to validate the physical association betweenPfnsLTPd-5 and PfGELP24. Distinct YFP yellow fluorescence was solely visualized in the co-expression group of PfnsLTPd-5 and PfGELP24, with no signals present in controls. Discussion

As small-molecule basic proteins widely present in plants, nsLTPs play a crucial role in lipid transport, cell wall development, antioxidant regulation, and cell membrane homeostasis [30]. Along with the acceleration of the molecular breeding process, nsLTPs, as key genes for stress resistance, have been studied in multiple species. Paulownia, a unique fast-growing tree species in China, gives full play to its ecological and economic values such as landscaping, wood utilization, and ecological protection, and has certain adaptability to adverse environments like drought and salinity. However, the molecular characteristics and stress-resistant regulatory mechanisms of the nsLTPs family have not been systematically analyzed, and the interaction relationship between PfnsLTPs and other stress-resistant key genes remains unclear. In this study, we identified 78 PfnsLTPs members, which exceeds that of A.thaliana (49) [6], Maize (63) [31], and Populus trichocarpa (42) [32]. Based on our previous genomic data, the P. fortunei genome contains 31,985 protein-coding genes [33], giving a relative PfnsLTPs family abundance of 0.2439% (78 members/31,985 genes). After normalizing against the total number of protein-coding genes from each reference genome, the relative abundance of AtnsLTPs was calculated as 0.1772% for A. thaliana [34], 0.1596% for maize [35], and 0.1210% for P.trichocarpa [36] (Table S4).Suggesting that the relatively higher abundance of the PfnsLTP family in P.fortunei, which lays a valuable genetic resource for excavating more stress-resistant targets.

Structural conservation and stress response characteristics of PfnsLTPs

The structural characteristics of the nsLTPs family constitute the molecular basis for their involvement in drought resistance regulation. nsLTP possess a conserved 8-cysteine motif domain, which can form a stable hydrophobic binding pocket. The specific structure is critical for binding hydrophobic molecules such as lipids and cutin monomers, and also serves as a core prerequisite for their participation in plant epidermal barrier formation and maintenance of cell membrane integrity [37]. Phylogenetic analysis reveals that the PfnsLTP family can be divided into six subfamilies, and members of different subfamilies exhibit significant divergence. These differences may related to their functional specificity. Promoter cis-regulatory element analysis indicated that most of PfnsLTPs are enriched in drought-responsive cis-acting elements including MBS. These conserved domains and drought-responsive elements have also been confirmed in A.thaliana [6], Setaria italic [38], and Nicotiana tabacum [39], indicating that the structural conservation of PfnsLTPs members and the distribution of stress-responsive elements are prerequisites for rapidly sensing drought signals and activating drought resistance responses, which also provides a structural biological basis for screening key candidate genes for drought resistance.

Potential functional of PfnsLTPs and their interacting proteins in drought and salt tolerance

In this study, PfnsLTPd-4, PfnsLTPd-5, PfnsLTPd-13, and PfnsLTPg-3 were consistently high expression under drought and salt stress treatments. Y2H, BiFC and GST pull-down validated that PfnsLTPd-5 interacted with PfGELP24, PfnsLTPg-3 is predicted to interact with PfGELP24, and PfnsLTPd-4 is predicted to interact with PfGELP84. GELP functions in lipid metabolism, cutin and wax deposition, lignin accumulation, and ROS detoxification [40, 41]. Ursache et al., [42] demonstrated that GELP could enhance suberin cross -linking via esterification, reducing water loss and maintaining osmotic balance under drought stress. Similarly, Wang et al., [43] reported that salt stress induces the expression of LpGDSL in Lilium pumilum, the activated LpGDSL accelerates lignin deposition and facilitates ROS scavenging, thereby improving the salt tolerance of the plants. While PfnsLTPd-13 is predicted to pectin methylesterases (PMEs), which can regulate cell wall properties by modulating the degree of pectin methylesterification, thereby influencing cell wall porosity, mechanical strength, and ion permeability [44, 45]. Etchells et al. [46] reported that PME5 regulates cell turgor and expansion by remodeling pectin methylation and interacting with expansins like EXPA17. Correspondingly, nsLTPs function as lipid transporters to shuttle cutin and wax precursors (fatty acids and glycerolipids) toward the cell wall surface, supporting the development of hydrophobic defensive layers. Therefore, we speculate that PfnsLTPd-13 may cooperates with PMEs to suppress Na⁺ entry under salt stress, and lessen drought-related water loss by adjusting cell wall hydrophobic and mechanical features. Taken together, these results indicate that PfnsLTPd-4, PfnsLTPd-5, PfnsLTPg-3, and PfnsLTPd-13 jointly improve drought and salt tolerance in P. fortunei. They may act via complementary regulatory pathways, including cuticle strengthening, ROS homeostasis maintenance and cell wall remodeling.Further transgenic overexpression and gene knockout assays are needed to verify their exact biological functions.

Conclusion

Our study identified 78 PfnsLTP genes in P. fortunei. Bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) and GST pull-down assays revealed that PfnsLTPd-5 physically interacts with PfGELP24, implying that these two proteins act synergistically to mediate plant stress tolerance. This work presents promising genetic targets for the molecular breeding of stress-resilient P. fortunei varieties.However, the biological functions of PfnsLTPd-5, as well as the molecular mechanism by which the physical interaction between PfnsLTPd-5 and PfGELP24 modulates intracellular signal transduction, remain to be further elucidated. Future research on the PfnsLTP family will combine in-depth analyses of motif characterization to consolidate the evolutionary classification.
