Poverty and poor choices: socioeconomic gradients of unhealthy behaviors among adults in a multi-center cross-sectional study in Iran
Authors: Satar Rezaei, Pegah Arefhosseini, Nasim Badiee, Hedayat Salari, Mohammad Bazyar, Mohammad Ranjbar, Eshagh Barfar, Seyed Fahim Irandoost, Javad Moghri, Edris Kakemam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveWhile the association between socioeconomic status (SES) and mortality/morbidity is well-documented, the specific relationship between SES and unhealthy behaviors in countries like Iran remains understudied. Therefore, this study aims to examine the socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of seven unhealthy behaviors among 3,518 adults from nine provinces in Iran.ResultsSignificant socioeconomic status (SES)-related disparities were observed across multiple health behaviors. The poorest groups exhibited higher rates of physical inactivity (53.4% in the poorest vs. 41.2% in the richest; inequality ratio: 1.3), infrequent dental flossing (63.7% in the poorest vs. 30.1% in the richest; inequality ratio: 2.1), infrequent tooth brushing (27.4% in the poorest vs. 8.5% in the richest; inequality ratio: 3.2), poor sleep quality (27.2% in the poorest vs. 9.7% in the richest; inequality ratio: 2.8), and breakfast skipping (58.2% in the poorest vs. 47.6% in the richest). Conversely, alcohol consumption was more prevalent among the richest group (22.5% in the richest vs. 10.7% in the poorest; inequality ratio: 0.5). Current smoking rates were 26.5% for the poorest and 21.2% for the richest. Concentration index analysis revealed pro-poor gradients for all unhealthy behaviors except alcohol consumption (CI = 0.1041, p < 0.001), with notable pro-poor gradients observed for physical inactivity (CI= -0.0586, p = 0.002), poor sleep quality (CI= -0.1997, p < 0.001) and tooth brushing (CI= -0.2542, p < 0.001). The sex-stratified analysis revealed distinct socioeconomic patterns: physical inactivity exhibited stronger pro-poor gradients in males (CI= -0.0701) compared to females (CI= -0.0483).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07426-6.Keywords: Unhealthy behaviors, Socioeconomic status, Inequality, Iran

Introduction

Unhealthy behaviors substantially increase chronic disease risk [1–3]. These behaviors are interconnected and influenced by broader social and environmental factors. This clustering may contribute to a cumulative impact on health risks. For instance, studies have shown that individuals engaging in multiple unhealthy behaviors are more likely to experience chronic diseases such as cardiovascular issues, diabetes, and mental health disorders [4]. Additionally, the clustering of these behaviors amplifies their negative effects, as seen in research linking smoking and physical inactivity to higher risks of premature morbidity and mortality [5]. According to Ford et al. (2012), avoiding smoking reduced the risk of early death by 56%, while regular physical activity lowered it by 47%, and maintaining a healthy diet decreased the risk by 26% [6].

Health behaviors encompass a range of lifestyle practices shaped by individual choices and structural opportunities, which are intrinsically linked to socioeconomic status (SES) as measured by education, income, and occupation [7, 8]. These behaviors are not merely personal decisions but are deeply influenced by the resources and constraints associated with an individual’s socioeconomic position. Higher SES individuals generally have better access to healthcare services, healthier food options, and health literacy resources, enabling them to adopt healthier lifestyles. Conversely, those with lower SES face structural barriers that limit their ability to engage in health-promoting behaviors. For instance, Lazzarino et al. (2014) compared representative samples from England and Thailand and found that unhealthy behaviors were more prevalent among lower SES) groups in England, whereas this association was less pronounced or even reversed in Thailand, likely due to cultural and contextual differences [9]. These findings highlight the complexity of the relationship between SES and health behaviors across different settings.

While global studies [10–14], have extensively documented the association between socioeconomic status and individual health behaviors, and some Iranian investigations [15, 16] have examined specific outcomes, there remains a critical gap: no study has applied concentration index methods to analyze co-occurring unhealthy behaviors across diverse provinces in Iran. Our study addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive, multi-behavior, multi-region assessment of socioeconomic inequalities, offering more actionable insights for targeted public health interventions.

Addressing unhealthy behaviors collectively is increasingly recognized as an effective public health strategy [17]. This approach acknowledges that behaviors such as smoking, physical inactivity, and poor dietary habits often cluster and share common social and environmental determinants. Findings from this study can guide policymakers in implementing more equitable, evidence-based health interventions in Iran.

Methods and materials

This cross-sectional study (2024–2025) examined socioeconomic inequalities in seven unhealthy behaviors among 3,518 Iranian adults aged 18 years and older, drawn from nine provinces representing approximately 41% of Iran’s population (34.9 million residents). Samples were selected using a multistage sampling method. First, Iran was divided into nine regions, and one province was randomly selected from each region. Within each selected province, the capital city was chosen and further divided into five areas (north, south, west, east, and central). From each area, 100 participants were selected via convenience sampling. Due to its larger population size, Tehran was similarly divided into five regions (north, south, west, east, and central), with 100 participants chosen from each. A total of 192 incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the initial 3,710 samples, resulting in a final sample size of 3,518. This approach ensured a diverse sample representing different socioeconomic strata within urban settings.

Data collection was conducted through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary 1: English version). The first part gathered sociodemographic information, including age, sex, education, monthly household income, health insurance status, and chronic disease history. The second part comprised seven questions assessing participants’ engagement in unhealthy behaviors.

We selected seven behaviors based on national burden of disease data and established public health frameworks, including the WHO NCD Global Monitoring Framework and the IraPEN program [18, 19]. These unhealthy behaviors have well-established associations with adverse health outcomes and are important for public health monitoring. The behaviors analyzed in this study include poor physical activity, smoking, alcohol consumption, poor sleep quality, tooth brushing, dental flossing, and breakfast skipping.

Poor physical activity is a key modifiable risk factor for chronic diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and cancer. Participants reporting at least 150 min per week of moderate-intensity activity or 75 min per week of vigorous-intensity activity were classified as having good physical activity; others were classified as having poor physical activity. Smoking status, assessed by asking whether participants currently smoke, formerly smoked, or never smoked, remains a major preventable risk factor for many chronic diseases. Alcohol consumption was included as it is a recognized risk factor for various health problems. Sleep quality, linked to multiple physical and mental health issues, was evaluated through participants’ self-rated overall sleep quality over the past month and categorized as good or poor.

Oral hygiene practices were assessed by questions on daily tooth brushing and dental flossing, given that inadequate oral care affects both oral and systemic health. Finally, breakfast skipping, which has been associated with negative metabolic and cognitive effects, was also examined. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, while categorical variables are represented using frequencies and percentages. We also estimated the prevalence of each unhealthy behavior by calculating the proportion of participants exhibiting these behaviors within the sample.

Economic status of the participants was assessed based on their monthly household income. Participants were asked to report their total monthly household income in Iranian Rials (IRR) using the following categories: less than 5 million IRR, 5 to 10 million IRR, 10 to 15 million IRR, 15 to 20 million IRR, and more than 20 million IRR. According to their responses, participants were then classified into five groups ranging from the poorest to the richest.

We assessed income-related health inequalities by evaluating both the absolute and relative gaps, as well as the overall gradient of inequality. To start, we calculated absolute and relative (20:20) gaps to quantify disparities in prevalence of unhealthy behaviors across income groups. The absolute gap measures the difference in the prevalence of unhealthy behaviors between the bottom 20% (poorest) and the top 20% (richest) income groups. It is calculated by subtracting the prevalence in the richest 20% from that in the poorest 20%. The relative gap, or 20:20 ratio, compares the prevalence of unhealthy behaviors in the poorest 20% to that in the richest 20% by dividing the prevalence in the poorest group by that in the richest group. This ratio indicates how many times higher (or lower) the prevalence is among the poorest compared to the richest. We then illustrated the gradient of inequality using concentration curves, which plot the cumulative share of the health variable (in this study, unhealthy behaviors separately) against the cumulative proportion of the population, ranked by household income from poorest to richest. A concentration curve that follows the 45-degree line indicates perfect equality in the distribution of the outcome variable. If the curve lies below this line, the outcome is more prevalent among higher-income groups (positive concentration index, CI); if above, it is more concentrated among lower-income groups (negative CI) [20].

Results

Table 1 presents the descriptive characteristics of the 3,518 participants included in the study. The mean age of the participants was 38.2 ± 14.6 years. The majority of participants were married (58.1%) and resided in urban areas (81.2%). Regarding education, 47.2% held a Bachelor’s degree, while 18.4% had a Master’s or Ph.D. A significant proportion (89.8%) reported having basic health insurance. Approximately 24.9% of the participants had at least one chronic condition. The distribution of monthly household income varied, with 37.7% earning more than 20 million IRR. Self-rated health status was predominantly good (45.8%), with 18.5% rating it as very good. Over half of the participants reported physical inactivity (58.2%), while 14.5% reported alcohol consumption. The frequency distribution of co-occurring unhealthy behaviors among participants reveals a substantial burden of multiple risk factors within the population (Supplementary 2 Table S2). The most frequent co-occurring unhealthy behaviors were poor tooth brushing combined with poor dental flossing, observed in 10.5% of participants. Additionally, current smoking paired with breakfast skipping was reported by 10.4% of the study population.

Absolute and relative gaps in prevalence of unhealthy behaviors

Absolute and relative differences in the prevalence of unhealthy behaviors were calculated for the entire sample, as well as for the poorest and richest socioeconomic groups. The results are presented in Table 2. Poor sleep quality, poor dental flossing, and poor tooth brushing were reported by 12.4%, 39.5%, and 13.2% of participants, respectively. Breakfast skipping was reported by 49.8% of participants, and 16.9% were current smokers. Significant disparities were observed between the poorest and richest quintiles across several unhealthy behaviors. For instance, poor dental flossing was reported by 63.7% of individuals in the poorest quintile, compared to 30.1% in the richest quintile, yielding an inequality ratio of 2.1 and an inequality gap of 33.6% points. Similarly, poor tooth brushing prevalence was over three times higher in the poorest group (27.4%) versus the richest (8.5%), with an inequality ratio of 3.2 and a gap of 18.9% points. Poor sleep quality was also notably more common among the poorest quintile (27.2%) compared to the richest (9.7%), corresponding to an inequality ratio of 2.8 and a gap of 17.5% points. In contrast, alcohol consumption was more prevalent among the richest quintile (22.5%) than the poorest (10.7%), resulting in an inequality ratio of 0.5 and a negative inequality gap of -11.8% points, indicating higher consumption in wealthier groups.

The concentration index and concentration curve findings

The concentration index (CI) and concentration curve (CC) analyses revealed distinct socioeconomic patterns in unhealthy behaviors (Table 3; Fig. 2). Behaviors such as physical inactivity, poor sleep quality, poor tooth brushing, and poor dental flossing demonstrated significant pro-poor inequalities, indicated by negative CI values (e.g., poor tooth brushing CI = -0.2542, p < 0.001) and CCs below the line of equality, reflecting higher prevalence among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Conversely, alcohol consumption exhibited a pro-rich pattern, with a positive CI (0.1041, p < 0.001) and CC above the equality line, indicating concentration among wealthier populations. Sex-stratified analyses further highlighted gender-specific trends: physical inactivity and poor dental flossing showed stronger pro-poor gradients in males and females respectively, while alcohol use was more concentrated among affluent females. Smoking presented mixed patterns, with a slight pro-poor tendency in males and a pro-rich tendency in females.

Discussion

This study reveals significant socioeconomic inequalities in unhealthy behaviors among adults, showing that individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds bear a higher burden of multiple risk factors, including poor dental hygiene, physical inactivity, inadequate sleep quality, and skipping breakfast. These findings suggest that economic challenges may limit access to essential resources, education, and supportive environments necessary for adopting healthy behaviors. Conversely, the higher alcohol consumption observed among wealthier groups indicates that social and cultural factors also influence health behaviors across different socioeconomic levels. The co-occurrence of several unhealthy behaviors—particularly among disadvantaged populations—underscores the need for integrated interventions. These behaviors often share common upstream determinants such as chronic stress, limited health literacy, and constrained access to supportive environments.

The findings of our study have also been observed in other countries. Gautam et al. (2025) reported that children and adolescents from lower socioeconomic backgrounds in Australia exhibited higher rates of unhealthy behaviors, including poor dietary habits and lower levels of physical activity, compared to their peers from higher socioeconomic groups [22]. Several factors contribute to the higher concentration of unhealthy behaviors among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. For example, poor sleep quality among individuals with lower economic status may be related to economic instability and environmental stressors commonly experienced in low-income households, such as overcrowded living conditions, neighborhood noise, and household tension [22–24]. Additionally, financial hardships and lower parental education are often associated with stressful environments that may contribute to disruptions in restful sleep [22]. These social and economic challenges play a significant role in undermining sleep quality, which is essential for overall health.

One important reason unhealthy behaviors tend to cluster within socioeconomically disadvantaged populations is that they often share common underlying mechanisms. First, shared stressors, such as financial hardship, neighborhood disorder, or job insecurity, create chronic stress that impairs individuals’ ability to maintain healthy routines. Second, limited agency—or the reduced sense of control and options available to individuals—leads to prioritizing immediate needs and coping strategies over long-term health behaviors. For example, when people feel powerless to change their circumstances, they may engage more frequently in behaviors that provide short-term relief but are harmful in the long run. Third, time constraints due to multiple jobs, caregiving responsibilities, or inflexible work hours reduce opportunities to engage in physical activity or prepare nutritious meals. Together, these factors contribute to the co-occurrence of multiple unhealthy behaviors within the same individuals or groups, reinforcing socioeconomic inequalities in health.

Our findings that physical inactivity is more concentrated among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups are consistent with results from a large national study in Iran using data from the PERSIAN cohort [25]. This comprehensive study, which included over 129,000 adults, reported significant regional variations in physical activity levels across the country. Importantly, it found that physical inactivity was more prevalent among individuals with lower socioeconomic status, and this pattern was observed in both men and women. Similar patterns have also been reported in studies from other countries, further supporting the association between socioeconomic disadvantage and higher rates of physical inactivity [26, 27]. The higher prevalence of physical inactivity among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may be driven by factors such as limited access to safe recreational spaces, lower health literacy, and greater time constraints due to economic pressures. These disparities highlight the need for targeted public health interventions that address social determinants of health to effectively promote physical activity in vulnerable populations.

Higher alcohol use among wealthier individuals likely reflects greater access despite cultural restrictions, consistent with socioeconomic consumption patterns reported in national surveys and studies of specific populations such as Iranian Kurds (10.1% in the richest vs. 5.4% in the poorest) [28–31]. Despite legal and cultural restrictions in Iran, alcohol consumption remains more accessible to wealthier groups due to affordability and private social settings. This may explain the higher reported use among these populations.

To address socioeconomic inequalities in unhealthy behaviors in Iran, targeted public health interventions are essential. Policymakers should focus on reducing risk factors and ensuring equitable access to preventive and therapeutic services, especially for disadvantaged groups. Strategies include fiscal policies such as subsidies for healthy foods and taxes on unhealthy products, expanding health literacy programs, and strengthening primary healthcare coverage in underserved areas. Integrating health promotion into schools and workplaces and tailoring interventions to local cultural and socioeconomic contexts can further reduce disparities. Increasing knowledge and awareness, particularly among low-income individuals, about the consequences of unhealthy behaviors can also help lower their prevalence and related inequalities. Multisectoral collaboration and ongoing policy evaluation are crucial for sustainable improvements in health equity in Iran.

Study limitations

This study had the following limitations:

Age restriction: The sample included only individuals aged 18 years and older, limiting the generalizability of the findings to younger populations who may have different health behaviors.

Cross-sectional design: The study’s design does not allow for causal inference, so the observed associations between socioeconomic status and unhealthy behaviors cannot establish directionality or changes over time.

Self-reported data: Behaviors such as smoking and alcohol consumption were self-reported, which may introduce social desirability bias and underreporting, especially given cultural and legal pressures in Iran. This could lead to underestimation of the true prevalence and affect the accuracy of associations.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant socioeconomic disparities in unhealthy behaviors among Iranian adults, with disadvantaged groups exhibiting higher rates of poor physical activity, poor tooth brushing, poor dental flossing, poor sleep quality, and breakfast skipping, while alcohol consumption was more common among wealthier individuals. These patterns reflect the complex interplay of economic status, cultural norms, and access to resources. To effectively reduce these disparities, public health strategies must integrate behavioral interventions with structural socioeconomic policies that address the root causes of inequality. Priority should be given to programs targeting multiple co-occurring unhealthy behaviors and tailored to gender-specific needs. Longitudinal research is needed to clarify causal pathways and evaluate the long-term effectiveness of integrated interventions. Reducing health disparities in Iran requires an intersectoral approach that combines behavioral health promotion with socioeconomic reforms. Such strategies are essential to achieving national health equity goals and improving quality of life for all population groups, in line with Iran’s Health Reforms and IraPEN initiatives.
