A scalable, and tunable braided yarn for micro and macro physiological sensing
Authors: Md Abdullah al Rumon, Jim Owens, Vignesh Ravichandran, Dhaval Solanki, Kunal Mankodiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Future wearables envision a fully integrated sensing system capable of monitoring vital signs and biomechanics in daily environments. Currently, achieving a balance between imperceptible sensing and seamless integration poses a significant challenge to developing durable and personalized wearable solutions. In this article, we report an ultra-thin, miniaturized, capacitive braided composite yarn (CBCY) that is scalable and tunable for both micro and macro sensing applications. By leveraging advanced braiding and winding techniques, we created a sensor that is conformable, robust, and can be seamlessly integrated into the flexible surface. To elucidate the sensor, we characterized its capabilities and provided comprehensive guidelines for diverse use cases. Additionally, to evaluate the sensor performance, two wearable applications are designed to monitor human respiration (micro-sensing) and elbow movements (macro-sensing). Our braided yarn offers a new array of features for wearable designers, ensuring imperceptible comfort and the unification of sensors and electronics for future smart textile applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18571-0.

Introduction

The vision of “imperceptible wearable electronics” has emerged as a transformative frontier in wearable technology, promising to seamlessly integrate sensing capabilities into our daily lives without compromising comfort and natural behavior1. This paradigm shift aims to turn everyday clothing and accessories into intelligent and adaptive interfaces capable of continuous health monitoring, allowing intuitive human-machine interaction2. The potential applications are vast, ranging from early disease detection and personalized medicine in healthcare to performance optimization in sports and more natural control interfaces for digital devices3,4.

Recent advances in materials science and flexible electronics have accelerated progress towards this vision. Researchers have explored various approaches to creating pliable and stretchable sensors that can be integrated into fabrics. These include conductive polymer composites5, metal-coated fibers6, and functional inks for textile printing7. Companies like Myant and Nextiles have made strides in incorporating conductive yarns into knitted fabrics, demonstrating the commercial potential of smart textiles8. Meanwhile, academic researchers have pushed the boundaries of sensor miniaturization and flexibility, with notable examples including electronic skin that can sense touch, temperature, and pressure9.

Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain in realizing truly imperceptible and reliable wearable sensing environments. Current textile-based sensors often struggle to achieve the sensitivity and reliability of traditional rigid electronics while maintaining the comfort and flexibility of regular fabrics10. Durability is a major concern, with many sensors showing degraded performance after prolonged wear11. Additionally, most existing solutions are optimized for either micro-scale (e.g., pulse, subtle muscle movements) or macro-scale (e.g., joint angles) measurements, but rarely together, limiting their versatility12.

The integration of sensing elements into textiles without compromising the fabric’s natural properties remains a significant hurdle. While progress has been made in incorporating conductive elements into fabrics, achieving uniform performance across large areas and complex garment shapes is still challenging13. Furthermore, many advanced textile sensors rely on complex, multi-step manufacturing processes that are difficult to scale to industrial levels, creating a gap between promising lab prototypes and commercially viable smart garments14.

Capacitive sensing has shown promise for wearable applications due to its high sensitivity, low power consumption, and compatibility with flexible substrates15. Recent studies have explored various approaches to textile-based capacitive sensors, including conductive fabric electrodes16, screen-printed sensors17, and fiber-based capacitors18. However, these designs often face challenges in achieving a wide dynamic range, maintaining sensitivity across different stretching conditions, or seamlessly integrating into textiles without compromising wear comfort19.

Addressing these multifaceted challenges requires an interdisciplinary approach that combines innovations in materials science, textile engineering, and electronics. There is a clear need for sensing solutions that can be readily incorporated into existing textile manufacturing processes to facilitate widespread adoption20. Ideally, such solutions would offer scalable fabrication, multi-scale sensing capabilities, and seamless integration with textiles, all while maintaining comfort and durability.

In this work, we present a new capacitive braided-composite yarn (CBCY) sensor that addresses many of these limitations. By leveraging advanced braiding techniques and composite materials, we have developed a versatile, durable, and easily integrable sensing platform. The CBCY sensor’s unique hierarchical structure enables differential sensing of both micro and macro-scale deformations within a single device, offering unprecedented versatility in wearable sensing applications. No direct quantitative comparisons exist to support the existing solutions regarding sensitivity, durability, and scalability.

Our comprehensive characterization demonstrates the CBCY sensor’s exceptional performance across various metrics, including response to different stretching speeds, hysteresis behavior, and long-term stability. To showcase its real-world potential, we developed two prototype wearable applications: a chest belt for respiratory monitoring and an elbow band for tracking joint movement. These examples demonstrate the sensor’s adaptability to various physiological sensing requirements and its potential to advance the field of imperceptible, multifunctional wearable technology.

Results

Capacitive braided yarn fabrication process

The fabrication technique for the capacitive braided composite yarn (CBCY) sensor, developed by Nautilus Defense21 and further characterized and reengineered in this paper, represents a significant advancement in wearable sensor technology. This process, illustrated in Fig. 1a–c, comprises three distinct stages, each contributing to the sensor’s unique internal architecture. The first stage involves the creation of the winded composite yarn (WCY), as shown in Fig. 1a. This fundamental multicomponent fiber bundle combines Kevlar and HPN (Heavy Poly Nylon)22 insulated copper filaments, leveraging their complementary properties. The thin, high-gauge copper provides flexibility, while the Kevlar compensates for the copper’s lower tensile strength and imparts longitudinal stability. Although alternative high-strength fibers like ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE) or carbon fiber might provide comparable tensile strength, Kevlar was selected for its well-documented balance of chemical stability, low elongation (due to its amide (-CO-NH-) linkages between aromatic rings), and ease of integration into textile-based sensor fabrication processes. Likewise, silver or stainless-steel fibers could be used for conductivity, but copper offers a favorable combination of high conductivity, ductility, and cost-effectiveness. Along with the insulation properties, the HPN layer minimizes friction between the copper and Kevlar.

The second stage, depicted in Fig. 1b, focuses on the production process of the braided composite yarn (BCY). This process intertwines three WCY strands, each containing helically wound copper filaments, with three bundles of Kevlar. The resulting coreless filament bundle exhibits a unique combination of high stiffness and natural flexibility due to its helical structure.

The final stage, illustrated in Fig. 1c, involves the creation of the CBCdY itself. This step integrates two sets of core spandex filaments with three BCY strands. The spandex, a polyurethane-polyurea copolymer, imparts overall stretchability to the sensor yarn. While other elastomers, such as silicone-based filaments or thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU), could be considered, spandex is already widely used in textile applications, ensuring straightforward integration and consistent repeatable mechanical behavior. The precise control of the yarn-drawing process, facilitated by take-up and tension rollers, ensures structural uniformity throughout the fabrication process. The resulting CBCY, with its sophisticated hierarchical structure, offers an unprecedented combination of sensing capabilities and mechanical properties. Figure 1d–f provides high-magnification images and size comparisons, revealing the intricate details of the 3 mm-wide braided yarn.

The fabrication process described above forms the basis for developing CBCY as a capacitive stretch sensor. Figure 1g, h shows the sensor’s capacitance sensing mechanism in stretched and unstretched states. The design uses three sets of copper filaments arranged in a double parallel plate configuration. The sensor’s mutual capacitance (Cmutual) is calculated from the arithmetic difference between two electrode configurations (Ca-GND) and (Cb-GND).

When the sensor experiences different stretch percentages (SP), the geometric relationships between the copper-copper, copper-Kevlar, and copper-spandex interfaces change, altering the sensor’s effective capacitive properties. As shown in Fig. 1m, the sensor’s average two-dimensional void area increases from 0.110 mm² in the unstretched state (SP1) to 0.661 mm² at 40% (SP2) strain. Interestingly, the void area then decreases to 0.501 mm² at 80% (SP3) stretch and further to 0.322 mm² at 120% (SP4) stretch. These voids and material rearrangements significantly impact the dielectric environment between conductive elements.

The dielectric constant (relative permittivity, εr) of the constituent materials dictates the sensor’s capacitive response. Kevlar (εr ≈ 3.85)23,24 and spandex- a polyurethane-based elastomer (εr ≈ 3–5)25 contribute moderate dielectric properties. In contrast, air has a very low dielectric constant (εr ≈ 1.0), so as voids expand and become air-filled during initial stretching, the average dielectric constant across the sensor decreases, reducing capacitance.

In the Micro range (MiR, 0–60% stretch), the structure undergoes unwrapping and expansion, causing increased void space and the introduction of more air into the dielectric path. This reduces the effective dielectric constant and results in a linear decrease in capacitance, with a sensitivity of − 0.302 mm⁻¹.

However, beyond ~ 40% strain, the structure begins to rewrap and compress due to tension in the composite materials. This brings copper elements into closer physical proximity, enhancing capacitive coupling despite the presence of the dielectric. Consequently, the effective dielectric constant increases due to the reduced air volume and greater influence from higher-εr materials. In the Macro range (MaR, 60–100% stretch), this leads to a linear increase in capacitance, though with lower sensitivity (0.039 mm⁻¹), due to limited further geometrical changes.

The sensor’s performance depends on two factors: the base stretch percentage of the sensor yarn and the expected stretch percentage during use. To test these properties, we developed a 15 cm long sensor base and tested it using a mechanical reciprocal system shown in Fig. 1k. We created an embedded system, shown in Fig. 1j, to measure the sensor’s capacitance. This system uses an ESP32 microcontroller and FDC1004, a 28-bit analog front-end chip, sampling at 50 Hz. An external battery powers the system to reduce electrical noise and ensure accurate measurements.

Characterization of the capacitive braided yarn

The CBCY sensor changes its capacitance when stretched by external forces. This change occurs due to variations in the sensor’s geometry, which affects its dielectric constant, the distance between conductive elements, and the effective area. Recovery time is another important factor for sensors, and textile materials typically have longer recovery times due to their viscoelastic properties. Because the two spandex filament sets serve as the core yarns, they bear the primary load during stretching and define the sensor’s overall elastic behavior. Kevlar and copper, braided around these cores, straighten or rewrap in response to the spandex’s elongation and recovery rather than relying on their own elastic limits. By acting as the main load-bearing component, spandex ensures uniform extension and retraction of the entire braided structure. Moreover, the spandex used in this study (10 denier, density ~ 1.20 g/cm³, ~ 50 μm diameter) provides both significant elasticity and a rapid recovery profile, allowing the sensor to withstand multiple stretch–release cycles without permanent deformation. When the applied stress is removed, it is the spandex’s elastic recoil, not the elasticity of Kevlar or copper, that restores the sensor to its original length, thereby delivering consistent and repeatable measurements across multiple tests.

Figure 2a shows the sensor’s performance when stretched up to 40% at five different speeds: S1 (150 mm/min), S2 (300 mm/min), S3 (450 mm/min), S4 (600 mm/min), and S5 (750 mm/min). In all cases, the base capacitance shifted by ~ 0.041%, which is negligible. We tested the sensor’s hysteresis by stretching 100 times at three distinct speeds (S1, S3, and S5) in 40% stretch, as shown in Fig. 2d, f. The base capacitance changed by up to 0.038%, which is minimal. At higher stretching speeds- S2 and S3 showed in d (ii) and d (iii), we observed differences in the sensor’s behavior during stretching (loading) and relaxing (unloading).

Figure 2d, f display the hysteresis performance in terms of capacitance versus stroke and stress versus stroke, respectively. For the S1 speed, the capacitance decreased from about ~ 42pF to ~ 38pF, with very little hysteresis between stretching and relaxing. The stress increased from about ~ 0.2 N to ~ 0.4 N, also showing minimal hysteresis. We saw similar results for the S3 speed.

However, at the S5 speed, we observed more pronounced hysteresis. The capacitance decreased from ~ 42pF to about ~ 38.5pF, with a noticeable difference between the stretching and relaxing curves. This indicates more energy dissipation during the cycles at higher speeds. The stress-strain relationship also showed greater hysteresis at S5, with stress increasing from ~ 0.2 N to ~ 0.4 N and a larger difference between the stretching and relaxing phases compared to S1 and S3.

The interactive and phase-changing experiment results are illustrated in Fig. 2b, c. The sensor was stretched up to 100% (150 mm) over the base length of the sensor of 15 cm. We dissected the sensor performance by stretching the sensor in five distinct ranges: 0–30 mm, 0–60 mm, 0–90 mm, 0–120 mm, and 0–150 mm. Figure 2c illustrates an additional minor peak that starts at 90 mm (60%) of stretch, which propagates extensively with the increase of stretch. For 120 mm (80%), the second peak becomes strongly visible with 39pF, and for 150 mm (100%), the peak (42.77pF) outsails the baseline (39.5pF). Similar performance was observed, which aligns more clearly when the test was separated from the base stretching in Fig. 2b, where the sensor dynamically changed the base stretching percentage. From 0 to 60 mm (40%), the capacitance decreased linearly no matter the initial strength percentage, but when the base stretching was placed over 40%, a second peak arises that propagates considerably. These characteristics represent a shift change of the sensor that solely depends on the functional stretch percentage and the initial stretch percentage. In other words, if the sensor is placed in 0% as the base stretch, the capacitance change will be negatively linear, but if the sensor is placed in 60% as the base, the capacitance change will be positively linear.

This behavior allows the sensor to operate in different linear regions depending on the pre-stretch condition. To interpret unknown stretch levels, such as within the 60–90 mm range, one must consider both the amplitude and shape of the signal. As shown in Fig. 2b, the signal amplitude varies significantly across displacement intervals, even when the waveforms appear visually similar in Fig. 2c. This enables displacement inference through amplitude thresholding or machine learning-based pattern recognition, providing a path for practical use even when the exact base strain is unknown.

The relative sensitivity of the sensor in terms of stress and stroke (stretch) can be defined as the slope of the traces in Fig. 2e, g by the consecutive regions. In Fig. 2e, the sensitivity negatively decreases when more stress is applied in the S1 region and positively increases in regions S2 and S3. Sensitivity was calculated − 0.2684 N− 1 in the 0 to 0.5 N region, 0.0667 N− 1 in 0.7 N to 1.3 N region, and 0.0445 N− 1 in 1.6 to 2.7 N region. In Fig. 2g, initially, the sensitivity negatively decreases in the S1 and S2 regions and then positively increases in the S3 and S4 regions. For strokes below 40 mm, 40–80 mm, 80–120 mm, and 120–150 mm, the sensitivities are − 0.0018 mm− 1, -0.0004 mm− 1, 0.0009 mm− 1, and 0.0033 mm− 1, respectively. Here, the sensitivity was highest in the lower stress region, also defined as the micro-sensing region (MiR) of 0–40% stretching. On the other hand, the second reliable sensitivity region lies in the higher stretch percentage defined as the macro-sensing region (MaR).

The sensing response of the CBCY was systematically evaluated over 2,000 repeated stretch–unstretch cycles at a 40% (60 mm) stroke and in S3 speed of 450 mm min⁻¹ (Fig. 2h). Under these conditions, the capacitance fluctuated between ~ 42.5 pF (unstretched) and ~ 37.5 pF (stretched) from cycle 0 to cycle 2000. Three highlighted segments labeled in blue, green, and red correspond to detailed magnifications of the capacitance behavior at intervals of 200–215, 1000–1015, and 1800–1815, respectively (Fig. 2h, i). Meanwhile, Fig. 2h(ii) depicts the normalized capacitance (C/Co) for both unstretched and stretched states over the same time intervals. From cycle 1 to 2000, the stretched baseline shifts from 1.000 to 1.011 (~ 1.1%), while the unstretched baseline shifts from 1.000 to 1.013 (~ 1.3%). Correspondingly, the absolute difference between unstretched and stretched capacitance increases from ~ 4.41 pF at 200 cycles to ~ 4.54 pF at 2000 cycles, indicative of a gradual internal rearrangement among the spandex, Kevlar, and copper.

Despite this modest drift, which remains under ~ 1.3% of the total signal range, CBCY continues to provide reliable strain measurements over prolonged mechanical loading. By normalizing the capacitance, minor drifts can be corrected to maintain consistent measurement fidelity. Consequently, while incremental realignments occur due to repeated stretching, the overall accuracy, reproducibility, and operational stability of the CBCY remain robust, underscoring its suitability for extended wearable or soft-robotic applications.

The ultimate strength of the CBCY was assessed via a uniaxial tensile test (Fig. 2-i), in which the sample was stretched continuously at 150 mm min⁻¹. A yield point (YP) emerged at approximately 158 mm, corresponding to ~ 105% elongation, beyond which both capacitance and stress rose markedly. This increase continued until reaching the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) at 182 mm, where the stress and capacitance peaked at 20 N and 55 pF, respectively, indicating a load transfer from spandex to Kevlar. Following a minor break, the stress and capacitance continued to climb in a near-linear fashion until the final fracture point (FP) at 219 mm (~ 146% stretch), at which the maximum recorded capacitance and fracture stress were 76 pF and 74 N, respectively.

Capacitive braided yarn-based physiological monitoring

To realize the physiological sensing application, two specific wearables were designed using the range classification model and tested. Figure 3 presents a comprehensive overview of the sensor tuning steps, fabrication process, and performance results for both elbow movement and respiration monitoring. Based on the interactive sensing mechanism, the results of the tested sensors were normalized and adopted a common sensing behavior. Later, the model (Fig. 3a) dissected the signal and identified the linear zones. Because of shifting the sensing variation, the overall signal was amplified into three stages of stretch percentage: (i) 1–40%, shows high negative sensitivity, (ii) 40–60%, shows low bidirectional sensitivity, and (iii) 60–99%, shows high positive sensitivity. This range-dependent behavior is crucial for optimizing the sensor’s performance in various physiological monitoring applications.

The fabrication specification was classified in Fig. 3b, where wearables were segmented as micro and macro sensing. For instance, respiration monitoring, classified as micro-sensing, requires detection of subtle chest movements, with average expansions of 3.74 cm for females and 3.72 cm for males. In contrast, elbow movement monitoring, categorized as macro-sensing, involves a broader range of motion, typically 130° to 154° for flexion and − 6° to 11° for extension. To accommodate these different requirements, we applied a 5% base stretch for the respiration sensor (monitoring inhale-exhale cycles) and a 60% base stretch for the elbow movement sensor (tracking flexion-extension). We enhanced the sensors’ structural integrity and stretch retention capabilities by adding a 50 μm silicone adhesive layer at room temperature (23 °C) and 30% humidity, which also increases the sensor’s base capacitance. This increased capacitance effect occurs primarily because silicone has a dielectric constant (ε ≈ 3.0–3.5)26, higher than air (ε ≈ 1.0). By replacing some of the air voids within the braided structure, the silicone layer raises the overall effective dielectric environment, thereby boosting the baseline capacitance. Additionally, the soft, low elastic modulus (100–125 kPa) minimizes excessive deformation and helps maintain the intended stretch percentage, further contributing to consistent capacitance readings over time.

Figure 3d, e presents the performance results of our wearable prototypes: the chest belt for respiration monitoring (Fig. 3b) and the elbow cuff for joint movement tracking (Fig. 3c). For respiratory monitoring, we initialized the sensor with a 5% base stretch. During inhalation, as the chest expanded, we observed a decrease in capacitance. Conversely, the exhalation resulted in an increase in capacitance. This clear, linear response allows for precise differentiation between inhalation and exhalation events. In Fig. 3d, blue peaks correspond to exhalation, while red valleys indicate inhalation, providing a clear visual representation of the breathing cycle.

The elbow movement sensor, shown in Fig. 3e, was fabricated with an initial 60% stretch to accommodate the wider range of motion. During testing, we found that hand flexion caused an increase in capacitance, while extension led to a decrease. The alternating blue and red data points in the graph collectively illustrate the flexion-extension cycle of the elbow joint.

These results demonstrate the CBCY sensor’s ability to adapt to different physiological monitoring needs through careful tuning of its base stretch and operating range. The sensor’s scalability and tunability make it a versatile platform for developing a wide range of future wearable applications, from subtle physiological monitoring to tracking large-scale body movements.

Despite these promising results, the scope of this research does not include comprehensive performance characterization under varying external conditions such as pressure, temperature, or humidity. Instead, our focus has been on examining the sensor’s internal mechanisms, structural characteristics, and viability for micro- and macro-scale applications. Future investigations would benefit from exploring how these environmental factors influence both the mechanical behavior and electrical response of the sensor, providing a more complete understanding of its potential for real-world implementations.

Discussion

In summary, we have presented a comprehensive development and characterization of a scalable and tunable capacitive braided yarn (CBCY) sensor. Our work encompasses the entire process, from fabrication to practical application, demonstrating the sensor’s versatility in various physiological monitoring scenarios.

We conducted extensive characterization of the CBCY sensor, focusing on its capacitive response to different stretch percentages, stretching speeds, and cyclic loading. Our analysis included detailed examinations of hysteresis behavior and tensile strength. A key finding is the sensor’s capacity to withstand at least 2000 continuous cycles with only nominal variations in relative capacitance, underscoring its durability and reliability. Furthermore, our fabrication approach yielded a sensor with remarkable tensile strength, maintaining functionality up to 146% stretch.

The CBCY sensor’s unique capacitance-shifting mechanism, coupled with the ability to adjust its base stretching, enables precise tuning for both micro and macro sensing applications. This adaptability is crucial for optimizing performance across a spectrum of physiological monitoring needs. To validate the sensor’s real-world applicability, we developed two wearable prototypes: a chest belt for real-time respiration monitoring and an elbow cuff for tracking flexion-extension movements. These applications demonstrate the sensor’s ability to capture both subtle physiological signals and larger-scale body movements accurately.

The tunable nature of our fabrication process positions the CBCY sensor as a promising candidate for a wide array of physiological monitoring applications. Beyond respiration and joint movement, potential applications include muscle activity monitoring, knee joint tracking, and mandibular movement assessment.

Looking forward, we envision the CBCY sensor serving as a foundational platform for developing advanced smart textile-based wearables. Its combination of flexibility, cost-effective fabrication, and broad manufacturing potential makes it an ideal choice for future innovations in wearable technology. This sensor technology opens new possibilities for creating comfortable, unobtrusive, and highly functional wearable devices for continuous health monitoring and human-machine interaction.

Methods

Materials

The manufacturing materials of the capacitive braided yarn and test components are described in the following sections.

Wind composite yarn (WCY)

We employed a parallel winding process to develop a multicomponent fiber bundle using insulated copper and Kevlar filaments. The copper filaments consisted of 46 AWG HPN (Heavy Poly Nylon) insulated wire22 with a 40-micron diameter. The Kevlar has a density of approximately 1.44 g/cm³ and a 0.7344 mm diameter. This winding process incorporated 12 sets of Kevlar alongside a single copper filament.

Braided composite yarn (BCY)

This step utilized the braiding machine using three sets of WCY to create a single BCY unit. The result came with a total of three copper filaments and 36 Kevlar filaments. BCY is the primary unit component for developing capacitive braided composite yarn.

Capacitive braided composite yarn (CBCY)

We utilized three individual sets of BCY to develop the Capacitive braided composite yarn. This stage involved braiding two center cores of spandex filaments (10 denier, density ~ 1.20 g/cm³, 50-micron diameter) with the three BCY. We utilized a braiding machine operating at 80 RPM for consistent production. The result came with a two-core spandex bundle and three groups of multifilament bundles, each consisting of three copper and 36 Kevlar bundles.

Sensor characterization

We developed a custom data acquisition system using an ESP32 microcontroller27 and an FDC1004 analog front-end chip28, sourcing components from Digikey. For mechanical testing, we used cotton-based denim fabric to minimize static charge, ensuring a secure grip during universal testing machine (UTM) procedures. Tests were conducted using a Shimadzu AGS-X29 series machine, programmed for time-synchronized measurements at a 50 Hz sampling frequency.

Wearable device design

Based on our sensor characterization results, we determined optimal base stretching percentages for micro and macro sensing applications. We constructed wearable prototypes (chest belt and elbow band) using stretchable soft ribbon, secured with vinyl-based Loctite fabric glue30. A 50 μm Ecoflex 00–3031 layer was added to enhance sensor placement and conformity.

Data collection system

We engineered a custom bioinstrumentation system for capacitance sensing and wireless data transmission. This system utilizes the FDC1004 chip for capacitance measurements and employs the ESP-NOW protocol for efficient peer-to-peer communication. This approach simplifies the communication process by operating at the data-link layer of the OSI model, allowing direct data exchange between devices via MAC addresses without the need for complex network infrastructure.
