PLATE-VS: a web server for protein–ligand assay curation and cross-target virtual screening datasets
Authors: Ao Xu, Yongchan Hong, Jordy Homing Lam, Vsevolod Katritch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPLATE-VS (Protein–Ligand Affinity-based Target Evaluation-Virtual Screening, https://www.drugbench.org/) is a free, openly accessible web server that integrates protein structural information, ligand activity data, and property-matched decoys to produce training-ready datasets for virtual screening and molecular machine learning. Unlike structure-only or assay-only resources, PLATE-VS addresses the bottleneck in obtaining clean protein–ligand datasets with principled train/test splits, spanning a range of difficulty, from those allowing higher levels of similarity with known ligand-receptor pairs to more challenging cross-target generalization. The server enables queries and inspection of data in various formats, returning interactive assay summaries with harmonized activities, curated metadata, a stratified panel of protein–ligand complexes, and downloadable split tables. These data and the associated Application Programming Interface can be used to facilitate the study of binding activity relationships in protein–ligand interactions.

Introduction

Structure-based virtual screening has become a cornerstone of computational drug discovery, enabling the prioritization of small-molecule candidates through protein–ligand interaction prediction at a scale beyond experimental reach. Machine learning and deep learning approaches have been widely employed for virtual screening, and recent advances have demonstrated substantial progress in both pose prediction and affinity modeling. Boltz-2 [1], for example, approaches free-energy-perturbation (FEP)-level accuracy, achieving Pearson correlations of 0.62 on an OpenFE subset of the FEP+ benchmark and 0.66 on a focused four-target FEP subset, supporting deep learning as a useful tool for accelerating drug discovery. Beyond affinity-focused predictors, recent protein–ligand learning methods span several distinct paradigms, including co-folding models for joint complex structure prediction such as AlphaFold 3 [2] and Chai-1 [3], as well as docking-oriented generative models such as NeuralPLexer3 [4] and DiffDock-L [5]. These methods have improved the prediction of bound complex geometries and, in some cases, enabled joint modeling of pose and affinity-related signals. However, recent analyses have also shown that strong apparent performance can still be confounded by memorization, stereochemical artifacts, and insufficiently controlled train–test similarity [6,7]. Taken together, these results suggest that further progress depends not only on better models but also on better data resources and benchmark design.

In particular, the field still lacks a dataset with a standardized format that covers a holistic spectrum of training and evaluation strategies, including affinity data for regression, geometries for structure posing, and decoys for contrastive retrieval power [6, 8–11]. While these raw data can be obtained from multiple primary sources, such as PDB [12], ChEMBL [13], and BindingDB [14], and can in principle be merged into a unified resource with overlapping information, such integration is not straightforward and is often confounded by data leakage arising from structural and chemical similarities. For instance, while PDBbind [8] has been the de facto gold standard for training and evaluation of protein–ligand scoring models because it combines affinity and structure data, a growing body of literature [9] suggests that, in the absence of principled clustering with respect to receptor similarity, models derived from it are highly susceptible to systematic bias and overfitting in affinity regression tasks.

Alternative clustered data sources such as PLINDER [15] and Runs-n-Poses [6] lack affinity annotations and are not specifically designed for protein–ligand binding affinity prediction; accordingly, they are better suited for pose benchmarking than for affinity-oriented training and evaluation. In addition, all the aforementioned datasets lack decoys, which are essential for realistic virtual screening tasks with an overwhelming number of negative non-binders. Although decoys can be generated through separate servers or algorithms such as DUD-E [11] and DEKOIS [10], a one-stop solution that simultaneously provides curated structural data, binding-affinity labels, and meaningful decoys while rigorously controlling for data leakage and chemical bias is still lacking.

To address this critical gap, we introduce PLATE-VS, a web server for protein–ligand assay curation and cross-target virtual screening dataset construction. PLATE-VS integrates structure-affinity data from multiple sources, including PDB [12], UniProt [16], and ChEMBL [17], and follows curation principles and preprocessing guidelines outlined in [18], resulting in 2 390 001 entries of active and decoy ligand binding data. Training and test partitions were then constructed using similarity-based binning across both protein and ligand dimensions, ensuring that sequence and chemical redundancy between splits is explicitly controlled and thereby providing a benchmark that minimizes structural leakage during the training and evaluation of machine learning models. Beyond dataset curation, PLATE-VS exposes a flexible query interface that supports retrieval of the closest entries, including both actives and their respective decoys, based on user-input chemicals and various metadata fields, enabling flexible but controlled access with configurable thresholds.

Materials and methods

PLATE-VS triage of data

PLATE-VS integrates both structural protein–ligand complexes and bioactivity annotations to retain data that are informative for virtual screening while minimizing obvious sources of noise and leakage (Fig. 1). Starting from PLINDER [15] as a structural resource, we selected a drug-focused subset of protein–ligand systems by filtering for high-quality biological assemblies, excluding low-resolution or overly complex entries, retaining only bona fide drug-like small-molecule ligands, and requiring reasonable protein–ligand interaction counts and ligand molecular weights. More details can be found in the Supplementary Data (Supplementary Data Retrieval and Cleaning Section). The resulting structures were then mapped to UniProt [16] targets and linked to ChEMBL [17] bioactivity records. ChEMBL annotations were further cleaned by restricting to exact Ki and IC50 measurements reported in nM, requiring high-confidence single-protein assays, removing mutant-related records and invalid entries, and excluding assays that were too small or excessively heterogeneous. To assess affinity label quality, we examined the cross-assay reproducibility for 14 076 unique compounds tested independently against the same target following the protocol described in [18]. This analysis reflects that the base filtering criteria in the PLATE-VS filtered set, consisting of 333 748 active records and 837 protein targets, is effective in producing consistent data. Qualified structure-affinity data are then clustered by pocket similarity following a community-detection based protocol in PLINDER [15] and by ligand similarity cutoffs. PLATE-VS provides property-matched decoys

Property-matched decoys are widely used in virtual-screening benchmarks to reduce trivial physicochemical bias and to test whether a method can distinguish active ligands from chemically plausible but topologically distinct alternatives [11, 19]. Because on-demand decoy generation is computationally expensive and many decoys can be reused across compounds with similar physicochemical profiles (see Table 2 for definition), PLATE-VS has precomputed a pool of 1.9 million cross-target decoys using DeepCoy [19]. For unseen user-submitted query SMILES, PLATE-VS enables property matching by computing molecular weight, clogP, hydrogen-bond donor and acceptor counts, rotatable-bond count, formal charge, and a Morgan fingerprint, and then retrieves decoys whose properties fall within the same matching windows used during curation. The matched candidates are ranked by fingerprint Tanimoto similarity to the query, and the top results are returned through the web interface and API. Property matching is implemented through PostgreSQL and the RDKit cartridge extension [20].

Strong cross-assay agreement (Pearson R 0.8) is observed in the minimal curation set (Fig. 1), though the maximal curation set yields even higher agreement (Pearson R 0.87), reflecting the quality of filtration as discussed. Note that stricter curation substantially reduces usable data points. Therefore, we recommend the PLATE-VS default set (333 748 data points; 237 873 unique active ligands) as the default resource for controlled virtual screening and machine-learning evaluation.

All three sets are stratified orthogonally as linkage clusters by considering pocket and ligand similarity separately. Protein targets are assigned to held-out clusters by linking UniProt entries with a Pocket Similarity (Pocket_Qcov) exceeding a threshold . Here, Pocket_Qcov represents the fraction of query pocket residues that are aligned to a pocket residue in the target, as described in [15]. Then, for targets retained in the training partition, each active compound is further labeled as train or test based on its minimum ECFP4-Tanimoto distance to any co-crystallized ligand of that target. Notably, training difficulty increases when data are lumped into increasingly dissimilar partitions, which the user can choose from, though a recommended difficulty level roughly balances the proportion of data points in train versus test splits (126 905 versus 153 080).

Virtual screening validation

A central design goal of PLATE-VS is to provide splits that reduce or eliminate, rather than mask, the protein- and ligand-side leakage that often inflates virtual-screening (VS) benchmarks. To quantify the operational consequences, we evaluated representative ligand-based machine learning (ML) models and structure-based docking, using the PLATE-VS test sets under two split regimes.

Benchmarking setup

We compare three method families: (i) classical ligand-based ML using ECFP4 fingerprints (RF, GBM, SVM) [21], and the same models augmented with frozen ESM2 protein embeddings (RF+ESM2, GBM+ESM2) [22], (ii) graph neural networks [23] with cross-attention [24] designs with ESM2 embeddings, and (iii) structure-based docking (GNINA [25] with minAff and CNN_VS scoring, UniDock2 [26]). All methods are evaluated against PLATE-VS property-matched decoys at a fixed actives:decoys ratio. Machine learning models are validated additionally with an established benchmark set PDBbind CleanSplit [27]. The hard split assigns protein clusters (70% Pocket_Qcov) atomically to train/val/test and removes test ligands with ECFP4-Tanimoto to any training co-crystal ligand, so test proteins come from unseen clusters paired with novel chemistry. The soft split retains the ligand filter but samples proteins within each cluster, allowing test proteins to share clusters with training proteins.

Hard-versus-soft split: enrichment results

We report per-target Bayes enrichment factors [28] EFB@1% and EFB@5% as the primary metric (Fig. 4). The EFB definition, pooled ROC-AUC, and external PDBbind CleanSplit [27] comparison are provided in the Supplementary Data (Supplementary Figs S8 and S9).

(A) Hard split: structure-based GNINA and UniDock2 dominate; classical ligand-only ML cluster near the random baseline. (B) Soft split: ligand-only models recover substantially. On the hard split (Fig. 4A), structure-based docking is the strongest method class, with GNINA minAff reaching per-target mean EFB@1% , while classical ligand-only ML cluster near or below the random baseline. Adding frozen ESM2 embeddings recovers a measurable but partial fraction of the lost signal (RF+ESM2 EFB@1% , GBM+ESM2 ). On the soft split (Fig. 4B), the same ligand-only models recover substantially (RF EFB@1% , GBM ), closing most of the soft-to-hard gap and indicating that cluster transfer is the dominant axis of difficulty. For external context, the same classical pipelines reach Pearson R –0.72 on PDBbind CleanSplit affinity regression (Supplementary Fig. S9), confirming that the PLATE-VS hard split exposes leakage-driven inflation that established benchmarks do not.

Usage example

One-stop summary of PLATE-VS data

The PLATE-VS web interface allows users to visualize and selectively download all activity records and associated property-matched decoys and all available complex structures for a specific target. In Fig. 3, we walk through the typical user experience using 5HT2A (UniProt ID: P28223), a serotonin receptor important for mediating the psychedelic response. Figure 3A displays paginated activity records for 5HT2A; selecting the check box allows inspection of the activity record, chemical structure and generated decoys. In the network view panel (Fig. 3B, https://www.drugbench.org/network-view), users can further retrieve similar chemical records, as well as the primary and secondary neighbors of specific chemical records in 5HT2A, for instance, CHEMBL253022, where the top 5 high-similarity neighbors (ECFP4-Tanimoto ) and neighboring records of these top 5 members. Figure 3C (https://www.drugbench.org/search-module) similarly shows how users can retrieve the nearest neighbors of a specific 5HT2A chemical record, ranked by ECFP4-Tanimoto similarity.

Accessing PLATE-VS data with an API client

While all PLATE-VS datasets are available on the web interface (https://www.drugbench.org/dataset-download), PLATE-VS also provides programmatic access to curated active and decoy datasets through a Python API client. The client exposes functions for target-based and ligand-based queries, and the available endpoints are summarized in Table 3. As an example, the active and decoy records associated with UniProt target 5HT2A can be retrieved with client.get_protein_ligands(”P28223”), we then docked the compounds into one of its experimental structures (e.g. PDB entry: 7WC6 packaged in CIF format), enabling users to benchmark their own docking pipelines and downstream analysis workflows. Implementation and availability

PLATE-VS is publicly available at https://www.drugbench.org/. The website is free and open to all users with no login requirement. The server has been tested on Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge across Linux, macOS, and Windows. We commit to maintaining PLATE-VS for at least five years. This submission is not a resubmission or update of a previous publication.

Conclusion

By integrating metadata-aware assay curation with coordinated protein-pocket and ligand-space clustering, PLATE-VS provides a practical, reproducible foundation for virtual screening and machine-learning benchmarking. Beyond serving as a benchmark resource, PLATE-VS is intended as enabling infrastructure for the open accumulation, standardization, and reuse of protein–ligand affinity data. In this sense, it complements the goals of Target 2035 [29], which call for large, openly shared protein–ligand datasets to accelerate computational hit finding and probe discovery across the proteome. Future releases will focus on expanded decoy coverage, richer programmatic access, and versioned benchmark snapshots that support reproducible method development and evaluation.
