‘Don’t tell the coach’: contact breast injury and breast pain in under-18 international female rugby players
Authors: Eloise Elizabeth Kirby, Melissa Elizabeth Annie Jones, Seren Lois Evans, Vicky M Gottwald, Julian Andrew Owen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesContact breast injury (CBI) and exercise-induced breast pain (EIBP) are common in adult female contact sports; however, incidence in adolescent players is unknown. The present study investigated the occurrence, mechanism and reporting behaviours of CBI and EIBP in under-18 international female rugby union.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was distributed to players (n=110, 17.0±0.7 years) participating in the Women’s U18 Six Nations.ResultsIncidence of CBI and EIBP was 39% and 43%, respectively. Seventy-seven percent of players affected by CBI had experienced multiple incidents in the previous year. Activities associated with the highest severity of EIBP were running, jumping and tackling, with 27% reporting that breast pain inhibited their performance. Concerningly, 64% of players did not report CBI, and no injuries were reported to coaching/medical personnel. Notably, over half of players did not consciously adopt strategies to prevent CBI, which may be attributed to various bio-psycho-social mechanisms.ConclusionWhile there is a worryingly high incidence of CBI and EIBP in adolescent female international rugby players, reporting remains low, and there is a lack of uptake of support/protective strategies. Further research is warranted to investigate the effectiveness of female-specific protective equipment and educational programmes, aiming to improve breast health.

Introduction

Participation in women’s rugby union is growing rapidly, with ~2.7 million players worldwide, accounting for a quarter of the playing population.1 Increased engagement among adolescents is a significant driver of this growth, with pre-teen players now representing 57% of all players, and 24% of these are girls.2 Despite this, there is a paucity of research on female-specific issues, such as those related to breast health.3 The location and lack of substantial support for the female breasts render them vulnerable to injury during exercise, particularly in contact sports like rugby union.4 5 This vulnerability is exacerbated by the presence of extensive superficial capillary networks, which increases the risk of contusions and haematomas.6 Contact breast injury (CBI) often occurs due to blunt force to the breast and has been reported in up to 62% of female adult team sport athletes.58 Female rugby players are also at risk of experiencing exercise-induced breast pain (EIBP), which is associated with excessive breast movement or friction during sport and is reportedly experienced by 44% of elite athletes.9 However, these issues are likely underestimated due to underreporting stemming from factors such as historical exclusion of breast health from the Sport Medicine Diagnostic Coding System3 and reluctance to report these injuries to male staff.5 6 To date, CBI and EIBP research has focused on adult athletes, with no research specifically on adolescent female athletes. CBI and EIBP have previously been linked to potential health complications, such as scar tissue and fat necrosis.10 Previous case studies also suggest that trauma to the developing breast may result in long-term asymmetries and potential associated psychological consequences.11 With increasing participation in women’s rugby, especially among adolescents undergoing breast development, addressing this issue is crucial, as recently highlighted by Dang and colleagues.12 Given that general injury risk increases with the standard of play,13 breast injury and pain within high-level adolescents are of particular interest.

A more comprehensive understanding of the epidemiology of CBI and EIBP in adolescent female athletes is essential for developing effective prevention and management strategies. Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to examine the prevalence, mechanism and reporting behaviours of CBI and EIBP in adolescent international female rugby players. A secondary aim was to examine the use of supportive and protective equipment to potentially mitigate these issues.

Methods

Participants

One hundred and ten players (age 17.0±0.7 years, body mass 74.5 kg±14.1 kg and 7.6±3.6 years of playing experience) were recruited from participating National team squads at the Under-18 Women’s Six Nations in 2024–2025. All participants provided informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained in accordance with the institutional Research Ethics Committee’s criteria (Ethics number 0282).

Data Collection

Participants received a breast health workshop from the researchers before completing a retrospective self-report Breast Health Questionnaire (BHQ). The workshop consisted of a short 15-min presentation that provided an overview of CBI and EIBP. This was delivered immediately before the distribution of the BHQ to aid the athlete’s understanding of breast health.14 The BHQ combined questions from previously published surveys on breast health,5 7 9 14 and questions on breast support and protection. The questionnaire consisted of four sections: (1) about you; (2) breast pain; (3) sports bras; and (4) breast injuries. All players completed sections 1 and 3. Sections 2 and 4 asked players if they had ever experienced breast pain or breast injury, respectively, with those who answered ‘yes’ being prompted to answer additional questions on mechanism, frequency, effect on performance and prevention strategies. The researchers were present throughout.

Data analysis

Survey responses were collated using a spreadsheet (MS Excel, 2021), and statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (R Core Development, 2021). Quantitative variables were summarised using the mean and SD for normally distributed data and the median for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. Self-reported bra sizes were converted into their respective cup volume in cubic centimetres.15 Associations between experiencing CBI or EIBP and potential predictors were analysed using χ² for categorical data or ordinal logistic regression for continuous data. A linear mixed model was applied to assess whether activity type had a significant effect (p<0.05) on EIBP severity. A Bonferroni post hoc produced pairwise comparisons between activities.

Results

Player demographics and prevalence of CBI and EIBP are reported in table 1. Thirty-nine per cent of players had previously sustained a CBI during rugby, with 77% experiencing more than one in the past year. Contact with another athlete was the most reported contributing factor (45%), followed by ground impact (26%). For the most severe injury among players, the most common classification was ‘bruise’ (59%), followed by ‘cut’ (23%) or ‘skin friction’ (11%). Ninety-five per cent reported their most severe injury healed within 2 weeks (‘less than 1 week’ and ‘1–2 weeks’ were reported by 42% and 53% of players, respectively).

Forty-three per cent (n=46) of players reported having previously experienced EIBP. A statistically significant association was found between cup volume and EIBP severity (p=0.049). The OR (OR=1.0021) suggests that each 1 cm3 increase in cup volume is associated with a 0.21% increase in the odds of reporting more severe EIBP. An increase of over 100 cm³ corresponds to a 23.5% increase in the odds of more severe EIBP. Activity type had a significant effect on reported severity, with tackling, jumping and running being associated with the most severe EIBP (p<0.001). Catching and jogging were also found to be associated with increased pain in comparison to walking. Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons revealed that tackling had significantly higher pain severity (0.49, p=0.016) compared with running, but there was no significant difference between tackling and jumping (0.22, p=1.000) or jumping and running (0.27, p=1.000).

Sixty-four per cent (n=28) of players did not report their most severe CBI. Of those who did report, 11 informed a parent and three informed a teammate, with no injuries reported to coaching or medical personnel. Whereas most players (95%) reported that CBI did not affect their training or competition performance, a quarter of players (27%) reported that EIBP did inhibit their performance. Discussion

This is the first study to investigate the prevalence, mechanism and reporting behaviours of CBI and EIBP in adolescent international female rugby players, including their use of supportive and protective clothing to help mitigate these issues. The main findings reveal a high prevalence of CBI, with 39% of players having sustained an injury during rugby training or games and 43% experiencing EIBP. Despite this prevalence, preventative strategies and reporting of injuries are lacking in this population. This highlights the urgent need for increased awareness and education, which is critical for the well-being and continued participation of these athletes.

Prevalence of CBI was similar to that observed across female adult team sports (36%–48%),5 6 but lower than reported in adult female rugby (62% in rugby league, rugby union and rugby sevens).7 8 As this study focused on Under-18 players, their limited experience and age at the onset of breast development may have resulted in these lower levels. Understanding the causes and mechanisms of CBI is essential for developing effective prevention strategies.16 17 Contact with another player accounted for around half of injuries, which is consistent with Brisbine and colleagues’ findings in adult players.7 These injuries are expected to result from tackle events, as highlighted by Bibby et al,4 who found the tackle accounted for 37.3% of CBI.4 7 Tackling is a key element of rugby and cannot be avoided;4 18 rather, preventative strategies should focus on reducing CBI risk, such as using breast protective equipment. World Rugby permits the use of ‘body padding’ which may be contained within a tight-fitting undergarment or inserted into compartments in the undergarment or jersey, provided they are not made of rigid material which could harm the player or an opponent.19 Whereas Brisbine et al7 reported contact with sport equipment accounted for a higher proportion of injuries than contact with the ground (29% and 16%, respectively), this pattern was reversed among adolescents, accounting for 16% and 26%, respectively. These causes were also identified by Bibby et al,4 although their inclusion of more specific categories (e.g., “rucks” and “mauls”) may have resulted in slightly lower reported rates compared with ours.

Nearly half of players had experienced EIBP, consistent with findings in elite (44%) and general (52%) populations.9 20 Around a third of players reported negative effects on performance due to EIBP, a finding similar to those in elite athletes (33%).9 In line with similar studies, jumping and running were associated with the most severe EIBP,4 9 likely due to increased overall breast movement.21 22 Tackling was also identified as being associated with higher levels of EIBP. Given that EIBP is typically attributed to increased breast motion or friction and CBI with direct contact, the reported pain may reflect CBI rather than EIBP. Consistent with research in adult female populations,23 breast size was significantly associated with the severity of EIBP, with increased cup volume increasing the likelihood of higher pain severity. ‘Bra removal’ and ‘holding the breast’ were the most common pain relief methods, reported by around half of players, yet neither is practical during sport. ‘Bra removal’ may also be indicative of bra pain rather than EIBP. Although a well-fitting sports bra is effective in preventing EIBP in 87% of female athletes,24 only a third specifically considered using their bra for prevention. Although the study did not assess bra fit, this low proportion highlights a potential gap in education and awareness. Given that breast bounce is the most prevalent breast concern in UK school girls,25 and poor bra fit knowledge is common among female athletes,14 26 targeted education in this population is particularly important.

Few players disclosed previous CBI, and none reported their most severe injury to medical or coaching personnel, aligning with trends in senior players.3 6 26 This is concerning as it prevents players from accessing treatment and may contribute to the misconception that breast injuries are not prevalent, as highlighted by Brisbine and colleagues,7 where most staff predicted that CBI affected less than 5% of players. Similarly, most did not adopt protective strategies beyond standard breast support.26 If worn, players opted for unisex or male-fit protective garments without breast plates, potentially due to limited awareness of female-specific products.27 This reinforces the need for education on breast health for both players and their support staff3 alongside further research on the effectiveness of female-specific protective equipment.28

Limitations

As this study relied on self-reported survey data, accuracy may be affected by subjective recall and under- or over-reporting. While prospective injury surveillance is typically recommended to mitigate these inaccuracies,29 30 reluctance to report may limit its effectiveness. Nevertheless, researchers were present during questionnaire administration to clarify questions and improve response accuracy, a process facilitated by a pre-survey workshop designed to improve participant understanding.14 A subsequent limitation is the inability to calculate the severity of CBI. While the BHQ asked players about the time taken to heal, it did not accurately collect time loss. Future studies should use prospective and retrospective accounts to corroborate injury rates and accurately calculate severity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CBI and EIBP are common in Under-18 international female rugby players, yet preventative strategies are underutilised. A concerning finding is that most players did not report their most severe injuries. Therefore, it is crucial to normalise conversations around breast health and increase awareness of CBI in rugby to encourage reporting. Additionally, education on the benefits of well-fitted sports bras is needed to prevent EIBP. Addressing these issues will contribute to the safety and well-being of female rugby players, supporting their continued participation in the sport.
