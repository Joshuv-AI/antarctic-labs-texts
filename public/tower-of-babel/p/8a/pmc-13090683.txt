Chronic inflammation is associated with increased anxiety in the Cancer Disparities Research Network
Authors: Holli Loomans-Kropp, Priya Das, Aaliyah Beneby, Yevgeniya Gokun, Laura Yonghua Bao, Wancai Yang, Ting-Yuan David Cheng, Jill Oliveri, Mohamed Elsaid, Roberto Benzo, Electra Paskett, for the Cancer Disparities Research Network Working Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSystemic chronic inflammation is associated with the development of diseases, such as diabetes and cancer. Recent research has shown that psychological state can influence inflammation, demonstrating the mind-body connection; however, there is limited knowledge on whether self-perception of quality of life has physiological effects on systemic chronic inflammatory biomarkers among healthy individuals. Furthermore, there is a gap in the literature on this connection among minoritized and underserved populations, despite known increased chronic stress. The objective of the current study is to evaluate the association between quality-of-life measures, such as anxiety, and blood-based inflammation markers in the Cancer Disparities Research Network (CDRN), which recruited a study population of individuals who were minoritized or medically underserved. CDRN participants were recruited from four institutions between January 2015 and April 2017 and asked to complete a baseline survey and provide a biospecimen. Biomarkers were measured using customized human analyte assay kits. Of the measured markers, six were significantly correlated with overall anxiety and not significantly correlated with other measured markers. Using multivariate linear regressions, we found that mild and moderate/severe anxiety were significantly associated with gp130 and MMP-1 concentration, and moderate/severe anxiety was associated with APRIL concentration. These findings emphasize the importance of psychological well-being and its potential long-term influence on physical health and disease progression.Keywords: Chronic inflammation, Molecular biomarkers, Quality of life, Health disparities, Anxiety

Introduction

In the United States, approximately 35% of the population has systemic chronic inflammation (Mainous et al., 2024). Chronic inflammation is linked to the development of a significant number of diseases, including cancer, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and neurodegenerative diseases, among other conditions (Furman et al., 2019). Molecularly, chronic systemic inflammation can lead to the dysregulation of intercellular pathways, DNA damage, and the production of reactive oxygen species, causing cellular damage, which can lead to disease development and negative health outcomes (Wu et al., 2014). In recent years, long-term inflammation has been linked to reduced quality-of-life measures, including anxiety and depression. Conversely, psychological disorders can lead to chronic stress and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis disruption, inducing systemic inflammation (Won and Kim, 2020). For example, emotional stressors, such as discrimination and financial, social, and career stress, which are linked to increased anxiety and other disorders, have been shown to trigger the release of cytokines, such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin (IL)-1β, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNFα), and IL-6, in response to these challenges (Johnson et al., 2019; Felger and Lotrich, 2013).

Further establishing this mind body connection, studies have shown that experiencing unfavorable social determinants of health, defined as the conditions where people are born, live, work, play, and age, such as those experienced by minoritized and underserved populations, can negatively impact one's biology, including dysregulated homeostasis with downstream consequences of increased risk of disease (Braveman and Gottlieb, 2014; Emeny et al., 2021; Bundy et al., 2023). For instance, high levels of chronic inflammation-associated biomarker matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-1 have been associated with increased incidence of obesity and cardiovascular disease and a positive correlation with psychological stress (Koh and Jaoude, 2016; Mahendra et al., 2012). IL-6 has long been implicated in the progression of chronic diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cancer, while demonstrating positive associations with clinical outcomes of depression (Grebenciucova and VanHaerents, 2023; Foley et al., 2024).

Despite a growing body of literature supporting the connection between psychological disorders and chronic inflammation, there remains a lack of knowledge on whether self-perception of quality of life has physiological effects on systemic chronic inflammatory biomarkers among healthy individuals. Furthermore, there is a gap in the literature on this mind-body inflammation connection as it relates to minoritized and underserved populations, despite known increased chronic stress. In the current study, we leveraged biospecimens and data collected as part of the Cancer Disparities Research Network (CDRN) to investigate the relationship between self-reported quality of life measures and a large panel of systemic chronic inflammatory biomarkers in a cohort of minoritized and medically underserved participants.

Materials and methods

Study design and participant population

This study was completed using data and biospecimens collected as part of the CDRN. Briefly, the CDRN was a regional unit of the National Cancer Institute's Geographic and Biospecimen Management of Cancer Health Disparities and focused on building support and management of cancer health disparities research, training, and infrastructure. The overall goal of the CDRN was to improve participation of minoritized and underserved individuals in biobanking research (Simon et al., 2014). CDRN participants were recruited from four institutions (University of Illinois Chicago, Tufts University, The Ohio State University Comprehensive Cancer Center (OSUCCC), and Fox Chase Cancer Center) between January 2015 and April 2017 and asked to complete a baseline survey (in-person, via telephone, or online), as well as have physical measurements taken and biospecimen (blood or saliva) collected. To be eligible for participation in the CDRN, participants had to meet the following eligibility criteria: 40-74 years of age; able to give informed consent; live in an Appalachian county or self-identify as White, African American, Hispanic, or Asian and (Mainous et al., 2024) live in a medically underserved area, as defined by the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) (Furman et al., 2019), have low literacy, defined as low proficiency in English or a score of 6 or less in the Rapid Estimate of Adult Literacy in Medicine Short Form (REALM-SF) or 3 or more on the Single Item Literacy Screener (Wu et al., 2014), have low income, defined as an annual income of $11,670 for a single individual and an annual income of $23,850 for a family of four, as defined by state, or (Won and Kim, 2020) be uninsured or receive subsidized health insurance coverage (i.e., Medicaid or state subsidized plans for low income adults). Exclusion criteria included individuals living in a nursing home, those who were pregnant, or those with a prior diagnosis of invasive cancer.

Compensation was provided for completing the baseline survey, which included collection of demographic factors, medical history, and lifestyle factors, among other variables, and giving a biospecimen. Institutional review board (IRB) approval from each participating institution was obtained for the original study, and local IRB approval was obtained for the current study. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Inflammatory biomarker measures

Biospecimen collection was completed at each individual recruitment site. For those who consented to provide blood, approximately 20 ml of blood was collected. All samples were then shipped to OSUCCC for processing (if not completed locally) and long-term storage at −80 °C. Plasma-based inflammatory biomarkers were measured using customized human analyte assay kits (R&D Systems Human Luminex Discovery Assay; Bio-techne, Minneapolis, MN, USA), including the following markers: aggrecan, APRIL, BAFF, CD163, chitinase 3-like 1, gp130, IFNα, IFNγ, IL-8, IL-10, IL-11, IL-12/IL-23p40, IL-19, IL-27, IL-28A, IL-34, IL-6Rα, MMP-1, MMP-3, Pentraxin3, TNFα, CD30, IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-12p70, IL-15, osteopontin, TNFRI, TNFRII, adiponectin, CCL5/RANTES, and MMP-2. Assays were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol, and all samples were run in duplicate. In brief, the assays were conducted in 96-well plates. Standards were reconstituted and serially diluted to generate a seven-point standard curve along with a blank using the provided diluent. Fifty microliters (50 μL) of standards, blank diluent, or plasma samples were added in duplicate to each well, followed by 50 μL of a diluted microparticle cocktail containing magnetic beads coated with the respective capture antibodies. Plates were incubated for 2 h at room temperature on an orbital shaker at 800 rpm, protected from light. After incubation, plates were placed on a magnetic plate and allowed to settle for 2 min. Wells were washed three times with 100 μL of wash buffer. Next, 50 μL of diluted Biotin-Antibody Cocktail was added to each well, and plates were covered and incubated for 1 h at room temperature on the shaker at 800 rpm. Following another three washes, 50 μL of diluted Streptavidin-PE was added to each well and incubated for 30 min at room temperature with shaking. After the final three washes, 100 μL of wash buffer was added to each well and incubated for 3 min at room temperature on the shaker. Plates were then read on the Luminex MAGPIX system Austin, TX, USA) using XPONENT software. Raw data were collected as mean fluorescence intensity, and analyte concentrations (pg/mL) were calculated using the standard curves generated from the seven-point standards and blank. Final data were exported as Excel files for statistical analysis.

Baseline questionnaire

Self-reported quality-of-life was measured using responses from the baseline survey. Participants provided responses regarding their overall quality of life and overall health, as well as level of anxiety, fatigue, and distress, in the last week. Responses were provided on a Likert scale (0-10), ranging from ‘as bad as it can be’ to ‘as good as it can be’. Participant responses for overall quality of life and overall health were also categorized as poor, fair, good, very good, and excellent, and responses for anxiety, fatigue, and distress were also categorized into none, mild, moderate, and severe based on their distributions. In our final analyses of anxiety, we collapsed moderate and severe anxiety into a single category to increase statistical power. To complete our analysis, we also extracted responses for age (continuous), sex, self-reported race (White, African-American/Black, Asian, Other/Multiple races), history of diabetes (yes vs no), high cholesterol (yes vs no), asthma (yes vs no), or arthritis (yes vs no), and aspirin (current or former vs no users) or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) (current or former vs no users). Socioeconomic status was calculated based on the modified Hollingshead scale (Hollingshead, 1975) by combining information on employment status, education, and income. The score ranged from 0 to 6, with a higher score indicating better SES. All three variables were categorized as the following: employment status (currently working, retired, other); education (more than high school, high school or GED, less than high school) and income (over $50K, $25K-$50K, under $25K). For the analysis, SES was categorized into three groups: SES scores of 0-1, 2-3 and 4-6 representing low-, middle- and high-level SES status, respectively. Physical activity was defined as number of days per week with exercise or physical activity of at least moderate intensity and categorized as 0 days per week, 1-4 days per week, 5-6 days per week or 7 days per week. Waist-to-hip ratio (WHR) was defined as ratio between waist and hip circumferences and was categorized as 0.95 or below (for males) or 0.8 or below (for females) as “Low Risk”, 0.96 to 1.0 (for males) or 0.81 to 0.85 (for females) as “Moderate Risk” and 1.0 or higher (for males) or 0.85 or higher (for females) as “High Risk” per World Health Organization guidelines (WHOE Consultation, 2008).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses, including medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), were reported for continuous variables, while frequency counts and percents were provided for categorical variables. Numeric Likert responses for anxiety, fatigue and distress were reversed so the higher scores (0 = as good as it can be and 10 = as bad as it can be) indicated worse conditions while numeric Likert responses for quality of life and overall health were left as written (0 = as bad as it can be and 10 = as good as it can be), with indication that higher scores meant better conditions. Spearman correlation coefficients were used to review correlations between various biomarkers as well as anxiety, fatigue, distress, quality of life, and overall health (all these measurements were on Likert scale). Due to the significant correlations observed with anxiety, we chose to focus our analyses on the associations with anxiety. Primary outcome measures were six biomarkers (gp130, MMP-1, Pentraxin3, APRIL, TNFRII, and adiponectin) that were significantly associated with anxiety and were not correlated with the other measured biomarkers so as to not evaluate those within the same or dependent, adjacent pathways. All these biomarkers were right-skewed and were natural-log transformed to better approximate the normality of residuals. Linear models were used to model each biomarker as outcome and anxiety (categorized as none, mild or moderate/severe) as our main predictor. Crude, as well as multivariate, models were ran adjusting for age, sex, race, SES status, arthritis, asthma, depression, diabetes, high cholesterol, aspirin use, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory (NSAID) use, physical activity, and waist-to-hip ratio. Additional adjusted models were run without including race as one of the covariates. Multicollinearity was reviewed for all the models via variance inflation factors. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Of the 333 participants enrolled into the CDRN, 210 provided blood samples. We utilized 190 of these blood samples in our inflammation arrays (Fig. 1). The blood samples came from three study sites: 69 (36.3%) were recruited from OSUCCC, 63 (33.2%) were recruited from Fox Chase Cancer Center, and 58 (30.5%) were recruited from Tufts University. The median age of the participants was 61.5 years (interquartile range, 56-66). A majority of the participants were female (63.7%), graduated high school or had a GED (38.2%) or at least some college (40.3%), had income below $25,000 annually (60.7%), and worked full- or part-time (45.4%) (Table 1). Most participants were current or former users of aspirin (63.4%) or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (53.0%). A minority of participants had a history of high cholesterol (38.4%), diabetes (23.%), asthma (15.3%), or arthritis (43.2%). Moderate or severe anxiety, fatigue, and distress were reported by 32.4%, 48.9%, and 38.9% of participants, respectively, and very good or excellent health and quality of life by 35.6% and 39.9%, respectively.

Discussion

In the current study, we showed that mild and moderate or severe anxiety were associated with increases in the inflammatory biomarkers gp130 and MMP-1, while only moderate and severe anxiety were associated with increased concentration of APRIL, suggesting a novel relationship between these markers and self-reported anxiety. These results lend support to the growing body of research demonstrating the importance of the mind-body connection in health and disease, and how perceived stress can influence physiological processes. This phenomenon is particularly apparent in the impact of quality-of-life measures on inflammation and inflammatory processes. Though investigations into anxiety and inflammation are limited, research into other mental health conditions and quality-of-life have shown similar associations. For example, a study of individuals with schizophrenia showed that high CRP was associated with reduced quality of life scores, including reduced psychological well-being (F et al., 2015). An additional study of individuals with metabolic syndrome demonstrated higher reported anxiety with higher CRP levels (Kim et al., 2018). Similar results were shown in a study of chronic pancreatitis, where high levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-7, IL-16, IL-8, and macrophage inflammatory protein-1α (MIP-1a) were associated with lower overall quality-of-life. Interestingly, IL-7, IL-16, and MIP-1α, as well as macrophage-derived chemokine (MDC). were also associated with fatigue, role functioning, and insomnia (Robinson et al., 2019).

Consistent with earlier studies, our research highlights a notable connection between systemic inflammation and measures of quality of life. However, we uncovered previously unreported relationships between the inflammation-associated biomarkers gp130, MMP-1, and APRIL. gp130 is an IL-6 family receptor and plays a crucial role in the development of the cardiovascular and nervous systems (Silver and Hunter, 2010). Soluble gp130 (sgp130) has been shown to regulate IL-6 signaling by selectively binding to the IL-6/soluble IL-6R (sIL-6R) complex, antagonizing its pro-inflammatory function (Lamertz et al., 2018). Though this may seem counterintuitive to the noted association with inflammation, sgp130 helps to balance trans, but not intracellular, IL-6 signaling, and may allow for simultaneous classic and trans-signaling (Baran et al., 2018). Studies in the inflammatory conditions SARS-CoV-2, rheumatoid arthritis, and HIV found no correlation between serum levels of IL-6, sIL-6R, and sgp130 (Ziegler et al., 2022; Robak et al., 1998; Aziz et al., 2023), suggesting free systemic IL-6 even in the presence of sgp130. Intriguingly, in our study, IL-6 concentration was not significantly correlated with any of the noted quality of life measures (anxiety, distress, fatigue) or gp130 concentration (Appendix A).

We also observed significant associations with anxiety and MMP-1 concentration. MMP-1 is a member of a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases, which proteolytically process the extracellular matrix (ECM), and are thought to act primarily by activating protease activated receptor 1 (PAR1) (Jabłońska-Trypuć et al., 2016; Beroun et al., 2019). In the brain, MMP-1 is associated with neuronal plasticity, and astrocyte-derived MMP-1 was found to decrease anxiety and social and memory deficits (Allen et al., 2016). Though little is known about the role of MMP-1 in psychological disorders, such as anxiety, there is compelling evidence suggesting a role for other MMP family members. Individuals with schizophrenia and major depressive disorder had significantly higher expression of the collagenase MMP-9, compared to non-affected controls (Domenici et al., 2010). Similarly, MMP-9 was significantly inversely associated with quality of life and positively associated with depression scoring (Yoshida et al., 2012). In a separate study, significantly higher MMP-2, MMP-7, and MMP-9 concentrations were observed among individuals with depression, compared to control; in line with these findings, tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase-2 (TIMP-2) was significantly reduced (Bobińska et al., 2016). APRIL has not been evaluated in the context of anxiety or other psychological disorders. APRIL has a well-established role in inflammation and immunity, such as through enhancing B-cell and plasma cell proliferation and survival, as well as class switching and recombination, and, as previously stated, anxiety and inflammation are linked (Mathur et al., 2023). With limited knowledge regarding the association between anxiety and inflammatory biomarkers—specifically gp130, MMP-1, and APRIL—our study identifies a novel role for these molecules in this context.

A primary strength of this study is the population, as the CDRN specifically enrolled individuals who were minoritized and underserved. These individuals are traditionally underrepresented in research, despite being disproportionately affected by disease and, relevant to the current study, report higher self-reported symptoms of anxiety (Kavelaars et al., 2023). Despite our innovative findings, this study has several limitations to note. First, quality of life measures were captured through single-item Likert scales, rather than as a composite score, such as the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 or Patient Health Questionnaire-9, which would allow for the more accurate capture of the extent of the reported disorders. Furthermore, there are many lifestyle and behavioral factors that may influence quality-of-life, such as diet quality, personal history, and social support, which we were unable to capture in the current study and account for in our analyses. However, despite not having these data, we were still able to identify significant associations between the measured biomarkers and self-reported quality-of-life; collecting these data in follow-up studies would allow for a more comprehensive analysis. Lastly, we evaluated the associations between these quality-of-life measures and inflammatory markers cross-sectionally; we are unable to infer the impact of heightened anxiety on inflammatory biomarker levels long-term or in response to acute stressors. With the novel relationships established in this study, follow-up studies should evaluate the longitudinal impact of anxiety on gp130, MMP-1, and APRIL concentrations; these studies could lead to the identification of other previously undiscovered biomarkers associated with psychological disorders.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we showed that self-reported anxiety is associated with increased systemic inflammatory biomarkers, specifically gp130, MMP-1, and APRIL, demonstrating the impact of perceived stressors on physiology. A growing body of research has indicated that cognitive and emotional states can modulate inflammatory processes, underscoring the significance of mental health in modulating disease incidence and pathology, particularly in minoritized and underserved populations. These findings highlight the significance of self-care practices and their potential to exert lasting effects on health and disease outcomes.
