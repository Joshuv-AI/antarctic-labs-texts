Role of root canal sealers and dentin surface pretreatment on pull-out strength of customized resilient ceramic posts-and-cores
Authors: Ahmed E Farghal, Mohammed Ahmed Alghauli, Mohamed Ellayeh, Ahmed Yaseen Alqutaibi, Marwa Alzemei, Samah Saker
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Endodontic treatment aims to retain human teeth and keep them functioning for a long clinical term [1]. Maintaining periodontal, endodontic, restorative, or prosthetic aspects is fundamental to fulfilling this goal. Severely destructed endodontically treated teeth, particularly those with less than 2 axial walls, are indicated for post-and-core crown restorations [2–5]. Preferably, ready-made intracanal posts have been applied regarding their simple applications and availability [3–5]. Moreover, they possess a close modulus of elasticity to the tooth structure and are assumed to have better clinical function with clinically acceptable fracture resistance [4, 6]. Although some evidence found no difference in clinical outcome between these resilient posts and metal posts, and the choice relied mainly upon the practitioner’s preference [7, 8], other evidence concluded a higher clinical survival rate of resilient glass fiber posts than metal ones [3].

Some clinical cases might require custom-made post-and-core crowns, such as in the case of severely destructed teeth and wide and flared pulp space, necessitating a better fit between restorative and underlying structures or practitioners’ preference for quick and straightforward clinical settings [9]. Some of the customized casted metal post-and-core crowns are known for their reduced esthetic appeal due to gingival and restorative metal shadow [7]. Clinical practice shifted increasingly towards customized ceramic post-and-core crowns. This esthetic era is characterized by applying high-strength ceramics such as zirconia oxide ceramics in the mid-2000s and transitioning to more resilient materials in the last decade [9]. Resin matrix ceramics (RMC) are highly resilient ceramic materials with close elastic modulus to the tooth structure and higher mechanical properties than composite resins, rendering them a good choice for customized post-and-core. Polymer infiltrated ceramic network (PICN) from the RMC family is considered a good material choice, owing to its stress-absorbing phenomenon compared with more rigid esthetic machinable ceramic materials [10, 11]. PICN post-and-core was reported to exert less apical stress concentration and reduce crack initiation and propagation potential than more rigid materials [12].

Considering the fulfillment of basic requirements for the long-term survival of endodontically treated teeth, complete radicular and coronal hermetic seals are crucial. Adhesive bonding is vital in ensuring both radicular and coronal seals in endodontically treated teeth. These hermetic seals are key preventive measures against microleakage [13, 14]. Adhesive dental restorations, particularly root canal posts, can provide good sealing and retaining ability; however, the long-term durability of the bond might be compromised due to endodontic failures or adhesive protocols and application professionalism [15]. For long and durable adhesion of root canal posts, various factors must be considered, including the type of sealer material [16], irrigant [17], smear layer thickness [18], and the configuration factor (C-factor), which is the ratio of bonded to unbonded surfaces in post preparations [19].

Epoxy resin-based endodontic sealers, considered a gold standard, possess excellent sealing properties and do not negatively impact the bond strength of resin-luted glass fiber posts; however, they lack bioactive properties [20–24]. Calcium silicate (bioceramic) sealers are increasingly popular as they offer biocompatibility, radiopacity, bioactivity, high pH, and calcium ion release.[25, 26].

Adhesion to enamel is more reliable and durable than adhesion to dentin [27], and adhesion to superficial dentin is significantly stronger [28], more stable, and durable [29] than deeper dentin. Moreover, coronal dentin adhesion is more reliable than adhesion to pulpal dentin [30]. These sequels underscore that adhesion to root canal dentin is the most complex procedure in adhesive dentistry. Needing special attention to several factors such as humidity control [29], smear layer and surface conditioning [31–33], root canal sealer and waiting time before luting [32], luting cement film thickness [33], post material, length, and diameter [33], surface pretreatment of metal and ceramic posts.[34, 35] However, other factors, such as luting resin type, might have little impact on pull-out strength [36].

To improve adhesion to radicular dentin, dentin conditioning is recommended [6, 37]. The process is known by applying a chemical agent, commonly a mild acid, to the dentin surface to remove the smear layer and induce partial demineralization of the superficial dentin, thereby exposing the collagen fibril network. The resultant modification of the dentin substrate increases its surface energy and permeability, facilitating the infiltration of adhesive monomers and enhancing the micromechanical interlocking and chemical bonding between the resin material and the dentin [38].

Considering dentin surface conditioning and sealer types, numerous laboratory studies were conducted. However, limited data are available on the impact of these factors on the retention of milled PICN post-and-core. To the best of our knowledge, only one study in the literature focused on the fracture resistance and pull-out strength of PICN posts [39]. The study presented the data in MPa, while the pull-out strength is not practical to calculate in MPa, as is the case with bond or push-out strength. However, given the impossibility of calculating the radicular and coronal surface area to which the post and core are bonded, it is considered a force to debond. Moreover, to better imitate the clinical situation, measuring the resistance to debonding of the entire root canal post-and-core assembly is necessary, rather than a cross-sectional piece.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the impact of two root canal sealers and three conditioning media on the pull-out strength of PICN post-and-core. The null hypotheses were: (1) Root canal sealers would not impact the pull-out strength of PICN post-and-core specimens. (2) Different dentin conditioning would not affect the pull-out strength of PICN post-and-core specimens.

Materials and methods

This laboratory was conducted at Taibah University Dental College and Hospital in Al-Madinah, Saudi Arabia, with an ethical approval number (TUCDREC/210323). Human extracted teeth were collected after extraction, subjected to cleaning, and subsequently stored in a 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C. Human teeth extracted for orthodontic purposes were collected, from which 80 mandibular premolars were selected based on being caries-free, crack-free, possessing straight roots, and featuring fully developed apices. A power analysis was conducted using SAS/STAT version 12.1 to determine the appropriate sample size necessary for achieving adequate statistical power. This calculation was based on the effect size of 2.05 derived from a previous study [40], specifically examining the difference between the two sealers. Statistical power was established at 95%, with an alpha error set at 0.05, and an allocation ratio of 1 was utilized. The required sample size for each group was initially calculated as 8; however, this figure was increased to 10 to account for potential dropout. This adjusted number was considered as the sample size for the endpoint group. Given that the study encompassed two main groups (differentiated by sealer type), each further subdivided into four subgroups (based on surface pretreatment), the final sample size amounted to 80 teeth.

Root canal cleaning and shaping with a ProTaper rotary endodontic file (ProTaper, Dentsply Sirona), applying a 5.25% sodium hypochlorite irrigation solution, followed by a distilled water rinse. Antioxidant solutions were delivered via syringe irrigation (5 ml/canal) for 30 s, rinsed, dried, and filled with #F3 master gutta-percha cone (Dentsply Sirona) and 2 endodontic sealers: AH Plus (Dentsply Detrey) for Group AH and Bio-C Sealer (Total Fill BC, FKG) for Group BC, (Table 1). After obturation, the cervical root canal openings were sealed with Cavit-G temporary restorative material (3 M ESPE). Specimens were then stored in saline solution at 37 °C for 4 weeks.

Teeth roots were vertically embedded in acrylic resin (Acrostone; Acrostone Medical Dental Supplies) with retention notches. Teeth were cut to a level of 1.5 mm above the cemento-enamel junction using a diamond disk. Post space was prepared by removing gutta-percha to a depth of 9 mm with a Pesso reamer (MANI Inc) and refined with #3 and #4 Largo drills (Dentsply). Root canal spaces were scanned with an intraoral scanner (Identica I500; Medit Corp). The scanned data were then sent to CAD software (exocad v2.2 Valletta; exocad GmbH), and the post-and-core space was designed with an 80 μm cement gap. The restorations were milled out from PICN blocks (Vita Enamic, Vita Zhanfabrik) using a 5-axis machine (ED5X; EMAR), and sprue sites were finished with a Ceragum Wheel (Bredent GmbH Co KG).

Teeth were divided into 4 subgroups (n = 10 each) based on dentin conditioning: Group N (control) received no conditioning; Group ED was pretreated with 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) for 30 s; Group T with 10% tannic acid (Fouz Chemical Company) for 30 s; and Group A with 10% ascorbic acid (Sigma Aldrich Chemie GmbH) for 30 s, with all groups rinsed and dried afterward. Each CAD-CAM post was then air-abraded for 5 s using 50-µm alumina particles at 0.25 MPa.

Post spaces were irrigated with 5 ml of 10% ascorbic acid for 30 s (extended from Albashaireh et al.‘s 15-second protocol) [37], followed by a distilled water rinse and drying. For tannic acid, a 10% concentration was applied for 30 s (exceeding Takahashi et al.‘s 1–5% protocol) [41], aiming to optimize dentin surface modification. The post spaces in each group were irrigated with 5 ml of their respective solution for 30 s, dried with paper points, then irrigated with distilled water, and dried again with paper points.

Self-adhesive resin cement was applied following the manufacturer’s instructions (Table 1). Posts were coated in resin cement on their apical two-thirds, inserted using a custom holder, and secured with a 20 N load for 10 min to ensure adhesion. Excess cement was removed, and the resin was cured for 40 s using a halogen light source at 600 mW/cm².

The pull-out force was evaluated using a universal testing machine (Lloyd Instruments Ltd) that applied a tensile load along the post’s long axis at 2 mm/min. The core portion of each post was secured in a custom device (Fig. 1). Failure modes were analyzed with an optic at 40x magnification and categorized as follows: 0: mixed failure, 1: adhesive failure between dentin and resin cement, 2: cohesive failure within the dentin, 3: cohesive failure within resin cement, 4: adhesive failure between resin and post, and 5: cohesive failure within the post material. Randomly selected specimens were further analyzed with SEM to assess bonding quality.

Schematic presentation of pull-out strength test

Data normality and homogeneity were assessed with Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene tests using SPSS v22.0 (SPSS Inc.). A two-way ANOVA tested retentive force measurements (N), and multiple comparisons were conducted with Tukey’s HSD post hoc test. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set for all analyses.

Discussion

This study evaluated the effect of different root canal sealers and dentin surface pretreatments on the pull-out strength of customized PICN post-and-core systems. Both null hypotheses—stating that neither sealer type nor dentin conditioning would affect pull-out strength—were rejected. Bioceramic sealers exhibited significantly higher pull-out strength than AH-Plus sealers, and different conditioning protocols significantly impacted retention.

The pull-out test was selected for its biomechanical relevance to occlusal stress vectors and its ability to simulate clinical conditions by evaluating the entire post-and-core assembly. It quantifies the tensile load needed to dislodge the post-cement-dentin complex, offering a comprehensive assessment of overall adhesive performance following surface pretreatments such as tannic or ascorbic acid conditioning [33, 42, 43]. In contrast, push-out testing remains valuable for analyzing shear bond strength within specific root segments (cervical, middle, apical) and for detecting localized weaknesses in resin-dentin hybridization or collagen degradation [11, 20].

Root canal preparation involves chemomechanical shaping, combining mechanical instrumentation with chemical irrigation. NaOCl is used for its antimicrobial efficacy and ability to dissolve organic tissues, while care is taken to avoid interactions with EDTA that could reduce NaOCl’s chlorine availability[44]. Sequential irrigation with NaOCl and EDTA, the gold standard, effectively removes the smear layer without compromising disinfection[45]. In this study, NaOCl was employed for cleaning and disinfection, whereas dentin conditioning used 17% EDTA alone, based on evidence that NaOCl can negatively affect the bond strength of adhesively luted posts [46, 47].

The findings of this study demonstrated that the calcium silicate-based sealer possessed significantly higher pull-out strength than the epoxy resin-based sealer for customized PICN post-and-core restorations in single-canal premolars, regardless of the dentin conditioning method. The literature reports co

nflicting results regarding these sealers [20–24, 48–59]. Most previous laboratory studies found higher push-out bond strength for epoxy resin-based sealers [21, 22, 24, 49–55], typically using irrigation protocols with 17% EDTA and 2.5–5.25% NaOCl, except one study using 15% EDTA and 4% NaOCl [55]. These discrepancies likely stem from methodological differences, as earlier studies mainly applied push-out tests to assess sealers alone [24], root canal sealers, and gutta-percha [20, 50–52, 55] or used modified models like parallel holes in root dentin [22]. One study evaluated sectioned posts cemented with Panavia F2.0 [21], differing from the full post-and-core pull-out testing conducted here.

To the best of our knowledge, only one study evaluated the pull-out strength of fiber posts cemented with RelyX Unicem, reporting superior strength for the epoxy resin-based sealer compared to the calcium silicate-based sealer [49]. This finding contradicts both the current study and another investigation using RelyX Unicem, which showed higher bond strength of fiber posts with calcium silicate-based sealers [23]. These discrepancies are expected, given differences in materials and methods: earlier studies used glass fiber posts, while the present study employed customized PICN post-and-core systems, incorporated air-particle abrasion, and assessed the pull-out strength of complete post-and-core restorations rather than the push-out strength of sealers and gutta-percha in root sections.

Conversely, some studies align with the present findings, reporting superior bond strength of calcium silicate-based sealers over epoxy resin-based sealers in push-out tests without posts [23, 24, 48, 56–59]. One study showed higher pull-out strength of fiber posts cemented with self-adhesive resin cement when combined with calcium silicate-based sealers compared to epoxy resin-based sealers [23]. Similarly, Smran et al. irrigated root canals with 17% EDTA and 5.25% NaOCl and found higher push-out bond strength for calcium silicate-based sealers before thermal aging [24]. These results were further confirmed by Khoury et al.[48], who, after preparing canals with rotary files, 5.25% NaOCl, and 17% EDTA, reported higher bond strength of glass fiber posts combined with calcium silicate-based sealers compared to those with AH Plus sealer, particularly in the apical and coronal thirds. The contradictions in the literature likely stem from differences in study design, testing methods, presence and type of posts, and irrigation protocols.

The type of irrigation solution could play a significant role in the pull-out strength of root canal posts when different sealers were utilized [59–61]. Moreover, smear layer removal is detrimental to the bond strength between calcium silicate-based sealers and dentin [62]. Furthermore, the cement used for root canal post-cementation might impact the bond strength of the utilized post, where a laboratory study found higher bond strength with calcium-based sealer when the posts were cemented with RelyX U200 and reduced bond strength with conventional resin cement (RelyX ARC) [23]. Reflecting the importance of selecting appropriate tooth surface pretreatment, type of cement, and adhesive protocol [63].

The root canal dentin conditioning before adhesion serves two purposes: promoting the integration of the smear layer into the resin-impregnated dentin matrix and enhancing the penetration of adhesive materials into dentinal tubules [64]. These effects lead to the formation of an interdiffusion zone, or hybrid layer, at the interface between the conditioned dentin and adhesive material [6]. The interpenetration of resin monomers into the partially demineralized dentin substrate is thought to improve micromechanical retention, enhance bond strength at the resin-dentin interface, and reduce microleakage, thus extending the restoration’s longevity [37, 64]. This was observed in the current study, where failure modes were primarily mixed, with the cement layer remaining attached to the root canal dentin. However, the efficacy of conditioning may vary based on factors like the type of conditioning agent, application time, and adhesive system used, suggesting the need for further research to optimize these parameters and assess their long-term clinical impact [31, 32, 37, 65].

The use of 17% EDTA is the gold standard for root dentin conditioning in posts adhesive protocols due to its ability to partially remove the smear layer while preserving the dentinal mineral phase, enhancing chemical adhesion between cement and dentin [37, 65]. Alternative agents like 10% tannic acid and 10% ascorbic acid have also been proposed. Tannic acid removes the smear layer, promotes collagen cross-linking to improve dentin elasticity and bond strength, inhibits matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) activity to reduce collagen degradation, and, when applied after sodium hypochlorite, neutralizes oxidative stress and enhances resin infiltration for durable adhesion [66–68]. Ascorbic acid reverses the oxidative effects of sodium hypochlorite, restores redox balance, improves resin polymerization and adhesive penetration, inhibits MMP activity, and mildly removes the smear layer without over-etching, leading to improved bond strength compared to EDTA-pretreated dentin [66, 69]. Albashaireh et al. [37] demonstrated that a 15-second application of 10% ascorbic acid improves the bond strength of root canal dentin to glass fiber-reinforced posts, while Takahashi et al. [41] showed that 10% tannic acid applied for 30 s effectively modifies the dentin bonding surface.

The presented study findings exhibited a highly significant enhancement in the pull-out bond strength of PICN post and core (P ≤.001) when luted to conditioned root canal dentin. The highest pull-out strength was observed for the 10% ascorbic acid conditioned group, followed by 10% tannic acid and then 17% EDTA, and all are significantly higher than the control group, irrespective of the sealer type applied. These findings aligned with a recently published study by Albashaireh et al. [37], which reported that irrigation with ascorbic acid before glass fiber-post adhesion improved pull-out strength compared with EDTA dentin surface pretreatment. Moreover, another study[70] found that EDTA removes the smear layer and results in better bond strength of glass fiber posts than NaOCl irrigation, which aligns with other laboratory studies [11, 42].

Ascorbic acid is beneficial in dental adhesive procedures, particularly for post-endodontic restorations. Its capacity to neutralize oxygen molecules explains its superior pull-out strength in the presented study [60]. Working on the smear layer enhances the polymerization process and mitigates the adverse effects of antioxidants [60]. Additionally, it can reverse the deproteinization of dentin caused by sodium hypochlorite. Regarding resin adhesives, ascorbic acid facilitates improved cross-linking of polymer chains, promoting a complete curing process [26]. These combined actions significantly improve the bonding strength and overall adhesion quality between the dental materials and the tooth structure [37].

On the other hand, EDTA had the lowest pull-out strength compared with ascorbic and tannic acid and higher than the control group; these align with a recently published study by Albashaireh et al.; further, the pull-out strength in the current study was higher than the previous study in the same tested groups (ascorbic acid and EDTA), owing to the different post material applied and the air-particle abrasion of the post-and-core crowns in the current study [37].

This study identified mixed failure as the most prevalent type across all experimental groups except the control group. These results indicate that retention of post-and-core is contingent upon a combination of micromechanical interlocking, sliding friction, and chemical bonding. Nevertheless, the study encountered limitations, including the absence of mechanical aging and thermal cycling. However, our research was specifically designed to focus on the immediate pull-out strength of ceramic posts and cores without aging. This approach provides important baseline data for understanding the initial mechanical properties. Another limitation is the utilization of only one type of CAD-CAM material and a single luting cement. Further research is recommended to enhance our understanding of the role of root canal sealers and dentin conditioning protocols on the pull-out strength of PICN post-and-core systems. Additional studies will help validate our findings, explore underlying mechanisms, and optimize clinical protocols for improved bonding performance and long-term success. Moreover, to expand upon the current study findings, future research should evaluate the retention of different CAD-CAM post materials in conjunction with various self-adhesive cements. Furthermore, additional investigations could concentrate on developing materials and techniques that enhance the synergy among micromechanical, frictional, and chemical bonding mechanisms.
