The percentage of normal glomeruli (“Normal” Glomerular Score) predicts kidney outcome in ANCA vasculitis
Authors: Christos Chatzikyrkou, Carolin Herzog, Maximilian J Ganz, Thorsten Wiech, Hermann-Josef Gröne, Peter R Mertens, Florian G Scurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIn the management of anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV), accurate prognostication of renal outcome is critical. However, the clinical utility of highly precise multi-variable models, such as the ANCA Kidney Risk Score (AKRiS), is often hampered by their complexity. This study aimed to validate a simple alternative: the “Normal” Glomerular Score (NGS), defined as the percentage of normal glomeruli.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 188 consecutive patients with biopsy-proven AAV-glomerulonephritis (GN) from a single European center (2001–24). Patients were categorized by NGS: high (>50%), moderate (26%–50%), low (10%–25%) and very low (<10%). We assessed concordance between NGS, Berden classification and AKRiS to determine whether NGS serves as a valid proxy for complex systems. The primary endpoint was death-censored progression to end-stage kidney disease.ResultsThe NGS strongly indicated presentation renal function; median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was 31.4 mL/min in the high NGS group vs 10.8 mL/min in the low cohort (P < .001). It correlated moderately-to-strongly with AKRiS (Rho = –0.714, P < .001). Over a 46.6-month median follow-up, dialysis rates were 12.8%, 23.7%, 42.9% and 42.6% in the high, moderate, low and very low NGS groups (P = .003). There was no statistical difference in dialysis rates (P = .98) or long-term eGFR (28 vs 30 mL/min, P = .51) between the low and very low groups, indicating a prognostic plateau below 25% normal glomeruli. Cox regression showed a significant hazard ratio for dialysis of 1.432 with each worsening category (95% confidence interval 1.144–1.792). The NGS achieved a C-index of 0.812, statistically inferior to AKRiS (0.831). Subanalysis of post-2015 patients confirmed NGS’s prognostic validity with modern rituximab-based induction.ConclusionNGS predicts long-term renal survival in AAV-GN. While not replacing multi-variable models like AKRiS for precise risk assessment in clinical trials, it offers a rapid, accessible estimation of functional nephron mass for bedside stratification.Keywords: ANCA-associated vasculitis, ANCA Kidney Risk Score, crescentic glomerulonephritis, “Normal” Glomerular Score, risk stratification

INTRODUCTION

Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) is a group of severe, systemic autoimmune disorders where renal involvement is a frequent and life-threatening manifestation that dictates prognosis [1, 2]. Occurring in up to 83% of patients with microscopic polyangiitis and 59% with granulomatosis with polyangiitis, ANCA-associated glomerulonephritis (AAV-GN) classically presents as a pauci-immune, necrotizing and crescentic glomerulonephritis [3–7]. The kidney biopsy is therefore indispensable, not only for confirming the diagnosis but, more critically, for prognostication by allowing for a direct assessment of active, potentially reversible inflammation versus chronic, irreversible damage [8, 9].

The quest to standardize this prognostic assessment has led to the evolution of several histopathological classification systems. An initial landmark effort, the Berden classification, categorized biopsies based on the predominant pattern of glomerular injury (focal, crescentic, sclerotic or mixed) [10]. However, a growing body of evidence has highlighted that chronic changes in the tubulointerstitial compartment are significant and independent predictors of long-term renal outcomes across a spectrum of kidney diseases.

This recognition spurred the development of scoring systems focused on chronicity, such as the Mayo Clinic Chronicity Score, which systematically grades the severity of interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy (IFTA) [11]. This paradigm shifts toward a more holistic assessment culminated in the creation of comprehensive, multi-variable prognostic models. The ANCA Renal Risk Score (ARRS) and its recently updated successor, the ANCA Kidney Risk Score (AKRiS), integrate the most predictive performance indicators from three domains: glomerular health (percentage of normal glomeruli), tubulointerstitial chronicity (percentage of IFTA) and clinical reality [baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) or serum creatinine] [12, 13]. These algorithmic scores have demonstrated excellent predictive performance with the AKRiS achieving a Harrell’s C-index of 0.831 in the initial developing cohort and of 0.833 in a subsequent validation study in an independent cohort of European patients [13, 14].

While combining glomerular and tubulointerstitial data with clinical parameters (e.g. eGFR) undoubtedly improves predictive precision, the resulting complexity can impede rapid clinical application. [15–18]. There is a clinical need for a “bedside” metric—a simple, heuristic score that conveys immediate prognostic information without the need for calculators or detailed quantification of tubulointerstitial fibrosis, which suffers from high inter-observer variability.

In response to this, Moura et al. recently proposed the “Normal” Glomerular Score (NGS), a metric defined with intentional simplicity as the percentage of histologically normal-appearing glomeruli [19]. The rationale for not combining this score with kidney function or tubulointerstitial fibrosis in the primary definition is to isolate the predictive value of the “glomerular reserve” itself, creating a risk stratification aid that is histologically pure and independent of acute hemodynamic fluctuations that affect serum creatinine. The central hypothesis is that this single parameter, which reflects the kidney’s residual structural reserve, can serve as a robust triage score.

The objective of the present study is therefore twofold. First, we aim to test the robustness and generalizability of the NGS by applying it to our own independent, single-center cohort of patients with AAV-GN. Second, we aim to directly compare the predictive performance of NGS and AKRiS to better understand the clinical utility and trade-offs of each score.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study cohort and patient characteristics

Consecutive patients presenting in our clinic between 2001 and 2024 with a clinical diagnosis of microscopic polyangiitis or granulomatosis with polyangiitis according to the four-step algorithm proposed by the European Medicines Agency were included [20]. The study was limited to patients with biopsy-proven active kidney involvement, with or without positive ANCA serology. Patients with positive anti-glomerular basement membrane (anti-GBM) antibodies (dual positivity) or anti-GBM disease were excluded. In cases where immune deposits [e.g. mild C3 or immunoglobulin A (IgA)] and necrotizing features were present, the primary pathology was determined by the intensity of immunohistochemical staining and the clinical context; only cases with pauci-immune rapid progressive crescentic glomerulonephritis were dominant and the immune deposits were included in the analyses. Ethics approval was obtained from the local Ethics Committee of the Otto-von-Guericke University Magdeburg (No. 74/09), and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided informed consent for the use of their clinical data.

Treatment protocol

All patients received standard care according to established protocols [21–24]. Patients were followed until their last clinical interaction before 31 May 2024.

Reflecting the standard of care at our center during the observation period, induction therapy primarily consisted of either cyclophosphamide pulses or rituximab. Rituximab was administered at a fixed dose of 500 mg weekly for 4 consecutive weeks (total 2 g), a protocol widely used in European centers as a therapeutic equivalent to the body surface area–adjusted dose (375 mg/m² × 4) used in the Rituximab in ANCA-Associated Vasculitis (RAVE) trial. Maintenance therapy with rituximab was not fixed but B-cell guided: re-dosing was triggered by the repopulation of CD19/CD20-positive B cells (>0/µL) or ANCA titer recurrence, rather than fixed temporal intervals, to minimize immunosuppressive burden. In select recent cases (n = 5), avacopan was utilized as an adjunct to reduce glucocorticoid exposure. Plasma exchange was offered to patients presenting with severe renal failure (serum creatinine >500 µmol/L) or diffuse alveolar hemorrhage, in line with PEXIVAS trial considerations. Specifically, 38 patients (20.2%) underwent plasma exchange.

Clinical definitions, assessment criteria and outcomes

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation. Nephrotic syndrome was defined by the presence of at least three of the following four criteria: proteinuria >3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia <3.5 g/dL, hyperlipidemia and edema. Nephritic syndrome was defined by the presence of active urinary sediment and nephrotic/nephritic syndrome by the presence of both. End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) was defined as permanent need of dialysis over 12 weeks after diagnosis and contrasted with the dialysis-dependent acute kidney injury (AKI) at diagnosis. Some patients with dialysis-dependent AKI at diagnosis improved their kidney function following treatment initiation and dialysis was discontinued, while others with non-dialysis dependent AKI progressed to ESKD in the course of disease.

Disease activity was assessed every 3–6 months post-therapy using the Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score (BVAS) [25]. CD19/CD20 B cells and ANCAs were determined concomitant to BVAS at every scheduled or unexpected outpatient clinic visit. Remission was defined as the absence of any clinical sign or symptom of activity and a BVAS score of 0. Relapse was defined as a return or increase of vasculitis activity requiring additional or new treatment and was stratified according to BVAS. The screening of ANCA-associated disease included indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) and immunoassays for proteinase 3 (PR3-ANCA) and myeloperoxidase (MPO-ANCA) [26]. MPO-ANCA and PR3-ANCA detection was performed by the EUROLINE MPO/PR3/GBM profile immunoblot (EUROIMMUN AG, Lübeck, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Histopathological examination of kidney biopsies

Histopathological evaluation of the kidney biopsies was performed in reference centers for nephropathology using light and electron microscopy, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence techniques. The absolute and relative numbers of glomeruli with “normal appearance,” “globally sclerosed” and “necrotic” were counted in each biopsy. Mesangial matrix expansion and mesangial cellularity were assessed according to the consensus report of the renal pathology society and tubular injury according to known validated classification systems [27–29]. A 'normal' glomerulus was strictly defined as one with normal size and architecture, exhibiting no scarring (segmental or global), collapse, lobulation, cellular crescents, fibrinoid necrosis, or wall abnormalities in the open capillary loops, and with normal total cellularity. The latter was defined as no increase in mesangial or endocapillary cells beyond the usual two to three mesangial nuclei per mesangial area and a single layer of endothelial an podocyte nuclei along capillary walls and the lack of infiltration by neutrophils, monocytes or other inflammatory cells within capillary lumina [30]. Acute and chronic tubulointerstitial injury was assessed according to known validated classification systems [29, 31]. For the purpose of our analysis, the histopathological prognostic score proposed by Berden, the clinicopathological ARRS proposed by Brix and its recent update, the ANCA Kidney Risk score (AKRiS) and the NGS were calculated for each patient [10, 12, 13, 19].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data are reported as mean (standard deviation), while non-normally distributed data are reported as median (interquartile range). For group comparisons, categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate. For continuous variables, Student’s t-test (for two groups), or analysis of variance (for more than two groups) were used for normally distributed data, while the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test were used for non-normally distributed data, respectively.

Time-to-event analyses for outcomes such as remission, relapse, ESKD and death were conducted using the Kaplan–Meier method. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to identify predictive factors for these outcomes, with results reported as hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Patients were censored at their last known follow-up if the event of interest had not occurred. To address the potential impact of evolving therapies (specifically the introduction of rituximab as first-line induction), a temporal subanalysis was performed comparing patients diagnosed before and after 2015. To assess the utility of the NGS cut-offs, we compared the model performance of the NGS (using 10%, 25% and 50% thresholds) against the established ARRS and AKRiS.

Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated to characterize associations between laboratory markers and clinical parameters. Coefficients of <0.4 were considered as indicating low, of 0.4–0.7 medium and >0.7 strong correlation [32]. A P-value <.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed with the use of SPSS software, v27 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and IBM SPSS Statistics Essentials for R.

As the NGS category declined from high to very low, the median eGFR decreased from 31.4 mL/min in the high group to 18.9, 10.8 and 13.7 mL/min in the moderate, low and very low groups, respectively (P < .001). Other markers of disease severity demonstrated a similar pattern: the prevalence of anemia increased from 47.1% in the high NGS group to 75.0% in the very low group (P = .007), and the median albumin-to-creatinine ratio rose substantially from 100 mg/g to 540 mg/g across the same groups (P = .006) (Table 1).

In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed between the NGS groups for several other relevant parameters. These included vasculitis subtype, ANCA specificity, complement levels (C3 and C4), C-reactive protein, the presence of renal-limited disease, and major comorbidities such as hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Furthermore, the groups did not differ with regard to the immunosuppressive therapy administered, disease relapse rates or the duration of follow-up (Table 2).

A gradient was evident for the composite endpoint of death or dialysis at 12 months, with the risk increasing from 7.8% in the high NGS group to 14.9% in the moderate and 23.7% in the low NGS group (Table 2). Over the entire follow-up period, patients with a higher baseline NGS maintained significantly better renal function; the median eGFR at last follow-up was 51 mL/min in the high NGS group, compared with 28 mL/min and 30 mL/min in the low and very low groups, respectively (P = .005). Notably, despite these profound differences in renal outcomes, the rate of disease relapse did not differ significantly across the NGS groups (P = .908).

Prognostic value of the NGS for renal survival

Cox regression analysis revealed a significant association between the NGS category and the primary endpoint of death-censored progression to dialysis, with an HR of 1.432 per category decrease (95% CI 1.144–1.792). This was corroborated by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, which demonstrated a distinct and statistically significant separation of renal survival curves among the four NGS categories (P < .001) (Fig. 2).

For the investigation of low vs very low stratification, we explicitly evaluated the discriminatory value of the lower cut-offs. The need for dialysis was nearly identical in the low (42.9%) and very low (42.6%) NGS groups (P = .98) (Supplementary data, Table S2a). Similarly, there was no significant difference in long-term eGFR outcomes between these two groups (median 28 vs 30 mL/min, P = .51), although patients belonging to the low NGS group had lower eGFR and more common dialysis dependent acute kidney at diagnosis (10.8 vs 13.7 mL/min, 39.5 vs 30.8%, P = .01) (Supplementary data, Table S2b). This suggests that the 10% cut-off does not provide additional risk stratification in this cohort, as both groups reflect a state of critical renal damage.

Regarding the critical analysis of cut-offs, we explicitly evaluated the validity of the 50% cut-off. Patients with >50% normal glomeruli (high NGS) exhibited significantly better long-term renal survival compared with the moderate (26%–50%) group (5-year dialysis-free survival of 87% vs 76%, P = .04). Merging these groups into a single >25% category would have obscured the superior prognosis of the high NGS group, which corresponds to the “focal” class in the Berden classification. Thus, the 50% cut-off point is clinically relevant for identifying patients with an excellent prognosis.

The need for dialysis at initial presentation was significantly more frequent in the low (39.5%) and very low (30.8%) NGS groups compared with the high (13.7%) and moderate (14.9%) groups (P = .010). This risk stratification was maintained over the entire follow-up period; the cumulative death-censored incidence of dialysis was 12.8% in the high NGS group, rising to 23.7% in the moderate group and to 42.9% and 42.6% in the low and very low groups, respectively (P = .003) (Table 2). In terms of data censoring and follow-up, a notable decrease in the “number at risk” is observed at 12 and 24 months in the survival tables. This is primarily attributable to the fact that a significant proportion of patients (n = 45) were recruited between 2021 and 2024 and had therefore not yet reached long-term endpoints at the time of analysis. This does not represent loss to follow-up, but rather the inclusion of incident cases.

Comparison of the NGS with established histopathological scoring systems

The NGS demonstrated a significant relationship with the Berden histopathological classification. All patients in the high NGS group belonged to the focal class, whereas the proportion of patients with a sclerotic classification increased substantially as the NGS decreased: from 0% in the high NGS group to 34.0% in the moderate, 57.9% in the low and 69.2% in the very low group (P < .001) (Table 3).

Given that the percentage of normal glomeruli is a foundational component of the ANCA Renal Risk Score (ARRS) and the ANCA Kidney Risk Score (AKRiS), a significant correlation with the simpler NGS was anticipated. Our analysis confirmed this, revealing a statistically significant negative correlation between the NGS and both the continuous ARRS (Spearman’s Rho = –0.825, P < .001) and the AKRiS (Rho = –0.714, P < .001) (Supplementary data, Table S3).

This strong correlation extended to risk category comparisons. For instance, no patients in the high or moderate NGS groups were classified as high-risk by the ARRS. Conversely, more than half of the patients in the low (55.9%) and very low (58.7%) NGS groups fell into the ARRS high-risk category (P < .001). A similar pattern was observed for the AKRiS, where the proportion of patients classified as high-risk rose from 4.3% in the high NGS group to 47.1% and 45.7% in the low and very low NGS groups, respectively (P < .001) (Table 3). In a model performance comparison, we compared the predictive performance of the scores for the outcome of ESKD (Tables 4a–4c). The AKRiS demonstrated the highest C-index (0.831), followed by the ARRS (0.824) and NGS (0.812).

DISCUSSION

This study provides an independent validation of the NGS as a prognostic score in AAV-GN. Our findings confirm that this simple metric, based on a single histological parameter, predicts the risk of progression to ESKD. The core strength of the NGS lies in its direct reflection of the kidney’s structural reserve—the organ’s capacity to withstand the inflammatory insult and maintain function. Our data show a clear and linear relationship between the percentage of spared glomeruli and both baseline eGFR and long-term renal survival, confirming the score’s foundational premise [33–36].

The central question of this analysis was how the heuristic simplicity of the NGS compares to the algorithmic precision of comprehensive scores like the AKRiS [12, 13]. As the NGS is a core component of the AKRiS, a significant correlation was expected. Our analysis not only confirmed this (Rho = –0.714, P < .001) but also demonstrated that the NGS is the primary driver of risk stratification within these more complex models. This explains why the NGS performs so well as a standalone metric: it captures the single most critical variable of functional nephron mass. The AKRiS then refines this prediction by adding two other important variables: the degree of IFTA, which quantifies chronic damage in the crucial tubulointerstitial compartment, and baseline serum creatinine, which provides a real-time snapshot of overall kidney function [37]. While the AKRiS likely offers greater precision and a wider dynamic range of risk [38], particularly at the extremes, our results highlight that the NGS provides valuable prognostic information and is easy to use [39, 40]. Essentially, the NGS functions effectively as a “triage metric.” While it does not replace the comprehensive AKRiS for complex cases, it offers a distinct advantage in cognitive simplicity.

This presents a practical trade-off for the clinician. The NGS is a reasonable “at-a-glance” score for rapid risk assessment and patient counseling. The ability to categorize a patient as “high risk” (<10% normal) or “low risk” (>50% normal) based on a single line in the pathology report—without calculating a composite score involving GFR and atrophy percentages—facilitates immediate risk communication at the bedside. Communicating that “fewer than 10% of your kidney’s filters appear normal” is a tangible and meaningful statement [34, 41]. Although it is more cumbersome to calculate, the AKRiS provides a risk percentage that may be superior for stratifying patients in clinical trials or for decision-making regarding high-risk therapies [38, 42].

Importantly, our survival analysis supports retaining the 50% cut-off originally proposed by Moura et al. Patients with >50% normal glomeruli exhibit a distinct, superior long-term renal survival trajectory compared with the moderate (26%–50%) group. This distinction is clinically vital: the high NGS group represents a “super-responder” cohort that may be suitable for rapid glucocorticoid tapering or steroid-sparing regimens (e.g. avacopan monotherapy). Merging these groups would obscure this opportunity for de-escalation.

However, our analysis identified a significant limitation at the lower end of the spectrum. We observed a prognostic plateau where patients with <10% and 10%–25% normal glomeruli had nearly identical dialysis rates (∼43%) and long-term eGFR outcomes. This suggests that once the normal glomerular reserve falls below 25%, the kidney reaches a tipping point of decompensation, and the 10% cut-off adds little discriminative value for renal survival in this population.

This finding highlight that the simplicity of the NGS is also its main limitation, creating a potential “heuristic trap” where an easy-to-use shortcut may lead to suboptimal clinical decisions. By collapsing all forms of injury into a single “non-normal” category, the score fails to distinguish between active, potentially reversible lesions like cellular crescents and irreversible, chronic damage like global sclerosis [42–44]. This distinction is the absolute cornerstone of therapeutic decision-making. Consider two patients, both with a “very low” NGS of 8%: for Patient A, this is driven by active cellular crescents, indicating a high potential for renal recovery with aggressive immunosuppression; for Patient B, this is driven by global sclerosis, where the same therapy would offer little benefit and significant risk. The NGS assigns them the same score, highlighting its inability to guide prescriptive, individualized therapy. Thus, while the NGS is an effective tool for risk stratification it is not a useful metric for guiding treatment choices. However, to our knowledge, none of the available prognostic scores have been used to allocate patients with AAV to a more or less intensive immunosuppressive regimen. In the current era of personalized medicine, it is therefore important to further test the complementary prognostic value of both NGS and AKRiS scores in a prospective, randomized, controlled study.

Another limitation is that NGS only indirectly captures the condition of the tubulointerstitium. Given that severe IFTA is a well-established, independent predictor of renal failure, any scoring system that does not directly quantify it is inherently incomplete [45–47].

Conversely, regarding the inclusion of patients with incidental immune deposits, our cohort reflects real-world practice where “pure” pauci-immune GN is part of a spectrum. The robustness of the NGS in this mixed cohort supports the concept that the cumulative loss of functional nephrons—regardless of whether the “second hit” is mild IgA deposition or hypertensive sclerosis—is the determinant of survival. Nevertheless, another critical limitation is the unaddressed challenge of inter-observer reliability. This study did not formally assess it, a significant omission that directly challenges the original claim by Moura et al. that the score “does not require expertise to quantify” [19]. The distinction between a truly normal glomerulus and one with subtle ischemic changes, mild mesangial hypercellularity or early segmental sclerosis is subjective and a known cause of variability among pathologists [48]. Without a formal study of inter-observer agreement, the reproducibility of the NGS remains unknown. The future of such quantitative scores likely lies in the use of digital pathology platforms coupled with validated artificial intelligence algorithms, which could standardize the identification of normal glomeruli and overcome the inherent subjectivity of human observation [49].

Furthermore, neither in our study nor in the original training cohort was inter-observer variability addressed. Moreover, while the final calculation is simple arithmetic, the primary input—the classification of each glomerulus as “normal”—is an act of expert interpretation. The distinction between a truly normal glomerulus and one with subtle ischemic changes, mild mesangial hypercellularity or early segmental sclerosis is subjective and a known cause of variability among pathologists [48]. Without a formal study of inter-observer agreement (e.g. using a Kappa statistic), the reproducibility of the NGS remains unknown, compromising its value as a standardized metric. Beyond that, there may be ultrastructural signs of even subtle glomerular injury that cannot be detected by standard light microscopy preventing [50].

The primary strength of this study is its design as an independent, external validation of the NGS in a well-characterized cohort from a tertiary referral center. Our patient population was systematically identified from a large-scale screening of over 1300 biopsies, a method that reduces selection bias and ensures a representative sample of AAV-GN. Furthermore, the comprehensive nature of our database—with detailed clinical, histopathological and long-term follow-up data—allowed for a head-to-head comparison of the NGS against multiple established scoring systems within the same patient cohort. Our cohort is unique in its temporal range, extending to 2024 and including patients treated with avacopan and B cell–guided rituximab maintenance. The fact that the NGS remains a predictor in this modern therapeutic era suggests that the “histological damage threshold” for non-recovery is biological and relatively immutable. Regardless of whether induction is achieved with cyclophosphamide or rituximab, if the glomerular reserve is lost, the outcome remains poor.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective, single-center design, while internally consistent, may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare systems with different demographic profiles or practice patterns. Our cohort was ethnically homogeneous, underscoring the need for future validation in larger and more diverse patient populations. Additionally, the specific treatment protocols of a single center may limit external validity. While this dosing is pharmacokinetically justified in our region, it deviates from the RAVE protocol. Furthermore, the exclusion of double-positive (anti-GBM/ANCA) patients means these findings cannot be extrapolated to that high-risk subgroup. Finally, as previously discussed, the inter-observer reliability of the NGS was not formally assessed in this study, and evaluating this remains an important step for its broader implementation in clinical practice. We did not evaluate the ultrastructural architecture of normal-appearing glomeruli by means of electron microscopy, a shortcoming that should be addressed in future studies.

CONCLUSION

We provide a validation of the original NGS in another independent cohort of patients with AAV. While complex multi-variable scores like the AKRiS are statistically superior, the NGS provides important prognostic information and it is easy to use. It is therefore a simple tool to assess the kidney’s structural reserve. However, its effectiveness in aiding important treatment decisions, such as the tailoring of immunosuppressive therapy, has not yet been tested.
