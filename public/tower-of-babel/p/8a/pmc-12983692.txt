Improving GIRFT compliance and patient experience of accessibility and shared decision making for elective hip and knee replacement: incorporation of a digital patient information leaflet

Introduction

NICE and GIRFT guidance

Ensuring patients receive clear, comprehensive information throughout their surgical journey is a central tenet of current national guidelines. NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) Guideline NG157 (2020) emphasises the importance of shared decision making (SDM) when considering elective primary hip and knee replacements, recommending that information be accessible and tailored to the individual’s needs, preferences and language or sensory requirements.1 This includes using formats that patients can understand and revisit, such as leaflets, audiovisual tools or interpreters when necessary. The GIRFT (Getting it Right First Time) Elective Hip and Knee Pathway further highlights that appropriate documentation of patient understanding, pain impact, conservative management and postoperative planning is essential for delivering standardised care.2

Montgomery ruling and importance of information

The Montgomery v Lanarkshire Health Board [2015] UKSC 11, para 87 decision mandates that clinicians ensure patients are made aware of any material risks involved in a proposed treatment, as well as reasonable alternatives. The ruling shifted the focus from a doctor-centred to a patient-centred standard, reinforcing the need for information delivery that patients can truly understand.3 This is particularly relevant in elective surgery, where informed consent must reflect not only clinical facts but also patient values. The literature in orthopaedic surgery has further emphasised the medicolegal consequences of inadequate consent processes, with calls for better standardisation of documented and verbal patient information.4

Inadequate information provision also carries clinical risks. Accessibility of information has broader implications for healthcare equity, adherence and outcomes. Poor accessibility contributes to disengagement, miscommunication and suboptimal recovery. The prevalence of litigation due to miscommunication highlights its importance: Majeed et al found that between 2008 and 2019, there were 310 litigation claims in trauma and orthopaedics specifically due to consent issues—most commonly in hip and knee surgery.5

Supporting this, recent evidence suggests that interactive digital tools used during the consent process are highly effective: in one systematic review, 85% of such interventions (11 out of 13 studies) demonstrated improved patient comprehension when compared with standard informed consent practices.6

Language barriers

Language can act as a significant barrier to care. The Office for National Statistics (2011) reported that 8% of the UK population speak a first language other than English, with nearly 900 000 individuals in England and Wales unable to speak English well or at all. These individuals are more likely to report their health as ‘not good’ and face challenges accessing healthcare.7 A 2018 review by NHS (National Health Service) Clinical Commissioning Groups highlighted a low awareness of interpreting services, indicating that language barriers are often unrecognised and unmet in practice.8

Recent studies from the USA have reinforced these concerns. One study conducted in South Florida found that, although wait times for shoulder arthroplasty were similar between English-speaking and Spanish-speaking patients, over 25% of orthopaedic clinics lacked qualified interpreter access, with only a minority offering professional interpretation services.9 A separate systematic review of 29 studies, encompassing over 360 000 orthopaedic patients, reported that limited English proficiency was consistently associated with poorer patient engagement and outcomes. This included lower completion rates of patient-reported outcome measures, reduced use of digital patient portals, increased length of stay and higher rates of discharge to non-home settings. In some cases, language barriers contributed to differences in anaesthetic techniques and delayed surgical intervention.10 Together, these findings underscore the need for multilingual, accessible formats in both written and digital preoperative education, to ensure equitable care for all patients.

Visual impairment in older adults

Older adults, who comprise the majority of patients undergoing joint replacements, are disproportionately affected by visual impairments, including cataracts, macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy. In our first audit cycle, 40% of patients had documented visual problems, with a further 8% at risk due to conditions like type 2 diabetes. A national UK survey reported that 12.4% of adults aged 65 years and over had visual impairment, with prevalence increasing markedly with age, rising from 3.1% in those aged 65–74 years to 35.5% in those aged 85 years and above.11 These impairments reduce the utility of standard paper leaflets, which rely on small print and static formatting.

However, recent research suggests that despite visual challenges, mobile technology remains highly accessible within this group. Thomas et al reported that among older US adults with vision impairment, 89.8% owned a mobile phone, compared with 61.5% owning a computer and 40.1% a tablet. After adjusting for demographics and comorbidities, phone ownership rose to 92%, just 3% lower than those without impairment.12 The study concluded that cell-based health interventions may be the most inclusive modality for engaging visually impaired older adults, helping to bridge the digital divide.

Patient-oriented mobile solutions, such as QR (Quick Response) code-linked digital information, leveraging devices this population already uses, offer a promising means of enhancing accessibility, understanding and retention, especially for those disadvantaged by visual impairment.

NHS Green Plan

Sustainability is a growing priority across the NHS. Our Trust Green Plan sets out clear commitments to reducing carbon emissions by 5% annually and cutting the overall carbon footprint by 25% within 5 years. Key strategies include minimising paper use, encouraging digital communication and promoting reuse and recycling across departments.13 This aligns with the broader NHS Net Zero Plan, which commits the health service to achieving net zero carbon emissions by 2040.14 Transitioning to digital patient information formats supports these goals by reducing reliance on paper, waste and printing costs, while enabling more efficient information sharing.

Methods

This quality improvement project was conducted at a District General Hospital as a two-cycle audit aimed at evaluating the provision and accessibility of PILs for individuals listed for elective hip or knee replacement surgery. The objective was to assess baseline compliance with guidance from NICE and GIRFT on SDM and patient education, and to evaluate the impact of introducing a digital PIL accessed via QR code on both documentation and patient experience.

Data collection took place in the preoperative elective orthopaedic clinics. Cycle 1 reviewed patients listed for surgery over a 6-week period from June to July 2024, and cycle 2 captured a re-audit group from a second 6-week window between September and October 2024. Patients were included if they were added to the elective hip or knee replacement waiting list during these periods and had face-to-face clinic assessments. Patients with incomplete records or who were not assessed in person were excluded. In total, 25 patients were reviewed in cycle 1 and 43 in cycle 2.

Clinic letters and TrakCare electronic records were examined to assess documentation against GIRFT and NICE NG157 recommendations. Specific domains included whether a PIL was provided, whether risks and benefits were discussed, whether the patient’s understanding and willingness to proceed were noted and whether information was given about spinal anaesthesia, surgical preparation and recovery expectations. Documentation was also reviewed for consideration of visual impairments and language barriers, given the high prevalence of sensory and communication challenges in the elective orthopaedic population.

Between the two audit cycles, a digital leaflet intervention was introduced. Patients were provided with a QR code at the end of their clinic appointment, linking to a redesigned online patient information resource. This leaflet included sections on the nature of the procedure, recovery timeline, anaesthetic options, wound care and returning to activities. Translation services were available through the Northern Care Alliance, and patients could revisit the information as needed. Clinical staff were encouraged to offer the digital leaflet and document its provision in the medical notes.

The second cycle assessed both the improvement in documentation and patient experience. In addition to standard record review, patients who received the digital leaflet were invited to complete a voluntary, anonymous feedback questionnaire. This used a Likert-scale format to evaluate ease of access, clarity of content, visual and language accessibility, likelihood of revisiting the information and preference for digital versus paper formats.

The primary outcome measures were the proportion of patients documented to have received information leaflets and the proportion noted to have been given a copy of the consent form. Secondary outcomes included the qualitative responses from the patient feedback survey. This project was registered with the local audit department and conducted in accordance with institutional quality improvement standards. As it involved a retrospective review of clinical documentation and anonymised feedback without altering routine care, formal ethical approval was not required. Given the modest sample size and the QIP (Quality Improvement Project) design, analysis was descriptive rather than inferential. Trends were compared across audit cycles to demonstrate direction of change. This approach was chosen to highlight practical improvements in documentation and patient engagement rather than to establish statistical significance.

This manuscript was prepared in accordance with the Standards for QUality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE) 2.0 reporting guideline15 and edited using the SQUIRE 2.0 reporting checklist16 which is provided as online supplemental file A.

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this quality improvement project. Patients contributed only as participants through provision of feedback via an optional survey.

Results

A total of 25 patients were reviewed in cycle 1 (June–July 2024) and 43 patients in cycle 2 (September–October 2024). Documentation across key shared decision-making and patient education domains was compared before and after the implementation of QR code-linked digital PILs.

In cycle 1, documentation of whether a PIL had been provided was notably low, with only 7% of hip patients and 9% of knee patients having evidence that a leaflet was offered. Following the digital intervention, this improved to 24% in hip patients and 36% in knee patients Similarly, documentation that a copy of the consent form had been provided remained low across both cycles, 30% and 28% for hips and 50% for knees in both audit periods, highlighting this as a persistent area for improvement.

Improvements were observed in nearly all domains of preoperative discussion and information sharing. Documentation of examination findings increased from 57% to 81% in hip patients and from 82% to 86% in knee patients. The proportion of patients with recorded understanding and willingness to proceed rose from 79% to 90% (hips) and from 73% to 100% (knees). Key educational elements such as risks and benefits of surgery remained consistently high across both cycles, though documentation of aspects like recovery expectations, spinal anaesthetic and wound care saw increases from ≤9% in cycle 1 to up to 36% in cycle 2. Patient feedback on the digital intervention was overwhelmingly positive. A positive response was indicated by a Likert scale response of agree or strongly agree. The majority (71%) found the QR code leaflet easy to use, with 100% of respondents either agreeing or strongly agreeing that the information was clear, easy to understand and provided all necessary details about their treatment. Furthermore, 86% said they would revisit the information prior to surgery, and 86% also expressed a preference for receiving information via QR code in the future. Feedback also revealed challenges that digital resources helped address. For instance, 29% of respondents reported struggling to read small text on paper-based materials, highlighting the accessibility advantage of digital formats.

Overall, the results demonstrate a marked improvement in documentation of patient education following the digital intervention, as well as strong patient support for the transition to more accessible, reusable and visually inclusive information delivery methods.

Discussion

Summary of key findings

This two-cycle quality improvement audit demonstrated that introducing QR code-linked digital PILs in elective hip and knee replacement clinics improved documentation and patient engagement. Documentation that a PIL had been offered rose from 7% (hip) and 9% (knee) in cycle 1 to 24% and 36%, respectively, in cycle 2. Other domains, including documentation of wound care, spinal anaesthesia and recovery expectations, increased from under 10% to around 30% following the intervention. Qualitative data were similarly encouraging: 100% of patients found the digital information clear and sufficient and 86% preferred it over paper formats. These findings support a shift towards digital information delivery in preoperative orthopaedic care.

Improvement in documentation

The improvement in documentation following the digital intervention aligns with national recommendations from NICE NG157 and the GIRFT pathway, both of which emphasise patient-centred communication and documentation of SDM.1 2 Our results are consistent with Dyke et al, who reported that transitioning from paper to digital consent in trauma and orthopaedics led to a complete elimination of illegibility and missing key risks, as well as a more consistent record of SDM. In their study, 72% of digital consultations achieved ‘gold-standard’ SDM compared with 28% on paper.17 Similarly, in our second cycle, documentation improved across almost all domains—including critical consent components such as the patient’s understanding and willingness to proceed, which increased from 79% to 90% for hips and from 73% to 100% for knees. This reflects better alignment with the Montgomery standard3 and reinforces the value of digital resources as both educational and medicolegally protective tools.4 5

Digital consenting and patient comprehension

Our findings mirror those of multiple systematic reviews. Kiernan et al demonstrated that 70% of digital consenting studies showed improved immediate comprehension, while 45% reported improved retention; benefits which we observed through high levels of patient-reported understanding and intent to revisit the information preoperatively.18 Furthermore, in Glaser et al’s review, 85% of digital interventions improved informed consent comprehension, without reducing satisfaction or increasing anxiety; findings echoed in our feedback, where 100% of patients found the digital resource easy to understand and 86% found it preferable to paper.6 Implementing an integrated digital consent process could be a viable further intervention to address our ongoing lower rates of consent documentation where no improvement was seen.

Parsons et al argued that digital information delivery empowers patients, promotes autonomy and creates an audit trail; benefits particularly valuable in high-volume elective surgery where consent processes can be variable.19

Patient attitudes and engagement

Patient engagement with the digital leaflets was high. 71% felt more engaged than with traditional formats, and 86% said they would use the resource again prior to surgery. This aligns with evidence from Azad et al, who found QR codes improved access and comprehension in an ophthalmology setting.20 Similarly, Almanasef reported that patients prefer digital formats for convenience and readability,21 while Hammar et al found greater satisfaction with electronic PILs due to clarity and personalisation.22 In our audit, patients commented on the ease of use and appreciated the ability to access the information on their own devices. This is particularly meaningful considering the high proportion of our population with documented visual impairment or comorbidities affecting access to care.

Robinson et al further emphasised the value of well-designed digital interventions, noting that tailored, interactive tools before and after surgery can significantly enhance the patient experience and knowledge retention.23 Our QR-linked leaflet incorporated these principles.

Sustainability and accessibility benefits

Digitisation aligns with both patient-centred and environmental priorities. Our Trust Green Plan calls for a 5% annual reduction in carbon emissions and emphasises reduced reliance on paper,13 in line with NHS England’s Net Zero goal by 2040.14 Our audit supports this agenda: the shift to QR-code resources reduces unnecessary printing, physical storage and waste, while also improving traceability and adaptability of patient education.

Ali reported that QR codes not only support sustainability but enhance real-time information access and reduce costs.24 Similarly, Youssef et al outlined the transformative role of digital tools in orthopaedics, emphasising their capacity to enhance communication and reduce care disparities.25 Waller et al also found that eHealth tools improved preoperative preparedness, satisfaction and reduced variation in care delivery—benefits clearly mirrored in our patient feedback.26

Limitations and areas for improvement

While documentation improved across most domains, absolute compliance remained below ideal levels. This reflects broader systemic challenges around consistent documentation. The intervention relied on verbal prompts and staff engagement, without automatic reminders or mandatory fields in electronic records. Embedding digital prompts into TrakCare or similar systems could further improve compliance.

The sample size was modest (25 patients in cycle 1; 43 in cycle 2), and while qualitative feedback was rich, it was obtained via voluntary survey, introducing potential bias. Future efforts should include larger populations across multiple centres and assess long-term outcomes such as consent quality, reoperation risk or legal challenge rates.

Future directions

This QIP supports scaling up digital-based information delivery across other elective specialties. Future iterations could include video explanations, voiceover guides for patients with reading difficulty or interactive modules to reinforce comprehension. Further work could compare digital tools against formal consent outcomes (eg, litigation, errors, re-consent rates) and evaluate whether embedding these formats into mandatory hospital workflows ensures more consistent documentation.

Conclusion

This project demonstrated that implementing QR-linked digital PILs significantly improved both the documentation of patient information delivery and patient engagement in elective orthopaedic care. The results align with growing literature on the benefits of digital consent models, patient preferences and sustainable practice. In an ageing and increasingly diverse population, these tools offer a scalable, accessible and patient-centred approach to improving surgical communication and SDM.
