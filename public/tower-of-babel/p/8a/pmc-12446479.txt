Spirostan and spirosol saponin derivatives from Solanum muricatum Aiton and their cytotoxic activities: insights into bioactive compounds and molecular mechanisms
Authors: Fathy A Behery, Mohamed A M El Gendy, Mohamed A Zarka, Mubarak A Alzubaidi, Hesham A Abou-Zied, Gerhard Bringmann, Usama R Abdelmohsen, Abeer H Elmaidomy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSolanum muricatum Aiton (Family: Solanaceae), commonly known as pepino, is a traditionally used edible plant with reported medicinal properties, though its bioactive compounds remain largely unexplored. This study aimed to isolate cytotoxic constituents from its aerial parts and investigate their anticancer mechanisms. Two new spirostan-type saponins (1–2), and one known spirosol saponin (3) were identified via spectroscopic analysis. Among them, the plant extract and compound 3 showed significant cytotoxicity against A549 and HepG2 human cancer cell lines. Computational studies suggested PI3K-alpha as a key molecular target, with compound 3 showing strong binding affinity and stable interactions as confirmed by molecular dynamics simulations. These findings highlight the potential of S. muricatum saponins, particularly compound 3, as promising anticancer agents targeting PI3K-alpha.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-03891-y.Keywords: Solanum muricatum, Spirostan, Spirosol saponins, HepG2, A549, Molecular dockingSubject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide, representing a serious public health challenge1. Characterized by uncontrolled cell growth and spread to other parts of the body, cancer can manifest in various forms, affecting nearly any organ2. The disease is often diagnosed in its advanced stages, making it difficult to treat and significantly reducing the chances of successful recovery3. The increasing prevalence of cancer, coupled with the high costs and side effects associated with current treatment options such as chemotherapy, radiation, and surgery, underscores the urgent need for safe, effective, and affordable therapeutic alternatives4. Despite significant advances in cancer research, the quest for novel anticancer agents continues to be a critical focus of scientific inquiry5. The development of treatments with fewer side effects and enhanced targeting of cancer cells is essential to improving the quality of life and survival rates for cancer patients6.

Solanum muricatum Aiton belongs to the plant family Solanaceae. It is known as pepino, sweet cucumber, or melon pear7. The plant is native to South America, specifically the Andes area of Peru and Chile, and is cultivated in temperate and tropical countries8–10. Pepino has both nutritional and medicinal values. Pepino fruits are used as a dessert and as one of the salad ingredients11,12. Traditionally, the fruit is used for diabetes mellites and stimulating urination13,14. The extract of the fruits as well as the leaves showed antioxidant, antiscorbutic, anti-diabetic, anticancer, anti-inflammatory, and antihypertensive effects8,15–18. The nutritive and medicinal values of pepino are attributed to the high content of polyphenols, vitamins, carotenoids, and essential minerals16,19,20.

Although Solanum muricatum has shown promising pharmacological effects, most studies to date have relied on crude extracts, with limited efforts to isolate and characterize individual bioactive compounds21. Only a few reports have documented the presence of polyphenols, flavonoids (such as quercetin), and polysaccharides in the fruits and leaves, attributing to them antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties22. However, detailed phytochemical analyses, especially targeting non-polar and glycosidic constituents like steroidal saponins, remain scarce23. Furthermore, mechanistic studies elucidating how these compounds exert their effects—such as specific molecular targets or signaling pathways involved in cytotoxicity—are lacking. This presents a significant knowledge gap that impedes the development of S. muricatum-derived therapeutics. A few studies have suggested that aqueous leaf or fruit extracts may inhibit tumor growth or metastasis, potentially via immunomodulatory or pro-apoptotic pathways15,24,25. For instance, Shathish and colleagues (2014–2015) demonstrated anti-metastatic and immune-regulating effects26. Despite these findings, no comprehensive investigation has combined the isolation of pure phytoconstituents with mechanistic exploration at the molecular level. Although Li GuiQin reported that pepino fruit juice had no effect on cancer27, several other publications confirmed the anticancer potential of pepino extracts. The mechanism of action of the plant is not clearly understood. However, the antioxidant properties of pepino play an important role against stress-related diseases such as cancer15. In addition, pepino extracts showed antiproliferative, apoptotic, antimetastatic, as well as immunomodulatory activities24,25,28,29.

Of particular note, there are few reports mentioning the isolation of pure secondary metabolites from pepino30. According to a literature review search, there is no conducted research on pepino that is cultivated in Egypt. In addition, the mechanism behind all pepino effects is not fully illucidated14. Besides, previous phytochemical investigations have reported the presence of steroidal and triterpenoid saponins in Solanum genus31,32.

This study aimed to isolate and characterize bioactive saponin derivatives from Egyptian-cultivated S. muricatum, evaluate their cytotoxic activity against A549 and HepG2 human cancer cell lines, and elucidate their potential molecular targets using in silico tools such as protein-protein interaction analysis, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations.

The present work addresses a critical gap in understanding the chemical basis and mechanism of S. muricatum’s anticancer potential by focusing on purified constituents rather than crude extracts. To our knowledge, this is the first report of isolating spirostan and spirosol saponins from Egyptian S. muricatum and investigating their interaction with oncogenic targets like PI3 K-alpha.

Materials and methods

Plant material

S. muricatum aerial parts were collected in August 2024 from the Faculty of Pharmacy, Beni-Suef. It was kindly identified botanically by Dr. Abd ElHalim A. Mohammed (Department of Flora and Phytotaxonomy Research, Dokki, Cairo, Egypt). A voucher specimen (2023-BuPD 105) has been kept in the Department of Pharmacognosy, Faculty of Pharmacy, Beni-Suef University, Egypt.

Chemicals and reagents

The solvents used in this work included n-hexane (boiling point (b.p.) 60–80 °C), dichloromethane (DCM), ethyl acetate (EtOAc), n-butanol, ethanol (EtOH), and methanol (MeOH) were purchased from El-Nasr Company for Pharmaceuticals and Chemicals (Egypt). High Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC), and deuterated solvents used for chromatographic and spectroscopic analysis, Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), and [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,3-diphenyltetrazoliumbromide] (MTT) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, Missouri, USA), including HPLC-methanol, HPLC-water, HPLC-acetonitrile, and MeOH Column chromatography (CC) was performed using silica gel 60 (63–200 μm, E. Merck, Sigma-Aldrich), and Sephadex LH-20 (0.25–0.1 mm, GE Healthcare, Sigma-Aldrich). Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was carried out using pre-coated silica gel 60 GF254 plates (E. Merck, Darmstadt, Germany; 20 × 20 cm, 0.25 mm in thickness). Spots were visualized by spraying with p-anisaldehyde (PAA) reagent (85:5:10:0.5, for the components absolute EtOH:sulfuric acid:para-anisaldehyde), followed by heating at 110 °C33. Penicillin-streptomycin, trypsin/EDTA solution, and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Doxorubicin HCl (Adricin®) was from EIMC United Pharma (EUP) (Egypt). Isopropyl alcohol, tissue culture flasks, and tissue culture plates were obtained from Fisher Scientific (Toronto, ON, Canada).

Spectral analysis

Proton 1H and distortionless enhancement by polarization transfer-Q (DEPT-Q) 13C NMR spectra were recorded at 400 and 100 MHz, respectively. Tetramethylsilane (TMS) was used as an internal standard in MeOH-d4, using the residual solvent peak (δH = 3.34, 4.78 and δC = 49.9) as the reference. Measurements were performed on a Bruker Advance III 400 MHz with BBFO Smart Probe and a Bruker 400 MHz EON Nitrogen-Free Magnet (Bruker AG, Billerica, MA, USA). Carbon multiplicities were determined using a DEPT-Q experiment. The ultraviolet radiation (UV) spectrum in MeOH was obtained using a Shimadzu UV 2401PC spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation – UV-2401PC/UV-2501PC, Kyoto, Japan). Infrared (IR) spectra were measured using a Jasco FTIR 300E infrared spectrophotometer. HRESIMS data were obtained using an Acquity Ultra Performance Liquid Chromatography system coupled with a Synapt G2 HDMS quadrupole time-of-flight hybrid mass spectrometer (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). HPLC chromatographic separations were conducted using an Agilent 1260 Infinity preparative pump (G1361 A), Agilent 1260 diode array detector VL (G1315 D), Agilent 1260 Infinity Thermostand column compartment (G1361 A), Agilent 1260 Infinity preparative autosampler (G2260 A) and a YMC-Pack ODS-A A-324 column (i.d. 10 × 300 mm, YMC, Kyoto, Japan).

Extraction and fractionation of S. muricatum

S. muricatum aerial parts (1 kg) were collected, air-dried in the shade for 1 week, then finely powdered using an OC-60B/60B grinding machine (60–120 mesh, Henan, Mainland China). The powder was extracted by maceration using 80% MeOH (5 L, 3×, 7 d each) at room temperature, and concentrated under vacuum at 45 °C using a rotary evaporator (Buchi Rotavapor R-300, Cole-Parmer, Vernon Hills, IL, USA) to afford 230 g crude extract then kept at 4 °C for biological and phytochemical investigations34–42.

Isolation and purification of S. muricatum derived compounds

Air-dried aerial parts of S. muricatum (1 kg) were powdered and extracted with 80% methanol (3 × 3 L). The combined methanolic extracts were evaporated under reduced pressure to obtain a dry residue (230 g), which was suspended in distilled water and partitioned successively with n-hexane, dichloromethane (DCM), and n-butanol (n-BuOH), yielding 10 g, 15 g, and 30 g, respectively. A part of the n-BuOH extract (20 g), was fractionated on a polyamide-6 column (50–160 μm, 1000 × 5 cm, 200 g) using gradient elution starting with water (H2O) and ending with MeOH in the order of increasing polarities (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 80 and 100%, 1000 mL each, 5 mL/min). The effluents were collected in fractions (200 mL each); each fraction was concentrated and monitored by TLC using system EtOAC: glacial acetic acid: formic acid: H2O 10:1:1:2 and PAA reagent. Similar fractions were grouped to provide three sub-fractions (I1–I3).

Sub-fractions I1 (1.0 g) were further fractionated on a silica gel column (63–200 μm, 65 × 1.5 cm, 50 g). Elution was performed using DCM: MEOH gradient mixtures in order of increasing polarities (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 80 and 100%, 250 mL each, 0.5 mL/min). The effluents were collected in fractions (20 mL each); each fraction was concentrated and monitored by TLC using system EtOAC: glacial acetic acid: formic acid: H2O 10:1:1:2 and PAA reagent to afford one promising sub-subfractions (50 mg, DCM: MEOH 15%) which was further purified on a Sephadex LH20 column (0.25–0.1 mm, 100 × 0.5 cm, 100 gm) which eluted with MeOH to afford compound 1 (15 mg), (Fig. 1).

Sub-fractions I2 (0.5 g) were further fractionated on a silica gel column (63–200 μm, 65 × 1.5 cm, 25 g). Elution was performed using DCM: MEOH gradient mixtures in order of increasing polarities (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 80 and 100%, 250 mL each, 0.5 mL/min). The effluents were collected in fractions (10 mL each); each fraction was concentrated and monitored by TLC using system EtOAC: glacial acetic acid: formic acid: H2O 10:1:1:2 and PAA reagent to afford one promising sub-subfractions (23 mg, DCM: MEOH 10%) which was further purified on a Sephadex LH20 column (0.25–0.1 mm, 100 × 0.5 cm, 50 gm) which eluted with MeOH to afford compound 2 (25 mg), (Fig. 1).

Sub-fractions I3 (2.0 g) were further fractionated on a silica gel column (63–200 μm, 65 × 1.5 cm, 100 g). Elution was performed using DCM: MEOH gradient mixtures in order of increasing polarities (0, 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 80 and 100%, 250 mL each, 0.5 mL/min). The effluents were collected in fractions (40 mL each); each fraction was concentrated and monitored by TLC using system EtOAC: glacial acetic acid: formic acid: H2O 10:1:1:2 and PAA reagent to afford one promising sub-subfractions (75 mg, DCM: MEOH 20%) which was further purified on a Sephadex LH20 column (0.25–0.1 mm, 100 × 0.5 cm, 100 gm) which eluted with MeOH to afford compound 3 (35 mg), (Fig. 1).

The final purity of the isolated compounds was confirmed by analytical HPLC (C18 column, MeOH: H₂O 80:20, isocratic), showing > 95% purity at 210 nm. Compound structures were confirmed by 1D and 2D NMR38–40,42–46.

Acid hydrolysis and sugar analysis

Sugar hydrolysis and GC-MS analysis of derivatives were carried out in accordance with Abbet et al. 2011, in order to support structural elucidation by providing additional information on the mass fragmentation patterns of the sugars moieties, thereby complementing the NMR46. Hydrolysis was performed individually on compounds 1 (1.0 mg) and 2 (1.5 mg). The mixture was extracted with CH2Cl2 (3 × 1.0 mL) after heating at 100 °C for 1 h, in 2 M trifluoroacetic acid (1 mL). The aqueous phase was freeze-dried before being redissolved in 200 mL of dry pyridine with 5 mg/mL L-cysteine methyl ester hydrochloride. The reaction mixture was heated at 60 °C for 1 h, before silylation with hexamethyldisilazane and chlorotrimethylsilane (Fluka) in pyridine (3:1:10, 300 mL) for 30 min, at 60 °C47. Pyridine was evaporated after silylation, and the solid residue was extracted with n-hexane. A 5890 Series II gas chromatograph linked to an HP 5971 A mass detector (Hewlett Packard, USA) was used for the GC-MS analysis. The separation was performed on a DB-225 MS column (30 m, 0.25 mm, I.D., Waters, USA); column temperature was 150 °C for 2 min, followed by a gradient of 58 °C/min to 210 °C, followed by a gradient of 10 °C/min to 240 °C. When the retention-durations of derivatized−reference sugars were compared to those obtained from samples, D-glucose (Rt 28.64 min), and L-rhamnose (Rt 21.96 min) were found in compound 1, while L-rhamnose (Rt 21.96 min) was found in compound 2.

Effect on human tumor cell viability

Human lung carcinoma A549, and human hepatoma HepG2 were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA). Both cell lines were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 100 IU/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and 10% FBS. Cells were continuously grown in culture flasks (75 cm2 tissue) in a humidified CO2 incubator at 37 °C and sub-cultured every 3–4 d.

Human tumor cell lines viability was determined using MTT assay that depends on the reducing ability of the viable cells to convert the yellow color solution of MTT salt to blue formazan crystals as described previously48. Cells were harvested using trypsin, counted using the trypan blue exclusion method, distributed in a 96-well tissue culture plate in a 30,000 cell per well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified CO2 incubator. The plant extract and its active compounds were dissolved in DMSO and increasing concentrations of each of them were incubated with the human tumor cells in serum free media at 37 °C in a humidified CO2 incubator for 24 h. Doxorubicin (100 µg/mL) was added to the experiment as a positive control that showed more than 75% cytotoxicity in both cell lines. Thereafter, the media were replaced with 100 µL MTT solution (1.2 mM MTT in PBS) and incubated for 2 h before replacing MTT solution with isopropyl alcohol (100 µL) for 1 h to dissolve the formed blue crystals of formazan. The color intensity was measured at 570/690 nm dual wavelength using a BIO-TEK Instruments EL 312e microplate reader (Bio-Tek Instruments, Winooski, VT). The percentage viability was determined relative to that of DMSO-treated cells (control), which was assigned as 100% viability.

Protein–protein interaction network analysis

To explore the potential cytotoxic and pharmacological effects of the isolated spirostan and spirosol saponins from S. muricatum, a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using the STRING database 12.035,39,50,51. This analysis focused on key molecular pathways associated with cancer progression, apoptosis, and cytotoxicity, considering interactions with a confidence score above 0.4. The Cytoscape35–37,51 platform was used for visualization and network interpretation, with the CytoHubba plugin identifying essential hub genes involved in the cytotoxic mechanisms of these bioactive compounds. The findings highlight critical proteins and molecular targets that may contribute to the anticancer potential of S. muricatum saponins, providing valuable insights into their therapeutic applications.

PharmMapper-based target prediction for isolated compounds from S. muricatum

In this study, PharmMapper (http://www.lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) was used to predict potential protein targets for the isolated spirostan and spirosol saponins from S. muricatum52. By submitting the 3D chemical structures of these bioactive compounds, the algorithm screened them against a comprehensive pharmacophore database, ranking potential targets based on structural alignment and binding affinity. This computational approach enabled the identification of high-confidence targets relevant to cytotoxicity, apoptosis, and cancer-related pathways. The ability to leverage 3D molecular data enhanced the accuracy of predictions, providing valuable insights into the mechanisms of action and possible therapeutic applications of S. muricatum saponins in anticancer research.

Gene ontology and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) pathway enrichment analysis

To further elucidate the biological functions and molecular mechanisms underlying the cytotoxic and anticancer effects of the spirostan and spirosol saponins isolated from S. muricatum, we conducted gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses53. These analyses provided functional insights into three major categories: Biological Processes (BP), including apoptosis regulation and oxidative stress response; Cellular Components (CC), identifying subcellular locations such as mitochondrial and cytoplasmic regions; and Molecular Functions (MF), focusing on key enzymatic activities and receptor interactions relevant to cancer pathways. The STRING database (version 12.0) was employed for enrichment analysis, ensuring high-confidence interactions and pathway associations. A stringent false discovery rate (FDR) cutoff of < 0.05 was applied to maintain statistical significance. Results were visualized using built-in enrichment analysis tools, allowing for comprehensive interpretation of the molecular networks involved in cancer progression and cytotoxicity. This approach facilitated the identification of potential therapeutic targets, advancing our understanding of the mechanistic pathways through which S. muricatum saponins exert their biological effects.

Molecular-docking studies

Molecular docking was performed using Discovery Studio 16.1.0.15 (BIOVIA, Dassault Systèmes). The 3D crystal structure of PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: (PDB ID: 8EXU)54 was retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Protein preparation was conducted using the Prepare Protein protocol, which included removal of water molecules, addition of missing hydrogens, and energy minimization using the CHARMm force field55. Ligand structures (compounds 1–3) were drawn using ChemDraw and converted into 3D SDF format, followed by energy minimization using Prepare Ligand protocol. Molecular docking was carried out using the CDOCKER algorithm, a grid-based molecular dynamics simulated-annealing docking method. The binding site was defined based on the co-crystallized ligand in the PDB structure. The best-scoring docking poses were selected based on CDOCKER interaction energy and visualized using Discovery Studio Visualizer to analyze binding modes and interactions56,57.

Molecular-dynamics simulation

To validate the molecular docking results and assess the stability of the protein-ligand complexes, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were conducted using GROMACS 2023.558. The selected protein target, PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: 8EXU), was prepared using UCSF Chimera59, with hydrogen atoms added for optimization. The CHARMM36 force field was applied for the protein, while CGenFF was used for the ligands60. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water box with a minimum extension of 1 nm in all directions, and sodium chloride ions were added to neutralize the system, maintaining a concentration of 150 mM. Energy minimization was performed using the steepest descent method, followed by NVT equilibration at 300 K and NPT equilibration at 1.0 bar to stabilize the system. A 150-ns production run was then carried out to capture system trajectories, allowing for further analysis such as root mean square deviation (RMSD) and binding energy evaluations. This molecular dynamic simulation framework provided robust validation of the stability and interaction potential of spirostan and spirosol saponins from S. muricatum with PI3 K-alpha, a key oncogenic target involved in cancer signaling pathways.

Statistical analysis and median inhibitory concentration (IC50) determination

The results of the human tumor cell viability curves were expressed as mean ± S.E.M., n = 4. These curves were statistically analyzed via one way ANOVA followed by Student–Newman-Keuls post hoc test using SigmaStat 3.5 program for Windows, Systat Software Inc. (San Jose, CA). The median inhibitory concentration (IC50) was determined using the best fitted line of a semilog figure of each viability curve, thereafter, we determined the concentration that causes 50% loss of viability. The computational data were tabulated using the statistical programmed GraphPad Prism version-9 (GraphPad, La-Jolla, CA, USA). All computational experiments were performed at least three times. *p < 0.05, Student’s t-test.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical investigation of S. muricatum

The sugar moieties in the saponins 1 and 2 were identified by GC-MS after acid hydrolysis followed by derivatization with L-cystein methyl ester, subsequent silylation, and comparison with derivatized reference sugars.

Based on the physicochemical, chromatographic-properties, the-spectral-analysis-from UV1, H, and-DEPTQ-NMR, as well as comparisons with the literature, the crude extract of S. muricatum aerial parts afforded the new saponins 1 and 2, along with the known alkaloid spirosol saponin 16β-H-22α-N-spirosol-3β-ol-5-ene-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(12)-[α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(14)]-β-D-glucopyranoside (3) (C45H74NO15, 868.5058, ten degrees of unsaturation), which had been previously isolated from S. surattense61 (Figs. 2 and 3).

Structures of compounds isolated from S. muricatum.

Selected HMBC () correlations of compounds 1–3.

The molecular formula of compound 1 was determined as C39H65O15 by HR-ESI-MS, which gave an adduct pseudo-molecular ion peak at m/z 773.4310 [M + H]+ (calcd 773.4323, eight degrees of unsaturation), suggesting a possible spirostane derivative core scaffold62, which is a characteristic class in Solanum genus63. The 1H and DEPT-Q 13C NMR data (Table 1, Figures S1–S4), along with the heteronuclear single quantum correlation experiment (HSQC) data of compound 1 (Table 1), suggested the presence of three tertiary methyl groups at δH 0.79, 0.89, and 1.06 assignable to the C-18, C-19, and C-27 methyl groups, one secondary methyl group at δH 1.14 (3 H, d, J = 6.7 Hz) ascribable to the C-21 methyl group, and two typical proton signals of H-26 [δH 3.35 (1 H, d, J = 10.6), 4.04 (1 H, dd, J = 2.4, 10.6)] (Table 1). In the 13C NMR spectrum of 1 (Table 1), the signals of C-3, C-5, C-6, C-15, C-16, C-22, C-25, and C-26 (δC 77.7, 56.3, 72.8, 78.5, 95.9, 113.9, 82.2, and 64.2) allowed the identification of 3β, 6α, 15β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane as the aglycon of 1, and the heteronuclear multiple-bond correlation (HMBC) experiment of compound 1 (Fig. 3) confirmed that. The anomeric region in the1 H NMR and 13C NMR spectra of 1 showed signals for two anomeric protons at δH 4.83 (1 H, d, J = 7.9 Hz, β-configuration) and 5.11 (1 H, d, J = 1.1 Hz, α-configuration), with their anomeric carbons at δC 102.7 and 102.1, according to the HSQC spectra, respectively (Table 1). The sugar fragments obtained from the acid hydrolysis of 1 were identified as D-glucose and L-rhamnose. In the HMBC spectrum (Fig. 3), a cross-peak due to long range correlations between the C-2` proton of glucose at δH 4.04 m and C-1`` of δC 102.1 indicated that rhamnose was the terminal saccharide unit linked to C-2` of the inner glucose. The position of the sugar residue in 1 was defined unambiguously to be at C-3 due to the long-range correlation observed between C-3 (δC 77.7) of the aglycon and H-1` (δH 4.83) of the inner glucose unit. The NMR data comparison between 1 and the known62 compound 5α-spirostan-3β,6α-diol-3-O-[β-D-glucopyranosyl-(16)-O-β-D-glucopyranoside] established the structure. Therefore, compound 1 was assigned as 3β, 6α, 15β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane-3-O-β-D-glucopyranosyl-(12)-O-α-L-rhamnopyranoside.

The molecular formula of compound 2 was determined as C39H65O15 by HR-ESI-MS, which gave an adduct pseudo-molecular ion peak at m/z C33H55O10 [M + H]+ (calcd 611.3795, seven degrees of unsaturation) suggested also a possible spirostane derivative core scaffold62. The 1H and DEPT-Q 13C NMR data (Table 1, Figures S5–S6), along with HSQC data of 2 (Table 1), suggested the presence of four tertiary methyl groups at δH 0.80, 0.72, 1.15, and 1.05 assignable to the C-18, C-19, C-21, and C-27 methyl groups, and two typical proton signals of H-26 [δH 3.35 (1 H, m), 4.03 (1 H, m)] (Table 1). In the 13C NMR spectrum of 2 (Table 1), the signals of C-3, C-5, C-6, C-15, C-16, C-20, C-22, C-25 and C-26 (δC 77.2, 52.0, 72.8, 30.0, 77.2, 71.6, 112.0, 82.8, and 64.2) allowed the identification of 3β, 6α, 20β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane as the aglycon of 2, and the HMBC experiment of 2 (Fig. 3) confirmed that. The anomeric region in the1 H NMR and 13C NMR spectra of 2 showed signals for one anomeric proton at δH 5.11 (1 H, d, J = 1.1 Hz, α-configuration), with its anomeric carbons at δC 104.3, according to the HSQC spectra, respectively (Table 1). The sugar obtained from acid hydrolysis of 2 was identified as L-rhamnose. In the HMBC spectrum (Fig. 3), the position of the sugar residue in 2 was defined unambiguously to be at C-3 due to the long-range correlation observed between C-3 (δC 77.2) of the aglycon and H-1` (δH 5.11) of the rhamnose unit. The NMR comparison between 2 and the also known62 compound 5α-spirostan-3β,6α-diol-3-O-[β-D-glucopyranosyl-(16)-O-β-D-glucopyranoside] established the structure. Therefore, compound 2 was assigned as 3β, 6α, 20β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranoside.

Effect of the plant extract and its derived compounds on human tumor cell viability

In an attempt to elucidate the effect on human tumor cell viability and the possible antitumor effects, we incubated the plant extract and its derived compounds with two human tumor cell lines, namely, human lung carcinoma A549, and human hepatoma HepG2 cells. As indicated in Fig. 3, the plant extract and compound 3 exhibited significantly high cytotoxic effects on both human tumor cells. The plant extract showed IC50 values of 8.5 µg/mL and 14.9 µg/mL for A549 and HepG2 cell, respectively. Similarly, compound 3 possessed IC50 values of less than 12.5 µM and 12.2 µM for A549 and HepG2 cells, respectively (Fig. 4), whereas compounds 1 and 2 displayed a moderate to weak cytotoxic effects on both cell lines (Fig. 4). These data may suggest antitumor properties of the plant extracts and its derived compound 3.

In agreement with our results, it was reported previously that S. muricatum possesses anticancer properties in vitro and in vivo. These effects were justified due to the presence of polyphenolic compounds, such as quercetin15,25,24. However, according to our literature review, the cytotoxic effects of the isolated spirosol saponin (compound 3) are relatively new.

Molecular docking analysis of S. muricatum compounds against PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: 8EXU)

Molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding interactions of bioactive spirostan and spirosol saponins from S. muricatum with PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: 8EXU) to investigate their anticancer potential. The OPLS-AA (optimized potentials for liquid simulations – all atom) force field was applied during the energy minimization process to ensure conformational stability and improve docking accuracy. Before docking, an extensive protein preparation protocol was executed, including protonation state optimization and geometrical refinement of the receptor-ligand complex. The docking scores (binding energy in kcal/mol) and RMSD refinement values are summarized in Supplementary Table S7, which provides an overview of ligand stability and interaction strength. Among the tested compounds, 16β-H- 22α-N-spirosol-3β-ol-5-ene-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(12)-[α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(14)]-β-D-glucopyranoside (3) exhibited the highest binding affinity (−7.443 kcal/mol, RMSD 1.692) among the bioactive compounds, suggesting a strong interaction with the PI3 K-alpha active site. Notably, its binding energy closely approaches that of the co-crystallized ligand (−7.901 kcal/mol, RMSD 1.028), indicating that compound 3 may act as a potential PI3 K-alpha inhibitor. Other compounds, including 3β, 6α, 20β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranoside (−6.859 kcal/mol, RMSD 1.812) and 3β, 6α, 15β, 25β-tetrahydroxy-5α-spirostane-3-O-β-D-glucopyranosyl-(12)-O-α-L-rhamnopyranoside (−6.688 kcal/mol, RMSD 1.365), also demonstrated moderate binding affinities with PI3 K-alpha. The docking pose analysis of compound 3 against PI3 K-alpha revealed a high binding affinity, positioning it among the most promising anticancer bioactive compounds from S. muricatum. A detailed interaction analysis (Fig. 11) showed that the co-ligand formed multiple hydrogen bonds with key catalytic residues, such as Asp933, Ser854, and Val851, stabilizing its interaction within the active site.

Molecular dynamics simulation analysis of compound 3 binding to PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: 8EXU)

To evaluate the binding stability, structural integrity, and molecular interactions of compound 3 (16β-H-22α-N-spirosol-3β-ol-5-ene-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(12)-[α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(14)]-β-D-glucopyranoside) within the PI3 K-alpha active site, a 110-ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed. The simulation was conducted using GROMACS 2023, applying the CHARMM36 force field for the protein and CGenFF parameters for the ligand to ensure an accurate representation of molecular interactions. The system was solvated in a TIP3P water model and neutralized with 150 mM NaCl to mimic physiological conditions. Following energy minimization using the steepest descent algorithm, the system underwent NVT and NPT equilibration at 300 K and 1 bar, respectively. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of both PI3 K-alpha and compound 3 was monitored throughout the simulation to assess structural stability (Fig. 13). The PI3 K-alpha backbone (red line) exhibited minimal fluctuations (0.3–0.4 nm), indicating stable conformation. Meanwhile, compound 3 (blue line) initially displayed slight deviations, stabilizing after 20 ns at an RMSD of 0.8–1.0 nm, suggesting an adaptive binding process. These findings indicate that compound 3 remains well-accommodated within the ATP-binding pocket, with early fluctuations reflecting conformational adjustments before achieving a stable, favorable binding pose.

Root mean square deviation (RMSD) analysis of PI3 K-alpha (PDB ID: 8EXU) and compound 3 during the 110-ns molecular dynamics simulation. The red line represents the RMSD of the PI3 K-alpha backbone, which remains stable throughout the simulation (~ 0.3–0.4 nm), indicating structural integrity. The blue line represents the RMSD of compound 3, showing an initial fluctuation phase followed by stabilization at ~ 0.8–1.0 nm after 20 ns, suggesting adaptive binding within the active site.

To further assess the structural integrity of PI3 K-alpha in complex with compound 3, the radius of gyration (Rg) was analyzed (Fig. 14). The Rg values remained stable (~ 3.08–3.12 nm) throughout the simulation, confirming that no significant expansion or collapse occurred in the protein-ligand complex. This stability indicates that compound 3 does not induce major conformational changes in PI3 K-alpha, which is crucial for maintaining protein functionality and effective inhibition of the kinase domain.

Radius of gyration (Rg) analysis of the PI3 K-alpha–compound 3 complex over the simulation time. The Rg values (~ 3.08–3.12 nm) remained stable, confirming that the protein-ligand complex maintained its compact structure without significant expansion or collapse.

Hydrogen bonding plays a pivotal role in the specificity and stability of ligand-protein interactions. The hydrogen bond analysis (Fig. 15) revealed that compound 3 maintains two to four hydrogen bonds on average, with peaks reaching six hydrogen bonds. Key residues involved in these interactions include Asp805, Glu768, and Ser919, which contribute to ligand stabilization in the ATP-binding pocket of PI3 K-alpha. These sustained interactions highlight the strong affinity and potential inhibitory activity of compound 3, reinforcing its stability and selectivity within the kinase domain.

To further validate the stability and favorability of the protein-ligand complex, the potential energy of the system was analyzed (Fig. 16). The energy profile remained stable around − 1,515,000 kJ/mol, with no significant deviations. The absence of sharp energy fluctuations suggests that the binding of compound 3 does not disrupt the structural or energetic stability of PI3 K-alpha, further supporting its compatibility and potential as a kinase inhibitor.

The molecular dynamics simulation confirms (16β-H-22α-N-spirosol-3β-ol-5-ene-3-O-α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(12)-[α-L-rhamnopyranosyl-(14)]-β-D-glucopyranoside) as a stable and strong PI3 K-alpha inhibitor, with minimal RMSD fluctuations, consistent Rg values, persistent hydrogen bonding, and stable potential energy. These findings suggest its thermodynamic favorability and potential to disrupt oncogenic signaling, positioning it as a promising anticancer candidate for further experimental validation.

Conclusion

In this study, two new spirostan saponins (compounds 1 and 2) and a known spirosol saponin (compound 3) were successfully isolated and characterized from the aerial parts of Solanum muricatum Aiton. Notably, the plant extract and compound 3 demonstrated significant cytotoxic activity against human lung carcinoma (A549) and human hepatoma (HepG2) cell lines. Through a comprehensive computational approach, including protein-protein interaction network analysis, molecular docking, and molecular dynamics simulations, this research elucidated the potential anticancer mechanism of these compounds, particularly focusing on the inhibition of PI3 K-alpha. Compound 3 exhibited the highest binding affinity to PI3 K-alpha and demonstrated stable interactions within the ATP-binding pocket, suggesting its potential as a promising PI3 K-alpha inhibitor. These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of S. muricatum derived saponins, especially compound 3, as a source of novel anticancer agents. Future research will validate the cytotoxic activity of compound 3 in vivo using appropriate animal models to assess its efficacy and safety in a complex biological system. This will provide crucial information on its bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, and potential toxicity. Further investigations will be also needed to fully elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which compound 3 exerts its anticancer effects. This includes exploring its impact on other key signaling pathways, its ability to induce apoptosis, and its effects on cell cycle regulation.
