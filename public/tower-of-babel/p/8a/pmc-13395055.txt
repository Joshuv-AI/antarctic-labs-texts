Tissue Factor‐High Ovarian Clear Cell Carcinoma Shows Immune‐Cold Features and Preclinical Activity of Tisotumab Vedotin
Authors: Yutaro Mori, Ryo Tamura, Kotaro Takahashi, Kaoru Yamawaki, Tatsuya Ishiguro, Teiichi Motoyama, Koji Okamoto, Kosuke Yoshihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTissue factor (TF; encoded by F3) is frequently overexpressed in ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC), but the biological features of TF‐high tumors and their therapeutic implications remain poorly defined. We analyzed F3‐high and F3‐low OCCC using bulk RNA sequencing (n = 112) and single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (n = 6; three F3‐high and three F3‐low cases). Bulk transcriptomic analysis showed enrichment of MYC and cell‐cycle pathways in F3‐high tumors, whereas immune and inflammatory pathways were enriched in F3‐low tumors. Single‐nucleus RNA sequencing showed that F3‐high tumors contained fewer immune cells and that malignant epithelial cells displayed attenuated interferon‐related programs. Transcriptomic analysis identified VTCN1, which encodes B7‐H4, as upregulated in F3‐high tumors and positively associated with F3 expression. Based on these findings, we evaluated TF and B7‐H4 expression by immunohistochemistry in a subset of tumors (n = 56), confirming their co‐expression at the protein level. In patient‐derived models, higher TF expression was associated with greater sensitivity to tisotumab vedotin (TV) in vitro, and TV treatment significantly suppressed xenograft growth in vivo. These findings support TF‐high OCCC as an immune‐cold subgroup characterized by proliferative transcriptional programs, frequent B7‐H4 co‐expression, and preclinical sensitivity to TV.

Introduction

Epithelial ovarian cancer (EOC) is a leading gynecologic malignancy [1]. Among its histologic subtypes, ovarian clear cell carcinoma (OCCC) is notable for primary resistance to platinum‐based, paclitaxel–carboplatin chemotherapy and, at advanced or recurrent stages, carries a poor prognosis [2, 3]. Multi‐omics studies have revealed recurrent genomic alterations in OCCC, including ARID1A and PIK3CA mutations; however, despite active development of therapies that exploit these lesions, no molecularly targeted therapy has yet been approved specifically for OCCC [4].

In parallel, increasing evidence indicates that OCCC is immunogenomically heterogeneous and can be stratified into immune‐enriched (“hot” or T‐cell–inflamed) and immune‐poor (“cold”/non‐immune) tumor microenvironment states by transcriptomic and immunophenotyping approaches [5, 6]. For example, immune‐pathway–based transcriptomic clustering identified an immune subtype comprising approximately 22% of OCCC, whereas the remaining tumors showed a non‐immune profile [5]. In our previous study, we similarly observed that immune‐cold tumors are common in OCCC [7]. Consistent with the predominance of immune‐cold disease, clinical benefit from PD‐1 blockade monotherapy has been observed in only a subset of patients: in the phase 2 PEACOCC trial of pembrolizumab in previously treated advanced clear cell gynecological cancers (85% ovarian), the best objective response rate was 25% and the 12‐week progression‐free survival rate was 42% [8]. Immunotherapy‐based combinations, such as PD‐1 blockade plus anti‐angiogenic therapy, have shown promising activity in early‐phase studies, but remain investigational [9]. Taken together, these data underscore the continued need for effective, disease‐specific treatments for advanced ovarian clear cell carcinoma, highlighting the importance of strategies that target tumor‐intrinsic, clinically actionable pathways in immune‐poor OCCC.

OCCC is also clinically characterized by cancer‐associated hypercoagulability and thrombosis, with higher rates in advanced‐stage disease [10]. Our previous study demonstrated that pretreatment hypercoagulability strongly correlates with clinical outcome in OCCC. Moreover, we showed that OCCC characteristically expresses high levels of tissue factor (TF; encoded by F3), which plays a central role in cancer‐associated hypercoagulability and tumor progression [7]. TF initiates the extrinsic coagulation cascade, and emerging evidence in other tumor contexts suggests that tumor‐cell TF can contribute to immune‐cold phenotypes by limiting effector T‐cell infiltration and function [11]. Therefore, therapeutic strategies targeting TF or TF‐associated hypercoagulability have potential as a disease‐specific approach for OCCC.

Tisotumab vedotin (TV) is a tissue factor‐directed antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) comprising a fully human anti‐TF monoclonal antibody linked to the microtubule‐disrupting agent monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE). Through internalization and intracellular linker cleavage, TV delivers MMAE selectively to TF‐expressing cells, inducing cytotoxicity. TV has demonstrated clinically meaningful activity in multiple solid tumors and is approved for recurrent or metastatic cervical cancer in multiple regions, where it has shown improved survival compared with standard chemotherapy in the phase 3 innovaTV301 trial [12]. Given the high prevalence of TF expression in OCCC and its association with hypercoagulability, TV represents a rational candidate for preclinical evaluation as a TF‐directed therapeutic strategy in this disease.

In this study, we aimed to characterize the transcriptomic and immune features of TF‐high OCCC, examine immune‐regulatory molecules including B7‐H4/VTCN1, and evaluate the preclinical activity of the TF‐directed ADC tisotumab vedotin in patient‐derived OCCC models to inform future biomarker‐enriched therapeutic strategies.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Samples

All procedures were performed using protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Niigata University, Teikyo University. All participants provided informed consent. Surgical specimens of OCCC tissues were excised from patients. Isolated surgical specimens were stored at −80°C, subjected to FFPE analyses, or used for establishment of cancer spheroids.

Bulk RNA‐Seq Analysis

The bulk RNA‐seq cohort analyzed in this study partially overlaps with the cohort reported in Tamura et al. (Gynecol Oncol 2021). Specifically, 93 cases were included in both studies and 19 additional cases were newly included here. A case‐level overlap across datasets (bulk RNA‐seq, snRNA‐seq, IHC, and patient‐derived models) is summarized in Table S1. Throughout this study, “F3‐high/low” indicates transcript‐based stratification defined in the bulk RNA‐seq cohort (and the snRNA‐seq cases selected by that stratification), whereas “TF‐high/low” refers to protein‐level tissue factor expression assessed by immunohistochemistry or tissue factor abundance in experimental models unless otherwise specified. For the primary discovery analysis, F3‐high and F3‐low tumors were defined as the upper and lower quartiles of F3 transcript abundance, respectively, to maximize transcriptomic contrast. This quartile‐based stratification was exploratory and was not intended to define a clinically validated cutoff. To assess the robustness of the findings, F3 expression was also evaluated using median‐based stratification and continuous‐variable analyses. Total RNA extraction and library preparation were performed as previously reported [7]. Briefly, total RNA was extracted from frozen samples of OCCC using TRIzol (#15596026, Invitrogen), and then subjected to cDNA library preparation by using a TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Prep Kit (RS‐20020595, Illumina), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, cDNA libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with the 2 × 100‐bp paired‐end read module.

Single‐Nucleus RNA‐Seq (snRNA‐Seq)

Nuclei isolation from frozen OCCC tissues and subsequent snRNA‐seq analysis were performed as previously described [13]. Briefly, 4000–8000 nuclei were processed on a Chromium Controller (10× Genomics) using the Single Cell 3′ Reagent Kit v3 (PN‐1000075). Sequencing was carried out on a HiSeq 2500 (Illumina), and reads were processed with Cell Ranger v3.0.2 (10× Genomics) against the GRCh38 (premRNA) reference to generate UMI count matrices.

Establishment of Tumor‐Derived Spheroids

Patient‐derived OCCC spheroids were established from surgical specimens using a previously described protocol for stable spheroid culture from gynecologic malignancies [14, 15]. Briefly, freshly resected tumor tissues were enzymatically dissociated, purified by density gradient centrifugation, and cultured on ultra‐low‐attachment dishes in serum‐free medium supplemented with basic fibroblast growth factor. Formed spheroids were serially passaged every two weeks using Accumax.

Lentivirus‐Mediated RNA Transduction

Lentiviral particles expressing human F3 under the EF1A promoter (LV[Exp]‐EGFP:T2A:Puro, VB240808‐1484jnp) and control particles expressing mCherry under the EF1A promoter (pLV[Exp]‐EGFP/Puro‐EF1A>mCherry, VB010000‐9298rtf) were custom‐produced by VectorBuilder (Guangzhou, China). Virus‐containing supernatants were directly provided by the manufacturer and used for infection according to the supplier's instructions. Cells were selected in the presence of 2 μg/mL puromycin after infection. F3‐overexpressing and control OCCC spheroids were subjected to exploratory transcriptomic profiling. Because this analysis was limited to two OCCC spheroids, individual gene‐level changes were interpreted descriptively, and pathway‐level trends were evaluated using Hallmark GSEA.

Quantitative Real‐Time Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR) Analyses

RNA was isolated from cells with an miRNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA), and cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using a PrimeScript First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). Gene expression was quantified by qRT‐PCR using gene‐specific primers (Eurofins Genomics) for F3 (forward 5′‐CTACTGTTTCAGTGTTCAAGCAGTGA‐3′; reverse 5′‐CAGTGCAATATAGCATTTGCAGTAGC‐3′) and ACTB (forward 5′‐TGGCACCCAGCACAATGAA‐3′; reverse 5′‐CTAAGTCATAGTCCGCCTAGAAGCA‐3′). Relative expression levels were calculated using the ΔCt method with ACTB as the internal control.

In Vitro Drug Sensitivity Assay

For the drug sensitivity assay, OCCC spheroid cells were treated with Tisotumab Vedotin (TV; kindly provided by Genmab A/S). Cell growth was evaluated using CellTiter‐Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (G7570, Promega).

Western Blotting Assay

Western blotting was performed as previously described [13]. Briefly, proteins extracted from F3‐overexpressing and control cells were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and probed with antibodies against Tissue Factor (TF; cat. no. REF4509; BioMedica) and β‐Actin (ACTB; cat. no. A5316, Sigma‐Aldrich).

Animal Experiments

To examine the effects of TV on xenograft tumors, OCCC spheroid cells were dissociated and suspended in 100 μL of medium containing 50% Matrigel and injected subcutaneously into the flanks of NOG (NOD/Shi‐scid IL‐2Rγnull) mice (CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan). When tumors reached approximately 100–500 mm3, mice were randomized to vehicle or TV at 10 mg/kg administered intraperitoneally once weekly. Tumor volumes were measured weekly (length × width × height × π/6). At endpoint, tumors were harvested for IHC of TF and B7‐H4. All animal procedures complied with institutional guidelines.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) Analyses

Primary or mouse xenograft tumors were fixed in neutral formalin, embedded in paraffin, and used for staining with hematoxylin and eosin. For immunostaining, the sections were stained following standard IHC methods, as previously described [13], with primary anti‐Tissue Factor (cat. no. REF4509; BioMedica), anti‐B7‐H4 (cat. no. ab209242; abcam). For each case, three randomly selected tumor areas were independently evaluated by two observers. All IHC assessments of TF and B7‐H4 in clinical OCCC specimens, including the positivity classifications used in Figure 3C–E and Table S8, were subsequently re‐evaluated and confirmed by an expert pathologist specializing in gynecologic pathology. The proportion of tumor cells exhibiting specific immunoreactivity was recorded as a tumor‐cell positivity proportion. For the primary TF/B7‐H4 co‐expression analysis, IHC positivity was defined exploratorily as staining in ≥ 50% of tumor cells, whereas tumors with < 50% positive tumor cells were defined as IHC‐negative. This tumor proportion–based cutoff was selected to identify tumors with broad protein expression and to evaluate TF/B7‐H4 co‐expression using a consistent framework, rather than to define a clinically validated predictive cutoff. For TF, a low positivity threshold of ≥ 1% tumor‐cell staining was also examined as a sensitivity analysis, because similar low thresholds have been used in clinical studies of tisotumab vedotin [12]. A ≥ 1% tumor‐cell staining threshold for B7‐H4 was also summarized as a low‐threshold prevalence analysis. Discrepancies between observers were resolved by joint review.

Statistical Analysis and Computational Methods

Statistical analyses were performed with R software. Continuous variables between two groups were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test unless otherwise specified. Associations between categorical variables, including TF and B7‐H4 IHC positivity, were evaluated using Fisher's exact test for 2 × 2 contingency tables, and two‐sided p‐values are reported. Other categorical comparisons were evaluated using the χ2 test, as appropriate. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient unless otherwise specified. Parameters such as number of replicates, the number of independent experiments, measures of center, dispersion, and precision (mean ± SD or SEM), statistical test and significance, are reported in Figures and Figure Legends.

For survival analyses, progression‐free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval between treatment initiation and disease progression, and overall survival (OS) as the interval between treatment initiation and death. Patients without events at last follow‐up were censored. Survival curves were generated by the Kaplan–Meier method and compared using the log‐rank test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were conducted using Cox proportional hazards regression models; multivariate analyses included parsimonious adjustment for stage to avoid overfitting.

Single‐nucleus RNA‐seq data were processed using Seurat (v4.3.2) and related R packages. Low‐quality cells were filtered based on mitochondrial gene percentage and feature counts, followed by normalization, dimensionality reduction (PCA and UMAP), and clustering.

For cell type annotation, low‐resolution clustering was performed using the FindClusters function with resolution 0.1 to generate 5 clusters. Differential expression was performed using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat with default parameters. One of these clusters highly expressed ovarian epithelial‐associated genes (PAX8, EPCAM). Other clusters highly expressed markers specific for cancer‐associated fibroblasts (COL1A1, COL3A1), endothelial cells (VWF, PECAM1), and macrophages (CD163, CSF1R) and other immune cells (PTPRC) as shown in Fig. S3B.

Bulk RNA‐seq data were processed as previously described [7]. Briefly, transcript abundance was quantified using kallisto (v0.43.0) and expressed as TPM. TCGA pan‐cancer TPM values processed with the same pipeline were obtained from the Google Cloud Pilot RNA‐Sequencing for CCLE and TCGA dataset (osf.io/gqrz9). TPM values were log2(TPM + 1)‐transformed for downstream analyses. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using ranked gene lists based on differential expression between the F3‐high and F3‐low groups. Gene sets were derived from the Hallmark collection of the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB). To evaluate cutoff robustness, additional GSEA was performed using median‐based F3 stratification. Tumors were divided into F3‐median‐high and F3‐median‐low groups, and preranked GSEA was performed using the same Hallmark gene sets and ranking strategy as the primary quartile‐based analysis. To further assess cutoff‐independent associations, single‐sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) scores were calculated for each tumor using Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB. Associations between F3 expression and ssGSEA scores were evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation analysis. p‐values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. Enrichment results were summarized using the normalized enrichment score (NES) and false discovery rate (FDR). Immune‐associated genes were defined using the ImmPort immunome gene list, which provides HGNC gene symbols annotated to the Reactome “Immune System” event and the Gene Ontology “immune system process” term. We intersected this immune gene universe with the preranked differential‐expression list (F3‐high vs. F3‐low) used for Hallmark GSEA. For visualization in Figure 3A, we curated a representative 39‐gene immune marker panel spanning five functional categories (Immune Checkpoints, Antigen Presentation, T‐cell lineage/CD8 markers, IFN‐gamma/Cytotoxic, and Myeloid/Innate). The 39‐gene panel and category assignments are provided in Table S2, and the full immune‐gene ranked list is provided as Table S3. For immune marker correlation analyses, Spearman's rank correlations were calculated between F3 expression and the selected immune marker genes in the bulk RNA‐seq cohort. p‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg method. For snRNA‐seq, pathway activity at the sample level was quantified using gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using Hallmark gene sets from MSigDB. GSVA scores of cancer cells were calculated and compared between the F3‐high and F3‐low groups.

Bulk RNA‐seq data from 56 OCCC tumors (F3‐high: n = 28, F3‐low: n = 28) were deconvolved into five compartments—cancer cells (Cancer), cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor endothelial cells (TECs), tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), and immune cells (IMCs)—using a CIBERSORT‐like linear support vector regression (SVR) approach. As a reference signature matrix, we used snRNA‐seq–derived average expression profiles of each compartment. For each sample, the log2TPM vector was modeled as a linear combination of the five signature profiles using ε‐insensitive linear SVR (C = 1.0), analogous to the original CIBERSORT framework. Negative coefficients were set to zero and rescaled to sum to 1, yielding relative cell fractions per sample.

Results

F3 ‐High OCCC Is Associated With Attenuated Immune Signatures in Bulk Transcriptomes

To investigate the molecular and biological significance of F3 in OCCC, we stratified tumors based on F3 transcript abundance into three groups according to bulk RNA‐seq data: F3‐high (top quartile, n = 28; median TPM = 7.212), F3‐intermediate (middle two quartiles, n = 56; median TPM = 5.258), and F3‐low (bottom quartile, n = 28; median TPM = 2.965) (Figure S1A). No significant differences were observed among the three groups in terms of clinical stage or overall prognosis (Figure S1C). To maximize transcriptomic contrast, downstream differential analyses were primarily performed between the F3‐high and F3‐low groups. GSEA comparing F3‐high and F3‐low tumors revealed positive enrichment of MYC_TARGETS_V1 and E2F_TARGETS in F3‐high tumors (NES = 2.18 and 2.06), whereas immune‐related pathways, including INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE and INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, showed negative enrichment scores (NES = −3.06 and −2.95), indicating preferential enrichment in F3‐low tumors (Figure 1B and Table S4). These results suggest that immune‐related signaling is relatively suppressed in F3‐high OCCC. To determine whether these findings depended on the original quartile‐based stratification, we performed additional robustness analyses using median‐based F3 stratification. Median‐based GSEA reproduced enrichment of interferon‐related and inflammatory pathways in F3‐median‐low tumors (Table S5). We next analyzed F3 expression as a continuous variable using Hallmark ssGSEA scores. F3 expression was inversely associated with interferon‐related and inflammatory Hallmark signatures, supporting the robustness of the immune‐related phenotype beyond binary stratification (Table S6). To further explore whether F3 expression could influence tumor‐cell intrinsic immune‐related programs, we examined F3‐overexpressing and control OCCC spheroids. F3 overexpression increased TF protein expression (Figure S2A,B) and, at the pathway level, showed directional attenuation of selected interferon‐related and inflammatory programs (Figure S2C).

Reduced Immune‐Cell Fractions in F3 ‐High Tumors Revealed by snRNA‐Seq

Next, we performed single‐nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA‐seq) on six OCCC tumors stratified by F3 expression levels determined from bulk RNA‐seq (F3‐high, n = 3; F3‐low, n = 3). After standard quality‐control filtering and batch integration, we obtained 52,636 high‐quality nuclei for downstream analysis. Unsupervised clustering followed by annotation using canonical marker genes identified major tumor‐associated cell populations, including malignant epithelial cells, cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor endothelial cells (TECs), tumor‐associated macrophages (TAMs), and immune cells (IMCs) (Figures 2A, S3A,B).

(E) Hallmark gene set variation analysis (GSVA) comparing F3‐high and F3‐low groups across malignant epithelial cells in snRNA‐seq data.

When comparing F3 expression between F3‐high and F3‐low groups across each cell type, all populations exhibited higher F3 expression in the F3‐high group, with particularly striking differences in cancer cells (Figures 2B, S3C). Sample‐level cell‐fraction analysis further demonstrated that F3‐high tumors contained a significantly lower proportion of immune cells than F3‐low tumors (Figure 2C). Furthermore, CIBERSORT‐like linear SVR deconvolution using the OCCC snRNA‐seq–derived signature matrix revealed that F3‐high tumors had a significantly higher cancer cell fraction than F3‐low tumors, whereas F3‐high tumors displayed reduced fractions of IMCs and TAMs (Figure 2D). Consistent with the bulk RNA‐seq findings, gene set variation analysis (GSVA) comparing F3‐high and F3‐low malignant cancer cells revealed lower GSVA scores for immune‐related pathways, including INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE and INTERFERON_ALPHA_RESPONSE, in F3‐high cancer cells (Figure 2E). Together, these data support that F3‐high OCCC is associated with an immunologically “cold” tumor phenotype, characterized by reduced immune‐cell fractions and attenuated immune‐related transcriptional programs within cancer cells.

VTCN1 /B7‐H4 Is Co‐Expressed With F3/ TF in an Immune‐Poor Tumor Context

To characterize the immune‐related transcriptional context of F3‐high OCCC tumors, we examined the expression profiles of immune‐related genes in both bulk RNA‐seq and snRNA‐seq datasets. In bulk transcriptomes, we extracted immune‐associated genes from the ranked gene list used for Hallmark GSEA (F3‐high vs. F3‐low) and organized them into five functional categories: immune checkpoints, antigen presentation, T‐cell lineage/CD8 markers, IFN‐gamma/cytotoxic genes, and myeloid/innate immunity markers (Figure 3A). Among these, VTCN1 was selectively upregulated in F3‐high tumors and positively correlated with F3 expression across the cohort (Spearman's ρ = 0.254, p = 0.0069, n = 112; Figures 3A and S4). In contrast, most antigen‐presentation genes, IFN‐gamma/cytotoxic genes, and T‐cell lineage/CD8 markers were shifted toward the F3‐low side (Figure 3A). Continuous‐variable correlation analyses further supported this pattern: F3 expression was inversely associated with multiple T‐cell lineage/CD8 and IFN‐gamma/cytotoxic markers (Table S7). We next examined whether VTCN1 expression was also enriched in cancer cells. Consistently, snRNA‐seq revealed markedly higher VTCN1 expression in F3‐high cancer cells than in their F3‐low counterparts, further reinforcing this association at single‐cell resolution (Figure 3B). To assess transcript–protein concordance for TF, we compared F3 transcript abundance according to TF IHC status in tumors with matched bulk RNA‐seq and IHC data. F3 expression was significantly higher in TF IHC‐positive tumors than in TF IHC‐negative tumors using the ≥ 50% tumor‐cell positivity cutoff (Figure 3C). We then evaluated TF and B7‐H4 protein expression by IHC in serial tumor sections. Representative TF/B7‐H4 double‐positive and double‐negative cases are shown in Figure 3D. Using the exploratory ≥ 50% tumor‐cell positivity cutoff, TF positivity was significantly associated with B7‐H4 positivity (two‐sided Fisher's exact test, p = 8.5 × 10−5; Figure 3E), supporting their co‐expression at the protein level. A sample‐level summary of TF and B7‐H4 IHC positivity using alternative tumor‐cell positivity thresholds is provided in Table S8. Collectively, these findings support B7‐H4 as a co‐expressed immune‐regulatory surface molecule within the TF/F3‐high immune‐poor context, rather than establishing B7‐H4 as a functional mediator of immune suppression.

Tisotumab Vedotin Shows Preclinical Activity in TF‐Expressing OCCC Models

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of tisotumab vedotin (TV) against OCCC, we first conducted drug‐sensitivity assays using patient‐derived OCCC spheroids (COC‐1 to COC‐6) and spheroids derived from high‐grade serous ovarian carcinoma (SOC‐1 to SOC‐3). F3 expression levels were significantly inversely correlated with TV IC50 values, indicating that models with higher F3 expression were more sensitive to TV (Figure 4A). Consistently, lentiviral overexpression of F3 enhanced TV sensitivity compared with vector controls (Figure 4B).

We next assessed TV efficacy in vivo using subcutaneous xenograft models established by implanting F3‐high OCCC spheroids into immunocompromised NOG mice. Mice were randomized to receive vehicle or TV (dose and schedule as described in Methods). TV treatment markedly suppressed tumor growth relative to vehicle controls (Figure 4C,D). IHC analysis of residual tumors showed reduced TF staining after TV treatment, with accompanying reduction in B7‐H4 staining in the same tumor specimens (Figures 4E and S5). Collectively, these findings show that TV exerts preclinical antitumor activity against TF‐expressing OCCC models.

Discussion

This study addresses an important gap in the OCCC literature: although our prior work established that OCCC frequently overexpresses F3 and that “immune‐cold” tumors are common [7], the biological and therapeutic context of F3‐high OCCC had not been fully characterized. We tackled this question by integrating bulk RNA‐seq and snRNA‐seq transcriptomics, complemented by IHC, and by functionally testing TV in patient‐derived spheroids and xenografts generated from clinical specimens. Across these orthogonal platforms, we consistently observed that F3‐high tumors are associated with attenuation of immune‐related pathways and reduced immune‐cell fractions. Taken together, these convergent data provide evidence in OCCC that F3‐high tumors tend to be immunologically “cold,” consistent with observations in several other cancer types [11, 16, 17]. Notably, the immune‐poor phenotype was supported not only by reduced immune‐cell fractions but also by attenuated immune‐related transcriptional programs within malignant epithelial cells, arguing against tumor purity alone as a sufficient explanation. This immune‐cold context prompted us to further examine immune‐regulatory molecules associated with TF abundance, including B7‐H4.

Mechanistically, the basis for immune suppression in TF‐high tumors is likely multifactorial. TF signaling through PAR2 has been shown in other cancers to activate pro‐angiogenic and immunomodulatory programs, including upregulation of VEGF, which may indirectly promote an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [18, 19]. In addition, our transcriptomic analyses demonstrated enrichment of MYC‐ and cell cycle–associated pathways in F3‐high tumors, together with attenuation of interferon‐related immune signatures. This proliferative MYC/cell‐cycle–high pattern is concordant with transcriptional features previously associated with immune‐cold OCCC in our prior molecular framework [7]. As a supportive analysis, F3 overexpression in OCCC spheroids increased TF protein expression and showed directional attenuation of selected interferon‐related and inflammatory pathways at the pathway level. Collectively, these observations raise the possibility that TF‐linked signaling and proliferative tumor‐intrinsic programs may contribute to attenuation of interferon‐related programs in TF‐high OCCC. Future studies integrating TF perturbation, assessment of downstream mediators (e.g., pERK, IL‐8, VEGF), and spatially resolved immune profiling will be important to clarify directionality and determine whether these pathways contribute to immune exclusion in vivo.

A second major finding of this study is the consistent co‐expression of TF and the immune‐checkpoint molecule B7‐H4 across orthogonal datasets. Across bulk RNA‐seq and snRNA‐seq, VTCN1 was enriched in F3‐high tumors, including within malignant epithelial cells at the transcript level. In the immune marker analysis, VTCN1 was shifted toward the F3‐high side, whereas antigen‐presentation genes, IFN‐gamma/cytotoxic genes, and T‐cell lineage/CD8 markers were shifted toward the F3‐low side. This pattern is more consistent with B7‐H4 co‐expression within an immune‐poor tumor context than with a T‐cell‐inflamed, checkpoint‐driven immune‐evasion phenotype. Consistently, IHC demonstrated a significant association between TF protein positivity and B7‐H4 protein positivity. This protein‐level analysis used an exploratory ≥ 50% tumor‐cell positivity cutoff to capture broad TF and B7‐H4 expression; a low ≥ 1% threshold classified most OCCC tumors as TF‐positive and was therefore less informative for broad‐expression stratification (Table S8). In F3‐overexpressing OCCC spheroids, VTCN1 showed only a modest, low‐abundance change; therefore, these perturbation data do not establish a robust direct F3‐to‐VTCN1 regulatory relationship (Figure S2A). Shared transcriptional regulators, cytokine‐mediated inflammatory cues, or epigenetic remodeling in dedifferentiated tumor niches could account for the parallel expression of TF and B7‐H4. B7‐H4 itself has been reported to suppress T‐cell activation and correlate with poor immune infiltration across multiple epithelial malignancies [20, 21]. Notably, similar immune‐excluding associations have been reported in other epithelial malignancies: in breast cancer, tumor‐cell B7‐H4 expression is linked to reduced intratumoral CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and impaired antitumor T‐cell responses, consistent with a role for epithelial B7‐H4 in shaping immune‐cold tumor microenvironments [22, 23]. Importantly, a recent histotype‐stratified immunohistochemistry study profiling ADC targets across ovarian cancer reported frequent B7‐H4 expression in clear cell ovarian carcinoma (80.7% with membrane histoscore ≥ 50), supporting the relevance of B7‐H4 as a commonly expressed surface antigen in this histotype [24]. However, our data do not establish a functional interaction between TF and B7‐H4 or demonstrate B7‐H4‐mediated immune suppression in OCCC. Together, these observations support the interpretation that TF‐high cancer cells frequently co‐express B7‐H4 within an immune‐cold, immune‐poor tumor context. Given that both TF and B7‐H4 are potentially targetable surface antigens, this co‐expression may have translational relevance for future therapeutic strategies. Nevertheless, our findings do not by themselves support immediate dual targeting. Future studies using multiplex IHC, spatial profiling, and functional immune models will be needed to determine whether TF/B7‐H4 co‐expression has spatial, immunological, or therapeutic consequences.

From a therapeutic standpoint, our study supports further evaluation of TF as a therapeutic target in OCCC and provides preclinical evidence of TF‐directed activity of TV. Although quantitative target expression does not always correlate tightly with ADC efficacy, multiple clinical datasets indicate that absence or loss of the target abrogates benefit (e.g., HER2‐negative settings for T‐DM1 and antigen loss for CD33/CD22‐directed ADCs) [25, 26, 27]. These findings underscore the principle that target presence is important, even when quantitative expression is not a perfect standalone predictor of response. Conversely, several trials have demonstrated enrichment of benefit in target‐high disease, supporting the notion that target abundance can modulate ADC efficacy [28, 29, 30]. In cervical cancer—where TV is approved—exploratory biomarker analyses across innovaTV 201, innovaTV 204, and the randomized phase 3 innovaTV 301 trial did not show a clear association between baseline tumor‐cell TF expression and clinical response [12, 31, 32]. This likely reflects, at least in part, near‐ubiquitous TF positivity and limited dynamic range in that setting (≈90%–95%). Moreover, ADC activity depends on multiple factors—binding, internalization, trafficking, linker cleavage, payload sensitivity, and the tumor microenvironment—so target abundance alone rarely serves as a sufficient predictor [33, 34]. In contrast, our in vitro analyses revealed a positive association between TF expression and TV sensitivity in patient‐derived OCCC models, and our in vivo analyses demonstrated antitumor activity of TV in TF‐expressing xenografts. We observed markedly heterogeneous TF expression with a broad dynamic range in OCCC specimens, suggesting that TF‐directed ADCs such as TV may have therapeutic potential in OCCC, particularly in tumors with broad or strong TF expression.

Although direct clinical evidence that TV enhances antitumor immunity is limited, preclinical studies and mechanistic reviews suggest that tissue factor–directed MMAE‐bearing ADCs may induce immunogenic cell death (ICD) and engage Fc‐mediated effector functions such as antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity and phagocytosis, potentially contributing to immune activation [35, 36]. Moreover, multiple recent studies have shown that ADCs can modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD), altering antigen presentation, and promoting cytotoxic T‐cell infiltration in selected contexts, providing further mechanistic rationale for combining ADCs with immune checkpoint inhibitors [37, 38]. These findings provide a biologically plausible framework through which targeted delivery of cytotoxic payloads, potentially complemented by Fc‐mediated functions, could influence tumor–immune microenvironment remodeling and support future investigation of TV‐based combinations.

This study has several limitations. First, xenograft experiments were performed in immunodeficient NOG mice and therefore cannot directly assess T‐cell‐mediated antitumor immunity, immune microenvironment remodeling, or the efficacy of TV–immunotherapy combinations in vivo. Second, xenografts generated from TF‐low OCCC cells failed to establish persistent tumor growth, precluding inclusion of TF‐low models as negative controls for in vivo TV testing and limiting evaluation of the lower bound of TF dependence; however, TF‐associated differences in TV sensitivity were assessed in complementary in vitro and patient‐derived spheroid models. Third, the F3 overexpression transcriptomic analysis was limited to two OCCC spheroids and should be interpreted as hypothesis‐generating. Fourth, although TF/B7‐H4 co‐expression was supported by transcriptomic and IHC analyses, we did not perform functional assays, multiplex IHC, or spatial profiling to establish whether their co‐expression is mechanistically linked or whether B7‐H4 directly contributes to T‐cell suppression in OCCC. In addition, thresholds used for F3/TF stratification and TF/B7‐H4 IHC positivity were exploratory and require external validation and assay harmonization across IHC and RNA modalities, ideally in independent cohorts with pre‐specified cutoffs. Finally, ADC efficacy is influenced by multiple factors beyond antigen expression, and the clinical value of TF expression as a candidate enrichment marker for TV response in OCCC remains to be determined in prospective clinical studies.

In conclusion, TF‐high OCCC is associated with a distinct gene expression pattern characterized by an immune‐cold, immune‐poor phenotype and frequent B7‐H4 co‐expression. A graphical summary of the study design and main findings is provided in Figure 5. TV showed preclinical antitumor activity in TF‐expressing patient‐derived OCCC models, supporting further evaluation of TF‐directed ADC therapy in OCCC. Prospective studies will be required to validate TF‐based enrichment strategies and to clarify the immunological and therapeutic significance of TF/B7‐H4 co‐expression.
