Jahn-Teller distortion-engineered self-propelled nanorobots for mitochondrial targeting and bioenergetic disruption in tumor therapy
Authors: Tianying Luo, Mingyi Zhang, Jia Xu, Dechao Yuan, Beibei Yin, Yuzhou Zhu, Shuang Yan, Meng Pan, Dong Mo, Xicheng Li, Xuyue Liang, Zhaojuan Qin, Hongxin Deng, Zhiyong Qian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial metabolism plays a pivotal role in tumor progression, yet effective therapeutic targeting remains constrained by limited tissue penetration and lack of spatiotemporal control. Herein, we present Jahn-Teller distortion-engineered, self-propelled nanorobots (IDP@Z@AP) that integrate catalytic oxygen generation, mitochondria-targeted drug delivery, and real-time 3D NIR-II photoacoustic (PA) imaging for precision tumor therapy. The nanorobots are fabricated by co-encapsulating a NIR-II photothermal agent (IR1048) and a mitochondria-targeting chemotherapeutic (DOX-TPP) within a ZIF-8 framework, followed by in situ anchoring of ultrasmall AuPt bimetallic nanozymes. Pt-induced Jahn-Teller distortion modulates the electronic structure of AuPt, enhancing glucose oxidase- and catalase-like activities. Under NIR-II laser irradiation, photothermal-enhanced cascade catalysis drives autonomous motion and catalyzes intratumoral O2 generation, facilitating deep tumor infiltration. In vitro studies reveal efficient mitochondrial targeting, resulting in significant mitochondrial membrane depolarization, intracellular ATP depletion, and suppressed cell migration and invasion. In vivo, 3D NIR-II PA imaging enables noninvasive visualization of nanorobot biodistribution and real-time mapping of catalytic oxygen generation within tumor tissues. This nanorobotic platform effectively modulates tumor hypoxia and enhances chemotherapeutic delivery to mitochondria, ultimately achieving potent tumor suppression. The work offers a smart, catalytically driven, mitochondria-targeted strategy with real-time therapeutic feedback for subcellular-level cancer therapy.Keywords: Self-propelled nanorobots, Mitochondria-targeting, Jahn-Teller distortion, Bioenergetic disruption, 3D NIR-II photoacoustic imaging

Introduction

Precise subcellular drug delivery, particularly to organelles such as mitochondria, remains a formidable challenge in cancer therapy [1,2]. As central regulators of cellular metabolism, apoptosis, and metastasis, mitochondria represent attractive therapeutic targets [3,4]. However, conventional chemotherapeutics often suffer from limited tissue penetration and nonspecific intracellular distribution, leading to inadequate drug concentrations at the organelle level, reduced therapeutic efficacy, and systemic toxicity [1,5,6]. The tumor microenvironment (TME) further complicates drug delivery due to its distinct pathological features, including a dense extracellular matrix (ECM), abnormal collagen deposition, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and hypoxia [5,[7], [8], [9]]. These physical and biological barriers impede nanoparticle diffusion, restrict interstitial transport, and activate drug resistance mechanisms [10]. For instance, ECM-induced solid stress limits passive nanoparticle penetration, while hypoxia-driven mitochondrial reprogramming promotes therapeutic resistance and tumor progression [11,12]. Therefore, there is a critical need for delivery systems capable of actively traversing ECM barriers and precisely delivering therapeutics to mitochondria, the subcellular hub of drug resistance.

Autonomous micro/nanorobots offer a promising strategy to overcome these barriers [[13], [14], [15]]. Unlike passive nanocarriers that rely on diffusion or endocytosis, self-propelled nanorobots actively navigate tumors via chemical or/and physical energy [[16], [17], [18]]. Recent advances, such as metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)-based catalytic nanorobots, have demonstrated enhanced mitochondrial targeting via endogenous fuel-driven motion [19]. Nevertheless, most current systems employ single-step catalytic processes and lack design strategies to optimize substrate adsorption or enhance catalytic efficiency, limiting their performance in complex tumor microenvironments [[20], [21], [22], [23]].

Notably, the Jahn-Teller effect, a well-known geometric distortion observed in coordination complexes and transition metal systems with electronically degenerate states, has emerged as an effective strategy to tailor catalytic properties by inducing lattice strain and modulating surface electronic configurations [[24], [25], [26]]. Building on this principle, we developed an autonomous nanorobot platform (IDP@Z@AP) based on sequential cascade catalysis to achieve deep tumor penetration and mitochondria-specific drug delivery (Scheme 1). These nanorobots are fabricated by co-encapsulating a mitochondria-targeting drug (DOX-TPP) and a near-infrared-II (NIR-II) photothermal agent (IR1048) within ZIF-8 MOFs, with ultrasmall AuPt nanoparticles precisely anchored on the surface. In the tumor microenvironment, the Au nanoparticles (AuNPs) exhibit glucose oxidase (GOx)-like activity, catalyzing glucose oxidation to produce gluconic acid and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), while the Pt nanoparticles (PtNPs) mimic catalase (CAT) activity, converting H2O2 into oxygen to generate active propulsion. This cascade reaction enables autonomous propulsion, active penetration, and efficient mitochondrial accumulation of nanorobots.

Schematic illustration of Jahn-Teller distortion-engineered IDP@Z@AP nanorobots for catalytic propulsion, mitochondrial targeting, and NIR-II PA tracking of tumor accumulation and catalytic oxygen generation.

Mechanistically, the AuPt alloy was designed to harness Jahn-Teller distortion as a strategic approach to modulate the electronic structure, elevate the d-band centre, and enhance glucose adsorption to boost catalytic efficiency. The photothermal effect induced by NIR-II laser irradiation is expected to further accelerate catalytic kinetics, thus enhancing propulsion and promoting mitochondrial enrichment. Real-time three-dimensional NIR-II photoacoustic (PA) imaging offers deep-tissue, high-resolution visualization of nanorobot distribution and intratumoral catalytic activity, establishing a synergistic theranostic paradigm that integrates real-time imaging with catalytic mitochondria-targeted therapy.

Together, this mitochondria-targeted nanorobot system provides a robust strategy to overcome ECM-mediated delivery barriers and subcellular therapeutic limitations. By integrating cascade catalysis, intelligent propulsion, and precision organelle targeting, the IDP@Z@AP nanorobots represent a significant step forward in the development of intelligent catalytic nanomedicine for precision cancer therapy.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of IDP@Z@AP bimetallic self-propelled nanorobots

Initially, an in situ self-assembly strategy was employed to construct the IDP@Z by co-incubating DOX-TPP and IR1048 with ZIF-8 precursors (zinc nitrate hexahydrate and 2-methylimidazole) [27]. Subsequently, a sequential ion-exchange and in situ reduction process was performed, during which Au3+ and Pt4+ ions selectively replaced Zn2+ within the ZIF-8 structure and were simultaneously reduced to form ultrasmall AuPt bimetallic nanoparticles, yielding the final IDP@Z@AP nanorobots (Fig. 1A) [27]. Quantitative analysis by inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) revealed that the loading efficiencies of Au and Pt nanoparticles in IDP@Z@AP reached 19.6 % and 9.99 %, respectively (Table S1). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses confirmed that both IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP exhibited uniform spherical morphologies, with average diameters of 157 nm and 155 nm, respectively (Fig. 1B a,b and Fig. S1).

High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) images (Fig. 1B c and Fig. S2) revealed abundant twinned grains with well-defined boundaries at the Au-Pt interfaces. As shown in Fig. 1B c, multiple sets of lattice fringes with distinct crystallographic orientations were observed within individual IDP@Z@AP nanoparticles, indicating pronounced structural heterogeneity at the nanoscale. Further HR-TEM analysis (Fig. S2A and B) confirmed that ultrasmall AuPt nanoparticles (∼2.9 nm) were uniformly anchored on the surface of IDP@Z. The measured lattice spacings of 0.220 nm and 0.201 nm were assigned to the (111) planes of Au and the (200) planes of Pt, respectively. The misalignment of these facets, as delineated by white dashed lines, suggested that lattice mismatch between Au (111) and Pt (200) induced interfacial tensile strain, which in turn promoted the formation of twinned domains. This strain-driven anisotropic growth mechanism is consistent with previous observations in bimetallic systems [28].

Selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns (Fig. 1B d) confirmed the high crystallinity of the AuPt nanoparticles [29]. Scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping (Zn, Au, Pt, P, Cl) verified the successful incorporation of the ZIF-8 framework, DOX-TPP, and IR1048 molecules (Fig. 1C and S3). The optical properties of IDP@Z@AP were subsequently examined. As shown in Fig. 1D, IDP@Z@AP displayed a pronounced absorption peak at ∼530 nm, which was absent in IDP@Z and is attributed to the localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) of Au nanoparticles [30,31]. Zeta potential and hydrodynamic diameter measurements revealed values of +13.6 mV and ∼184.5 nm, respectively (Fig. S4A). It was unstable in PBS and serum-free medium with gradient increase particle size (Fig. S4B and C). However, the particle size of IDP@Z@AP remained stable in deionized water and serum-containing medium within 14 days, which indicated its feasibility of intravenous administration after forming suitable protein corona. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis confirmed the crystalline nature of the nanorobots (Fig. 1E). The diffraction peaks of AuPt nanoparticles were indexed to face-centered cubic (fcc) structures of Au and Pt. A continuous shift of characteristic peaks from Pt (PDF#08–0644) to Au (PDF#04–0784) was observed in IDP@Z@Pt, IDP@Z@Au, and IDP@Z@AP, indicating the formation of AuPt alloys on the IDP@Z surface. Additionally, the XRD pattern of ZIF-8 exhibited reduced peak intensity upon loading of IR1048 and DOX-TPP (Fig. S5), likely due to coordination interactions between Zn2+ and the encapsulated molecules [32,33]. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis further supported the heterostructure composition, showing characteristic signals for Zn, Au, Pt, P, and Cl, in agreement with the EDS results (Fig. S6). Fluorescence spectroscopy revealed that IR1048 (∼1100 nm, NIR-II region) and DOX-TPP (∼595 nm, visible region) exhibit distinct emission peaks with no spectral overlap, indicating that their signals can be clearly distinguished without mutual interference during fluorescence measurements (Fig. S7) [34].

Following the successful fabrication of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots, their photothermal properties were systematically evaluated. Upon 1064-nm laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2), the temperature of the IDP@Z@AP aqueous solution increased significantly in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 1F). Specifically, at a concentration of 200 μg/mL, IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP heated up to 53.3 °C and 56.3 °C, respectively (Fig. 1F and Fig. S8A). Control experiments further demonstrated that IR1048 exhibited significant photothermal heating under the same irradiation conditions, whereas DOX-TPP showed negligible temperature increase, confirming that the photothermal effect of IDP@Z@AP originates primarily from IR1048 rather than DOX-TPP (Fig. S8B). In addition, after five cycles of NIR-II laser irradiation (1.0 W/cm2), IDP@Z@AP exhibited good repeatability in its heating and cooling behavior, suggesting stable photothermal conversion performance (Fig. 1G). To further verify the structural stability of IDP@Z@AP under photothermal treatment, TEM characterization was performed before and after NIR-II laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min). As shown in Fig. S8C, the nanorobots retained their overall morphology and structural integrity post-irradiation, indicating good photothermal robustness. The calculated photothermal conversion efficiency (η) of IDP@Z@AP was 75.23 %, which is notably higher than that of IDP@Z lacking surface-anchored bimetallic nanoparticles and also surpasses other IR1048-based photothermal agents reported previously (Fig. 1H and Fig. S8D) [35]. This enhancement is likely attributed to the LSPR effect of AuPt nanoparticles [36,37].

Given its strong photothermal performance, the NIR-II PA properties of IDP@Z@AP were further investigated using the Vevo LAZR-X imaging system [38]. In vitro PA imaging (Fig. 1I) revealed that IDP@Z@AP generated concentration-dependent PA signals, consistent with the optical absorption characteristics (Fig. 1I and Fig. S8E). A strong linear correlation was observed between PA signal intensity and the concentration of the nanorobots (Fig. 1J).

Electronic reconfiguration

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to investigate changes in the chemical states of Au and Pt, as well as variations in their binding energies (BEs). As shown in the Zn 2p spectrum (Fig. 2A), IDP@Z exhibited two characteristic peaks at 1021.6 and 1044.7 eV, corresponding to Zn2+ 2p3/2 and 2p1/2, respectively. After modification into IDP@Z@AP, both peaks shifted by 0.3 eV to higher binding energies (Fig. 2B), suggesting a reduction in electron density around Zn atoms, likely due to the formation of Zn-related defects during synthesis [39].

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) characterization of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots. High-resolution Zn 2p spectra of IDP@Z (A) and IDP@Z@Au (B); Au 4f spectra of IDP@Z@Au (C) and IDP@Z@AP (D); Pt 4f spectra of IDP@Z@Pt (E) and IDP@Z@AP (F).

The high-resolution Au 4f spectrum displayed three pairs of peaks attributed to the spin-orbit coupling of Au 4f7/2 and Au 4f5/2. The dominant peak pair at 84.0 and 87.3 eV corresponds to metallic Au0, while the other peaks at 85.6/89.1 eV and 87.3/90.4 eV are attributed to Au+ and Au3+ oxidation states, respectively [40,41]. A fraction of Au0 was partially oxidized to Auδ+ on the surface of IDP@Z@Au and IDP@Z@AP (Fig. 2C and D), possibly due to strong interactions between Au nanoclusters and Zn defect sites. The Pt 4f spectrum was deconvoluted into two doublets (Fig. 2E and F). The peak at ∼70.9 eV corresponds to metallic Pt (Pt0, 4f7/2), while the remaining peaks are ascribed to Pt2+, confirming the presence of oxidized Pt species [42,43]. Notably, the BE variations of Au3+ and Pt2+ between monometallic and bimetallic samples (Fig. 2) suggested the formation of Au-Pt alloy structures [44]. Compared to monometallic counterparts, IDP@Z@AP exhibited an increased proportion of Pt2+ and a slight decrease in Au3+, indicating that interfacial strain at the Au-Pt junctions induced electronic redistribution within the alloyed domains.

Cascading catalysis and self-propelled motion

The glucose oxidase (GOx)-like catalytic activity of IDP@Z@AP was first evaluated in vitro. In this system, AuNPs catalyzed the oxidation of glucose to generate gluconic acid and H2O2 [45,46]. Upon addition of IDP@Z@AP to glucose solutions, H2O2 production was observed in a glucose concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3A). At a glucose concentration of 200 mM, IDP@Z@AP generated up to 45.7 μM H2O2 within 5 h at 25 °C. After 1 h incubation at room temperature, IDP@Z@AP exhibited a glucose conversion efficiency of ∼25.5 %, outperforming IDP@Z@Au (15.4 %) and free AuNPs (10.3 %) (Fig. 3B). This enhanced catalytic efficiency of IDP@Z@Au compared with free AuNPs is likely due to the prevention of AuNPs aggregation within the ZIF-8 framework, which preserves a high surface area and facilitates electron/proton transfer during glucose oxidation [[46], [47], [48]].

Furthermore, steady-state kinetic analysis using the Michaelis-Menten model revealed that IDP@Z@AP possessed the lowest Michaelis constant (Km = 22.4 mM) and highest maximum velocity (Vmax = 3.53 × 10−8 M s−1), outperforming IDP@Z@Au (Km = 30.40 mM, Vmax = 2.77 × 10−8 M s−1) and free AuNPs (Km = 40.38 mM, Vmax = 1.69 × 10−8 M s−1) (Fig. 3C and Fig. S9), indicating enhanced catalytic affinity and efficiency [27]. Notably, IDP@Z@AP exhibited superior performance over IDP@Z@Au, suggesting that the incorporation of Pt atoms plays a critical role in boosting catalytic activity. This enhancement may stem from the electronic modulation and improved glucose adsorption affinity induced by Pt doping, which facilitates substrate activation and subsequent oxidation [41]. To simulate the acidic microenvironment resulting from catalytic oxidation of glucose into gluconic acid, a high glucose concentration (200 mM) was employed in the degradation assay, enabling evaluation of the nanorobot's acid-responsiveness under intensified catalytic conditions. As a result of gluconic acid generation, the IDP@Z@AP nanorobots gradually degraded over 24 h of incubation, as confirmed by TEM imaging (Fig. S10). In addition, we also conducted TEM analysis under tumor-mimicking conditions with 5 mM glucose, which demonstrated that IDP@Z@AP maintained its nanostructure (Fig. S10), thereby confirming its colloidal stability and functional integrity under physiologically relevant conditions. This structural disassembly facilitated the release of DOX-TPP, which was further accelerated by photothermal stimulation under NIR-II laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min) (Fig. 3D). Subsequently, catalase (CAT)-like activity was assessed. After the addition of glucose (200 mM), a rapid increase in O2 production was detected within 1 h at 25 °C (Fig. 3E). Upon NIR-II laser irradiation, O2 generation increased by ∼60 % compared to the non-irradiated condition, suggesting that the CAT-like activity was enhanced by the photothermal effect. Given that O2 generation can drive motion, the self-propelling behavior of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots was further examined via nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) under various conditions (Fig. 3F). In the absence of glucose, IDP@Z@AP exhibited random Brownian motion. In glucose-containing solutions, under laser irradiation, the nanorobots exhibited prolonged and directional movement, with velocities increasing proportionally to glucose concentration (Fig. 3G). Mean-squared displacement (MSD) analysis further supported this behavior: IDP@Z@AP in water exhibited linear MSD profiles consistent with Brownian diffusion, whereas parabolic MSD curves in glucose solutions indicated active propulsion (Fig. 3H) [49,50].

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the cascade catalytic system efficiently oxidizes glucose to generate gluconic acid and H2O2, which is subsequently decomposed into oxygen to fuel nanorobot propulsion. Notably, NIR-II laser irradiation further enhances catalytic efficiency and augments nanorobot motility (Fig. 3I).

Jahn-Teller distortion-mediated electronic modulation and catalytic enhancement on alloyed surfaces

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to elucidate how alloying modulates the electronic structure and adsorption behavior of Au, Pt, and Au-Pt surfaces supported on ZIF-8. Given that (111) facets are the most thermodynamically stable and predominant in the synthesized nanoparticles (Fig. 1E) [51,52], all models were constructed based on Au (111), Pt (111), and bimetallic AuPt (111) surfaces. The calculations revealed that alloying Pt with Au markedly upshifts the surface d-band center: the d-band centroid of AuPt (111) is positioned at −2.02 eV, notably higher than that of pure Au (−3.94 eV) and slightly above Pt (−2.25 eV) (Fig. 4A). This upshift indicates an increased density of d-states near the Fermi level, consistent with the experimentally superior activity of the bimetallic catalyst [53]. According to d-band theory, such a shift facilitates stronger adsorbate binding and accelerates interfacial electron transfer, thereby enhancing the rate of catalytic conversion [54].

(B) Energetically favorable adsorption sites of glucose molecules on Au (111) and AuPt (111) surfaces, with ZIF-8 as the substrate. (C, D) Partial density of states (PDOS) showing Au-glucose orbital interactions on Au (111) and AuPt (111), respectively. (E) Calculated Pt-Pt and Au-Au bond lengths in Au, Pt, and AuPt structures.

To further investigate the surface reactivity, glucose adsorption energies were calculated at various binding sites (Fig. 4B). Glucose exhibits significantly stronger binding on AuPt (111) (−0.24 to −0.11 eV, most stable at the bridge site) than on Au (111) (−0.06 to −0.04 eV), attributed to the synergistic electronic and geometric effects introduced by Pt. Partial density of states (PDOS) analysis (Fig. 4C and D) confirmed a stronger overlap between Au d-orbitals and the O 2p orbitals of glucose on AuPt (111), particularly near the Fermi level. This pronounced Au–O orbital hybridization in the alloy facilitates charge transfer to the adsorbed glucose, thereby activating the molecule for oxidation.

To gain structural insight into the electronic reconfiguration observed in XPS spectra (Fig. 2), we further analyzed bond lengths and charge distributions within the AuPt alloy. DFT calculations showed that Pt-Pt bonds (2.868 Å) remain nearly unchanged from pure Pt (2.856 Å), whereas Au-Au bonds expanded from 2.890 Å (pure Au) to 2.933 Å in the alloy. This elongation indicates lattice distortion induced by Pt-Au misfit strain, supported by a broadened bond-length distribution. The resulting interfacial strain generates more unsaturated catalytic sites [55], consistent with the twinned structures observed in TEM (Fig. 1B c) and the ∼60 % enhancement in catalytic activity (Fig. 3B).

The interfacial strain also drives charge redistribution between Au and Pt. In the alloy, Au atoms acquire more negative charge while Pt atoms become slightly positive (Fig. S11), indicating electron transfer from Pt to Au. Consequently, XPS reveals a higher fraction of cationic Pt (Pt2+) and a slight reduction of Au3+ in the AuPt sample, signaling strain-induced partial phase segregation of Pt. In effect, two distinct categories of Pt sites (electron-rich and electron-deficient) emerge in the bimetallic system. Such electronic polarization at the Au-Pt interface is known to boost catalytic activity by optimizing the binding of reaction intermediates [56]. These structural and electronic effects, including the upshifted d-band center, enhanced orbital hybridization near the Fermi level, stronger glucose adsorption, and interfacial charge redistribution, can be collectively attributed to a Jahn-Teller-induced orbital distortion mechanism (Fig. S12). The Jahn-Teller distortion arising from asymmetric d-orbital occupancy in Pt atoms induces lattice relaxation and localized strain, which in turn alters the hybridization environment of surface atoms. These geometric and electronic perturbations collectively contribute to the upshift of the d-band center, enhancing catalytic reactivity.

In summary, alloying Au with Pt profoundly tunes the surface d-band electronic structure and interfacial strain, which synergistically strengthens reactant adsorption and electron transfer. These effects rationalize the observed superior catalytic performance of the Au-Pt alloy compared to monometallic Au or Pt.

Active mitochondrial targeting and antitumor activity in vitro

To evaluate the mitochondrial-targeting ability of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots, 4T1 cells were incubated with various formulations, including DOX, DOX-TPP, DOX@Z, IDP@Z, and IDP@Z@AP. Mitochondria and nuclei were stained with MitoTracker Green and Hoechst 33342, respectively, while the red fluorescence of DOX or DOX-TPP was used to track subcellular localization (Fig. 5A). Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) imaging further revealed that under glucose-containing conditions, cellular uptake of IDP@Z@AP was markedly increased compared to glucose-free conditions (Fig. S13A and B), which may be attributed to glucose-catalyzed O2 generation enhancing self-propulsion and facilitating cellular internalization. Intracellular trajectory mapping (Fig. S13C) indicated that nanorobots exhibited sustained and directional motion in the presence of glucose, while movement in glucose-free medium was largely random, consistent with catalytic propulsion–driven transport within the cytoplasm. Quantitative colocalization analysis using Pearson's correlation coefficient (PCC) revealed distinct differences among the groups (Fig. 5B). The PCC value of DOX-TPP (0.33) was significantly higher than that of free DOX (0.18), indicating that the hydrophobic and positively charged TPP+ moiety enhances mitochondrial affinity by leveraging the mitochondrial membrane potential. DOX@Z showed relatively poor mitochondrial colocalization (PCC = 0.29), whereas replacing the drug with DOX-TPP (IDP@Z) led to an increased PCC of 0.36. Notably, IDP@Z@AP exhibited the highest PCC value of 0.77, suggesting that catalytic propulsion further facilitates mitochondrial accumulation. These results demonstrated that TPP-guided and catalytically-propelled delivery enhance mitochondrial targeting. Such active targeting promotes drug accumulation within mitochondria, potentially amplifying mitochondrial damage and dysfunction.

To evaluate the cytotoxicity associated with mitochondrial drug delivery, cell viability assays were conducted after 24 h of incubation with nanorobots or control formulations. The IDP@Z@AP-treated group exhibited the lowest cell viability (Fig. 5C and Fig. S14), indicating the most potent therapeutic efficacy. This enhanced cytotoxicity is primarily attributed to the efficient accumulation of DOX-TPP within mitochondria, which is further promoted by the oxygen-generating capability of surface-anchored AuPt bimetallic nanoparticles. The continuous production of O2 facilitates the self-propelled motion of the nanorobots, thereby improving intracellular transport and mitochondrial-level drug delivery efficiency. To further confirm tumor cell death induced by IDP@Z@AP, a live/dead cell assay was performed using calcein acetoxymethyl ester (calcein-AM) and propidium iodide (PI), which stain viable and dead cells with green and red fluorescence, respectively. As shown in Fig. S15, the IDP@Z@AP + Laser-treated group exhibited a higher proportion of red-stained (dead) cells, consistent with the results of the cell viability assays.

Mitochondrial metabolism plays a central role in regulating cancer metastasis. As the cellular powerhouse, mitochondria generate ATP to sustain energy-intensive processes such as cell migration and invasion [57]. Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in metastatic progression, we further investigated the effect of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots on the metastatic potential of cancer cells. Wound healing and transwell invasion assays were conducted to assess cell motility and invasiveness, respectively. In the wound healing assay, cells treated with IDP@Z@AP combined with laser irradiation exhibited the lowest wound closure rate (5.76 %), indicating a marked inhibition of migratory capacity compared to other groups (Fig. 5D and E). A transwell invasion assay using a Matrigel-coated membrane was then performed to evaluate the ability of 4T1 cells to penetrate the extracellular matrix barrier. After 24 h of incubation with various formulations (Fig. 5F), the IDP@Z@AP + laser group showed the most significant suppression of cell invasion, with the lowest number of cells traversing the Matrigel barrier (Fig. 5G). These results demonstrate that IDP@Z@AP nanorobots, under NIR-II irradiation, effectively disrupt cellular motility and invasive behavior.

Taken together, IDP@Z@AP nanorobots exhibit superior mitochondrial targeting, enhanced cytotoxicity, and robust suppression of cancer cell migration and invasion. The synergy between TPP-mediated subcellular localization and catalytic self-propulsion enables precise and efficient organelle-level drug delivery, offering a promising mitochondria-targeted strategy for cancer therapy.

Mitochondria-targeted disruption of function and energy metabolism

The active mitochondrial targeting of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots enhances drug accumulation around mitochondria, thereby amplifying mitochondrial damage and dysfunction. To evaluate this effect, a series of in vitro assays were conducted in 4T1 cells to assess mitochondrial function, bioenergetic status, and structural integrity. JC-1 dye was used to monitor mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm), where healthy mitochondria with high ΔΨm exhibit red fluorescence due to J-aggregate formation, whereas depolarized or damaged mitochondria exhibit green fluorescence from monomeric dye [38]. The red/green fluorescence intensity ratio serves as an indicator of mitochondrial depolarization and early-stage apoptosis. In this study, 4T1 cells were incubated with various formulations for 8 h, followed by JC-1 staining. Cells treated with IDP@Z@AP under laser irradiation exhibited the lowest red/green fluorescence ratio of 0.15 among all groups (Fig. 6A and B), indicating that these nanorobots trigger more pronounced and earlier mitochondrial depolarization, thereby initiating a stronger apoptotic response.

Given the critical role of glucose in cellular energy metabolism, its depletion can effectively inhibit glycolysis and reduce intracellular adenosine-5′-triphosphate (ATP) production [58]. As shown in Fig. 6C, ATP levels in the control and laser-only groups remained close to 100 %. Upon 1064 nm laser irradiation, a mild decrease in ATP level was observed due to the photothermal effect. Notably, the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group exhibited the most pronounced ATP depletion, with ATP levels reduced to 42.6 %, indicating that the enhanced cascade catalytic activity efficiently consumed intracellular glucose and disrupted cellular energy homeostasis. Western blot analysis was conducted to examine the cellular stress response after various treatments by quantifying the expression of HIF-1α, HSP70, and HSP90 (Fig. S16A–C and Fig. 6D). Compared with the PBS and laser groups, treatment with IDP@Z@AP led to a notable downregulation of all three proteins, suggesting attenuation of hypoxia and laser-induced heat stress. Notably, the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group exhibited the most substantial reduction, indicating a stronger suppression of the tumor stress adaptation mechanisms. The observed downregulation of HIF-1α may be attributed to enhanced oxygenation mediated by catalytic oxygen generation. Meanwhile, the decreased expression of HSP70 and HSP90, typically induced under hyperthermic stress, suggested that this treatment effectively suppresses tumor thermotolerance. Together, these findings underscore the potential of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots to improve antitumor efficacy by modulating cellular stress responses under NIR-II laser irradiation.

To comprehensively assess the effects of different treatments on mitochondrial respiration, both real-time oxygen consumption rate (OCR) profiling and quantitative bioenergetic analysis were performed. As shown in Fig. 6E, OCR measurements following sequential injections of oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A (Rot/AA) revealed distinct respiration patterns among the groups. The PBS-treated group maintained the highest OCR across all phases, indicating intact mitochondrial respiratory function. In contrast, IDP@Z@AP treatment notably suppressed OCR at all stages, and this effect was further enhanced by 1064 nm-laser irradiation in the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group. These observations were further corroborated by the quantitative analysis in Fig. 6F, which extracted two key parameters–basal respiration and spare respiratory capacity–from the OCR curve. Compared with PBS and IDP@Z groups, IDP@Z@AP-treated cells showed substantial reductions in both parameters, with the most pronounced inhibition observed in the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group. Specifically, basal respiration dropped by more than 50 %, and spare respiratory capacity was nearly abolished, indicating a severe impairment of mitochondrial metabolic flexibility. These findings indicate that IDP@Z@AP nanorobots can induce severe mitochondrial dysfunction under NIR-II laser irradiation. This effect may arise from a combination of catalytic oxygen generation and intracellular glucose depletion, which together lead to mitochondrial membrane depolarization and cellular energy collapse. The resulting loss of spare respiratory capacity compromises the metabolic adaptability of tumor cells, rendering them more susceptible to therapeutic stress and thereby enhancing the overall antitumor efficacy of the nanorobots.

Fig. 6G shows representative biological transmission electron microscopy (Bio-TEM) images of 4T1 cells subjected to different treatments. In the control group, mitochondria exhibited intact double membranes and well-defined cristae (blue arrows), consistent with normal morphology. In cells treated with IDP@Z@AP, mitochondrial swelling and cristae disruption were observed, along with the appearance of double-membrane autophagosomes (orange arrows), indicative of early-stage mitophagy. In the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group, extensive ultrastructural damage was evident, including ruptured mitochondrial membranes, loss of cristae integrity (purple arrows), and the presence of electron-dense nanorobots enriched at mitochondrial sites (red arrows), providing direct visual evidence of their intracellular localization. These features confirm the intracellular presence of nanorobots and the activation of mitochondrial quality control mechanisms, such as mitophagy and autolysosome formation.

Taken together, these results demonstrated that IDP@Z@AP nanorobots not only achieve precise mitochondrial targeting but also induce mitochondrial dysfunction, energy collapse, and autophagy activation, thereby amplifying therapeutic efficacy. Such mitochondria-targeted strategies represent a promising direction for overcoming drug resistance and metabolic plasticity in cancer therapy.

Enhanced tumor penetration and therapeutic efficacy in 4T1 tumor spheroids

Mitochondria-targeted nanomotors have been shown to induce mitochondrial dysfunction and trigger mitochondria-mediated apoptosis [19]. Given the enhanced diffusion of the NIR-II actuated nanorobots, it was assumed that the IDP@Z@AP nanorobots could penetrate deep into the tumor. Therefore, 4T1 tumor spheroids were constructed to assess the tumor penetration ability of the nanorobots. As shown in Fig. 7A, Z-stack confocal fluorescence imaging revealed that DOX-TPP and IDP@Z exhibited limited fluorescence restricted to the spheroid periphery, indicative of poor penetration. IDP@Z@AP showed moderate improvement, but signal intensity still declined with depth. Remarkably, the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group displayed significantly stronger and more homogeneous DOX fluorescence across multiple Z-planes, suggesting enhanced tissue penetration and uniform intratumoral distribution. These qualitative observations were quantitatively supported by fluorescence intensity profiles shown in Fig. 7B and C, which mapped signal distribution across spheroid diameters. The IDP@Z@AP + Laser group exhibited the highest and most evenly distributed fluorescence over a ∼200 μm range, whereas other groups demonstrated steep signal attenuation or surface-localized accumulation. The enhanced penetration is primarily attributed to laser-induced photothermal activation of the cascade catalytic process. Upon NIR-II irradiation, local heating accelerates AuPt-mediated glucose oxidation to H2O2 and its subsequent decomposition into O2. The elevated O2 production enhances nanorobot propulsion, enabling active movement through the tumor matrix and overcoming diffusion limitations in solid tumors.

We next evaluated the anti-tumor efficacy of nanorobots in the tumor spheroids over a 6-day treatment cycle. Bright-field images revealed that spheroids in the PBS and Laser groups continuously expanded over 6 days (Fig. 7D and Fig. S17), with no significant morphological disruption. Treatment with IDP@Z slightly delayed spheroid growth, while IDP@Z@AP induced modest structural fragmentation, indicating some level of therapeutic activity. Upon NIR-II laser irradiation, both IDP@Z + Laser and IDP@Z@AP + Laser groups exhibited visible spheroid shrinkage and increased disintegration by day 6, suggesting enhanced antitumor effects under photothermal activation. Notably, the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group showed the most pronounced morphological collapse and size reduction. Quantitative analysis corroborates these observations (Fig. 7E). The average spheroid diameter in the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group was significantly reduced over time, reaching less than half that of the control by day 6. In contrast, spheroids in the PBS, Laser, and IDP@Z groups exhibited continuous growth. The enhanced efficacy of IDP@Z@AP is likely attributed to the synergistic effects of mitochondrial-targeted DOX delivery, cascade catalytic oxygen generation, and photothermal enhancement, which collectively disrupt tumor spheroid integrity and proliferation.

These findings underscore the importance of active penetration and organelle-level targeting in overcoming diffusion barriers in 3D tumor models, and highlight the therapeutic potential of NIR-II-responsive nanorobots in solid tumor treatment.

In vivo 3D NIR-II PA imaging of nanorobots-mediated tumor accumulation and cascade catalytic process

To noninvasively monitor the tumor accumulation and cascade catalytic process of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots, we employed real-time three-dimensional (3D) photoacoustic (PA) imaging in the second near-infrared window (NIR-II, 1000–1700 nm), which enables deep tissue penetration and high spatial resolution [59,60], 3D NIR-II PA signals were acquired using the Vevo LAZR-X PA imaging system. Prior to in vivo application, the PA performance of IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP was validated under physiological conditions (Fig. 1D and J). Following intravenous (i.v.) injection of IDP@Z or IDP@Z@AP into 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, 3D-rendered PA images were obtained at tumor sites (Fig. 8A). A time-dependent increase in PA signal was observed, reaching a maximum at 24 h post-injection. Compared to that of background, the PA signal intensity increased by 6.1-fold for IDP@Z and 7.4-fold for IDP@Z@AP (Fig. 8B). Notably, the PA intensity in the IDP@Z@AP group was 1.2 times higher than that in the IDP@Z group, indicating improved tumor accumulation and retention. This enhancement is likely driven by glucose-triggered cascade catalysis, which generates local oxygen and facilitates self-propulsion of the nanorobots, thereby improving their intratumoral penetration. These in vivo observations are consistent with previous results in 3D tumor spheroid models, where IDP@Z@AP exhibited active motion and superior tissue infiltration.

To further evaluate the nanorobot-mediated cascade catalytic process in vivo, we performed simultaneous 3D NIR-II PA imaging of oxygenated hemoglobin (OxyHb) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (DeoxyHb) in tumors. Time-course 3D-rendered B-mode PA/ultrasound images of OxyHb, DeoxyHb, and IR1048 were acquired using spectral unmixing, revealing dynamic changes in hemoglobin oxygenation between the IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP treatment groups (Fig. 8C). While the tumor oxygen saturation (sO2) levels were initially comparable between groups, a significant divergence emerged after 6 h post-injection, with the IDP@Z@AP group exhibiting a markedly elevated sO2 level. At 24 h, the sO2 in the IDP@Z@AP group reached 43.3 %, approximately threefold higher than that in the IDP@Z group (14.8 %) (Fig. 8D). This observation indicates that O2 generated through cascade catalytic reactions mediated by IDP@Z@AP nanorobots effectively alleviated intratumoral hypoxia.

Collectively, in vivo NIR-II PA imaging validated that IDP@Z@AP nanorobots actively accumulate within tumors and induce localized cascade catalysis, leading to microenvironmental reoxygenation, which may potentiate therapeutic efficacy.

In vivo antitumor efficacy of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

To evaluate the therapeutic potential of the IDP@Z@AP nanorobots in vivo, we established a 4T1 tumor-bearing mice model in BALB/c mice and implemented a treatment regimen involving intravenous administration followed by NIR-II laser irradiation (Fig. 9A). 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were treated with various formulations, and tumor progression was monitored over 14 days. Before evaluating in vivo antitumor efficacy of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, the biodistribution was investigated through inductively coupled plasma-mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) based on Au element content. As a result, IDP@Z@AP nanorobots mainly distributed in the liver and spleen region, demonstrated appreciable tumor enrichment (6.2 %ID/g at 1 day post-injection) (Fig. S18A). Meanwhile, based on the result of the in vivo 3D NIR-II PA imaging, laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min) was performed on tumors for in vivo cancer therapy in 24 h post intravenous injection of PBS, IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP, respectively.

(B) Photographs of excised tumors collected from different treatment groups after 14 days. (C) Tumor growth curves of mice with different treatment. (D) Histological studies with hematoxylin & eosin (HE) staining of tumor sections after 14-day therapy. Scale bars: 75 (white) and 100 (black) μm. (E) Representative immunofluorescence staining of HSP70 (green) and HSP90 (red) in tumor tissue sections after various treatments. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue). (F) Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of HSP70 and HSP90, and immunofluorescence staining of HIF-1α (green) in tumor tissues from Laser, IDP@Z + Laser, and IDP@Z@AP + Laser groups. Therapeutic effects were evaluated by the changes of tumor volume. It showed that the treatment of laser or IDP@Z alone was inefficient with no significant difference when compared with the control (PBS group). Whereas the IDP@Z@AP treatment alone (without laser irradiation) displayed a 36.5 % tumor growth inhibition. In the presence of laser, IDP@Z suppressed 66.5 % tumor growth on day 14, and the treatment of IDP@Z@AP exhibited the best tumor suppression with a decrease in tumor size even complete growth inhibition (Fig. 9B and C, S18B). Besides, IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP induced temperature rise by 9.6 and 11.3 °C in tumor tissue, respectively (Fig. S18D).

The biosafety of designed materials has been confirmed, showing no obvious variations in body weights of mice (Fig. S18C), and no identifiable histopathological damage through hematoxylin & eosin staining of the main organs, blood biochemical and hematological data of mice (Figure S19 and Figure S20). Additionally, the ZIF-8 framework in IDP@Z@AP undergoes biodegradation under physiological and mildly acidic conditions, leading to the release of Zn2+ ions. Zn2+ is an essential trace element in mammals and is generally well tolerated at low concentrations [61,62]. Together with the biosafety data, these findings indicate that the Zn2+ release from IDP@Z@AP under the tested therapeutic regimen does not cause systemic toxicity. Consistently, the staining of tumor slices showed the most significant necrosis in group of IDP@Z@AP with laser, consistent with the in vivo anti-tumor results (Fig. 9D).

To further elucidate the molecular stress response induced by treatment, we evaluated the expression of heat shock proteins HSP70 and HSP90, as well as the hypoxia-associated marker HIF-1α, in tumor tissues using immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry. As shown in Fig. 9E, immunofluorescence staining revealed pronounced upregulation of HSP70 (green) and HSP90 (red) in the Laser and IDP@Z + Laser groups compared to the PBS group, indicating a strong heat shock response. This upregulation is a hallmark of conventional photothermal therapy, in which HSP70 and HSP90 act as adaptive molecular chaperones that protect cells from heat-induced proteotoxic stress by promoting refolding of misfolded proteins and suppressing apoptosis [63]. In contrast, tumors treated with IDP@Z@AP + Laser group exhibited downregulation of HSP70 and HSP90, consistent with mitigated proteotoxic stress due to nanorobots-mediated mitochondrial disruption. This attenuation of the heat-shock response aligns with strategies that target mitochondrial function to inhibit HSPs expression, thereby undermining the energy-dependent chaperone induction and sensitizing tumor cells to hyperthermia [64,65].

These findings were corroborated by immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 9F), where reduced HSP70 and HSP90 expression levels were observed in the IDP@Z@AP + Laser group compared to IDP@Z + Laser and laser-alone controls (Fig. S21). This suppression of stress protein expression is indicative of enhanced therapeutic efficiency and reduced cellular resilience to hyperthermia, likely resulting from mitochondrial disruption and metabolic collapse induced by the nanorobots. In parallel, immunofluorescence staining of HIF-1α revealed the lowest levels in tumors treated with IDP@Z@AP + Laser. This observation suggested that the cascade catalytic activity of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots efficiently generates oxygen in situ, thereby alleviating tumor hypoxia. The downregulation of HIF-1α further supports the hypothesis that this treatment not only exerts photothermal and chemotherapeutic effects, but also modulates the tumor microenvironment.

Collectively, these findings confirmed that IDP@Z@AP nanorobots under NIR-II laser activation mitigate both hypoxic and proteotoxic stress in tumor tissues, enhancing the therapeutic outcome by disrupting adaptive cellular mechanisms.

Conclusions

We developed a mitochondria-targeted, cascade-catalytic, and self-propelled nanorobot platform (IDP@Z@AP) that integrates precise drug delivery, metabolic disruption, and real-time therapeutic monitoring. The nanorobots were constructed by encapsulating a NIR-II photothermal agent (IR1048) and a mitochondria-targeted chemotherapeutic agent (DOX-TPP) within a ZIF-8 framework, followed by precise surface anchoring of ultrasmall AuPt bimetallic nanoparticles via ion-exchange and in situ reduction. Upon NIR-II laser activation, the nanorobots initiate a photothermally enhanced cascade reaction to catalyze glucose oxidation and H2O2 decomposition, generating oxygen to power autonomous motion and facilitate deep tumor penetration and organelle-level delivery.

DFT calculations revealed that Pt alloying shifts the d-band center upward, enhancing glucose adsorption and activation. Asymmetric occupation of Pt d-orbitals induces Jahn-Teller distortion, leading to interfacial strain, lattice deformation, and the formation of abundant unsaturated catalytic sites. Functionally, IDP@Z@AP nanorobots triggered mitochondrial depolarization and ATP depletion in vitro, effectively impairing cancer cell migration and invasion. In 4T1 tumor spheroids, the system demonstrated excellent tissue penetration and robust tumor growth inhibition. In vivo, 3D NIR-II PA imaging confirmed tumor enrichment and localized oxygen generation, alleviating hypoxia and enabling dynamic visualization of therapeutic progression. Upon NIR-II irradiation, the nanorobots induced marked tumor suppression and necrosis, accompanied by downregulation of HIF-1α, HSP70, and HSP90, reflecting mitochondrial dysfunction and inhibition of tumor adaptation to hypoxia and thermal stress.

In summary, this work presents a versatile nanoplatform that integrates Jahn-Teller-enhanced catalysis, autonomous propulsion, and subcellular targeting, offering a promising strategy for microenvironment-modulating and imaging-guided precision oncology. Future studies focusing on scalable manufacturing, long-term biosafety evaluation, and clinical validation will be crucial to facilitate the potential translation of this nanoplatform into clinical applications.

Fabrication and characterization of IR1048/DOX-TPP@ZIF-8 (IDP@Z) and IR1048/DOX-TPP@ZIF-8@Au/Pt (IDP@Z@AP)

Briefly, DOX-TPP (4.0 mg) and IR1048 (10 mg) were first dissolved in 1 mL of DMSO and then mixed with 2 mL of an aqueous 2-methylimidazole solution (970 mg) under stirring for 5 min. Subsequently, 2 mL of Zn(NO3)2·6H2O solution (25 mg/mL) was added dropwise to the mixture under magnetic stirring and allowed to react for 10 min. The resulting product was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min and washed twice with deionized water and methanol, respectively. The precipitate was then vacuum-dried overnight at 60 °C to yield ZIF-8 encapsulated IR1048 and DOX-TPP, referred to as IDP@Z.

To prepare IDP@Z@Au3+/Pt4+, 10 mg of IDP@Z was dispersed in 10 mL of methanol, followed by the addition of 100 μL of 50 mM HAuCl4 and 100 μL of 50 mM H2PtCl6. The mixture was stirred at room temperature overnight to allow ion exchange of Zn2+ with Au3+ and Pt4+. The product was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, washed three times with methanol, and vacuum-dried overnight at 60 °C.

For in situ reduction, 10 mg of IDP@Z@Au3+/Pt4+ was redispersed in 5 mL of methanol and treated with 1 mL of freshly prepared NaBH4 solution (5.0 mg/mL). Upon color change from dark green to purple, indicating the formation of bimetallic nanoparticles, the precipitate was harvested by centrifugation, washed with deionized water, and dried under vacuum at 60 °C. The final product denoted as IDP@Z@AP, featured ultrasmall Au/Pt nanoparticles anchored on the surface of the ZIF-8-based hybrid.

TEM and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) analyses were performed to characterize the morphology of aqueous IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP suspensions. Elemental mapping was performed using scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS; Thermo Fisher, USA). UV–vis spectra were recorded on a Hitachi U-2900 spectrophotometer. Quantitative analysis of Au, Pt, and P contents was carried out using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES).

DFT calculations

First-principles density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP 6.4.2) [52,70], within the framework of the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) and the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional [71]. The projected augmented wave (PAW) method was employed to describe the interaction between the ionic cores and valence electrons [72]. A plane-wave basis set with a kinetic energy cutoff of 450 eV was used. The partial occupancies of the Kohn–Sham orbitals were treated using Gaussian smearing with a width of 0.01 eV. The electronic self-consistency criterion was set to 10−5 eV.

Equilibrium lattice constants were optimized using a 2 × 2 × 2 Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh for Brillouin zone sampling. For surface structure optimizations, a 2 × 2 × 2 gamma-point grid was applied. The Au (111), Pt (111), and AuPt (111) surfaces were modelled using four-layer slabs with a 4 × 4 unit cell in the lateral direction. A ZIF fragment layer containing 16 N, 33C, 26H, and 6 Zn atoms was placed beneath the metal surface. A vacuum layer of 15 Å was added along the z-direction to prevent spurious interactions between periodic images. During relaxation, the top two layers of the metal slab were fully relaxed, while the bottom layers were fixed. In the AuPt alloy model, the Au:Pt atomic ratio was set to 2:1.

In vitro anticancer activity and anti-metastatic potential of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

Mitochondrial apoptosis

To evaluate early mitochondrial apoptosis, 4T1 cells (2 × 105 cells per dish) were seeded onto confocal glass-bottom dishes and cultured overnight. The cells were treated with the following formulations: (1) PBS; (2) Laser (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min); (3) DOX; (4) DOX-TPP; (5) IDP@Z; (6) IDP@Z + Laser; (7) IDP@Z@AP; (8) IDP@Z@AP + Laser. The concentration of DOX in all groups was maintained at 5 μg/mL. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with PBS and stained with JC-1 dye (10 μg/mL) for 20 min. The fluorescence signals of J-aggregates (red) and J-monomers (green) were acquired by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). The fluorescence intensity ratio of J-aggregates to J-monomers was quantified using ImageJ software to assess mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm).

Cell viability

4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells (1 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated with PBS, Laser, DOX, DOX-TPP, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP in RPMI-1640 medium for 4 h. Subsequently, IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP groups were exposed to laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) for 10 min. After an additional 20 h of incubation, cell viability was quantified via CCK-8 assay according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Scratch wound healing assay

4T1 cells were seeded into six-well plates and cultured overnight in a complete RPMI-640 medium. Upon reaching approximately 90 % confluency, a uniform scratch was introduced across the cell monolayer using sterile 200-μL pipette tips. The wells were gently washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove cellular debris, and the initial wound area was recorded using a microscope. Subsequently, cells were treated with PBS, Laser, DOX, DOX-TPP, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP in RPMI-1640 medium with 1 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) for 4 h. Following treatment, the IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP groups were exposed to laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2) for 10 min, followed by an additional 20 h of incubation. Wound closure was imaged at 0 h (immediately after scratching) and 24 h post-incubation using an optical microscope. The scratch area was quantified using ImageJ software, and the wound healing rate was calculated accordingly.

Cell invasion assay of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

A Transwell assay was performed to assess the invasive potential of 4T1 cells. Matrigel (80 μL, 1:8 dilution, Corning) was added to the upper chamber of 24-well Transwell inserts (pore size: 8.0 μm) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to form a gel barrier. Then, 100 μL of RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1 % FBS containing 5 × 104 4T1 cells was added to the upper chamber, while 600 μL of RPMI-1640 medium containing 10 % FBS was placed in the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. PBS, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP solutions (each with an equivalent DOX concentration of 5.0 μg/mL) were added to the upper chamber. The cells were incubated for 4 h, subjected to laser irradiation (1064 nm, 1.0 W/cm2, 10 min), and then further incubated for 20 h.

After incubation, non-invading cells remaining on the upper surface of the membrane were removed and washed three times with PBS. The cells that had invaded through the Matrigel and membrane to the lower surface were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1 % crystal violet for 10 min. Fluorescence images were acquired, and the number of invasive cells was quantified from five representative fields using ImageJ software.

Regulation of HIF-1α, HSP70 and HSP90 by IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

4T1 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured overnight to achieve ∼70 % confluence for cell adhesion. After incubation with PBS, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP for 24 h, the cells were irradiated for 10 min and then further incubated for 20 h. Total protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B). Protein concentration was determined with a BCA protein assay kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS/PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 5 % nonfat milk in TBST at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HIF-1α (1:1000), HSP70 (1:1000), HSP90 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:1000). On the following day, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100000 dilution) at room temperature for 1 h, followed by washes with TBST. Protein bands were visualized using the Molecular Imager ChemiDoc XRS + system (Bio-Rad, USA).

Mitochondrial bioenergetics analysis

The oxygen consumption rate (OCR) of 4T1 cells was assessed using a Seahorse XF24 extracellular flux analyzer (Agilent, USA). Cells (2.5 × 104 per well) were seeded into XF24 plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5 % CO2. The medium was replaced with PBS, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP-containing medium (DOX concentration: 5.0 μg/mL) and incubated for 4 h. The cells were subsequently subjected to laser irradiation for 10 min and incubated for an additional 20 h. Thereafter, the culture medium was removed, leaving 50 μL per well, and cells were washed twice with 1 mL XF assay medium. Then, 450 μL of fresh XF assay medium was added to each well, and the plate was incubated at 37 °C in a non-CO2 incubator for 1 h before OCR measurement. OCR was recorded before and after sequential addition of oligomycin (10 μM), FCCP (10 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (5 μM) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Cell living/death evaluation

4T1 cells (2 × 105/mL) were seeded into 48-well plates and incubated with PBS, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP (equivalent DOX concentration: 5.0 μg/mL) in complete culture medium for 4 h. The cells were then irradiated with a 1064-nm laser at a power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for 10 min. After an additional 20 h incubation, cell viability was assessed by co-staining with calcein-AM and propidium iodide (PI). Fluorescence images were captured using a CLSM (Zeiss, Germany).

Tissue penetration and growth inhibition on 4T1 tumor spheroids

Tissue penetration ability

4T1 cells (1.0 × 106 per well) were seeded into a low attached six-well plate and cultured at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere for 4–7 days to facilitate the formation of tumor spheroids. The culture medium was refreshed with RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10 % FBS every two days. IDP@Z@AP or IDP@Z (20 μg/mL) was added to the tumor spheroids and incubated in either glucose-free or glucose-containing RPMI-1640 medium (4.5 mg/mL glucose) for 3 h. After incubation, the spheroids were irradiated with a 1064-nm laser for 10 min and further incubated for an additional 1 h. The spheroids were subsequently washed three times with PBS and imaged using a CLSM. The intrinsic fluorescence of DOX (excitation: 488 nm; emission: 620 nm) was captured and analyzed using ZEN2 software (Zeiss). Z-stack imaging was performed from the top to the equatorial plane, and 3D fluorescence intensity was quantified accordingly.

Growth inhibition of 4T1 tumor spheroids

4T1 tumor spheroids (cultured for 7 days) were treated with PBS, IDP@Z, or IDP@Z@AP, with PBS-treated spheroids serving as the control group. After 4 h of incubation, the spheroids were irradiated with a 1064-nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 10 min. The inhibitory effect on tumor spheroid growth was evaluated by measuring spheroid diameters on days 0, 2, 4, and 6 using an inverted microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan).

Tumor model construction

Female BALB/c mice (4 weeks old, 15–20 g) were obtained from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co., Ltd. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, and were approved by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital (approval number: 20230307030). To establish the tumor model, mice were subcutaneously injected with 1 × 106 4T1 cells per mouse. When the tumor volume reached approximately 50 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned for subsequent in vivo experiments.

Three-dimensional (3D) NIR-II photoacoustic (PA) imaging and photothermal (PT) imaging of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

3D NIR-II PA imaging studies

To evaluate the three-dimensional NIR-II photoacoustic imaging performance in vitro, IDP@Z and IDP@Z@AP at various concentrations (0–500 μg/mL) were prepared by accurately weighing dried nanorobot powders and dispersing them in deionized water, following the methods reported in previously published articles [38,67,73]. These dispersions were then loaded into capillary tubes, and their NIR-II PA signals were captured using the Vevo LAZR-X system (VisualSonics Inc., USA) with excitation wavelengths ranging from 1200 to 2000 nm. The system was configured with the following parameters: frequency = 40 MHz, PA gain = 50 dB, gain = 13 dB, imaging depth/width = 10.00/14.08 mm, and acquisition wavelength = 1251 nm. Deionized water served as the negative control.

For in vivo 3D NIR-II PA imaging, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with IDP@Z or IDP@Z@AP (4 mg/mL, 100 μL, n = 5 per group). PA signals from the tumor regions were acquired before and at predetermined time points post-injection (0, 1, 2, 4, 6, 24, 28 and 32 h). Imaging was performed using the Vevo LAZR-X system with the following parameters: frequency = 40 MHz, excitation wavelength = 1251 nm, wavelength range = 1200–2000 nm, PA gain = 49 dB, gain = 29 dB, imaging depth/width = 10.00/14.08 mm, 3D step size = 0.30 mm, pulse repetition rate = 20 Hz, and laser fluence = 20 mJ/cm2.

To assess dynamic changes in hemoglobin oxygenation levels during treatment, multi-wavelength 3D PA imaging (PA-Mode 3D, Multiwavelength mode) was conducted using the same system. The imaging parameters were as follows: frequency = 40 MHz, wavelength range = 680–970 nm, PA gain = 40 dB, gain = 29 dB, imaging depth/width = 15.00/14.08 mm, and 3D step size = 0.30 mm.

Photothermal (PT) imaging of IDP@Z@AP nanorobots

For photothermal imaging, 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were intravenously injected with IDP@Z or IDP@Z@AP (4 mg/mL, 100 μL, n = 5 per group). After 24 h, tumors were irradiated with a 1064-nm laser (1.0 W/cm2) for 10 min. Real-time temperature changes and infrared thermographic images were captured using a thermal imaging camera (Fluke, USA).

In vivo biodistribution

The in vivo distribution of nanorobots was evaluated based on gold (Au) content using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). When tumor volume reached 50–80 mm3, IDP@Z@AP (4 mg/mL, 100 μL) was intravenously administered via the tail vein. At 1 and 3 days post-injection, mice were euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney), along with tumor tissues, were harvested, weighed, and digested in aqua regia at 80 °C for 72 h. The Au content in each sample was quantified using ICP-MS.

In vivo antitumor evaluation

Tumor-bearing mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5 per group): (a) PBS, (b) Laser, (c) IDP@Z, (d) IDP@Z@AP, (e) IDP@Z + Laser, and (f) IDP@Z@AP + Laser. Mice in the corresponding groups received intravenous injections of IDP@Z or IDP@Z@AP (4 mg/mL, 100 μL, dissolved in PBS), while control mice received an equal volume of PBS. At 24 h post-injection, mice were anesthetized, and groups (b), (e), and (f) were exposed to 1064-nm laser irradiation at a power density of 1.0 W/cm2 for 10 min. Real-time thermal images were captured using an infrared thermal imaging camera. Following treatment, tumor volumes were measured using a digital caliper and calculated with the formula: volume = (tumor length) × (tumor width)2/2 (mm3). Relative tumor volumes were normalized to the initial size prior to treatment. After 14 days, all mice were euthanized, and tumors as well as major organs were harvested for histological analysis.

Tumor and organ tissues were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (P1110, Solarbio), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned using a microtome (Leica RM2235, Germany). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (G1005, Servicebio) was performed on tumor sections according to standard histological procedures. For immunohistochemistry, tumor sections were incubated with anti-HIF-1α (1:200), anti-HSP70 (1:200), and anti-HSP90 (1:200) primary antibodies. Stained sections were imaged using a virtual slide microscope (VS120, Olympus, Japan). For immunofluorescence analysis, images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Leica DM4000B, Germany).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was performed. For comparisons between two groups, one-tailed or two-tailed unpaired Student's t-tests were used as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Statistical significance is denoted as ∗P < 0.05; ∗∗P < 0.01; ∗∗∗P < 0.001.
