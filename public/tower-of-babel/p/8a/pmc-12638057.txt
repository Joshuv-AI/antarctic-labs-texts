Food Compounds Associated With Disease Activity in Inflammatory Bowel Disease
Authors: Marie Meima, Joost Westerhout, Marjolein Meijerink, Sabina Bijlsma, Fiona van Schaik, Bas Oldenburg, Geert Houben
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiet plays a modulatory role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), but specific relevant dietary factors have yet to be identified. To advance our understanding, we performed multivariate analyses of food compound intakes using dietary data from an IBD cohort.MethodsWe used dietary and disease status data from IBD patients at the University Medical Center Utrecht, The Netherlands. Intake levels for 768 compounds from 135 patients were calculated. Using random forest analyses, we explored associations between compound intake and IBD activity by comparing patients who experienced flares (n = 41) with those who remained in remission during follow-up. We assessed the potential biological relevance of the most significant compounds (n = 35) by examining literature on IBD, gut health, or immunomodulation.ResultsRandom forest models showed specificities and negative predictive values up to 77% and 79%, respectively. The intakes of the 35 top-ranked compounds were primarily positively associated with IBD remission (n = 29). Most of these were fatty acids (n = 24). Other compounds positively associated with remission were 4-hydroxyproline, ethanol, vanillin, heptadecanoyl carnitine, and haem iron. For 11 of the 35 compounds, unambiguous effects on IBD or the gut were available in literature, with 7 of these compounds being consistent with our findings. For 11 compounds, immunomodulatory effects were reported in literature. Thirteen compounds lacked relevant literature on effects, highlighting them as candidates for further research.ConclusionsWe identified several compounds that may play a modulatory role in IBD. Further research is needed to clarify associations found in our study and validate their biological significance.

Key Messages

What is already known?

Diet plays a modulatory role in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), but specific relevant dietary factors have yet to be identified.

What is new here?

We use an innovative method to identify food compounds that modulate IBD by translating dietary data into 768 individual compounds and applying multivariate statistics, hereby uncovering potential key modulators of the disease.

How can this study help patient care?

We provide a foundation for identifying compounds that could be used in potential (co)therapies and offer insights for developing improved evidence-based dietary guidelines for IBD patients or disease prevention.

Introduction

Food plays a crucial role in health and disease, including chronic diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD).1,2 However, consistent evidence for a well-defined diet to modulate the course of disease in IBD is presently lacking.3–6 The commonalities among most of the studied diets include avoiding canned and processed foods while promoting the consumption of (specific) fruits, vegetables, lean meat, and chicken.4 However, the diverse nutritional approaches and recommendations regarding certain foods make studies difficult to compare. For example, the Groningen anti-inflammatory diet and the Mediterranean diet encourage wheat consumption, while most other IBD diets exclude wheat.4 Similarly, there are conflicting recommendations among these diets regarding dairy, potatoes, coffee, alcohol, and refined sugar.4

Studies on the relationship between diet and IBD have mainly examined food groups or selected nutrient groups.7–9 Such studies have provided valuable insights, but food consists of large numbers of different compounds. It has been estimated that humans in different parts of the world collectively consume approximately 30 000 distinct compounds derived from all diverse plant and animal food sources.10 Each of these compounds has a unique structure and may have specific health effects, either individually or in combination with other compounds. Research focused on food compounds will offer a comprehensive insight into the effects of individual compounds and their combinations.

We hypothesize that specific (combinations of) food compounds are associated with the disease behavior of patients with IBD, with certain compounds either exacerbating or mitigating disease symptoms. Therefore, we used dietary data from an IBD cohort and applied a multivariate model to identify food compound intake differences associated with disease behavior in IBD patients. To achieve this, we used our previously established coupling system,11 which links food items coded according to the Dutch food coding system NEVO to food compounds databases, allowing for the efficient translation of generic dietary data into compound intake data.

Methods

The primary objective of this study is to identify food compounds that are associated with variations in IBD disease activity. To achieve this, we used a random forest analysis on dietary data from an IBD cohort. We then evaluated the biological relevance of the findings by comparing the food compounds identified through the RF model, that is, those potentially involved in disease activity, with available IBD and gut-related data from the literature.

Cohort Data Description

We obtained patient cohort data from the University Medical Center Utrecht, the Netherlands, which included dietary intake and health status information for 135 patients with Crohn’s disease (CD) (n = 73) or ulcerative colitis (UC)/IBD unclassified (n = 62). Patients were aged between 19 and 68 years (56% female) and were in clinical and/or endoscopic remission at baseline.

During a median follow-up of 29 months (interquartile range [IQR] 20-48 months), 41 out of 135 patients (30%) developed a disease flare. The median time between baseline and relapse was 14 months (IQR 6-23 months). Flares during follow-up were defined as (1) a step-up in IBD medication, (2) endoscopy showing active disease, (3) hospitalization due to active IBD, or (4) surgery related to active IBD. Median body mass index was 23 (kg/m2) for patients who got a flare during follow-up, versus 25 (kg/m2) for patients who remained in remission during follow-up. Further details on this cohort, including smoking status, IBD diagnosis, disease duration, and medicine use, can be found in Opstelten et al.. For this study, patients were retrospectively categorized into 2 groups based on whether they remained in remission or experienced flare-ups during follow-up. These groups are referred to as the remission group and the flare group. At baseline, patients filled out a validated Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) specifically adapted for patients with IBD, capturing the intake of foods consumed during the previous month to assess individuals’ habitual dietary intake.7 Consumed foods were coded according to the Dutch food coding system NEVO (https://nevo-online.rivm.nl/).

Food Compounds Data

Previously, we coupled 2 international food compounds databases, FooDB and Phenol-Explorer, to the Dutch food coding system NEVO to efficiently translate generic dietary data from Dutch cohorts to food compounds data. This methodology, as detailed in Meima et al., involved a systematic coupling approach that consisted of manually matching food items from NEVO to similar items from FooDB and Phenol-Explorer.12 The coupling resulted in a combined food compounds database with concentration levels for 1160 compounds across 2152 food commodities. Using this database and FFQ data (Section “Cohort Data Description”), we successfully translated the dietary data of the 135 IBD patients into compound intake levels (mg/day).11

Analyses

Compounds were included in the analysis only if they were consumed by at least 80% of the study population. We used principal component analysis to uncover patterns and outliers in the dataset. To identify compounds associated with disease flares and remission, we employed random forest analysis. This method utilized food compound intake data to distinguish between patients who experienced disease flares during follow-up and those who remained in remission.

Since a random forest analysis requires complete data, we imputed missing values by fitting a log-normal consumption distribution using the fitdistrplus R package (version 1.1-8), stratified by sex and disease status during follow-up (remission or flare). Next, we used random sampling from the fitted consumption distribution to replace the missing data. Finally, our analysis included a total of 768 compounds, of which most were polyphenols (35%), followed by fatty acids (11%), terpenes (8%), elements, and amino acids.

Principle component analysis

Residuals of the model were computed using the principal component model, and leverage was assessed. A subject was considered an outlier if it showed both high leverage and large model residuals. For all principle component analyses, data were standardized, where each variable was scaled to a mean of 0 and variance of 1 prior to analysis. This scaling method ensured that variables with smaller values were not dominated by those with larger values. No outliers were identified in the data.

Random forest analysis

Decision trees were constructed by randomly selecting food compounds and using random subsets of the dataset for each tree. The dataset was initially split into a training set (75% of the patients) and a validation set (25%), ensuring that the proportion of patients with a flare in both subsets was representative of the full dataset. Next, we ensured equal sample sizes for both flare and remission groups when training the model.

Due to considerable variability in food compound consumption and the relatively small dataset, we generated 100 different training and testing datasets by randomly sampling the full dataset 100 times. For each unique training dataset, 10 random forest models with 1000 trees each were applied and combined into a single model to predict the disease status during follow-up in each corresponding unique testing dataset to evaluate overall model performance.

Next, we compared the ranking of food compounds based on their importance, indicated by the GINI index,13 for each training dataset. The random forest algorithm estimates the GINI index of a food compound by measuring the increase in prediction error when the data for that compound is permuted while others remain unchanged. Minimal loss in prediction accuracy implies low importance.14 To identify the most relevant food compounds, we determined the median rank for each food compound across the 100 different training datasets.

We created multiple models using decreasing subsets of the top-ranked food compounds. Specifically, we developed 12 models with 768, 500, 250, 100, 75, 50, 35, 25, 20, 15, 10, and 8 compounds. Each subsequent model included the top compounds from the model with 768 compounds. This allowed us to systematically assess the impact of the number of compounds used on the statistical outcomes of the random forest analysis and thereby identify an optimal model—the minimal number of compounds needed to obtain a model with the best overall performance. For each model, we calculated the sensitivity and specificity as well as positive and negative predictive values (PPV, NPV) and accuracy to evaluate its performance.

All random forest analyses were performed using the Random Forest R package15 in the R environment (Version 4.3.3).

Biological Relevance of Selected Compounds

The 35 compounds that contributed most to the random forest were further scrutinized. A brief search in PubMed was performed for each compound with queries “*compound name* AND inflammatory bowel disease” and “*compound name* AND inflammatory.” Based on the collected literature, for each compound, we stated whether it had a (suspected) role in IBD, or secondarily in gut health, and, if neither was available, whether it reportedly had immunomodulatory properties.

Of the selected compounds, 25 (71%) were fatty acids, including saturated fatty acids, unsaturated fatty acids, short-chain fatty acids, polyunsaturated fatty acids, and monounsaturated fatty acids. The other selected compounds were vanillin, cellobiose, ethanol, heptadecanoyl carnitine, 4-hydroxyproline, kaempferol-3-glucoside, kaempferol-3-O-acetyl-glucoside, acetylglycine, haem iron, and molybdenum.

Selected compounds were derived from less than 10 to hundreds of different foods (see Table 1 “NEVO unique codes (n)”), within a large variety of food categories. Only 4-hydroxyproline was found in one category, that is, meat products.

Literature on Biological Effects of the Selected Compounds

The information found in the literature search on plausible effects of the 35 selected compounds on IBD, the gut, or the immune system is summarized in Table 1. Most food compounds are present in a wide variety of food products at significant concentrations, as presented in the column “NEVO unique food entries” in the table. We omitted to present specific food sources in Table 1, as categorizing these foods into broad, generic groups would undermine our objective of focusing on specific food compounds. Moreover, it could lead to misinterpretations, such as incorrectly assuming that an entire food category is beneficial or harmful in IBD. Our findings on intakes of dairy, fatty dairy products, and fatty acids from these (see Discussion), illustrate such possible misinterpretation.

Compounds with higher intake in the remission group

Evidence for biological activities was limited for most of the compounds, including the fatty acids. Of the compounds consumed in higher amounts by participants who remained in remission (n = 29), butyric acid, vanillin, remainder linolenic acid (ie, the portion of linoleic acid that remains after accounting for identified isomers), trans + cis linoleic acid, capric acid and 4-hydroxyproline reportedly attenuated inflammation in IBD, were gastroprotective, improved barrier function, or were associated with a reduced risk of developing IBD. Of these, butyric acid was the only compound with clinical data. Myristic acid and palmitic acid were reported to be either impair intestinal integrity or increase the risk of flares. For 10 compounds, immunomodulatory effects were present. For the remaining 11 compounds, no data or inconsistent data were found in the literature search regarding effects on IBD, the gut, or the immune system.

Compounds with higher intake in the flare group

For the compounds with higher intakes in the flare group (n = 6), the literature indicates that molybdenum might exacerbate colitis and promote dysbiosis, while kaempferol-3-glucoside and cellobiose have been shown to attenuate experimental colitis in mice. Trans-11-docosenoic acid had suspected immunomodulatory effects. The other 2 compounds with higher intake in the flare group, no data, or inconsistent data were found in the literature search regarding effects on IBD, the gut, or the immune system.

Discussion

In our previous work, we integrated 3 food compound databases to translate generic dietary data into food compound data.12 Combining these integrated databases along with FFQs, we calculated food compound intake levels.11 In the current study, we applied a multivariate random forest analysis on intake values of 768 food compounds to identify food compounds that potentially play a beneficial or harmful role in IBD activity. We showed that random forest models based on up to 75 compounds showed optimal overall performance. The models performed particularly well in predicting disease remission, with specificities and NPVs up to 77% and 79%, respectively.

The compounds that contributed most to the models were fatty acids. As model performances increased only very slightly when more than 25-35 compounds were included in the models, we further examined the 35 compounds that contributed most significantly to the models. However, as model performances still slightly increased with larger sets of compounds, up to 75, the compounds and associated intake values for the next 40 compounds with the highest GINI indices, ranked 36-75, are provided in Table SB of this paper.

The contribution to the random forest model by a compound may result from an actual biologically relevant difference in intake between patients remaining in remission and those experiencing flares during follow-up. However, for some compounds, it may also be due to coincidence or due to the possibility that a compound co-occurs in the diet with other compounds with biological effects. The results of our study therefore should be considered exploratory, providing leads for further research. To follow up on these leads, as a first step, we performed a brief literature search to identify information on the possible effects of the 35 selected compounds on IBD, the gut, or the immune system.

Clinical data related to IBD have been reported for butyric acid. Butyric acid plays a crucial role in regulating epithelial barrier function and intestinal homeostasis in digestive health and IBD,35,53 and exerts significant immunological effects by supporting the differentiation of naive T cells into anti-inflammatory T cells, and enhancing the tolerogenic response of immune cells.54 Clinical studies have shown that high microencapsulated sodium butyric acid supplementation of 1000 mg/day for 12 months helped to remain in clinical remission in UC, but failed to show efficacy in patients with UC and CD in a dose of 150 mg daily for 12 weeks.53 Our study found a notable difference in habitual butyric acid intake between patients in remission (mean intake of 662 mg/day) and those experiencing flares (435 mg/day). This difference aligns with relevant doses found in the above-mentioned literature and supports the idea that butyric acid intake may have positively contributed to maintaining disease remission in our study population.

Clinical data showed that high ethanol intake has a detrimental effect on the intestinal epithelial barrier,48 while moderate alcohol intake has been reported to reduce inflammation.34 Ethanol intake in our study population was moderate for both the patients developing a flare and those remaining in remission, with quantities comparable to an average of 3.4 and 5.0 glasses of wine per week, respectively.

For five compounds that showed higher intakes in the remission group, that is, capric acid, trans + cis linoleic acid, remainder linolenic acid, vanillin, and 4-hydroxyproline, in vivo data indicated a beneficial role in IBD or on gut health. For molybdenum, a higher intake was observed in the flare group, which aligns with existing literature, as animal studies indicate that molybdenum may exacerbate colitis and promote dysbiosis.47

Some of the results found in our study were contradictory to the effects described in the literature. For myristic acid, intake was higher in the remission group, while this compound was associated with an increased risk of flares in UC patients.21 Palmitic acid, which also showed higher intakes in the remission group, has been reported to impair intestinal integrity.15,55 Also, kaempferol-3-glucoside and cellobiose exhibited findings that contradicted previous research, as these were found to reduce colitis in mice models.41,42,45 These counterintuitive associations may be the results of the factors mentioned above, that is, coincidence and co-occurrence.

For 11 selected compounds with data in literature, the existing studies were not specific to IBD or gut health. These all concerned fatty acids with suggested immunomodulatory activity, either pro- or anti-inflammatory, making these interesting candidates for further research on their possible role in IBD.

Our method excels at pinpointing potential key compounds from a large dataset, which is a significant strength. Compared to univariate methods, random forests can capture nonlinear relationships and interactions, better reflecting the complexity of dietary intake, where compounds can modulate physiological processes over time, potentially influencing the absorption and metabolism of other compounds.

Our analysis demonstrated that the selected compounds are dispersed across a wide range of foods from different categories. Thus, their intakes cannot be attributed solely to the consumption of a specific food category or product group. This is best illustrated by the fatty acids identified in our study. Many of these fatty acids, including butyric acid, are found in high concentrations in butter and fatty cheeses. Interestingly, patients in remission consumed less dairy and cheese overall than those in the flare group. However, their intake of dairy-derived fatty acids was higher due to greater consumption of fatty butter and fatty cheese types. This insight would not have been gained if the analyses were conducted at a food group or nutrient group level.

In interpreting the results of studies on the influence of diet on disease activity, it is important to consider the possibility of reverse causation. While this does not undermine the significance of the correlations between compound intakes and disease activity found in our study, dietary beliefs and advice can shape food choices and, in turn, compound intakes. IBD patients often avoid fatty and dairy products, perceiving them as flare triggers, which aligns with the lower fat and dairy consumption observed in our study population compared to the general Dutch population.56 Frequent flares may further have reinforced these dietary restrictions. However, while fatty foods might worsen symptoms, certain fatty acids, like butyric acid, could be beneficial. Thus, our patients who experienced flares may have unintentionally exacerbated their condition by more strictly avoiding fatty dairy products than those in remission. Therefore, a dietary intervention that includes increased consumption of fatty dairy products or supplementation with fatty acids derived from dairy could be a valuable next step in developing supplementary therapies for IBD.

Our methodology also has limitations. Despite considerable efforts to standardize compound nomenclature and combine synonyms,12 compound synonyms could still occur in our data. However, we analyzed the data with and without accounting for synonyms, and the GINI indices of the compounds showed minimal variation, with the top 35 compounds remaining unchanged.

Due to the nature of an FFQ, we combined compound intakes from foods consumed together, and foods consumed at different time points of the day. Therefore, our approach may overlook possible temporal nuances of effects of and interactions between food compounds consumed. Also, the food compounds databases used in this study do not include all compounds present in food, as many compounds have not yet been identified, quantified, or included in databases. For a detailed discussion on the strengths and limitations of using food compounds databases, we refer to Meima et al..

In the current study, we did not differentiate between patients with CD and UC, as the study power would have been insufficient. Literature suggests that associations between food and IBD vary, with stronger and different associations observed for CD compared to UC.57 Future studies with larger cohorts should be conducted to apply our method and distinguish between these 2 forms of IBD.

The approach we used is applicable to any disease and can be used to study potential associations between food compound intake and disease risk or activity, provided that suitable dietary intake and disease status data are available. We emphasize that further research is needed to clarify associations found in our study and validate their possible biological significance. Overall, our approach provides a foundation for identifying compounds that could be used in potential (co)therapies and offers insights for developing improved evidence-based dietary guidelines for (IBD) patients or disease prevention.
