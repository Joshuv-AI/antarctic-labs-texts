Carboxyhemoglobin: a potential participant in neonatal hemolytic jaundice accompanied with myocardial injury
Authors: Xin Qing, Jimo Zhu, Yu Luo, Binzhi Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal jaundice is commonly seen in early postnatal days, and a considerable proportion of cases are caused by hemolysis. It has been found that hemolytic jaundice in neonates is often accompanied with myocardial injury, which may pose a threat to their health. However, the pathogenic mechanism remains unclear. This review focuses on the potential mechanisms of myocardial injury associated with hemolytic jaundice, particularly highlighting the role of carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) as a potential contributor. Emerging evidence suggests that elevated COHb levels may alter the oxygen dissociation curve, thereby exacerbating myocardial hypoxia and subsequent cellular damage, especially in neonates who are highly sensitive to hypoxia. The review aims to improve early prediction, detection, and intervention for hemolytic jaundice-associated myocardial injury, while providing novel therapeutic strategies and potential targets.Keywords: Jaundice, Hemolysis, Carboxyhemoglobin, Myocardial injury, Neonates

Neonatal hemolytic jaundice refers to a pathological condition characterized by the accelerated destruction of red blood cells (hemolysis) in newborns due to maternal–fetal blood group incompatibility, erythrocyte enzyme deficiencies, or structural abnormalities of the red blood cell membrane [1, 2]. This leads to the production of a substantial amount of unconjugated bilirubin that exceeds the liver's metabolic capacity, resulting in a significant elevation in serum bilirubin levels (hyperbilirubinemia) and clinically presenting as jaundice, characterized by the yellowing of the skin, mucous membranes, and sclera [3–6]. According to the latest report of the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) database [7], approximately 2.5 million newborns worldwide develop hemolytic jaundice annually, representing 5%–10% of all neonatal jaundice cases. Globally, the estimated prevalence of neonatal hemolytic jaundice within the first 28 days of life ranges from 3.5‰ to 5.0‰, and approximately 31,000 newborns die annually as a result of hemolytic jaundice, accounting for 28% of all neonatal deaths related to jaundice. Hemolysis typically occurs within the first 24 h after birth and progresses rapidly, posing a major risk for the development of severe hyperbilirubinemia [8]. However, severe hyperbilirubinemia not only predisposes individuals to bilirubin encephalopathy (kernicterus), resulting in permanent neurological impairment, but also contributes to multiorgan dysfunction impacting the cardiac, hepatic, gastrointestinal, hematopoietic, and immune systems [9]. Despite significant advancements in economic conditions and healthcare standards, the disease burden of neonatal hemolytic jaundice should not be underestimated, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. In clinical practice, attention is often focused on neurological complications, whereas damage to other organs, particularly the heart, is frequently overlooked. Currently, the underlying mechanisms of myocardial injury in this context remain unclear and have yet to reach a consensus. According to the hemolytic metabolic pathway and the existing research findings, the concentration of carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) is significantly elevated in neonates with hemolytic jaundice, which can induce myocardial injury by disrupting the oxygen (O2) metabolism and exerting cytotoxic effects. Therefore, COHb may serve as a potential molecular mediator in the pathogenesis of myocardial damage in neonates with hemolytic jaundice.

Biological characteristics and metabolism of COHb

Pathways of COHb production

COHb is a specific complex formed by the binding of hemoglobin (Hb) with carbon monoxide (CO). The pathways of COHb production can be divided into three categories: exogenous exposure, endogenous metabolism and pathological regulation. Collectively, these pathways constitute a dynamic equilibrium network that governs COHb levels. In recent years, with advances in molecular mechanism research and innovation of detection technologies, the pathways involved in COHb production and regulation have been progressively elucidated. Consequently, its physiological and pathological significance has been reevaluated and redefined.

Exogenous CO exposure

Exogenous exposure remains the predominant factor contributing to elevated COHb levels, with environmental and industrial sources being the primary contributors. The incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and biomass, such as vehicle exhaust, releases substantial amounts of CO. Indoor pollution, including emissions from gas stoves and heating equipment, can lead to a marked increase in CO concentrations under poorly ventilated conditions, and the COHb level can reach 5% to 10% after short-term exposure [10]. Furthermore, both smoking and emerging inhalation sources, such as electronic cigarettes and heated tobacco products, can significantly contribute to CO exposure either through direct inhalation or via the release of CO from high-temperature pyrolysis products. Prolonged exposure results in a marked elevation of COHb levels. Specifically, traditional smokers may exhibit COHb levels as high as 10%, demonstrating a dose-dependent association with smoking quantity [11].

Endogenous CO production

Endogenous CO production is centered on enzymatic pathways, with the heme oxygenase (HO) system playing a pivotal role. Specifically, HO-1, the inducible isoform of HO, catalyzes the degradation of heme to release CO, which accounts for approximately 86% of endogenous CO [12]. In addition, certain gut microbiota, such as Bacteroides species, are capable of generating CO through the catabolism of heme precursors, thus constituting another important source of endogenous CO [13].

Abnormal COHb production under pathological conditions

Neonatal hemolysis is predominantly attributed to three major etiologies: isoimmune hemolysis, erythrocyte enzyme deficiencies, and erythrocyte membrane defects [14]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, isoimmune hemolysis occurs primarily due to ABO or Rh blood group incompatibility. In such cases, maternal antibodies, such as anti-A, anti-B, or anti-D antibodies, cross the placenta, bind to corresponding antigens on fetal erythrocytes, and subsequently trigger their destruction, leading to hemolysis [15, 16]. Hemolysis resulting from erythrocyte enzyme deficiencies is frequently seen in glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency. G6PD serves as a crucial enzyme in the pentose phosphate pathway. In individuals with G6PD deficiency, the synthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) is insufficient, thereby compromising the production of glutathione (GSH). GSH serves as a critical antioxidant that protects erythrocytes from oxidative stress. When oxidizing substances, such as fava beans or sulfonamide drugs consumed by the mother, are transferred to neonate with G6PD deficiency via breast milk, these substances induce oxidative damage to red blood cells (RBCs), resulting in cellular destruction and hemolysis [17]. Hemolysis caused by erythrocyte membrane abnormalities is relatively rare. It refers to a group of disorders caused by the premature destruction of circulating red blood cells due to structural or functional defects in the RBC membrane. Irrespective of the underlying cause of hemolysis, the hemoglobin released after the destruction of RBCs is subsequently decomposed into globin and heme. The heme is then degraded by HO-1 into biliverdin, free iron, and equimolar amounts of CO [18]. The released CO binds to hemoglobin, forming COHb. This compound circulates throughout the bloodstream and ultimately reaches the lungs, where it dissociates, enabling CO to be exhaled [19, 20].

Moreover, systemic inflammatory conditions such as sepsis and severe pneumonia can lead to the upregulation of HO-1 expression through the mediation of inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [21]. This process facilitates heme degradation and CO production, ultimately contributing to an increase in COHb concentration [22].

Metabolic kinetics of COHb

Kinetics of COHb formation and dissociation

CO binds to the ferrous iron (Fe2⁺) of deoxygenated Hb to form a hexacoordinate complex. Owing to the steric hindrance imposed by the distal histidine residue, the association rate constant of CO with Hb is lower than that of O2 [23], indicating that O₂ binds to Hb more rapidly at the initial stage. The binding of CO to Hb exhibits positive cooperativity. The first CO molecule binds to deoxygenated Hb in the tense (T) state, inducing a conformational transition toward the relaxed (R) state, and thereby enhancing the binding affinity of the remaining heme sites [24].

Once CO binds with Hb, the CO–Fe2⁺ bond formed is highly stable, resulting in an extremely low dissociation constant of CO from Hb, which is substantially lower than that of O2 from Hb. This high binding stability underlies the markedly greater affinity of CO for Hb, which is approximately 200 to 250 times higher than that of O2 for Hb [25].

Key differences in COHb metabolism between neonates and adults

Stability of COHb: The composition of Hb in neonates differs from that in adults. Following birth, fetal Hb (predominantly HbF) is gradually replaced by adult Hb (mainly HbA); however, a considerable amount of HbF remains in circulation during the neonatal period. Specifically, the median concentration of HbF is approximately 69.5% in term neonates and 75.5% in preterm neonates [26]. HbF comprises two α-globin chains and two γ-globin chains, whereas HbA is composed of two α-globin and two β-globin chains. Notably, the γ-globin chains lack several critical amino acid residues present in β-globin, such as histidine at position 63, which contributes to the enhanced affinity of HbF for O2 [27]. Given that CO competes with O2 for binding to Hb, the increased affinity of HbF for CO results in greater stability of the formed COHb.

Endogenous CO production and clearance rates: Given the elevated hematocrit levels and the reduced lifespan of RBCs during the neonatal period, the rate of endogenous CO production is approximately twice that of adults [28]. The elimination of CO primarily depends on lung diffusion and exhalation. However, the underdeveloped alveolar structure in neonates, characterized by a reduced surface area and lower gas exchange efficiency, imposes limitations on CO elimination [29]. Although neonates exhibit a higher respiratory rate, their small tidal volumes and limited alveolar ventilation further restrict the efficiency of pulmonary CO clearance. Consequently, the rate of CO clearance in newborns is significantly lower as compared to that in adults.

Taken together with the high affinity of HbF for O2, the rapid production rate of CO, and the limited capacity for CO clearance, the half life of COHb in neonates is markedly longer than that in adults. This implies that neonates exposed to the same concentration of CO as adults are more likely to achieve higher COHb levels, and their tissues are subjected to elevated COHb levels for a longer duration as compared to adults.

Detection of COHb

Methods for the detection of COHb

A range of methods are available for detecting COHb, including gas chromatography (GC), spectrophotometry, and noninvasive techniques [30]. Given COHb is converted into CO in the lungs and subsequently eliminated via exhalation, measurement of end-tidal carbon monoxide (ETCO) provides a noninvasive estimation of systemic COHb levels. Notably, the 2022 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) guidelines recommend ETCO measurement as an adjunctive tool for diagnosing neonatal hemolytic disease [8]. Over the past decades, the clinical implementation of ETCO detection has been constrained by the technical limitations of available equipment. Recently, CapniaInc. (Redwood City, USA) developed an innovative ETCO monitoring device known as the CoSense ETCO Monitor, which employs a nasal cannula for noninvasive measurement. Although the system boasts a compact design and an intuitive user interface, its widespread adoption in hospitals is still restricted by insufficient clinical validation, high implementation costs, and the requirement for specialized training and ongoing technical support [31]. GC provides several advantages, including improved sample stability, superior accuracy, and high specificity. However, its clinical application is restricted due to the high technical requirements and complex operational procedures. In contrast, spectrophotometry is not only easier to implement but also requires smaller blood samples volumes and offers shorter processing times as compared to GC [32]. The blood gas analysis method represents a modified spectrophotometric method. In addition to the advantages of conventional spectrophotometry, it allows for faster measurement speed and is particularly suitable for point-of-care testing in emergency departments and intensive care units, thus establishing itself as the preferred method for routine COHb measurement in clinical settings.

Special considerations for COHb monitoring in neonates

For this particular group of neonates with limited blood collection and rapid fluctuations in their clinical condition, the methods for measuring COHb levels must meet the criteria of high sensitivity, minimal sample volume, and rapid detection. Therefore, blood gas analysis and ETCO are recommended. Although blood gas analysis offers superior accuracy compared to ETCO, the latter provides a non-invasive alternative that eliminates the pain of blood collection.

Because of their distinct physiological characteristics, neonates exhibit a higher baseline COHb level than adults. A retrospective study conducted on 76 healthy term neonates who were not exposed to exogenous sources of CO revealed that the 95% CI of COHb levels ranged from 1.23% to 1.32%. Furthermore, COHb levels demonstrated a negative correlation with postnatal age. Specifically, the average COHb levels of healthy term neonates were observed to be 1.35%, 1.26%, and 1.01% on the 1st, 7th, and 14th days after birth, respectively [33]. This finding aligns with the metabolic alterations induced by the postnatal circulatory transition in neonates. As neonates adapt to extrauterine life, HbF is gradually replaced by HbA, and the level of endogenous COHb shows a physiological decline. When the COHb level exceeds the upper limit of the physiological range in healthy neonates, it may indicate potential exposure to exogenous CO (e.g., maternal smoking, CO contamination in the home environment) or an increase in endogenous CO production (e.g., ABO/Rh hemolytic disease, acute onset of G6PD deficiency). In such cases, immediate clinical intervention is imperative, along with a comprehensive investigation into the source of exposure and an evaluation of any potential tissue damage.

COHb in newborns with hemolytic jaundice

COHb in newborns with hemolytic jaundice

Based on the mechanisms of hemolysis and metabolic process, it can be deduced that the production of CO is elevated in neonates with hemolytic jaundice, leading to a corresponding increase in COHb levels. This increase is positively correlated with the severity of hemolysis. A retrospective cohort study conducted in 2020 [32] compared 73 neonates with hemolytic jaundice (study group) to 112 neonates with non-hemolytic jaundice (control group). The average COHb concentration in the study group was higher than that in the control group (2.51 ± 0.68%vs.0.99 ± 0.56%), and the cut-off value of COHb ≥ 2.2% was determined for diagnosing hemolysis, with a sensitivity of 80.8%, specificity of 95.5%, and the diagnostic likelihood ratio of 18.1. In accordance with the previous research findings [34–36], it confirmed that neonates with hemolytic jaundice exhibit an abnormal increase in COHb levels, and a positive correlation was observed between COHb levels and total serum bilirubin concentrations. Hariri et al. evaluated the effectiveness of COHb as a diagnostic criterion for patients with hemolytic anemia (HA) [37]. After adjusting the COHb value for age and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, the study revealed that the level of COHb was negatively correlated with the concentration of Hb. This indicates that an elevated level of COHb is associated with more severe hemolysis. Although the subjects in this study were not newborns, the pathophysiology and metabolic processes of hemolysis are comparable. Therefore, this conclusion may be theoretically applicable to neonates with hemolytic jaundice. Nevertheless, further studies are warranted to validate this conclusion in neonatal populations in the future.

In summary, the concentration of COHb in neonates with hemolytic jaundice exhibits an abnormal increase and is positively correlated with both the severity of jaundice and the degree of hemolysis in hemolytic patients. These findings suggest that the COHb may serve as a potential auxiliary indicator for assessing the severity of jaundice and predicting the occurrence and severity of hemolysis in neonates with hemolytic jaundice.

The value of COHb as a marker for evaluation of hemolysis

COHb and indirect bilirubin

Indirect bilirubin is a terminal product of hemolytic reactions and serves as an important basis for diagnosing jaundice. Although its measurement is well-established and accurate, the metabolism and excretion of bilirubin are influenced by hepatic function as well as post-hepatic factors such as biliary obstruction [38]. As a result, its specificity for hemolysis is limited, and it cannot reliably distinguish whether jaundice is caused by hemolysis. In contrast, COHb is less affected by dysfunction of other organs or metabolic pathways, offering higher specificity. Therefore, COHb can serve as a reliable indicator for reflecting both the occurrence and severity of hemolysis. Moreover, COHb levels can be rapidly determined using a blood gas analyzer, typically within 30 s to 1 min, whereas the measurement of indirect bilirubin often requires tens of minutes or even longer. This rapid turnaround time renders COHb particularly valuable for the early detection of hemolysis, providing a significant temporal advantage in clinical decision-making. In conclusion, COHb demonstrates superior diagnostic value compared to indirect bilirubin in the context of hemolysis.

COHb and reticulocytes

Reticulocytes (Ret) serve as a direct indicator of compensatory erythroid hyperplasia in the bone marrow. In response to hemolysis, the kidneys are stimulated to secrete erythropoietin (EPO), which promotes the proliferation and differentiation of erythroid progenitor cells within the bone marrow; thereby, compensating for the loss of RBCs in peripheral blood. Consequently, the number of Ret released into the peripheral blood increases. Therefore, the Ret count not only reflects the compensatory capacity of bone marrow hematopoiesis but also serves as an important laboratory parameter for assessing the activity of hemolytic diseases and evaluating treatment efficacy. However, this response is subject to a certain degree of delay, typically manifesting as an increase in reticulocyte count 3 to 5 days after the onset of hemolysis. Moreover, it may be influenced by various factors such as bone marrow reserve capacity, nutritional status (e.g., deficiencies in iron, folate, or vitamin B12), EPO levels, and medications [39]. In contrast, as an indirect product of hemolysis, COHb demonstrates a higher degree of timeliness, enabling it to reflect the hemolytic state in an almost real-time manner. Thus, COHb is recommended as a preferable preliminary screening marker for hemolysis in clinical practice, facilitating earlier detection and timely intervention.

COHb and lactate dehydrogenase

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) is an enzyme indispensable for cellular metabolism and is virtually present in all living cells [40]. Upon the destruction of RBCs, LDH is released. The more erythrocytes are destroyed, the more LDH will be released. Consequently, the degree of LDH elevation can reflect the severity of hemolysis. However, LDH lacks specificity, as conditions such as hypoxia, myocardial infarction, liver diseases, tumors, and muscle injuries can also lead to a marked elevation in LDH levels [41]. Therefore, it is imperative to integrate clinical findings and other hemolysis-related markers for accurate assessment. In comparison, COHb exhibits relatively higher specificity and provides a more reliable reference value for diagnosing hemolysis compared to LDH.

COHb and the hemolysis triad tests

The hemolysis triad tests consist of the direct antiglobulin test (DAT), the free antibody test, and the RBC antibody release test. Among these, the DAT is widely regarded as the gold standard for diagnosing immune-mediated hemolysis [42]. However, this test is not only time-consuming but also exhibits limited correlation with the severity of hemolysis and is incapable of quantifying the rate of RBC destruction [8]. Although COHb is not the gold standard for diagnosing hemolysis, its levels can be rapidly measured using a blood gas analyzer. As mentioned above, COHb levels are positively correlated with the degree of hemolysis and can reliably reflect the severity of hemolysis. Therefore, COHb holds significant clinical value in timely predicting hemolysis and assessing its severity.

Impact of treatment for neonatal hemolytic jaundice on COHb level

The treatment for neonatal jaundice with hemolysis primarily encompasses phototherapy, intravenous administration of immunoglobulin (IVIG), intravenous infusion of albumin, and exchange transfusion (ET). Phototherapy, which facilitates the excretion of bilirubin through isomerization, serves as the first-line treatment for jaundice. Visible light can dissociate CO from COHb by substantially reducing the affinity between CO and Hb [43], thereby enabling phototherapy to decrease COHb levels. At present, all studies on the photolysis of COHb are exclusively based on the theoretical models in vitro, with no corresponding in vivo data available. Future research could prioritize exploring this area in vivo. IVIG is utilized in the treatment of moderate to severe hyperbilirubinemia caused by alloimmune hemolysis, such as induced by Rh or ABO incompatibility. It effectively mitigates the risk of ET [44, 45]. The underlying mechanism involves blocking Fc receptors within the reticuloendothelial system [46], thereby inhibiting the destruction of sensitized erythrocytes, slowing the hemolytic rate, and reducing heme release. As heme release diminishes, the production of CO is reduced, leading to a corresponding decrease in COHb levels. Albumin is frequently used in critically ill neonates with hypoalbuminemia or significantly elevated free bilirubin levels. It binds to free bilirubin, thereby reducing the concentration of bilirubin and reducing the associated risk of neurotoxicity [47]. However, albumin does not directly inhibit hemolysis, and there is currently no evidence suggesting that albumin infusion affects COHb concentrations. As a rescue therapy for severe hyperbilirubinemia, ET effectively and rapidly eliminates sensitized erythrocytes, excess bilirubin, and maternal antibodies. In this process, pre-formed COHb in the circulation is also directly removed. In addition, by reducing the number of sensitized erythrocytes, the subsequent rate of hemolysis and the production of COHb are significantly diminished.

Neonatal hemolytic jaundice is often accompanied by myocardial injury

The correlation between bilirubin level and degree of myocardial injury

Clinically, considerable attention has been focused on central nervous system injury (kernicterus) caused by severe hyperbilirubinemia. With the progression of research, growing evidence suggests that neonatal hyperbilirubinemia not only leads to neurological damage but also exerts toxic effects on multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular and hepatic systems. To date, the etiology and mechanisms underlying hyperbilirubinemia-induced myocardial injury remain unclear, although it is hypothesized that bilirubin itself plays a central role. Elevated bilirubin levels may compromise normal cardiomyocyte metabolism and function through multiple mechanisms, including disruption of cell membrane integrity, induction of oxidative stress via the accumulation of oxygen free radicals, and inhibition of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production [48, 49]. A series of retrospective studies have consistently demonstrated a positive correlation between serum bilirubin levels and the severity of myocardial injury, indicating that elevated serum bilirubin concentrations are associated with progressively increasing troponin levels and aggravated myocardial damage in affected neonates [50–53]. However, conflicting evidence reveals no significant correlation between total bilirubin levels and the severity of myocardial injury in neonates with hyperbilirubinemia, implying that myocardial damage may not necessarily increase proportionally with rising bilirubin concentrations [9, 54–57]. These inconsistencies imply that the pathogenesis of myocardial injury in neonatal hyperbilirubinemia goes beyond bilirubin toxicity alone and may involve additional unidentified factors.

The correlation between hemolysis and myocardial injury

Lin et al. reported that neonates in the ABO hemolysis and glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) deficiency groups exhibited higher serum levels of myocardial injury markers such as creatine kinase isoenzymes (CK-MB) and troponin, compared to those in the perinatal factors, infection, or breastfeeding groups (all of which were non-hemolytic cases), despite comparable total bilirubin levels across the groups [58]. Similarly, another study reported no statistically significant difference in peripheral blood total bilirubin levels between the hemolytic and non-hemolytic groups, yet CK-MB and troponin levels in the hemolytic group were markedly elevated, exceeding both the normal reference ranges and the values observed in the non-hemolytic group [59]. Taken together, these findings highlight that myocardial injury in neonatal hyperbilirubinemia may not be solely attributed to bilirubin toxicity but may also involve metabolic pathways associated with hemolysis.

Potential mechanisms of COHb involvement in myocardial injury

The toxicity of CO and COHb can impact multiple organ systems throughout the body. Given its high metabolic requirements, the heart is particularly vulnerable [60], which may be explained by several mechanisms as illustrated in Fig. 2.

The mechanisms underlying CO/COHb-induced myocardial injury

Impaired oxygen affinity and oxygen release capacity of hemoglobin

As previously mentioned, as compared to O₂, CO is easier to combine with Hb to form COHb, thereby reducing the O₂-carrying capacity of blood. This disrupts tissue oxygenation and leads to tissue injury, even at low CO concentrations. Simultaneously, the binding of CO to Hb induces a conformational transition from the low-affinity T-state to the high-affinity R-state. This allosteric effect further increases the O2-binding affinity of the remaining subunits within the Hb tetramer, resulting in a leftward shift of the oxygen dissociation curve and thereby further impeding O2 delivery to tissues [43]. In addition, myoglobin (Mb) contains the second most abundant heme in the human body and functions as an oxygen reservoir in skeletal muscle, thereby ensuring a steady supply of oxygen for muscle function [24]. CO binds with Mb to form carboxymyoglobin (COMb), thereby diminishing its capacity for oxygen storage [61] and further exacerbating hypoxia. Organs with high metabolic demands, such as the heart, are particularly susceptible to hypoxia-induced damage due to their elevated O2 requirements [62].

Mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress

CO binds directly to cytochrome C oxidase or indirectly to enzymes within the cytochrome P450 system. This binding blocks electron transport in the mitochondrial respiratory chain, thereby impairing oxygen utilization and reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, ultimately leading to cellular energy failure. Simultaneously, this process is associated with the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), including superoxide anion (O₂⁻), nitric oxide (NO), hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂), and hydroxyl radical (•OH). These ROS can induce oxidative stress, which compromises mitochondrial integrity and function [63–66]. As a result, the structural and functional integrity of cardiomyocytes is impaired. Furthermore, CO induces inflammation by elevating cytosolic heme levels and upregulating HO-1 expression, further exacerbating intracellular oxidative stress [43] and aggravating myocardial injury.

The inflammatory cascade response

Excessive CO competes with nitric oxide (NO) that is bound to hemoglobin (Hb) on the surface of platelets, leading to the displacement of free NO and subsequent platelets activation. Free NO may react with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, a highly reactive oxidant that intensifies bioenergetic deficits by inhibiting the activities of mitochondrial complexes I and II [67]. Hypoxia induces the denudation of endothelial cells, exposing subendothelial collagen. This subsequently initiates platelet adhesion via the vWF-GPIb/V/IX pathway and aggregation mediated by fibrinogen cross-linking through GPIIb/IIIa) [68], leading to platelet activation. Upon activation, platelets release P-selectin and CD40 ligand (CD40L), which further stimulate neutrophil degranulation and promote the release of myeloperoxidase (MPO) and ROS. Consequently, this amplifies the inflammatory responses and exacerbates cardiomyocyte injury [69].

Physiological characteristics of neonates

Owing to the intrinsic physiological characteristics and developmental features of the neonates, even when the concentrations of CO and COHb in neonates with hemolytic jaundice do not reach the levels typically associated with CO poisoning, myocardial damage can still occur through the aforementioned mechanisms. Previous research has demonstrated that the COHb concentration in most neonates with hemolysis generally ranges from 2.0% to 3.0%, exceeding the normal threshold of 2% established for nonsmokers by the UK National Poisons Information Service (TOXBASE) [70]. As previously stated, HbF in the neonates exhibits a high affinity for CO and their capacity to clear CO is limited. Therefore, the half-life of COHb in neonates is longer compared to adults, leading to prolonged myocardial cell damage. In addition, neonates demonstrate increased sensitivity to fluctuations in CO/COHb levels due to their elevated baseline oxygen consumption, immature development, limited adaptability and regulatory capacity [71]. It has been evidenced that under equivalent exposure durations to high CO concentrations, neonates exhibit a greater susceptibility to developing clinical symptoms compared to their mothers, despite showing no significant difference in COHb concentrations between the two groups. Moreover, the severity of symptoms in neonates tends to be more pronounced than that observed in mothers [72]. These findings suggest that for this vulnerable population, even exposure to low concentrations of CO/COHb can be sufficient to induce myocardial injury.

Notably, Elbayiyev et al. demonstrated that red blood cell distribution width (RDW) may rise in response to enhanced erythrocyte destruction and could serve as a predictive marker for neonatal myocardial injury [73]. This finding further underscores the clinical utility of hematological parameters in reflecting erythrocyte metabolism and destruction, indicating that biomarkers such as COHb may also hold significant value in assessing hemolysis and its associated complications in neonates.

Summary and prospect

Hemolytic jaundice complicated by myocardial injury in neonates represents a critical issue in clinical pediatric practice that should not be overlooked. However, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood and lack a consensus in the field. In recent years, with the deepening understanding of the biological roles of COHb under pathological conditions, accumulating evidence indicates that COHb, a product of hemolysis, is positively correlated with the degree of hemolysis and severity of jaundice. Furthermore, COHb may play a significant role in the occurrence and progression of myocardial injury through multiple mechanisms. COHb serves as both a critical indicator reflecting hemolytic activity and an important mediator of myocardial damage. COHb aggravates myocardial hypoxia and dysfunction by competitively inhibiting the oxygen transport and release of Hb, disrupting mitochondrial energy metabolism pathways, and inducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. This effect is particularly pronounced in neonates, whose myocardium is more vulnerable and sensitive due to their immaturity.

Based on the current findings, COHb shows potential as an early clinical warning indicator and a biomarker for risk assessment. It is recommended to incorporate routine monitoring of COHb levels into the clinical management of children with hemolytic jaundice. Specifically, when COHb levels exceed 2%, clinicians should be highly alert to the possibility of myocardial injury and promptly conduct comprehensive evaluations, including assessment of myocardial enzymes (e.g., CK-MB), cardiac injury markers (e.g., cTn), N-terminal pro-B type natriuretic peptide (NT-pro BNP), electrocardiography (ECG), and cardiac function. As a convenient, real-time, and highly specific marker of hemolysis, COHb can assist not only in evaluating the severity of hemolysis and jaundice, but also in predicting myocardial injury. In the future, it may be considered to integrate COHb into the grading systems and complication risk prediction models for neonates with hemolytic jaundice, so as to optimize early clinical intervention strategies.

Although certain progress has been made in the research on COHb within the pathological process of hemolysis, several critical gaps remain. First, the majority of existing studies rely on cross-sectional or retrospective analyses, with a notable absence of large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies to validate the causal relationship between COHb levels and myocardial injury. Second, there is currently no established standardized detection time window or clinical reference thresholds, which are crucial for evaluating the severity of hemolysis and the risk of myocardial damage. The variability in detection methods, sample sources, and assessment metrics across studies also impede the comparability of data and the wide applicability of research findings. Moreover, the molecular mechanisms underlying COHb’s contribution to myocardial injury remain largely hypothetical, lacking comprehensive experimental validation and mechanism elucidation.

Future research endeavors should prioritize multicenter, prospective, large-cohort clinical studies to clarify the causal relationship between COHb and myocardial cell injury and to establish a universally recognized clinical warning threshold for COHb. This would facilitate early detection and timely intervention in clinical practice to prevent the progression of myocardial damage. Furthermore, in-depth explorations into the interaction between COHb and neonatal myocardial metabolic pathways, as well as signal transduction mechanisms, are warranted to identify specific molecular targets, thereby providing novel insights and a theoretical basis for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies. In addition, efforts should be directed toward standardizing COHb detection techniques and establishing standardized clinical pathways, enabling the routine integration of COHb monitoring into the neonatal disease management framework.
