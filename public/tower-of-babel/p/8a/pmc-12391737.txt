Are resin sealants effective to treat caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin compared to composite resin restorations? Systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Larissa Yumi Ito, Letícia Simeoni Avais, Elis Carolina Pacheco, Leticia Maira Wambier, Alexandra Mara de Paula, Taíse Alessandra Hanzen, Alessandra Reis, Denise Stadler Wambier, Ana Cláudia Rodrigues Chibinski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis systematic review analyzed effectiveness of resin sealants in arresting caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin compared to restorations in deciduous/permanent teeth. Seven databases were searched for randomized clinical trials (December/2024), considering the outcomes “caries progression”, “material retention”, “tertiary dentin formation”, with a minimum 6 months follow-up. Risk of bias was assessed with RoB 2; meta-analysis used risk difference as effect measure in random effects model and Cochran Q test / I2 statistics for heterogeneity; GRADE assessed the evidence certainty. From 6251, 6 papers addressing deciduous (3 high; 3 low risk of bias) and 3 permanent teeth (1 high; 2 low) were included. In deciduous teeth, difference wasn’t observed for “caries progression” [0.02 (-0.04–0.08; p = 0.54); I2= 0 % (p = 0.79)] or “material retention” [0.09 (-0.01–0.19); I2= 35 % (p = 0.18)]. In permanent teeth, there was no difference for “caries progression” [-0.01 (-0.08–0.06); I2 = 30 % (p = 0.23)] and “tertiary dentin” [0.04 (-0.00–0.09); I2 = 0 % (p = 0.51)]; “material retention” favored restorations after 48 months [0.08 (0.04–0.12); I2 = 0 % (p = 0.34)]. In conclusion, sealants can be used to treat initial dentin caries lesions in deciduous and permanent teeth; however, the certainty of the evidence is low and moderate, respectively, and the findings must be taken carefully.Keywords: Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Pit and fissure sealants, Dental restoration

Introduction

The treatment thresholds for operative versus nonoperative intervention in the early or moderate stages of caries development is a discussion topic for some time. A paper published in 2010, reporting the outcomes from the 2007 ORCA Symposium, has already stated that the indications for pit and fissure sealants were shifting, from a purely preventive measure to a therapeutic procedure suitable for treating carious lesions in enamel and the outer part of dentin [1]. More recently, an expert Delphi Consensus recommended that, non-cavitated lesions, radiographically extending into the outer third of the dentine, should be managed with microinvasive methods, i.e. dental sealants [2].

The rationale for using pit and fissure sealants to treat initial dentin caries lesions is to create a physical barrier between the tooth and the oral environment, preventing nutrient diffusion from the biofilm to the carious lesion [3]. This alters the cariogenic environment, reducing the number and complexity of microorganisms while promoting the defensive responses of the dentin-pulp complex. Over time, the deprivation of nutrients leads to the cessation of microbial metabolism, arresting carious lesions and promoting remineralization of the carious dentin kept in the tooth, as well as the deposition of tertiary dentin in both permanent and deciduous teeth [3]. This entire process occurs without unnecessary loss of tooth structure [2].

This is a non-surgical, ultraconservative approach for dentin caries lesions classified under ICDAS code 4 (“underlying dark shadow from dentin with or without localized enamel breakdown”) or code 5 (“distinct cavity with visible dentin”) [4].

Thus, the traditional protocol for treating a dentin carious lesion - total removal of carious tissue and its replacement by a dental restorative material - is no longer the only option. Based on the principles of minimally invasive dentistry, all the efforts should be made to postpone the placement of restorations, preventing the tooth from entering the so-called “tooth death spiral” [5]. This approach preserves tooth tissues and maintain tooth vitality [6]. Additionally, it enhances tooth longevity, since restorations, once placed, will eventually require replacement, leading to further tooth tissue removal and dental structure weakening [7].

Historically, a randomized clinical trial coordinated by Mertz-Fairhurst contributed to the foundation of this new approach [8]. The study demonstrated, over a 10 years period, that frank cavitated dentin carious lesions could be treated with sealed composite resin restorations without carious tissue removal, following a non-surgical restorative protocol.

Other clinical studies have demonstrated the efficacy of pit and fissure sealants for sealing and arresting carious lesions in both deciduous and permanent teeth (Borges et al., 2012; Dias et al., 2018; dos Santos et al., 2022; Daniela Hesse et al., 2007; D Hesse et al., 2014; Pellegrinetti, MB, Imparato, e da Silva, RC 2007; Vasconcelos et al., 2017; Alves et al., 2017; Bakhshandeh, Qvist, e Ekstrand 2012; Qvist et al., 2017).

Given these findings, it would be beneficial for the dental community to develop a study summarizing the available evidence on this topic. This would provide more precise estimates about the potential benefit (or drawbacks) of this non-surgical approach compared to the most common invasive protocol (dental restoration), ultimately guiding clinical decision-making in caries management. Therefore, the purpose of this paper is to conduct a systematic review and metanalysis to address the following focused research question, structured according to the PICO acronym: Are resin sealants (intervention) effective to arrest early dentin caries lesions (outcome) in posterior primary and permanent teeth (participant) compared to composite resin restorations (comparator)?

Material and methods

The methodology description followed the recommendations of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2021) [9]. This study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under registration number CRD42020159587.

Eligibility criteria

We included randomized clinical trials (RCTs) with either a parallel or split-mouth design that investigated the use of pit and fissure resin sealants for arresting caries lesions in the outer half of dentin in primary or permanent teeth, compared to restorative treatments. Studies were required to have a follow-up period of at least six months. The restorative treatments considered included procedures involving total or selective caries removal, followed by restoration with any adhesive dental material. Studies were excluded if they involved teeth with a frankly open cavity in enamel (more than 3–4 mm of diameter) or if the dentin carious lesions extended beyond half of the dentin thickness. All searches were conducted without restrictions on time and language.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was “caries lesion progression” and the secondary outcomes were “material retention” and “tertiary dentin formation”. Data extracted from eligible studies were dichotomized for all the outcomes, which were analyzed separately for deciduous and permanent teeth.

For the deciduous teeth, it was possible to evaluate “caries lesion progression” and “material retention” with a follow-up of 6–24 months. For permanent teeth, these outcomes were evaluated at 6–18 months, 24–36 months, and more than 48 months. The secondary outcome, “tertiary dentin formation”, was only possible to evaluate for permanent teeth.

The outcomes “caries lesion progression” and “tertiary dentin formation” were evaluated through radiographic examination; “material retention” (partial/total loss vs. presence) was assessed through clinical examination.

In the study protocol registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), another secondary outcome was planned, which was “secondary caries”. However, data regarding this outcome was not available in the retrieved paper, which didn’t allow the analysis of this outcome.

Information sources and search strategy

The search strategy initially developed for the MEDLINE database via PubMed using controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free keywords based on the concepts of the previously defined PICO question. The strategy was then adapted for other electronic databases, and the following databases were also inspected: Cochrane Library, LILACS, Scopus and Web of Science (Table 1). Additionally, grey literature was searched through Scholar Google (10 first pages) and the reference lists of all the included studies were manually searched. The last search in all databases was conducted in December 2024.

Study selection and data collection process

All articles retrieved from the search process were uploaded into the reference manager EndnoteX9 (Thompson Reuters, New York, NY, USA). Duplicate studies were first removed using the software, followed by a manual second round of duplicate removal conducted by one of the authors.

Next, two reviewers (L.Y.I and L.S.A) screened titles and abstracts to exclude non-eligible articles. If either one reviewer was uncertain about a study’s eligibility, it was retained for the next phase, where eligibility was determined after full-text reading.

Full texts of the articles that met all the inclusion criteria were obtained. Each included study was identified using a combination of first author’s name and the year of publication. Data from each eligible study were collected using individualized, pre-tested forms that recorded details such as study design, number of participants, clinical protocols, follow-up periods, results for each outcome. This process was carried out by two authors (L.Y.I and L.S.A) in an independent manner. In cases of disagreement, a third author (A.C.R.C.) was consulted to resolve any uncertainties. For studies with multiple published follow-ups, data were compiled into a single form to prevent overlapping information.

Study risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers (L.Y.I. and L.S.A.) assessed the risk of bias of the included articles using the Cochrane Collaboration’s Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2). This tool is structured into a predefined set of domains, each addressing different aspects of trial design, conduct, and reporting.

According to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 5.1.0, http://handbook.cochrane.org), each domain was judged based on the risk of bias it introduced. These judgments were determined using an algorithm that analyzed responses to signaling questions. Judgments can be 'low' risk, 'high' risk, or express 'Some concerns' for each domain. If a study had at least one domain rated as “some concerns”, the overall study was classified as having “some concerns” regarding the risk of bias. If at least one domain was considered ‘high’ risk, the study was judged as “high risk” of bias. When 3 or more domains were considered to have some concerns, the study is also judged as having a “high risk” of bias. During data selection and quality assessment, any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion and, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer (A.C.R.C.).

Summary measures and synthesis of the results

All the studies included in the systematic review were subjected to meta-analysis. The analyses were conducted using the Review Manager 5.3 software (Review Manager version 5, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark) to calculate the overall risk difference (RD) with a 95 % confidence interval. A random-effects model was applied, as it is the most appropriate approach for studies involving different populations. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test, which evaluates the null hypothesis that all studies share the same effect size; and I2 statistic, which describes the proportion of observed heterogeneity attributable to true variation in effect sizes. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential differences in outcomes based on follow-up periods for permanent teeth.

Assessment of certainty of the evidence using grading of recommendations assessment, development and evaluation reporting bias assessment - GRADE

The certainty of the evidence for each outcome was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach (http://www.gradeworkinggroup.org/). GRADE classifies the certainty of evidence into four levels: very low, low, moderate, and high. High certainty indicates strong confidence that the true effect is close to the estimated effect. On the other extreme, very low certainty suggests little confidence in the estimate effect, meaning the true effect may differ substantially from what was measured [10].

For RCTs, GRADE considers five factors that may lower the certainty of evidence: risk of bias, imprecision, inconsistency, indirectness, and publication bias. Each factor may be rated as having “no limitation” (no downgrade), “serious limitations” (downgrade by one level) or “very serious limitations” (downgrade by two levels). The GRADEpro Guideline Development Tool, which is available online (www.gradepro.org), was used to create a summary of findings table as suggested in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions.

Results

Study selection

The search strategy was initially conducted on March, 2024 and updated on December, 2024. A total of 6251 articles were retrieved. After removing duplicates, 2941 articles were identified. Following the title and abstract screening, a total of 21 articles remained. The flowchart illustrates the study selection process at different stages (Fig. 1).

Full-text articles were obtained, and only 10 RCTs met the inclusion criteria for qualitative assessment. Of these, six studies evaluating primary teeth [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16] and three focused on permanent teeth [3], [17], [18], [19]. Nine studies were excluded for the following reason: 1) the procedures target enamel carious lesions (n = 2) [20], [21]; 2) the primary outcome was dental pain (n = 2) [22], [23]; 3) teeth were sealed using resin-modified glass ionomer cement (n = 1) [12]; 5) overlapping study (n = 1) [18] – only the paper with the longest follow-up period was included [3].

Among these studies, one employed a split-mouth design [11], two followed a parallel design [12], [13] and the remaining did not report their study design [14], [15], [16]. The inclusion criterion for teeth was the presence of carious lesions involving dentin, not exceeding half its thickness, as detected radiographically, and with an enamel opening of ≤ 3–4 mm. The evaluation of carious lesions was conducted by a single examiner in three studies [11], [12], [13], while the remaining studies did not report examiner details [14], [15], [16]. All procedures were performed by a single operator using rubber-dam during both sealing and restorative treatments [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16].

The sealing material was resin sealant in four studies [11], [13], [14], [15] and a flowable resin in two studies [12], [16]. Before sealant application, one study performed the enamel conditioning with phosphoric acid alone [11], while the remaining five studies applied phosphoric acid plus adhesive system [12], [13], [14], [15], [16].

For the group of teeth that received restorations, the protocol included enamel access with high speed rotating burs followed by complete removal of carious dentin tissue with rotating burs and hand instruments in three studies [11], [15], [16] and hand instruments for selective caries removal in three studies [12], [13], [14]. Composite resin was the restorative material in all studies [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], and restorative technique included enamel conditioning with phosphoric acid and the application of bonding agents. All studies assessed the outcomes both clinically and radiographically.

One study employed a parallel design [19], while the others didn’t report [3], [17]. The clinical and radiographic criteria for the carious lesion to be included in the studies follow the same criteria used for primary teeth. The evaluation of carious lesions was conducted by a single researcher in two studies [3], [17], whereas in the third study, the same provider also performed the assessment [19]. Regarding the operators, procedures were performed by dental students and a researcher [3](Alves et al., 2017), while in another [17], the same researcher carried out both procedures and assessments; in the third study, 68 dentists performed the procedures [19].

Two studies reported the use of rubber-dam during sealing and restorative procedures [3], [17], while the third did not specify whether this step was performed (Qvist et al., 2017). The sealing material used was resin sealant in all studies [3], [17], [19]. The sealant application protocols varied: one study used only phosphoric acid [3]; another used phosphoric acid conditioning followed by dehydration of the dental surface with ethanol [17], and the third study stated that the operators followed their usual clinical procedures as per manufacturer instructions [19].

For the restoration procedures, complete caries removal were performed using low-speed rotating burs in one study [3]; another used hand instruments for excavation, but did not specify whether total or selective removal of carious dentin was performed [17]; the third study also performed complete removal, but did not indicate whether rotating burs or hand instruments were used [19]. All studies used composite resin as the restorative material [3], [17]. The restorative protocol involved conditioning with phosphoric acid and use of bonding agents in two studies [3], [17],while in the third study the dentists followed their usual clinical procedures based on instructions from the manufacturers [19]. All studies assessed the outcomes both clinically and radiographically.

Assessment of the risk of bias

At the study level for primary teeth, three studies were considered as having low risk of bias [11], [12], [13], while the remaining three were judged to have high risk of bias [14], [15], [16] (Fig. 2). Although none of the studies reported patient blinding, we considered this limitation unlikely to affect the measured outcomes. Therefore, we deemed the lack of participant blinding as a low-risk factor for bias.

Summary of the risk of bias assessment according to the Cochrane Collaboration tool for deciduous and permanent teeth.

At the study level for permanent teeth, two studies were considered to have low risk of bias [3], [19] and one study was classified as high risk, due to concerns regarding randomization process and deviations from the intended interventions [17] (Fig. 2). As with the primary teeth studies, none of the permanent teeth studies reported patient blinding. However, we assessed this limitation as having a low risk of bias, consistent with our evaluation for deciduous teeth studies.

Meta-analysis

Primary teeth

All studies eligible for systematic review were included in the quantitative analysis [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [24]. Data from caries lesion progression by radiographic evaluation were meta-analyzed, as well as data from the secondary outcome (material retention); formation of tertiary dentin was not reported by any papers. Although meta-analyses were initially planned for different follow-up periods, only one paper presented data from 24 months follow-up. Since a meta-analysis requires the combination of data from at least two studies, we opted to group all available studies into a single meta-analysis. For consistency, only data from the longest follow-up period reported in each study were included in the analysis.

Caries progression

The results from a metanalysis on the data from caries progression in deciduous teeth after a follow-up of 6–24 months showed a risk difference (RD) of 0.02 (95 %CI −0.04 – 0.08; p = 0.54); no superiority of either treatment was observed. No heterogeneity was detected (I2=0 %; p = 0.79) (Fig. 3).

Forest plots of caries lesion progression and material retention in primary teeth.

Material retention

The results from a metanalysis on the data from material retention in deciduous teeth after a follow-up of 6–24 months showed a risk difference (RD) of 0.09 (95 %CI −0.01 – 0.19; p = 0.07) and a low level of heterogeneity was detected (I2=35 %; p = 0.18) (Fig. 3). Again, there is no difference between experimental and control treatment groups.

Permanent teeth

All studies eligible for systematic review were included in the quantitative analysis [3], [17], [19]. Data from caries lesion progression by radiographic evaluation were meta-analyzed, as well as data from the secondary outcomes (material retention and tertiary dentin formation). As eligible studies reported outcomes from different follow-ups, we merged the follow-ups of 6 months to 18 months, 24 months to 36 months and 48 months, to conduct subgroups meta-analyses according to follow-up periods.

Caries progression

In the short-term meta-analysis of 6–18 months, a RD of 0.01 (95 %CI −0.01–0.02; p = 0.39) was observed and heterogeneity was not detected (I2 = 0 %; p = 0.36). In the middle-term meta-analysis of 24–36 month, a RD of 0.02 (95 % CI −0.00–0.04; p = 0.12) was observed; heterogeneity was considered moderate (I2 = 53 %; p = 0.12). In the long-term meta-analysis of 48 months, a RD of −0.02 (95 % CI −0.06–0.02; p = 0.34) were observed, with a low level of heterogeneity (I2 = 30 %; p = 0.23) (Fig. 4). There was no difference between treatments for all the follow-up periods.

Forest plots of caries lesion progression, material retention and tertiary dentin formation in permanent teeth.

Material retention

In the short-term meta-analysis of 6–18 months, a RD of 0.05 (95 % CI −0.03–0.13; p = 0.22) were observed; heterogeneity was considered low (I2=42 %; p = 0.19). In the middle-term meta-analysis of 24–36 month, a RD of 0.06 (95 % CI 0.01–0.10; p = 0.01) was observed; heterogeneity was considered low (I2 = 10 %; p = 0.33). In the long-term meta-analysis of 48 months, a RD of 0.08 (95 % CI 0.04–0.12; p = 0.0002) were observed, with a low level of heterogeneity (I2 = 0 %; p = 0.34) (Fig. 4). No difference between treatments was detect for 6–18 and 48 months follow-ups.

Tertiary dentin formation

In the meta-analysis of 12–48 months, a RD of 0.04 (95 % CI −0.00–0.09; p = 0.06) were observed without difference between groups; heterogeneity level was considered low (I2 = 0 %; p = 0.51) (Fig. 4).

Assessment of the certainty of evidence

The certainty of the evidence for primary teeth was graded as low for both outcomes (caries progression and material retention), considering a follow-up period of 6–24 months. The domains that led to this judgement were the risk of bias of the primary studies and the small sample size of the included papers that didn’t reach the optimal information size for dichotomic data (Table 4).

Discussion

This systematic review found no difference in caries progression or material retention between pit and fissure sealants and restorations after 24 months of follow-up in primary teeth. Similarly, for permanent teeth, no difference was observed in caries progression or tertiary dentin after 48 months; however, material retention was superior for restorations during long-term observation periods (24–36 and 48 months).

Some methodological decisions regarding our systematic review should be highlighted. First, we focused on resin-based pit and fissure sealants, as they demonstrate higher retention rates compared to glass ionomer sealants [25], along with higher resistance to wear and compressive forces, micromechanical adhesion to the tooth structure, and a greater number of available papers, which allowed for a meta-analysis. Additionally, we chose to analyze deciduous and permanent teeth separately. This choice was not based on the structural differences between the two, as it was has been shown that these differences didn’t affect the sealant adhesion [26], but rather due to the shorter lifespan of deciduous teeth, for which lower longevity rates may be acceptable.

Different systematics reviews have indicated that sealing caries lesion in the outer 1/3 of dentin is a suitable procedure when there is no cavity or only a small enamel discontinuity [27], [28], [29]. However, a recent overview [27] assessed the methodological quality of some available systematic reviews as critically low [30], [31] or low [28], [29], highlighting the need of new reviews on the topic, such as ours.

A recent Cochrane review about the theme concluded that there is insuficient evidence to determine whether sealing with sealants is better, worse or equivalent to conventional restorative treatment, restoration with selective caries removal or no treatment [32]. The evidence from this Cochrane review for deciduous teeth is based on only two papers comparing sealant with selective caries removal, involving just 82 teeth [12], [13]. In contrast, we included six studies [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], expanding the sample to 187 teeth. Our findings showed that both procedures are equally effective in halting caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin. However, although our sample size was larger than that of the Cochrane review [32], it was still not large enough to provide a high level of certainty regarding the evidence, and further research is needed to support this finding. A similar result was observed when evaluating the sealant retention in deciduous teeth, after a follow-up period from 6 to 24 months. The obtained risk difference for both outcomes is very small, not statistically significant and the two treatments are comparable, but the certainty of the evidence is low.

When permanent teeth were considered, our results were similar to those found in other systematic reviews conducted by Griffin and collaborators [31] and Schwendicke and collaborators [32], in that both treatment groups had similar performances regarding caries progression. Again, our review was able to include three papers comparing resin pit and fissure sealants and restorations in permanent teeth [3], [17], [19], while the aforementioned systematic review [32] included only one study [3]. As for the secondary outcome “material retention”, we observed that dental restorations performed better than dental sealants over 48 months of follow-up. However, for permanent teeth, this period may be considered relatively short compared to the data on composite restorations, which have follow-up periods extending up to 30 years [33]. For permanent teeth, the certainty of evidence is higher than for deciduous teeth, and it was classified as moderate.

Considering that it is expected some loss of the sealants after 48 months, a logical question arises: is there a need to re-seal the teeth? This is particularly relevant because the sealed teeth already have caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin and our main goal is to keep the lesion isolated from the biofilm and the nutrients. Unfortunately, we cannot address this question with the data obtained in this systematic review. There are no papers on permanent teeth that report resealing of a completely or partially lost sealant in this specific situation, nor longer follow-up periods to evaluate the potential progression of carious lesions without resealing. However, there is one about deciduous teeth [13], which reported that the carious lesions didn’t progress in any of the treated teeth. It must be emphasized that those deciduous teeth were partial or total losses occurred, the teeth were preventively resealed. This strategy may serve as a basis for future research protocols in permanent dentition.

Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that even with sealant loss in permanent teeth, caries progression rates remained similar between sealant and restoration groups. One possible explanation is that the resin sealant penetrated the pits and fissures, and despite its clinical loss, residual resin remains at the bottom of the fissures, similar to what has been observed with glass ionomer sealants [34]. These remnants could potentially block nutrient entry, keeping the dentin lesion isolated from the oral environment, allowing its arrestment. However, this remains speculative, and further research is needed to substantiate this hypothesis. The application of sealant modifies the anatomy of the fissure, facilitating mechanical biofilm removal at the fissure entrance. This physical alteration may also contribute to lesion arrest by reducing biofilm accumulation and maintaining the lesion’s isolation from cariogenic challenges. In addition, behavioral factors—such as oral hygiene practices, dietary habits, and adherence to preventive care—are also determinants in the caries process and they play a pivotal role in halting lesion progression.

A third outcome evaluated was the formation of tertiary dentin. This analysis was only possible for permanent teeth, as no studies about deciduous teeth addressed this topic. Our findings suggest that tertiary dentin formation can be induced by both dental procedures. However, the certainty of the evidence was judged as low, primarily due to the small sample and the risk of bias in one included study [17]. When analyzing the individual studies included in the meta-analysis [3], [17], both highlighted that tertiary dentin formation is a common occurrence after dental sealing, though it may take time before to become detectable on radiographic exams. One study used radiographic subtraction as a method to quantify mineral deposition after cavity sealing over shorter periods [3], offering an alternative approach for early detection.

That being said, until new diagnostic tools become available to confirm whether carious lesions are completely sealed, visual inspection remains the primary method of assessment. From this perspective sealant integrity is an important outcome.

Annual failure rates of restorations range from 0.08 % to 6.3 % [35], while the mean failure rate of pit and fissure sealants retention in permanent teeth are 19.0 % at two years, increasing substantially over time (36.4 % at four years; 61 % at nine years) [28]. A similar increase in sealant failure rates can be expected in deciduous teeth as well [26]. Therefore, it is reasonable to conclude that using sealants to manage caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin requires close monitoring and good patient compliance with follow-up visits. This observation is a common statement in papers on this topic [3], [13], and we support this, since the isolation from the cariogenic environment is the core condition to guarantee the defensive responses from the dentin-pulp complex, the mineral deposition and the formation of tertiary dentin.

It has already been observed that the need for retreatment is significantly higher in lesions that were treated with pit and fissure sealant compared to those that received noninvasive or minimally invasive treatments in permanent teeth [29]. However, use of resin sealants on caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin can postpone and even prevent restorative treatment, provided the sealant remains intact and adhered to the tooth [3], [17]. For deciduous teeth, the possibility of avoiding dental restorations is even more interesting, as their shorter life cycle reduces the likelihood of requiring retreatments before exfoliation. From a Pediatric Dentistry perspective, this can be considered a more “child-friendly” approach. In case of permanent teeth, sealing caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin may help delay or even prevent the tooth from entering the so-called “tooth death spiral” [5], ultimately enhancing its longevity and functional lifespan.

Given these observations, it is fair to state that pit and fissure sealants can serve as an alternative to dental restorations for caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin, provided that the enamel opening does not exceed 3–4 mm. In addition to halting lesion progression, the sealing procedure is less time-consuming and less invasive as it does not require dental tissue removal, anesthesia, and may reduce the patient’s anxiety levels. Also, it can be speculate that the use of pit and fissure sealants may be more cost-effective than conventional restorative treatment. Unfortunately, no studies have specifically evaluated this aspect. Existing research on the cost-effectiveness of sealants for caries prevention has demonstrated favorable results in high-risk populations compared to non-sealing [36], [37], primarily because the cost of restoration is higher than that of sealant application [28]. Since teeth with caries lesions in the outer 1/3 of dentin sealed with pit and fissure sealants may require resealing over time, this remains a crucial area for future research.

This review has some limitations. One of the concerns is the relatively small number of datasets included in the analysis. In most cases, the sample size didn’t reach the optimal information size threshold, reducing the certainty of the evidence. Additionally, we didn’t assess the performance of different brands of pit and fissures sealants or the potential influence of dentin adhesives before sealing, due to the limited number of available studies.

On the other side, our review had well-defined inclusion criteria, focusing specifically on resin pit and fissure compared to dental restorations. This approach eliminated potential bias from glass ionomer cement, which exhibits a significantly different clinical behavior. Furthermore, we included papers not only in English, but also in Portuguese, which expanded the sample compared to other similar reviews.

Finally, we strongly encourage further high-quality studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods to validate and strengthen the findings of this review.

Conclusion

It is concluded that there is no difference in caries progression when lesions in the outer half of dentin, whether in deciduous or permanent teeth, were treated with either composite resin restoration or resin-based sealant. However, this conclusion is based on low-certainty evidence for deciduous teeth and moderate-certainty evidence for permanent teeth, and therefore it should be interpreted with caution.
