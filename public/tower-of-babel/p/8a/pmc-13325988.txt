Impact of resistance training for lowering blood pressure in older adults
Authors: Deepika Laddu, Xuan Cai, Adam D Gepner, Christopher W Sundberg, Norinna B Allen, Jun Ma, Shane A Phillips
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe INERTIA study evaluated the preliminary effects of a 12-week progressive resistance training (PRT) intervention on changes in blood pressure (BP) in older adults.MethodsOlder adults (≥60 years) with low muscle strength and no diagnosed CVD were randomized (2:1) to 12 weeks of PRT (n=50) or assessment-only control (n=21). The primary outcome was between-group difference in 12-week changes in systolic (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP). Secondary outcomes were changes in muscle strength (chest press, leg press), and physical function (short-physical performance battery, SPPB). Two-way repeated measures ANOVA assessed between-group effects (LS mean) and within-group changes.ResultsSeventy-one participants (mean (SD); age, 70.1 (6.3) years); 76% female, 43% non-white; 42% on antihypertensive therapy) enrolled, and 61 completed follow-up. Baseline SBP/DBP was 130 (20.2)/82 (12.3) mmHg. Compared with control, PRT produced greater reductions for DBP (difference in change, −4.2 mmHg; 95% CI -8.3, -0.1), but not for SBP (−5.4 mmHg; 95% CI -13.0, 2.2). Within the PRT group, SBP (mean change -5.6 mmHg; 95% CI -11.1, -0.1) and DBP (−3.9 mmHg; 95% CI -6.8, -0.9) decreased significantly, with minimal change in controls. Strength gains (chest press, 11.0 kg; leg press, 63.6 kg) were greater in PRT than controls (all p<0.001), while between-group improvements in SPPB (PRT: 2 points, controls: 1.2 points) were not significant.ConclusionIn older adults, PRT significantly increased muscle strength and reduced DBP beyond controls, with a trend towards clinically meaningful improvements in SBP and physical function. Larger, adequately powered trials are warranted to confirm these findings.Keywords: Older adults, Hypertension, Resistance training, Blood pressure, Muscle strength

Introduction

Nearly 70% of U.S. adults aged ≥60 years have elevated blood pressure (BP) or hypertension, a prevalence that is expected to rise as older adults become the nation’s largest demographic by 2030 [1]. Although achieving guideline-recommended BP targets substantially reduces CVD events and mortality [2], BP control in older adults remains suboptimal [3]. This challenge is compounded by age-related declines in muscle strength and function, which commonly coexist with frailty and sarcopenia, and are independently associated with hypertension and increased CVD risk [[4], [5], [6]]. Low muscle strength is consistently associated with high BP, greater hypertension prevalence, and increased CVD risk [7,8]. Older adults with low muscle strength represent a particularly vulnerable group, in whom reduced physical activity, declining functional capacity, and worsening cardiovascular health potentiate one another [9]. Interventions that target muscle strength may therefore provide a clinically relevant, non-pharmacological approach to improving BP control while simultaneously addressing functional limitations common in later life.

Resistance training has emerged as an effective lifestyle intervention for BP reduction [10], with meta-analyses and randomized control trials demonstrating meaningfully reductions in systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) are comparable to those achieved with aerobic exercise [[11], [12], [13]]. However, most existing evidence is derived from studies from generally healthy, young to middle-aged adults [12,[14], [15], [16], [17]], with considerable heterogeneity across training prescriptions and limited representation of older individuals with chronically low muscle strength [11,18]. Progressive resistance training (PRT), defined by systematic increases in external load to induce strength adaptations, has not been consistently delivered or evaluated in older, deconditioned populations with elevated CVD risk. Although aerobic exercise remains the cornerstone for BP management, older adults with low muscle strength often face unique barriers to sustained aerobic exercise participation, including limited exercise tolerance and functional constraints [10]. In this context, supervised PRT may offer a feasible and functionally relevant alternative that simultaneously targets BP control and strength [10,19].

The INERTIA (Impact of Interventional Exercise and Resistance Training on Blood Pressure Control In Older Adults) trial was designed as a pilot study to evaluate the preliminary effects of a 12-week PRT intervention on changes in BP, muscle strength and physical function in older adults with low muscle strength and elevated blood pressure or hypertension. We hypothesized that PRT would result in greater BP reductions at 12 weeks compared to controls, and that secondary improvements in muscle strength and function would accompany BP reductions.

Methods

Overview

The INERTIA trial was designed as a parallel-assignment randomized controlled (RCT) trial with the primary outcome as a change in systolic (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) after 12-weeks, comparing the PRT condition with assessment-only controls (Supplement Fig. 1). Secondary outcomes included changes in muscle strength, and physical function. The full INERTIA protocol is described elsewhere [20].

This study was conducted at the University of Illinois, Chicago (UIC). Participants were recruited from February 01, 2020 to July 31, 2023, with the first participant randomized on March 06, 2020 and data collection occurring through December 18, 2023. Recruitment was paused for approximately 6 months (March 2020-October 2020) due to COVID-19–related shutdowns, with ongoing restrictions in recruitment throughout the study period. This study was approved by UIC institutional review board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Population

Ambulatory, community-dwelling, older adults (≥60 years old) were recruited from both clinical and community settings. Specific eligibility criteria are listed in Table 1. Consented participants underwent a two-step screening process: first, cognitive function was assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment, with scores ≥22, adjusted for years of education, indicating eligibility; second, grip strength (Jamar dynamometer) was measured to identify clinically meaningful weakness, using established operational definitions for men (<30kg) and women (<20kg) [21], based on the average of two trials. Participants meeting both criteria proceeded to baseline testing.

Study visits

Eligible participants completed baseline assessments in a research laboratory over two experimental visits, returning at 12 weeks to re-assess outcomes. (Supplement Fig. 1). Participants assigned to the PRT intervention returned for an additional follow-up at 6 weeks to evaluate progress. Participants were asked to take their usual anti-hypertensive medications as prescribed and refrain from caffeine and physical exercise for 24 h before the visit.

SBP and DBP were measured in duplicate in the upper arm with an automated BP monitor (Omron Healthcare 3 Series, BP7100; Japan), following 5-10 minutes of seated rest and 1 minute interval between measurements. If SBP differed by >5 mmHg between readings, a third measurement was taken, and the average of the two closest measures was recorded [22].

Participants then completed the short physical performance battery (SPPB), comprising of three tests to assess lower extremity physical function was administered: a 4-meter walk at usual pace; time to complete 5 unassisted chair stands using a straight-backed, nonpadded, flat-seated, armless chair; and, 3 standing balance tests, each held for 10 seconds with the stances progressing in difficulty [23]. Each test was scored on a 0 to 4 scale and summed for a total score range of 0 to 12, with 0 indicating the lowest physical performance, and scores of 12 indicating the highest performance [23].

On visit 2, participants completed standardized strength testing for chest press and leg-press to determine the 1-RM. Testing followed established guidelines and was conducted on pressurized resistance machines under the supervision of a certified exercise physiologist [24]. The 1-RM was repeated at 12-weeks for all participants, and was used in the PRT group to individualize training loads, with a mid-intervention reassessment at week 6 to readjust training prescriptions.

Other covariates

Anthropometrics, including body mass index (BMI,) calculated as weight (kg) divided by height (m2), waist and hip circumference were obtained by trained study staff following standardized procedures [25]. Appendicular lean mass (kg/m2), representing the sum of lean body mass of the upper—and lower extremities, was estimated by dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry. Participants reported demographic and behavioral characteristics, and medical history, including prior diagnosis or treatment of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, thyroid disease, and musculoskeletal disorders. Participants brought current medications (prescription or non-prescription), which staff recorded on a medical inventory form. Administered questionnaires assessed depressive symptoms (Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D), [26] physical function (RAND-36 Short-Form Physical Function) [27], and physical activity level (CHAMPS) [28] to characterize the study sample.

Randomization

A randomization scheme was generated by a computer algorithm in REDCap using permuted block method with stratification on baseline BMI and biological sex to ensure allocation concealment. After completing all baseline assessments, participants were randomized in a 2:1 allocation procedure to 12-weeks of supervised PRT, or the assessment-only control group to maximize intervention exposure, given limited existing data on PRT-related BP outcomes in older adults. By design, participants were aware of their treatment assignments, as was the exercise trainer and select study staff who scheduled visits; however, the investigators, outcome assessors, and data analyst remained blinded throughout the trial and analysis.

Intervention

The PRT intervention followed guideline recommendations for older adults, available at the time(24,29) with the full protocol published previously [20]. Briefly, participants came to a research gym on two non-consecutive days per week to complete a ∼45 minute supervised PRT session. BP and heart rate were assessed before and after each session to ensure absence of exercise contraindications. During each session, participants completed upper—and lower-body exercises within the same session, in an alternating sequence (e.g., chest press, seated leg press, seated latissimus pull-down, knee/leg extension, shoulder press, leg curls, and calf-raise exercises) to allow adequate recovery of the working muscle groups between successive exercises [29]. All exercises were completed using pressurized weight machines, except calf raises which were body weight only.

Training intensity progressed gradually following overload principles to optimize strength gains [24]. Load was increased by 5%-10% when a participant completed ≥12 repetitions in at least 2 sets, after which repetitions were reduced. Training intensity was further guided by participants’ relative perceived level of exertion using the Borg scale [30]. The goal was for participants to progress toward—and maintain a moderate-to-high intensity workload (∼80% of 1-RM) for 3 sets of 8–12 repetitions/set by 12 weeks (Supplement Fig. 1).

During the initial weeks, prescriptions were designed to allow for musculoskeletal adaptation and emphasized proper technique to minimize excessive muscle soreness and injury risk. Participants began training at a low-to-moderate intensity (40-50% of baseline 1-RM for 2 sets of 10-15 repetitions/set); increased load in week 2 (60-65% 1-RM), progressed to 3 sets of 8-12 repetitions/set by week 3 and advanced to 70-75% 1-RM during weeks 4-5. From weeks 6−12, emphasis was placed on progressing toward or maintaining 80% 1-RM for 3 sets of 8-12 repetitions/set. Sets were performed to the point of muscle fatigue but not failure in order to avoid overexertion [29]. Following weighted exercise, participants completed up to 25 bodyweight calf-raises to promote muscular power and endurance. Rest intervals between sets ranged from 2-5 minutes, depending on the participant’s functional status.

Assessment-only controls (AO-C)

Control participants received exercise educational material developed by the National Institutes on Aging Go4Life® every 3 weeks over 12 weeks. Materials included handouts on non-specific at-home exercises focused on endurance, balance, strengthening, and flexibility, and an exercise log for recording daily activities (type and duration). Participants received a pre-stamped envelope for returning completed logs; however, no reminders were issued by staff to prompt their return.

Intervention process measures

Feasibility was defined as the number of participants who completed the primary outcome assessment at 12-weeks (i.e. number completed/number randomized). Intervention adherence, defined as exercise session completion, was calculated as the number of exercise sessions completed divided by the number of sessions expected (24 total). Participants randomized to PRT who did not initiate the intervention due to COVID-19 disruptions were excluded from this calculation; ≥80% session completion was considered feasible. Acceptability and safety were also evaluated to determine whether proceeding to a larger trial is warranted. Acceptability was assessed weekly using a 5-item Likert-scale [[1], [2], [3], [4]], with a mean score ≥3.0 indicating acceptability. Safety was monitored by tracking adverse events associated with the study, reported within 24 hours and during supervised sessions.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall, and for intervention and control participants. Continuous variables with normal distribution were presented as the mean (standard deviation), while skewed continuous variables were presented as a median with the interquartile range. Categorical variables were presented as percentages. Between-group differences were assessed using Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used to assess covariate balance, providing a sample-size-independent measure of baseline comparability between groups.

Spearman correlation coefficients (ρ) were used to examine bivariate associations between muscle and physical function measures and SBP and DBP at baseline (week 0), week 12, and for the change over the 12-week intervention period. The primary analysis focused on BP outcomes (SBP or DBP, mmHg) at week 12, using an intention-to-treat approach, with baseline values carried forward to conservatively account for missing week-12 BP data. Between-group differences in BP change from baseline to week 12 and within-group change in the intervention and control groups were evaluated using two-way repeated measures ANOVA with Tukey-Kramer adjustment for multiple comparisons. Primary treatment effects were reported as unadjusted least-squares mean differences with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Similar analyses were applied to secondary outcomes assessing muscle strength and physical function. To evaluate the influence of individual covariates on estimated treatment effects, we also fit separate models: adjustment for sex and BMI (randomization covariates); adjustment for baseline value of the respective outcome to reduce bias from baseline imbalances; and, for BP outcomes, adjustment for anti-hypertensive therapy (yes/no) to account for potential confounding by medication use.

In secondary post-hoc analyses, the temporal changes in BP among PRT group were examined using linear mixed effects models for repeated measures, with time (baseline, 6-weeks, 12-weeks) as the repeated-measures factor and individuals as the random-effects term. Effect modification was assessed in subgroups defined by antihypertensive medication use (yes and no) via two-way repeated measures ANOVA (PRT and controls) or linear mixed model (PRT only) with Tukey-Kramer adjustment and further adjustment for sex, baseline BMI and BP. All analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc), and R, version 4.5.1 (The R Foundation). All tests were 2 sided and statistical significance was defined as p-value < 0.05.

Results

Fig. 1 displays the CONSORT diagram showing participant flow through the study. We approached 333 individuals for participation; 177 (53.2%) responded, and were eligible for MoCA and grip strength screening. Of these 90 (50.8%) consented and 77 were randomized to either the PRT or control conditions. Five participants randomized prior to the state-mandated shutdown period withdrew due to pandemic-related changes in household circumstances, and one withdrew after requesting additional compensation. The final analytic sample included 71 participants, among whom 61 (79%) completed 12-week assessments. Exercise session completion rate was 82%. The majority of participants reported high intervention acceptability (average score of 4 ‘strongly agree’) across 4 domains: 1) the exercises were fun; 2) exercises were worth my time; 3) directions for the exercises were clear; and 4) I received guidance I needed from the research staff. No adverse events related to study participation were reported.

Across baseline and week 12 visits, muscle strength showed weak, non-significant correlations with SBP and DBP (Supplemental Table 2). SPPB scores at week 12 were inversely associated with changes in SBP, and 12-week changes in SPPB were inversely associated with changes in both SBP and DBP change. In contrast, DXA-derived appendicular lean mass showed consistent, moderate positive correlations with BP outcomes at baseline and week 12 in both the PRT and control groups.

Primary outcome patterns

At 12-weeks, mean SBP decreased by −5.6 mmHg (95% CI -11.1, -0.1) in the PRT group and by −0.17 mmHg (95% CI -8.6, 8.3) in the control group, with no between-group differences in change (−5.4, 95% CI -13.0, 2.2). Average DBP followed a similar pattern with mean reductions of −3.9 mmHg (95% CI -6.8, -0.9) in the PRT group, nonsignificant changes in the control group 0.33 mmHg (95% CI -4.2, 4.9), but with between-group differences in DBP change favoring PRT group (−4.2 mmHg, 95% CI -8.3, -0.1; p=0.045) over the control group (Fig. 2). Estimated effects for SBP and DBP remained unchanged after adjustment for demographic variables, baseline measures and antihypertensive therapy (Supplement Table 3).

Temporal changes in BP within the PRT group are shown in Supplement Table 4. Mean changes in BP within the PRT group were evident by 6 weeks and remained significant at 12 weeks as compared to baseline. Linear mixed-effects models showed SBP decreased within the PRT group by −5.8 mmHg (95% CI −10.7, −0.90) and DBP by −4.3 mmHg (95% CI −6.8, −1.7) over 12 weeks, with only minimal attenuation in adjusted models, (all p<0.05).

Interactions between intervention groups (PRT vs. control) and baseline antihypertensive therapy use, BP control status, and time were not statistically significant, indicating no evidence of effect modification (Supplement Table 5).

Changes in secondary muscle strength and physical function

Compared with controls, PRT resulted in greater gains in chest press 1RM (difference in change 11.0 kg; 95% CI, 5.3, 16.7), and leg press 1RM (difference in change, 63.6 kg; 95% CI, 35.6, 91.7), with significant group x visit interactions for both outcomes (both p≤0.001; Table 3). Between-group differences in grip strength and SPPB scores were not statistically significant; however, both groups showed significant within-group improvements over 12 weeks. In the PRT group, grip strength increased by 4.2 kg (95% CI, 2.0, 6.3) and SPPB scores improved by 2.0 points (95% CI, 1.3, 2.7). Controls also demonstrated improvements in grip strength (5.0 kg; 95% CI, 1.7, 8.2) and SPPB scores (1.2 points; 95% CI, 0.1, 2.3). No changes in muscle mass were observed in the intervention or control group .

Discussion

To our knowledge, INERTIA is among the first RCT to evaluate the BP lowering potential of a resistance training intervention in older adults specifically selected for clinically defined low muscle strength, using established cut-offs. In this single-site pilot study, feasibility was supported by high adherence, with 79% retention, and strong acceptability ratings. PRT significantly reduced both SBP and DBP, but between-group differences were significant only for DBP. PRT also improved upper- and lower-body muscle strength and lower extremity physical function. Overall, these findings indicate PRT is a feasible, acceptable, and potentially effective non-pharmacological strategy for lowering DBP in older adults, warranting further evaluation in a larger, fully-powered randomized trial.

PRT is increasingly recognized as promising lifestyle strategy for BP management [10,31]. However, its independent effects in older adults with significant muscle deconditioning [32], remains unclear. In the context of persistent challenges in BP control in late life, and the established association between low muscle strength and BP dysregulation [33], this pilot trial evaluated the feasibility and preliminary BP effects of PRT in older adults with low muscle strength. Overall, PRT was well-accepted and resulted in significant reductions in DBP over 12 weeks (–4.2 mmHg versus controls, –4.3 mmHg from baseline), consistent with prior randomized trials of resistance training in middle-aged to older adults with untreated elevated BP or hypertension [14,34]. For example, Heffernan et al. [34] reported DBP reductions of ∼ 6mmHg in 21 older adults following 12 weeks of PRT, while Banks et al., observed reductions of ∼5mmHg after 9 weeks of structured PRT [14]. Results from meta-analyses in older adults [18,35,36], including those receiving antihypertensive therapy, similarly demonstrate average DBP reduction ∼3mmHg and 4 mmHg in pre-hypertensive and hypertensive populations, respectively [36]. Notably, in INERTIA, adjustment for antihypertensive medication use did not modify our findings, and effect modification was not observed. Although clinically meaningful reductions in SBP were observed in the PRT group, between-group differences (–5.4 mmHg) did not reach statistical significance, and within-group reductions (–5.6 mmHg), while significant, should be interpreted as exploratory given the pilot design, and baseline SBP imbalance between groups. This pattern mirrors prior exercise trial by Stewart et al, [37] who reported similar significant with-in group SBP reductions following 6 months of combined aerobic and resistance training, without corresponding between-group differences. From a prevention perspective, the observed DBP reductions and favorable SBP trends in INERTIA are clinically promising as even modest reductions in SBP (∼5 mmHg) or DBP (∼ 3 mmHg) in adults aged >65 years are associated with ∼9% decrease in CVD risk [38]. Together, these findings support the potential of PRT as an adjunct for BP management in late life, while underscoring the need for adequately powered trials to confirm SBP effects.

Despite the well-established health benefits of PRT, fewer than 25% of older adults meet national recommendations for muscle strengthening activities, with chronic conditions recognized as common barriers [39]. This gap highlights the need for interventions that are feasible for older adults with low muscle strength and functional limitations. The updated American Heart Association Resistance Training Guidelines currently recommend incorporating eight to ten resistance training exercises on at least 2 days a week, with intensity progression prescriptions for older adults to promote gains or preserve muscle strength and function over time [10]. In INERTIA, PRT was associated with marked improvements in chest (+11.0 kg) leg strength (+63.6 kg) relative to controls, consistent with robust trial evidence that PRT effectively improves muscle strength and functional performance in older adults [40]. In contrast to strength gains, changes in muscle mass were minimal, which is not unexpected given that a 12-week intervention may be insufficient to induce meaningful hypertrophy, even if it can mitigate age-related declines. Rather, in older adults, PRT more reliably improves muscle strength, power and function, likely reflecting preserved neuromuscular adaptability with aging [19]. Although between-group differences in lower extremity function were not statistically significant, the magnitude of improvement favored PRT: SPPB scores increased by an average of 2 points in the PRT group—nearly double the gain observed in controls (+1.2 points). Improvements observed in controls may reflect study related activity changes (e.g., increased activity prompted by study participation, engagement with exercise education materials), consistent with their modest, nonsignificant increases in CHAMPS scores at 12 weeks (1572.49 kcal; 95% CI −857.7, 4002.7). Notably, participants in both groups started with moderate functional status—levels that our group has shown to be associated with higher risk for adverse cardiovascular and mortality outcomes [[41], [42], [43]]. With a 0.5-1 point increase in SPPB regarded as clinically meaningful [44], the observed gains highlight the potential value of PRT for enhancing functional capacity in this population. As muscle weakness and functional limitations increasingly intersect with cardiovascular risk in older adults [45], their concurrent improvements alongside BP in the PRT group underscore PRT’s potential as an integrated strategy for muscle health and BP management.

Promoting long-term uptake of PRT among older adults presents unique challenges compared with aerobic training, as PRT often requires equipment, supervision, technical instruction to safely perform exercises—barriers that may be amplified among individuals with low muscle strength and functional limitations [10,19]. Although the focus of INERTIA was placed on improving BP, by targeting individuals with clinically defined muscle weakness, INERTIA closely aligned recruitment and engagement strategies with patient-centered goals such as improving function, mobility and independence, which likely contributed to high intervention acceptability. Practical supports—including transportation compensation, flexible scheduling, and consistent participant engagement efforts (e.g., use of INERTIA study branding, birthday cards, monthly progress letters, quarterly newsletters) sent to all participants, helped address common access and time-related barriers and supported adherence. Although long-term exercise behavior was not assessed, participants’ preference for supervised, in-person delivery highlights the importance of structured support for overcoming perceived complexity and safety concerns in this population. Future trials should test scalable delivery models with integrated behavioral support strategies to enhance adoption and maintenance of PRT.

This pilot study has notable limitations. Baseline BP levels were heterogeneous, and antihypertensive medication use was allowed to enhance feasibility; however, medication changes after the baseline visit were not tracked and, although unlikely, may have influenced BP outcomes. Eligibility was based on low muscle strength (exposure) rather than high BP, which may have reduced population specificity and constrained the magnitude of observed BP changes. Additionally, the modest sample size may have limited statistical power to detect significant between-group differences despite clinically meaningful reductions in SBP. Many participants had BP values near guideline-recommended targets at baseline, potentially limiting further reductions. Future trials incorporating ambulatory blood pressure monitoring may better characterize day-to-day BP adaptations to PRT, beyond single clinic-based assessments obtained pre-and post-intervention. Psychological and self-reported functional measures were collected at baseline and post-intervention, but were not analyzed in the present report; these outcomes will be examined in a future secondary analysis to further characterize the effects of PRT beyond physiological outcomes. Attrition was higher than anticipated (21%), with about one-third of withdrawals due to COVID-19 disruptions that likely affected participants’ ability and willingness to participate.

Despite these limitations, feasibility outcomes, including retention and adherence, were consistent with those reported in other exercise studies of older community-dwelling adults [[46], [47], [48]], particularly after accounting for those who withdrew during the pandemic-related shutdowns. While quality evidence on retention remains limited in older adult populations [49], acceptability, adherence and exercise completion in INERTIA were high. Additional strengths include recruitment of a diverse, high-risk population, delivery of a structured yet personalized training protocol with high scalability potential, and inclusion of an attention−control group.

In conclusion, INERTIA provides preliminary evidence that PRT is a feasible and promising lifestyle intervention that can produce clinically meaningful reductions in DBP in older adults with low muscle strength. The observed improvements in muscle strength and function further support the relevance of targeting muscle health as part of a comprehensive BP management strategy in aging populations. A larger, adequately powered trial is warranted to confirm DBP effects and more definitively evaluate benefits for SBP and physical function.
