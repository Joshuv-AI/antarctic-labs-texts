Early Experience With Beta-Lactam Therapeutic Drug Monitoring in an Outpatient Parenteral Antimicrobial Therapy (OPAT) Program
Authors: Kemar O Barrett, Christina G Rivera, Omar Abu Saleh, Margaret Pertzborn, Sara E Ausman, Andrew D Rule, Kellie Arensman Hannan, Ryan W Stevens, Erin F Barreto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe early experience with beta-lactam therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) in a high-volume outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy program. In selected patients, TDM informed dose optimization and toxicity mitigation. Illustrative cases highlight feasibility and emerging lessons supporting beta-lactam TDM in outpatient care.

Outpatient parenteral antimicrobial therapy (OPAT) allows patients to receive prolonged intravenous antimicrobial treatment outside the hospital and is widely recognized as an effective and cost-efficient model of care. However, adverse events remain common during OPAT, necessitating close monitoring and individualized management [1, 2]. Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) is routinely used for select antimicrobials such as vancomycin and aminoglycosides, but remains underutilized for beta-lactam antibiotics, particularly in outpatient settings [1, 3].

Beta-lactam pharmacokinetics can be highly variable, especially among patients with extremes of body weight, fluctuating kidney function, and/or acute illness [3, 4]. This variability contributes to unpredictable dose-response relationships and increases the need for individualized dosing strategies. In the OPAT setting, beta-lactam optimization is further challenged by care transitions that may fragment clinical oversight, limited laboratory access that can delay or preclude TDM, and reduced ability to promptly recognize toxicity or treatment failure. Despite growing interest in beta-lactam TDM, practical experience describing its implementation and clinical impact in OPAT remains limited [1, 4]. Recent OPAT-focused analyses have highlighted the potential for precision dosing but include only small numbers of beta-lactam–treated patients, underscoring the need for additional real-world data [5].

Here we describe early real-world experience with beta-lactam TDM in an established OPAT program, with emphasis on patient selection, feasibility, and clinical impact, illustrated through representative clinical cases.

METHODS

Study Design and Patient Selection

We describe a retrospective, single-center case series of adult patients (≥18 years) enrolled in a high-volume OPAT program (typical daily census > 300 patients) who underwent beta-lactam TDM between April 2023 and August 2025. Eligible patients received cefepime, piperacillin–tazobactam, or meropenem during OPAT. Beta-lactam TDM was not strictly protocolized; instead, testing was performed at the discretion of the infectious diseases (ID) care team (including ID pharmacists) for select patients including those planning to discharge (ie, within 1 week).

Sample Collection and Laboratory Testing

Beta-lactam serum concentrations were obtained prior to hospital discharge or during routine OPAT laboratory monitoring. In the inpatient setting, sample collection and coordination were performed as part of pharmacist standard TDM workflows. Sample timing was coordinated with patients, home infusion services, and outpatient laboratories, with collection typically occurring near the end of a dosing interval or at prespecified time points to inform pharmacokinetic interpretation. Concentrations were assayed by the Mayo Clinic Clinical Mass Spectrometry laboratory which has established beta-lactam testing capability [6].

OPAT Team Workflow and TDM Interpretation

Beta-lactam TDM was coordinated by an ID pharmacist, who placed laboratory orders, verified sample timing, reviewed results, and adjusted medication orders (when relevant) via collaborative practice agreement. Drug concentrations were interpreted using organism-specific MICs, patient-specific clinical factors, and institutionally developed pharmacokinetic tools. Recommendations were communicated to the OPAT medical team and documented in the electronic health record using a standardized pharmacist note. Clinical impact was described with any TDM-informed intervention, including changes in antimicrobial dose, dosing frequency, infusion strategy, or agent selection, as well as identification of subtherapeutic or supratherapeutic exposure prompting enhanced clinical monitoring.

RESULTS

During the study period, 1160 OPAT patients received cefepime, meropenem, or piperacillin–tazobactam, of whom 35 (3.0%) underwent TDM. Therapeutic drug monitoring was most frequently performed for patients receiving cefepime (21/861 cefepime-treated patients; 60% of TDM episodes), followed by piperacillin–tazobactam (8/211 piperacillin/tazobactam-treated patients; 23% of TDM episodes) and meropenem (6/162 meropenem-treated patients; 17% of TDM episodes). Detailed characteristics of TDM utilization, indications, and associated interventions stratified by beta-lactam agent are summarized in Table 1. Of the 35 TDM episodes, 19 (54%) were performed prior to hospital discharge and 16 (46%) were obtained after transition to the outpatient setting. Among patients who underwent TDM prior to discharge, levels were obtained a median of 41 hours before discharge. Most patients were discharged to home with home infusion (57%). Musculoskeletal and orthopedic infections accounted for most clinical syndromes (49%) in patients who underwent TDM. Other infection types included abdominopelvic (17%), pulmonary (11%), bacteremia (11%), and SSTI and other infections (2% each). Microbiologic data were available in 83% of cases, with Pseudomonas species most frequently isolated.

The most common indications for TDM included extremes of weight, unstable kidney function, and suspected beta-lactam toxicity (each 23%). One patient had more than one documented indication for TDM. The mean turnaround time from sample collection to result availability was 1.2 days, and TDM-informed interventions were implemented at a mean of 1.6 days from sample collection. Post-TDM antimicrobial regimen adjustments were made in 18 patients (51%), most commonly involving changes in dosage (15%) or frequency of administration (26%). Less frequently, TDM prompted changes in infusion time or substitution with an alternative agent (6%). Subtherapeutic (39%) and supratherapeutic (22%) drug exposure were the most common drivers of regimen modification. Regimen changes were also attributed to adverse effects, changes in renal function, or other clinical considerations (each 17%).

Overall, beta-lactam therapy was completed for the intended duration in 27 patients (77%), while 8 (23%) patients discontinued therapy early. Reasons for early discontinuation included patient-related factors (n = 4; declining further intravenous therapy, or transition to hospice), abnormal laboratory parameters (n = 2; new-onset neutropenia with absolute neutrophil count < 500 cells/µL and acute interstitial nephritis), and microbiologic factors (n = 2; suboptimal beta-lactam exposure in the setting of elevated MICs and microbiologic failure).

DISCUSSION

Herein, we describe real-world experience with beta-lactam TDM in an OPAT program, highlighting patient selection, clinical impact, and feasibility through illustrative cases. Although beta-lactam TDM is well described in inpatient settings, its application in OPAT remains limited, in part due to logistical complexity and uncertainty regarding patient selection [1, 4, 7]. Our experience suggests that targeted beta-lactam TDM can meaningfully inform clinical decision-making in OPAT, particularly when safety concerns, pharmacokinetic variability, or elevated organism MICs are present.

Determination of the optimal OPAT patient for use of beta-lactam TDM is important. In our series, TDM was most frequently performed in patients with suspected beta-lactam toxicity, extremes of body weight, unstable renal function, or concern for inadequate drug exposure due to elevated MICs. These indications reflect scenarios in which population-based dosing is less reliable and the clinical consequences of under- or overexposure may be magnified in the outpatient setting. Notably, TDM was commonly performed during transitions of care, underscoring its role in supporting safe discharge planning and early outpatient dose optimization and highlighting the need for careful collaboration between inpatient and outpatient care teams.

Clinical impact, defined as an intervention taken by the care team in response to drug level results, was observed in more than half of patients undergoing TDM, most often taking the form of adjustments to the dosage or frequency of administration. The 4 illustrative cases provided demonstrate both safety- and efficacy-driven applications of TDM in this specific care context.

Feasibility is a key consideration for beta-lactam TDM in OPAT. In the outpatient setting, patients were referred to a Mayo Clinic laboratory site for sample collection, and no home-based collection was performed. Medication administration at a Mayo Clinic infusion center was not required; however, the timing of doses and sample collection was carefully coordinated by the OPAT pharmacist through communication with patients, caregivers, and nursing facility teams when applicable. Turnaround times for TDM results and subsequent interventions were short, supporting the practicality of integrating TDM into OPAT workflows when available through a local laboratory. Some challenges included sample timing, transport delays, and occasional misrouting of specimens. Despite these barriers, a pharmacist-led workflow with centralized laboratory testing and standardized interpretation allowed TDM results to be incorporated into OPAT management for carefully selected patients [8]. Notably, TDM was used in a small proportion of our OPAT patients, reflecting its current role as a targeted rather than routine strategy. While this approach may not be scalable to all OPAT patients, it represents a meaningful and feasible tool to optimize the care of select individuals alongside a motivated multidisciplinary care team.

As a single-center series without a comparator group, we cannot compare TDM-guided beta-lactam care to routine care. However, published experience describing beta-lactam TDM in OPAT remains limited, with prior reports including only small numbers of treated patients [5]. Our findings therefore contribute additional real-world data to a still-emerging evidence base. Selection bias is inherent, as testing was clinician- and/or pharmacist-directed rather than fully protocolized. Clinical outcomes such as cure or relapse were not systematically assessed in this series because the indication for anti-infective therapy and TDM varied. Nevertheless, given the very limited literature informing the application of beta-lactam TDM in OPAT, our findings provide direct insights into the types of clinical questions it can be used to address.

CONCLUSION

The use of beta-lactam TDM is feasible and frequently informs antimicrobial management. Illustrative cases highlight how TDM can support toxicity mitigation and dose optimization including in the setting of difficult-to-treat infections and infections due to drug-resistant organisms. These early lessons support further prospective evaluation to better define the place in therapy and outcomes, both clinical and toxicological, for beta-lactam TDM in OPAT care.
