Why Intraosseous Arteriovenous Fistulas Can Lead to Acute Subdural Hematomas: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Mayuko KUNII, Tomoki KIDANI, Nobuto HIRAI, Tomofumi TAKENAKA, Shogo FUKUYA, Akihiro TATEISHI, Katsumi MATSUMOTO, Masaaki TANIGUCHI
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intraosseous arteriovenous fistulas are rare vascular anomalies, with an increasing number of reports describing successful endovascular treatments in recent years. Although some cases of acute subdural hematoma caused by intraosseous arteriovenous fistulas have been reported, the precise mechanism remains unclear. In this study, we present a case of surgical treatment for an intraosseous arteriovenous fistula with acute subdural hematoma. A 58-year-old woman presented to our hospital with a sudden headache and nausea. Computed tomography revealed a left acute subdural hematoma with an abnormal diploic space in the frontal bone. Digital subtraction angiography confirmed an intraosseous arteriovenous fistula supplied by the middle meningeal artery and draining into the diploic vein. Because of concerns about potential tumor involvement, direct surgery was performed. Intraoperatively, vascularized tissue surrounding the fistula extended through the dural defect and connected to the subdural space, suggesting a possible cause of the acute subdural hematoma. The feeder and draining vein were dissected and removed along with the abnormal tissue. Histopathological analysis revealed fibrous tissue with hemorrhage and increased vascularity but no tumorous changes. Postoperative angiography confirmed successful arteriovenous fistula occlusion, and the patient recovered with minimal neurological deficit. This case suggests that intraosseous arteriovenous fistulas may cause acute subdural hematoma owing to the anatomical feature of vascularized tissue around the fistula extending through the dural defect into the subdural space.

Introduction

Intraosseous arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) are rare vascular anomalies characterized by an abnormal connection between the dural artery and the diploic vein.1) Although some cases are linked to trauma, their exact cause often remains unclear.2) Recent reports describe patients presenting with symptoms such as tinnitus, headache, and intracranial hemorrhage, many of whom have been treated with endovascular interventions.3-5) Although intraosseous AVFs have been associated with acute subdural hematoma (ASDH), the precise mechanism by which a fistula located in the diploic vein directly causes subdural hematoma remains uncertain.6,7)

In this report, we describe our experience performing direct surgery on an intraosseous AVF associated with an ASDH. Our findings revealed anatomical features of the intraosseous AVF that may contribute to the development of ASDH.

Case Report

A 58-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital with a sudden-onset headache and nausea. She had no history of trauma, antithrombotic medication use, or radiation therapy. Computed tomography revealed a left ASDH with midline shift and an abnormal diploic space in the left frontal bone (Fig. 1A and B). Blood tests showed no abnormalities in blood counts or coagulation parameters. Computed tomography angiography identified a suspected vascular abnormality within the diploic space (Fig. 1C). The patient was initially managed conservatively. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) on the second day of hospitalization confirmed an intraosseous AVF. The arterial supply originated from the anterior branch of the left middle meningeal artery and drained through the diploic space into the left anterior temporal diploic vein and the pterygoid plexus. There was no flow in meningeal veins (Fig. 1D-H). Given the presence of a diploic space abnormality and the potential for a tumorous lesion, direct surgical occlusion was performed on hospital day 8.

AVF: arteriovenous fistula

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia. Using a high-speed drill, the cortical bone around the abnormal defect was carefully removed, enlarging the diploic space to a circular shape approximately 2 cm in diameter (Fig. 2A). The diploic space contained vascularized tissue extending toward the diploic vein (Fig. 2B and C). The dura surrounding the vascularized tissue, including the feeder, was cauterized, and the draining vein toward the diploic vein was coagulated and dissected. In addition, a dural defect was identified at the same location, which was suggested to be a potential cause of the ASDH (Fig. 2D and E; see Supplementary Video).

Postoperative DSA performed the following day confirmed complete resolution of the AVF. The patient recovered well and was discharged on hospital day 11, with a modified Rankin Scale score of 1. Histopathological examination of the excised tissue revealed large blood vessels with fibrous tissue, hemorrhage, and an increase in small blood vessels but no evidence of tumorous changes (Fig. 3). Follow-up DSA at 6 months showed no recurrence. The Ethical Review Board of the Osaka Neurological Institute approved this study (approval number: OR 02-3). Because this was a retrospective study, informed consent was not required.

Discussion

Intraosseous AVFs are rare, but the number of reported cases has been increasing in recent years. Sixteen cases of symptomatic supratentorial intraosseous AVF have been documented to date (Table 1).2,3,5-16) The cause remains unclear in many cases, although some have been linked to trauma, idiopathic causes, or thrombosis. Common symptoms include tinnitus, headache, and intracranial hemorrhage. Among reported cases, eight involved intracranial hemorrhage, including the present case.5-7,11,13,15,16) ASDH has been reported in six cases, none of which were associated with cortical venous reflux (CVR).6,7,11,15,16) Transarterial embolization and transvenous embolization using coils, Onyx, and n-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate have been reported as effective treatments for symptomatic intraosseous AVF.

Of all reported ASDHs, 2.0% to 6.7% are non-traumatic.17) Although AVFs have been reported to cause hemorrhage without CVR or increased venous pressure, the precise mechanism remains unclear,15) largely because most intraosseous AVFs are treated endovascularly, limiting the availability of direct anatomical findings. Cases of pure ASDH due to AVF without CVR are rare,18,19) with only five reported cases of intraosseous AVF, including the present case.6,7,15,16) In one case, craniotomy was performed for hematoma removal after transarterial embolization, and clots were observed on the inner surface of the dura mater near the shunt site, suggesting meningeal vein dilation secondary to diploic vein obstruction as the bleeding source.6) In another case, angiography revealed meningeal vein dilation, also suggested as the bleeding source.15) A third case identified the meningeal vein as the draining vein, although the precise bleeding site was not clearly described.16) In the remaining case, the shunt point contained an aneurysmal sac directed toward the subdural space, which was presumed to be the source of hemorrhage.7)

Notably, this case was not associated with CVR and exhibited no venous outflow into the meningeal veins; rather, venous drainage occurred exclusively through extracranial pathways. To our knowledge, no previous reports have described an intraosseous AVF with ASDH presenting with intraosseous-to-subdural tissue continuity and a dural defect, as observed in our case. Histopathological examination did not reveal characteristic AVF findings, such as intimal thickening in arteries, vessel elongation, or prominent venous dilation.20) However, intraoperative findings provided two important anatomical features. First, the shunt point was presumed to lie within the diploic vein in the diploic space, with no involvement of meningeal veins, as is also supported by angiographic findings. Second, the bleeding point was the vascularized tissue including the diploic vein in the diploic space. Although the exact bleeding point could not be identified during surgery, the continuity of this vascularized tissue from the diploic space through the dural defect strongly suggested this region as the bleeding source of ASDH.

In conclusion, although intraosseous AVFs have been known to cause ASDH, many anatomical aspects remain unclear. In this case, direct surgical findings revealed vascularized tissue extending from the fistula to the subdural space along with a dural defect, suggesting a possible mechanism for the development of subdural hematoma due to intraosseous AVF.
