Contribution characteristics of nitrogen accumulation and distribution to grain yield of maize
Authors: Linyu Liu, Linzheng Liao, Xu Yan, Ailin Tian, Chao Xia, Haodan Zhang, Rui He, Rui Li, Junlan Liu, Guoqing Sun, Qin Huang, Xuewei Yin, Qiang Li, Yun Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A two-year field experiment (2019–2020) was conducted in Yongchuan, Chongqing, and Deyang, Sichuan, to investigate the differences in nitrogen (N) accumulation and grain yield formation between maize varieties with contrasting N efficiencies. Additionally, we clarify the contribution characteristics of N accumulation and distribution to the grain yield of maize in Southwest China. Two maize varieties—N-efficient Zhenghong 311 (ZH 311) and N-inefficient Xianyu 508 (XY 508)—were tested under four N levels (0, 120, 240, and 360 kg·ha-1). Kernel number per ear showed the strongest correlation with N accumulation (R2 = 0.8186) and weakest correlation with 100-kernel weight (R2 = 0.2479). ZH 311 exhibited a significantly higher ear row number, kernel number per row, total kernel number per ear, and grain yield than XY 508. However, its 100-kernel weight was lower, indicating that N accumulation primarily regulates grain yield by modulating the kernel number per ear. N accumulation peaked during the V12–R1 (12-leaf to silking) stages, with substantial contributions from the SS–V6 (emergence to 6-leaf; R2 = 0.7737), V12–R1 (R2 = 0.7129), and R1–R3 (silking to milking stage; R2 = 0.7277) stages. Root (R2 = 0.6804), stem-sheath (R2 = 0.8440), and leaf (R2 = 0.8590) N accumulation was positively correlated with grain yield. Root N accumulation contributed the most to yield at the R3 stage, whereas stem-sheath N accumulation had a highly significant contribution in the V12, R1, and R3 stages and leaf N accumulation in the V6, V12, and R3 stages. ZH 311 exhibited significantly higher N accumulation at all growth stages, ear row number, kernel number per row, total kernel number per ear, and grain yield compared to XY 508, while its 100-kernel weight was lower. The N-efficient variety ZH 311 increased grain yield by enhancing kernel number per ear in the early stages (thereby expanding sink capacity) and later strengthening the supply from source organs (stems, leaves, and roots) to ensure assimilate availability.Graphical abstract

Introduction

Maize, one of the world’s three Main cereals, ranks first globally in total production and average yield. In China, Maize cultivation covers 20 million hectares with a total output of 289 million tons, positioning the country as the world’s second-largest maize producer [1]. As the second-largest maize cultivation area in China, the Southwest region plays a pivotal role in ensuring the sustainable development of China’s maize industry and global food security [2]. In recent years, rapid urbanization, land desertification, soil pollution, and other challenges have led to a continuous decline in both arable land area and quality. China’s per capita arable land is now less than half the global average [3, 4]. Consequently, enhancing the grain production efficiency per unit area has become a critical challenge for safeguarding food security and promoting sustainable agriculture in China and beyond.

Nitrogen (N) is one of the most essential nutrients for plants, serving as a primary component of proteins, amino acids, enzymes, and other critical biomolecules, and is vital for plant growth, development, and yield formation [5, 6]. Wu [7] demonstrated that moderate N fertilization effectively enhanced N accumulation in maize, thereby increasing the yield. Similarly, Yang [8] found that rational N application promoted N accumulation in maize, significantly improving both yield and quality. Collectively, these studies indicated that N application within an optimal range enhances crop N accumulation, thereby increasing productivity and quality [9]. Crop growth is governed by source-sink relationships, wherein source organs (leaves, stem-sheaths) produce or export photoassimilates [10], while sink organs (grains, fruits) receive and store these compounds [11]. Differential nitrogen (N) allocation between source and sink organs critically influences crop growth and yield formation [12]. Utilizing 15N tracer technology, Zhu demonstrated that N distribution in rice follows the hierarchy: grains > leaves > stems > roots [13]. Similarly, Gu reported that summer maize prioritizes N allocation to grains > stem-sheaths > leaves [14]. Liang documented that N-efficient rapeseed varieties remobilize N from source to sink organs, specifically relocating N from senescing leaves to newly formed leaves, thereby enhancing nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) [15]. Effective transport and accumulation of nitrogen to grains ensures adequate nutrient supply for grain filling [16]. Concurrently, nitrogen redistribution within source-sink organs promotes crop growth, prolongs photosynthetic duration, and enhances carbohydrate supply, thereby further elevating crop yield [17]. N allocation also plays a critical role in the crop yield. Rational N distribution optimizes crop growth and development, ultimately increasing the yield [18]. In maize, N is primarily allocated to leaves during vegetative growth but shifts to grains during reproductive growth, enhancing grain N accumulation and yield [19]. Thus, both N accumulation and spatiotemporal allocation are indispensable for crop growth, yield components, and quality.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that N accumulation and allocation in crops significantly influence yield. However, the contribution characteristics of N accumulation and allocation to yield across different growth periods, stages, and organs remain unclear. In this study, two maize varieties with contrasting N efficiencies—Zhenghong 311 (ZH 311) and Xianyu 508 (XY 508)—were used as experimental Materials. Field experiments were conducted in Southwest China during 2019 and 2020 to investigate the effects of N fertilization on N accumulation, allocation, and yield across various growth periods, stages, and organs in maize varieties with differing N efficiencies. The objectives were to: (1) elucidate the contribution patterns of N accumulation and allocation to grain yield in southwestern maize systems, (2) reveal differences in source-sink dynamics (specifically N accumulation-allocation dynamics) and their contributions to grain yield between maize varieties with contrasting N efficiencies, and (3) establish a theoretical foundation and technical framework for high-yield, high-quality maize cultivation and efficient nutrient utilization in the region.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

The experimental varieties selected were the N-efficient maize variety “Zhenghong 311” (ZH 311) and the N-inefficient variety “Xianyu 508” (XY 508), previously screened in preliminary trials [20–22]. Both varieties are major cultivars in Southwest China with similar growth duration in this region.

Experimental design

Field experiments were conducted in 2019 and 2020 at two sites: Xinjian Village, Zhongjiang County, Deyang, Sichuan (31°00'N, 104°36'E) and Wujian Town, Yongchuan District, Chongqing (29°21'N, 105°54'E). Both locations are representative of the hilly regions of Southwest China. The experimental design was consistent across both sites, using a randomized complete block design with two maize varieties (ZH 311 and XY 508) and four N levels: N0 (0 kg·ha−1), N120 (120 kg·ha−1), N240 (240 kg·ha−1), and N360 (360 kg·ha−1). This resulted in eight treatments, each replicated thrice. Individual plots measured 8 × 5 m (40 m2). N fertilizer (urea) was split equally for application at sowing and at the V12 stage (12-leaf stage). Phosphorus (calcium Superphosphate, 600 kg·ha−1) and potassium (KCl, 150 kg·ha−1) were used as basal fertilizers. Maize was planted using alternating wide-narrow row spacing (1.4 m and 0.6 m) at 52,500 plants·ha−1. Field management practices, including irrigation, weed control, and pest and disease management, followed local high-yield protocols.

Indicator determination

Determination of dry matter

Five representative plants with uniform growth were sampled from each plot at the key growth stages, i.e., V6 (6-leaf stage), V12 (12-leaf stage), R1 (silking stage), R3 (milking stage), and R6 (physiological maturity). Plants were dissected into roots (calculated based on 0–80 cm of root biomass), stem sheaths, leaves, and ears (separated into kernels and cobs in R6). The samples were oven-dried at 105 °C for 30 min to deactivate the enzymes and then dried at 80 °C to a constant weight. The dried samples were weighed to determine dry Matter accumulation and then ground and sieved through a 60-mesh sieve for subsequent N-content analysis.

Determination of N content

The N content in the roots, stem sheaths, leaves, and ears across different growth stages was determined using the Kjeldahl method [23]. The total N accumulation in maize plants was calculated based on organ-specific biomass and N concentration data.

Production and its composition

Twenty consecutive plants, excluding border rows, were randomly selected from each plot for evaluation of yield components, including ear row number, kernel number per row, total kernel number per ear, and 100-kernel weight, at physiological maturity (R6 stage). Additionally, a 10 m2 area (excluding edge rows) was Manually harvested to determine the actual grain yield. Both yield and kernel weight data were adjusted to a standardized moisture content of 14.0%.

Data analysis

Data were collected and organized using Microsoft Excel. Least significant difference (LSD) tests were performed using SPSS to assess significant differences among nitrogen (N) levels and between varieties. Multiple linear regression combined with mediation effect analysis in SPSS was employed to analyze direct and indirect path coefficients between nitrogen accumulation and yield components. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Quadratic regression analysis of the relationship between nitrogen accumulation and yield, along with visualization, was conducted using GraphPad Prism 5.0 software.

The greater response of XY 508 to elevated N levels (vs. ZH 311) Suggested that ZH 311 is more sensitive to excessive N input, whereas higher N application preferentially enhances root N accumulation in XY 508.

Differences in grain yield and yield components

Site, year, variety, N application, and their interactions significantly influenced maize yield and its components (Fig. 3). Both varieties had higher ear row numbers, total kernel numbers per ear, 100-kernel weights, and grain yields in Sichuan than in Chongqing, whereas those in Chongqing had higher kernel numbers per row. The yield and its components were notably higher in the 2019 growing season than in 2020. Across both sites and Years, ZH 311 (N-efficient) demonstrated a significantly greater ear row number (+ 9.53%), kernel number per row (+ 11.88%), total kernel number per ear (+ 22.49%), and grain yield (+ 13.80%) than XY 508 (N-inefficient) but a lower 100-kernel weight (− 4.34%), highlighting the Superior yield performance of ZH 311. N application significantly enhanced ear row number, kernel number per row, total kernel number per ear, 100-kernel weight, and grain yield, with varietal differences in responsiveness. ZH 311 exhibited increases of 8.93% (ear row number), 11.70% (kernel number per row), 21.55% (total kernel number per ear), 6.14% (100-kernel weight), and 20.33% (grain yield) compared with that under N0, whereas XY 508 exhibited greater improvements (9.62%, 13.54%, 24.30%, 8.51%, and 29.40%, respectively). The highest enhancement was observed for the total kernel number per ear, whereas the lowest was observed for 100-kernel weight. Discussion

N accumulation is fundamental for crop growth, biomass production, and yield formation, with N application significantly influencing N accumulation and, consequently, grain yield [24, 25].

This study demonstrated that N accumulation at all growth stages was significantly and positively correlated with grain yield (Fig. 5), with the highest correlation observed at the V12 stage (R2 = 0.9016**). This was consistent with the findings of Jiang [26]. Maize underwent concurrent vegetative and reproductive growth during V12, and elevated N accumulation supported inflorescence development. The number of inflorescences directly determines the kernel number per ear, thereby governing the yield, explaining the strong correlation between V12 N accumulation and grain yield [27]. ZH 311 exhibited significantly higher N accumulation than XY 508 across all stages and sites, with the disparity widening as the plants matured. This was consistent with the findings of Wang [28] who reported that N-efficient varieties Maintain higher root N accumulation during late reproductive stages, enhancing N uptake and retention. ZH 311 also outperformed XY 508 in terms of ear row number (+ 9.53%), kernel number per row (+ 11.88%), total kernel number per ear (+ 22.49%), and grain yield (+ 13.80%), although its 100-kernel weight was lower (− 4.34%). This underscored the fact that the yield advantage of N-efficient varieties stems primarily from an improved kernel number per ear, corroborating the findings of Su [29].Fig. 5Relationship between nitrogen accumulation and yield at different growth stages. Further analysis revealed positive correlations between N accumulation and the number of ears (R2 = 0.9021**), kernel number per row (R2 = 0.8556**), total kernel number per ear (R2 = 0.8186**), 100-kernel weight (R2 = 0.2479), and grain yield (Fig. 6). The total kernel number per ear showed the strongest correlation with N accumulation, whereas the 100-kernel weight was the weakest, indicating that N accumulation primarily regulates yield by modulating the kernel number per ear, as reported by Mueller [30, 31]. Notably, grain yield correlated most strongly with total kernel number per ear (R2 = 0.9134**) and least strongly with 100-kernel weight (R2 = 0.5410), further confirming that N accumulation influences yield via kernel number (Fig. 4). Path analysis revealed that while the direct effect of total kernel number per ear on yield was moderate (0.5278), its indirect effects through kernel number per row (0.4820) and ear row number (0.8866) were substantial. This highlighted the critical role of V12-stage N accumulation in determining the kernel number per row and ear row number, which collectively drive the total kernel number and final grain yield, consistent with the findings of Kong [32].Fig. 6Correlation between maize yield and yield composition. During crop growth, source-sink limitations exist. Crops increase kernel number per ear through carbohydrate supply from source organs (leaves and stem-sheaths) to sink organs [33]. When the increase in kernel number per ear exceeds the Supply capacity of source organs, photoassimilate allocation per kernel decreases, reducing 100-kernel weight and preventing yield improvement [34] Conversely, when sink organ receptivity cannot fully utilize photoassimilate supply from source organs, stable kernel weight cannot compensate for low kernel number per ear, also limiting high yield [10] In this study: Nitrogen application induced the highest increase in kernel number per ear but the lowest increase in 100-kernel weight. ZH 311 exhibited 22.49% higher kernel number per ear Yet 4.34% lower 100-kernel weight compared to XY 508. This indicates that ZH 311 prioritized sink capacity expansion (kernel number per ear) to enhance yield, while source Marginal Limitations constrained simultaneous increases in kernel weight. For XY 508, lower kernel number per ear allowed Sufficient photoassimilate allocation per kernel, increasing 100-kernel weight – consistent with findings by Chen [35]. Crucially, ZH 311 Maintained higher N accumulation than XY 508 across all growth stages. During late reproductive phases, it sustained: Elevated N accumulation in leaves and stem-sheaths, Prolonged leaf area duration (LAD) Extended grain filling period, Enhanced total Supply capacity through leaf photosynthesis and stem-sheath translocation. This demonstrates that the N-efficient variety ZH 311: (1) Expanded sink capacity during early growth (2) Enhanced source-sink Supply capacity during late growth thereby ensuring superior yield over the N-inefficient XY 508.

Crop N accumulation and utilization vary significantly across developmental stages, leading to differential contributions of stage-specific N accumulation to grain yield. N accumulation in rice peaks during the jointing–heading stage and is lowest during the tillering–jointing stage, with the former showing the strongest correlation with grain yield [36, 37]. In wheat, N accumulation is highest during the jointing–flowering stage and lowest during the overwintering–regreening stage, with jointing–flowering accumulation contributing the most to yield [38].This study revealed that maize N accumulation peaked during the V12–R1 stage and was lowest during R3–R6, which was consistent with the findings of Wang [39]. Vigorous vegetative growth and early ear differentiation demand substantial N for structural development and metabolic processes during V12–R1, whereas a decline in the root uptake capacity during R3–R6 limits N accumulation [40].

Grain yield was significantly correlated with N accumulation during SS–V6 (R2 = 0.7737*), V12–R1 (R2 = 0.7129*), and R1–R3 (R2 = 0.7277*) (Fig. 7). This contrasted with the findings of Cao [41], who reported the highest yield contribution from R1–R3 accumulation, likely because their study overlooked root N accumulation [42, 43]. Pre-anthesis root N accounted for more than 20% of the total accumulation, reducing the apparent contribution of SS–V6 in prior studies. Our results showed that SS–V6 was the second-highest contributor after V12–R1, highlighting this discrepancy. ZH 311 exhibited significantly higher N accumulation than XY 508 across all stages, with pronounced advantages during V6–V12 and R3–R6. This aligned with the results of Cui [44], who observed greater N-efficient variety advantages under low-N conditions. The amplified disparity in the later stages likely stemmed from the fertilization regime (basal + V12 topdressing) in which soil N availability declines post-application, favoring N-efficient varieties with superior uptake and retention.Fig. 7Relationship between nitrogen accumulation and yield at different growth periods. Vegetative organs form the foundation for nutrient uptake and biomass production in plants, directly influencing crop yield [45, 46]. Roots are the primary organs for nutrient and water absorption and are critical in N accumulation and yield [47, 48]. Zhan [49] demonstrated that enhancing root N accumulation effectively improves maize grain yield, whereas Ma [50] reported that N-efficient varieties consistently exhibit higher root N accumulation across growth stages than N-inefficient varieties, which is a key factor in their yield superiority. In the present study, root N accumulation did not differ significantly between the two varieties in V6 (6-leaf stage), V12 (12-leaf stage), R1 (silking), or R3 (milking). However, ZH 311 (N-efficient) showed significantly higher root N accumulation than XY 508 (N-inefficient) in R6 (maturity). This contrasted with the findings of Zhang [51], Likely because of the classification of ZH 311 as an N-efficient absorption type characterized by superior N uptake capacity per unit root mass. Despite comparable root N accumulation in the early stages (pre-R3), ZH 311 Maintained higher total N accumulation. Root senescence and declining uptake capacity in XY 508 exacerbated N limitation by R6, leading to significantly lower root N accumulation than that in ZH 311, which was consistent with the findings of Li [52].

Photosynthates serve as the material foundation for crop yield, and N accumulation in photosynthetic organs enhances photosynthesis, increases dry matter accumulation, and increases grain yield [53]. This study revealed that ZH 311 exhibited significantly higher N accumulation in the stem sheaths and leaves across all growth stages than XY 508, with the largest disparity observed at maturity (R6 stage). This aligned with the findings of Wang [28], who attributed the yield advantage of N-efficient varieties to their robust root N uptake during the late reproductive stages, sustaining N accumulation in vegetative organs, delaying senescence, and ensuring continued photosynthate production [54].

Conclusion

Maize nitrogen accumulation exhibited positive correlations with ear row number, kernel number per row, kernel number per ear, 100-kernel weight, and grain yield, with kernel number per ear showing the strongest association and 100-kernel weight the weakest. Grain yield was positively linked to nitrogen accumulation in roots, stem-sheaths, and leaves, Stem-sheath nitrogen accumulation contributed most significantly to grain yield, with root nitrogen accumulation at R3 stage demonstrating the most significant contribution, stem-sheath accumulation during V12-R3 stages providing substantial input, and leaf accumulation at both V6–V12 and R3 stages exerting notable effects. Crucially, V12-stage nitrogen accumulation contributed most profoundly to grain yield by directly determining kernel number per row and ear row number, while nitrogen accumulation during SS–V6, V12–R1, and R1–R3 stages also significantly enhanced yield. This regulatory pathway operates primarily through nitrogen-mediated expansion of sink capacity (via increased kernel number per ear) coupled with reinforced source Supply during late reproductive phases. The nitrogen-efficient variety ZH 311 consistently outperformed the nitrogen-inefficient XY 508 in nitrogen accumulation across all growth stages, ear row number, kernel number per row, kernel number per ear (+ 22.49%), and grain yield, though exhibiting lower 100-kernel weight (−4.34%). These results confirm that nitrogen-efficient varieties overcome sink limitations through early-stage sink capacity expansion while maintaining robust source organ functionality during late growth stages to support sink filling—a physiological mechanism explaining ZH 311’s superior nitrogen use efficiency that provides theoretical and technical frameworks for breeding and cultivation of high-NUE maize genotypes.
