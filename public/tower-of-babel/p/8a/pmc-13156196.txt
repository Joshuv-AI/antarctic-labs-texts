The PKD Daily: Development and Content Validation of a Hybrid Diary for Tracking Urinary Events in Adults with Autosomal Dominant Polycystic Kidney Disease (ADPKD)
Authors: Meaghan O’Connor, Lynne Broderick, Laura Tesler Waldman, Elizabeth Costa, Michelle Carty, Sasikiran Nunna, Dorothee Oberdhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionFor patients with autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD), the only approved treatment is tolvaptan, a twice-daily medication that works to slow kidney function decline. Patients on tolvaptan report a considerable burden related to the drug’s aquaretic effects which can intensify immediately following a dose of the medication.MethodsA targeted literature review and individual concept elicitation (CE) interviews with nephrologists and adult patients with ADPKD taking tolvaptan were conducted to inform the development of a hybrid diary, programmed as a smartphone application. A sample of adults with ADPKD taking tolvaptan tested the diary for 7 days; a subset also participated in cognitive debriefing (CD) interviews. The diary was then revised and finalized.ResultsCE interviews confirmed the importance of tracking urinary frequency and urgency and timing of tolvaptan doses in real time. Results from the usability/feasibility test and CD interviews confirmed the understandability and relevance of the diary and provided critical insights to improve its content and functionality. The resulting PKD Daily includes two daily entries to report dose timing, overnight urination, and daily impacts of frequent/urgent urination; real-time entries to capture frequency/urgency of daytime urination; and reminders to encourage regular data entry.ConclusionOriginally developed for use in a clinical trial, the PKD Daily can also provide valuable information to clinicians and patients to support treatment decisions and inform dose selection in clinical practice, becoming a valuable part of a clinician’s toolbox. Further, the development of the PKD Daily can serve as a case study for the successful creation of a hybrid diary.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is a genetic disease characterized by the development of progressively enlarging fluid-filled cysts in the kidneys. Over time, these cysts destroy kidney tissue and lead to kidney failure, requiring patients to rely on kidney replacement therapies such as dialysis and transplant. The cysts can result in pain, hematuria, and urinary tract infections [1, 2]. Further, ADPKD is a systemic disease which can also present with early onset hypertension, liver cysts, intracranial aneurysms, and a reduced ability for patients to concentrate urine adequately. Patients with ADPKD experience specific impacts related to the urine concentration defect that causes their baseline urine output to be elevated compared to a general population from an early age [3] and manifests in various urinary complaints including urgent and frequent urination, nocturia, and elevated urine volume [2].

Currently, the only approved pharmacological treatment for ADPKD in Europe, the USA, and Japan is tolvaptan, a vasopressin antagonist, which leads to increased fluid output through its mechanism of action [2]. Patients treated with tolvaptan are advised to increase their fluid intake to prevent dehydration. All this contributes to an increased urinary burden experienced by patients [4].

To better quantify the day-to-day urinary burden, including how those effects relate to dose timing of tolvaptan and frequency of urinary events (i.e., frequent and/or urgent urination, nocturia, pollakiuria, polyuria, and gross hematuria), it is essential to capture continuous, real-time information. Diaries and ecological momentary assessments (EMAs) are a mode of data collection that are well suited to collecting information on a frequent and/or real-time basis. Daily diaries, in which data are collected once per day at a regular time or during a regular time period, are commonly used to collect patient-reported experiences and behaviors that occur frequently [5]. Event-driven (also called event-based) diaries, a type of EMA, also capture day-to-day patient experiences and behaviors [6] but can help reduce recall bias because data are entered each time an event of interest occurs. Thus, an event-driven diary can potentially help researchers better understand relationships between events [7]. Electronic or digital data capture methods enhance the usefulness of daily and event-driven diaries by employing timestamps to document real-time information, including automatic prompts to users, and displaying previously entered data, encouraging users to continue providing feedback and entering data and thereby limiting missing data [7, 8].

Hybrid daily and event-driven diaries have been used successfully to collect patient-reported data in disease areas in which patients experience symptoms on a recurring basis, such as irritable bowel syndrome [9, 10]. The hybrid approach combines the strengths of daily and event-driven self-reporting of symptoms and impacts related to a health condition. However, while best practices have recently been published for the development of event-driven eDiaries for use in clinical trials [8], there is little published information on the development and use of hybrid diaries. This article describes the development of the PKD Daily, a hybrid diary accessed through a smartphone application (app) designed to track frequent and urgent urination while on tolvaptan. Although specific to ADPKD, the development process and features of the PKD Daily can be adapted for use in myriad conditions.

Methods

Development of the PKD Daily followed a multiphase study design (Fig. 1) in accordance with best practices in patient-reported outcome (PRO) measure development [11–13]. This study was reviewed and approved by WCG IRB (IRB study number 1334669). All participants received a study information sheet with details about the study prior to being interviewed. Phase I: Targeted Literature Review and Concept Elicitation Interviews

A targeted literature review aimed to identify essential concepts for consideration during the diary development process (Fig. 2). Two searches were conducted in PubMed to identify articles published between 2011 and 2021; these were supplemented with hand searching. Search 1 focused on the experience of urinary events (i.e., frequent and/or urgent urination, nocturia, pollakiuria, polyuria, and gross hematuria) in patients with ADPKD. After title and abstract screening and subsequent full text review, 12 records (11 articles, 1 poster) were included in the data extraction process. Search 2 focused on the development of daily or event-driven diaries as PRO measures. Results from the literature review informed the interview guides for exploratory, one-on-one, concept elicitation (CE) interviews with clinicians with experience treating ADPKD and one-on-one CE interviews with adults with ADPKD taking tolvaptan (“patients”). CE interviews and the data they produce are an essential part of the PRO measure development process, highlighting concepts that are important and relevant to patients and their care providers [11, 14].

Interviews followed sample-specific, semi-structured interview guides. Clinician interviews aimed to explore urinary events identified in the literature review; identify additional urinary events that should potentially be measured in a hybrid diary for patients with ADPKD taking tolvaptan; elicit recommendations on functional aspects of a hybrid diary (e.g., recall period, frequency of administration, response options); and gather insights and recommendations on the patient CE interview eligibility criteria (e.g., whether all participants or only some should be on tolvaptan). Patient interviews similarly sought to better understand the aquaretic effects of tolvaptan; gather insight into users’ experiences of the number and timing of tolvaptan doses; and elicit preferences for reporting on the aquaretic effects on a daily or real-time basis.

Clinician and patient participants (nephrologists treating patients on tolvaptan and adults with ADPKD taking tolvaptan, respectively) were both recruited using purposive sampling. Clinicians were recruited by email through Otsuka’s existing network of nephrologists, while patients were recruited through patient panels, social media posts, and dedicated messages sent in collaboration with the Polycystic Kidney Disease Foundation (PKDF). All interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers (MO’C, LB) using online video/conference calling software, audio-recorded with the participant’s consent, and later transcribed verbatim, removing any personal identifying information. Each participant took part in 1 CE interview. Transcripts were reviewed for accuracy before being coded in NVivo software by 2 members of the study team (MO’C, LB) using inductive and deductive thematic analysis [15, 16]. The study team assessed saturation of concepts within the patient sample informally throughout the data collection process and formally (dividing the transcripts into three groups) after completing coding. No new concepts emerged during the third and final set of interviews, confirming that saturation of concepts had been reached and no additional interviews were needed.

Phase II: Diary and App Development

Guided by results from the literature review and clinician and patient interviews, key concepts (and the frequency of evaluation, i.e., once daily or in real time throughout the day) were selected for inclusion in the PKD Daily. Items from the ADPKD Impact Scale (ADPKD-IS) [17] were also reviewed and adapted for inclusion in a once-daily, end-of-day (EOD) entry. The CE results also informed decisions regarding the functionality of using a smartphone app to administer the PKD Daily. The study team drafted a technical requirements document with diary instructions, items and response choices, skip patterns, and other detailed notes regarding functionality and formatting for use in app programming. Multiple rounds of beta testing were conducted on both Android and iOS platforms; each round resulted in text revisions and functionality improvements.

Phase III: Diary Evaluation and Refinement

To test the usability and feasibility of the PKD Daily in the context of the app, participants (adults with ADPKD taking tolvaptan), used the app for 7 consecutive days. This timeframe was selected to encompass variation within users’ daily lives, such as work- versus non-workdays. As this study was intended to test functionality and not to draw conclusions about the frequency of events or the relationship between dose timing and events, 7 days was a sufficient period. Participants were recruited through purposive sampling; participation in the CE interviews did not preclude participation in this phase. In-app instructions provided guidance through the consent and initial setup process (e.g., selecting initial and EOD entry times) and instructed patients on how to use the app. Reminders to complete diary entries were sent through the app as push notifications. Upon completion of the 7-day test period, each user completed a brief, in-app exit survey about their experiences with the PKD Daily. Data were summarized through descriptive univariate analysis to examine overall compliance rates and item-level responses to both the diary items and exit survey.

A subset of the feasibility and usability test participants also completed 60-min, one-on-one cognitive debriefing (CD) interviews during which they read aloud and reflected on each screen from the PKD Daily. CD interview participants also answered targeted questions about the diary’s usability, comprehensibility, and comprehensiveness of the items, text, and functional features. All interviews were conducted by trained qualitative researchers (MO’C, LB), audio-recorded, and transcribed. Each transcript was reviewed for accuracy before two members of the study team (MO’C, LB) coded and analyzed (using inductive and deductive approaches) the CD interview transcripts in NVivo software. Coding and analysis focused on identifying any potential problems with the text or functionality or other areas for improvement.

Results from the usability/feasibility test and CD interviews were used to update the PKD Daily’s content and programming notes; revisions were captured in an item-tracking matrix.

Results

Phase I: Targeted Literature Review and Concept Elicitation

Few published articles described the patient experience of ADPKD and associated urinary events such as frequent and/or urgent urination or what Cole et al. (2011) refer to as “urinary concepts.” Those that did confirmed the urinary concepts as common side effects of tolvaptan but provided little detail on how patients describe them. Four symptoms (pain [18–23], nocturia [17, 23], polyuria [21, 23–26], and gross hematuria [19, 20, 23]) and one impact (anxiety [20, 27]) were identified as potentially relevant for inclusion in the hybrid diary. All 11 studies that described the development of a diary as a PRO measure implemented similar designs to create their diaries, but none described the creation of a hybrid (daily plus event-driven) approach [28–38]. Recall periods included once- or twice-daily, or weekly. Three studies described the use of a smartphone app for data capture and included feasibility and usability tests to collect data related to study goals and evaluate the app functionality and determine user acceptability [39–41].

Five nephrologists, all of whom had discussed with and/or prescribed tolvaptan to their patients and were involved in clinical research, participated in one-on-one interviews. Clinician experience of caring for patients with ADPKD ranged from 7 to 13 years. All five clinicians confirmed frequent and urgent urination as a key part of the tolvaptan experience and recommended measuring frequency in counts (i.e., the exact number of urinary events) or numeric rating scales (e.g., 0–10). They agreed that each entry should be quick (e.g., 3–4 min) and simple, limiting user burden as much as possible. Most felt it would be sufficient/appropriate for patients to complete the diary three times per day: (1) at the beginning of day (reporting on events that occurred while sleeping), (2) midday (reporting on events since waking), and (3) before bed (reporting on events since midday). The clinicians advised against measuring pain, gross hematuria, anxiety, and urine volume, as none of these concepts were critical to the tolvaptan experience (and, in the case of volume, would be difficult to measure via an app), but suggested exploring the role of diet (e.g., salt, protein intake) in urinary events. Finally, clinicians recommended recruiting a demographically diverse sample for the patient interviews, with special attention to age and time on tolvaptan/tolvaptan status (i.e., new to the medication, titrating to a new dose, or on a consistent maintenance dose).

Patients reinforced the important concepts endorsed by the clinicians (Table 2). All patients reported experiencing frequent and/or urgent urination while taking tolvaptan. Most described the frequency of urinary events as consistent throughout the day (n = 11, 73%)—typically every 1–1.5 h—and night (n = 10, 67%). Factors driving urinary frequency included taking a dose of tolvaptan (i.e., increased thirst/liquid intake and urinary events tend to occur more frequently immediately after taking a dose) and dosing changes (i.e., starting tolvaptan or adjusting the doses usually intensifies urinary frequency). Participants reported scheduling the second tolvaptan dose for the early afternoon to reduce the likelihood of sleep interruptions caused by nocturia. While urinary urgency was manageable at home where restroom access was easy, it could be challenging when traveling (e.g., driving, flying, taking trains) and in other scenarios with limited restroom access. Although many participants (n = 9, 60%) said they had not changed their daily routines to accommodate frequent/urgent urination, descriptions of life on tolvaptan suggested otherwise. A few participants (n = 3, 20%) described carefully planning to limit time away from restrooms (e.g., shortening hikes or other outdoor activities) or confirm restroom access (e.g., always sitting in the aisle seat during air travel). All participants reported always having water or another drink readily available and maintaining a low-salt, low-protein diet and/or reducing alcohol intake to avoid exacerbating thirst. Most (n = 11, 73%) reported taking their tolvaptan doses regularly, adhering closely to their established schedules and often relying on recurring smartphone alarms as reminders to take both doses each day. On specific occasions, often related to travel, just under half (n = 7, 47%) described taking strategic breaks from tolvaptan (usually missing no more than 1–2 doses) when they knew frequent urination would be difficult to accommodate.

When asked for input on the diary, participants were divided on how often daytime events should be entered into the app: 6 (40%) preferred real-time entries, and 7 (47%) preferred entries at set intervals (e.g., every 2 h), but were not opposed to real-time entries, noting that real-time entries would likely provide more accurate data. Participants were also divided on the ideal way to report urgency: 8 (53%) preferred a Likert scale while 7 (47%) preferred a 0–10 scale. Nearly all (n = 14, 93%) agreed that nighttime events should be recorded in the morning, not in real time, so as not to prolong disruptions to sleep. Participants suggested the app include push notifications/reminders and a visible summary of the day’s entries to support diary adherence. They further emphasized that all entries should be simple and require little effort (e.g., no typing, just “clicking”).

Phase II: Draft Hybrid Diary, the PKD Daily

Using findings from the literature and CE interviews, the study team drafted the content and programming instructions for a hybrid, event-driven and daily diary, the PKD Daily. The draft diary included two daily entries (“initial” and EOD) to capture nocturia and impacts on daily life (adapted from the ADPKD-IS16), respectively, and the ability to report urination events and their associated urgency in real time. Real-time entries were set to automatically record a timestamp, and urgency was scored on a 5-point, Likert-type scale. The diary was programmed to include reminder notifications to prompt users if they had not made a real-time entry in 3 or 6-h and the ability to backfill missed urinary events if a user forgot to report in real time. Notifications were turned off between the EOD and initial entries to reduce interruptions to users’ sleep. The app was designed to work on smartphones using Android and iOS operating systems.

Phase III: Diary Evaluation and Refinement

Fifty adults taking tolvaptan for ADPKD participated in a 7-day usability and feasibility test of the PKD Daily. Participants ranged in age from 23 to 71 years old (average 43 years). Slightly more women (n = 29, 58%) than men (n = 21, 42%) participated. Most participants identified as white (n = 40, 80%). Half of the usability and feasibility test participants (n = 25) also participated in CD interviews and 7 of those participants (28%) had participated in a CE interview earlier in the study. More than half of the CD interview participants were female (n = 14, 56%) and most identified as white (n = 19, 76%). Time on tolvaptan was collected for 23 of the 25 CD interview participants; the majority (n = 21, 84%) had been on tolvaptan for more than 2 months at the time of the interview. Most participants completed the test on an iPhone (n = 21, 84%).

Almost three-quarters of participants found the app easy to set up (n = 36, 72%) and use (n = 35, 70%). Most (n = 37, 74%) found the 3- and 6-h notifications helpful. Participants estimated spending an average of 9.4 min per day using the PKD Daily, and nearly all (n = 46, 92%) found the amount of time spent on the diary each day acceptable. The average number of real-time entries during the day ranged from 8.7 (SD = 3.4) to 10.3 (SD = 3.6). The majority of the urgency ratings for real-time entries were “a little urgent” (n = 1366 events, 39.9%) or “somewhat urgent” (n = 1067 events; 31.1%). Responses to the items adapted from the ADPKD-IS showed that during the 7-day test, most urination events occurred when the user had access to a restroom “most of the time” (n = 90 events, 32.4%) or “all of the time” (n = 135 events, 48.6%). Just one participant reported that it was “very difficult” or “extremely difficult” to engage in daily activities during the 7-day test because of frequent or urgent urination.

Nearly all CD interview participants (n = 24; 96%) reported that the PKD Daily was easy to understand and use overall, describing it as “user-friendly,” “easy to navigate,” and “intuitive,” although two participants who had only been taking tolvaptan for 2 months or less at the time of the test found the diary challenging to manage in conjunction with the intense experience of starting tolvaptan. CD participants suggested four types of revisions to the PKD Daily: (1) clarifications to text/word choice, (2) improvements to the setup process, (3) edits to the functionality to better align with users’ schedules and habits, and (4) edits to make the draft diary more useful and/or informative to users. Some revisions were implemented immediately (e.g., changes to the text), while others were appropriate for future implementation, as they depend on the programming capabilities of the platform ultimately hosting the app.

Revisions to the text and word choice included clarifying the setup process, instructions, and 3- and 6-h notifications. Revisions to improve the user experience of setup included a different look to the time entry feature (a spinning wheel versus a digital entry screen), more screens during the setup process with less information per screen, and keeping the setup instructions available as a resource throughout the use period. Revisions to better align the diary with participants’ schedules/habits included the ability to enter the timing for the second dose of tolvaptan in real time, versus waiting until the end of the day; customize notification/entry times to set one time for work-/weekdays, and another for weekends/non-workdays; complete the initial or EOD entries at any time, rather than waiting for the notification/scheduled time; customize the 3- and 6-h reminders (e.g., every 1.5 or every 2 h) to better align with the frequency of urinary events experienced; and edit initial or EOD entries. Finally, revisions to make the diary more useful to patients included the ability to view past entry data (beyond just the past 24 h), a reminder to take their second dose of tolvaptan, the ability to track fluid intake, and the ability to access the app through a smartwatch. PKD Daily

Using findings from all phases of data collection, the study team revised the PKD Daily. The resulting diary focuses on four urinary events of interest, i.e., the aquaretic effects of tolvaptan: micturition urgency, pollakiuria, polyuria, and nocturia. The PKD Daily captures these effects by measuring the timing of urination events relative to tolvaptan dosing, the urgency and number of daytime urinary events, and the number of nighttime urinary events. The PKD Daily also links to the ADPKD-IS by measuring the daily impact of daytime urinary frequency and urgency and nighttime frequency.

The PKD Daily is designed for use as an app on Android smartphones or iPhones. Instructions, items, and response options are written in plain English to promote readability.

The diary includes two set entries per day (“initial” and EOD entries) and real-time, ongoing entries throughout the day. The initial entry tracks urinary events from the previous night and the timing of the user’s first dose of tolvaptan. The EOD entry captures the timing of the user’s second dose of tolvaptan and administers three questions adapted from the ADPKD-IS16 to evaluate the impact of frequent and urgent urination on the user that day. Initial and EOD items asking about the timing of tolvaptan doses also include options for “I skipped this dose (on purpose),” and “I missed this dose (not on purpose)” to afford users flexibility and accommodate strategic breaks from the medication. Initial and EOD entries occur at the same time each day at times selected by the user, and push notifications prompt the user to complete the entries at those set times. Real-time entries, entered throughout the day as a urinary event occurs, capture the date and time of the event along with an urgency rating (using a 5-point scale from “not at all urgent” to “extremely urgent”). Users report real-time entries by clicking a plus sign (“+”) button that brings up a digital clock automatically populated with the current time to reduce the amount of “clicking” for users. Users can backfill missed entries by following the same process and adjusting the digital clock.

To ensure timely collection of data and avoid missing data, the app sends push notifications if a user has not entered a real-time event for 3-h, and again if 6-h have elapsed without an entry. A history of the day’s real-time entries is visible on the app’s home screen. The 3- and 6-h notifications are silenced between the EOD and initial entry times to avoid disrupting users during sleep. However, users can continue to make real-time entries in that period if they wish.

Discussion

Although several peer-reviewed articles describe the development of daily diaries (both paper and electronic), event-driven eDiaries, and EMAs, to date there is little published information on the development of a diary that combines the unique strengths and features of daily diaries with event-driven assessments, such as EMAs. This article helps fill that gap.

The PKD Daily is a hybrid diary that aims to better understand the relationship between tolvaptan dose timing and the medication’s subsequent aquaretic effects. The diary was designed to balance the need for data capture in research and clinical settings (i.e., data that can help better describe the relationship between dose timing and urinary events) and the preferences and experiences of individuals taking tolvaptan. Although the PKD Daily was developed prior to the publication of the best practices event-driven eDiary development advocated for by Mowlem et al. (2025), the process used and many of the features of the diary, both in content and functionality, align with those recommendations [8]. Each real-time entry generates a timestamp and one question about urgency. Initial and EOD entries capture tolvaptan dose timing and basic information about daily impacts of urinary events. Push notifications act as reminders to support users’ compliance with required items and real-time entries. Flexible language, including the use of “initial” (rather than “morning”) and “end-of-day” (rather than “nighttime”) entries each day, accounts for nontraditional schedules, and multiple options for reporting dose timing—where users can enter a time or select “I skipped this dose (on purpose),” or “I missed this dose (not on purpose)”—reflect the real-world practices of adults taking tolvaptan.

Results from the feasibility and usability test of the PKD Daily and subsequent CD interviews provided insightful suggestions for future iterations of the diary to improve its functionality and usefulness to patients, and, in turn, the quality of the data collected. For example, feedback from CE interview participants indicates that tolvaptan users often take their second dose in the early afternoon to avoid too many nighttime events. In their practical advice for patients starting and acclimating to tolvaptan, Müller et al. (2022), on behalf of the ERA Working Group on Inherited Kidney Disorders, the European Rare Kidney Disease Reference Network, and Polycystic Kidney Disease International, similarly recommend taking the second dose early in the afternoon to avoid “excessive nocturia” [4]. A real-time entry feature to capture the timing of a user’s second dose may help with timing recall and medication compliance, since the notification to enter dose timing may serve as a reminder to take the second dose. Although the CD interview participants felt their real-time entries were mostly accurate in reporting the timing of urinary events, the ability to make entries on a smartwatch may further improve recall, compliance, and accuracy, as users could make real-time entries without having to carry their phones. Although these updates are specific to the PKD Daily, they nevertheless provide insights that could apply across diaries, encouraging developers to adopt features that closely align with users’ habits and existing resources. This is particularly relevant as more research explores the use of digital health technologies such as smartwatches. Finally, while the PKD Daily was developed with the intent to implement in a clinical trial setting, it could—particularly with the revisions that would better align with users’ schedules/habits—be a useful tool for patients and their doctors. The information collected from the PKD Daily would provide users with concrete data which they could share with their doctors. Furthermore, the information collected from the PKD Daily, once aggregated, could help doctors better inform their patients who are considering starting tolvaptan, again aligning with the recommendations provided by Müller et al. [4].

Study limitations included a lack of a diverse patient sample in terms of race and ethnicity, time on tolvaptan, and the overlap in the patient samples across study phases. Recruitment challenges were expected with a rare disease such as ADPKD, and the study team made every effort to recruit a diverse sample by partnering with the PKDF, which has access to a large, nationwide community of individuals with kidney disease. Nevertheless, as the PKD Daily is further tested and refined, efforts should be made to seek feedback from a more diverse group of individuals with ADPKD. This study also had strengths, including its alignment with measure development guidelines, such as the best practices for event-driven eDiaries from Mowlem et al. (2025) [8], and its patient-centered study design. Initial CE interviews allowed the patient voice to be foundational to instrument development, the CD interviews resulted in the revised PKD Daily reflecting patient participants’ feedback, and the bring-your-own-device aspect minimized participant burden. Finally, the direct development of the PKD Daily in its smartphone app form eliminated the need for a first round of CD interviews with a paper version of the diary’s content and allowed users to immediately experience and reflect on the diary’s intended administration mode. Because the PKD Daily was developed for clinical trial use, it assumes that the trial includes data collection about concomitant diuretic use and a baseline assessment of urinary events pre-treatment with tolvaptan as well as relatively consistent fluid intake throughout the trial. In other use cases, the diary might need to be supplemented with concomitant medication and fluid intake records.

Conclusion

The PKD Daily provides a useful case study in the development of hybrid diaries for better understanding the patient experience of a medication and its side effects. In the case of tolvaptan, the PKD Daily aims to provide researchers with important insights into the burden patients may experience in association with aquaretic effects such as frequent and urgent urination. The PKD Daily was originally developed to support endpoint-relevant data collection in a clinical trial. However, with further use and psychometric testing, data from the PKD Daily, or a similarly structured diary, could also provide valuable information to clinicians and patients for shared decision-making to support treatment decisions and inform dose selection in clinical practice. The PKD Daily, or other hybrid diaries like it, could therefore be a valuable part of a clinician’s toolbox, helping to identify areas for new research that can improve the patient experience.
