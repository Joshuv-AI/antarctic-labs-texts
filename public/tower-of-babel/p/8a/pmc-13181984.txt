A taste of (dis)trust: do social cynicism and gratitude predict and reciprocate addiction-like eating?
Authors: Hong Mian Yang, Bryant P H Hui, Hengyue Zhang, Haofeng Ling, Susana Jiménez-Murcia, Fernando Fernández-Aranda, Anise M S Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAddiction-like eating, characterized by impaired impulse control over food consumption and intense food cravings, is closely linked to overweight and poses both physical and mental health risks. Drawing on a social cognitive perspective, this study examined whether and how social beliefs (i.e., social cynicism) and social emotions (i.e., gratitude) are associated with addiction-like eating and tested their longitudinal interplay.MethodsA two-wave longitudinal survey with a six-month interval was administered via an online crowdsourcing platform in mainland China. At Wave 1 (W1), the sample comprised 1448 adults (50% females, Mage = 32.46 years, SD = 7.72), of whom 660 remained at Wave 2 (W2). Cross-lagged panel modeling was conducted to examine the reciprocal associations among social cynicism, gratitude, and addiction-like eating over time.ResultsSocial cynicism at W1 predicted higher levels of addiction-like eating at W2 (β = 0.07, p = .008), and vice versa (β = 0.08, p = .012). Gratitude at W1 predicted lower addiction-like eating at W2 (β = –0.10, p < .001), whereas W1 addiction-like eating predicted reduced W2 gratitude (β = –0.05, p = .031). Moreover, a significant unidirectional path was observed from gratitude at W1 to reduced W2 social cynicism (β = –0.16, p < .001).ConclusionsFindings revealed a bidirectional link between lower gratitude/higher social cynicism and greater risks of addiction-like eating. Targeted intervention strategies such as gratitude-centered reappraisal may serve as a complement to traditional symptom-focused approaches in addressing addiction-like eating patterns.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01586-x.Keywords: Food addiction, Social axioms, Gratitude, Cross-lagged panel model, Longitudinal design

Background

Addiction-like eating is a maladaptive eating pattern characterized by loss of control over eating and preoccupation with food [1]. While frequently observed in individuals with overweight and metabolic disorders, the clinical and public health significance of this eating pattern extends beyond weight status itself [2–5]. Research underscores its substantial psychosocial burden, including strong associations with depression, anxiety, emotional dysregulation, and heightened impulsivity [2, 6, 7]. It commonly co-occurs with other eating disorders and substance use and is linked to impaired self-control [6, 8–10]. Physiologically, addictive eating patterns are associated with neurobiological alterations, such as dysregulation of brain reward pathways and reduced cognitive control, which may directly contribute to adverse health outcomes [11, 12]. Therefore, addressing addiction-like eating is important given its multifaceted impact on mental health, behavioral regulation, and physiological well-being.

Addiction-like eating

Addiction-like eating is conceptualized as a behavioral addiction [1], despite its overlaps in terms of some symptoms (e.g., compulsive eating and loss of control over eating) with formally diagnosed eating disorders such as bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder [13]. However, situating it within an addiction framework holds distinct theoretical and clinical value. Traditional diagnostic models of eating disorders place greater emphasis on complex interactions with psychosocial factors, such as emotion dysregulation, body image distress, and excessive preoccupation with body weight and shape [14, 15]. For example, individuals with bulimia nervosa may engage in binge-purge behaviors to cope with negative emotions and concerns about weight control [14]. Binge-eating disorder is characterized by recurrent episodes of binge eating without compensatory behaviors, accompanied by a sense of loss of control during eating, as well as feelings of shame and disgust [15]. In contrast, the core of addiction-like eating focuses on the direct impact of food (especially those with high calory like sugar and fat and ultra-processed foods) on the brain’s reward system, sharing similar neurobiological mechanisms with substance use disorders, and it particularly highlights intense craving, tolerance, and withdrawal tendencies toward foods [16–19]. This perspective does not aim to replace existing diagnoses but serves as a complementary framework. It helps explain the behavioral mechanisms in some individuals—especially those with persistent, loss-of-control eating who do not meet criteria for typical eating disorders—and provides a rationale for interventions adapted from addiction science (e.g., cognitive-behavioral interventions targeting craving) [18, 20]. The validity of this complementary perspective is supported by converging evidence that addiction-like patterns of eating are not confined to eating disorder populations. These patterns have been consistently documented in the general population, among individuals with high body mass index (BMI) without comorbid eating disorders, as well as in other clinical groups without eating disorders (e.g., individuals with gambling disorder), underscoring the conceptualization of addiction-like eating as a transdiagnostic construct rather than a phenomenon confined to eating disorders [21–24].

Exploring significant correlates of addiction-like eating from a social cognitive perspective

Eating behavior is influenced by the dynamic interplay between individuals and their social environments [25, 26]. Within this framework, social cognitions—the mental processes that shape interactions with society and are expressed through social beliefs and social emotions —warrant greater research attention [27]. Social cognitive theory has been applied to the study of both eating behaviors and various addictive behaviors [28], including substance abuse and social media use [29, 30], primarily from the perspective of self-regulation. While these studies highlight the explanatory value of social cognitions for eating and addictive behaviors, they often neglect the core assumption of social cognitive theory and fail to consider that behavioral patterns, whether adaptive or maladaptive, exist in a reciprocal relationship with individuals’ cognitions (e.g., beliefs) and environmental conditions (e.g., resource availability) [31]. As a result, empirical evaluation of these reciprocal processes remains limited [30, 32]. Moreover, prior research suggests that beyond behavior-specific cognitions (e.g., outcome expectancies), more generalized social cognitions—such as social axioms—can function as risk or protective factors in the development of addictive behaviors. For example, social axioms have been implicated in Internet gaming disorder [33]. Yet, research examining the role of generalized social cognitions in addiction-like eating remains scarce. To address this gap, the present longitudinal study investigates whether a generalized social cognition (i.e., social cynicism) and a social emotion (i.e., gratitude) predict addiction-like eating. It also explores potential feedback loops to test whether addiction-like eating influences these cognitions and emotions.

Social cynicism and addiction-like eating

Social cynicism is a central dimension of social axioms, which are generalized social beliefs about oneself, the social environment, and the broader physical and spiritual world [34]. Social cynisism, in particular, reflects the belief that human nature is fundamentally dark, society is corrupt, and social institutions are untrustworthy [34, 35]. This worldview fosters negative attitudes towards social life [36], and prior research has consistently highlighted its “toxic” qualities, including negative associations with prosocial behaviors and with mental health indicators such as flourishing [37, 38]. More recently, social cynicism has been identified as a risk factor for behavioral addictions. For example, it has been positively associated with gambling frequency and is predictive of a higher tendency towards Internet gaming disorder [33, 39]. To date, however, no research has examined the role of social cynicism in eating behaviors. Therefore, investigating whether this generalized social cognition constitutes a risk factor for maladaptive eating addresses an important gap in existing research.

Social cynics are more likely to perceive others’ behavior as hostile (e.g., “colleagues intentionally ignore me”). This cognitive bias elevates their interpersonal stress and negative emotions [40, 41], which are established triggers for addiction-like eating [42]. Consequently, addiction-like eating may serve as their maladaptive coping strategy for managing negative emotions and stressful life events [43]. Social cynicism is also linked to greater reliance on escape- and avoidance-oriented coping [40, 44]. Taken together, individuals high in social cynicism may tend to engage in avoidance coping through excessive or dysregulated eating, thus increasing their vulnerability to addiction-like eating. Accordingly, we hypothesized that baseline social cynicism would predict higher levels of addiction-like eating at follow-up (Hypothesis 1).

Addiction-like eating may also intensify individuals’ cynical views towards the world. First, core features of addiction-like eating—such as loss of control, craving, and preoccupation with food-related cues—may directly undermine an individual’s sense of control and self-efficacy [45, 46]. This chronic frustration with self-regulation may generalize into a broader perceived lack of control and distrust toward the external world, including social institutions and others’ motives, which characterize social cynicism. Second, negative emotions such as guilt and shame that arise in response to uncontrolled eating—even in the absence of external weight stigma—may themselves foster a defensive, cynical worldview in which society is perceived as unjust and others as untrustworthy [45, 47, 48]. Therefore, we hypothesized that baseline addiction-like eating would predict higher levels of social cynicism at follow-up, even after controlling for BMI (Hypothesis 2).

Gratitude and addiction-like eating

Gratitude is conceptualized as a social emotion reflecting heartfelt recognition of and appreciation for kindness or support from others [49, 50]. It is often regarded as a positive socio-emotional strength arising from social cognitive processes, such as attributing success to external support (e.g., recognizing personal achievements as facilitated by others) and appraising benefactors’ intentions as stemming from genuine altruism rather than self-interest [51, 52]. A growing body of research has demonstrated that gratitude promotes both physical and psychological well-being and serves as a protective factor against psychological distress and disorders [53–56], including in patients with substance use disorders [57–59]. Negative associations between gratitude and behavioral addictions such as gaming disorder have also been reported [60].

Although no published study has directly examined the association between gratitude and addiction-like eating, evidence from intervention research indicates gratitude-based practices can reduce body dissatisfaction and dysfunctional eating behaviors [61, 62]. From an evolutionary perspective, gratitude functions to promote reciprocal altruism and prosocial relationships, enhancing long-term well-being even at the cost of short-term self-interest [52]. Notably, the prosocial behaviors and relationships fostered by gratitude may serve as a buffer against social isolation, which is recognized as a key maintaining factor for sub-clinical disordered eating [63, 64]. Gratitude encourages individuals to focus on delayed benefits and is associated with higher self-control [65–67], a well-documented protective factor against addictive behaviors [68]. Moreover, gratitude, as a positive socio-emotional strength, has been shown to enhance self-regulatory capacity, increase positive affect, and reduce substance abuse [69–72]. Higher levels of gratitude may directly buffer stress responses and decrease the likelihood of resorting to addiction-like eating as an emotional coping strategy. The facilitating role of gratitude in promoting healthy eating behaviors has been demonstrated in prior experimental research, which found both direct effect and indirect effect via reductions in negative emotions of gratitude on healthy eating behaviors [73]. From this viewpoint, we hypothesized that baseline gratitude would predict lower levels of addiction-like eating at follow-up (Hypothesis 3).

Addictive behaviors, including eating, provide immediate pleasure through short-term dopamine release. However, repeated engagement may blunt reward system sensitivity over time [74, 75]. Individuals with addiction-like eating may increasingly rely on food as a primary source of pleasure and become less responsive to positive experiences (e.g., receiving help from others), thereby reducing their capacity for gratitude. Addiction-like eating also requires sustained attention to food-related cravings, cues, and consumption opportunities [76, 77]. This narrowing of attention may displace the cognitive resources need to notice, appreciate, and savor positive life events. By remaining immersed in cycles of perceived loss of control and craving, individuals may find it more difficult to mentally pause and experience gratitude in the present moment [78]. Thus, addiction-like eating may directly undermine the experience of gratitude. Accordingly, we hypothesized that baseline addiction-like eating would predict lower levels of gratitude at follow-up (Hypothesis 4).

The present study

To sum up, the current study extends social cognitive theory by examining the longitudinal, bidirectional associations of addiction-like eating with both a generalized social cognition (social cynicism) and a social emotion (gratitude). Specifically, we investigate not only whether social cynicism and gratitude predict subsequent addiction-like eating but also whether addiction-like eating, in turn, influences these social processes. In doing so, we address the reciprocal dynamics emphasized by social cognitive theory. In addition, we consider previous cross-sectional research among working adults as preliminary evidence for a negative association between cynical beliefs and gratitude [79, 80]. Building on these findings, the present study further explores their dynamic interplay. To this end, we employ a cross-lagged panel model (CLPM; see Fig. 1 for the proposed model illustrating all four hypotheses) with both autoregressive and cross-lagged paths among gratitude, social cynicism, and addiction-like eating across two waves of data collection. This design allows for testing the two hypothesized bidirectional relationships: (a) between social cynicism and addiction-like eating, and (b) between gratitude and addiction-like eating. Findings from this study are expected to advance our understanding of the social cognitive mechanisms underlying addiction-like eating. They may also inform the development of preventive and intervention strategies that promote adaptive social beliefs and emotions to mitigate maladaptive eating patterns.

Methods

Procedure and participants

The present study comprised a two-wave longitudinal questionnaire survey, using the convenience sampling method via WenJuanXing (www.wjx.cn) to collect responses from Chinese adults. WenJuanXing is a Chinese online survey platform for research use with over 3 million pooled participants [81]. For this anonymous online survey, WenJuanXing distributed the first wave (W1) and second wave (W2) questionnaires in January 2024 and July 2024, respectively. Based on their records, 2,106 participants read the study purpose, signed the online informed consent, and completed the survey at W1. Among them, 658 were removed due to unreasonable response times or failure on two attention checks, leaving 1448 valid responses. All 1448 respondents were invited to take part in the follow-up questionnaire six months later, and 660 completed both waves and passed the validity checks. Ethical approval for the present study was obtained from the department of psychology of the affiliated university of the corresponding author (Reference#: DPSY/2023-23).

A sample of 1,448 Chinese adults, including 724 males (50%) and 724 females (50%), took part in the W1 survey, with ages ranging from 18 to 69 years (Mage = 32.46 years, SD = 7.72). At W2, the sample consisted of 303 males (45.9%) and 357 females (54.1%). The demographic characteristics of all participants are shown in Table 1. Measures

Addiction-like eating

The validated Chinese version of the Addiction-like Eating Behavior Scale was used to measure participants’ addiction-like eating behaviors in the past 12 months [83]. It has shown good validity and reliability among Chinese adults [83]. The scale is a 5-point Likert scale (ranging from 1 = never to 5 = always) and consists of 15 items (e.g., “I continued to eat despite feeling full”). A higher sum score indicates a higher risk of addiction-like eating. In the current study, the scale showed satisfactory internal reliability with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.92 at W1 and 0.93 at W2.

Social cynicism

In order to assess participants’ social cynicism beliefs, we adopted the social cynicism subscale from the short form of the Social Axioms Survey validated in a Chinese sample [35]. The subscale consists of four items (e.g., “Opportunities for people to get wealthy promote dishonesty”). Participants responded on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree. A higher mean score indicates greater endorsement of social cynicism beliefs. In this study, the subscale showed acceptable internal reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.72 at W1 and 0.73 at W2.

Gratitude

The Chinese version of the 6-item Gratitude Questionnaire-6 [50], validated among Chinese adults [84], was used to measure gratitude levels. This 7-point Likert scale ranges from 1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree. A sample item is “I have so much in life to be thankful for.” A higher mean score indicates a higher level of gratitude. The scale showed acceptable internal consistency reliability, with Cronbach’s alpha values of 0.75 at W1 and 0.78 at W2.

Demographic variables

Respondents reported their age (in years), sex (female or male), BMI (by reporting height and weight), and perceived socioeconomic status (from lower to upper class with a single item) in the questionnaire.

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics, reliability estimates, and Pearson correlations were computed in SPSS 27. To test the proposed model incorporating both hypothesized bidirectional and autoregressive effects, a CLPM analysis was conducted using Mplus 8.3 [85]. To address missing data arising from participant attrition between the two waves, full information maximum likelihood (FIML) estimation was employed, allowing for maximum use of available data, including those who dropped out at W2. Following CLPM analysis guidelines, the effects of all four demographic characteristics (i.e., sex, age, BMI, and perceived socioeconomic status ) were also controlled for in the psychological variables measured at W1 [86]. In addition to the Chi-square (χ2) test, model fit indices including the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI > 0.90), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.90), root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA < 0.06), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR < 0.08) were used to evaluate the goodness of fit of the proposed CLPM [87, 88].

In particular, there was a positive correlation between social cynicism and addiction-like eating, while gratitude was negatively correlated with addiction-like eating and social cynicism.

Testing the proposed CLPM

Results of the cross-lagged panel analysis indicated that the proposed model had excellent fit: χ2(12) = 13.44, p=.338, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.998, RMSEA = 0.009, 90%CI [0.000, 0.029], SRMR = 0.011 (for the resultant model, see Fig. 2). After controlling for autoregressive effects and demographic covariates, social cynicism at W1 significantly predicted higher risk of addiction-like eating at W2 (β = 0.07, p = .008), and higher addiction-like eating at W1 similarly predicted increased social cynicism at W2 (β = 0.08, p = .012), providing evidence for Hypotheses 1 and 2. Furthermore, gratitude at W1 predicted lower risks of addiction-like eating at W2 (β = –0.10, p < .001), and baseline addiction-like eating showed a significant and negative prospective effect on W2 gratitude (β = –0.05, p = .031), consistent with the expected effects outlined in Hypotheses 3 and 4. We also found that gratitude at W1 significantly predicted lower levels of social cynicism at W2 (β = –0.16, p < .001), while the path from W1 social cynicism to W2 gratitude, though negative as expected, was non-significant (β = –0.03, p = .227).

Sensitivity analyses

Given the substantial attrition between waves, sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of the findings against potential bias. First, a complete-case analysis was performed with the subsample of 660 participants who provided data at both time points. The two-wave CLPM demonstrated good fit in this subsample, χ2(12) = 13.44, p = .338, CFI = 0.999, TLI = 0.998, RMSEA = 0.013, 90% CI [0.000, 0.043], SRMR = 0.011, with the significance of all cross-lagged paths remaining unchanged (see Supplementary Table S1).

Second, the model was re-estimated using multiple imputation to handle missing data in the full initial sample (N = 1,448). This analysis also yielded satisfactory model fit, χ2(12) = 12.47, p = .409, CFI = 1.000, TLI = 0.999, RMSEA = 0.005, 90% CI [0.000, 0.028], SRMR = 0.011, and replicated the pattern of significant paths (see Supplementary Table S2). In both sensitivity analyses, demographic variables were all controlled for.

In sum, the consistent results across both analytical approaches confirm that the primary results are robust.

Discussion

This two-wave longitudinal study provides the first evidence of a reciprocal relationship between social cynicism, gratitude, and addiction-like eating. Specifically, W1 social cynicism (positively) and gratitude (negatively) significantly predicted W2 addiction-like eating, while W1 addiction-like eating predicted higher levels of W2 social cynicism and lower levels of W2 gratitude. In other words, the current findings support all Hypotheses 1 to 4, illustrating mutual influences between social cognitions/emotions and addictive eating behaviors.

Social cynics’ negative views of other people and society can cause attentional biases toward negative information in life [89]; they hence experience high levels of stress [90], which can trigger eating as a maladaptive coping strategy to deal with such stress as well as a means to gain a sense of trust and security that they cannot easily get in social interactions [91]. By contrast, gratitude orients individuals to see the goodness and kindness in others, and in this way, they can build good interpersonal relations and maintain a high-quality social network that can provide both satisfaction of relatedness needs and abundant social support [92, 93]. In this way, individuals may be less likely to rely on food, instead of social support, to deal with a variety of life stressors. On the other hand, negative consequences of addiction-like eating such as body image disturbance [94], negative social comparison [95], and self-stigma or perceived social stigma related to overweight and high BMI [96] may intensify individuals’ cynical views of the world and weaken their tendency to see the kindness in and support from the external world, resulting in higher levels of social cynicism and lower levels of gratitude. While the current study confirmed a bidirectional association between social cognitive factors (i.e., social cynicism and gratitude) and addiction-like eating, future research is warranted to explore more specific underlying psychological mechanisms, such as the potential mediating role of perceived stress, social support, and weight-related stigma.

Apart from the main findings regarding the reciprocity between social cynicism/gratitude and addiction-like eating, the present study also explored the longitudinal associations between social cynicism and gratitude. We found that gratitude predicted lower levels of social cynicism, but the reverse was not observed. This asymmetry may reflect the role of positive social emotions in promoting better social functioning. For example, grateful individuals tend to receive greater social support and maintain more stable, beneficial relationships [97, 98]. Moreover, people with high gratitude tend to attribute their successes to the support of others and experience more positive interpersonal attitudes [51]. Indeed, according to Fredrickson’s broaden-and-build theory [99], gratitude serves as an active and positive emotional contributor to the formation of cognitions. By expanding attentional flexibility and mindsets (e.g., the strength to notice and appreciate all the positive things in life) and constructing social resources (e.g., beneficial social bonds and alliances) [49, 51], it facilitates the deconstruction of a cynical cognitive framework. Conversely, social cynicism, as a negative cognition accompanied by narrowed attention and thoughts (e.g., attentional bias towards negative information and hostile attribution bias) [89], may not allow generating constructive internal (e.g., affective) states, explaining the observed unidirectional pathway. Taken together with our findings that gratitude predicts both lower levels of social cynicism and reduced addiction-like eating, it is possible that gratitude exerts an indirect effect on addiction-like eating through its influence on social cynicism. This hypothesis could be tested in future longitudinal studies with three or more waves.

Implications

There are several notable theoretical and practical implications of the current study. From a theoretical perspective, the findings indicate that generalized social cognitions and emotions are salient factors in addiction-like eating. The bidirectional relationships between addiction-like eating and both social cynicism and gratitude provide empirical support for the reciprocity between “person” and “behavior” highlighted in social cognitive theory [31]. Future research could examine “environmental” factors (e.g., social stigma against overweight and social support) as predictors of behavioral addictions and further evaluate the triadic reciprocal determinism posited by the theory.

From a sociocognitive perspective, this study identified bidirectional associations between social cognitive factors (lower gratitude/higher social cynicism) and increased risk of addiction-like eating, as they mutually exacerbate each other over time, suggesting important implications for both early-stage prevention and treatment. From a social cognitive perspective, the following prevention strategies may help disrupt this bidirectional association: (1) Cultivating gratitude through daily practices, such as keeping gratitude journals to activate a positive emotional-social support loop [71, 100]; (2) Hosting cognitive appraisal workshops (e.g., using attributional retraining to correct automatic hostile attribution biases) to reduce cynical beliefs about others and society [101]; (3) Encouraging individuals with addiction-like eating symptoms to seek support from family members and mental health professionals, who can foster conditions that elicit gratitude and counteract the social alienation perpetuated by cynicism. By targeting and mitigating the reciprocal relationship between social cognitive factors (i.e., social cynicism and gratitude) and addiction-like eating, this three-pronged prevention strategy may reduce their mutual reinforcement and lower associated risk. In addition to informing prevention strategies, our findings suggest that social cynicism and gratitude may serve not only as screening indicators for at-risk cases but also as targets for adjunctive intervention. Clinicians may also benefit from considering how social experiences shape these cognitive factors and contribute to maladaptive eating patterns, integrating this perspective into clinical practice.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has the following limitations. Firstly, the use of convenience sampling may limit the generalizability of its findings to broader adult populations. Future research could examine the replicability of these findings in larger, more chronologically and ethnically diverse samples using probability sampling. Secondly, our two-wave design with a 6-month interval provided the first but preliminary longitudinal evidence, which may not capture shorter-term emotional or behavioral fluctuations or longer-term developmental patterns (e.g., between-person changes in social cognitions). Thirdly, while self-reported measures allowed a cost-effective means of assessment, further studies may benefit from incorporating objective measures (e.g., daily records of eating frequencies and food consumption) to complement subjective reports. Through more intensive longitudinal designs or multi-method approaches, a more comprehensive understanding of addiction-like eating could be gained in subsequent research.

Conclusions

Addiction-like eating has been recognized as a pressing public health concern, contributing to psychological distress and increased risk of overweight [102]. Taking the social cognitive perspective, this two-wave longitudinal study provides the first empirical evidence of reciprocal associations among generalized social cognitions (i.e., social cynicism), social emotions (i.e., gratitude), and addiction-like eating. Specifically, the findings indicate that social cynicism can exacerbate addictive eating patterns, whereas gratitude can serve as a protective factor against such behaviors. These results may point to clinically actionable pathways, suggesting that social-cognitive interventions—particularly gratitude-centered reappraisal—may complement traditional symptom-focused approaches in mitigating addiction-like eating, especially among individuals with cynical attitudes towards society.
