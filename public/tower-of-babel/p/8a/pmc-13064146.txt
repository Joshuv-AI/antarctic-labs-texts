Evaluation of equity in quality improvement for multiarterial grafting in females and patients with low socioeconomic status: retrospective analysis of a statewide collaborative database
Authors: Catherine Wagner, Patricia F Theurer, Melissa Clark, Chang He, Thomas Braun, Fouad Azoury, Hisham G Qandeel, Dimitrios E Magouliotis, James Martin, Andrew Pruitt, Ahmet C Topcu, Gorav Ailawadi, Donald S Likosky, Michael P Thompson, Francis D Pagani, Robert B Hawkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesQuality collaboratives improve quality of care at the hospital and collaborative levels, but less is understood about how such efforts affect patient-level disparities. This study evaluated how a quality improvement (QI) effort (increasing multiarterial grafting during coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG)) translated into populations which historically receive lower-quality care (females and patients of low socioeconomic status).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingAll non-federal hospitals in the state of Michigan that perform cardiac surgery and participate in a statewide collaborative database (n=33).ParticipantsPatients undergoing first-time, isolated CABG receiving at least two bypass grafts from 2011 to 2022 were identified.Primary endpointsAssociation of sex and socioeconomic status with multiarterial grafting was evaluated across the study period. The distressed community index (DCI), a socioeconomic ranking (0—not distressed, 100—severely distressed), was matched to the patient’s zip code. Hierarchical regression modelling was performed to associate DCI and sex with multiarterial grafting, incorporating patient factors and hospital and surgeon effects. A sex-surgery year and DCI-surgery year interaction term was performed to assess the change in the rate of multiarterial grafting.ResultsA total of 40 322 patients underwent CABG at 33 centres with a median age of 66 years and 24% were female. The rate of multiarterial grafting was 15%, although lower among females (10% vs 17%) and the highest (vs lowest) DCI quartile (14% vs 18%). After risk adjustment, females were less likely to receive multiarterial grafting (ORadj 0.51 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.58), p<0.001). Patients living in zip codes with a higher DCI had lower adjusted odds of receiving multiarterial grafting (ORadj 0.35 per 10-point increase (95% CI 0.24 to 0.51), p<0.001), a finding that was independent of study time (sex-year and DCI-year interaction terms p>0.05).ConclusionsDespite a large overall increase in multiarterial grafting due to QI efforts, females and patients with low socioeconomic status had lower rates of multiarterial grafting. QI efforts should be evaluated both overall and among patients who historically receive lower quality care to improve quality and equity.Keywords: Quality Improvement, SURGERY, Cardiac surgery, Health Equity

Introduction

Physician-led quality collaboratives have pioneered quality improvement (QI) across hospital systems.1,4 When focusing on improving a quality measure, collaboratives often track the quality measure at the hospital or collaborative level, share these results in real-time with the invested parties and work together to identify strategies to increase utilisation of the quality measure. Through active QI, quality collaboratives are effective in increasing quality of care, improving adherence to guideline-recommended therapies and reducing the overall rate of morbidity at the hospital and collaborative level.5,8

However, less well studied is how QI is distributed at the patient level. Specifically, there may be certain patient groups where improvement is concentrated or lacking, which could inadvertently lead to patient-level disparities.9,12 Evaluation of how disparities are affected by QI initiatives could inform future efforts by quality collaboratives to both improve patient care and eliminate long-standing disparities.

In that context, this study evaluated how quality collaborative efforts translated to populations who are historically less likely to receive high-quality care. A statewide quality collaborative’s initiative to increase multiarterial grafting during coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) was evaluated in traditionally marginalised populations (eg, females, patients from low socioeconomic status),13 14 to evaluate how QI efforts affect patient-level disparities.

Methods

Quality collaborative approach to QI

The Michigan Society of Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgeons Quality Collaborative (MSTCVS-QC) is a statewide quality collaborative that was formed by surgeons and payors with the common goal of improving cardiac surgical care for patients in the state of Michigan. The MSTCVS-QC has decades of experience in QI, and the collaborative has strong relationships with surgeons in the state and has been effective in changing surgeon practice.15,8 15 16

Multiarterial grafting (eg, use of multiple arterial conduits vs venous conduits during CABG) is associated with superior graft patency and improved long-term survival and is now a class 1 recommendation during CABG.17,19 Despite the strong evidence for multiarterial grafting, it is historically underused in the USA.20 Due to the increasing evidence for multiarterial grafting, the MSTCVS-QC identified multiarterial grafting as a QI initiative in 2016, and undertook a three-pronged approach for QI to increase utilisation.1 7 First, experts in multiarterial grafting were invited to give talks and share evidence and techniques for multiarterial grafting. Second, unblinded data of each hospital’s overall rate of multiarterial grafting were shared at the MSTCVS-QC quarterly meeting. At least one surgeon representative from each hospital must attend the quarterly meeting as a requirement to participate in the quality collaborative. Finally, a group of expert coronary surgeons in the MSTCVS-QC wrote best practice recommendations for the implementation of multiarterial grafting during CABG.

Data and methods availability

Because of the sensitive nature of the data collected for this study, patient-level data are unavailable to be accessed. All analytic methods and codes are available on reasonable request. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines were followed.

Data sources

The primary data source was the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Adult Cardiac Surgery Database (STS ACSD) through the MSTCVS-QC. The MSTCVS-QC consists of all 33 non-federal centres in the state of Michigan performing cardiac surgery. All centres share their STS ACSD data and outcomes with the MSTCVS-QC. MSTCVS-QC employs data managers to audit submitted data for accuracy.

The distressed community index (DCI) is an economic measure to describe social vulnerability and disparity based on zip code, created by the Economic Innovation Group.21 DCI is available for over 99% of the population of the USA. DCI is based on seven measures: (1) per cent of the population without a high school diploma (or equivalent); (2) per cent of vacant houses; (3) per cent of adults in the prime age (25–54) not working; (4) per cent of population living at or below the poverty line; (5) ratio of the median household income in the zip code to the median household income of the surrounding metropolitan area, or state if the zip code is rural; (6) per cent change in the number of jobs; (7) per cent change in the number of businesses.21 Based on these seven components, each zip code receives a score from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating a more distressed community. DCI is a previously established measure to describe socioeconomic factors that may contribute to outcomes disparities among vulnerable populations.22 23

Patient cohort

Patients undergoing isolated CABG with two or more bypass grafts at one of the 33 participating centres in the MSTCVS-QC were identified from 2011 to 2022. Patients with a history of mediastinal radiation, prior cardiac surgery, emergent/salvage status and subclavian stenosis were excluded as these are ‘acceptable’ reasons in the STS ACSD to not use the internal mammary artery for bypass grafting, which is a standard arterial graft to use during CABG (online supplemental consort figure 1).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was receipt of multiarterial grafting during isolated CABG. Receipt of multiarterial grafting was determined using the STS ACSD descriptions of the type of conduit used in bypass grafting and specifically the number of bypasses performed with an arterial conduit.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD or median with IQR based on normality. Categorical variables are presented as a number and a percentage. Groups were compared with a Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed continuous variables and χ2 test for categorical variables.

A hierarchical logistic regression was performed to model predictors of multiarterial grafting. Our covariates of interest were patient sex, race and DCI. DCI was modelled as a continuous variable. The model also included patient-specific factors that are known to impact the potential risk versus benefit of multiarterial grafting. Specifically, body mass index, diabetic control (eg, no diabetes, oral agents, insulin), chronic lung disease severity and immunosuppression were included to account for risk in the use of bilateral mammary arteries. Preoperative creatinine was included to account for the risk of using the radial artery. Age was included as older patients may have less benefit from arterial grafts. The surgery year was included to account for changes in practices that may lead to differential rates of multiarterial grafting over the study period. Surgeon and hospital effects were modelled as crossed random effects because some surgeons practise at multiple hospitals concurrently. Marginal predicted means were used to calculate risk-adjusted rates of multiarterial grafting by sex and year. Interaction terms for sex and race, sex and DCI, and race and DCI were included in the model and were not significant; thus, they were not included in the final analysis.

Finally, to assess if QI efforts narrowed disparities in multiarterial grafting, interaction terms between sex and year, and DCI and year, were evaluated to determine if there were differential rates of odds in multiarterial grafting by year by the exposures of interest. If the interaction term between the exposure and year was statistically significant, the yearly odds of multiarterial grafting differed significantly by the exposure (ie, females had a higher odds of multiarterial grafting each year than males, therefore the disparity was narrowing).

The alpha level for statistical significance was 0.05. All analyses were performed with SAS V.9.4 by CH.

Patient and public involvement

Due to the sensitive nature of data in this study and data use agreements, patients and the public were not involved in the study design or conduct of the study. It was not appropriate or possible to involve patients or the public in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Patient demographics and operative characteristics

A total of 40 322 patients underwent first-time isolated CABG at 33 centres. The median age was 66 (IQR 59–73) years and 24% (n=9639) were female (table 1). Females were more likely to be black (11% vs 6%) than males and had a higher median DCI (51, IQR 25–72) than males (43, IQR 18–66), indicating a lower socioeconomic status. Females had more comorbidities, including a higher prevalence of diabetes (55% vs 45%), and chronic lung disease (37% vs 31%), and were more likely to be an urgent (vs elective) case than males (66% vs 60%). Mirroring these results, patients from zip codes with a higher DCI (the top quartile of DCI) were older and more likely to be black than patients from zip codes with a lower DCI (the bottom quartile of DCI) (online supplemental table 1). Patients from zip codes with a higher DCI had more comorbidities and were more likely to be an urgent (vs elective) case. Females received fewer bypass grafts than males (table 2). However, patients from zip codes with a higher DCI received more bypass grafts compared with patients from zip codes with a lower DCI (online supplemental table 2).

Multiarterial grafting

The unadjusted rate of multiarterial grafting was 15%, and the radial artery was used in 10% of patients and the right internal mammary artery was used in 7% of patients (p=0.16) (figure 1). The rate of multiarterial grafting was lower among females (10% vs 17%) and patients from zip codes with a higher DCI (top quartile DCI vs bottom quartile DCI, 14% vs 18%). While overall odds of receiving multiarterial grafting increased throughout the study period (2012 vs 2018, ORadj 0.20 (95% CI 0.17 to 0.22), p<0.001) (figure 2), females were less likely to receive multiarterial grafting compared with males (ORadj 0.51 (95% CI 0.45 to 0.58), p<0.001). Overall, this corresponded to a risk-adjusted rate of 1.50% (95% CI 0.94% to 2.37%) for females and 2.96% (95% CI 1.89% to 4.60%) for males (figure 3A). Similarly, patients from zip codes with a higher DCI had lower odds of receiving multiarterial grafting (ORadj 0.35 per 10-point increase (95% CI 0.24 to 0.51), p<0.001), corresponding to risk-adjusted rates of 1.95% (95% CI 1.24% to 3.05%) for patients from higher DCI and 2.32% (95% CI 1.47% to 3.63%) for patients from lower DCI (figure 3B). Race (black vs white) was not significantly associated with multiarterial grafting (ORadj 0.88 (95% CI 0.76 to 1.02) p=0.24). The impact of sex and DCI on odds of multiarterial grafting did not differ by surgery year (p>0.05), thus these disparities did not narrow over time and instead were persistent throughout the study period.

Discussion

This study evaluating how QI efforts translated to patient-level disparities has three main findings. First, quality collaborative efforts successfully increased multiarterial grafting for all patients. Patients who underwent surgery after QI efforts had fivefold increased odds of multiarterial grafting than those prior to the QI efforts. Second, there were disparities in the receipt of multiarterial grafting, with females and patients from highly distressed zip codes less likely to receive multiarterial grafting. Third, these disparities in receipt of multiarterial grafting by sex and socioeconomic status persisted despite improved quality overall. Taken together, measuring and addressing disparities should occur at the onset and throughout QI initiatives to equitably improve care for all patients.

Prior work has found that at-risk patients (eg, females, patients from highly distressed zip codes) undergoing CABG may be less likely to receive multiarterial grafting.1314 20 22 24,28 Although there are long-term benefits of multiarterial grafting, multiarterial grafting can increase the risk of short-term complications, which may influence surgeon perception of the risk versus benefit of multiarterial grafting, particularly in females and patients from highly distressed zip codes who are higher risk with more comorbidities.14 22 23 Additionally, for sex disparities specifically, females have smaller anatomy which may make the use of multiarterial grafting more technically challenging.29 The challenges of multiarterial grafting in females and patients from highly distressed zip codes were reflected in this study, as these patient groups were less likely to receive multiarterial grafting. However, despite these challenges, this study shows that utilisation of multiarterial grafting can be increased in these patient groups and highlights an opportunity to continue to improve care for these at-risk populations.

Although QI efforts successfully increased multiarterial grafting, there were persistent disparities in the surgical quality of care. Prior work has found that QI measures may inadvertently contribute to persistent disparities, if QI is concentrated in patients already receiving high-quality care, and if the success of an intervention is measured overall, without stratification by at-risk groups.9 10 Applying these findings, disparities in multiarterial grafting may have persisted because: (1) patients where multiarterial grafting is the least technically demanding and lowest risk (eg, males and patients from not distressed zip codes, ‘low hanging fruit’)—may be the first to see benefit from QI efforts and (2) there was not intentional measurement and tracking of disparities at the onset of the QI effort.9 10 12 Quality collaboratives have already drastically improved quality of care for patients, but there is an opportunity to leverage pre-existing QI strategies and infrastructure to not only increase quality of care for patients, but to eliminate long-standing gaps in care for the most at-risk patients. Successful QI should not only include uptake of practices, but also equitable practice across all populations.

Our work has several implications for key stakeholders. First, for quality collaboratives, evaluation of the impact of QI efforts could be evaluated specifically for at-risk cohorts (ie, by sex, race and socioeconomic status) instead of simply at the collaborative level to narrow disparities in care. Second, payors could introduce metrics that evaluate quality in at-risk populations, such as the rate of multiarterial grafting in females or patients with low socioeconomic status. These metrics could draw greater attention to variation in procedural quality and may lead to more resources and attention to narrow disparity. Though such metrics risk disadvantaging surgeons serving at-risk populations without adequate risk adjustment, financial incentives have proven particularly effective.30 31 Finally, the profession of cardiac surgery has pioneered measurement of surgical quality. The next frontier may be through not just measuring quality, but actively working with the data and surgeons together to ensure that all patients have access to high-quality care.

This paper has several limitations. First, female patients and patients from areas of lower socioeconomic status had greater comorbidity burden than males and patients from areas of higher socioeconomic status, and our findings may be due to unmeasured confounding or selection bias. However, the STS database is among the most clinically granular and prior work has found females and patients with low socioeconomic status may be less likely to receive multiarterial grafting and high-quality care. Our work highlights an opportunity to identify how these disparities are affected by QI efforts. Next, no angiographic data were available to determine eligibility for multiarterial grafting, nor was the performance of the Allen or Barbeau test used to determine eligibility for radial artery harvest, which may lead to selection bias. However, most patients receiving at least two bypass grafts are likely eligible for multiarterial grafting, except for patients who receive a bypass graft to the right coronary artery with 70%–89% stenosis, and there are no clear sex or socioeconomic status differences in collateral ulnar blood supply to explain the differences found in this study. Additionally, DCI is based on a patient’s zip code, and not a measure of an individual’s socioeconomic status. However, DCI is a metric for the socioeconomic status of a patient’s community, which may contribute to known drivers of outcome, such as access to care and available resources. Moreover, incorporation of DCI into risk models has been shown to improve surgical risk adjustment in postoperative outcomes. DCI may be a valuable tool to ‘measure’ social risk factors that contribute to certain populations’ worse outcomes.22 23 Finally, there may be centre-level factors such as volume or availability of an advanced practice provider to assist with harvest that may contribute to the disparity identified in this study. To account for surgeon and centre level variation, a hierarchical model with surgeon and hospital as random effects was performed, but greater evaluation of multiarterial grafting and disparities by hospital may identify strategies to narrow gaps in practice patterns.

In summary, quality collaboratives are an incredible tool that led to dramatic improvement in multiarterial grafting for patients undergoing cardiac surgery in the state of Michigan. However, despite overall improvement, there were persistent disparities in the receipt of multiarterial grafting for females and patients from socially distressed zip codes. The existing quality collaborative system can be leveraged to successfully narrow these disparities and improve care for all patients. To do so, measurement and tracking of disparities at the onset and throughout QI efforts is essential for equitable QI.
