Endotracheal bleeding after arterial switch operation: Think of a bleeding major aortopulmonary collateral artery
Authors: Benjamin von der Emde, Luigi Di Pasquale, Robert Cesnjevar, Oliver Kretschmar, Walter Knirsch, Hitendu Dave
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) in the setting of d-transposition of the great arteries (d-TGA) are a rare and occult finding, as they are often asymptomatic and difficult to detect in preoperative imaging. Consequences of undiagnosed MAPCAs in d-TGA are varied. Rarely, they lead to a catastrophic perioperative bronchial bleeding. Early identification and coiling are crucial. We report the case of a male neonate with d-TGA and ventricular septal defect (VSD) who experienced intrabronchial bleeding by the end of the arterial switch and VSD closure procedure, leading to inability to adequately ventilate and perioperative extracorporeal membrane oxygenation. Early identification and coil embolization were critical in identifying and managing the bleeding collaterals. This case underscores the need for employing emergent cath-angiography to diagnose previously unknown MAPCAs and thus salvaging the child from a dreadful but treatable cause. To our knowledge, this is one of the very few successfully treated cases of arterial switch operation complicated with MAPCA-induced perioperative bronchial bleeding.

INTRODUCTION

Major aortopulmonary collateral arteries (MAPCAs) are congenital vessels that arise from either the aorta or its first branch vessels and are distally connected to the pulmonary arterial vasculature.[1] Patients with d-transposition of the great arteries (d-TGA) present a rare aberration, with few cases published in the literature.[23] Most MAPCAs remain clinically irrelevant; however, complications such as acute respiratory distress syndrome, left heart dilation, or arrhythmia have been reported.[2] In most extreme cases, MAPCA hemorrhage can lead to hemodynamically relevant blood loss, as well as respiratory failure.[3]

Here we present the case of a term-born neonate with MAPCA hemorrhage after arterial switch operation (ASO), along with its diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

CASE REPORT

A term-born (gestational age: 39 + 3, birthweight 3400 g) boy with d-TGA, along with a nonrestrictive atrial septal defect as well as a large inlet-outlet ventricular septal defect (VSD), was transferred to our hospital on day one of life.

The child was admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) for continuous monitoring, and prostaglandin E1 infusion was started. The patient underwent an ASO with LeCompte maneuver, and VSD closure with two patches (the outlet extension of the VSD was underestimated while sizing the patch, resulting in a need for a second patch) on day 12 of life. Intraoperatively, the patient also displayed an anomalous coronary pattern, with the left coronary artery (LCA) and right coronary artery both arising from a large, right-sided sinus, with the LCA making an anterior loop. The left-sided sinus gave rise to a small conal artery. Mobilization and transfer of the coronary arteries were performed without complications. There was no decisive impression of excessive noncoronary return in the left heart during the course of the operation.

After a difficult, but successful ASO (cardio-pulmonary bypass [CPB] time: 352 min, aortic clamp time: 211 min), cardiac structures and myocardial function were sufficient to wean from CPB, with intraoperative echocardiography showing sufficient biventricular function under minimal inotropic support with milrinone (0.5 µg/kg/min) and a small residual VSD (<3 mm). However, persistent intrabronchial bleeding leading to ventilatory failure necessitated veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) support, with direct cannulation of both the right atrium and the ascending aorta.

The amount of bronchial bleeding undulated, and a post-CPB diffuse bleeding was hypothesized. Due to persistent bronchial bleeding with no conclusive source in bronchoscopy, angiography was performed on postoperative day (POD) one. Angiography revealed the presence of three MAPCAs supplying the right upper lobe, with one showing a rupture into the bronchus, which explained the intrabronchial bleeding. Two of the MAPCAs draining to the right upper lobe were embolized using a coil (Co. Balt), while the other vessel was deemed too small for intervention. MAPCA coiling led to the immediate cessation of pulmonary bleeding. The patient could be weaned off ECMO on POD four. However, due to high-output chylothorax and pulmonary hypertension aggravated by residual VSD, the patient could not be weaned off mechanical ventilation.

After 3 weeks of conservative anti-failure therapy with levosimendan and milrinone (to counter systolic as well as diastolic cardiac dysfunction), pulmonary artery (PA) banding to neutralize the residual VSD-induced shunt was performed on POD21. PA banding was selected over surgical VSD closure due to the high risk of reoperation (inability to close the defect completely and the risk of complete AV block) as well as the high likelihood of spontaneous VSD closure. Simultaneous pleurectomy and pleurodesis were performed. The effect on pulmonary edema is evident in Figure 2. Due to postoperative high-output chylothorax, as well as transfusion-related lung injury, the patient could only be successfully extubated on POD54.

After a prolonged recovery (involving conservative therapy for chylothorax followed by decortication) due to complications mentioned above, the patient was discharged from the ICU 4 months after the initial operation and left the hospital after a further 3 weeks in a very good general condition. At discharge, he was under cardiac antifailure, antiarrhythmic, and antibiotic therapy (captopril, hydrochlorothiazide, spironolactone, iron supplements, amiodarone, amphotericin B, and co-trimoxazole). There were no clinical signs suggestive of neurological or other organ dysfunction at the time of discharge.

Surgical PA de-banding, reconstruction, and direct closure of a mid-muscular (unrelated to the peri-membranous) small VSD was performed at 1.5 years of age.

At the 5-year follow-up, the child is thriving, showing normal signs of physiological and cognitive development.

Transthoracic two-dimensional echocardiography shows normal biventricular function and valve competency with no residual VSD.

CASE DISCUSSION

During early fetal development, the lung buds are supplied by segmental arteries that arise from the aorta. Around day 40 of gestation, these segmental arteries atrophy, and branches derived from the sixth brachial arch, which will become the pulmonary arteries, develop as the exclusive supply of the lungs. In some cases, especially in the setting of restricted pulmonary arterial blood flow (such as in patients with pulmonary atresia and tetralogy of Fallot), the segmental arteries persist as MAPCAs.[1]

These vessels result in a left-to-right shunt, distributing high-pressure oxygenated blood from the aorta to the pulmonary circulation, leading to pulmonary and left ventricular hypercirculation, which can result in cardiorespiratory failure.[1] While MAPCAs in pulmonary atresia often are the only source of pulmonary blood flow in some lung segments, the MAPCAs in d-TGA are almost always superfluous (subsidiary blood supply to the lungs).

MAPCAs, as a perioperative complicating factor, have been well documented in association with certain congenital heart diseases, such as left ventricular hypoplasia and pulmonary atresia, where their prevalence ranges from 53% to 77% of all cases.[1] In these cases, depending on the singularity of the duality of blood supply to the pulmonary segment in question, MAPCAs are either unfocalized or coil embolized/surgically closed.[4] However, very few reports exist on MAPCAs coexisting with d-TGA.

In a case series published by Doulamis et al., it was found that five of 671 patients (0.8%) with d-TGA after ASO had clinically relevant MAPCAs.[5] This differs quite significantly from numbers published by Wipf et al., who found that relevant MAPCAs could be identified in 15% (15/98) of all patients after ASO.[2]

This discrepancy could be because Wipf et al. and Doulamis et al. performed cardiac catheterization (CC) at a different rate after ASO, and only about half of all MAPCAs found on CC can, in fact, be detected by echocardiography.[2]

Presence of hemodynamically relevant MAPCAs in d-TGA could have significant perioperative consequences. They may cause significant steal due to left-to-right shunt from the systemic circulation, resulting in low cardiac output, coronary and visceral malperfusion. This, in turn, may lead to prolonged postoperative ICU stay and a higher rate of complications like necrotizing enterocolitis. This report aims to sensitize the pediatric cardiac caregivers so that rapid diagnostic and therapeutic measures can be initiated. In our case, the uncontrolled bronchial bleeding during the ASO would warrant immediate CC to mitigate the impact of bleeding, transfusions, and ECMO.[15]

To this date, no significant and reliable parameters for the prediction of MAPCA have been found.[2] However, if a patient shows unusually low cardiac output before or after ASO, excessive noncoronary return to the left atrium or intrabronchial bleeding, collaterals could be suspected. Unfortunately, these parameters are unreliable and can be absent, as was the case in our patient.[25]

Clinically, MAPCAs usually become symptomatic with signs of increased pulmonary blood flow and corresponding reduced systemic cardiac output, rather than hemorrhage. As a result, patients suffer cardiac and respiratory failure with pulmonary edema and pleural effusions. After successful interventional closure of MAPCAs, symptoms resolve rapidly, and patients can be weaned off ventilation.[678] We only found one other case report, in which MAPCA-related pulmonary hemorrhage was severe enough to warrant postoperative ECMO treatment.[9] In that case, reported by Golej et al., persisting respiratory failure could not be managed, even after MAPCA coiling, resulting in the death of the patient 10 days postoperatively.

CONCLUSIONS

If a patient shows unusually low cardiac output before ASO, excessive noncoronary return intraoperatively, slow recovery after ASO, with signs of respiratory and or cardiac failure and/or pulmonary hemorrhage, MAPCAs should be suspected and immediately sought after using CC and angiography.
