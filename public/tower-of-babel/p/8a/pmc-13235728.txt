Malicious poisoning of a canine with the neuromuscular blocking agent rocuronium: a case report with postmortem quantitation
Authors: John P Buchweitz, Justin A Zyskowski, Kevin Cajigas, Jason W Brooks
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRocuronium bromide is a non-depolarizing neuromuscular blocking agent (NMBA) commonly used in anesthesia for its rapid onset and intermediate duration of action. While its therapeutic use has been well-documented in humans and experimentally in dogs, this report describes the first known case of its malicious use in the fatal intoxication of a canine. Herein we describe the case of a six-year-old male Plott Hound found deceased under suspicious circumstances. Postmortem investigation revealed focal hemorrhages near potential injection sites, and toxicological analysis via liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry confirmed the presence of rocuronium in muscle tissue, heart blood, and urine. The dog had no surgical history or indication for anesthesia. However, rocuronium concentrations mirrored those seen in anesthetized human patients under ventilatory support. The owner, a licensed pharmacist, was suspected of drug theft and aggravated animal abuse. This case highlights the lethality of NMBAs when misused outside of the clinical setting and further underscores a collaborative, multi-institutional, multidisciplinary approach to veterinary forensic investigations.

Introduction

Rocuronium bromide is a monoquaternary aminosteroid non-depolarizing NMBA with an established use in both human and veterinary anesthesia [1–3]. In clinical practice, rocuronium has been demonstrated to have a rapid onset with an intermediate duration of neuromuscular blockade, making it suitable for procedures requiring endotracheal intubation and muscle relaxation [4]. Rocuronium acts by competitively binding to nicotinic cholinergic receptors at the neuromuscular junction, and its effects are readily reversible with neostigmine, edrophonium, and sugammadex [5, 6].

Despite its therapeutic utility in the clinical setting, rocuronium has been implicated in multiple cases of intentional misuse and fatal intoxication in humans [7–11]. In one case, a 35-year-old male fatally injected himself with hospital-sourced rocuronium, resulting in respiratory arrest due to diaphragmatic paralysis [10]. Similarly, Smędra et al. (2021) reported a suicide involving a paramedic who administered a fatal combination of morphine, etomidate, diazepam, and rocuronium while on duty. In both cases, the presence of rocuronium in postmortem samples confirmed its potential role in lethal respiratory paralysis.

To date, cases involving the malicious use of rocuronium in a veterinary context remain undocumented. This report describes a unique case for its intentional misuse in the intoxication of a canine, with implications for both forensic toxicology and veterinary medicine.

Case history

In mid-July 2024, the Harford County Sheriff’s Department was notified by a concerned citizen regarding the suspected improper disposal of controlled drug substances and paraphernalia in a residential waste receptacle. On-site investigation revealed multiple needles, syringes, and glass medication vials. One vial had been mostly stripped of its identifying label, while the second retained its original label bearing the drug name “Succinylcholine” (Fig. 1A and B). The discovery of these items occurred two days after the ­unexpected death of a dog residing in a neighboring home, prompting heightened concern and suspicion by those reporting the finding.

(B) Yellow capped vial with stripped label and National Drug Code identifier visible. Images of FDA-approved manufacturer drug labels for Succinylcholine Chloride and Rocuronium Bromide offset to the right of the vials.

The canine decedent was a 6-year-old male Plott Hound with an unremarkable medical history apart from occasional episodes of gastrointestinal upset characterized by the vomiting of bile and grass. The animal’s physical and behavioral condition had otherwise been assessed as normal. The dog was found suddenly dead in the home. The dog’s male owner initially arranged for routine cremation through a local veterinary clinic, where the body was frozen prior to its transportation to the crematorium. According to neighbors, however, the owner had previously expressed outward verbal displeasure with the animal. Of particular importance to the case, this individual was employed as a licensed pharmacist at a local hospital. He therefore possessed the knowledge of, and potential access to, neuromuscular blocking agents, making him a primary subject of the investigation.

Law enforcement personnel subsequently intercepted the animal’s remains prior to cremation and transported the carcass to the Animal Diagnostic Laboratory at The Pennsylvania State University for a forensic necropsy.

Necropsy

On postmortem examination, the dog was in good nutritional condition, with a body condition score estimated at a 5 out of 9 on the Purina body condition scale. There was no evidence of recent antemortem medical or surgical intervention. Following reflection of the skin, there was a 10 cm × 5 cm area of hemorrhage and edema in the subcutis and skeletal muscle of the right shoulder (supraspinatus) and a 5 cm × 2 cm area of hemorrhage in the subcutis and skeletal muscle of the left ventral neck near the jugular groove in the area of the mandibular salivary gland. Thoracic and abdominal viscera and the brain were grossly within normal limits. No significant microscopic changes were detected in the tissues examined. Fecal parasitology was negative. Urine, heart blood, and skeletal muscle were collected at the time of necropsy and frozen. Frozen samples were submitted to an AAVLD fully accredited diagnostic laboratory (Michigan State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory) for toxicological analysis.

Toxicology

Submitted drug vial labels were closely examined and found to have evidence for National Drug Code identifiers still intact (Fig. 1A and B). The codes were compared with the National Drug Code database and were consistent with FDA-approved labels for succinylcholine chloride and rocuronium bromide (Fig. 1). Subsequent confirmation of these analytes in the drug vials and quantitation in tissues and bodily fluids were performed by in-house, validated, liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) methods. Details of these methods are described herein and in the supplemental files.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Rocuronium bromide, succinylcholine chloride, succinylmonocholine chloride (United States Pharmacopeia Reference Standard), verapamil (Cerilliant reference material certified at 1.0 mg/mL in methanol), and ammonium formate were obtained from Millipore-Sigma (St. Louis, MO). Reagent-grade acetonitrile was obtained from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA). Deionized water was provided with a Milli-Q Ultrapure Water System from Millipore Corporation (Bedford, MA).

Standards and calibrants

Samples were quantitated by standard addition. Succinylcholine and succinyl monocholine were prepared as neat standards in acetonitrile at concentrations of 0, 2, 5, and 10 ng/mL. Rocuronium was prepared as neat standards in acetonitrile at concentrations of 0, 25, 50, 100, and 250 ng/mL with a fixed concentration (50 ng/mL) of verapamil.

Extraction procedure

Bodily fluids

For blood and urine, to 200 µL of sample, 400 µL of acetonitrile was added. Samples were vortexed for 10 min and then centrifuged at 7098 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was split into 5, 100 µL aliquots spiked with either succinylcholine, succinylmonocholine, or rocuronium at the concentrations provided above.

Muscle tissue

For muscle tissue, 0.5 g of sample and 2 mL of acetonitrile or acetonitrile internal standard were added. The sample was homogenized in a Precellys Homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France) and then centrifuged at 3407 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was split into 5 equal aliquots and spiked with either succinylcholine, succinylmonocholine, or rocuronium at the concentrations stated above.

Quantitative LC–MS/MS

Analyte separation was achieved using a Shimadzu LC30AD ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) followed immediately by a Thermo Fischer Scientific (Waltham, MA) Syncronis HILIC 100 angstrom column (50 × 2.1 mm i.d., 1.7 μm). Identification and quantitation were accomplished using an ABSciex 6500+ triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (ABSciex, Framingham, MA) with an electrospray ionization source in the positive ionization mode. Chromatographic separation and analyte detection by means of multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) transition settings are provided in Supplementary Table SI for succinylcholine and succinylmonocholine and Supplementary Table S2 for rocuronium. Retention times were as follows: (a) rocuronium, 4.30 min and its internal standard; (b) verapamil, 1.63 min; (c) succinylcholine, 0.37 min; and (d) succinylmonocholine, 0.42 min (Fig. 2). Quantitation was achieved by interpolation of the standard addition curve to the X-axis.

Results and discussion

The medicinal vials discarded in the waste receptacle provided significant evidence regarding which medication was used in perpetrating the animal’s death. The first vial examined was a red top vial that was unopened with no apparent puncture site on the rubber stopper. It was manufactured by Meitheal Pharmaceuticals with product code 801H4AAM90-02 and NDC code 71288-719-10, consistent with a succinylcholine chloride injectable. The sample was opened, drawn into a syringe, solubilized in the mobile phase, diluted, and injected into the LC-MS/MS. MRM transitions confirmed succinylcholine. The second vial was a yellow top vial—received with the label mostly stripped away, but the NDC code 0409-9558-31, consistent with a 10 mL vial of rocuronium bromide injectable distributed by Hospira (Lake Forest, IL), was still visible. Lot number and expiration were not intact. The vial was mostly empty with a small volume remaining. The vial was injected with mobile phase, diluted, and injected into the LC-MS/MS. Full spectral analysis and MRM transitions confirmed the presence of rocuronium.

The presence of rocuronium in the tissues at any detectable level outside of the scope of anesthesia is strongly indicative of a death due to non-natural causes. The concentrations of rocuronium in the blood (2400 ng/mL) were similar to those reported in humans under full surgical anesthesia with ventilatory support [12]. It is posited by the authors that this blood concentration of rocuronium in an unsupported patient would likely have resulted in complete or near-complete neuromuscular paralysis with subsequent respiratory failure and death within several minutes of intravenous or intramuscular injection.

The significance of the focal hemorrhages in the shoulder and neck was not initially determined. It is possible that one or both could represent injection sites based on toxicology findings. A small foreign body was observed in the stomach, but its significance was not clear. There were no indications that the object resulted in a severe obstruction or that the presence of the object contributed to death. It is possible that the reported episode of vomiting two days prior to death was the result of this foreign body. No other traumatic injuries were detected. No other physical or medical condition likely to result in death was detected by the analyses performed.

Quantitation of rocuronium and succinylcholine in the skeletal muscle and biologic fluids was subsequently completed (Supplementary Table S3). The presence of rocuronium in the submitted skeletal muscle (Fig. 3A) supports the idea that this was a potential injection site for the drug. Furthermore, identification of the drug in the heart blood and urine was also consistent with its use at concentrations capable of inducing neuromuscular paralysis. The absence of succinylcholine and its succinylmonocholine metabolite (Fig. 3B and C) was consistent with finding an unopened/unused vial of the drug in the waste receptacle.

Conclusion

Necropsy and toxicology findings are consistent with death due to an overdose of rocuronium. Rocuronium and other drugs of this class are administered intravenously during some surgical procedures as part of a comprehensive anesthetic plan to facilitate tracheal intubation and to relax skeletal muscles during surgery. NMBA’s can decrease breathing and other vital processes to the extent that death will ensue if ventilation is not supported. Administration of rocuronium outside of the context of a surgical procedure with anesthesia and ventilatory support would generally be considered inappropriate. However, this dog had no recent history of surgery or anesthesia.

This case represents the first documented instance of a malicious rocuronium intoxication in a canine. The detection of rocuronium in postmortem tissues and fluids at clinically significant concentrations confirms its role in the animal’s death, likely by respiratory paralysis. This case underscores the importance of collaborative, cross-disciplinary approaches to forensic veterinary cases.
