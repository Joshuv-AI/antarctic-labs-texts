Examining Recruitment and Retention Strategies for Underserved Populations in Thyroid Cancer Clinical Trials: A Cross‐Sectional Analysis
Authors: Ryan Sherry, Ethan Wood, Payton Clark, Merhawit Ghebrehiwet, Andrew Wilson, Josh Autaubo, Alexandra Koontz, Jean‐Maria Langley, Alicia Ito Ford, Matt Vassar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective(s)Thyroid cancer incidence has risen sharply in the US, with disparities in diagnosis and treatment outcomes disproportionately affecting marginalized populations. Despite mandates for diversity in clinical trials, underrepresentation of these groups remains a critical barrier to equitable health outcomes. This study evaluates recruitment and retention strategies in thyroid cancer trials and highlights areas for improvement to enhance inclusivity and generalizability.MethodsThis cross‐sectional analysis examined the reporting of recruitment and retention strategies for historically marginalized populations in thyroid cancer clinical trials. Following PRISMA guidelines, we searched MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane databases for trials published between 2018 and 2023. Eligible studies assessed thyroid cancer treatment interventions and were conducted in locations with a national or multinational Ethnic Fractionalization Index (EFI) of ≥ 0.3. Data extraction included trial characteristics and reporting of recruitment and retention practices. Descriptive analyses were performed using Stata.ResultsOf 708 articles identified, 28 clinical trials met inclusion criteria. None reported recruitment or retention strategies targeting underrepresented populations, nor established diversity goals. The absence of such strategies highlights persistent gaps in addressing the needs of marginalized groups in thyroid cancer research.ConclusionThe lack of effective recruitment and retention strategies in thyroid cancer trials perpetuates the underrepresentation of marginalized populations, limiting the generalizability of findings. To address these disparities, clinical trials must adopt inclusive methodologies and transparent reporting practices, supported by policy changes and stronger enforcement of diversity mandates. These steps are essential for ensuring equitable health outcomes and advancing the quality of thyroid cancer research.Level of Evidence3Keywords: clinical trials, recruitment strategies, retention strategies, thyroid cancer, underserved populations

Introduction

Thyroid cancer represents a growing public health concern in the United States as incidence and mortality rates have markedly increased over the last several decades [1]. According to the National Cancer Institute (NCI) of the National Institutes of Health (NIH), there are an estimated 44,020 new cases in 2024, with a projected 2170 deaths [2]. While thyroid cancer has a 98.4% 5‐year relative survival rate, early diagnosis and proper treatment are needed to see improved outcomes among those diagnosed [3]. However, recent literature highlights disparities in thyroid cancer presentation and outcomes, especially among historically and intentionally excluded populations, such as racial minorities (including Black, Hispanic, and Asian individuals) and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups, who are consistently underrepresented in clinical trials [4, 5].

Given these trends, it is crucial to examine the recruitment and retention of these specific populations—racial/ethnic minorities and patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds—in thyroid cancer clinical trials [6]. Underrepresentation of these groups limits the external validity of study findings and perpetuates existing health inequities by producing results that may not apply to all patient populations [7, 8, 9]. Although the 1993 NIH Revitalization Act required the inclusion of representative patient samples in clinical trials, disinvested populations—groups historically subject to structural neglect, including racial/ethnic minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged communities—remain significantly underrepresented [10, 11]. A review conducted 20 years after the passage of the Revitalization Act revealed that less than 2% of the 10,000 clinical trials funded by the NCI met the NIH's diversity goals [10, 11]. Moreover, less than 1% (150 of 10,000) of these trials primarily focused on ethnic or minority populations [10, 11].

These statistics highlight the urgent need for inclusive recruitment strategies that address the barriers underrepresented populations face. While such strategies may be included in clinical trial protocols, they are often underreported in published manuscripts, making it difficult to evaluate their effectiveness in improving diversity in thyroid cancer trials. This study aims to explore whether current recruitment and retention strategies in thyroid cancer trials sufficiently address these vulnerable populations and to provide recommendations for more inclusive practices.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

In our cross‐sectional analysis, we examined recruitment and retention strategies directed toward historically marginalized populations in clinical trials for thyroid cancer. We did not adhere to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines as we applied systematic review methods. Instead, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) to ensure transparency and accuracy of our reporting [12]. Our protocol was submitted to the Institutional Review Board (IRB), which determined that this study did not involve human subjects according to the US Code of Federal Regulations [13]. The IRB at Oklahoma State University Center for Health Sciences additionally granted this study approval. We uploaded our study protocol, raw data, and Google Form a priori to Open Science Framework (OSF) to promote openness and transparency [14]. This study was performed alongside other studies in different fields of medicine with similar methodologies.

Use of Language

To ensure equity‐focused language in our writing, we used Advancing Health Equity: A Guide to Language, Narrative, and Concepts by the American Medical Association (AMA) as a reference [15].

Populations of Interest

In this study, we defined historically and intentionally excluded populations as those traditionally underrepresented in clinical trials and who experience significant health disparities in thyroid cancer outcomes. These populations include racial and ethnic minorities, such as African Americans, Hispanics, and Asians. Additionally, we considered socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals from low‐income backgrounds who may face barriers to accessing healthcare and who are often underrepresented in clinical research despite the increased incidence of thyroid cancer with age. We examined whether the clinical trials in our analysis addressed recruitment and retention strategies aimed at these specific populations, focusing on whether efforts were made to include racial/ethnic minorities and socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals.

Search Strategy

Our search strategy involved several steps. First, we searched Cochrane for systematic reviews related to thyroid cancer using the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews of Interventions. We examined each systematic review to identify key search terms for developing database‐specific search strategies. We further processed the terms to determine whether Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms (e.g., Thyroid Neoplasms [MeSH Terms]) were available in PubMed. On May 28, 2024, we searched two medical literature databases, MEDLINE (PubMed) and Embase (Elsevier), for thyroid cancer clinical trials. All results were uploaded onto Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai/), a systematic review platform, where two authors (R.S., E.W.) screened records in a masked, duplicate manner.

Defining Treatment Interventions

Inclusion criteria for this analysis required that each clinical trial evaluate a specific treatment intervention for thyroid cancer. These interventions encompassed a variety of therapeutic approaches, including pharmaceutical interventions such as the use of tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) like lenvatinib, cabozantinib, and pazopanib for radioiodine‐refractory or advanced thyroid cancers; combination therapies targeting BRAF mutations or RET‐altered thyroid cancers; and immunotherapies like pembrolizumab and spartalizumab. Additionally, surgical interventions were included, such as total thyroidectomy with or without prophylactic central neck dissection for papillary thyroid cancer. Some trials also evaluated radiation therapies, including external beam radiation and intensity‐modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), either alone or in combination with chemotherapy agents like doxorubicin. This diversity in treatment approaches ensured that a broad range of therapeutic strategies for thyroid cancer was evaluated within the scope of the study. Eligibility

To meet inclusion criteria, a study must be: (1) published between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023, (2) a clinical trial with human subjects, (3) assessing thyroid cancer treatment interventions, (4) conducted in a location with a national or multinational Ethnic Fractionalization Index (EFI) of ≥ 0.3. The EFI is a measurement used to quantify ethnic diversity within a population, representing the probability that two randomly chosen individuals from a given country or region belong to different ethnic groups [16]. An EFI value closer to 1 indicates a highly diverse population, whereas a value closer to 0 indicates a more homogenous population.

We selected national‐level EFI thresholds (≥ 0.3) to ensure that included trials were conducted in sociocultural contexts where diverse recruitment was feasible. Although clinical trial enrollment often occurs at local or institutional levels, national EFI provides a standardized, globally comparable metric of ethnic diversity across countries and multinational studies. This allowed consistent application of eligibility criteria across trials while acknowledging that local participant demographics may vary.

The EFI is frequently used to assess national population diversity and to evaluate the potential for ethnic heterogeneity within clinical trial populations [17]. Accordingly, we used EFI values at the national level to assess the diversity of the countries in which included trials were conducted, recognizing that this metric captures broader contextual diversity rather than site‐specific enrollment characteristics.

While we recognize that recruitment for clinical trials often occurs at more localized levels (e.g., state or county), national‐level EFI data was selected due to its availability and consistency across international sites, allowing standardized comparisons across trials conducted in different regions. Studies were excluded if they were a (1) secondary analysis, (2) erratum, (3) corrigendum, (4) interim analysis, or (5) trial update.

The selected time frame was intended to focus on contemporary recruitment and retention practices in thyroid cancer clinical trials published between January 1, 2018, and December 31, 2023. This period reflects recent efforts following the implementation of guidelines and recommendations aimed at increasing diversity in clinical research and may better capture current approaches to inclusion of historically marginalized populations.

Data Extraction: General Characteristics

Investigators R.S. and E.W. independently extracted data in a masked, duplicate fashion using a pilot‐tested Google Form. Data points were recorded for all included clinical trials, including first and corresponding author names, corresponding author email addresses, total number of authors, first names of each author, year of publication, funding source(s), intervention(s) and their category (e.g., therapeutic, behavioral, supplemental/holistic, other), study duration, use of randomization, use of masking (none, single, double, or greater), population of focus (generally by disease), location (country or countries), exclusion of comorbidities, age (range and mean), target efficacy outcomes, intention‐to‐treat sample size, and loss to follow‐up. Information about sex/gender and race/ethnicity was recorded in a yes/no format. Any discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through discussion between R.S. and E.W., with a third author (P.C.) available for adjudication. Because disagreements were reconciled during the extraction process, percent agreement and Cohen's kappa could not be meaningfully calculated; this consensus‐based approach is consistent with prior meta‐research methodologies.

Data Extraction: Specific Characteristics

For this study, we operationalized recruitment strategies as any proactive method used to increase participation among underrepresented groups. Examples included community partnerships, culturally tailored materials, translated documents, transportation or childcare assistance, financial incentives, outreach events, or collaboration with primary care or community organizations. Retention strategies were defined as efforts to maintain participant enrollment after recruitment, such as flexible scheduling, reminder systems, compensation for follow‐up visits, and procedures designed to reduce participant burden. A strategy was coded as present only if it was explicitly described in the published manuscript.

Recruitment and retention practices were abstracted for each included trial, including any reported sampling methods, incentives, advertisements, collaborations with community organizations, participant eligibility criteria related to race or ethnicity, community outreach programs, transportation or childcare support, non‐English language options, and measures intended to foster cultural humility. Additional variables included reporting of planned diversity goals, ethical considerations, initiatives to reduce dropout among inequitable groups, and any stated challenges or limitations related to recruitment or retention. Following extraction, R.S. and E.W. reconciled discrepancies through discussion, with a third author (P.C.) available for adjudication.

Statistical Analysis

We used Stata 18 SE (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) to summarize the general characteristics of included studies and the reporting of recruitment and retention strategies using frequencies and percentages.

For trials conducted across multiple countries, we calculated either an unweighted or weighted average Ethnic Fractionalization Index (EFI). If country‐specific sample sizes were not reported, an unweighted mean EFI was calculated by averaging the EFI values of each participating country. If country‐specific sample sizes were available, a weighted EFI was calculated by multiplying the number of participants enrolled in each country by that country's EFI, summing these products, and dividing by the total sample size.

Results

Trial Exclusion and Inclusion Criteria

Our initial search return from Rayyan yielded 708 articles. After withdrawing 56 duplicates, 652 remained. From this sample, we excluded 575 articles that did not match the inclusion criteria. We then conducted a full‐text screen of the 77 retained articles to ensure reliability and validity, resulting in 49 additional articles being excluded. This rigorous process led us to our final sample size of 28 clinical trials, representing the culmination of our research efforts. We outlined rationales for detail exclusion, as shown in Figure 1.

General Characteristics

The clinical trials reviewed in the study were conducted between 2018 and 2023. The geographical distribution of study locations included both the United States and international sites (Table 1). However, we did not include any studies that conducted clinical trials exclusively at international locations outside the US. Among the 28 articles reviewed, 15 (53.6%) were international with US participating sites, while 13 (46.4%) included only US sites. Variation was seen among interventions, with the therapeutic model being used in 26 of 28 (92.9%) articles (Table 1).

Recruitment and Retention Strategies

None of the 28 included trials reported recruitment strategies targeting underrepresented populations in their published manuscripts. Specifically, no studies described the use of community partnerships, culturally tailored recruitment materials, translated documents, transportation or childcare assistance, financial incentives, outreach initiatives, or flexible scheduling. In addition, none of the trials reported planned diversity goals or ethical considerations related to diversity‐focused recruitment. Study‐level details regarding recruitment and retention reporting for each included trial are provided in Table S1.

Similarly, none of the included trials reported retention strategies aimed at supporting continued participation or reducing dropout among underrepresented populations. No studies described follow‐up support mechanisms, compensation for continued participation, or procedures designed to reduce participant burden.

Challenges and Limitations

No studies discussed limitations or challenges concerning recruiting or retaining participants from diverse populations in their manuscripts. This omission could indicate a gap in reporting critical strategies to ensure diversity within clinical trials. It also suggests the need for improved transparency in how recruitment and retention practices are implemented, especially for underrepresented populations.

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight a persistent gap in thyroid cancer clinical trial reporting. Despite longstanding recognition of underrepresentation in clinical research and prior recommendations to improve inclusivity, none of the 28 clinical trials reviewed (0/28) explicitly reported recruitment or retention strategies aimed at improving diversity. This absence of reported strategies limits the generalizability of clinical trial outcomes and may exacerbate existing health disparities among underrepresented racial and ethnic groups [18, 19, 20].

Importantly, the lack of reported recruitment and retention strategies does not necessarily indicate that such efforts were absent at the protocol level. Rather, these strategies may have been implemented but not described in published manuscripts. However, insufficient reporting restricts the ability to evaluate the scope, effectiveness, and reproducibility of recruitment approaches, underscoring the need for more transparent reporting practices within trial publications. Additionally, because participant race and ethnicity were inconsistently reported across trials, we could not assess whether studies achieved diverse enrollment despite the absence of reported recruitment strategies; future research incorporating actual enrollment demographics would strengthen understanding of diversity outcomes in thyroid cancer clinical trials.

Beyond the absence of reported strategies, none of the included trials set specific diversity goals or discussed challenges related to recruiting or retaining participants from underrepresented populations. This finding suggests that diversity considerations remain insufficiently integrated into the design, reporting, or evaluation of thyroid cancer trials, despite widespread recognition of inequities in clinical trial participation.

Future thyroid cancer trials should adopt more comprehensive and intentional recruitment and retention approaches to mitigate the disparities highlighted in this study [21]. Decentralized trial designs, flexible scheduling, transportation or childcare support, and financial reimbursement may reduce logistical barriers that disproportionately affect marginalized populations [11, 19]. A mini‐review conducted by Chen et al. emphasizes the need for increased funding and multilevel interventions to enhance access to thyroid cancer clinical trials for underrepresented patient populations [22, 23, 24].

At the policy level, stronger enforcement of diversity‐related accountability measures is needed. Although the NIH Revitalization Act of 1993 mandated the inclusion of underrepresented populations in clinical research, compliance remains inconsistent [10, 25]. Requiring transparent reporting of recruitment and retention strategies, along with routine audits of participant demographics, may help ensure that trials better reflect the populations most affected by disease.

This study has several strengths, including strict adherence to PRISMA guidelines, masked duplicate screening, and use of the Ethnic Fractionalization Index to contextualize trial settings across multinational studies. However, limitations should be acknowledged. Our analysis relied exclusively on published manuscripts, which may not fully reflect recruitment efforts described in trial protocols. Additionally, inclusion was limited to English‐language publications, and the observational design precludes causal inference. Future research should examine trial protocols and registry data to better understand how recruitment strategies are implemented and reported.

Conclusion

The underrepresentation of marginalized populations in thyroid cancer clinical trials remains a critical barrier to achieving equitable health outcomes. Our findings underscore the absence of reported recruitment and retention strategies for these populations, revealing a significant gap in both practice and reporting. To improve the inclusivity and generalizability of thyroid cancer research, it is imperative that future trials incorporate patient‐centered strategies, detailed recruitment and retention plans, and transparent reporting practices. These efforts will contribute to more equitable clinical trial outcomes and better health outcomes for all individuals affected by thyroid cancer.
