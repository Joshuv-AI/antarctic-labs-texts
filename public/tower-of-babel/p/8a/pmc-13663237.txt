“Let my mother come and may it smell like strawberries”: a qualitative study of illness experiences in school-age children with cancer (6–12 years)
Authors: Sibel Dağliyar, Emine Güdek Seferoğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren diagnosed with cancer experience a complex process that affects their physical, emotional, and social well-being. Understanding these experiences from the child’s perspective is essential for delivering high-quality, child-centered nursing care. This study aimed to explore the illness experiences and future perceptions of school-age children (6–12 years) with cancer through their narratives and creative expressions.MethodsA qualitative study with a hermeneutic phenomenological approach was conducted using a multimodal design. Data were collected from 13 children using the This Is My World workbook, which integrates drawings and narrative expressions. This approach enabled children to communicate their experiences in developmentally appropriate ways. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis.ResultsThree main themes emerged. Living with Illness reflected the impact of treatment-related changes on body image, perceived similarity to peers, illness-related challenges, privacy concerns, and expectations for compassionate care. Emotional Experiences revealed complex emotions, coping ability, fear, loneliness, and children’s need for emotional closeness and a sense of safety, often expressed through poignant wishes such as “let my mom come, let it smell like strawberries.” My Future reflected both children’s uncertainty about the future and their hopes for recovery, returning to everyday life, and achieving their personal dreams.ConclusionSchool-age children actively interpret their illness within interconnected emotional, social, and developmental domains. Their narratives reflect a strong desire for normalcy, sensitivity to privacy, and a need for safe, familiar environments. Pediatric oncology nursing should prioritize child-centered care, respect the child’s autonomy and privacy, and consider using multimodal tools to enhance communication and psychosocial support.Keywords: Pediatric oncology nursing, Child-centered care, Multimodal qualitative research, Children’s drawings, School-age children, Privacy, Sensory experiences

Background

Childhood cancer is a multifaceted process that encompasses not only physical challenges but also profound emotional, social, and developmental dimensions from the moment of diagnosis. According to World Health Organization (WHO) data, approximately 400,000 children and adolescents are diagnosed with cancer globally each year. The WHO’s “Global Initiative for Childhood Cancer” (GICC), launched in 2018, aims to increase the global survival rate to at least 60% by 2030 while simultaneously enhancing the quality of life for survivors [1]. In Turkey, 52,907 pediatric cancer cases aged 0–19 years were registered between 2002 and 2024, and the 5-year survival rate was reported as 70.9% [2].

Despite improvements in survival rates, the psychosocial impact of the treatment process on children remains a fundamental component of pediatric oncology care. Children diagnosed with cancer encounter numerous demanding experiences, including frequent hospitalizations, invasive procedures, chemotherapy-induced side effects, and the disruption of daily routines. This trajectory can significantly influence a child’s self-perception, sense of control, social relationships, and future expectations [3–5]. Specifically, school-aged children (6–12 years) are affected multidimensionally, as they are at a developmental stage where they can establish cause-and-effect relationships and assign meaning to their illness. In this age group, the disease and treatment process lead not only to physical limitations but also to social and developmental setbacks resulting from disengagement from school life and peer interactions [6–8].

Current approaches in pediatric oncology care advocate for a holistic evaluation of children’s psychosocial well-being alongside clinical outcomes [4]. In this context, assessing children’s experiences directly through their own narratives is of critical importance. Self-reported data facilitate a more accurate identification of psychosocial needs and contribute to the planning of child-centered care [9–11]. As healthcare professionals in constant interaction with the child and family, nurses play a pivotal role in understanding these experiences [12, 13]. Such an approach supports the individualization of care and the development of interventions sensitive to children’s emotional requirements [14].

Qualitative research methods provide a robust framework for an in-depth examination of children’s lived experiences from their own perspectives [15]. However, as children may face challenges expressing themselves through direct questioning, the use of creative methods such as drawing gains prominence. Integrating drawings with verbal and written expressions allows for a more comprehensive disclosure of children’s experiences [16]. Nevertheless, there is a noted scarcity of such multi-modal data collection approaches in the literature [17–19]. This study aims to examine children’s experiences of illness and hospitalization through their own expressions and to provide implications for oncology nursing practice in delivering child-centered psychosocial care.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was conducted within a hermeneutic phenomenological framework informed by the philosophy of Martin Heidegger, emphasizing interpretation and meaning-making in lived experience. Rather than providing a purely descriptive account, the study aimed to explore how children diagnosed with cancer understood and experienced their illness, treatment process, emotions, and daily lives.

Consistent with interpretative phenomenological approaches in qualitative research, themes were developed through an iterative analysis of children’s written narratives, drawings, and verbal explanations to identify shared patterns across participants’ experiences.

The study was conducted between January 2021 and May 2022 at the pediatric oncology–hematology clinic of a university hospital during the COVID-19 pandemic, a context that may have influenced children’s hospital experiences, social interactions, and emotional states. The central phenomenon was defined as “the experience of being diagnosed with cancer between the ages of 6 and 12 years.” Reporting followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [20].

Setting and participants

The study was conducted at the Pediatric Hematology-Oncology Clinic of a university hospital, which serves as a national tertiary referral and research center providing specialized care for children and adolescents (aged 0–18 years). The clinic operates with a 37-bed capacity across oncology and hematology units, managing a wide spectrum of complex conditions including solid tumors (e.g., sarcomas, neuroblastoma) and hematological malignancies (e.g., leukemias, lymphomas).

Care is delivered by a multidisciplinary team consisting of 16 specialized nurses and 8 pediatric sub-specialists. The facility integrates medical oncology with rehabilitation and psychiatric consultation services, fostering a holistic environment for both treatment and psychosocial support. Its status as a major referral hub ensured that the study sample included participants from diverse geographical and socio-economic backgrounds across Turkey.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure variation in illness and treatment experiences. Inclusion criteria were: (1) being between 6 and 12 years of age; (2) currently receiving inpatient treatment; (3) voluntary participation; (4) parental/guardian consent; and (5) literacy in Turkish.

Participants were recruited using purposive sampling to ensure variation in illness and treatment experiences and to obtain rich descriptions of children’s lived experiences, rather than to compare experiences across different stages of treatment (e.g., diagnosis, remission, or relapse). Inclusion criteria were: (1) being between 6 and 12 years of age; (2) currently receiving inpatient treatment; (3) voluntary participation; (4) parental/guardian consent; and (5) literacy in Turkish.

The sample consisted of 13 hospitalized children diagnosed with cancer. In phenomenological research, sample adequacy is determined by the depth and richness of experiential data rather than numerical representativeness. Data collection continued until sufficient interpretative depth and thematic saturation were achieved. Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are presented in the Results section.

Data collection

Data were collected using the therapeutic workbook This Is My World, developed by Zadeh and Wiener (2011). This 46-page workbook is a therapeutic expressive tool that combines guided activities such as writing, drawing, sentence completion, storytelling, and play-based tasks to facilitate children’s expression of illness experiences, emotions, and perceptions of their lives. The workbook was designed to be implemented gradually in order to support children in expressing their thoughts and feelings more comfortably [21].

Permission to use the workbook was obtained from the original author via email. The workbook was translated into Turkish by two independent academics whose native language is Turkish and who were affiliated with departments of English Language and Literature. Following translation, the content was reviewed by an expert panel consisting of three pediatric nursing academics, two specialists in child psychology and development, and five children aged 6–12 years. During the adaptation process, some pages from the original workbook were removed following expert evaluation because they contained content related to the theme of death, and such content could have negative emotional effects on the participating children. Therefore, these pages were excluded as an ethical and precautionary measure to minimize potential psychological distress while preserving the core purpose and essential content of the original workbook. Based on these expert evaluations, the final Turkish version consisted of 40 pages.

All participants received the same workbook and completed the same activities. The workbook activities enabled children to express their perceptions of themselves, their families, peers, school life, illness experiences, and future expectations. The selected workbook activities included drawing tasks, sentence completion exercises, open-ended written prompts, and expressive activities related to self-perception, emotions, family relationships, illness experiences, fears, coping, and future expectations. Themes were developed through the integrated analysis of children’s written responses, drawings, and verbal explanations generated within these activities. The sessions in which children verbally explained their drawings to the researcher were not audio- or video-recorded.

During the data collection phase, eligible participants were identified in collaboration with the pediatric oncology clinical team. The researcher, who was also a nurse working in the pediatric oncology unit, approached eligible children and their families after consultation with the clinical team regarding the child’s physical and emotional condition at the time of recruitment. Detailed information about the study and participation procedures was provided to children and their parents prior to enrollment, and written informed consent/assent was obtained. Sociodemographic and clinical data were collected using a researcher-developed demographic and clinical information form. The workbook was then introduced to the children, and each participant was provided with a set of coloring materials, including colored pencils, crayons, and felt-tip pens.

Children completed the workbook independently and at their own pace, without assistance from their parents or healthcare professionals, to preserve the authenticity of their responses. The researcher provided only standardized instructions on how to complete the workbook and clarified task instructions when needed, without influencing or guiding the children’s responses. Children were asked to complete the workbook activities over an approximate period of 45 days. Upon completion, they were invited to verbally describe their drawings in their own words. The workbook served as a facilitative tool to encourage children’s narratives rather than as a structured interview guide. Children’s verbal explanations, together with their written responses and drawings, constituted the study data and were documented by the researcher for subsequent analysis.

Thus, three types of data sources were obtained: (1) children’s written expressions, (2) drawings, and (3) verbal explanations accompanying the drawings. The written and visual materials produced through the workbook were examined using document analysis, which involves the systematic analysis of written and visual materials [22]. The textual and visual data were subsequently analyzed using qualitative analysis techniques and organized into themes and categories.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed within a hermeneutic phenomenological framework informed by Heideggerian philosophy. This approach emphasizes not only the description of lived experiences but also the interpretation of the meanings individuals attribute to those experiences [23]. Accordingly, children’s written expressions, drawings, and verbal explanations accompanying the drawings were analyzed together to interpret how children made sense of their illness experiences. The analytic process was conducted within an interpretative framework integrating participatory visual methods, the draw-and-tell approach, visual content analysis, and thematic analysis principles.

The study employed participatory visual methods and a “draw-and-tell” approach. Within this approach, children created drawings and subsequently explained the meanings of their drawings in their own words. Thus, drawings were treated not as diagnostic projective tests, but as participant-generated expressive forms reflecting children’s experiences. This approach enabled visual and verbal data to be interpreted within the children’s own experiential contexts.

The analysis process was conducted through iterative stages involving the interconnected evaluation of textual and visual data. Initially, all written expressions, drawings, and verbal explanations were repeatedly reviewed to achieve familiarity with the data. Meaning units were defined as words, expressions, or visual elements within children’s written narratives, explanations of drawings, and visual representations that conveyed meanings related to their illness experiences. Subsequently, meaning units were identified and coded inductively. Codes reflecting similar meanings were grouped into categories. These categories were then transformed into subthemes and overarching themes through continuous comparison and interpretative reflection.

Visual data were analyzed using principles of visual content analysis and interpretative visual examination [24]. Figures, spatial organization, use of color, emphasized objects, omitted elements, and symbolic representations within the drawings were evaluated. However, visual materials were not interpreted independently; rather, children’s own verbal explanations accompanying the drawings were considered a central component of the interpretative process.

To enhance interpretative depth and reduce potential researcher subjectivity, the drawings were also independently evaluated by a clinical psychologist experienced in child psychology. The psychologist reviewed the drawings by considering the child’s age, gender, developmental characteristics, emotional expressions, and symbolic elements. These evaluations were used not as independent diagnostic interpretations, but as supportive interpretative data during the thematic analysis process.

In the final stage, findings obtained from textual and visual analyses were integrated through iterative comparison to identify shared experiential patterns across participants. Final themes were developed through ongoing discussion and interpretative reflection among the research team.

Rigor and trustworthiness

To ensure the scientific rigor of the study, the criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were addressed in line with established qualitative research standards [25].

Credibility was enhanced through methodological and analyst triangulation. Data were collected from multiple sources, including children’s written expressions, drawings, and their accompanying verbal narratives. To strengthen interpretive depth and reduce potential researcher bias, the drawings were also reviewed by an independent clinical psychologist experienced in child psychology. This evaluation was used to support the interpretation of visual data rather than to generate independent themes. In addition, the primary researcher’s role as a nurse within the clinical setting enabled prolonged engagement with participants, facilitating the development of trust and supporting the authenticity of the data.

Dependability and confirmability were ensured through a systematic audit trail and analyst triangulation. The data were initially coded by the research team and subsequently reviewed by an independent researcher with expertise in qualitative methods. Codes and categories were compared and refined through iterative discussions until consensus was achieved. Although formal member checking was not conducted, children’s own verbal explanations of their drawings were incorporated into the analysis to enhance interpretive accuracy and support the credibility of the findings.

Transferability was supported through a detailed description of the research setting, participant characteristics, and data collection procedures, enabling readers to assess the applicability of the findings to similar pediatric oncology contexts. All raw data, including written texts, drawings, and analytic documents, were systematically documented and preserved to ensure transparency and traceability throughout the research process. Reflexivity was maintained through continuous reflection on the researcher’s dual role and its potential influence on data collection and interpretation. Furthermore, the researchers’ training in qualitative methods and pediatric drawing analysis contributed to the depth and rigor of the analytical process.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to the commencement of the study, ethical approval was obtained from the Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Kütahya Health Sciences University (Date: December 22, 2020; Decision No: 2021/04–20), and administrative permission was obtained from the institution where the study was conducted. Written permission to use the This Is My World workbook was obtained from the original author via e-mail.

Written informed consent was obtained from parents using an Informed Voluntary Consent Form, and written assent was obtained from participating children using a developmentally appropriate Child Assent Form. Children and their families were informed about the purpose of the study, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality, and their right to withdraw from the study at any time without affecting the child’s treatment or care.

To ensure confidentiality and anonymity, participants’ identities were replaced with codes (e.g., C1–C13) throughout the data analysis and reporting process. All data were stored securely and used solely for research purposes.

Results

The study sample consisted of 13 school-age children diagnosed with cancer. Of the participants, 9 were female and 4 were male. The ages of the participants ranged from 7 to 12 years. The mean age was 10.38 ± 1.7 years. All participants had completed at least one cycle of chemotherapy, and none had a history of amputation surgery. The participants were diagnosed with various hematological and solid malignancies, primarily B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia, followed by lymphomas and sarcomas. Themes and subthemes

Theme 1: the experience of living with illness

This theme reflects how children experienced living with cancer through bodily changes, illness-related challenges, coping strategies, expectations regarding privacy, and perceptions of compassionate care. Their narratives illustrate that illness was experienced not only as a medical condition but also as a disruption to everyday life, relationships, and interactions with the healthcare environment.

Subtheme 1: body image

When describing themselves, the children identified the best and worst parts of their bodies, as well as the areas affected by the illness or parts they wished to change, using the following statements:

“The best part of my body is my brain; the worst parts are my heart and my foot.” (C1)

“The best part of my body is being tall; the worst part is having leukemia.” (C4)

“The worst things: My sickness, my hair (being bald), and my body hair.” (C2)

“If I could, I’d change my weight, because I’ve gotten so thin because of the illness.” (C12)

In a drawing made by one of the children, it was noted that the human figure had very long hair and was missing fingers. The psychologist pointed out that this omission might indicate that the child is experiencing self-esteem issues and emotional distress (Fig. 2).

“I want my hair to come back so much. I miss my hair a lot.” (C11)

These bodily changes appeared to symbolize the visibility of illness in children’s daily lives. Hair loss and weight loss, in particular, seemed to transform the body into a marker of difference, shaping how children perceived themselves in relation to others. Thus, illness was experienced not only physically but also as a disruption in self-identity and bodily continuity.

Subtheme 2: perceived similarity to peers

The children expressed that they do not actually perceive themselves as different from healthy children, defining their identity through their interests and daily activities.

“I’m no different from them! Riding a bike, playing games, and skating are my favorite things.” (C12)

“Volleyball, listening to music, talking, playing games… I love them!” (C5)

These findings suggest that children may develop a form of resistance to identifying themselves primarily through an illness identity. Defining themselves through play, sports, and social activities may reflect an effort to maintain a sense of normalcy despite the illness experience. Rather than placing illness at the center of their lives, children appeared to preserve continuity in their existing sense of self and everyday identity.

Subtheme 3: challenges of living with illness

Children described living with illness as a challenging experience that disrupted their daily routines, family life, and physical well-being.

“Being in the hospital makes me so tired. Our whole routine is messed up.” (C1).

“My family is all broken up because I’m in the hospital, and that really wears me down.” (C2).

Although hospitalization was often described as difficult, some children also acknowledged receiving increased attention and care during this period.

“Being in the hospital makes me unhappy, but people take care of me more.” (C13).

Children also described invasive procedures and treatment-related side effects as some of the most difficult aspects of living with illness.

“The worst day I ever had was the day I came to the hospital. Because I was going to get chemo and have a port put in.” (C3).

“I take chemotherapy medicine… sometimes it makes my tummy feel sick.” (C4).

“On the days I take the medicine, sometimes I throw up, and I don’t feel like eating.” (C12).

“It makes me feel better, but it’s hard to drink because it tastes so yucky.” (C13).

These findings suggest that the challenges of living with illness extended beyond physical symptoms to include disruptions in daily routines, changes in family life, invasive procedures, and treatment-related discomfort. Together, these experiences illustrate that living with illness affected not only children’s physical well-being but also the continuity of their everyday lives.

Subtheme 4: privacy please

From the children’s expressions, it was determined that they felt embarrassed during medical procedures and expected more attention to be paid to their privacy.

“I want them to be more careful when they are looking at my private parts.” (C12)

“Sometimes I feel uncomfortable when they have to uncover me; I wish they could do it in a more private way.” (C3)

Children’s expressions regarding privacy suggest that, during the treatment process, not only physical care needs but also expectations related to the protection of bodily boundaries were important. In particular, during procedures requiring exposure of the body, children appeared to experience a greater need for privacy, respect, and control. This suggests that privacy, for children, may be associated not only with physical protection but also with emotional comfort.

Subtheme 5: compassionate care

This subtheme was formed based on the children’s expectations, opinions, and experiences regarding healthcare professionals. The children expressed their expectations from those around them in terms of receiving love and respect, and not having their illness be the constant topic of conversation:

“Being loved makes me happy, but I don’t want people talking about me.” (C1)

“I want them to stop talking about my sickness.” (C4)

Some children expressed their desire for attention and being treated with kindness:

“I like it when people get me gifts or toys, and when they take an interest in me.” (C11)

“I want them not to leave me alone, and to be respectful and kind.” (C13)

The children described their experiences with healthcare staff through the professionals’ attitudes, communication styles, and care practices. Being cheerful, treating them well, and showing interest were prominent themes in their narratives:

“My doctors and nurses always treat me well. I want them to ask how I am when they come into the room, to show me love, and to be compassionate and friendly.” (C3)

“They take really good care of me and they are always smiling.” (C10)

Some children mentioned that they disliked the treatment procedures and expected more understanding during those times:

“Sometimes the medicine makes me really moody, and I want them to be understanding about that.” (C11)

Additionally, some children shared their suggestions regarding the hospital environment:

“If I were in their place, I would decorate my room better and make it look nice.” (C10)

“The hospital would be better if my dad could stay here with us too.” (C13)

“The hospital would be better if it was a quieter place.” (C1)

“The hospital would be better if the beds for the parents were more comfortable for my mom.” (C11)

“The hospital would be better if there were fun things for kids to do.” (C12)

These expressions suggest that children understood their care experiences not only through treatment procedures, but also through relational support, emotional security, and the child-friendly characteristics of the hospital environment. Children’s expectations for love, attention, and understanding further indicate that the need to feel emotionally acknowledged and valued was also prominent during the care process.

Theme 2: emotional experiences

This theme reflects children’s emotional experiences during the illness process, including their complex emotional situations, coping ability, fears, experiences of loneliness, and need for emotional security.

Subtheme 1: complex emotional situations

Regarding their perception of the illness process, the children expressed experiencing multiple conflicting positive and negative emotional states simultaneously. Emotions such as irritability, sadness, anxiety, fatigue, and frustration were voiced alongside traits like courage, perseverance, empathy, and helpfulness:

“I am… sometimes angry, smart, bored, sad, sometimes naughty, brave.” (C1)

“Determined, disciplined, intelligent, angry, frustrated, hurting, unstable, anxious, courageous…” (C6)

“Kind-hearted, emotional, helpful, beautiful, shy, confused, anxious.” (C8)

The use of characteristics reflecting both vulnerability and strength suggests that children’s self-perceptions during the illness process were not fixed, but instead shifted according to their experiences and circumstances. In particular, the use of seemingly contradictory expressions such as “brave” and “anxious” indicates that children navigated multiple emotional states while coping with illness.

Subtheme 2: coping ability

It was determined that when faced with a negative situation, children resorted to various coping mechanisms such as trying to stay calm, redirecting their attention, reading books, playing games, spending time with technological devices, sleeping, and spending time with their families:

“I try to be calm. I focus on something else. I find things to keep me busy. I play with some gadget. I read books. I try to turn my sadness into happiness somehow. I spend time together with my family.” (C1)

“When I’m sad, I think about getting better. I think about happy times. I think about the times I was happy at my house. If I’m mad at someone, I feel like yelling at them, like yelling at everyone. But usually, I just sleep, watch cartoons, or play games on the phone.” (C11)

Conversely, some children expressed that they had difficulty coping with the negative experiences they encountered:

“I act all cold and moody. I take deep breaths. Talking helps too. I look at my phone. I eat or drink something. I just think, ‘I’m already ugly anyway, nobody understands me, everyone is so bossy and they just don’t think about how I feel.‘” (C2)

“I think, ‘Why did this happen? I didn’t deserve this, I wish I wasn’t here.’ I scream. I read books. I go outside. I spend a little time on my phone.” (C10)

These expressions suggest that children’s coping processes were not always stable or effective. While some children appeared to rely on hope, distraction, and family support as emotional resources, others struggled with feelings of helplessness and being misunderstood. This finding highlights the emotionally fluctuating and fragile nature of coping during the illness experience.

Subtheme 3: fear

When asked about the thoughts that came to mind when they felt afraid or anxious, the children described the intense fear they experienced during the illness process:

“I’m just scared! I’m scared of being away from my family and something bad happening to them.” (C1)

“Please don’t let it hurt when they take my blood! Please don’t put the port in too fast.” (C3)

In a drawing made by one of the children, a scene from a computer game was depicted. In the psychologist’s assessment, it was noted that the scene might reflect the child’s internal perception of threat and anxiety, and might carry an implicit awareness of the fear of being alone, a high level of anxiety, and the struggle between life and death (Fig. 3).

“A place from a game I play on the computer.” (C1)

Children’s narratives regarding fear suggest that, during the illness process, their fears were not limited to physical pain but also involved separation and loss of control. In particular, being away from their families and undergoing invasive procedures emerged as prominent aspects of children’s experiences of fear.

Subtheme 4: loneliness

The children associated being away from school and their friends with feelings of sadness and longing:

“I miss my friends so much.” (C5)

“When I come to the hospital, when I can’t find anything to do, and when nobody talks to me, I feel really lonely.” (C4)

Conversely, some children stated that they preferred to be alone:

“I feel happy when I’m by myself.” (C1)

The experience of loneliness appeared to have a dual nature. On the one hand, children described being separated from their friends as a source of loneliness and longing. On the other hand, some children expressed a preference for being alone. These expressions suggest that being alone may have different meanings for different children and may reflect an individual need for personal space and rest or, in some cases, emotional withdrawal or self-protection during the illness process.

Subtheme 5: “let my mom come, let it smell like strawberries!”

This subtheme encompasses the children’s expressions regarding family and the sense of security during the illness process. Being with family members, the home environment, and especially the mother figure came to the forefront in the children’s narratives:

“My safe place is my home. My mom is with me, and everywhere she is smells like strawberries. I feel brave when I’m with my mom.” (C7)

“My safe place is anywhere my family is. It makes me feel safe because we are all together.” (C1)

There were children who expressed that they did not feel safe in the hospital environment, especially when they were left alone:

“I get nervous when strangers keep coming in and out of my room, so being alone makes me really anxious.” (C13)

The children defined their safe spaces not only through family members but also through physical spaces belonging to them. Elements of the home environment, in particular, were associated with a sense of security and protection. One child defined their own bed as a safe space. Regarding this drawing, the psychologist’s assessment noted that the child drew only the bed and used bright, vibrant tones; it was stated that this situation might be related to the child’s orientation toward a safe and protected space (Fig. 4).

“I specifically drew my own bed. Not my hospital bed. When I’m scared at night, I pull up the covers in my own bed, tuck my hands and feet in, and I feel safe.” (C12)

In another drawing, a child imagined a hospital room enriched with play materials, decorations, and a dedicated space for the mother. In the psychologist’s assessment, this drawing reflected the child’s need for a safer, more familiar, and home-like hospital environment, where the presence of the mother was considered an important source of comfort and security (Fig. 5).

“This is my dream hospital room. There’s a PlayStation, balloons, decorations, and a special bed just for my mom.” (C11)

This subtheme suggests that children’s sense of safety during illness was shaped primarily through close relational bonds, particularly the presence of their mothers. The expression “may it smell like strawberries” appears to transform the mother figure into a sensory symbol of warmth, comfort, and emotional closeness. Children’s association of safety with home, familiar objects, and family presence further suggests that the hospital environment could be experienced as unfamiliar and emotionally distressing. The children’s drawings also indicate that they envisioned a hospital environment that more closely resembled the safety, familiarity, and emotional comfort of home. These findings indicate that children’s feelings of safety were closely connected not only to physical protection, but also to familiarity, emotional closeness, and continuity in daily life.

Theme 3: my future

This theme reflects children’s thoughts, hopes, and concerns about the future during the illness process. While some children expressed uncertainty about what the future might hold, others focused on recovery, returning to everyday life, and achieving their personal dreams.

Subtheme 1: future uncertainty

Some children expressed uncertainty about what the future might hold during the illness process. Their narratives reflected concerns about the course of the disease, the possibility of prolonged treatment, disruption of education, and the inability to carry out future plans.

“Anything can happen at any moment.” (C4)

“All the bad things that might happen to me. Not being able to go to school again. Not knowing how my illness will progress.” (C5)

“What if something happens to me? What if this process takes too long? What if I can’t do the things I planned?” (C2)

These narratives reflected children’s uncertainty about the course of their illness and its possible consequences for their future lives.

Subtheme 2: my dreams

The children described the illness as a condition they did not want in their lives and expressed their desires to recover, be discharged from the hospital, and return to their lives before the illness. Going to school, waking up at home, regaining their former physical appearance, and being able to perform their daily activities were prominent in the children’s narratives:

“The things I don’t want in my life are the sickness, the IVs, and the medicine. I want to get better, leave the hospital, go back to my school, and be able to wear my uniform.” (C8)

“I want to get rid of this illness and beat it now. I want to be able to walk and run again.” (C3)

“If I could change anything in my life, I would change my illness. I want to wake up in my own bed at home.” (C12)

Children’s dreams for the future suggest that they understood recovery not only as the disappearance of illness but also as a return to everyday life, their family environment, and their lives before the illness. In particular, the emphasis on school, home, physical ability, and daily routines indicates that children associated recovery with restoring a sense of normalcy, belonging, and continuity in their lives.

Discussion

In this study, the experiences of children diagnosed with cancer regarding their illness and treatment trajectory were examined through their own narratives and drawings. The findings were structured under three primary themes integrated with the conceptual framework of the study: (1) The Experience of Living with Illness, (2) Emotional Experiences, and (3) My Future. These results reveal that children’s illness experiences constitute a multilayered structure involving not only physical but also emotional, relational, and future-oriented dimensions.

Theme 1: the experience of living with illness

The first theme illustrates how children construct meaning around their daily lives during the illness process through body image, perceived similarity to peers, challenging experiences, privacy needs, and expectations of compassionate care.

The Body Image sub-theme indicates that changes in physical appearance—particularly alopecia and weight loss—exert a significant impact on children. Findings suggest that these treatment-related changes directly influence the child’s self-perception. The literature emphasizes that alopecia and physical alterations negatively affect body image, becoming a visible symbol of the disease [26, 27]. This highlights the necessity for psychosocial support targeting body image in pediatric oncology care.

Furthermore, the Perceived Similarity to Peers sub-theme demonstrates that children resist being defined solely by their “patient” identity. Participants emphasized their similarities to peers in terms of daily activities and play, indicating an identity perception that rejects stigmatization. This aligns with literature stating that the social and emotional needs of children with chronic illnesses are similar to those of their healthy peers [8, 28, 29].

The burden of the illness is further detailed in the Challenging Experiences sub-theme, which reveals that hospitalizations, disruptions in family routines, and physical side effects such as chemotherapy-induced nausea and fatigue impose a heavy psychological weight. These findings are consistent with studies reporting that cancer treatment creates both physical and psychological challenges for children [4, 30].

Furthermore, the Privacy Please sub-theme reveals a strong need for bodily privacy during hospital interventions. Discomfort during invasive procedures suggests that prolonged treatment may threaten a child’s sense of bodily integrity and control [31], making the preservation of privacy a cornerstone of child-centered care [32].

Finally, the Compassionate Care sub-theme outlines expectations from healthcare professionals. Children seek kindness and a gentle approach, expressing a desire for “normalization” where the illness is not the sole focus of conversation. Since staff communication styles directly affect the care experience, compassion-based practices are vital in pediatric oncology nursing [12, 13, 33].

Theme 2: emotional experiences

This theme encompasses the profound emotional landscape of the illness, explored through complex emotional situations, coping ability, fear, loneliness, and children’s sense of safety and emotional closeness.

The Complex Emotional Situations sub-theme shows that children can experience multiple, conflicting emotions simultaneously, such as feeling both brave and sad. This is consistent with research showing that life-threatening illnesses trigger multidimensional emotional responses in children [34, 35].

The Coping Ability sub-theme demonstrates that children are capable of developing active coping strategies in response to the emotional and practical challenges of the illness process. Methods such as distraction, play, and spending time with family emerged as frequent coping mechanisms. This finding parallels existing literature on pediatric coping strategies [36–38]. However, the variation in coping success among children suggests that nurses must prioritize individual differences when bolstering these skills.

The Fear sub-theme highlights children’s anxiety regarding painful medical procedures, hospitalization, and separation from family. Fear of death and distress during procedures corroborate literature documenting the prevalence of pain and death anxiety in pediatric oncology [5, 8, 10, 11, 39]. Thus, clear, reassuring, and developmentally appropriate communication is critical.

The Loneliness sub-theme reflects the social isolation children experience due to their separation from school and peers. These findings indicate that being away from their usual social environments leads children to feel different from their healthy counterparts [7, 40]. This sense of isolation is often increased by chemotherapy and frequent hospitalizations, which further intensify feelings of being alone [40]. At the same time, some children expressed a preference for being alone, suggesting that solitude may have different meanings for different children, including an individual need for personal space and rest or, in some cases, emotional withdrawal or self-protection during the illness process. Therefore, supporting social and digital communication is essential to help children maintain meaningful connections with their peers while recognizing individual differences in how children experience periods of solitude [38, 41, 42].

Finally, the “Let my mom come, let it smell like strawberries!” subtheme indicates that children defined security largely through their families, especially their mothers. Parental presence emerged as the primary source of safety, and children consistently associated safe places with home and familiar environments. The literature similarly demonstrates that parental presence reduces anxiety and enhances children’s sense of security during hospitalization [43, 44]. To bridge the gap between the unfamiliar hospital environment and home, children often rely on familiar people, personal belongings, or comforting environmental features to foster a sense of safety [45]. Consistent with this, the children’s drawings suggested a desire for hospital environments that incorporated developmentally appropriate and child-friendly features promoting play, familiarity, comfort, and emotional security rather than focusing solely on medical care [33]. In contrast, the frequent presence of unfamiliar people in hospital rooms was perceived as threatening and contributed to feelings of insecurity. Together, these findings suggest that pediatric oncology settings should support family involvement while also creating predictable, emotionally safe, and child-friendly environments that promote children’s sense of security and well-being.

Theme 3: theme 3: my future

The final theme reflects children’s future-oriented perceptions and expectations. Although children maintained hope for recovery, returning to everyday life, and achieving their future aspirations, some also expressed uncertainty regarding the progression of their illness, the possibility of not returning to school, and whether they would be able to achieve these goals.

The My Dreams sub-theme shows that children maintain a strong future orientation despite their diagnosis. Findings reveal a desire for recovery, discharge, and returning to daily life, suggesting that they do not view the illness as an all-encompassing definition of their existence. Similarly, the literature notes that children continue to make future plans as a way of maintaining hope [8, 17].

Overall, this study demonstrates that children’s experiences of cancer extend beyond medical treatment and are shaped through embodied, emotional, relational, and future-oriented experiences. The findings highlight that child-centered care in pediatric oncology requires not only clinical support but also an interpretative and relational approach that acknowledges children’s perceptions of body image, privacy, emotional complexity, and family-based safety needs. Addressing these dimensions may contribute significantly to improving the quality of pediatric oncology care.

Strengths and limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its methodological design, which prioritized the “child’s voice” through a multimodal data collection approach. By integrating the therapeutic “This Is My World” workbook with drawings and verbal narratives, the study successfully transcended the limitations of traditional interview techniques, which often fail to capture the complex emotional landscapes of school-aged children. Furthermore, although the data were collected at a single center, the facility is a major national tertiary referral and research center in Turkey. Its status as a national hub ensured that the study sample included participants from diverse geographical, cultural, and socio-economic backgrounds across the country, rather than being limited to a specific local population. This diversity significantly enhances the transferability of the findings to other pediatric oncology settings. Additionally, the inclusion of an independent clinical psychologist to evaluate the drawings provided an objective layer of interpretation, reducing researcher bias. The primary researcher’s prolonged engagement in the clinical setting and strict adherence to COREQ guidelines further ensured the rigor and transparency of the research process.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be considered. Although the sample size was appropriate for an in-depth phenomenological exploration of children’s illness experiences, the findings should be interpreted within the context of the specific clinical setting and participant group. In addition, the heterogeneity of diagnoses and treatment phases (induction, remission, and relapse) among participants may have contributed to variability in children’s emotional and experiential responses. Furthermore, the data collection period (January 2021–May 2022) coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic, during which social restrictions and changes in hospital routines may have influenced children’s experiences of hospitalization, separation, and social interaction. Lastly, although children’s drawings provided rich experiential insights, visual materials remain open to multiple interpretations. Despite the involvement of an experienced clinical psychologist, some symbolic and emotional dimensions of the children’s inner experiences may not have been fully captured.

Conclusion

This study provides a nuanced understanding of how school-age children diagnosed with cancer interpret and make sense of their illness and treatment experiences through their own narratives and drawings. The findings demonstrate that children’s experiences extend beyond physical symptoms, encompassing interconnected emotional, social, and developmental dimensions. Children actively construct meaning, negotiate identities beyond the “patient” role, and express complex emotional responses, including fear, loneliness, and hope.

The multimodal design of the study enabled access to dimensions of children’s inner worlds that may remain underexplored through conventional approaches. In particular, the integration of drawings with children’s own explanations offered valuable insights into their perceptions of body image, safety, and future expectations. Despite the challenges of the illness trajectory, children maintained a clear orientation toward recovery, normality, and connection with their families.

Taken together, these findings underscore the importance of recognizing children as active agents in their care and highlight the need for systematically integrating their perspectives into pediatric oncology practice, particularly to inform the development of psychosocial and child-centered care interventions.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings indicate that pediatric oncology care should be expanded beyond a predominantly biomedical focus to more fully incorporate children’s psychosocial needs into routine practice. Children’s experiences related to body image changes, privacy concerns, and emotional distress suggest that psychosocial assessment should be embedded as a continuous component of care rather than addressed only when problems arise.

Effective communication with children requires approaches that are developmentally appropriate and responsive to their expressive capacities. Incorporating creative and participatory methods, such as drawing and narrative-based tools, may enhance communication, facilitate emotional expression, and enable healthcare professionals to better understand children’s experiences.

The hospital environment also plays a critical role in shaping children’s experiences of care. Children’s emphasis on play, family presence, and a more home-like atmosphere points to the importance of designing care settings that support emotional well-being in addition to clinical treatment. The children’s longing for familiar and pleasant sensory experiences—captured in poignant desires such as “smelling like strawberries”—suggests that healthcare policies should move toward sensory-friendly environments that mitigate the sterile clinical atmosphere. Efforts to strengthen family involvement, ensure privacy during medical procedures, and provide developmentally appropriate recreational opportunities may contribute to a greater sense of safety and comfort.

Within this context, nurses are uniquely positioned to identify and respond to children’s psychosocial needs due to their continuous interaction with patients and families. Strengthening competencies in child-centered communication and psychosocial care through targeted education and training may enhance the quality and responsiveness of pediatric oncology services.

Recommendations for future research

Further research is needed to deepen the understanding of children’s illness experiences through the use of multimodal qualitative approaches that allow for richer and more comprehensive data. Expanding studies across different clinical settings and including more diverse participant groups may help to capture the influence of contextual and cultural factors on children’s experiences.

Longitudinal research designs would be particularly valuable in examining how children’s perceptions, emotional responses, and coping strategies evolve throughout the illness trajectory. Such approaches may provide insight into critical periods where psychosocial support is most needed.

Future studies should also focus on developing and evaluating interventions that integrate creative expression methods, such as drawing and storytelling, into routine clinical practice. Investigating the effectiveness of these approaches in improving communication, emotional expression, and psychosocial outcomes may contribute to evidence-based, child-centered care models.

A more comprehensive understanding of pediatric oncology care may be achieved by incorporating multiple perspectives, including those of children, family members, and healthcare professionals, within the same research framework.
