Ichnofossils in volcanic glass from palaeoproterozoic hydrothermal vents were burrowed by microorganisms probably seeking phosphate
Authors: Dominic Papineau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIchnofossils in basaltic glass are putative microscopic trace fossils occurring on modern seafloor and throughout Earth history. While their biological origin remains debated, it is unknown why microorganisms thrive in volcanic glass. Here we show ichnofossils associated with biosignatures from sedimentary interbeds of pillow basalt in the ~ 1870 million years old Flaherty Formation, Belcher Group, Canada. These rocks are associated with metre-size, pinnacle-shaped structures of silicified basalt, pahoehoe basalt with paleo-caliche, and isolated pods of gossan indicating shallow marine hydrothermal venting. Trails of spheroidal ichnofossils composed of titanite and organic matter surround abundant nanoscopic-size apatite and lepidocrocite, suggesting they caused rock dissolution to obtain phosphate. Stable isotopes provide complementary biosignatures for possible chemolithotrophy. While there is evidence for abiotic carbon-based reactions, the biological origin of spheroidal ichnofossils is supported by 13C-depleted organic matter and calcite, suggesting they were burrowed by microorganisms likely seeking phosphate in volcanic glass near hydrothermal vents. Subject terms: Palaeontology, Geochemistry, Element cycles, Origin of life

Introduction

Hydrothermal systems have been microbial habitats since the beginning of the rock record1–4, and they have been suggested as the cradle of life on Earth5–7. Volcanic glass associated with pillow basalt and hydrothermal vents has been proposed as a habitable environment that can be colonised by filamentous and coccoidal microorganisms leaving trace fossils, or ichnofossils, composed of titanite [CaTi(SiO4)O]8–10. Examples of such purported microbial-rock interaction in Precambrian volcanic glass have been reported from Archaean (4031–2500 Ma (million years old)) pillow basalt and hyaloclastite rocks from South Africa, Western Australia, and Canada8,11–14. Morphologies reported for Precambrian titanite ichnofossils in basaltic glass include microscopic burrows, tubules, spiral filaments, and spheroids, which have all been variably debated as ichnofossils, pseudofossils, and microbial fossils8–10,13–20. Independent lines of evidence for a biological origin of ichnofossils in Archaean basalt include co-occurring 13C-depleted carbonate and organic matter (OM)8,11,18 and 34S-depleted sulphides12. However, there is a far greater morphological diversity of filamentous alteration textures in modern basaltic glass from the deep-sea, including various types of tunnels, branching, curvature, roughness, packing density, and sizes that may not all be biological in origin21.

Hence, the biological origin of ichnofossils in basaltic glass from the rock record is debated and these objects have been considered dubiofossils with a possible abiotic origin based on the following observations: 1- variable filament diameters19, 2- occurrences of ambient inclusion trails13,16, 3- possibility of later metamorphic dendrite production10,19, 4- ichnofossils without OM22, and 5- similarities with chemical gardens23. Two other elusive problems with the purported biological origin of ichnofossils include a paucity of direct observations for living microorganisms inside burrows in nature and the absence of evidence for a specific reason to make burrows in basaltic glass. For the latter point, it has been speculated that microorganisms burrowing in basaltic glass search for nutrients; however, there is currently no supporting evidence. For the former point, laboratory experiments have shown that basaltic glass palagonitisation (i.e. mafic glass alteration to clays, zeolites, and/or oxides) can be induced by colonial cyanobacteria that cause alkaline pH (>9) or low pH by other types of bacteria24; however, there is little supporting evidence from nature25. Resolution of this problem is important for evolutionary biology and exobiology because the use of ichnofossils in basalt as biosignatures could provide clues for the early evolution of life on Earth as well as an approach to search for life on other planetary surfaces9,20,26. Therefore, for ichnofossils to be considered reliable biosignatures, these open questions require answers. Three validating biogenicity criteria for ichnofossils include26: 1- a demonstrated syngenetic origin, 2- ichnofossil morphologies and distribution suggestive of microorganisms and not of ambient inclusion trails, and 3- elemental and isotopic evidence consistent with microbial metabolisms. In this work, a detailed documentation of field outcrops of Palaeoproterozoic hyaloclastite is provided along with geochemical and petrographic observations that yield evidence for both biological and abiotic carbon cycling.

Results and discussion

Palaeoproterozoic hydrothermal vent exhalation

The studied rocks come from 1852 to 1870 Ma hydrothermally altered sediments in the Flaherty Fm. of the Belcher Group in Nunavut, Canada (Fig. 1a; see also Supplementary Information). This volcano-sedimentary rock formation hosts pillow basalt layers (Fig. 1b, c) and starts with columnar basalt conformably overlying the Kipalu granular iron formation (Supplementary Fig. 1a). Sedimentary interlayers in the basaltic pile include hydrothermally altered pillow basalt (Supplementary Fig. 1b), wavy beds of carbonate-volcaniclastic rocks with white chert (Supplementary Fig. 1c), cross bedded volcaniclastic sedimentary rocks (Supplementary Fig. 1d), and thin layers of sulphidic black shales (Supplementary Fig. 1e). The cross-bedded volcaniclastics and black shale thus point to a sub-aqueous depositional environment. Some interbeds include pahoehoe basalt partly covered with white-colour paleo-caliche, indicating a transient subaerial depositional environment (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 1f). Notably, interbedded metre-sized pinnacle structures of silicified basaltic glass with chaotic wavy laminations and rims of silicified accretionary lapilli (Fig. 1e) point to shallow marine hydrothermal vent chimneys comparable to modern alkaline shallow vents27. Furthermore, these occur in the same bed, along strike, and within a few metres of rusty pods of weathered gossan (Fig. 1f) as well as rounded, concretionary outcrops of calcite-rich hyaloclastite (Fig. 1g). Hyaloclastite with decimetre size concretions exhibits circularly concentric, septarian, or finely laminated patterns that are centimetre in size (Fig. 1h) and consistent with patterns from desiccation for septarian features and abiotic chemically oscillating reactions for circularly concentric patterns. In experiments, chemically oscillating reactions are known to spontaneously form such fractal patterns during the decarboxylation of carboxylic acids28,29. Hence, field evidence of gossan pods and pinnacle structures points to hydrothermal exhalation and alteration of volcanic rocks, while the associated pahoehoe basalt points to these structures having formed in shallow marine environments, whereas concretionary carbonate structures suggest abiotic carbon cycling during post-depositional diagenesis.

Microscopic spheroidal ichnofossils associated with apatite

Hyaloclastite rocks from the Flaherty Fm. (Fig. 1g, h, Supplementary Fig. 2a) preserve several possible biosignatures in the form of OM and microbial-like textures in chloritised [(Mg,Fe2+,Fe3+,Al)5-6(Si,Al,Fe3+)4(O,OH)18] basaltic glass fragments, which are commonly palagonitised to titanite, can be inferred to have an altered basalt precursor. The Ti has been suggested to come from the microbial dissolution of basaltic glass, and that fine-grained titanite crystallises early during this alteration, which is important for the preservation of ichnofossils11,15. The calcite [CaCO3] matrix of Flaherty hyaloclastite is ubiquitously peppered with finely disseminated OM, rendering it dark brown in colour (Supplementary Fig. 2b, c). A common type of possible ichnofossil structure inside palagonitised glass fragments includes microscopic spheroids, which have a unimodal size distribution between 8 and 23 μm in diameter (average is 14.2 ± 3.2 μm; n = 53). They also co-occur with nanoscopic size apatite [Ca5(PO4)3(OH,F,Cl)] and lepidocrocite [FeOOH] (Fig. 2a–e). In fact, μRaman hyperspectral images show that these microscopic spheroids are composed of titanite and OM, which has the characteristics of highly disordered OM (i.e. kerogen) including broad G–peaks around 1607 cm–1 and D1–peaks around 1334 cm–1 (Fig. 2e). The microscopic titanite spheroids occur as directionless, linear arrays linked by a central thread of OM (Fig. 2b, c). These microscopic spheroids are different from the ‘strings of beads’ reported from the Palaeoarchean Euro basalt and in modern oceanic crust22,30, because they are not unidirectional, and they are uniform in size, rich in both titanite and OM, and closely associated with nanoscopic apatite and lepidocrocite. However, the origin of the thread of OM linking dozens of spheroidal ichnofossils remains ambiguous. It could be the result of specialised microorganisms growing as a community, or perhaps a diagenetic alteration that caused a collapse of microbial biomass, or also, possibly, remobilised OM from elsewhere. However, the latter explanation is unlikely because there are no cracks in this or other chloritized basaltic glass fragments where foreign OM could have remobilized through and also because the host matrices of both the basalt and hyaloclastite are low in porosity, and lack evidence for remobilised OM. Besides, the finely disseminated OM in the calcite matrix has the same Raman spectrum as in the thread of OM, indicating a common origin, which is also inconsistent with a scenario where the OM is not syngenetic and remobilised from elsewhere. The first two possibilities are difficult to exclude, however, and could have occurred soon after sedimentation or after burial.

Phosphorous-enriched titanite has been reported from 2720 Ma palagonitised hyaloclastite, but dismissed as a replacement of unknown origin13. In the Flaherty Fm, co-occurring crystals of apatite and lepidocrocite are of two kinds: rounded nanoscopic crystals a few tens of nanometres in size (Fig. 2c–f) and euhedral microscopic crystals a few microns in size (Fig. 2c, d, g). Both occur predominantly in the chloritised basaltic glass fragments. In fact, combined optical and μRaman images show that the nanoscopic size apatite occurs in the vicinity of the microscopic spheroids (mostly more than 10 μm away), as opposed to inside or in contact with spheroidal ichnofossils. In comparison, transmission electron microscopy reveals poikilotopic inclusions of OM in microscopic apatite crystals (Fig. 2i–n) immediately adjacent (mostly within less than 10 μm) to the titanite spheroids (Fig. 2b, c, g) embedded in the ferruginous and magnesian chlorite of basaltic fragments. Energy dispersive spectroscopic analyses independently confirm the presence of carbon in chlorite and apatite (Fig. 2o). Lepidocrocite is known to co-occur with OM in modern ichnofossil from chloritised basaltic glass and can be identified by its Raman spectrum9. Therefore, while occurrences of ferric and ferrous phases like lepidocrocite and chlorite suggest iron-based redox reactions participated in palagonitisation, the presence of apatite with poikilotopic OM inclusions is akin to nanoscale textures in similarly aged fossiliferous phosphorites31, which represent another important biosignature for the Flaherty spheroidal ichnofossils. Lastly, the Flaherty spheroidal ichnofossils are also distinct from larger amygdales located in the rims of chloritised basaltic glass fragments because the latter have cores filled with matrix calcite and sizes larger than those of microbial cells (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Observations of micron-size apatite immediately adjacent to the spheroidal ichnofossils and of nanoscopic apatite in the vicinity of spheroidal ichnofossils suggest that the original microorganisms may have caused the oxidative dissolution of basaltic fragments to obtain phosphate, which could have simultaneously oxidise ferrous iron in the basaltic glass to produce the ferric mineral lepidocrocite. Alternatively, these apatite crystals could have originated from decomposing biomass of original microorganisms during diagenesis when organic phosphate leaked. However, this scenario is not immediately consistent with the co-occurrence of the ferric hydroxide mineral lepidocrocite, nor with the distribution of apatite mostly away from the spheroidal ichnofossils. Next, the possibility that the phosphate was remobilised from elsewhere and passively precipitated in the basaltic glass fragment is also not considered likely because apatite is uncommon in the calcite matrix of these hyaloclastite rocks and also because apatite is not found in chloritised basaltic glass fragments without ichnofossils. Lastly, because there is limited palagonitisation in this region and no ichnofossil, it is unlikely that this apatite distribution could be explained by the presence of polyphosphate-accumulating bacteria, as tentatively proposed for modern altered basaltic glass25. Hence, the possibility that some apatite originated from decayed biomass cannot be entirely rejected because microscopic apatite crystals sometimes occur in contact with spheroidal ichnofossils and, also, because all observed apatite crystals contain some OM (Fig. 2j, m). However, nanoscopic size apatite crystals are petrographically grouped with nanoscopic size lepidocrocite (Fig. 2d), which points to a common origin, and they occur in the chlorite matrix in the vicinity of spheroidal ichnofossils, while also notably away from them (Fig. 2c). These observations thus suggest that the original microorganisms were causing the oxidative dissolution of basaltic glass with organic acids to release minor phosphate bound in the basalt to be used in their growth. In this case, however, the phosphate was not yet biologically assimilated and instead precipitated as nanoscopic-sized apatite crystals along with precipitates of lepidocrocite. In any case, the petrographic distribution, the abundance of nanoscopic apatite and lepidocrocite crystals, and the associated spheroidal ichnofossils suggest the precursor phosphate and iron both originated from the basaltic glass fragment. Hence, it is possible to conclude that the original microorganisms were actively seeking phosphate in this environment and that the titanite, lepidocrocite, and apatite are microbially induced alteration products of the original basaltic glass. Besides, due to the highly heterogeneous petrographic distribution of apatite only in some chloritised basaltic glass fragments, phosphate quantification is unlikely to point to a phosphorous enrichment or its mechanism. Future comparisons with similar occurrences in modern deep-sea basaltic glass could yield a more specific mechanism underlying the inferred microbially-induced dissolution of basaltic glass.

Combined polarised microscopy and μRaman images have also revealed the presence of parallel-aligned tubular ichnofossils composed of titanite and OM (Fig. 3a–d), analogous to other occurrences, such as in the Nuvvuagittuq jasper3,4. While some tubular ichnofossils may contain a few nanoscopic size apatite crystals or are associated with apatite in the adjacent calcite matrix (Fig. 3b), the OM-rich tubular ichnofossils do not contain apatite (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 4). Some tubular ichnofossils also have different levels of OM varying from titanite interiors enriched in highly disordered OM (kerogen; Fig. 3d) to interiors with little to no OM at all (Fig. 3b). Transmission electron microscopy of a tubular ichnofossil with abundant OM shows numerous nanoscopic size grains of OM inside titanite, and their absence from matrix chlorite, which occurs as euhedral tabular crystals with perfect 180o cleavages and sizes of a few hundred nanometres (Supplementary Fig. 3). Uniformitarian comparisons for the Flaherty tubes show most similarity with titanite tubes from the 1980 Ma Pilgujärvi Fm. in Pechenga; however the latter are also segmented, branching, and curved17. The Flaherty tubes also share morphological resemblance to tubes in ferruginous sedimentary rocks from seafloor hydrothermal areas like the modern Jan Mayen volcano32, Palaeozoic (between 542 and 251 Ma) Ural Mountains33, and Hadean (before 4031 Ma) of Nuvvuagittuq3,4,34, where tubes are 20–25 μm in diameter, forming small groups of parallel-aligned individuals. Microscopic tubes of titanite in glass rims of Archaean pillow basalt and hyaloclastite have been considered both biologically formed by microbial activity8,17 and abiotic in origin, formed by metamorphic dendritic growth13,19. However, dendrites or frutexites do not occur in any of the studied Flaherty rock specimens. While abiotic ambient inclusion trails are sometimes considered responsible for the formation of some tubular ichnofossils in volcanic glass13,16, no microscopic tube in the Flaherty hyaloclastite hosts terminal mineral inclusions. Alternatively, the Flaherty tubes could also be abiotic biomorphs like those grown in chemical gardens23; however, these are not known to be composed of titanite and OM, nor associated with apatite and carbonate, nor in groups of parallel-aligned individuals with consistent diameters. Besides, abiotic structures located between palagonitised basaltic glass fragments from the Southeast Indian Ridge have previously been considered as abiotic and ‘tubular’ 35; however, they are distinct from the Flaherty tubes because they do not exhibit cylindrical geometry. Hence, the Flaherty tubes can be considered biosignatures because of their parallel aligned mode of occurrence, mineralogy consistent with decayed biomass29, content of OM, and uniformitarian comparisons with other examples in the rock record, also considered biological in origin.

Abiotic and biological carbon cycling

Organic-rich titanite in Flaherty hyaloclastite is commonly found in palagonitised rims of chloritised basaltic glass fragments (Supplementary Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 3), inside ichnofossils, and in partially to completely replaced fragments (Figs. 2a–c). While OM is ubiquitous in the sparry calcite matrix, there are also occurrences of millimetre-sized clumps of OM (Fig. 3e, f). Some OM appears to have been affected by diagenetic chemically oscillating reactions, which produce self-similar patterns with circular concentricity and twins (Fig. 3g, h). The chemically oscillating reactions model is increasingly recognised to explain the abiotic origin of botryoidal and concretionary mineralisation during the decomposition of biomass28,29. In palagonitised fragments from concretionary hyaloclastite in the Flaherty, occasional subspherical cavities of botryoidal patterns enriched in OM and associated with apatite, anatase, and titanite (Fig. 3e, f) suggest the presence of mineralised decomposed biomass. This OM is spectroscopically identical to OM elsewhere in hyaloclastite rocks and thus arguably indigenous and syngenetic with their host rocks, as also supported by the estimated peak metamorphic temperature of 280 ± 30 °C36,37. Such peak metamorphic temperatures are inconsistent with remobilised OM; however, it was noted that hydrothermally altered basalt from the same interbed as an ichnofossil-bearing hyaloclastite rock contains veins of remobilised Fe-oxide (Fig. 3i), which may have an early, post-depositional origin. Furthermore, glassy basalt on the modern seafloor contains botryoidal Mn-oxide minerals considered biological in origin38, whereas botryoidal patterns in palagonitised glass are considered alteration patterns21. In both these cases, however, no specific process has been proposed to explain occurrences of these specific mineralised patterns, unlike for botryoidal patterns in deep-sea ferromanganese nodules39. In addition, concretionary structures are common at the decimetre scale as shown before (Fig. 1h), but also at the centimetre scale in black shale, where they deflect layers of phyllosilicate and carbonate micrite with wrinkly laminations of OM in (Fig. 3j). Therefore, chemically oscillating reactions during abiotic decarboxylation of biomass is the proposed model for the formation of concretionary and botryoidal patterns in seafloor volcanic glass, which is supported by their common association with OM38, ferric-ferrous oxides and phyllosilicates, and characteristic patterns, all of which suggest iron-based redox reactions with OM akin to those in chemically oscillating reactions28. These observations also collectively suggest that associations of OM, apatite, carbonate, and titanite represent mineralised diagenetic phases formed after the abiotic decomposition of biomass. However, we also distinguish the likely diagenetic origin of the latter mineral assemblages with OM and apatite in tubes from those associated with trails of microscopic spheroidal ichnofossils surrounding nanoscopic apatite, lepidocrocite, and OM, which were more likely microbially-dissolved and then precipitated within chlorite.

It has been argued that water-rock interactions with basaltic glass can sustain chemolithotrophic metabolisms such as iron and sulphate reduction if OM or H2 are present40. The calcite-dominated matrices of hyaloclastite, concretionary carbonate, and sparry calcite systematically have negative δ13Ccarb values down to −10.9‰ (Fig. 4; Supplementary Fig. 5, Table S1). Such 13C-depleted compositions are comparable to those in Palaeoarchean hyaloclastites from Barberton and attributed to oxidised microbial OM8,11,25,41. This phenomenon can occur during diagenesis when either abiotic or microbially-mediated oxidation of biomass takes place, and 13C-depleted CO2 is produced from precursor biomass (Berner, 1990), which can then be mineralised into diagenetic carbonate minerals. It is unlikely that later low-grade metamorphism or hydrothermal solutions during or after deposition imparted such fractionation, because of the ubiquitous occurrence of OM in the sparry calcite matrix of hyaloclastite rocks.

Another biosignature in hyaloclastite is the OM enrichment, up to 3.1%wt total organic carbon, with δ13Corg values between −33 and −29 (Supplementary Fig. 5, Table S1), suggesting various possible microbial metabolisms, including chemolithotrophy, but also microbial activity. Flaherty pyritiferous black shales and sparry calcite also have up to 3%wt total organic carbon with δ13Corg values down to −37.5‰, whereas hydrothermally altered basalt has OM with values between −30 and −33‰ (Supplementary Fig. 5, Table S1). These compositions could indicate the biosignature of secondary productivity dominating over primary producing biomass, but also of chemolithotrophic metabolisms such as methanotrophy and acetyl CoA pathway41,42. This is because microbial biomass dominated by oxygenic phototrophs typically has δ13Corg around −27‰, whereas methanotrophy yields biomass that is much more 13C-depleted. Alternatively, although abiotic carbon isotope fractionation by Fischer-Tropsch synthesis in the original seafloor hydrothermal vent environment cannot be entirely dismissed, it is not considered a likely explanation here because (1) there is no evidence for H2 production from the serpentinization of ultramafic rocks in the Flaherty Fm., which is a needed reactant in Fischer-Tropsch synthesis43, (2) abiotic carbon isotope fractionation is usually less than 19‰ in modern hydrothermal vent systems with Fischer-Tropsch synthesis44,45, and (3) this interpretation is not usually considered to explain the presence of OM within ichnofossils in basaltic glass. Likewise, an origin of OM from calcite decarbonation (or reduction) during later metamorphism is incompatible with the higher temperature of 430 °C where these reactions typically occur46 because peak metamorphic temperatures in Flaherty are about 280°C, based on Raman spectra of OM36,37, which is consistent with other estimates from the McLeary and Kasegalik Formations in the Belchers group between about 230 and 300 °C47,48. One last consideration with respect to these 13C-depleted compositions in calcite and OM is that they may also be related to the proposed Shunga-Francevillian Event (SFE), which is when a massive amount of OM was oxidised in the aftermath of the Great Oxidation Event49. The main similarities to this contemporary event in the Flaherty include bulk δ13Corg values down to −37‰ and bulk δ13Ccarb values down to −11‰, similar to the Zaonega Fm. in northwest Russia, where the minimum measured δ13C values were −39‰ for OM and −14‰ for carbonate49. Hence, we conclude that the Flaherty carbon isotope compositions originated from microbial biomass, possibly from chemolithotrophs, which was oxidised during diagenesis and contemporarily to the SFE.

Additionally, pyrite from Flaherty black shale has systematically positive δ34Ssulphide values between +2 and +12‰ (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. 5, Table S1). Given sulphate minerals in the underlying Kasegalik Fm. have δ34Ssulphate around +28‰50, if these values also represent Flaherty seawater sulphate, then the measured slightly positive δ34Ssulphide values most likely represent microbial metabolic fractionation such as microbial sulphate reduction. For instance, microbial sulphate reduction can fractionate sulphur isotopes by more than +15‰ if sulphate concentrations are >200 μM51. However, the possibility of abiotic Rayleigh-type distillation to explain the measured range of positive δ34Ssulphide values is not unambiguously consistent with the expected higher δ34Ssulphide values than ambient seawater sulphate for such a process. While other microbial metabolisms, such as microbial elemental sulphur reduction or disproportionation, cannot be excluded52, mantle sources of sulphur cannot explain both the 34S enrichments and the 10‰ range of δ34Ssulphide values in the Flaherty Fm. Hence, isotopes of carbon and sulphur are most consistent with chemolithotrophic microbial metabolisms in the Flaherty hydrothermal seafloor environment.

Conclusions

The Palaeoproterozoic Flaherty shallow marine hydrothermally exhalative environment preserves evidence for abiotic chemically oscillating reactions in the form of concretionary and botryoidal mineral patterns formed during biomass decarboxylation. Occurrences of diagenetic minerals like apatite and 13C-depleted calcite also suggest biomass oxidation. Major biosignatures include microscopic spheroidal ichnofossils of titanite with a consistent and unimodal size distribution, occurring as unoriented strings with OM, and separately associated with nanoscopic apatite and lepidocrocite, whereas tubular ichnofossils have a similar diameter, parallel alignment and few associations with apatite. It is inferred that the Flaherty microscopic spheroidal ichnofossils were formed by microorganisms that were likely seeking nutrients such as phosphate and iron to sustain chemolithotrophic lifestyles, whereas the tubes were also formed by microorganisms, but their apatite formed during biomass decomposition. All OM and most carbonate are strongly 13C-depleted, pyrite is 34S-enriched, and uniformitarian observations collectively suggest multiple different biosignatures, including possible chemolithotrophy. It is possible that these carbon isotope compositions reflect the contemporary Shunga-Francevillian Event occurring on the edge of the Superior Craton. While each observation may be explained by an abiotic process if taken alone, they are instead argued to represent evidence for microbial activity because they occur together in the same hydrothermally influenced rocks. Hence, the reliability of ichnofossils in volcanic glass from the modern deep-sea, geological record, or other planetary surface requires careful documentation of co-occurring biosignatures, each lending additional support for their biological origin.

Methods

Petrography by optical and scanning electron microscopy

Optical microscopy was performed with an Olympus BX51 polarising microscope with 5×, 10×, 20×, 50×, and 100× long working-distance objectives (i.e. without immersion oil needed) on polished thin sections about 30 μm in thickness (finished with 0.25 μm Al2O3). Petrographic images of targets were collected in reflected light, plane polarised light, and cross-polarised light on the polished thin sections, whereas unpolished rock slabs were scanned in reflected light by a document scanner with a thin film of DI water. Scanning electron microscopy in back-scattered electron and for energy dispersive spectroscopy was used to characterise the morphology and composition of palagonitised spheroids on the edge of chloritised basaltic glass fragments using a JEOL JSM-6480L SEM. Operating conditions were 15 kV accelerating voltage, working distance of 10 mm, and electron beam current of 1 nA on a polished thin section coated with about 20 nm of Au prior to analysis.

Raman micro-spectroscopy

Raman micro-spectroscopic imaging was performed with an α300 WITec Confocal Raman Imaging system using a 532 nm laser with a power of 4–7 mW at up to 1000× magnification. A 50 μm diameter optic fibre was used to collect inelastically scattered photons, and a 600 lines/mm grating was used to generate spectra with a bandwidth of about 4000 cm−1 along with 4 cm−1 of spectral resolution. Hyperspectral imaging was done with the 100× objective at the maximum spatial resolution of 3 pixels per micron (diffraction-limited) and collected at 0.5 μm depth from the sample surface to minimise contamination, which was not detected. Raman spectra for each pixel (360 × 360 nm) were acquired with a dwell time of 0.6 s. Raman hyperspectral images of mineral associations were generated by mapping peak intensity from each spectral scan using a data acquisition and processing29,31. Cosmic ray reduction was applied to correct raw average Raman spectra with a filter size of 2 and a dynamic factor of 8. A background subtraction using a polynomial of order 5 was also applied to some spectra to better highlight peaks partly hidden by high fluorescence.

Stable C and S isotopes by Isotope ratio mass spectrometry

The carbon isotope composition of OM was measured for micro-drilled powders from slabs. A dental micro-drill with a 1.7 mm diameter stainless steel bit was used to generate powders from slightly polished slabs, with the first millimetre of powder discarded as potentially contaminated. Drilled powders were collected onto muffled aluminium foil and poured into muffled glass vials (550 °C for at least 4 h) until analysis. Total organic carbon analyses were performed with a Costech ECS 4010 elemental analyser (EA) linked to a Thermo Fisher Delta V IRMS through a Conflo IV interface. Sulphur isotope analyses were performed on a separate CE2500 EA directly linked to the IRMS. Powdered samples were weighed in muffled Ag boats (around 5 mg of powder) for decarbonation with ultrapure 6 N HCl (Sequanal Grade, Thermo Scientific). Samples for sulphur isotopes were weighed in tin boats. Decarbonated samples were then introduced by the EA autosampler for combustion at 1020 °C in the presence of ultrapure O2. Long-term tests (>12 months) on in-house standards gave reproducibility better than ±0.1‰ for δ13Corg (NBS 22), better than ±0.2‰ for both δ13Ccarb and δ18Ocarb (CHI), and better than ±0.3‰ for δ34S (sulphanilamide). During each analytical session, standards for OM (Peru mud), carbonate (NBS 19), and sulphur (CDT) were intercalated with unknowns and yielded a 1σ reproducibility of 0.3‰ (n = 6), 0.1‰ and 0.1‰ (n = 3), and 0.3‰ (n = 3), respectively for δ13Corg, δ13Ccarb, δ18Ocarb, and δ34SCDT (Table S1).

Focused Ion beam nanofabrication and lift-out

Focused ion beam (FIB) milling and lift-out were performed with a Leo 1540 Zeiss Focused Ion Beam and a Zeiss Cross Beam 350, both equipped with a Ga beam and various detectors, including for secondary electrons, backscattered electrons, and Energy Dispersive Spectroscopy (EDS). A ca. 1 μm Pt shield was deposited on the surface of the gold-coated target prior to milling. A focused 30 keV 30 nA Ga+ primary beam was then used to sputter away material from both sides of the deposited Pt shield, followed by in situ thinning with 3 nA, 1 nA and 300 pA. Using a microscopic W needle, the FIB lamellae were then lifted out and welded with Pt onto a Cu TEM half-grid. After cutting the needle, the lamellae were thin down with progressively smaller beam currents (down to 50 pA, 3 keV). The final lamellae sizes were about 15 × 10 × 0.1 μm.

Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed using a JEOL JEM-F200 TEM equipped with a cold field emission gun and a JED2300T Super X-Ray EDS system. Analyses were performed with a beam with 200 keV accelerating voltage and emission current around 14.6–15.0 μA focused on each FIB lamellae. Bright field (BF-TEM) images were first collected, followed by dark field (DF-TEM) images in the scanning transmission electron microscopy mode (STEM). Elemental maps were also collected in the latter mode (TEM-EDS), and spot analyses were performed to help characterise the composition of the different minerals in the lamellae. Full integration time for EDS maps was between 6 and 10 min. Data was processed with the Aztec software.

Helium microscopy

Surface analysis of the targeted area with nanoscopic size apatite and lepidocrocite was performed using the Zeiss Orion NanoFAB. The gold coat was first removed by exposure to a 1.6–0.8 pA focused beam of Neon, and secondary electron images were acquired using a 0.4 pA focused beam of He+. The He+ and Ne+ ions were accelerated to 25 KeV and 15 KeV respectively. Contamination of the surface of the polished thin section by organic matter was minimised by cleaning the Orion NanoFab sample chamber using an oxygen plasma o system.
