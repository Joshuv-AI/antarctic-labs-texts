A severe and fatal course of statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy: a case report
Authors: Akrum Saleh, Damien A Sighaka Sighaka, Nitya Panyala, Hassaan Abid, Mina Batarseh, Rodney Joe O Yuhico
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionStatin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy is a rare but serious complication of statin therapy, marked by progressive muscle weakness and elevated creatine phosphokinase levels that persist despite discontinuation of the drug. We present a case in which statin-associated myopathy was initially misattributed to hepatic dysfunction due to elevated liver enzymes. Despite early recognition and immunosuppressive therapy, the patient experienced relentless decline, ultimately leading to death. This case highlights the progression and potential severity of SINAM, emphasizing the need for early consideration/screening and intervention.Case reportA 59-year-old White male with a history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type II diabetes, and benign prostatic hyperplasia was started on statin therapy (atorvastatin 20 mg daily) for primary prevention of coronary artery disease. He developed progressively worsening generalized fatigue and weakness, most prominently affecting the upper extremities. Lab tests revealed elevated liver enzymes (ALT and AST), prompting discontinuation of statins. Despite this, symptoms and enzyme levels did not improve. Extensive evaluation, including viral, autoimmune, and gastrointestinal workups, yielded negative or normal results, except for elevated inflammatory markers and creatine phosphokinase, which suggested muscle involvement. Imaging and liver biopsy were unremarkable. Neurological assessment did not explain the symptoms. Given the elevated CPK and persistent symptoms, statin-related myopathy was suspected. Treatment with corticosteroids prednisone, immunosuppressants (methotrexate, azathioprine), and eventually IVIG failed to treat the disease. The patient’s condition deteriorated over 3 years, with worsening muscle weakness, recurrent aspiration, respiratory failure requiring multiple hospitalizations, intubation, and ultimately tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube placement. The diagnostic complexity and overlap with hepatic pathology can delay recognition and treatment. Introduction

Statins are commonly prescribed lipid-lowering agents with well-established benefits in the primary and secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease. Statins are competitive inhibitors of the enzyme 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, which catalyzes the rate-limiting step in hepatic cholesterol biosynthesis. By inhibiting this enzyme, statins reduce the synthesis of cholesterol in hepatocytes, leading to a compensatory upregulation of hepatic LDL receptors and thus increased clearance of circulating low-density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) from the plasma. This mechanism is central to their lipid-lowering and atheroprotective effects.

Although very rare and variably reversible, HMG-CoA reductase associated myopathy could be fatal. The prevalence of statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy associated with anti-HMGCR antibodies, is very rare. The data indicates an incidence of approximately 2 to 3 cases per 100,000 statin-treated patients. In the case report, we present a rare and severe example of this condition. In a 59-year-old male, initially, his elevated liver enzymes were misattributed to hepatic dysfunction. However, despite early statin cessation and extensive multidisciplinary evaluation, the patient experienced relentless clinical deterioration, ultimately requiring tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube placement, and culminating in death after three years of progressive decline. This case underscores the diagnostic challenges posed by statin-induced myopathies, particularly when liver enzyme elevations obscure the underlying muscular pathology. It also highlights the potential for poor prognosis despite immunosuppressive therapy, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical suspicion and early intervention.

Case description

A 59-year-old White man with a medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidemia, type II diabetes mellitus, and benign prostatic hyperplasia, and with no known family history of neuromuscular or autoimmune disorders, was evaluated during a routine health maintenance visit with his primary care provider. Because of elevated lipid levels, atorvastatin 20 mg daily was initiated for primary prevention of coronary artery disease in accordance with guideline recommendations.

Over the following 5 months, he developed progressively worsening generalized fatigue and weakness, most prominently affecting the upper extremities, along with unintentional weight loss and constant myalgias.

After 2 years of symptoms improvement (during that time the patient was lost to follow up), he again started to experience progressive clinical deterioration. An outpatient trial of azathioprine was initiated at 50 mg daily and subsequently increased to 100 mg and he was followed for symptoms improvement. Over the next few months, the patient became wheelchair-bound, demonstrated significant dysphagia, and had markedly weakened upper and lower extremities. Due to this, plans to begin rituximab therapy were discussed, but initiation was delayed due to loss of insurance coverage. While attempting to escalate therapy, the patient experienced aspiration events, declining respiratory effort, and increasing dependency in activities of daily living, prompting admission to our ICU unit for management of septic shock and intubation. Inpatient escalation of therapy was pursued with addition of IVIG at 2 g/kg administered over 3 days, followed by plans for maintenance dosing of 1 g/kg after 4 weeks. Unfortunately, IVIG did not result in meaningful clinical improvement. The patient continued to deteriorate and required prolonged intubation, followed by placement of a tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube for long-term airway protection and nutritional support. Given his status and in accordance with the patient’s preferences and wishes, a transition to hospice care with comfort measures was planned under the involvement of the palliative care team. Eventually, the patient passed away after experiencing continuous functional decline and failed response to therapy.

Discussion

Statins are among the most commonly and widely prescribed medications due to their proven effectiveness at reducing cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. The benefits of this class of medications are considerable and well-established. These include prevention of cardiovascular disease, strokes, and cardiovascular death. Statins lower cholesterol by inhibiting HMG-CoA reductase, the key enzyme responsible for the rate-limiting step in hepatic cholesterol synthesis. This inhibition decreases cholesterol production in the liver, which in turn upregulates LDL receptors on hepatocytes and improves the clearance of LDL cholesterol from the bloodstream[1].

While generally well-tolerated, statins are associated with a spectrum of muscle-related adverse effects, which range from mild myalgias to rare but serious conditions such as statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy (also known as HMG-CoA reductase-associated myopathy). This immune-mediated condition is characterized by progressive muscle weakness, markedly elevated creatine phosphokinase levels, and the presence of anti-HMG-CoA reductase antibodies[2].

Pathophysiology involves an immune-mediated process in which autoantibodies target 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl-coenzyme A reductase, the pharmacologic target of statins. Statin exposure upregulates HMGCR expression in regenerating muscle fibers, which may increase the immunogenicity of the enzyme and trigger the production of anti-HMGCR autoantibodies in genetically susceptible individuals[3]. It has also been theorized that statins’ lipophilicity may influence their uptake into muscle tissue. Most statins, except for pravastatin and rosuvastatin, are lipophilic and are believed to have a longer residence time in tissues such as myocytes. This characteristic has been suggested as a possible factor in increasing the risk of statin-associated muscle symptoms, though this remains unproven, as all statins can cause these symptoms and rhabdomyolysis. However, no clear link between these properties and statin-induced necrotizing myopathy was established [4, 5].

The prevalence of statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy, also known as immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy associated with anti-HMGCR antibodies, is very low. The data indicates an incidence of approximately 2 to 3 cases per 100,000 statin-treated patients. This aligns with the American Heart Association’s scientific statement, which describes statin-associated autoimmune myopathy as a “very rare” complication. Deaths from statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy are exceedingly rare and not quantifiable from current cohort or pharmacovigilance data which is variably reversible with statin discontinuation [6, 7]. Atorvastatin is the most frequently associated with statin-induced necrotizing myopathy, accounting for approximately 76.2% of reported cases, as reported in a retrospective multi-site cohort study which took place in Australia and the UK [7].

In the case we discussed above, the 59-year-old male, initially, presented with elevated liver enzymes, which was misattributed to hepatic dysfunction, and despite early statin cessation and multidisciplinary evaluation, he experienced clinical deterioration. Ultimately requiring tracheostomy and gastrostomy tube placement, and culminating in death after about 3 years of progressive decline. This case underscores the challenges posed by statin-induced myopathies, particularly when liver enzyme elevations obscure the underlying muscular pathology. It also highlights the potential for poor prognosis despite immunosuppressive therapy, emphasizing the need for heightened clinical suspicion and early intervention [2, 8].

To our knowledge, this case represents one of the few documented instances of fatal HMG-CoA reductase myopathy with such a prolonged and refractory course, contributing valuable insight into the natural history, diagnostic complexity, and therapeutics involved with this rare condition [9–11].

At present, there is limited standardized management protocol or approved therapeutic regimen for the treatment of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. However, discontinuation of statin therapy is considered a critical initial step in the management process [12], and the initiation of additional immunosuppressive treatment is typically required. Corticosteroids are often used as first-line agents, often in combination with methotrexate or azathioprine. If patients do not respond to therapy, the use of rituximab or intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) may be warranted [13]. Treatment can tailored based on disease severity, patients with very mild statin-associated muscle disease, simply discontinuing the statin and closely monitoring symptoms and creatine kinase levels may be sufficient. However, treatment should begin promptly if weakness fails to improve or worsens. For patients with moderate disease, initial management typically involves dual therapy with prednisone at 1 mg/kg/day and a steroid-sparing agent such as methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil, or tacrolimus to limit steroid toxicity and enhance disease control. Intravenous immunoglobulin is also an effective option, sometimes even as monotherapy, particularly for patients who cannot tolerate corticosteroids, such as those with diabetes or other contraindications [14, 15]. Patients with severe disease characterized by marked proximal weakness, dysphagia with aspiration risk, respiratory muscle involvement, cardiac manifestations, or rapid clinical progression should receive immediate triple therapy. This regimen includes high-dose corticosteroids (often beginning with an intravenous bolus), a steroid-sparing immunosuppressant such as azathioprine, methotrexate, tacrolimus, or mycophenolate mofetil, and intravenous immunoglobulin [14, 16]. For refractory disease, rituximab is the most commonly used biologic agent. However, in anti-HMGCR cases specifically, only about one-third of patients achieve clinical improvement [17].

Clinical outcomes can vary significantly and are influenced by the timing of diagnosis and the prompt initiation of appropriate therapy. Early recognition and intervention are crucial for improving prognosis. In this case, the gradual progression of symptoms and the notable laboratory finding of elevated liver enzymes initially guided the diagnostic workup toward hepatic and neurological etiologies. Upon exclusion of these causes, an autoimmune evaluation was conducted, which confirmed a diagnosis of immune-mediated necrotizing myopathy. Despite initial improvement, the disease ultimately progressed and led to the patient's demise [18].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this case underscores the complexities and challenges associated with diagnosing and managing statin-induced necrotizing autoimmune myopathy, particularly in cases where findings may be misattributed to other conditions such as hepatic dysfunction. It highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for autoimmune myopathies in patients receiving statins. Despite being a rare condition, the potential for severe outcomes necessitates prompt recognition and intervention. Current management strategies emphasize the discontinuation of statins along with initiating immunosuppressive therapies. The variability in clinical outcomes reinforces the need for further research to enhance understanding and treatment protocols, particularly for cases with prolonged and refractory courses.
