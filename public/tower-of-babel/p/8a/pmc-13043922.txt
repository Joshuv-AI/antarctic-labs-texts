Increasing EEG electrode density improves decoding of visual categories and source localization: an exploratory ultra-high-density EEG study
Authors: Leonhard Schreiner, Sebastian Sieghartsleitner, Christoph Kapeller, Michael Jordan, Josef Scharinger, Rossella Spataro, Harald Pretl, Christoph Guger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding how the brain processes visual information is central to cognitive neuroscience. This exploratory study investigates the potential of ultra-high-density (uHD) electroencephalography (EEG) to capture neural dynamics during visual processing. We demonstrate that increasing EEG electrode density enhances spatial precision, improves the decoding of visual information, and facilitates more accurate source localization. Using a uHD EEG system with 512 electrodes focused over the occipital region, we recorded brain responses to four visual categories: faces, bodies, objects, and patterns. Our results show that higher electrode density facilitates more accurate single-trial classification of visual stimuli, achieving an average accuracy of 73% using uHD EEG and regularized linear discriminant analysis. Comparisons across different electrode layouts revealed that reducing electrode distance substantially affects decoding performance more than expanding the electrode coverage area. Additionally, higher electrode density resulted in more detailed topographic activation maps and improved source localization of the face-selective N170 component in the fusiform gyrus. While based on a limited number of participants, these findings highlight the critical role of electrode layout in visual EEG paradigms and demonstrate the promise of uHD EEG for high-resolution investigations across both research and clinical domains.

Introduction

Decoding the neural mechanisms of visual processing is crucial for understanding how humans perceive and interact with their environment. Various methodologies, such as functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI)1,2, electroencephalography (EEG)3, magnetoencephalography (MEG)4, electrocorticography (ECoG)5, and stereoelectroencephalography (SEEG)6,7 have been used to investigate the neural processes underlying the visual system in the brain. The primary visual cortex (V1) has long been a central focus of many research activities, as it plays a fundamental role in processing contrast, angles, and colors8. These aspects were first systematically described in a study by Hubel and Wiesel9, who conducted pioneering work with monkeys.

Among the available methodologies, EEG stands out due to its excellent temporal resolution, which makes it particularly suitable for investigating the rapid dynamics of early-stage visual processing. The visual evoked potential (VEP), a time-locked response to visual stimuli, is a well-established marker of visual system function10,11. Advances in decoding have revealed that EEG signals encode rich information about stimulus identity, enabling the classification of complex visual categories8,12. These advances have sparked growing interest in how the spatial sampling of EEG influences decoding performance.

Specifically, high-density and ultra-high-density (uHD) EEG configurations have been proposed to improve the extraction of stimulus-specific neural representations by capturing more localized activity, particularly over occipital regions. Our study contributes to this line of research by providing high-resolution data from a small, well-controlled sample, offering insight into the spatial limits and potential benefits of uHD EEG.

Several studies suggest that increasing electrode density over the occipital lobe enhances decoding performance, as reflected by higher classification accuracies for different visual stimulus categories8,13. High-density EEG recordings indicate that an electrode spacing of approximately 10 mm may be optimal for capturing non-local brain activity, as scalp smearing distorts spatial patterns more than temporal–spectral properties14.

Accurate decoding, however, also relies on the choice of classification algorithms that can extract relevant neural information. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) has been widely adopted in neuroscience and Brain-Computer Interface (BCI) research for decoding neural responses, particularly in studies of visual perception using both EEG and MEG modalities15. Its strength lies in its simplicity, computational efficiency, and interpretability, especially for single-trial classification16–18. Although more complex classifiers, such as convolutional and recurrent neural networks (CNNs, RNNs), have demonstrated improved performance in various EEG classification tasks, including visual decoding19–21, these approaches often require large datasets and extensive training. Moreover, these classifiers usually lack interpretability, making it challenging to link classification outcomes to specific neural processes. In contrast, LDA offers more transparent decision boundaries and is well-suited for smaller datasets, making it particularly appropriate for controlled experimental studies where interpretability is essential.

In parallel with advances in classification techniques, substantial progress has also been made in EEG source localization, enabling more spatially resolved interpretations of brain activity. Advancements in computational power, high-resolution MRI, and source localization algorithms have opened new possibilities for refining source analysis from EEG22. Recent studies suggest that a high electrode count significantly enhances spatial precision in source estimation23. In EEG research, electrode placement is often guided by the anatomical locations of underlying brain regions relevant to the study’s focus, particularly when investigating functionally specialized cortical areas24. However, to achieve more accurate source localization, whole-scalp EEG coverage is often used, as it provides a more comprehensive view of brain dynamics23.

Given our interest in early-stage visual processing, we employed a region-specific setup focused on the occipital cortex. For example, in studies examining localized brain functions, precise electrode placement can improve signal capture and analysis13,25,26. By concentrating on a specific area, we aimed to reduce setup complexity while still achieving our research objectives.

Using VEPs as time-locked components for source localization is a common and well-established practice27,28. Several toolboxes, such as FieldTrip29 and Python MNE30, support source localization. Given MNE’s accessibility, popularity, comprehensive documentation, and extensive algorithmic options (e.g., sLORETA, beamforming), we selected it as the source estimation method for our study. MNE has been successfully applied in source localization studies involving high-density EEG and region-specific analyses30,31. For example, recent high-resolution EEG source localization techniques have significantly enhanced the precision of localizing epileptogenic zones through EEG source imaging. These developments have improved the accuracy of identifying seizure onset areas, thereby aiding in the presurgical evaluation of epilepsy32–34.

Standard EEG systems typically use 64–256 scalp electrodes, while the uHD EEG system applied in our study extends up to 1024 channels35. This system, featuring flexible electrode grids, enables enhanced spatial resolution for measuring brain activity. Notably, the uHD EEG system has also been employed in motor research to decode fine motor skills, such as single-finger movements36, hand gestures37, grasping tasks38 as well as non-invasive mapping of the central sulcus39. Other research explores non-invasive EEG-based decoding for language processing, specifically applying the uHD EEG over Broca’s area and other language-related regions40. A previous study utilized the uHD EEG system in combination with the FieldTrip29 toolbox to perform source localization in response to vibrotactile stimulation of individual fingers. The findings demonstrated that event-related potentials (ERPs) elicited by vibrotactile stimulation of fingers on both the left and right hands could be effectively employed in source estimation using dipole fitting and minimum norm estimation techniques41.

Here, we address the challenge of capturing fine-grained spatiotemporal neural dynamics in visual processing by increasing spatial electrode density in EEG. We strategically positioned 512 uHD EEG channels over the occipital area to investigate neural correlates of visual processing by utilizing VEPs across four stimulus categories. We included downsampled electrode subsets of the uHD EEG system, according to standard EEG systems (10-20, 10-10, extended 10-10, and 10-05), to systematically investigate the effects of decreasing spatial sampling density on decoding performance, topographical maps, and source localization. Classification analysis demonstrated high single-trial accuracy, with performance decreasing significantly at lower electrode densities. A systematic comparison of sensor configurations revealed that increasing electrode density improved classification accuracy more effectively than increasing the total electrode coverage area. Source localization identified early activation in the occipital cortex, followed by later responses in the ventral temporal cortex, with the N170 component precisely mapped to the fusiform gyrus for face stimuli. These findings highlight the potential of uHD EEG for precise neural decoding, providing initial evidence that supports further investigation in larger cohorts.

Results

This study investigates visual processing using the uHD EEG system to examine the neural dynamics elicited by various visual stimuli. The results cover detailed analyses of visual evoked potentials, spatial distributions of brain activity, classification performance, source localization, and the impact of different electrode layout configurations.

The temporal generalization maps reveal that neural patterns elicited by visual stimuli remain distinct over time, enabling accurate classification. For all the maps shown in Fig. 3, the One-vs-Rest (OvR) Area Under the Curve (AUC) method was chosen. At approximately 0.07 s post-stimulus, a significant increase in AUC is observed, reaching its peak at approximately 0.2 s, as indicated by the red regions on the generalization maps (Fig. 3a) and the diagonal decoding performance plots (Fig. 3b, c). Although high AUC values are confined to a brief period around 150 ms, this aligns with the known timing of category-specific visual processing and supports the effectiveness of our decoding approach in capturing transient, yet meaningful, neural patterns. Figure 3a, b reflect results from the analysis of the uHD EEG data. The areas surrounding the maximum AUC (red) often display blue regions, indicating low AUC or below chance levels. This phenomenon is usually attributed to cross-temporal interference or model overfitting, where the classifier trained on one temporal feature performs poorly when tested on a non-overlapping temporal window43. Areas marked in green represent chance-level performance.

In the OvR classification problem (Fig. 3a, b), subjects S1 and S3 achieved a peak AUC of approximately 0.8 around 0.14 s after stimulus onset. Subject S2 reached a similar maximum AUC of 0.8 slightly later, at ~0.16 s. Subject S4 demonstrated somewhat lower performance, with a peak AUC of 0.74.

Figure 3c shows the diagonal decoding performance extracted from the TGM across all electrode subsets. Decoding accuracy increases with sensor electrode density, reflecting improved spatial sampling. In addition to achieving greater peak accuracy, accuracy increases earlier. This indicates that higher spatial resolution enhances classification performance and allows earlier detection of discriminative neural signals.

Improved temporal dynamics with higher spatial sampling align with prior findings that volume conduction and limited spatial resolution can delay or distort the timing of scalp-recorded neural events. Enhancing spatial resolution, through denser arrays or source localization, allows for a more accurate recovery of underlying temporal dynamics. The shift toward earlier decoding onsets in denser configurations supports the notion that better spatial resolution also enhances EEG’s effective temporal resolution44.

Impact of electrode subsampling on topographies and source localization

Figure 5 compares EEG layouts of varying electrode density to assess their impact on surface topographies and source localization, using subject S1’s response to face stimuli. The full uHD EEG montage is contrasted with downsampled subsets (10-20, 10-10, extended 10-10, 10-05), all interpolated on the scalp using the same method50. The uHD setup revealed dynamic scalp activity and distinct early and late components, with dorsal and ventral views highlighting increased fusiform activation with higher density. Notably, the spatial resolution of the uHD EEG enabled the visualization of sharp spatial boundaries and localized peaks in activity, which appeared blurred or diffuse in lower-density systems. While the 10-20 layout lacks coverage in the area of interest (occipital pole), intermediate layouts, especially the extended 10-10 layout, provided improved but still limited detail. Overall, higher electrode density enhances spatial precision and the interpretability of the neural responses. Discussion

This study presents a focused, proof-of-concept investigation into the influence of EEG electrode density on visual processing using ultra-high-density EEG. Although the number of participants was limited, the results provide critical methodological insights into how increased spatial sampling impacts neural decoding accuracy and source localization precision.

Spatial dynamics of visual evoked potentials in uHD EEG

The VEPs show similar time-wise behavior across subjects, with an initial peak at around 100 ms, followed by an accumulation at around 200 ms, with only subject S4 having a delayed behavior and just one prominent but increased peak in the evoked potential. Our study used relatively long stimulus durations (0.6, 0.8, and 1 s), aligning with transient VEPs51. Given the nature of our stimulus, it fits within the flash VEP category, which is characterized by a series of positive and negative waves over a broader temporal range. This matches the expected dynamics and morphology described for standard transient responses. The amplitude of the peaks is around a maximum between 6 and 10 microvolts, as reported in other literature10,27,51. The selection of a bandpass filter with a high-pass cutoff at 1 Hz and a low-pass cutoff at 40 Hz effectively captures the primary components of the VEPs, which predominantly reside within this frequency range. This filtering approach enhances signal clarity by attenuating low-frequency drifts and high-frequency noise, thereby improving the accuracy of VEP measurements52. However, our low-pass filter settings are close to the limit for optimal performance, as low-pass filters can sometimes cause delays in visual evoked potentials53. This effect could contribute to the observed delay in VEPs compared to findings from other studies51,54. The early component appears in the occipital cortex around V1, progressing to regions such as the ventral temporal cortex at later stages. Recent evidence highlights its role in propagating visual signals from the occipital cortex to ventral regions, supporting specialized cognitive, behavioral, and affective functions through interactions with multiple cortical and subcortical systems55,56.

In line with previous work57–60, we observed that VEP morphology exhibited greater similarity across tasks within subjects than across subjects performing the same task. This pattern reflects known intersubject variability in early components, which can arise from differences in cortical folding, source orientation, skull conductivity, and electrode positioning. Although all participants share the same fundamental VEP components, their scalp-level projections may vary in shape and timing. Arousal and attentional states further contribute to this variability. Interestingly, the feature weight topographies (see Supplementary Fig. 5) derived from classification were more consistent across subjects than the raw VEPs, suggesting that similar neural processes are modulated across individuals despite anatomical and physiological differences. Notably, medial occipital electrodes consistently contributed most to decoding performance across subjects. While a quantitative similarity analysis across individuals in the spatial and temporal domains would be of interest, such an analysis would require spatial normalization (e.g., through source alignment), which was beyond the scope of this study.

Higher spatial resolution in EEG enhances neural decoding of visual categories

The results presented in Table 1 highlight the classification performance of the 4-class problem across different electrode system layouts, offering insights into the influence of electrode density on decoding accuracy. The uHD EEG system consistently outperformed all configurations, achieving the highest average accuracy (73.2%) across subjects and reaching a peak performance of 76.6% for subject S3. In contrast, the traditional 10-20 system yielded the lowest average accuracy (57.2%), with subject S4 showing the poorest performance at 53.0%. Importantly, these differences in accuracy are not only numerical but also statistically meaningful. As the electrode density decreased, from the uHD EEG system to subsets resembling the 10-05, e10-10, standard 10-10, and finally, the traditional 10-20 layout, there was a consistent decline in classification performance. This trend was particularly evident in the mean accuracies, where each reduction in density corresponded to a noticeable drop in decoding performance. Our findings align with the study by Robinson et al.,13 which demonstrated that “super-Nyquist” density (SND–14 mm sensor distance) EEG arrays capture more neural information than Nyquist density (ND–28 mm sensor distance) arrays, as evidenced by the superior classification accuracy achieved with the uHD EEG system in our analysis. However, in comparison, their study investigated six different visual stimuli varying in two low-level visual features. While our results support these findings, they should be interpreted cautiously due to the limited number of participants. Further research is required to assess the generalizability of these effects across more diverse populations.

We use the TGM to identify when specific mental content becomes decodable in brain activity, revealing the time course of neural representations. This approach reveals the temporal organization of information-processing stages by examining how classifiers generalize across time and experimental conditions. The temporal generalization results in Fig. 3 highlight robust classification performance across subjects with high AUC scores. The observed pattern is an increase in classification accuracy starting at 0.07 s post-stimulus, peaking around 0.14–0.2 s, and decreasing afterward. A trend is noticeable across all pairs containing the object class, which generally exhibit slightly lower performance. The generalization maps and the AUC time plots show a slight dip after the initial peak at 0.13–0.15 s, followed by a second peak around 0.2 s, closely correlated to the peak timing of the VEP plots. This temporal behavior is similar to that observed in other studies using object categories as stimuli and plotting temporal cross-decoding matrices averaged across participants61,62. Adjacent blue regions, reflecting below-chance performance, are notable patterns observed in the temporal generalization analysis. These may occur when rLDA weights derived from an earlier time point are applied to a later time point, and the VEP’s polarity (i.e., sign) at the earlier time point is opposite to that at the later time point. This polarity difference leads to the rLDA score being flipped, resulting in predictions of the opposite class and, consequently, below-chance performance. Such discrepancies may arise from neural patterns during these periods being inconsistent with the features learned by the classifier or from those time windows reflecting noise or overlapping processes unrelated to the specific visual stimulus43.

Classification accuracy as a function of electrode distance and electrode coverage area

The findings presented in Fig. 2 highlight the critical role of spatial electrode configuration in influencing classification performance in VEP-based BCIs. Across all four subjects, classification accuracy improved substantially with reduced electrode distance than with increased electrode coverage area alone. This suggests that denser spatial sampling, enabled by uHD EEG, offers greater benefits for decoding neural responses than simply expanding the covered area with standard electrode layouts13,26. The superior performance of the uHD EEG configuration, indicated by the white-filled circle, underscores this advantage. Despite each uHD electrode covering a smaller area than conventional electrodes, the dense layout supports higher accuracies in the 4-class classification task. In comparison, the 10-10 layout, marked by the white star, offers broader coverage but yields lower classification accuracy, reinforcing the importance of high spatial resolution over sheer coverage area.

Interestingly, while classification accuracy continues to improve as electrode spacing decreases, the observed trend points toward a potential saturation point around 12 mm sensor distance. Below this threshold, gains in performance become less pronounced, indicating a possible optimal range for electrode spacing that balances decoding accuracy with practical considerations like setup complexity and hardware constraints. These results underscore the importance of spatial resolution for decoding accuracy, but broader validation across more subjects and experimental designs is necessary to confirm the robustness of this trend. Future work could explore the potential of neural networks to further boost classification accuracy, especially in large-scale or real-time applications, albeit at the cost of reduced model interpretability.

Source localization and combined analysis

The uHD EEG system’s high spatial resolution and advanced source localization techniques allowed for the precise localization of cortical activation patterns for each stimulus class. Our analysis focused on the early and late components of the VEP, particularly those around 100 and 200 ms, consistent with findings from Francesco Di Russo et al.10, who explored similar components using source localization from scalp EEG and comparison MRI measurements.

In our study, the early component was observed as a negative deflection in the VEP, corresponding to the first activation unit in source localization. Following this, a positive peak was identified as the second component, reflecting a subsequent increase in AU activity at the source level. MRI studies investigating visual processing in the primary visual cortex demonstrate findings consistent with our research, which identifies the involvement of comparable brain regions, particularly within the visual cortex (V1 and VTC)63,64.

Our findings align with this study’s emphasis on the ventral temporal cortex as a critical region for visual categorization and recognition, as found by Grill-Spector and Weiner63. Almost all stimulus categories elicited activation in the VTC during the late component, with faces showing particularly pronounced activation around the N170 event-related potential. The activation patterns we observed further support the idea that the VTC integrates visual information at multiple levels of abstraction to achieve efficient visual categorization. The observed activation patterns support the view that the ventral temporal cortex integrates visual information across multiple levels of abstraction to facilitate efficient visual categorization. Furthermore, the progression of neural signals from the primary visual cortex toward both dorsal and ventral streams underscores the complex temporal and spatial dynamics underlying visual processing65. ECoG studies further corroborate the temporal and spatial patterns observed in our source estimation results66,67. For instance, a study by Martin et al.67 demonstrated that high-frequency broadband (HFB) responses exhibit a temporal propagation pattern similar to ours. Their findings showed that activation begins around 70 ms post-stimulus in medial occipital regions (V1, V2, V3) and sequentially propagates to lateral occipital areas (LO-1, LO-2), ventral occipital regions (hV4, VO-1, VO-2), dorsal occipital areas (V3A, V3B), and finally to the posterior parietal cortex (IPS-0 to IPS-4). These results highlight the structured progression of visual processing across the cortical hierarchy68.

However, our source-level findings did not comprehensively investigate the specific cortical areas associated with each stimulus category. Conducting such analyses could offer deeper insights and enable more precise conclusions about the role of distinct regions in processing different categories. Our additional analysis, however, specifically focused on face processing, providing detailed insights (see Supplementary Fig. 8) into the neural dynamics and cortical areas involved in this category. We found that specifically in the timeframe of 160–180 ms post-stimulus, we found higher activation in the VTC, marked explicitly in the fusiform face gyrus, compared to all other stimulus categories, as reported in a broad base of literature45–48.

Benefits of uHD EEG

The utilization of uHD EEG systems markedly enhances the spatial resolution of activation maps, surpassing the capabilities of standard EEG system layouts. The increased electrode density facilitates more precise localization of neural activity, enabling detailed 3D topographic mapping. For instance, a study employing the uHD EEG system for mapping the central sulcus via median nerve stimulation demonstrated refined topographies and distinct changes in somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs) across adjacent sensors39. Another study utilizing the system for decoding individual finger movements demonstrated precise activation maps, capturing detailed movement-related event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/S) patterns36.

Similarly to those studies, our findings indicate that uHD EEG provides high-resolution visual evoked potentials, allowing for generating precise 3D topographic maps. We consider the consistency of evoked potentials to be particularly advantageous for advancing analyses with high-density electrode systems. Integrating these maps with MRI data enhances their anatomical relevance, offering more accurate representations compared to traditional two-dimensional topography maps or template models, for example, based on MNI15269. While previous studies using standard-density EEG have successfully localized activity in visual areas such as the fusiform gyrus70, it remained unclear to what extent uHD EEG provides advantages in capturing fine-grained spatial details or improves the robustness of source localization. Although all data originate from the same recordings, downsampling allows us to systematically evaluate the impact of electrode density on both scalp-level spatial patterns and reconstructed cortical sources. The comparisons aim to demonstrate the added value of dense spatial sampling, particularly for decoding and mapping visual processes with higher fidelity. This integration broadens the scope for detailed anatomical and functional insights, paving the way for potential clinical applications71.

Limitations

To ensure optimal performance, users should be aware that the system works best when the scalp is shaved, making this practice essential for effective use. Additionally, increasing the number of channels in the uHD EEG system introduces form factor challenges, leading to longer setup times and reduced user comfort. A limitation of our study is the partial scalp coverage provided by the current uHD EEG system, which may restrict the investigation of cortical regions beyond the area of interest. To address this, future studies should consider systems with full scalp coverage or add targeted electrode grids to capture a more comprehensive view of cortical activation. Expanding spatial coverage would also allow a more detailed analysis of the spatial and temporal dynamics associated with different stimulus categories. Alternatively, combining standard low-density EEG layouts with uHD grids in specific regions or placing sparse sensor arrays across the scalp could help mitigate these limitations while maintaining practical usability. While the results are promising, the limited number of subjects represents a constraint, and future studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm and extend these findings. The absence of an online attention monitoring component in our study represents a limitation, as we could not verify or control the attentional engagement of participants during the experiment. In future research, incorporating real-time attention assessments is recommended to ensure consistent levels of attention across subjects, thereby obtaining more reliable VEP measurements51. Another limitation of this study is that decoding was not performed on inverse data. Future work should explore source-level decoding with appropriate cross-validation to prevent information leakage.

Conclusion

While previous studies have substantially characterized the neural correlates of visual processing, our study demonstrates that ultra-high-density EEG provides quantitative and qualitative improvements that cannot be achieved with conventional EEG layouts. Specifically, the high spatial resolution of uHD EEG enhances decoding performance, spatial pattern clarity, and the precision of source localization.

Our classification analyses show a marked improvement in decoding accuracy with the full uHD setup compared to spatially reduced electrode subsets. This enhancement can be attributed to the increased number of electrodes, which provides more data and allows for a finer sampling of the scalp’s electrical field. These findings are consistent with our evaluation of classification accuracy as a function of electrode distance and electrode coverage area: performance increases more substantially with a higher number of electrodes and reduced electrode distance than with increases in electrode coverage area. This supports the argument that spatial density is critical in extracting discriminative features for neural decoding.

Moreover, the spatial patterns derived from the uHD EEG data exhibit more precise and defined topographies. Conventional montages often rely heavily on spatial interpolation between electrodes to estimate activity in unmeasured regions, leading to smoothed and potentially less accurate representations of neural topographies. In contrast, the dense spatial sampling of uHD EEG minimizes the reliance on interpolation, allowing us to detect sharper borders and finer-grained spatial features in the recorded data.

Importantly, these spatial improvements translate into superior source localization. In the analysis of the N170 component, a well-established marker of face processing, we observed that the uHD EEG significantly elevated the activation estimates in the fusiform gyrus compared to lower-density layouts. The increased signal power and spatial fidelity provided by the uHD setup contributed to more robust and anatomically precise source estimates. This finding highlights the potential of uHD EEG to advance source-level analyses of event-related potentials and to uncover subtle neural dynamics that may be obscured in conventional setups.

In summary, while the underlying visual processes examined in this study have been previously described in the literature, our work demonstrates that uHD EEG enhances these processes’ resolution, accuracy, and interpretability. The benefits observed in decoding accuracy, spatial pattern definition, and source localization collectively provide compelling evidence of the added value of uHD EEG for cognitive neuroscience research.

Future research should focus on low-level visual features, such as contrast, orientation, and spatial frequency, to better understand their role in early visual decoding and their contribution to higher-level cognitive processes. Expanding the range of stimuli to include dynamic and naturalistic inputs could further explore real-world visual processing. At the same time, advancements in individualized neural models and real-time decoding applications offer promising avenues for tailored insights. Moreover, studies involving diverse populations and pathologies could provide a more profound understanding of cortical hierarchies, enhancing the precision and translational potential of uHD EEG systems in neuroscience and clinical applications.

Subjects and experimental protocol

Four healthy male participants (S1, S2, S3, and S4) with an average age of 35 years (SD = 10 years) participated in the experiments without prior training. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and the EEG studies were approved by the ethics committee IRCCS Sicilia under approval number U148/19. To ensure optimal contact between the uHD EEG grids and the scalp, participants’ heads were shaved and cleaned with medical alcohol before the experiment. During the experiment, subjects sat approximately 1 meter from a computer screen (see Fig. 6a). Participants were instructed to focus on the screen and the content displayed.

Visual stimuli were presented on a screen connected to the acquisition computer via HDMI (Fig. 6b). The experiment was designed using the paradigm presenter application from g.HIsys Professional (g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria) in MATLAB/Simulink. Each visual stimulus was presented on a gray background for 0.75, 1, or 1.25 s, followed by a randomized inter-stimulus interval of 0.6, 0.8, or 1 s during which the gray screen remained visible. Each class had a pool of stimuli with the following counts: Face: 46, Body: 41, Pattern: 28, and Object: 57. The experiment was divided into ten runs, with short breaks between runs to allow participants to rest. Each run lasted approximately 3 min, resulting in 80 trials per run (4 × 20). In total, we collected 200 trials per class and subject.

A photosensor placed in the bottom right corner of the screen (Fig. 6a) detected a white square during stimulus presentation, which turned black during the inter-stimulus intervals. This luminance change triggered a TTL signal routed through the g.TRIGbox (g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria) (Fig. 6b) to generate precise trigger pulses. These pulses were looped back into the g.HIamp (Fig. 6b) to synchronize timing accurately across biosignal amplifiers.

For subjects S1 and S3, a software trigger recorded via USB was aligned with the hardware trigger by adjusting for a delay calculated from measurements on subjects S2 and S4. This adjustment ensured the accurate timing of the VEPs across all four subjects.

Ultra-high-density EEG system

The uHD EEG system (g.Pangolin, g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria) features an electrode distance of 8.6 mm and an electrode diameter of 5.9 mm (Fig. 6b). Flexible electrode grids were affixed to the subject’s scalp using medical adhesives, requiring head shaving before application. These adhesives have individual recesses for each electrode, which were filled with conductive Elefix gel (Nihon Kohden, Tokyo, Japan) to ensure optimal skin contact and minimal impedance.

The system recorded data from four subjects, using two biosignal amplifiers per subject for the simultaneous acquisition of 512 EEG channels (Fig. 6b). Each 16-channel electrode grid was connected to a pre-amplifier, which interfaced with the biosignal amplifiers via slim HDMI cables. To enhance the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), the pre-amplifiers were positioned directly at the recording sites on the electrode grids. EEG was recorded in a monopolar configuration, with the ground electrode placed on the mastoid using disposable Ag/AgCl electrodes (Kendall™ H124SG, Cardinal Health, Dublin, Ireland). Each biosignal amplifier sampled 256 channels at 512 Hz.

Electrode co-registration from MRI data

Topography plots were created, and source reconstruction was performed based on brain models generated from anatomical MRI scans of the four subjects. Brain and skull structures were reconstructed in FreeSurfer (Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Cambridge, MA, USA) using T1-weighted MRI data72. Each MRI scan was performed with a standard 10-20 cap that included MRI markers, allowing us to use MRI artifacts for accurate co-registration and electrode placement (see Fig. 6d). Electrode placement and co-registration were completed with custom montage software (g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria), resulting in a montage of 512 electrodes positioned above the visual cortex.

Evoked potentials (EPs) are often extracted using higher upper cutoff frequencies, as reported in literature (e.g., 100 Hz73, 200 Hz74). All filters used were zero-phase throughout the data processing, ensuring no phase shift was introduced into the signal and preserving the temporal integrity of the neural data.

Visual evoked potentials and topographies

The VEP plots were generated for each picture category and subject. Average plots were created by combining all trials within each picture category to analyze temporal dynamics. To convey spatial information, VEP traces were color-coded according to channel numbers. This color scheme is illustrated in Fig. 7, where the electrodes on the head are shown in the same colors as their corresponding traces in Fig. 7c. For a more detailed spatial analysis, topography plots were generated using methods described in previous studies36,76 with customized montage creator software.

Topographies were generated based on early and late VEP components post-stimulus (Fig. 7d). The timing was manually selected based on the peaks observed in the VEP. The topographies were interpolated and plotted onto the scalp surface of the reconstructed brain models for each subject. The averaged VEPs for each class, incorporating all artifact-free trials, were used to generate the topographic maps. For each bad channel, a simple weighted mean of up to four nearest good channels is calculated based on Euclidean distances, effectively restoring missing data while considering spatial relationships77.

Classification

For the stimuli classification task, VEP signals were processed, band-pass filtered from 1-20 Hz, and downsampled by a factor of 12, reducing the sampling frequency to approximately 42.67 Hz. The downsampling was done to reduce the feature count while preserving the majority of information in the VEPs, and the downsampling factor of 12 was chosen according to the Nyquist theorem. The VEPs were baseline-corrected using the pre-stimulus interval. This preprocessing was performed to ensure clean and interpretable data for visualization (e.g., topographic plots and signal traces) and classification (Fig. 7b).

The classification aimed to distinguish between four stimulus classes: faces, objects, body parts, and patterns, resulting in a 4-class classification problem for each subject. The feature set consisted of the product of the number of retained channels (after removing bad channels) and the number of time points in the downsampled signal from 0 to 500 ms post-stimulus. These features served as input to the classification models.

rLDA with an alpha of 0.75 was employed as the classifier (Fig. 7e). Single-trial classification was conducted using a 10-fold CV framework. Specifically, a 10×10 fold CV was implemented, involving 10 repetitions of 10-fold CV for each subject. The accuracy for each iteration was recorded, and the mean accuracy across all iterations was reported as the final performance metric.

To understand the temporal dynamics of discriminative power, we employed a temporal generalization method (TGM)43,78 and used the AUC as the performance metric. Note that a performance metric like the AUC is recommended when using the temporal generalization approach, as it is “criterion free”43. As previously described, these metrics were computed using the same rLDA classifier with an alpha parameter of 0.75. Note that for the TGM analyses, we kept the initial band-pass filter of 1–40 Hz to preserve temporal dynamics. A downsampling factor of 6 was chosen, resulting in a new sampling frequency of approximately 85.33 Hz. The classification strategy used to assess temporal behavior was the OvR method, where a separate classifier was trained for each class to distinguish it from all others, effectively breaking the problem into multiple binary classifications. Performance was evaluated based on the average accuracy and AUC across these classifiers. The classification task was split into six binary classification problems (one for each pair of stimulus classes) as an additional analysis. The decoding performance for these binary problems was averaged across all four subjects to generate additional plots.

To summarize the decoding performance over time, the diagonal of the temporal generalization matrix, where training and testing occurred at the same time, was extracted. This diagonal reflects time-resolved decoding performance. The AUC, which evaluates the classifier’s ability to distinguish between conditions, was used to quantify performance, and time-resolved plots were generated. The temporal generalization method was also performed using 4-class classification based on single-trials and 10 repetitions of a 10-fold CV. The resulting temporal generalization matrices reflect decoding performance over time, and classification accuracies for each time–time pair were reported to assess the dynamics of class separability.

Subsample classification

In an additional analysis, we investigated the impact of electrode density on classification performance. Specifically, the classification was repeated with subsets of electrodes corresponding to standard EEG configurations. These subsets resulted in feature sets with reduced dimensionality, as the number of channels used for classification was limited to the selected subsets. This reduction allowed us to evaluate the impact of lower spatial resolution on classification accuracy while maintaining the same temporal resolution, subjects, and even cross-validation seeds, as the same 10×10CV framework was employed.

Electrodes were selected to align with the 10-10 system (highlighted in red), the extended 10-10 system (highlighted in red and blue), and the 10-05 system42 (doubled density of red and blue), as derived from the uHD EEG sensor arrangement (Fig. 6c). For each standard EEG electrode (e.g., Oz), the closest uHD EEG electrode, determined by the smallest Euclidean distance, was used as an approximation. Importantly, standard EEG electrodes without a neighboring uHD electrode within 10 mm were not approximated as they fall outside the covered area. Accuracy represents the dependent variable, and Layout is a fixed effect representing the respective subsampled electrode configurations (10-20, 10-10, e10-10, 10-05, uHD EEG). Classification accuracy as a function of electrode distance and electrode coverage area

We investigated how electrode spacing and coverage area influence classification accuracy by creating various subsets of electrodes and adjusting the sensor area. Each subset underwent preprocessing, feature extraction, and classification (4-class classification in a 10 × 10 CV framework) to evaluate performance. The subsets were based on the 10-10 electrode system and varied in spacing and covered area. We increased the number of sensors to explore electrode spacing while keeping them evenly distributed. For example, in subject S1, the 10-10 system started with 19 electrodes. We then progressively doubled, tripled, and quadrupled the number of electrodes until we reached the complete uHD EEG system.

For the area analysis, we expanded the covered surface on the scalp by averaging neighboring electrodes by adding one, two, three, etc., nearest neighbors. Specifically, for a given number of neighboring electrodes, e.g., n = 2, this would result in the signal of a given uHD EEG electrode and its two nearest neighbors being averaged. The starting electrodes were again the 10-10 system locations, and nearest neighbors were determined using the Euclidean distance. This approach allowed us to compare our results meaningfully with standard EEG systems, as the scalp area covered by electrodes plays an important role. For reference, a single uHD EEG electrode covers 27.3 mm² of the scalp, while a traditional g.SCARABEO electrode (from g.tec medical engineering GmbH, Austria) covers 113 mm², approximately four times the area of a uHD EEG electrode.

The function represents y as the accuracy, x as the distance/area, β1 as the intercept, β2 as the linear coefficient, and β3 as the reciprocal coefficient. This function was chosen based on the assumption that decreasing electrode distance initially leads to increased accuracy, as the occipital lobe is sampled at greater spatial resolution. However, excessively low electrode distance may lead to redundant channels and thus a plateau or even drop in decoding performance. Source localization

We performed source localization from four subjects and four stimulus classes using the ultra-high-density EEG and Python MNE toolbox30 (Fig. 7f). Raw data from each subject was loaded, divided into four classes, and converted from microvolts to volts. Channels were labeled numerically and grouped per class, with raw data visualizations and a 1–200 Hz band-pass filter applied. Data was segmented into 1.2-second epochs (from −0.1 s to 1.1 s) to capture responses across visual classes. Events were generated for each class, and epochs were visually inspected for separation. The epochs were re-referenced to the average reference to standardize the signal across channels. As explained earlier, channel positions were loaded from coordinate files extracted from our custom montage creator for plotting the topographies. The sensor coordinates were converted to meters and assigned synthetic labels. A custom montage was created and visualized to ensure the correct placement of electrodes on the MRI. Subject anatomical data from FreeSurfer79 were accessed to generate a source space using a 6-mm grid (spacing = ‘oct6’) and a BEM model with standard conductivities (0.3, 0.006, 0.3 for brain, skull, and scalp). BEM solution and the head-MRI transformation file were used to align EEG and MRI data precisely.

Forward solutions were computed for each class, and sLORETA-based80 inverse operators were applied. Noise covariance was approximated using ad hoc covariance models. Source localization was performed using an SNR of 3.0, yielding lambda squared (λ²) regularization for the sLORETA application. We visualized the activation in arbitrary units from source localization, calculated as the absolute magnitude of current density estimates (amplitude) at each source location, normalized to account for depth bias and spatial resolution.

Source estimates were visualized on the brain’s surface model to illustrate the spatiotemporal dynamics of activation patterns. Images were generated at 10 ms intervals, offering a detailed view of the cortical activation elicited by each stimulus class. These visualizations highlighted the progression of cortical activation over time for each condition. Single frames can be compiled into videos to facilitate comparison with topographical scalp activation maps and corresponding VEPs.

Subsampling of electrodes for topographies and source localization

To evaluate the benefits of uHD EEG for topographical mapping and source localization, we compared results obtained using the complete uHD montage with those from spatially downsampled subsets. Electrodes were selected to align with established standards: the 10-20 system, the 10-10 system, the extended 10-10 system, and the 10-05 system. As mentioned in the Subsample Classification methods section, these subsets were derived from the original uHD EEG sensor arrangement (Fig. 7c). Topographies and corresponding VEPs were generated using these subsampled layouts, and a linear mixed model was employed to determine whether differences in classification accuracies were statistically significant. The same selected electrodes were also used for subsample-based source localization, which involved re-running the entire source localization pipeline. The boundary element model (BEM) and head-MRI transformation files were applied to ensure accurate EEG-MRI co-registration across all electrode configurations.
