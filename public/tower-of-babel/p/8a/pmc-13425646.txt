‘Protected Percutaneous Coronary Intervention’: A High-risk Proposition
Authors: Salman S Allana, Ki Park
Published: 2026

In March 2026, the highly anticipated clinical trial, The Controlled Trial of High-Risk Coronary Interventions with Percutaneous Left Ventricular Unloading (CHIP-BCIS3), was presented at the American College of Cardiology Scientific Session in New Orleans by Divaka Perera.1

Complex percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in patients with left ventricular dysfunction is high risk and can be associated with prolonged ischemia from coronary lesion modification leading to hemodynamic compromise, arrhythmias, MI, and, potentially, cardiogenic shock. Microaxial left ventricular flow pumps (such as Impella CP) can potentially diminish these risks by unloading the left ventricle via afterload reduction, increasing coronary and end-organ perfusion and providing hemodynamic support.2 Elective use of mechanical circulatory support (MCS) devices, such as Impella CP, during these high-risk interventions has been referred to as ‘protected PCI’.

Observational-only studies demonstrate improved procedural cardiovascular hemodynamics and sometimes more complete revascularization with mechanical circulatory support (MCS) use, despite higher-risk patient profiles.3 Use of these devices, however, has been associated with higher risk of bleeding and vascular complications and the risk–benefit ratio remains debated.4

There has been a paucity of randomized trials to support the ‘protected PCI’ approach. Previously, the use of intra-aortic balloon pump in high-risk PCI showed no significant difference in major adverse events compared with a standard approach.5 The PROTECT II randomized controlled trial that was initiated to compare intra-aortic balloon pump with Impella 2.5 in patients undergoing high-risk PCI was stopped early due to futility.6

The CHIP-BCIS3 trial sheds light on this important subject. The trial included 300 patients with left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤35% undergoing complex PCI.1 The patients were randomized to the elective upfront periprocedural use of Impella CP, a microaxial flow pump or to standard care. The primary outcome was a composite of death, stroke, spontaneous MI, cardiovascular hospitalization or periprocedural myocardial injury, with results of all analyses reported as a win ratio. At a median follow-up of 22 months, 36.6% of pairwise comparisons favored Impella CP use, and 43% favored standard care with a win ratio that was not statistically significant. Overall, Impella CP use for high-risk PCI did not reduce the incidence of the primary outcome. Secondary analysis revealed a trend towards higher risk of death and periprocedural MI among the pump recipients and a significantly higher risk of death from cardiovascular causes. In addition, the bleeding and vascular complications were numerically more frequent in the pump recipients.

These findings are eye-opening because the use of Impella CP for high-risk PCI has increased considerably in the past several years, especially after the Food and Drug Administration approved of the device for ‘protected PCI’, even for mildly reduced LVEF.7 This study supports that routine use of this strategy is not beneficial. Moreover, it is paradoxical that the rates of periprocedural MI were higher in the Impella CP group, as this is an outcome interventionalists hope to reduce with this strategy.

Skeptics may ask some appropriately important questions. Does the trial apply to patients with cardiogenic shock? Was the coronary artery disease really complex? Was there significant crossover between the two arms? Was there a difference in the rates of complete revascularization between the two groups? Was complete revascularization performed in the index procedure or staged?

The trial excluded patients with ST-elevation MI and cardiogenic shock. The patient population was high risk, with median LVEF of 27% and coronary artery disease of high complexity. The median Syntax score was 38 and median British Cardiovascular Intervention Society jeopardy score was 12. Left main intervention and use of calcium modification was intended in more than two-thirds of the population and retrograde chronic total occlusion PCI intended in approximately 27% of the patients. None of the patients in the standard therapy arm underwent elective insertion of the Impella CP device.

Bailout MCS was required in only nine patients in the standard group, eight of whom received intra-aortic balloon pump, thus reassuring that if Impella CP is not used upfront, bailout MCS predominantly intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) use is an acceptable strategy in most patients. Both groups achieved similar rates of complete revascularization with a median coronary revascularization index of 67% in both arms and a residual Syntax score of 13–14. Importantly, higher number of patients in the standard-care arm underwent planned staged PCI (17.8%) as compared to the Impella CP arm (6.8%).

Overall, the CHIP-BCIS3 trial cautions against the routine use of elective Impella CP placement for high-risk PCI. Traditionally, interventional cardiologists have used the device to potentially mitigate the risks associated with high-risk PCI and thus tolerate the complications associated with the device. This appears to be a high-risk proposition. That said, most interventional cardiologists would attest to having patients who could not have tolerated high-risk PCI without Impella CP support. There is potential heterogeneity of risks among patients undergoing high-risk PCI. The LVEF and coronary artery disease complexity are just two factors that contribute to but do not completely account for the risks. Does the LVEF need to be much lower than the study population to derive a benefit from elective Impella CP for high-risk PCI? We, as a community, need to identify these risk factors, perform future studies to examine them, and possibly develop risk scores to help recognize the patients who may benefit from this strategy. Until then, high-risk PCI without Impella CP support and more staged procedures should be the default strategy with periprocedural use of mechanical circulatory support reserved for patients who develop periprocedural hemodynamic decompensation or rising cardiac filling pressures. Perhaps, there is a stronger role of continuous hemodynamic monitoring during high-risk PCI. Further studies are needed to address this. The results of the PROTECT IV clinical trial (NCT04763200), which will enroll more than 1,200 patients, are eagerly awaited and will shed more light on this subject.
