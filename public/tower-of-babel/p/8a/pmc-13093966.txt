Hsp70s regulate circadian rhythm by interacting with PERIOD
Authors: Xianguo Zhao, Pengfei Lv, Yuansheng Huang, Yifan Zhang, Xiaoyu Wu, Xingzhuo Yang, Juan Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCircadian rhythms synchronize life with the environment. Although the molecular feedback loop underlying these rhythms has been well-characterized, our understanding of cellular clocks remains incomplete. In this study, by applying cellular and biochemistry approaches, we reveal the involvement of Hsp70 family proteins in regulating the core clock feedback loop by modulating the condensation formation of the PERIOD (PER) protein. Mutations in Hsp70s lead to circadian rhythm defects in a temperature-dependent manner. Further mechanistic investigation uncovered that Hsp70s compete with TIM for binding to PER. Notably, Hsp70s are essential for reducing PER liquid condensates. By generating a complete null allele of per, we demonstrated that per functions downstream of Hsp70s. Our findings on the role of Hsp70s in regulating PER provide new insights into the cellular mechanisms governing circadian rhythms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02805-3.Keywords: Circadian rhythm, Condensation formation, Hsp70, Period

Background

Circadian rhythms are a universal feature of life, ensuring that organisms’ physiology remains synchronized with the environment, thereby enhancing their adaptability to their habitats. In Drosophila, circadian rhythms are controlled by clock neurons through core clock genes. The core clock gene regulatory loop includes period (per), timeless (tim), Clock (Clk), and cycle (cyc) [1]. Among these, PERIOD (PER) is one of the most critical regulators of circadian rhythms. Discovered in a screen for circadian regulators in Dr. Benzer’s lab in 1971, the per01, perS, and perL mutants have significantly advanced our understanding of circadian regulatory mechanisms [2]. While phosphorylation and nuclear localization of PER partially explain these mutant phenotypes [1], identifying new regulatory factors is essential for a deeper understanding of the circadian regulatory loop. Although mouse PER2 has been reported to lack phase separation [3], Drosophila PER exhibits phase separation in S2 cells [4]. Previous studies have shown that Drosophila clock proteins (PER and CLK) form discrete foci at the nuclear envelope during circadian repression, playing a key role in the subnuclear localization of core clock genes to regulate circadian rhythms [5]. However, it remains unclear whether and how phase separation regulates Drosophila circadian rhythms in vivo.

Phase separation is a dynamic process in which proteins undergo changes in response to various conditions, such as heat. For example, temperature rapidly shifts ELF3 between active and inactive states via phase separation in plants [6]. In Arabidopsis, liquid-liquid phase separation of RBGD2/4 is required for heat stress resistance [7]. Similarly, in human cells, reversible phase separation of HSF1 is necessary for an acute transcriptional response during heat shock [8]. These findings raise the intriguing question of whether and how phase separation functions in the regulation of circadian rhythms, particularly under temperature fluctuations.

Heat shock proteins (Hsps) are a family of proteins whose expression is induced by elevated temperatures and other stress conditions. Hsps are well-known for their roles in temperature tolerance [9] and protein maturation [10]. Further studies have demonstrated that Hsps play a critical role in maintaining protein homeostasis by facilitating protein disaggregation and phase separation [11–13]. Notably, circadian rhythms in heat shock gene expression have been observed in prokaryotic species (Synechocystis) and plants [14, 15]. For instance, Hsp83 is necessary for maintaining rhythmicity under free-running conditions after entrainment by both light and temperature cycles [16]. However, it remains unclear whether and how Hsps directly interact with core circadian rhythm components. In this study, we investigate the role of Hsp70 family proteins in maintaining the core clock regulatory loop by regulating PER condensation formation.

Methods

Fly strains and rearing

Fly strains were reared on standard yeast-containing fly food and maintained in an incubator under a 12:12 h light-dark cycle at a constant temperature of 25 °C and 60% humidity. The fly lines used in this study include: w1118 (BDSC:5905), hsp70A-,hsp70Ba304 (BDSC:8844) and hsp70A-,hsp70B- (BDSC:8841) was obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC). The per01 strain was provided by Dr. Herman Wijnen’s lab (University of Southampton, UK). perL and perS were provided by Dr. Francois Rouyer’s lab (Université Paris-Saclay, France). The tim01 was generously provided by Dr. Michael Rosbash’s lab (Brandeis University, USA). The perCKO was generated in this study using Crispr lines for mutant generation (BDSC: 83099 and 51324).

w1118 is a genetic background control. Its function is to ensure that the observed phenotypes are not caused by other unknown genetic differences, but are indeed due to the experimental manipulation.

The hsp70A-,hsp70B- mutant is a compound deficiency mutant constructed in the standard genetic background strain w1118 using ends-outgene targeting. It incorporates two independent deletions: Df(3R)Hsp70A, which removes the Hsp70Aa and Hsp70Ab genes at cytogenetic region 87A2-87A3, and Df(3R)Hsp70B, which deletes the Hsp70Ba, Hsp70Bbb, Hsp70Bb, and Hsp70Bc genes at region 87B12-87B14/15. Both deletions are marked by replacement with a miniwhite gene serving as a visible reporter. In the homozygous state, this mutant completely lacks six key Hsp70 genes, leading to a severely attenuated classical heat shock response. The well-defined w1118 background minimizes confounding effects from unrelated genetic variations. This strain serves as a classic genetic tool for studying Hsp70 gene function, heat stress tolerance, and proteostasis mechanisms.

Assays for circadian rhythm detection and data analysis

To detect and analyze circadian rhythms, locomotor activity recordings were conducted using previously described methods [17–19]. Briefly, flies approximately 3 days old were placed in glass tubes containing fly food on one end. The tubes were sealed with cotton wool plugs and positioned within Drosophila Activity Monitors (DAM system, TriKinetics, Waltham, MA) to record locomotor activity throughout the experiment. Environmental conditions were carefully controlled using light- and temperature-regulated incubators. The light-dark and temperature cycles were programmed to follow consistent 12:12 h rectangular cycles.

Activity data were recorded every minute. Based on these data, total activity counts per 30-minute intervals and actograms of locomotor activity were generated using the Fly Toolbox in MATLAB software. Daily average histograms were created using FaasX software (Fly Activity Analysis Suite for MacOS X), generously provided by Dr. Francois Rouyer (Université Paris-Saclay, France). Periods were calculated for each individual fly using X2 periodogram analysis and then pooled to obtain a group average for each genotype. Flies with a power of 10 or higher and a “width” value of 1.5 or greater (indicating the number of peaks above the periodogram’s 95% confidence line in 30-minute increments) were considered rhythmic.

To evaluate light-mediated phase delays in circadian behavior, flies were first entrained for 3 days under 12-hour light:12-hour dark cycles (~ 3500 lx LED white light). To induce an 8-hour phase delay, the light phase was extended to 20 h on day 4, followed by a return to the normal 12-hour dark:12-hour light cycles. The magnitude of the phase shift was calculated as the difference between the evening peak on the day preceding the phase shift and the corresponding peak on the indicated day after the phase shift.

To evaluate temperature-mediated circadian behavior in Fig. 6, flies were first entrained for 3 days under 12-hour light:12-hour dark cycles. A 1-hour heat shock (35 °C) was administered to the flies beginning at ZT12 on day 4. Following this treatment, the temperature was restored to 25 °C. The flies were subsequently maintained under constant darkness (DD) for a period of six days, and the robustness of circadian rhythms was quantified as the percentage of rhythmic individuals per genotype.

Plasmids construction

Plasmids were constructed using the standard ClonExpress technique (Vazyme, #C115). cDNAs, including per, perS, perL, per1-252, per233-464, per465-842, per843-1218, dbt, GFP, and Hsp70Ba, were individually subcloned into the pAC-4V5 plasmid. Additionally, cDNAs for Hsp70Ba, Hsp83, and Tim were cloned into the pAC-3HA plasmid. cDNAs for per was cloned into the pAC-FLAG plasmid. A truncated version of the PER protein, which contains deletions in the regions responsible for phase separation (amino acids 409–430, 878–912, 998–1019), was subcloned into the pAC-4V5 plasmid. The pAC-4V5 plasmid was used as blank control in relevant experiments.

Cell culture, transfection, drug treatment, heat shock treatment, and immunostaining

S2 cells were cultured in serum-free insect cell culture medium (HyClone, #SH30278.02). Transfection was carried out using the X-tremeGENE™ HP DNA Transfection Reagent kit (Roche, #6366236001) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Experiments involving drug and heat shock treatments were conducted 48 h after transfection. For drug treatment, 100 µM of the Hsp70 inhibitor VER-155,008 (Target Mol, #T7010) was dissolved in the media. For heat shock treatment, cells were exposed to 38 °C for 30 min, followed by a recovery period at 25 °C for 4 h.

For immunostaining, S2 cells were adhered to coverslips via ConA treatment. The adherent cells were washed in 1× PBS, fixed for 15 min with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature, washed three times with 1× PBS, and then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After permeabilization, cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated for 1 h with a blocking solution consisting of 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at room temperature, prior to antibody incubation. Fixed cells were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature with rabbit anti-V5 antibody (Abcam, #ab9116) at a dilution of 1:500. To study the co-localization of X-4V5 (where X represents per, perS, or perL) heat-induced SGs with X-3HA, a mix of rabbit anti-V5 antibody (1:500 dilution, Abcam, #ab9116) and mouse anti-HA antibody (1:200 dilution, Cell Signaling Technology, #2367) was used, and cells were incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature before detection. The secondary antibodies were detected using goat anti-rabbit FITC (1:150 dilution, ABclonal, #AS011) and goat anti-mouse Alexa 568 (1:200 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A1104). Imaging was performed using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope, with either a 20x or a 63x oil objective.

Experiments accessing the properties of PER condensates

Fluorescence Recovery After Photobleaching (FRAP) analysis is commonly used to study protein condensates [20–22]. After photobleaching, the Per-GFP signal quickly recovered, indicating that the Per protein had high mobility and freely interacted with its cytoplasmic pool under normal physiological conditions. For half-FRAP experiments, Per-GFP in Drosophila S2 cells was analyzed using a Zeiss LSM 800 system with a 488 nm laser. The laser power for bleaching was set to 2–5% [22]. In this study, S2 cells were transfected with pAC-Per-GFP plasmids. Forty-eight hours after transfection, the cells were plated in 35 mm culture dishes with 1×PBS and incubated at 38 °C for 30 min before imaging. Samples (50 µL) were placed onto 4-well chambered LabTek coverslips pre-treated with 15% PEG, thoroughly rinsed, and the laser power for bleaching was adjusted to 4% in the regions of interest. Before photobleaching, two images were captured. The bleaching parameters (laser intensity and scan time) were set to achieve approximately 95% bleaching in the shortest time possible. For half-FRAP analysis, which involves both the bleached and non-bleached halves, it is essential to optimize the bleaching step (intensity and bleaching area) to reduce any unintended bleaching of the non-bleached portion. Half-FRAP experiments continued until the signals in both halves either converged or the signal in the non-bleached half returned to its pre-bleach value. Images were taken 38 cycles after photobleaching using a 63× oil objective.

When S2 cells expressing PER-GFP were treated with 1,6-hexanediol—a compound known to disrupt liquid-like condensates, unlike the control 2,5-hexanediol—a rapid dispersion of protein condensates was observed [22]. Prior to seeding, coverslips were washed with 70% ethanol and allowed to dry. After 48 h of transfection, the S2 cells were treated with 1×PBS, 10% 1,6-hexanediol (10% 1,6-HD), or 10% 2,5-hexanediol (10% 2,5-HD) for 3 min at room temperature. The cells were then fixed immediately with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 20 min at room temperature, washed with PBST, stained with DAPI, and mounted using an antifade reagent (Solarbio). Slides were imaged with a Leica SP8 confocal microscope using a 63× oil immersion objective.

For in vitro droplet fusion and fission experiments of PER-GFP condensates, live cell imaging was performed on cells expressing PER-GFP. Living cells were observed using a Zeiss LSM 800 confocal microscope equipped with a 63× oil immersion objective and a live cell chamber. Forty-eight hours after transfection, S2 cells were plated in a 1.5 mL tube with 1×PBS and incubated for 30 min at 38 °C before imaging. The media was replaced with fresh imaging media, and the cells were imaged for 15 min, with one frame captured every 30 s.

For the in vitro phase separation and protein buffering assay, purified PER protein was mixed with 100 mM NaCl in a phase separation buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5) and 5%–10% (w/v) PEG 8000 (Sigma). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 1 min. In the protein buffering assay, PER protein was incubated with HSP70Ba protein in the same phase separation buffer, also containing 100 mM NaCl, as shown in Fig. 2L. Finally, 5 µL of each sample was pipetted onto a coverslip and imaged using a Leica SP8 microscope with a 63× oil immersion objective and differential interference contrast (DIC). The PER and HSP70Ba proteins were extracted from S2 cells expressing PER and HSP70Ba using Protein A/G Beads 4HF (EASYBIO, #BE6868), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Drosophila brain dissection and immunostainings

Adult flies were entrained for 3 days under light-dark (LD) cycles at 25 °C. Samples were collected at the indicated time points, as described in the figure legends. Drosophila heads were dissected in PBS after fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 2–3 h at room temperature. After rinsing three times in PBS with 0.5% Triton X-100 (PBST), the brains were blocked in PNT (10% goat serum and 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 2 h at room temperature. Following blocking, the brains were incubated overnight with primary antibodies at 4 °C with gentle rotation. The primary antibodies used were rabbit anti-Per (1:5000 dilution, a gift from Dr. Jeffrey Price’s lab at the University of Missouri-Kansas City, USA) and mouse anti-Lamin C (1:500 dilution, Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, #LC28.26). The mouse anti-dFmr1 antibody (Anti-Drosophila Fmr1 antibody 6A15, #ab10299) was kindly provided by Dr. Yongqing Zhang’s lab at the Institute of Biophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The samples were then washed three times with PBST before incubation with the appropriate secondary antibody for 3 h at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were goat anti-rabbit-FITC (1:150 dilution, ABclonal, #AS011) and goat anti-mouse-Alexa 568 (1:200 dilution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, #A1104). After secondary antibody incubation, the samples were washed three times with PBST at room temperature. Finally, the brain samples were mounted on glass slides with an antifade mounting medium (Solarbio, #S2110). Fluorescently labeled brains were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 63x oil objective. Images were acquired as a z-stack and processed using Adobe Photoshop CS6. To quantify the number of PER foci, ImageJ was employed to count PER foci within the same focal plane of s-LNvs.

The treatment of fly brains with 1,6-Hexanediol (1,6-HD) followed a standard protocol. Fly heads were dissected in 1× PBS. The brains were then treated with either 10% 1,6-HD or 10% 2,5-hexanediol (2,5-HD) in 1× PBS for 5 min at room temperature. Cell/tissue lysis, immunoprecipitation and Western blot

For the immunoprecipitation assay, S2 cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection, washed, and resuspended in 150 µL of RIPA Lysis Buffer (strong) (CWBIO, #CW2333S). The samples were incubated on ice for 20 min to promote cell lysis. Afterward, the cell extracts were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for further analysis. This supernatant was incubated with anti-V5/anti-HA antibodies (ABclonal) for 4 h at 4 °C with rotation. Protein A/G plus beads (EASYBIO, #BE6868), which were washed three times in PBS, were then added to the lysate/antibody mixture and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were separated from the liquid by centrifugation at 4 °C, and the beads were thoroughly washed. Proteins were recovered directly in SDS-PAGE sample buffer. For Western blot analysis, mouse anti-V5 (ABclonal, #AE017) and mouse anti-HA (ABclonal, #AE008) antibodies were used. The “Control” in figures denotes the expression plasmid backbone (pAC) used to generate the stable cell lines.

For immunoprecipitation using Drosophila heads, at least 500 fly heads (w1118 and tim01) were homogenized and lysed in 500 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1.0% NP-40, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0), supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The lysates were incubated on ice for 15 min and then centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered using a cell strainer and then further centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. Approximately 50 µL of the supernatant was saved as the input. To the remaining supernatant, 5 µg of PER antibody was added and incubated at 4 °C with rotation. After 4 h, 100 µL of protein G plus bead slurry (Invitrogen, Cat#88848) was added and incubated for at least 12 h at 4 °C. The beads were separated from the liquid using a magnetic rack and washed three times by rotating the beads with 500 µL of lysis buffer at 4 °C for 5 min each. Finally, the samples were used for Western blot. The following antibodies were used: anti-HSP70 (ABclonal, Cat# A12948, 1:1000), anti-PER (a gift from Dr. Jeffrey Price’s laboratory at the University of Missouri-Kansas City, U.S.; 1:5000), and anti-β-Tubulin (ABclonal, Cat# AC021, 1:2000).

Briefly, 25–30 adult flies were collected at the indicated time points as described in the figure legends and text. Fly heads from each genotype/condition were homogenized and lysed in RIPA Lysis Buffer (strong) (CWBIO, #CW2333S), supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The antibodies used were anti-PER (1:5000 dilution, a gift from Dr. Jeffrey Price’s laboratory) and anti-Actin (1:2000 dilution, ABclonal, #AC026).

Structural modeling and binding affinity calculation of protein complexes

The full-length amino acid sequences of PER, HSP70Ba, and TIM were retrieved from the NCBI Protein database. The 3D structures of the protein complexes were predicted using the AlphaFold Server [25]. For each interacting pair, five models were generated. We selected the top-ranked model (Top-1) for downstream analysis based on the highest confidence scores, specifically the Interface Predicted Template Modeling score and the predicted Local Distance Difference Test. To ensure structural feasibility, the raw AlphaFold models were refined using the AMBER 22 software package [26]. The tleap module was employed to add missing hydrogen atoms and generate topology files under the ff14SB force field [27]. Subsequently, the complexes were subjected to energy minimization to relieve steric clashes and optimize local geometry. The minimization protocol consisted of 2500 steps of steepest descent followed by 2500 steps of conjugate gradient algorithms, with a convergence criterion of a root-mean-square (RMS) gradient less than 0.1 kcal/mol·Å. Finally, the binding affinity and binding free energy of the energy-minimized complexes were calculated using the PRODIGY web server [28].

Results

Mutations in Hsp70s result in temperature-dependent defects in circadian rhythm

To investigate the role of Hsp70s in circadian rhythm regulation, we first examined the circadian phenotype of Hsp70A−, Hsp70B− mutants, which lack functional Hsp70 family proteins. The results showed that, in female flies, the percentage of rhythmic flies was significantly lower in the mutants compared to the w1118 control group under constant dark (DD) conditions, after 3 days of light/dark (LD) entrainment (12 h of light followed by 12 h of dark) (Fig. 1A). Notably, the rhythmicity defects were temperature-dependent, with the phenotype being much more severe at higher temperatures (Fig. 1A). In male flies, the mutant allele showed a weaker phenotype compared to females (Table S1, Fig. S1A-C). Based on these observations, we hypothesize that variations in Hsp70s may mediate the effects of both light and temperature on circadian rhythm.

To test this hypothesis, we conducted temperature entrainment and phase delay experiments. In the temperature entrainment experiment, flies were kept in an all-dark incubator and exposed to a 12-hour 29 °C/12-hour 18 °C temperature cycle for 6 days, followed by a stable 25 °C condition. Measurements of circadian parameters revealed that the Hsp70s mutants had compromised temperature synchronization, as evidenced by a significantly lower percentage of rhythmic flies after temperature entrainment in the dark (Fig. 1B). In male flies, the mutant allele showed a weaker phenotype compared to females (Table S2, Fig. S1D). In the phase delay experiments, we delayed the phase by eight hours for both the Hsp70A−, Hsp70B−, and control groups. The results showed a significant decrease in phase shift (hours) in the Hsp70s mutants (Fig. 1C-E). These findings emphasize the necessity of Hsp70s for both temperature and light entrainment.

In conclusion, these results collectively suggest that Hsp70s are required for maintaining proper circadian rhythm.

Hsp70s regulate the liquid phase separation of PER

To determine the mechanism of Hsp70s in circadian rhythm regulation, we examined clock gene expression via immunostaining in Hsp70 mutants. We observed PER condensate formation in s-LNvs clock neurons dissected from Drosophila brains of w1118 and Hsp70 mutant flies (Fig. 1F–K, Fig. S1E). This phenomenon was not observed in l-LNvs clock neurons (Fig. S1F). Both s-LNvs and l-LNvs are clusters of clock neuron which localized in Drosophila brain. These results suggest that PER protein exists in distinct phases under normal conditions.

To further investigate endogenous PER protein phase separation, we examined tissues with low endogenous Hsp70s expression. Previous studies have shown that the testis exhibits lower levels of endogenous Hsp70s [29]. Through immunostaining of PER in w1118, we found that PER formed condensates in specific cell types within the testis (Fig. S2A–C). Specifically, PER formed small condensates during early spermatogenesis (Fig. S2A’–C’). Fewer condensates were observed in per01 compared to w1118, confirming the presence of endogenous PER condensates in these tissues (Fig. S2D–F’). These findings indicate that, under normal conditions, PER protein exists in different phases in tissues with low Hsp70s expression.

To further explore the role of Hsp70s in PER condensate formation, we investigated the cellular localization of Hsp70s and PER in S2 cells, which do not produce endogenous PER [30]. By transfecting S2 cells with clock proteins under the control of the actin promoter, we found that only PER formed condensates upon heat shock, but GFP, CLK, TIM and CYC did not show the condensation formation (Fig. S3A–F). Co-transfection and colocalization analysis revealed partial colocalization between Hsp70s and PER in these condensates (Fig. S3G–J). Based on previous literature [31–33], we infer that these condensates are stress granules. This was confirmed by colocalization with Fmr1, a known stress granule marker [34] (Fig. S3K). Immunostaining in clock neurons showed no colocalization between Fmr1 and PER (Fig. S3L), indicating that PER exhibits stress granule properties in S2 cells under heat shock conditions, distinct from its behavior in endogenous settings.

Inhibition of Hsp70 family proteins (Hsp68 and Hsp70Ba) led to increased PER condensate formation (Fig. S3O–P). Heat shock promoted PER condensate formation regardless of Hsp70 inhibition (Fig. S3O–P). However, these condensates dissolved after a 4-hour recovery period following heat shock (Fig. S3O–P). In contrast, inhibiting Hsp70s prevented PER from recovering from condensate formation (Fig. S3O–P), demonstrating that Hsp70s are required for PER recovering under heat shock conditions.

Bioinformatic predictions based on the PER protein sequence support the hypothesis that PER undergoes liquid-liquid phase separation (LLPS). We used the PScore predictor [34] to calculate the likelihood of phase separation, where a higher score indicates a greater probability of LLPS. Typically, a PScore > 4 is considered high [35]. The overall PScore for PER was 8.21, suggesting a strong propensity for LLPS. Additionally, using PSPhunter [36], we identified potential LLPS-driving sequences in PER, located at amino acids 410–430, 878–912, and 998–1019 (Fig. S3M). To validate this prediction, we examined the cellular localization of PER with these amino acid regions deleted. The results showed that condensates no longer formed (Fig. S3N), suggesting that Hsp70 regulates PER LLPS.

We conducted photobleaching experiments to assess the properties of PER condensates [37, 38]. The results suggested that PER condensates undergo LLPS under both normal and heat shock conditions (Fig. 2A–D), recovering within 300 s after photobleaching. The compound 1,6-hexanediol (1,6-HD), which specifically disrupts liquid-like condensates, significantly reduced heat shock-induced PER condensates compared to the control compound 2,5-hexanediol (2,5-HD) [22] (Fig. 2E, F). Similarly, PER condensates in s-LNvs clock neurons from Drosophila brains with the Hsp70A−, Hsp70Ba304 mutation were sensitive to 1,6-HD treatment, significantly reducing endogenous PER condensates compared to 2,5-HD treatment (Fig. 2G–I). Fission-fusion experiments, which assess the mobility of condensates, demonstrated dynamic changes in PER condensates over time (Fig. 2J, K). In vitro experiments further confirmed that PER forms condensates, whose abundance is modulated by Hsp70 proteins (Fig. 2L-M). Collectively, these findings suggest that PER undergoes LLPS.

In conclusion, Hsp70 facilitates the release of PER from liquid condensates. Loss of Hsp70s leads to increased PER condensate formation, resulting in the retention of PER in the cytoplasm.

Hsp70 proteins interact with the PER protein to facilitate its nuclear entry

To investigate how Hsp70s regulate the condensation formation of PER, we examined the expression and localization of PER at different time points, which are crucial for regulating the circadian rhythm. Real-time qPCR showed no significant changes in per expression in Hsp70A−,Hsp70Ba304 mutants except at ZT18 (Fig. 3A). Western blot analysis revealed reduced levels of PER protein in the Hsp70A−,Hsp70Ba304 mutants, with specificity of the PER antibody confirmed in per01 (Fig. 3B-D). When investigating the cellular localization of PER at ZT22, we observed that in Hsp70A−,Hsp70Ba304 mutants, PER predominantly localized in the cytoplasm rather than the nucleus in both s-LNvs and l-LNvs (Fig. 3E, S1G). These results suggest that mutations in Hsp70s stabilize and facilitate the nuclear localization of PER in vivo.

It is well known that the formation of the PER/TIM complex is critical for their translocation to the nucleus. To examine whether Hsp70s affect the formation of this complex, we performed immunoprecipitation experiments to test the interaction between Hsp70s, TIM, and PER. These experiments were conducted by transfecting gene expression plasmids into S2 cells. In S2 cells transfected with tagged TIM, PER, and Hsp70Ba, both TIM and Hsp70Ba were detected in the immunoprecipitated PER products (Fig. 3F). This result indicates that both TIM and Hsp70Ba interact with PER. Furthermore, we found that Hsp70Ba competes with TIM for binding to PER. A comparison of transfected cells with and without Hsp70Ba showed that the presence of Hsp70Ba weakened the interaction between PER and TIM (Fig. 3F, G). Additionally, when cells were subjected to heat shock, the interaction between TIM and PER was less pronounced in cells without co-transfected Hsp70Ba (Fig. 3F, G). Precise mapping using the PER fragments results in the TIM binding fragments in PER (Fig. S4A). This suggests that the increase in endogenous Hsp70s following heat shock treatment may compromise the interaction between PER and TIM. Furthermore, the immunoprecipitation experiments showed that TIM did not interact with Hsp70Ba in cytoplasm (Fig. S4B, S5A).

In vivo experiments provided consistent results. We found that in Tim01 mutants, compared to controls, the interaction between PER and Hsp70 was significantly stronger, as detected by immunoprecipitation in head tissues (Fig. 3H, I). These findings suggest that Hsp70 plays a role in regulating the abundance of PER.

We next investigated the relationship between PER condensates and the key circadian kinase DBT. Interestingly, we found that DBT exhibited strong co-localization with both PER condensates and, to a lesser extent, with Hsp70Ba (Fig. S5B, C). The recruitment of DBT to PER condensates suggests that liquid-liquid phase separation may serve as a mechanism to concentrate PER with its kinase, thereby potentially facilitating efficient phosphorylation within these biomolecular condensates. The observed co-localization between DBT and Hsp70Ba raises the intriguing possibility that Hsp70s may not only regulate the substrate PER but also interact with or modulate the kinase DBT, perhaps to ensure the precise timing of PER phosphorylation. This coordinated recruitment positions Hsp70s as central regulators that orchestrate the phosphorylation-dependent delay within the circadian clock by leveraging the properties of phase separation.

Taken together, these results indicate that Hsp70 proteins regulate the dynamics of PER in the context of circadian rhythm regulation.

HSP70 modifies the phenotypes of per mutants and affects period length by altering binding affinity

To investigate the protein interaction properties of various per mutants, we compared the interaction efficiency between PERS, PERL and wild-type PER with Hsp70Ba. The perS allele of the period gene results in a shortened circadian period, whereas the perL allele produces a lengthened period. The results revealed that PERL exhibited a stronger interaction with Hsp70Ba, as indicated by a significantly higher PERX-V5/Hsp70Ba-HA ratio (Fig. 4A, B). In contrast, the interaction between PERS and Hsp70Ba did not differ significantly from that of wild-type PER (Fig. 4A, B), consistent with its wild-type-like behavior in condensation formation. Additionally, the interactions between PERS and PERL and Tim were not significantly different from those observed with wild-type PER (Fig. 4C, D). More importantly, we observed competition between Hsp70s and TIM proteins for binding to PER. Co-transfecting Hsp70Ba and Tim with Per, PerL or PerS showed that PerL, which had the strongest interaction with Hsp70Ba, recruited the fewest TIM proteins (Fig. 4E). By calculating the ratio of TIM to Hsp70Ba pulled down by PER and its mutants, we found that wild-type PER primarily interacted with TIM. However, the interaction between PERL and Hsp70Ba was significantly stronger than that of the wild-type PER (Fig. 4F). These findings support the conclusion that Hsp70s compete with TIM for interaction with PER and preferentially bind to different forms of the PER protein. The binding affinity of Hsp70s to PER forms follows the order: PERL > PERS > PER. Consistent with these results, in clock neurons, PERL did not form condensates in the presence of Hsp70A−,Hsp70Ba304 mutations (Fig. S4C). Furthermore, the loss of Hsp70s led to an increased nuclear localization of PERL compared to PER (Fig. 3E). This result suggests that the abnormally high affinity of PERL for Hsp70s prevented its phase separation into liquid condensates.

To determine whether the interaction between PERL and PERS with TIM responds to increased Hsp70 protein levels, we repeated the experiments in Fig. 2K and L with PERL and PERS. We found that the relative interaction between PERL and TIM was significantly weaker than that between PERL and Hsp70s after heat shock (Fig. 4G, H). Consistent with previous results, Hsp70s competed with TIM for binding to PERL. In contrast, the interaction between PERS and TIM was unaffected by the increase in Hsp70Ba protein levels or heat shock treatment (Fig. 4I, J).

Further evidence of Hsp70 regulation of PER was provided by the genetic interactions between Hsp70 mutants and the two per mutants. First, we assessed the temperature compensation properties of the per mutants and per/Hsp70s double mutants. The perL mutant, which displayed significant defects in temperature compensation, showed restored temperature compensation to the level of the Hsp70 mutation when the Hsp70 mutation was introduced at 18 °C, 25 °C, and 29 °C (Table S1). The Hsp70A−, Hsp70B− mutation only partially restored the period shortening caused by perS at 18 °C and 25 °C (Table S1).

Next, we examined the temperature entrainment features of the per mutants and per/Hsp70s double mutants. The flies were subjected to a temperature cycle of 18 °C for 12 h, followed by 29 °C for 12 h for 6 days, and then maintained at a constant temperature of 25 °C to measure the circadian rhythm. We found that the Hsp70 mutants partially suppressed rhythmicity defects in both perL and perS (Table S2). The period length alteration in the perL mutant was suppressed by the Hsp70A−, Hsp70B− mutant, in contrast to perS (Table S2).

In conclusion, the study of PERS and PERL revealed their different affinities for interacting with Hsp70s. The PERL mutation resulted in a greater capacity to interact with Hsp70s compared to wild-type PER, which likely leads to the loss of condensation formation in PERL. Additionally, our study shows that the Hsp70 mutants exhibit varying degrees of suppression of the phenotypic defects caused by the per mutants.

Hsp70s function downstream of per to regulate circadian rhythm

Based on our findings, we conclude that Hsp70s regulate circadian rhythm through per. To investigate this, we examined the genetic interaction between Hsp70s mutants and per01, a null allele of per that lacks rhythmicity. Surprisingly, rhythmicity was restored in the per01; Hsp70s double mutant (Fig. 5A). Further sequence analysis of per01 suggested that the reminiscent protein sequence may play a role (Please refer to the next paragraph for evidence). To validate the genetic relationship between per and Hsp70s, we generated a new per null allele, perCKO, which produces a truncated protein containing only the first 14 amino acids of PER followed by 23 novel amino acids (Fig. S6A). Unlike per01, Hsp70s mutants failed to rescue the perCKO phenotype (Fig. 5A), demonstrating that Hsp70s function upstream of per in regulating circadian rhythm. Further experiments elucidated the mechanism behind the restored rhythmicity in per01 in the absence of Hsp70s. Analysis of the residual PER protein in per01 revealed a truncated 463-amino acid PER protein (Fig. 5B). We identified the interaction regions of PER with Hsp70Ba, specifically amino acid fragments 233–463 and 843–1218 (Fig. 5C). These regions overlap with amino acids 410–430, 878–912, and 998–1019 of PER (Fig. 5C, Fig. S3M), which are predicted to regulate PER condensation formation through Hsp70s. Importantly, the truncated PER1–463 protein in per01 interacts with both Hsp70s and TIM, exhibiting significantly higher affinity for Hsp70s compared to full-length PER (Fig. 5D-E). Consistently, interaction affinity predictions indicated stronger binding between truncated PER1–463 and Hsp70s compared to full-length PER (Fig. 5F, S6B–E). Interaction affinity predictions indicated that the truncated PER1–463 in per01 shows comparable affinity for Hsp70s and TIM (Fig. 5F), explaining the phenotypic differences in per01 with or without Hsp70s mutations. These results indicate that the loss of circadian rhythm in per01 is dependent on Hsp70s.

In summary, we propose a working model for the role of Hsp70s in circadian rhythm regulation (Fig. 5G). In the cytoplasm, PER exists in either liquid condensates or a released form. PER forms complexes with either TIM or Hsp70s, and Hsp70s facilitate PER recovering and release from liquid condensates. The released PER can interact with TIM, undergo phosphorylation, and enter the nucleus. Hsp70s serve as interaction partners for PER, and changes in their interaction in various per mutants can alter protein-protein interactions, cellular localization, liquid-phase properties, and phosphorylation states of PER. Thus, Hsp70s play a critical role in maintaining a proper cytoplasmic repository of PER. The loss of Hsp70s not only disrupts temperature-dependent circadian rhythms but also modifies multiple phenotypes caused by per mutations, suggesting that Hsp70s provide a protective mechanism to mitigate stress on the core clock regulatory machinery.

Hsp70s is involved in temperature dependent circadian regulation

To determine the importance of Hsp70s in circadian regulation, we examined their function under conditions of temperature change.

First, we assessed the interaction between Hsp70s and PER at different temperatures. Quantitative analysis indicated that the interaction strengthened with increasing temperature (Fig. 6A, B). This result suggested a potential role for Hsp70 in modulating protein dynamics in response to temperature variation.

Second, to evaluate the circadian response to thermal stress, we applied a brief heat shock (35 °C for 1 h at ZT12) to both w¹¹¹⁸ (control) and Hsp70 mutant flies before returning them to constant darkness (DD) at 25 °C. Measurement of percentage rhythmicity over the following five days revealed that Hsp70 mutant flies exhibited a significantly higher level of arrhythmicity (Fig. 6C, D). This finding indicated that Hsp70 helps stabilize the circadian rhythm during high‑temperature stress.

Discussion

In this study, we found that Hsp70 family proteins play a crucial role in regulating the core clock mechanism by controlling PER condensation formation. As Hsp70 responds to various stress conditions, this mechanism may mediate changes in circadian rhythms under these conditions. Together with the protein condensation formation regulation of other circadian rhythm-related proteins [37], our findings highlight the importance of protein condensation formation in the regulation of circadian rhythms.

The condensation of PER was observed in both the cytoplasm and nucleus [4, 5, and this study]. Based on our data, Hsp70 facilitates the release of PER from the condensates, which is necessary for PER to interact with TIM and enter the nucleus. Hsp70’s regulatory function helps balance the available quantity of PER for its action. However, it remains unclear whether PER is regulated by a similar mechanism in the nucleus or if the dynamics of nuclear entry and export influence PER condensate formation.

Our discovery that the kinase DBT is recruited to PER condensates and co-localizes with Hsp70s unveils a previously unappreciated layer of regulation. It suggests that PER condensates are not merely inert storage depots but active enzymatic hubs where phosphorylation is enhanced. The concomitant presence of Hsp70s points to a chaperone-assisted phosphorylation mechanism, wherein Hsp70s facilitate the proper conformation of PER for DBT-mediated phosphorylation and may simultaneously regulate DBT itself. This integrated mechanism ensures that the critical time-delaying step of PER phosphorylation is both efficient and robust against environmental perturbations, such as temperature stress.

This study demonstrates that condensation formation is involved in the core regulatory machinery of the circadian rhythm. condensation formation primarily functions to modulate protein concentrations, which can either enhance or inhibit biochemical reactions or buffer protein concentrations [38–40]. Based on our data, we propose the existence of a Per protein pool that undergoes condensation formation. Hsp70s facilitate the release of Per from this concentrated phase. Interestingly, while Hsp70s contribute to condensation formation, they are not required for it, suggesting the presence of a mechanism that maintains equilibrium in Per protein levels. Therefore, our findings support the conclusion that Per condensation formation plays a crucial role in buffering its concentration. The regulation of Per by Hsp70s provides an additional layer of control over circadian rhythm stability, particularly in response to temperature changes or stress conditions. It would be interesting to investigate how this mechanism operates in temperature compensation or other environmental conditions.

The observed phenotypic differences in Hsp70 alleles between sexes may be caused by sexual dimorphism in the gene regulatory networks that involve Hsp70s. For instance, in Drosophila, the expression pattern of Hsp70 family proteins differs between males and females [41]. A similar sex-biased expression pattern has been discovered in golden apple snails (Pomacea canaliculata) [42]. Overall, these studies suggest that Hsp70 expression exhibits stronger oscillations in females compared to males. Therefore, it is worth investigating whether these distinct patterns confer specific benefits to either sex.

The role of the Hsp70 family in per regulation may differ between species. For instance, in monarch butterflies, Hsp68 undergoes methylation and mediates the interaction between PER and the CLK-BMAL complex [43]. It would be valuable to explore whether the Hsp70 family can also undergo methylation in Drosophila and whether the Hsp70 family regulates the phase transition of PER in other Lepidoptera species, such as monarch butterflies. Furthermore, it is important to test whether the mechanism identified in this study is conserved in vertebrates.

Conclusions

This study reveals that Hsp70 chaperones regulate circadian rhythms by controlling the condensation formation of the PERIOD (PER) protein, ensuring proper clock function. Hsp70s compete with TIM to bind PER, preventing excessive PER condensation, and their mutation disrupts rhythms in a temperature-dependent manner. By demonstrating that PER acts downstream of Hsp70s, the work provides new mechanistic insights into how molecular clocks maintain stability under environmental fluctuations. These findings advance our understanding of circadian regulation and could inform therapies for clock-related disorders.
