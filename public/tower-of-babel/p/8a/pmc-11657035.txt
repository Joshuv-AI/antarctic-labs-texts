Microneedles with Implantable Tip‐Accumulated Therapeutics for the Long‐Term Management of Psoriasis
Authors: Fatma Moawad, Yasmine Ruel, Nastaran Rezaei, Jérôme Alsarraf, André Pichette, Jean Legault, Roxane Pouliot, Davide Brambilla
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMethotrexate is successfully used as the gold standard for managing moderate‐to‐severe psoriasis. However, the low bioavailability and short half‐life of the oral pills and the invasiveness of the parenteral injections make these suboptimal therapeutic options. Microneedles, bridging the advantages of the former forms, are successfully used to deliver methotrexate for different therapeutic purposes. However, the utilized dissolving microneedles demand frequent administration, potentially compromising patients’ compliance. Additionally, the high toxicity of methotrexate prompts a quest for safer alternatives. Phloretin, a natural compound with confirmed antipsoriatic potential, emerges as a promising candidate. Herein, microneedle patches with separable, slow‐degrading tips are developed for the sustained delivery of methotrexate and phloretin, as a comprehensive solution for long‐term psoriasis management. Both compounds are individually loaded at varying doses and display sustained‐release profiles. The developed microneedle patches demonstrate high mechanical strength, favorable drug delivery efficiency, and remarkable antipsoriatic potential both in vitro in keratinocytes and in vivo in a psoriasis mouse model. Comparative analysis with two subcutaneous injections reveals a similar antipsoriatic efficacy with a single patch of either compound, with prominent phloretin safety. Therefore, the developed patches present a superior alternative to methotrexate's current marketed forms and provide a viable alternative (phloretin) with comparable antipsoriatic efficacy and higher safety.Keywords: methotrexate, microneedles, phloretin, PLGA, psoriasis, sustained release

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic, relapsing, immune‐mediated papulosquamous skin disorder with a genetic basis and both cutaneous and systemic manifestations. Clinically, the most prevalent form, psoriasis vulgaris (≥90% of total psoriatic cases), is characterized by the presence of inflammatory plaques with erythema, and thickened and scaly skin. These manifestations are mainly located in the scalp, knees, elbows, and trunk. Histologically, the affected skin areas exhibit parakeratosis, acanthosis, increased dermal vascularity, and an influx of inflammatory cells.[ 1 ] With a global impact on more than 125 million people, psoriasis significantly diminishes the quality of their lives. Most importantly, it is commonly associated with various comorbidities, e.g., psychiatric, rheumatologic, metabolic, and cardiovascular complications. Several therapeutic options are available, including topical, photo‐based, conventional systemic, and biological therapy. Selection of the optimal therapeutic regimen is based on disease severity and location of the lesions in the body, amongst others.[ 2 ]

Methotrexate (MTX) is the first‐line systemic therapy for managing moderate‐to‐severe psoriatic cases. Its antipsoriatic effect stems from inhibiting cell replication, suppressing immune cell activation, and alleviating the associated inflammation.[ 3 ] Commercially, MTX is available as oral pills and parenteral injections; each with its limitations, i.e., saturable absorption‐induced low bioavailability of the former, invasiveness‐triggered low compliance of the latter, and a short half‐life (≈6 h) with both, necessitating repeated dosing.[ 4 ] Driven by the frequent administration and long‐term use under such chronic cases, MTX provokes, to different extents, serious side effects, including gastrointestinal, renal, hepatic, pulmonary, and bone marrow toxicities,[ 5 ] prompting the exploration of safer alternatives.

Indeed, over the last decades, researchers have explored numerous natural compounds. A subset of polyphenolic flavonoids has demonstrated promise by interfering with key pathways implied in psoriasis initiation and progression.[ 6 ] Among these, phloretin (Ph) has shown an interesting antipsoriatic potential through possessing anti‐inflammatory activity, suppressing immune cell activation, as well as exhibiting antiproliferative properties.[ 7 ] Such potential has been validated in our recent study by evaluating the in vitro antiproliferative and anti‐inflammatory activity of Ph in cocultures of human psoriatic keratinocytes and T lymphocytes.[ 8 ] An activity that was surprisingly comparable to that of the first‐line therapy MTX. In addition, Ph possesses antioxidant properties that could further ameliorate its antipsoriatic potential.[ 9 ] Previous studies have documented the participation of oxidative stress in psoriasis pathogenesis.[ 10 ] However, Ph has a short half‐life following both oral and parenteral administration[ 11 ] and very low bioavailability following oral delivery, owing to its low hydrophilicity and absorption (log P = 3.5).[ 11 , 12 ] Both limit its therapeutic applications.

From this point, the transdermal route could offer a great alternative for delivering both MTX and Ph. Yet, conventional transdermal dosage forms may not be the best option. MTX is a hydrophilic dicarboxylic acid (log P = −1.85). Its permeation through the skin is hindered by its moderately high molecular weight (454.44 Da) and its dissociation into a negatively charged moiety under physiological conditions.[ 13 ] Conversely, Ph is a hydrophobic small compound with a demonstrated favorable permeation profile as previously evidenced, yet, it lacks clarity regarding its penetration across the different skin layers.[ 14 ] Successful penetration into different layers of the skin is crucial for achieving full antipsoriatic activity. Moreover, the poor aqueous solubility of Ph hinders its formulation into conventional transdermal dosage forms.[ 14 , 15 ]

Microneedles (MNs) present an intriguing drug delivery technology that blends the advantages of both invasive and non‐invasive dosage forms, by possessing arrays of micron‐sized projections that pierce the skin in a minimally invasive way to deliver their cargo into/through the skin. MNs have demonstrated versatility in delivering a wide variety of pharmaceuticals, including small[ 16 ] and macro‐molecules,[ 17 ] to address various conditions such as cardiovascular disorders,[ 18 ] immune‐mediated disorders,[ 19 ] cancer,[ 20 ] tissue regeneration and regenerative medicine,[ 21 ] vaccination,[ 22 ] and diagnosis,[ 23 ] via both transdermal[ 24 ] and non‐dermal[ 25 ] applications. Several types of MNs exist. Among them, dissolving MNs stand out as the most commonly studied one, owing to their superior characteristics: simple manufacturing, safe disposal, material diversity, biocompatibility, and higher drug loading compared to the others.[ 26 ] Upon application into the skin, the needles dissolve to mediate immediate, controlled, or sustained delivery of medications.[ 16 , 27 ] For psoriasis, MNs can play a significant role in delivering high local drug amounts into the skin. While dissolving MNs have previously been employed for the immediate delivery of MTX, the need for frequent administration persisted,[ 28 ] escalating the treatment cost and diminishing patient compliance. Addressing this concern, the Donnelly group has reported using dissolving MNs to deliver MTX nanocrystals for the sustained release of the compound.[ 29 ] However, MTX exhibited fast release from these crystals, whereas >60% of MTX was released within the first 8 h. In addition, the fabrication of the nanocrystals and their subsequent loading into MNs is a time‐consuming process with a high expected drug waste. Besides, the drug delivery efficiency of these patches was relatively low (25%), necessitating frequent administration to maintain a therapeutic level of MTX. The reported low drug delivery efficiency is primarily caused by loading the nanocrystals along the entire MN shafts and the incomplete insertion of these shafts into the skin. To address this issue, a recent study reported the development of MN patches with swellable, separable tips for sustained MTX delivery.[ 30 ] However, these patches demonstrated a very rapid release profile, with ≈100% MTX being released within 40 min in vitro, demanding more frequent administration than the previous example. Indeed, in the in vivo studies, the patches were applied several times over a one‐week experimental period to achieve a therapeutic effect. Similar to the previous study, the preparation process of these MN patches is time‐consuming, involving the initial manufacture of the needles, crosslinking the tips, and subsequent loading of MTX into these swellable tips. This multi‐step manufacturing process raises concerns about its reproducibility. Regarding Ph, MNs have not been investigated for its delivery for therapeutic purposes, to the best of our knowledge, thus far.

In this line, herein, we report the development of MNs with separable, slow‐degrading tips designed to mediate sustained transdermal delivery of both MTX and Ph for the long‐term management of psoriasis. This approach could thereby reduce the administration frequency of the loaded compounds and enhance patient adherence to the treatment. Essentially, we provide a comparative analysis of the efficacy of Ph as a potential antipsoriatic agent to the well‐established antipsoriatic compound MTX, offering a new therapeutic option for managing psoriasis with a higher anticipated safety given its natural origin. In addition, we present a more straightforward loading and fabrication process, introduce a new platform for the transdermal delivery of the relatively new compound Ph, and offer a sustained‐release drug delivery system for both compounds under study. The separable tips are made from the poly(lactic‐co‐glycolic acid) (PLGA) copolymer, spiked with a small amount of the polylactic acid (PLA) polymer. The main body of the patches is made from fast‐dissolving materials, designed to ideally dissolve within a few minutes after application into the skin and release the medicated tips. The developed MN patches were subjected to a comprehensive set of characterizations to assess their effectiveness. These assessments encompassed evaluation of the capacity of the needles to pierce the skin, verification of the successful loading of both compounds, fine‐tuning their release to achieve sustained‐release profiles, gaining insights into the potential release mechanisms, investigating their post‐loading integrity and stability inside the developed MNs, and eventually assessing their antipsoriatic potential both in vitro in keratinocytes and in vivo in imiquimod (IMQ)‐induced psoriasis mouse model.

Results and Discussion

Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐tipped MN Patches

Morphological Examination

Using the molding technique (Figure S1, Supporting Information), 10 × 10 pyramidal arrays of MNs were prepared. For the initial optimization of the manufacturing process, Cyanine‐5 (Cy5) dye was loaded in the PLGA tips. As shown in Figure 1A (i, iii), two distinct layers were formed: the separable PLGA tips and the water‐soluble hyaluronic acid (HA) part of the needles. The dye distribution was confined only to the upper part of the needles (tips), guaranteeing full control over the release. Moreover, microscopic images (Figure 1A (iv), B) showed seamless adhesion between the two layers, suggesting that no breakage of the needles is anticipated during insertion into the skin. The morphological characteristics of the tips can be seen clearly in Figure 1A (ii): dense, tailless, and sharp, indicating their potential to easily penetrate the skin and separate completely beneath the skin surface. All detailed characteristics were further confirmed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging. Figure 1C showcases intact, sharp‐tipped MNs with excellent adhesion between the two layers.

Mechanical Analysis

As a drug delivery platform, MNs should have sufficient mechanical strength to reliably penetrate the skin for optimal drug delivery efficacy. Accordingly, we tested the mechanical properties of the developed patches. As illustrated in Figure 1D, the manufactured MN patches had high mechanical strength (>25 N/patch), enabling successful skin penetration as previously reported.[ 31 ] Images in Figure 1E,F show the impact of the mechanical testing on the integrity and morphology of the needles. Only slight compression was observed, with no signs of separation of the two layers or breakage of the needles during testing, ensuring high performance of the fabricated patches.

Ex Vivo Penetration, Implantation, and Delivery Efficiency Assessment

Subsequently, the ability of these tips to pierce and separate under the skin surface was tested ex vivo on excised porcine and rat skin. More than 90% needle penetration and tip implantation within only 5 min of patch application were recorded (Figure 1G,H), ensuring a short wearing time of the patches and minimizing the risk of skin infection/inflammation. The rapid tip detachment can be attributed to the high solubility of the HA. Penetration depth was evaluated as well through histological sectioning of the tested skin tissue. The needles showed >100 µm penetration depth (Figure 1I), ensuring effective separation of the tips under the skin surface and high localization of the drug at the active sites of the disease. Given the excellent penetration and implantation profiles of the developed MN patches, high drug delivery efficiency was expected. Indeed, the drug delivery efficiency of these patches was investigated ex vivo and found satisfactory (67.6%; Figure 1G). Collectively, the results demonstrate an efficient and rapid separation of the tips, with favorable delivery efficiency.

Fabrication of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches

Using the micromolding/vacuum combination procedures, Ph‐loaded MN patches were successfully prepared. For the preparation of MTX‐loaded MNs, we applied a slight modification by forming the tips using the micromolding/centrifugation technique. The insolubility of MTX in the tip‐forming matrix did not favor filling under vacuum, whereas the drug precipitated as a thin film on the surface of the molds resulting in a very low loading. Indeed, this observation was in agreement with our previously reported results.[ 32 ] All developed MN patches (both MTX‐ and Ph‐loaded) were in the form of 10 × 10 pyramidal arrays with dimensions of 200 × 200 × 500 µm (W × L × H). Tip‐forming solutions or suspensions with varying concentrations of Ph or MTX, respectively, were prepared and used to form the needles. This approach aimed to evaluate the potential for loading different doses of these compounds within this type of MN.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches

Morphological Examination

As shown in Figure 2A (i, ii), both MTX and Ph‐loaded MN patches were successfully prepared with a complete restriction of the distribution of both compounds to the separable tips, ensuring ultimate control over their release. Successful loading was indicated by the yellow and brown staining of the tips, corresponding to MTX and Ph respectively. Additionally, the size of the medicated tips (≤half the length of the needles) guarantees a high drug delivery efficacy, considering the incomplete penetration of the needles into the skin owing to its elasticity and bed‐of‐nails effect.[ 33 ] The morphology and solid state of both compounds were examined using SEM. Both compounds were found in a crystalline state as shown in Figure 2A (iii, iv). MTX exhibited a tetragonal structure with uniform size (≤5 µm), while Ph showed bigger rod‐like crystals (≤50 µm). Importantly, the integrity, sharpness, and exterior texture of the MNs were unaffected by loading either compound, as confirmed by the SEM imaging (Figure 2A (v,vi)).

Mechanical Analysis

To investigate the impact of loading different amounts of each compound on the mechanical properties of the needles, the mechanical strength of the developed MNs was tested using a texture analyzer and compared to the blank MNs. As shown in Figure 2B, the mechanical strength of the needles increased upon loading MTX compared to the blank MNs, a trend observed with MNs formed with different MTX concentrations. Conversely, the mechanical strength of MNs prepared with different concentrations of Ph was lower than that of the blank MNs. Yet, all MN patches maintained mechanical strength higher than the skin penetration threshold.[ 31 ] Furthermore, both MTX and Ph‐loaded MNs showed slight compression with no actual separation or breakage of the tips (Figure 2C), suggesting that loading either compound had no significant impact on the MN behavior.

Solid State Analyses

The solid‐state of the loaded compounds could dramatically affect their release from different drug delivery carriers/systems, including MNs.[ 34 ] With this in mind, we investigated the state of both compounds inside the fabricated MNs using both X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) techniques. The analysis was performed for the MTX and Ph‐loaded patches, as well as their individual raw components (MTX, Ph, PLGA, and PLA). As shown in the XRD spectra (Figure 2D), both polymers (PLGA and PLA) exhibited an amorphous state confirmed by the presence of broad wide peaks in their corresponding spectra, and both compounds (MTX and Ph) exhibited a crystalline state marked by the presence of numerous sharp peaks. MTX characteristic XRD peaks appeared in the 2θ range of 9–28°, with the main high‐intensity ones appearing at 9.2, 12.8, 21.3, 23.4, 26.8, and 27.8°. Those of Ph appeared in a 2θ range of 13–44° and the main high‐intensity ones appeared at 13.4, 18.5, 22.4, 26.4, and 27.3°. Following incorporation into MNs, the peak intensities of both compounds were reduced. Yet, both compounds retained their characteristic ones, suggesting a preservation of their crystalline state within the MNs. The reduction in the intensity of the peaks could be explained by the disruption of the crystalline structure by the amorphous polymeric network, as previously reported.[ 35 ]

These results were further confirmed by DSC analysis of the medicated patches and the raw components. As shown in Figure 2E and Figure S2 (Supporting Information), both polymers exhibited an amorphous nature, with glass transition temperatures of 50 °C and 62 °C and degradation peaks at 381 °C and 377 °C for PLGA and PLA, respectively. The broad endothermic peak in the area between 50 and 150 °C, and the peak at 272 °C in MTX thermogram refer, respectively, to its dehydration and decomposition. The sharp narrow endothermic peak at 274 °C in Ph thermogram refers to the melting of its crystals. All peaks corresponding to MTX and Ph were retained following their incorporation into MNs, confirming the presence of both compounds in crystalline forms within the MNs and agreeing with the XRD data. These results also guarantee the stability of both compounds under the employed manufacturing and drying conditions of the MNs (all procedures were conducted at temperatures below 100 °C).

Drug Content Evaluation

By varying the concentration of each compound in the initial MN matrix, we were able to successfully load different amounts of each compound within the fabricated MN patches. A direct linear relationship between the loaded amount and the initial concentration of each compound was observed. The loaded amount of MTX per patch increased from 53.01 ± 27.57 µg to 86.25 ± 9.29 and 139.62 ± 19.26 µg, while the loaded amount of Ph per patch increased from 78.46 ± 6.28 µg to 92.87 ± 6.42 and 140.44 ± 21.68 µg, at initial concentrations, respectively, of 25, 50, and 75 mg mL−1 of each compound (Figure 3A). Thus, the loaded dose per patch could be controlled by manipulating the initial concentration of the compounds, allowing the preparation of MN patches with varying doses potentially optimized to different clinical needs, corresponding to different disease severities.

Solubility Study

To establish appropriate release mediums achieving the sink conditions, we conducted a solubility study of both compounds in PBS (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Considering the demonstrated high solubility of MTX in PBS and the tested amount of MTX in the release study (MTX content/patch), 1 mL PBS proved sufficient to attain the sink condition. On the other side, the low solubility of Ph in PBS rendered it insufficient to achieve the sink condition for the specified amount of the compound/patch. For that, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added at a concentration of 0.3% w/v. The chosen concentration of SDS was selected based on a previous study.[ 36 ]

In Vitro Release Evaluation and Degradation Tracking

The in vitro release behavior of both compounds from the fabricated MN patches was evaluated and the results are indicated in Figure 3B. Both compounds exhibited sustained‐release profiles with 42.73% ± 4.3%9 and 69.77% ± 13.68% of MTX and Ph, respectively, were released within 48 h. The difference in the release behavior could be explained on a crystal‐size difference basis of the loaded MTX and Ph. In detail, the bigger crystals of Ph tend to compromise the polymeric network of the PLGA/PLA polymers, resulting in less control over its release. Conversely, the smaller crystals of MTX have a lower impact on the integrity of the polymeric network. Over and above, tracking the degradation of the tips in the release medium revealed that they maintained most of their size and shape over 3 weeks (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This suggests effective control over the release of the remaining portion of both compounds over a few more days. Notably, porosity in the tips was observed after 1 week, suggesting swelling of the polymeric tips.

To better understand the release mechanism of both compounds, the release data were analyzed using various mathematical models. The calculated parameters are shown in Table 1 . The release of both compounds followed the Korsmeyer–Peppas's release kinetics (Figure S5, Supporting Information): non‐Fickian (anomalous) diffusion for MTX (n = 0.509) and super case II transport for Ph (n = 1.044). Fundamentally, this model was developed to describe the release of active pharmaceutical compounds from polymeric matrices. The different release modes of the two compounds can be attributed to differences in their hydrophobicity. The low hydrophobicity of MTX facilitates its diffusion through the formed dissolution medium‐filled pores within the polymeric matrix, as well as through the relaxation of the polymeric chains themselves, conforming to the non‐Fickian diffusion mode. The high hydrophobicity of Ph aligns perfectly with the super case II transport mode, where the drug release is predominantly controlled by the relaxation of the hydrophobic polymeric chains.[ 37 ]

In Vitro Antiproliferative Activity and Cytocompatibility Study

A significant pathological feature of psoriasis is the hyperproliferation of the keratinocytes. A primary aspect of the antipsoriatic mechanism of MTX is its antiproliferative effect.[ 38 ] In addition, Ph has demonstrated a notable antiproliferative activity in various cell lines, including human psoriatic keratinocytes, which is advantageous in psoriasis management.[ 7 , 8 , 39 ] Such impact of Ph on keratinocytes may be exerted directly[ 39 ] or indirectly by inhibiting the inflammation‐triggered proliferation of the cells.[ 40 ] In this study, we assessed the efficacy of both compounds, post‐loading activity, and stability inside the fabricated MNs by evaluating their in vitro inhibitory effect on the proliferation of the immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCat). HaCat cell line was selected for this investigation for its widespread acceptance, reproducibility, and reliability as an in vitro model for studying various immunological, inflammatory, and therapeutic responses in skin diseases.[ 41 ] As shown in Figure S6B (Supporting Information), Ph began to exhibit its inhibitory effect at concentrations exceeding 100 µm, with a half‐maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value equal to 174.4 µm (Figure S6C, Supporting Information). Consequently, three concentrations around this IC50 were selected and tested (100, 150, and 200 µm). The same concentrations were applied for testing MTX, enabling a comparative analysis. To assess the post‐loading activity, both drugs were extracted from fresh prepared MN patches, tested, and compared to standard solutions, while to evaluate the stability within the MNs, both compounds were extracted from 3‐month stored MN patches, tested, and compared to the efficacy of the fresh prepared MNs. As indicated in Figure 3C,D, both compounds significantly inhibited cell proliferation compared to the negative control. For MTX, the insignificant difference between the inhibitory effect (at 150 and 200 µm) of MTX standard solution and MTX extracted from both fresh and stored MNs confirms the retention of the compound's activity during the fabrication/drying processes of the MNs. This also ensures the stability of the compound inside the MN patches under the applied storage conditions (patches stored on the shelf, at room temperature, and in the dark). On the other hand, Ph exhibited significantly lower inhibitory activity at all tested concentrations when both types of MNs (fresh and stored) were compared to the Ph standard solution. This implies a partial loss of Ph activity under the employed fabrication/drying conditions. Yet, the difference in the antiproliferative effect of Ph extracted from both types of MNs (fresh and stored) was insignificant at all tested concentrations, suggesting the stability of the compound within the MNs under the specified storage conditions. Furthermore, cells treated with the blank MN extract did not show any reduction in viability, serving as evidence of the biocompatibility and cytocompatibility of the MN components. It is important to note that cell viability was assessed 24 h after treatment with Ph and 6 days after treatment with MTX. MTX is a slow‐acting compound with a time‐dependent impact on apoptosis induction and cell proliferation inhibition, a behavior that has been tested (MTX‐induced inhibition was insignificant for up to 72 h of treatment; data not shown) and previously documented.[ 42 ] The observed consistent effect upon treating the HaCat cells with various concentrations of MTX (Figure S6A, Supporting Information) could be explained by the relatively low sensitivity of this cell line to MTX, owing to the low mitotic activity of the keratinocytes.[ 43 ] Fundamentally, MTX exerts its antiproliferative effect by interfering with nucleic acid synthesis during cellular replication. On the contrary, the high mitotic activity of psoriatic epidermal cells in vivo renders them more sensitive to folic acid antagonists, making them a primary target of MTX.[ 43 , 44 ]

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study

Study Design and Treatment

To further validate the efficacy of the fabricated patches, their antipsoriatic efficacy was evaluated in vivo in an IMQ‐induced psoriasis mouse model. For comparison, two groups received subcutaneous (SC) injections of either MTX or Ph and one group received blank MNs. All groups were compared to the IMQ disease model and the healthy control (untreated mice). MN patches with the highest dose of each compound (139.6 µg for MTX and 140.4 µg for Ph) were applied once throughout the entire study period, while the reference SC injections were given twice. Each SC injection corresponded to half the MN dose of each compound, i.e., 69.8 µg of MTX or 70.2 µg of Ph. The total dose of each compound administered to the MN and SC groups was identical. As shown in Figure 4A, except for the healthy control, IMQ was applied to all groups for 7 days. All treatments started on day 3, which marks the onset of visibly clear (Figure 4B) and statistically significant symptoms (at p < 0.0001, Figure 4C; Figure S7, Supporting Information) compared to the healthy control, i.e., typical psoriatic manifestation including erythema, scaling, and increased skin thickness. The beginning of the treatment corresponded to the MN application and the first SC injection, while the second SC injection was administered on day 5.

Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI)

As depicted in Figure 4B and supported by Figure 4C and Figure S7 (Supporting Information), the 7‐day application of IMQ has successfully induced psoriasis signs, such as erythema, scaling, and thickened skin, in all IMQ‐treated groups. Starting from day 3, the total PASI scoring has markedly increased compared to the healthy control (p < 0.0001), with no difference noticed among the IMQ‐treated groups. On day 5, the PASI score in the MTX and Ph MN groups decreased significantly compared to the disease model (p < 0.01), while the blank MN and the 2 SC groups remained insignificant. At this point, the difference in the PASI score between the MTX MN group and the Ph MN group and between the MTX SC group and the Ph SC group was insignificant, indicating equal efficacy of Ph compared to MTX. By the end of the experiment (day 7), the PASI scoring for all MTX and Ph‐treated groups showed a dramatic reduction compared to the disease model (all significant at p < 0.0001). Compared to the healthy control, there was no statistically significant difference in the PASI score of the MTX MN, Ph MN, and MTX SC groups, However, the Ph SC group showed a significantly different PASI score (p < 0.0001). It is noteworthy that the differences between MTX MNs and Ph MNs, and between MTX MNs and MTX SC groups were insignificant on day 7. Meanwhile, the Ph SC group exhibited a lower overall improvement by the end of the study, compared to MTX SC and Ph MN groups (both at p < 0.01). This could be explained by the shorter half‐life and faster elimination of Ph following SC injection, compared to MTX SC and Ph MNs, respectively. Interestingly, the blank MN group also demonstrated a reduction in the PASI scoring by the end of the study, statistically significant compared to both the disease model and healthy control (both at p < 0.0001). This reduction could be attributed to the emollient and hydrating properties exerted by the dissolved HA from the needles and patch backing during the application of the patches, contributing partially to alleviating the symptoms of the disease. Similar cases documented the efficacy of local emollients as adjuvant antipsoriatic therapy.[ 45 ]

Body Weight Measurement

As a safety marker, the body weight of the mice in all groups was monitored over the entire experimental period. As illustrated (Figure 4D), all groups except the healthy control showed a reduction in body weight starting from the onset of symptoms (day 3), triggered by the IMQ‐induced consistent inflammation. By the end of the study, only the disease model and the blank MN group exhibited the highest reduction in their body weight, suggesting a higher disease severity compared to the MTX and Ph‐treated groups. Only the reduction in body weight in the blank MN group was statistically significant compared to the healthy control (p < 0.01). In conclusion, body weight was found to correlate with the severity of the disease.

Spleen Measurement

As a primary site implied in the IMQ‐induced systemic inflammation/immune response,[ 46 ] the spleens from all mice were collected, weighed, and employed as an indicator of disease induction and impact of the various treatments on the induced systemic inflammation/immune response (Figure 4E; Figure S8, Supporting Information). Indeed, the application of IMQ dramatically increased the spleen mass index, confirming successful disease induction with both topical and systemic manifestation. Different Ph and MTX treatments have effectively reduced the spleen mass index, illustrating their immune‐modulating properties. The difference in the spleen mass index between the MTX MN, Ph MN, MTX SC, and Ph SC groups was statistically insignificant. These results are in excellent agreement with the previously reported spleen measurements in IMQ‐induced psoriasis models.[ 47 ]

Histology and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Epidermal thickening, epidermal protrusion, and infiltration of inflammatory cells into the epidermal and dermal layers are typical pathological signs in psoriasis.[ 48 ] The epidermal thickness, shown in Figure 5A and quantitatively represented in Figure 5E, was in excellent agreement with the detailed PASI score results. The thickness of the epidermis (taking into account the epidermal protrusions) dramatically increased following the application of the IMQ cream, 293.77 ± 31.79 µm epidermal thickness of the disease model versus 28.36 ± 4.42 µm epidermal thickness of the healthy control. However, following the application of the different treatments, the epidermal thickness greatly and significantly decreased. The greatest reduction was observed in the Ph MN group (82.64 ± 14.72 µm), followed by the MTX MN group (87.99 ± 34.73 µm), MTX SC group (89.83 ± 15.90 µm), and finally by the Ph SC group (93.85 ± 23.82 µm). Yet, the difference in the epidermal thickness between the four treatment groups was statistically insignificant. Interestingly, the blank MN group showed a marked and statistically significant reduction in skin thickness (156.99 ± 4.20 µm) compared to the disease model (p < 0.0001). However, this reduction remained statistically lower compared to all MTX and Ph‐treated groups (p < 0.0001). The blank MN results align with the PASI score results of the blank MN group and could be attributed to the same reasons described in the mentioned section. Moreover, the number of epidermal protrusions was pronouncedly high in the disease model and dramatically decreased following treatment in the different groups. From another perspective, the skin sections of different groups showed variable inflammatory cell infiltration, marked by the dark blue dots in both the dermal and epidermal layers. The highest level of infiltration was observed in the disease model, followed by the blank MN group, and then the MTX and Ph‐treated groups. The least infiltration was observed in the healthy control, as expected.

On the other side, histological analysis of the major organs collected from different groups revealed outstanding biocompatibility and excellent biosafety profiles of all the treatment options, evidenced by the lack of any apparent signs of tissue inflammation or necrosis (Figure S9, Supporting Information). However, representative histological images of the spleens (Figure 5D) displayed a significant expansion and subsequent reduction in the size of the white pulp upon application of the IMQ cream and the treatments, respectively. This observation explains the exacerbated systemic inflammation/immune response upon IMQ application and its subsequent alleviation by the treatment, aligning with all the above‐described results.

From another perspective, immunohistochemical analysis of the collected skin samples from different groups showed great variability in the expression of the proliferation biomarker Ki67 and the number of CD3+ cells, as shown in Figure 5B,C and illustrated in Figure 5F,G, respectively. The biomarkers Ki67 and CD3 are used as indicators of keratinocyte hyperproliferation and T‐cell infiltration, respectively. In detail, a significant elevation in Ki67+ cells was observed in the disease model, followed by a marked reduction to varying degrees in the different treatment groups (MTX and Ph, both MNs and SC injections). Similarly, a pronounced and statistically significant elevation in the number of CD3+ cells was noted, with a subsequent reduction in all treatment groups compared to the healthy control. Interestingly, the number of CD3+ cells in the treatment groups was statistically insignificant compared to the healthy control. In contrast to the PASI score and the epidermal thickness findings, the blank MN group did not exhibit any reduction in Ki67 expression or the number of CD3+ cells. This suggests that the effect of HA is restricted to the phenotypic features of the disease rather than the systemic ones.

Altogether, both MTX and Ph administered in different forms equally alleviated the phenotypic and histological signs of the disease in the mice's skin.

Consistent with the previous findings, a marked cutaneous overexpression of various pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF‐α and the interleukins IL‐17A and IL‐6, was observed following disease induction. Blood Biochemical Analysis

The biosafety of the used treatment options was evaluated by monitoring both liver and kidney functions at the end of the study, via blood biochemical analysis. As illustrated in Figure 6D, only the MTX SC group exhibited an elevated level of the liver enzyme aspartate aminotransferase (AST), which was statistically significant from the MTX MN, Ph MN, and Ph SC groups (all at p < 0.05). An elevation in the level of the alanine aminotransferase (ALT) enzyme was also noted in the same group; however, it was statistically insignificant compared to the healthy control (Figure 6E). Fundamentally, MTX is well‐known for its hepatotoxicity.[ 50 ] The difference in the level of the liver enzymes between the two administered dosage forms of MTX (MNs and SC), at equivalent doses, can be attributed to the higher plasma concentration of MTX achieved following the SC administration of the compound. Oppositely, MNs were designed for the sustained delivery of the compound, resulting in a lower plasma concentration of the drug over the treatment period. Conversely, Ph showed a high biosafety profile following delivery via MNs and SC injections. Only a slight elevation in the urea level was observed following Ph SC injections (Figure 6F), however, the level remained within the normal range (Table S1, Supporting Information).[ 51 ]

To summarize, the collective in vivo results demonstrated comparable antipsoriatic efficacy of Ph and MTX‐loaded MNs. Furthermore, the findings indicated that the same dose delivered via a single MN patch had an equivalent efficacy to that delivered via two invasive SC injections, despite the lower delivery efficiency of the MNs. The high efficacy of the MNs can be attributed to their ability to provide continuous drug exposure through sustained delivery, along with the additional adjuvant effect of HA in alleviating the phenotypic features of the disease. The demonstrated comparable efficacy, reduced invasiveness, and enhanced safety profile of the developed medicated MN patches compared to the standard SC injections underscore the superiority of our MN systems.

Outlook and Perspectives

Psoriasis is a stigmatizing disease that significantly influences the life quality of its patients, causing both physical and psychological distress. The prolonged and repeated administration of medications poses a challenge to patient compliance and consequently the therapeutic outcome. MNs emerged as a promising drug delivery system for psoriasis that offers the potential to deliver various medications locally or systematically, outperforming the conventional methods in different aspects such as drug localization in psoriatic lesions, systemic bioavailability, and invasiveness.[ 52 ] To help patients regain their daily functionality, herein, we developed MNs with separable slow‐degrading tips for the long‐term delivery of different antipsoriatic compounds: MTX as a well‐established antipsoriatic medication and Ph as a potentially safer alternative. The developed patches are effective, safe, and economically viable, owing to their component availability, straightforward manufacturing process, reduced dosing frequency, and lack of special storage requirements (supported by the stability study on keratinocytes (section 2.3.7)). The patches are self‐administrable with a short wearing time to avoid further complications and eliminate any wearing‐associated inconvenience. Besides, the biocompatibility of the implanted polymers, the safe elimination of their degradation products (lactic and glycolic acid),[ 53 ] and the lack of biohazard waste generation (given the complete separation of the sharp tips under the skin surface) ensure a high level of safety. Additionally, the positive correlation between the initial concentration of the medications and their MN loading enables controlled loading during scaling up.

However, the long‐term stability of the loaded compounds and the potential impact of varied storage conditions have not been evaluated, posing significant efficacy concerns. Moreover, clinical translation may demand enlarged patches, potentially compromising MN mechanical strength, insertion/implantation ability, and consequently the drug delivery efficiency. Another unaddressed translational challenge is MN sterilization. Unlike solid and hollow MNs, dissolving MNs are less amenable to terminal sterilization techniques, e.g., steam autoclaving and dry sterilization, owing to the potentially destructive impact on the needles.[ 52 , 54 ] Gamma irradiation, a commonly used sterilization technique for dissolving MNs, also affects the integrity and mechanical strength of the tips. Additionally, it alters the release profiles of the loaded compounds as a result of the irradiation‐induced degradation of the tip‐forming polymers (PLGA and PLA).[ 55 ] Alternatively, MNs can be manufactured entirely under an aseptic setup, however, the cost of the patches may increase.

Conclusion

In summary, the developed MN patches present a promising platform for delivering different therapeutic options for the long‐term management of psoriasis, with reduced dosing frequency. The needles met the essential requirements for an effective transdermal delivery system: high mechanical strength, satisfactory delivery efficiency, efficient tip‐release within a short wearing time, and satisfactory penetration depth, ensuring high localization and enhanced permeation of the loaded compounds into the skin layers. The loaded compounds exhibited robust loading, demonstrating a linear relationship with their initial concentration in the manufacturing solution. Moreover, they exhibited sustained‐release profiles over a few days. Assessing the antipsoriatic potential of the developed patches, by evaluating their antiproliferative activity in keratinocytes, provided valuable insights into the activity and stability of the loaded compounds inside the manufactured MNs. In vivo application in a psoriasis mouse model demonstrated the efficacy of the developed patches as viable therapeutic options for psoriasis. Notably, the developed patches outperformed the corresponding parenteral injections in terms of reduced invasiveness, lower administration frequency, sustained yet minimized systemic exposure, and diminished associated toxicity. Eventually, our developed delivery system holds significant translational potential for the delivery of the medications under study as well as other antipsoriatic therapeutic options, given its efficacy, biosafety, and biocompatibility.

Materials—Cells and Animals

Immortalized human keratinocytes (HaCat) were purchased from AddexBio (San Diego, CA), and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in an incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2, in a humidified atmosphere. Passage of the cells was done twice a week or at a confluency of 70–90%, using 0.25% trypsin/EDTA. Male Balb/C mice (age = 7–8 weeks and weight = 22–24 g) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Québec, Canada) and housed in a pathogen‐free environment at the Université de Montréal in a 12‐h dark/light cycles with unlimited access to food and water. All animal experiments were conducted following the guidelines of the animal experimentation ethics committee (CDEA) at the Université de Montréal according to the approved protocol #22‐070. Animals were allowed to acclimatize for a week before performing any experiments.

Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Fabrication and optimization of PLGA‐tipped MN patches

For the initial optimization of the MN fabrication process, MNs with Cy5‐loaded separable tips were prepared. The tips were prepared using the micromolding technique illustrated in Figure S1 (Supporting Information); simply by casting 50 µL of a PLGA solution, mixed with a small amount of PLA and containing Cy5 at a concentration of 0.2 mg mL−1, onto the PDMS molds. A vacuum (50 mbar) was applied for 2 min to drive the polymeric solution into the mold cavities. Subsequently, the molds were centrifuged (Sorvall ST 16R, ThermoFisher Scientific, Osterode, Germany) for 30 min at 2300 g and a controlled temperature of 25 °C. After removing the excess polymeric solution, the molds were re‐centrifuged under the same specified conditions for an additional 30 min to concentrate the polymeric solution in the lower parts of the cavities, forming the MN tips. To prepare the casting solution, PLGA and PLA were dissolved in acetone at a ratio of 9:1, with a total polymeric concentration of 5%. Water was added to the polymeric solution at a percentage of 10%, to slow down acetone evaporation during MN fabrication. PLA was included to enhance the mechanical strength of the needles, based on previous studies.[ 56 ] The needles were allowed to dry in an oven at 70 °C for 24 h. Eighty microliters of the backing solution, consisting of a mixture of HA and dextran (8:1) dissolved in water at a concentration of ≈1.1 g mL−1, were then applied on the surface of the molds. A vacuum (50 mbar) was applied for 2 min to form the water‐soluble part of the needles and the patch backing. Following a 2‐day drying period in a humidity chamber (Forma Environmental Chamber, ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), at 25 °C and 60% humidity level, the patches were gently de‐molded and stored at room temperature until further use.

Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Morphological Examination

All developed patches were examined under a stereomicroscope (AxioZoom.V16, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) to confirm the formation of the double‐layered MNs and define the localization of the loaded dye. In addition, the ability of the employed conditions to form intact MNs with sharp tips, capable of penetrating the skin, was assessed using the stereomicroscope and confirmed using a field‐emission SEM (JSM‐7600, JEOL, MA). MNs were examined under the stereomicroscope in both bright‐field and fluorescence mode, and images were recorded and processed using the Zen 3.1 software (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH, Oberkochen, Germany). For SEM analysis, one MN patch was affixed on a stainless‐steel sample holder using a double‐sided carbon tape, sputter‐coated (Quorum, Q150T ES plus, Sussex, UK) with a thin layer of chromium (5 nm), and visualized at an acceleration voltage of 10 kV.

Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Mechanical Analysis

The strength of the developed MNs was evaluated using a TA. XT‐plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK). Briefly, a single MN patch was positioned on the metal platform of the instrument, with the needles facing upward. The test began by moving a 6‐mm diameter cylindrical probe toward the metal platform and allowing it to travel a distance of 0.35 mm through the needles at a speed of 0.1 mm sec−1. The pre‐test and post‐test speeds were set at 0.5 mm sec−1 and the trigger force was maintained at 0.49 N. The experiment was done in a return‐to‐start compression mode and the effect of compression on MNs was visualized using both the stereomicroscope and SEM. Eventually, a force‐displacement curve was created by plotting the force (N) against the traveled distance (mm).

Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Ex Vivo Penetration, Implantation, and Delivery Efficiency Assessment

The ability of the developed MNs, at the used polymeric ratio/concentration and under the applied fabrication conditions, to penetrate the skin was tested ex vivo using excised porcine cadaver skin. In brief, one MN patch was applied to the skin for 1 min using a spring‐loaded applicator (Micropoint Technologies Pvt. Ltd., Singapore). After patch removal, a layer of aqueous trypan blue solution (0.4% w/v) was applied to the skin surface and kept in place for 5 min to stain the penetration sites of the needles. The residual dye solution was then wiped with a swab and the skin was examined under the stereomicroscope to visualize the penetration pattern of the MNs. Moreover, an MN‐pierced skin sample was directly frozen in optimum cutting temperature gel at −80 °C, histologically sectioned (10 µm thick sections), stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and examined under the stereomicroscope to visualize the penetration sites and assess the penetration depth.

Furthermore, the implantation efficiency of the tips was assessed ex vivo on excised rat skin. Briefly, one MN patch with Cy5‐loaded tips was applied to the skin using the spring‐loaded applicator, kept in place for 5 min, and removed. The skin was then gently wiped with a wet swab to remove any protruding tips above its surface. Both the skin and patch backing were examined under the fluorescence stereomicroscope to visualize the implanted fluorescent tips. Eventually, the penetration and implantation efficiencies were calculated, respectively, by dividing the number of the blue‐colored spots (generated by the trypan blue staining) or the fluorescent spots (generated by the detachment and implantation of the Cy5‐loaded tips) by the total number of MNs per patch.

To evaluate the drug delivery efficiency of the developed patches, the peeled patches from the implantation study were carefully collected and sonicated individually for 2 h with 1 mL DMSO/patch to fully dissolve the remaining Cy5 in the patches. The initial content of Cy5 in the patches was evaluated similarly by extraction in DMSO, enhanced by sonication. The fluorescence intensity of the Cy5, both remaining (Fr) and initial (Fi), was measured at excitation/emission wavelengths of 633/680 nm, using a Spark multimode microplate reader (Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). The drug delivery efficiency was then calculated by subtracting the remaining Cy5 in the patches from the initial Cy5 content, using the formula: ((Fi – Fr) / Fi) X 100.

All skin samples were shaved one day before the experiments and stored at −20 °C. On the day of the experiments, the skin samples were thawed and equilibrated in PBS (pH = 7.4) for at least 30 min before use.

Fabrication of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches

MNs loaded with Ph were fabricated using the same method described earlier (section Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Fabrication and optimization of PLGA‐tipped MN patches). For MTX‐loaded MNs, a minor modification was applied based on the previously reported methodology.[ 32 ] In brief, the tips were formed by casting 30 µL of the MTX‐PLGA suspension onto the mold surface and centrifugation for 5 min at 2300 g and 25 °C. This filling step was repeated three more times to ensure the complete filling of the mold cavities. Drying and patch‐backing formation procedures were the same as described in section Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Fabrication and optimization of PLGA‐tipped MN patches. To assess the possibility of loading different amounts of each compound, polymeric suspensions or solutions containing different initial concentrations (25, 50, and 75 mg mL−1) of MTX or Ph, respectively, were prepared and used to form the tips. All de‐molded patches were protected from light and stored in ambient laboratory conditions until use. Blank MN patches were prepared in a similar manner to be used as controls in some experiments.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Morphological Examination

All developed MTX and Ph‐loaded MNs were visualized under the stereomicroscope and SEM, as described in section Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches, to determine the localization of both compounds in the MNs and evaluate the effect of loading on the sharpness and overall integrity of the developed MNs.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Mechanical Analysis

Mechanical analysis was performed for all the manufactured MTX and Ph‐loaded MNs, as described in section Fabrication and Characterization of PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches. Force‐displacement curves were constructed and compared to the blank MNs.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Solid State Analyses

To investigate the physical solid state of raw MTX and Ph, both compounds were distributed as a thin layer of powder on a carbon tape fixed to a sample holder, both were covered with a thin layer of chromium (5 nm) using a sputter‐coater under vacuum, and visualized under the SEM at an acceleration voltage of 5–10 kV. The solid‐state of MTX and Ph inside the developed MNs was investigated using both XRD and DSC. Both analyses were performed on MTX, Ph, PLGA, and PLA raw materials, as well as MTX and Ph‐loaded MNs. This could also provide information about the stability of MTX and Ph under the manufacturing and drying conditions of the MNs.

XRD analysis was performed using a D8 advance diffractometer (Bruker, Berlin, Germany) equipped with graphite‐filtered Cu Kα (ʎ = 1.5418 A°) radiation source over a 2θ range of 5–100°, at room temperature. The initial scanning rate was 15° min−1 with increments of 0.05°. An amperage and voltage of 40 mA and 40 kV, respectively, were used throughout the whole study.

DSC analysis was performed using a Jade DSC 4000 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) in a temperature range of 25–450 °C, at a heating rate of 20 °C min−1, and under a dynamic nitrogen atmosphere (20 mL min−1). Aluminum crucibles and covers (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA) were used for all analyses, with ≈5 mg of samples used each time.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Drug Content Evaluation

To evaluate the content of the loaded compounds at different initial concentrations, one patch of the medicated MNs was placed in a 7‐mL amber‐colored glass vial (Supelco, Sigma‐Aldrich), and 2 mL of either PBS (1X, pH = 7.4; 137 mm NaCl, 10 mm Na2HPO4, 2.7 mm KCl, 1.8 mM KH2PO4) or absolute ethanol were added for the extraction of either MTX or Ph, respectively. Extraction of MTX was facilitated by prior dissolution of the PLGA tips in dichloromethane, and the extraction of both compounds (MTX and Ph) was enhanced by sonication (Branson 2510R‐MT, Branson Ultrasonics Corporation, Connecticut) for 2 h at room temperature, with intermittent vortex. Subsequently, the vials were centrifuged (Sorvall ST 16R, ThermoFisher Scientific, Osterode, Germany) for 10 min at 4700 g and 25 °C, the supernatant was filtered using a 0.22 µm membrane filter (Basix, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), and analyzed using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), as detailed in the chromatographic analyses section (section Chromatographic Analyses), to assess the MN content of both MTX and Ph.

Characterization of MTX and Ph‐Loaded PLGA‐Tipped MN Patches—Solubility Study

A solubility study of both MTX and Ph in PBS was performed according to the reported method,[ 57 ] with minor modifications, for the precise selection of the proper medium composition to achieve the “sink conditions” for the in vitro release studies. In brief, an excess of each compound (10 mg) was transferred to an Eppendorf containing 0.5 mL PBS (1X, pH = 7.4). The tubes were allowed to rotate for 24 h at room temperature in a mini tube rotator (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Subsequently, the tubes were centrifuged in a microcentrifuge (TOMY MX‐200; TOMY Kogyo Co., Tokyo, Japan) at 10 000 rpm for 10 min at 25 °C. The supernatant was filtered using a 0.22 µm membrane filter (Basix, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), properly diluted, and analyzed via HPLC (section Chromatographic Analyses) to determine the saturation solubility of both compounds in PBS.

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Disease Induction

A psoriasis mouse model was established by daily application of 5% w/w IMQ cream (Aldara P, Bausch Health, Québec, Canada), for 7 consecutive days. A quantity of 62.5 mg of the cream was evenly applied and gently rubbed every morning over an area of 2 × 3 cm on the shaved back of the mice. Psoriasis‐like symptoms began to appear on the third day of application. Vaseline was applied to a group of healthy mice, as a control, at the same time and in the same manner. One day before IMQ/Vaseline application, the dorsal skin of the mice was shaved and depilated with Nair cream (Church & Dwight Canada Corp., Ontario, Canada).

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Study Design and Treatment

Mice were randomly divided into seven groups (four mice per group, except the healthy control (n = 3)), as follows: 1) The healthy control group: received Vaseline only (no treatment). 2) Disease model group: received IMQ only (no treatment). 3) IMQ/MTX/SC group: received IMQ + SC MTX. 4) IMQ/MTX/MN group: received IMQ + MTX‐loaded MN patch. 5) IMQ/Ph/SC group: received IMQ + SC Ph. 6) IMQ/Ph/MN group: received IMQ + Ph‐loaded MN patch. 7) IMQ/blank/MN group: received IMQ + blank MN patch. All MN groups received only one MN patch, prepared with the highest initial drug concentration (75 mg mL−1) and applied on day 3 of the IMQ application. The patches were applied under anesthesia (3% isoflurane; Fresenius Kabi, Ontario, Canada) using the spring‐loaded applicator for 15 min and were kept in place for an additional 45 min before removal of the patch backings, to ensure the complete separation of the tips. For the SC groups, the mice received the same doses of MTX and Ph as the MN groups, divided into two injections administered on days 3 and 5 of the IMQ application. MTX was injected in saline, while Ph was dissolved in DMSO and diluted with saline before injection. Dose division in the SC groups was attributed to the short half‐lives of both compounds following parenteral injections, limiting the therapeutic window against this disease model as previously reported.[ 59a ]

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI)

Disease development and progression were monitored by assessing the skin scaling, thickness, and erythema throughout the study period, as previously described.[ 63 ] The three parameters were assessed every alternate day and were scored independently from 0 to 4 based on the severity: 0 (none); 1 (mild); 2 (moderate); 3 (severe); and 4 (very severe). The thickness of skin folds in three different locations of the skin was measured using a digital caliper and the average was calculated.

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Body Weight Measurement

The body weight of the mice was measured daily, and weight reduction was closely monitored. Besides, the mice were observed for any signs of inflammation and/or infection at the MN application and SC injection sites.

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Histology and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

On day 8 of the IMQ application, the mice were euthanized, and skin samples from the application sites of the IMQ and the various treatments were collected, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, 4‐µm sections were prepared, and stained with H&E for histological assessment, and with anti‐Ki67 and anti‐CD3 antibodies for IHC analyses. Major organs (spleen, heart, liver, kidney, and lung) were also collected, fixed in 10% buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and stained with H&E for safety evaluation. All sections were observed under the stereomicroscope and micrographs were recorded. Histology images were analyzed using Zen software to measure epidermal thickness, while IHC images were analyzed using ImageJ software (version 1.54, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) to quantify Ki67 and CD3‐stained cells. Ki67 expression and the number of CD3‐stained cells in skin tissue were quantified in three randomly selected microscopic sections per mouse.

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Enzyme‐Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

To evaluate the cytokine levels in the skin samples, 50 mg of the flash‐frozen skin from each mouse was homogenized using a Tissue‐Tearor homogenizer (PowerGen 700, Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) in 1 mL tissue protein extraction reagent in the presence of 1X protease inhibitor cocktail. Homogenization was done for 30 sec on ice and the samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 20 000 g and 4 °C to precipitate the tissue debris. The collected supernatants were aliquoted and used for total protein quantification using BCA assay and subsequently for measuring TNF‐α, IL‐6, and IL‐17A levels using the corresponding mouse ELISA kits, following the manufacturer's protocols.

In Vivo Antipsoriatic Efficacy Study—Blood Biochemical Analysis

On day 8, blood was collected via intracardiac puncture using 25 G needles and allowed to coagulate for 30 min at room temperature. To separate the serum, the blood samples were centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 g and 4 °C. The serum was then used to evaluate the AST, ALT, and blood urea levels. The analysis was performed by the veterinary diagnostic center at the Université de Montréal.

All separations were performed using a Shimadzu 2030C 3D plus HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a photodiode array detector. Data acquisition and analysis were done using LabSolutions Lite software (version 5.92, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan).

Statistical Analyses

GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA) was used for data processing and analysis throughout the whole study. Statistical analyses were performed using the student's t‐test and analysis of variance (ANOVA; one and two‐way) followed by Tukey's post‐hoc multiple‐comparison test. All results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of ≥3 replicates. Statistically, p < 0.05 was considered significant and marked using *, as follows: (*) p < 0.05, (**) p < 0.01, (***) p < 0.001, and (****) p < 0.0001.
