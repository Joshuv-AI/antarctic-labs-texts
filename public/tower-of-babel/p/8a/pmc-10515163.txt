Mixed, nonclassical behavior in a classic allosteric protein
Authors: Paul J. Sapienza, Jeffrey P. Bonin, H.P. Dinusha Jinasena, Kelin Li, Henry Dieckhaus, Konstantin I. Popov, Jeffrey Aubé, Andrew L. Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceA major unresolved problem in structural biology today is how proteins achieve allostery, where ligand binding of a protein at one site alters activity at a distant site. Allosteric regulation is widespread in cells and is of great interest for protein and drug design. Classic models of allostery employ switching between two conformations, but in classical allosteric systems (e.g., with subunit symmetry), the allosteric conformational dynamics have not been observed in detail. Here, using NMR spectroscopy in the enzyme chorismate mutase, we observe coordinated switching of residues throughout much, but not all, of the protein. The observed dynamics share some features of the classic models, yet show that the allosteric mechanisms are likely to be more complex in many systems.

Allostery lies at the heart of biological regulation. Originally proposed for symmetric, multisubunit enzymes, allosteric control of protein activity has been thought to derive from switching between two conformations, with one more active than the other (1, 2). In the Monod–Wyman–Changeux (MWC) model, the more active R (“relaxed”) conformation can interconvert spontaneously with the less active T (“tense”) conformation in the absence or presence of ligands. Consistent with the model, R and T conformations have been confirmed in many allosteric proteins (3–9). However, the switching dynamics between these end states has not been directly observed in detail, and hence questions about ligand-independent switching, the concertedness and synchrony of switching, extent of spontaneous switching, and the role of effectors have gone unanswered in these systems. In addition, some allosteric proteins appear to access three or more conformations (10–14), utilize only dynamics (entropy) and not conformational change to drive allostery (13, 15–17), or reveal the same ligand can act as an agonist in some contexts and as antagonists in others (18), raising the question of whether the simple models are adequate. Many of these finer details were revealed by NMR and its unique ability to provide site-specific structural and dynamic information (19). In the last decade, an ensemble allostery model (EAM) (20, 21) was put forth to encompass classic models and explain the expanded repertoire described above. Despite this powerful framework and toolkit, we are still generally unable to design allostery or predict mutations in existing proteins that will add allosteric features or modulate function in a rational way. This underscores the need for deep study of multiple systems so that themes may emerge, and design goals may be achieved.

Chorismate mutase, CM, is a symmetric homodimer, possessing all the classical hallmarks of an allosterically regulated enzyme, with multiple inputs influencing its activity (22, 23). It lies in the aromatic amino acid biosynthetic pathway, catalyzing the conversion of chorismic acid to prephenate, which serves as a branch point between the synthesis of Tyr/Phe and Trp. Activities of apo and Tyr-bound CM have a sigmoidal dependence on substrate concentration, indicating homotropic subunit cooperativity and physical communication between the two active sites 45 Å apart. CM activity is feedback regulated by its allosteric effector molecules Tyr (or Phe) and Trp, which inhibit and activate catalysis, respectively, largely through effects on Km (23, 24). Both amino acids bind to the same pair of effector-binding sites at the homodimer interface located distally from the active sites (Fig. 1). Interestingly, Trp removes homotropic cooperativity between the active sites as evidenced by hyperbolic activity curves (22, 24–27). X-ray crystal structures have shown that the effectors help to drive CM into T and R conformations (28). With its full complement of allosteric features, moderate size, and favorable solution properties, CM represents a rich system for testing dynamic and structural features of classically allosteric proteins that have been difficult to observe due to transient events, instability of specific allosteric states, or challenging NMR properties.

Here, we use methyl-Transverse relaxation optimized spectroscopy (TROSY) (29) NMR spectroscopy to provide extensive detail on allosteric mechanisms. One impediment to defining the allosteric dynamics of CM is that it has been unclear whether apoCM or TrpCM is T- or R-like, since no structures of these wild-type forms exist (28). By establishing clear methyl footprints for T and R states, we show here that, unexpectedly, both are in the T conformation. By following NMR line broadening using the relaxation dispersion technique, we observe large-scale, synchronous switching between T and R conformations as a dynamic equilibrium, but only when allosteric activator is present. In the absence of effector, the enzyme undergoes a more muted, partial—yet related—switching motion to an alternate state. The clarity of T and R methyl CM spectra and the ability to modulate structure and dynamics with allosteric effectors yield a high-resolution picture of dynamics that both confirm elements of the classical two-state models while at the same time revealing more complex features such as mixed T:R states and an allosteric “model” that apparently changes depending on whether activator is present or not. Collectively, we show that this classical allosteric enzyme does not adhere to classical models, but rather requires other, nonbinary models (e.g., EAM) to explain the body of data.

Results

Chemical Shift Markers of CM Allosteric States.

The conformational variabilities of CM were monitored by NMR observation of protonated isoleucine-δ, leucine-δ, and valine-γ (ILV) methyl groups in an otherwise deuterated labeling background. The 1H-13C HMQC spectra of apo and Tyr-bound CM overlay nearly perfectly, indicating they occupy the same conformational state (Fig. 2A). This shows that apoCM resides in the T conformation since the TyrCM spectrum is a reference for this state. To provide additional reference, T226I-CM bound to Trp (TrpCMT226I) activator was compared to apoCM. TrpCMT226I was discovered as a constitutively active CM variant (30) that crystallized into the R conformation (28), and the distinct positions of its methyl peaks are consistent with it being in a different conformation from T (Fig. 2B). As a further validation of the R conformation methyl spectrum, TrpCMT226I was compared to a ternary CM complex bound to both Trp and an active site transition state inhibitor (TSI) (31, 32) (Figs. 1A and 2C), which adopts the “super-R” (sRCM) conformation(28). Although these two spectra do not overlay perfectly, there is a much closer resemblance of TrpCMT226I to the super-R complex than the T conformation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), consistent with TrpCMT226I adopting the R conformation. These comparisons highlight how clearly methyl-TROSY spectra can identify distinct allosteric conformations and underscore the value of NMR chemical shift markers of structure.

The R-state has a distinct HMQC fingerprint as evidenced by significant differences between spectra of apoCM and the constitutively active T226I mutant bound to Trp (B). The R-form is similar to the tryptophan-TSI bound super-R state (C and SI Appendix, Fig. S1).

Unexpected Structural State of Activator-Bound CM.

Surprisingly, the methyl spectrum of TrpCM overlays very well with the T conformation (Fig. 3A). Simple allosteric theory predicts for an activating effector like Trp to favor the R conformation, but the tight overlay with the apo (and Tyr-bound) form, especially when compared with the R fingerprint, clearly indicates that the T form dominates. Thus, both apoCM and TrpCM are in the T conformation in solution. How does Trp dramatically stabilize substrate binding if the unbound structure is virtually the same as the inhibited form? During the course of Trp additions monitored by 1H-15N TROSY-HSQC, we noticed that upon saturation a number of amide peaks disappeared, most extensively in helices 2, 8, 11, and 12 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Closer inspection of the TrpCM methyl HMQC spectra showed that many peaks appeared broadened relative to apo or TyrCM, suggesting extensive dynamics.

Locations of several R-state resonances are shown by gray arrows indicating R chemical shifts are not observed. TrpCM stands out as having significantly more conformational exchange on the µs–ms timescale (ΔR2, see Methods) than the other CM states (B). Residues in the EBR are highlighted within dashed boxes.

To probe dynamics on the µs–ms timescale underlying the broadened TrpCM spectra, we acquired methyl 1H single-quantum (SQ) Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill (CPMG) relaxation dispersion (33) and 13C Chemical Exchange Saturation Transfer (CEST) (34) experiments. These experiments (conducted at 15 °C to optimize fitting, see Methods) detect sampling of alternative states (or “excited” states) in dynamic equilibrium with the dominant ground conformation. For processes in the optimal timescale window, the relaxation data can be quantitatively fit to extract the populations for states A and B (pA, pB), the exchange rate between them (kex = kA-B + kB-A), and the difference in their chemical shifts (Δω = |ωB – ωA|). The latter can be especially informative when chemical shifts are known for specific conformational states. For TrpCM, both 1H methyl CPMG and 13C methyl CEST experiments showed a high level of conformational exchange on a ~1 ms timescale (Fig. 4). In the 1H CPMG dispersion dataset, ~60 methyl groups in ~47 residues showed significant curvature (a flat dispersion “curve” indicates lack of motion on the relevant timescale), with 36 methyl groups exhibiting “Rex” values (maximum − minimum R2,eff) > 10 s−1. I27 and I225 methyl groups show particularly large amplitude dispersion curves. In the 13C CEST dataset, not only can the same three dynamic parameters be extracted, but the sign of the 13C Δω is also obtained since the precise chemical shift of the excited state can be directly observed. In agreement with the 1H CPMG data, the 13C CEST profiles show large amplitude dynamics, and in some cases very large chemical shift changes are clear (Fig. 4). Excellent quality fits were obtained for both datasets, analyzed independently. Data were fitted both locally (individual methyl groups) and as a collective global motion for 24 residues (31 methyl groups) that could be fit as a group (Fig. 4, full fitting results in SI Appendix, Table S1). The global kex value is in excellent agreement between 1H and 13C datasets, with kex = 670 s−1. The populations differed between 1H and 13C relaxation datasets, yielding a global excited state population from the 1H dataset of 7.7% (13C-CEST derived populations −4.5%-differ due to buffer components, see SI Appendix, Supplemental Discussion). Importantly, even in the global fits, Δω values are fitted locally as they are unique to each methyl site.

The first three rows are for methyl groups that were fit globally with a single kex and single population of high-energy, minor state (pB), albeit with different values for the two CM forms. Note the amplitudes of the 1H CPMG curves and distance of the “dips” from the main peaks in the 13C CEST profiles are greater for TrpCM than apoCM, reflecting a higher population of the minor state and larger chemical shift changes between major and minor states for TrpCM. The last row gives representative probes from the EBR (I52) or the active site (V197) that were fit locally with different kex values.

Direct Observation of T to R Switching of TrpCM in the Absence of Substrate.

Since TrpCM is primarily in the T conformation, we hypothesized that the dynamic process detected in these relaxation experiments corresponds to transient switching to the R conformation, a process which has yet to be directly observed for a classically allosteric protein at high-resolution. Testing this hypothesis requires correlation of the dynamic, methyl-specific Δω parameters to the corresponding known difference in chemical shifts between the T and R conformations, Δδ. Methyl assignments for Ile and Thr residues are known for the T conformation (under different conditions) (22, 35), and therefore Ile/Leu/Val assignments were acquired for our samples. The methyl assignment strategy (described in SI Appendix) employed a combination of backbone assignments, methyl correlation experiments, Nuclear Overhauser Effect Spectroscopy (NOESY) analysis, and mutations. For the R conformation, assignments were made on TrpCMT226I for ILV methyl types. For the methyl residues that could be well-fit in the global set, the correlation plot of Δω (from relaxation dispersion/CEST) with Δδ (from assigned chemical shifts) yielded highly linear correlations for both 1H and 13C chemical shift differences (Fig. 5 A–D). This is a clear indication that for TrpCM, residues in the global set are switching directly and in a synchronous manner to the R conformation at a population of ~8%. Moreover, the substrate is not needed to sample the R conformation. The linear correlation of shift differences also validates the TrpCMT226I variant of CM to be an excellent model for the R conformation. We note that even with the very high quality of methyl relaxation profiles, it was not necessary to employ three-state fits, suggesting structural intermediates in the timescale window transparent to CPMG or CEST are not connected to T–R switching.

Deviations from T to R Dynamics in the Active Site and EBR (Effector-Binding Domain).

While the linear correlation for the global methyl set points to a simplicity of allosteric dynamics for TrpCM, there are numerous sites that switch on different timescales and/or to a different end state. Many are in the EBR (discussed below) and in sites near Trp, and a few other sites in the catalytic domain are evident based on the dispersion curve fitting results (Fig. 5C). To gain the broadest view of T–R vs. non-T–R switching motions from the 1H dispersion data, we plotted 1H Rex vs. the 1H chemical shift difference (Chemical Shift Perturbation or CSP) between T and R conformations (Fig. 5E). The extensive T–R set of residues cluster in a central group, and two clear sets of outliers are seen above (red) and below (magenta) the line, showing regions of significant deviation from universal T–R switching. With red and magenta points corresponding to residues in the EBR and active site, respectively, further consideration was given to these ligand-binding regions.

In the active site, L19, V20, and V197 all show lowered Rex relative to the T–R set (Fig. 5 E and F), reflecting an active site that is not fully participating in the T–R motion. One likely reason for deviation at the active site is substrate access. From the T (2 csm) and R (1 csm) structures, and especially from the sRCM structure with TSI bound (3 csm), the active site is occluded and must open to allow substrate entry. Thus, observation of alternate dynamics at the active site may be important for proper function. V197 switches at a much faster rate of 6,000 s−1 (10× the rate of T–R switching) and is located at the most likely position for gating access to substrate. V20 was included in the global T–R set by virtue of similar kinetics and agreement of the 13C Δω; however, the 1H Δω agrees poorly with the T–R CSP (Fig. 5A). Its relaxation properties are therefore affected by some different process. Finally, based on its T–R CSP, L19 1H dispersion is predicted to reflect T–R switching with Rex ~ 9 s−1, but Rex is ~0, again, showing deviant behavior at this location. So, while sampling of the R state in the absence of active site ligand is consistent with a conformational selection model (MWC), the active site configuration and dynamics reported here require partial induced fit as well.

In contrast to the active site, the EBR in TrpCM exhibits enhanced Rex values and µs–ms motion. From the EBR, five methyl groups were included in the global fit described above that report on the T–R conformational switch: L64δ2, I74, I90, I103, and L104 (Fig. 5), with the latter two sites at the boundary between the EBR and the main domain. The remaining 7 EBR ILV residues either show µs–ms switching on a faster (L50, I52, L67) or slower (I98) timescale (SI Appendix, Table S1), show no µs–ms motion (L60), or were completely broadened away or not assignable (L95) for TrpCM. Overall, these data reveal an EBR that has more complex dynamic behavior than the catalytic domain and, as with the active site, deviates from a simple two-state conformational allosteric switch, although with several methyl groups included in the global T–R set, the EBR µs–ms dynamics are partially coupled to the Trp-activated T–R motion. Together with the active site deviation from T–R switching, these additional motions suggest allosteric dynamics of greater complexity than the binary models (MWC, KNF, and combinations thereof) that rely on two conformations. Such additional degrees of freedom may allow for more independent control by different inputs (e.g., effector binding, distant substrate binding).

Mixed TR State Shows Subunits Switch Independently in the Presence of Trp.

The T to R equilibrium observed in TrpCM is one hallmark of the MWC model. However, because TrpCM has a 92% population in the low-affinity T form, the activity curve is expected to retain a sigmoidal shape if substrate binding and the associated stabilization of the R state results in a mandatory change to R in the opposite subunit. Yet as previously shown by us (27) and multiple groups (22–25), Trp produces a fully hyperbolic curve (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We envision two ways to reconcile this paradox: 1) The R-state sampled in the presence of Trp is incidental and does not have an intrinsically higher affinity for substrate, or 2) Trp binding uncouples the two active sites such that the two subunits switch independently in the presence and absence of substrate. Scenario one is less likely due to the dramatically different electrostatic environments of the T vs. R active sites, in which the latter is expected to make binding of the highly charged substrate significantly more favorable (36). Turning to the uncoupling hypothesis, one way to differentiate T:T–R:T vs. T:T–R:R equilibria in the absence of substrate, is to examine Δω values for residues at the subunit interface showing exchange. Such probes could sense the status of the opposite subunit. Perfect adherence to the lines of unit slope in Fig. 5 would suggest concerted switching of the two subunits, and deviation would imply a mixed excited state, possibly T:R. Unfortunately, only two main domain ILV probes are within 6 Å of the opposite chain; I148, which is perfectly overlapped with another resonance, and V211. V211 shows similar kinetics with the global T–R group and its 13C Δω is on the line in Fig. 5B. However, the V211 1H Δω is one of the largest outliers in Fig. 5A, consistent with independent switching of the subunits. Additional evidence for mixed TR states comes from monitoring the midpoint of a TSI titration, in which two major peaks for each ILV probe are observed, one in the same position of the symmetrical starting material (TrpCM), and the other overlapped with the symmetrical TSI saturated state (Fig. 6A). The former is likely comprised of two states, T:T and T:SR, and the latter derived from SR:SR and T:SR. These same spectra show a third, low-intensity peak positioned precisely where the R state resonance is expected (Fig. 6). Parsimony suggests this minor state represents T:SR to R:SR exchange in the empty subunit of the singly bound state, as is observed in the substrate-free enzyme, albeit with a slower exchange rate. ZZ-exchange(37) spectra directly confirm the exchange between the empty subunit of the major T:SR state with a minor R:SR state (Fig. 6) and prove the assertion of major peak composition above. Based on these data, the global T–R switching (Figs. 4 and 5) appears to reflect independent T–R conversion of the TrpCM protomers (T:T to T:R). Clearly this is at odds with the MWC model. Collectively, the evidence for mixed states and subunit uncoupling in CM provides a solution to the paradox of a highly skewed T to R equilibrium and a hyperbolic binding curve.

Mechanistic Insights into the T to R Switch from 13C Chemical Shifts.

What is the nature of the T–R conformational switch, and are there identifiable underlying dynamic processes? The differences between T and R structures of CM have been described (28), but the differences in tertiary structure are subtle (backbone rmsd = 1.2 Å, 1 csm vs. 2 csm) when assessed after aligning a single subunit (Fig. 1B). Yet, the large change in methyl peak positions (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S1) show a high sensitivity to the modest structural changes, and this is confirmed from further analysis of side-chain chemical shifts. An advantage of ILV methyl NMR is that 13C chemical shifts can be used to determine populations of specific rotamers (38, 39). In the catalytic domain, we observe clear rotamer differences in T (apo, Trp) and R (TrpCMT226I) states of CM for I27, I225, and L230, which are clustered together as part of the hydrophobic core in the catalytic domain (SI Appendix, Table S2). The populations of gauche–rotamers go from 85/63/92% in the T forms to 0/11/13% in R (TrpCMT226I), respectively. A critical role of I225 in the T–R transition is supported by the observation that the I225T/T226I double mutant is no longer constitutively active, indicating the effect of Ile226 is dependent on an Ile225 context (26). Somewhat smaller changes in rotamer populations are also found for I26 and I237. Thus, individual side-chain rotamer switching is one of the clearest structural indicators—and convenient to observe by NMR—of the allosteric conformational change. Rotamer analysis of the T (apoCM, TrpCM) and R (TrpCMT226I) states by molecular dynamics (MD) supported this interpretation, as clustering the trajectories by rotamer chi angles resulted in strong overlap between apoCM and TrpCM, while TrpCMT226I remained well separated in rotamer space (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Dynamic rotamer averaging in ILV side chains is a ubiquitous feature in globular proteins and it generally occurs on a much faster timescale (ps–ns) (40–43) than TrpCM T–R interconversion (milliseconds). Although it is formally possible that the rotamer wells for these side chains are exceptionally stable and rotamer switching is the committed step in the large-scale conformational change, methyl order parameters suggest otherwise. Boehr and coworkers reported low methyl S2axis values for I27, I225, and I237 (0.49, 0.24, 0.4 respectively) (44), and the magnitude of these values suggest significant rotamer averaging on the ps–ns timescale (45, 46). Therefore, we hypothesize that the localized nature of rotamer shifting may be on the path toward, but not sufficient for the T–R switch, and serve as a link between low-energy transitions that frequently occur on the ps–ns timescale and the slower, high-barrier transitions of tertiary and quaternary structures. This is consistent with MD, since TrpCM undergoes some T–R rotamer transitions by ILV residues such as I27, and L230, as well as by critical non-ILV markers of the switch. However, tertiary and quaternary metrics do not indicate global T–R transition on the timescales of the simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Conformational Landscape is Fundamentally Changed without Activator.

Given that TrpCM exhibits T–R switching at equilibrium in the absence of active site ligands, we thought apoCM might show similar dynamics, albeit with a lower population of the high-energy R-state. 1H CPMG relaxation dispersion and 13C CEST datasets were acquired for apoCM under matched conditions. In contrast to TrpCM, the methyl groups of apoCM exhibited a much lower degree of dynamics on the µs–ms timescale (Figs. 2B and 4 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). Significant 1H Rex is observed in ~28 methyl groups of ~23 residues, with much lower dispersion magnitudes (Fig. 3B): only 10 methyl groups exhibited 1H Rex > 10 s−1 in contrast to 36 for TrpCM. Relaxation dispersion curves from 16 residues could be fit as a single group to kex = 390 s−1 and pb = 3.2%, and 10 of those residues also undergo synchronized T–R motions in TrpCM, mostly in the catalytic domain (e.g., I27, L104, I129, L205, V211, I225, I233). However, fitting of Δω values and plotting vs. ΔδT–R show a poor correlation with R chemical shifts, with some methyl groups showing agreement but key core residues (above) sampling other conformations with chemical shifts closer to T than those sampled in TrpCM (Fig. 5 A and C, Insets and SI Appendix, Fig. S6). The conformational change associated with the apo high-energy state is not only lower in magnitude than the T–R transition, but it is also more limited in scope. Whereas large chemical shift differences between T and R are distributed throughout most of the molecule, the chemical shift differences between the ground and excited states of apoCM are essentially restricted to the EBR and a portion of the dimer interface (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Furthermore, the apo Δω values do not correlate with, and on the whole, exceed the magnitude of difference between the apo and TrpCM ground states (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) as shown previously (47). Considering both 1H and 13C relaxation data, we note that only ~5 methyl groups show consistency with T–R switching in apoCM, which is ~20% of the participation in TrpCM. Additionally, the small (and negative) 13C Δω values of I27, I225, and L230 show these methyl groups do not sample alternative rotamers in the excited state as is observed in TrpCM. Thus, unlike TrpCM, apoCM switches to some distinct alternate conformation, of which a low minority of residues have R character. In support of this, MD shows apoCM lacks the dihedral flexibility on the ps–ns timescale that was shown to be “on path” to the T–R switch in TrpCM (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). However, because this major slow motion is mostly composed of the TrpCM T–R group, the collectivity and interresidue couplings are innate to the enzyme, and Trp binding strengthens and grows this “proto-TR” movement group. Additional faster motions (1,500 s−1, pb 5.8%) are seen for four methyl groups from within the EBR and V245, and three additional EBR methyl groups (V42, I52, I69) could only be fit locally. Taken together, these data show that apoCM has a predisposition to undergo switching by a not-fully formed (relative to T–R group) movement group but does not access the R state, and that binding of tryptophan fundamentally changes the landscape and interconnects the catalytic domain to execute clean T-to-R switching. At the same time, the active sites are coupled in the apo enzyme and decoupled by Trp based on their different activity curves. Interestingly, TyrCM also lies on this dynamics–activity continuum in that the µs-ms motions of the main domain and EBR of the inhibited form are even more restricted than ApoCM (Fig. 3B). Enhanced dynamics in activator-bound enzymes has been seen in other systems although presumably to facilitate flux (48, 49). In this case the dynamics were examined in the context of how activator modulates conformational dynamics between allosteric states.

Discussion

Despite its central importance as “the second secret of life” (50, 51), allostery remains remarkably enigmatic. The clearest cases of regulated conformational switching come from symmetric homooligomers, but even in these cases there are major questions regarding the execution of conformational change, the sampling of T and R (or other) states, and how these are modulated by regulatory effectors and the sequential binding of substrate. It is also now appreciated that there is significant allosteric energy, independent of conformational change, in the form of conformational entropy. For these reasons NMR, with its ability to monitor structure, interconversion between structure, and even conformational entropy, has emerged as a critical tool to test and develop allosteric models.

Here, using methyl-TROSY NMR, we show that the catalytic domain and part of the EBR of CM undergoes the type of synchronized switching between T and R conformations present in the MWC model, but only upon addition of Trp effector. Even though the dynamic equilibrium in the absence of substrate excludes KNF and evokes key elements of MWC, or “binary” allosteric models, several features are inconsistent with such a pure concerted mechanism. First, in TrpCM where a dynamic equilibrium exists, much of the EBR and key parts of the active site move asynchronously with the catalytic domain and/or do not switch to R. Second, the Trp-activated T–R switching does not explain the hyperbolic functional curve in the context of MWC; rather, some sort of uncoupling is required (e.g., mixed T:R states) and we observed this here. Third, apoCM does not sample R, but rather some alternate conformation. Last, in effector-free CM, we previously showed that binding a substrate analog in one protomer converts that protomer’s structure but not the unbound subunit (27), which is further proof that CM is capable of forming T:R asymmetric dimers.

Having excluded pure MWC and KNF models, the conformational dynamics in CM strongly suggest more complex ensemble-based models are at play. Furthermore, the differential dynamics data of the apo and Trp forms, together with their activity profiles, how that the allosteric model is not constant in all forms. In other words, the conformational repertoire does not just change as a matter of degree depending on the effector status as is the case with MWC enzymes like hemoglobin or aspartate transcarbamylase. In CM, the same input, substrate binding in one subunit, can have completely different effects in the absence or presence of allosteric activator. This has been observed in a dimeric system that toggled between MWC and KNF mechanisms with different ligands (52). This phenomenon in CM is summarized in Fig. 7, where in the presence of tryptophan, we directly observe several states that are consistent with the MWC model, such as an unbound T to R equilibrium, but also observe mixed T:R states. Collectively, this array of behavior is only allowed under a generalized TR allosteric model, or the EAM. Importantly, the EAM employs a subunit coupling term, and our data support a model where Trp not only changes the ensemble to allow sampling of the R state, but also uncouples the two main domains such that the activity profile is hyperbolic (Fig. 7 A and B). By contrast, if effector is removed, the array of states with significant occupancy, and hence the allosteric model, appears completely different. The R-state is not sampled until binding of substrate to the local subunit, which is reminiscent of the KNF model (Fig. 7). However, there is a dynamic equilibrium in the absence of substrate, that, while not R, includes the same key amino acids as those involved in the R-state switch. In addition, removal of the Trp allosteric input manifests as functional coupling between the two active sites and sigmoidal activity (Fig. 7 B and C); however, the precise nature of this coupling remains a mystery aside from CSPs reported by us previously (27). Notably, the scheme in Fig. 7 makes no claims about the role of conformational entropy, which is encompassed by the EAM in the form of stability terms. We acknowledge a likely role of entropy in this system because the EBR has significant disorder, and the degree of disorder is modulated by effector and ligand status (Fig. 3). In fact, a previous study estimating differences in conformational entropy between TyrCM and TrpCM, showed that entropy alone contributes enough free energy to explain differences in activity (44). However, clear and meaningful differences in T vs. R state structures show a significant/dominant structural equilibrium component. This tug of war between multiple large and sometimes opposing free energy terms highlights the fact that nature need not adhere to a script or limited menu of models to achieve allosteric outcomes. Taken together, the variation in structural, dynamic, and allosteric repertoire we see in the apo and Trp forms is not allowed in either a strictly concerted mechanism, the KNF model, or pure dynamic allostery, but is allowed by more complex schemes such as the EAM, and importantly, it does permit interdependent regulation by two different inputs: effector and substrate concentration.

CM does not adhere to a single 2-state allosteric model. In panels A, B, and C, circle = T, and square = R, and filled shape signifies substrate bound, and filled square is sRCM. The T state of the apo and Trp-bound states are shown in different colors because Trp modifies the T-ensemble in ways that include access to the R state (this work) and changes in conformational entropy (44). The CM behavior reported here contrasts with previous observation of entirely asynchronous motion in the allosteric TRAP oligomer (53) and the resolution (in terms of distribution of methyl groups observed and capture of dynamics) of MWC behavior observed in aspartate transcarbamoylase (54, 55). We highlight these other two systems as each, along with CM, is considered classically allosteric in the sense that they are symmetrical oligomers, exhibit homotropic allostery, and respond to effectors. However, the data here show that CM behavior is anything but classic and the fact that these three classical systems reveal three different “mechanisms”, underscore the complexity of the problem, the requirement of ensemble-based models, and the need for studying multiple systems at high resolution.

The uniquely detailed picture of allosteric dynamics presented here answers several questions and raises many others. We anticipate that more critical insights into allostery will be possible in the CM system with many of the tools and reference spectra in place. With the ability to observe global and local allosteric transitions on a residue-by-residue basis and further refinement in experimental design, contemporary NMR complemented by MD and thermodynamic measurements has the potential to tease out many of the important maneuvers allosteric proteins execute to achieve action at a distance.

Materials and Methods

Expression and Purification of CM.

ILV-U-[2H,15N] CM was expressed by transforming BL21*(DE3) cells with pET21a carrying the N-terminal-TEV-cleavable CM gene, and subsequently following the M9+ protocol for isotopic enrichment (56). At the appropriate time, 60 mg of the Ile and 100 mg of the LV precursors were added. See SI Appendix, Methods to match the multiple precursor isotopic labeling formats with the NMR applications. Purification using Ni-affinity chromatography and S200 size exclusion chromatography was as described (27).

Synthesis of TSI.

Synthesis of the TSI was as described previously (27, 32, 57) and the concentration was determined using the NMR-based PULCON (58) approach using spectroscopically quantified trp and tyr solutions as references.

Backbone and ILV Methyl NMR Resonance Assignments.

Detailed methods are provided in SI Appendix, Methods. Briefly, backbone assignments of Apo and TrpCM were obtained by analysis of TROSY versions of standard triple resonance experiments as described previously (27). The backbone of TrpCMT226I was assigned similarly except this mutant proved refractory to a standard high pH/2 M urea-based back exchange protocol, so assignment of the ~20 amides that did not exchange with H2O during purification were note observed. ILV methyl resonance assignments were obtained through a mixture of NOESY NMR experiments, through bond NMR experiments, mutagenesis, and automated assignment routines (SI Appendix, Methods).

2D NMR Spectroscopy and Spin Relaxation.

Protein and ligand concentrations, buffer composition, and other acquisition parameters related to NMR experiments described in this work are reported in SI Appendix, Table S4. ITC determined KD values measured by Gorman et al. (22) were used as a guide to ensure effector-bound samples were saturated. Unless otherwise stated, all spectra were recorded on a Bruker Avance III HD 850 MHz spectrometer with a TCI H-C/N-D 5 mm cryoprobe controlled by Topspin 3.5 pl 7. 1H-15N TROSY experiments were acquired with the trosyf3gpphsi19.2 Topspin pulse program. ILV 1H-13C HMQC spectra were acquired employing a 90°-240°-90° 1H decoupling pulse during t1 (59). A single 2D ZZ-exchange 2D spectrum of TrpCM with 0.5 molar equivalents of TSI relative to subunits was acquired with a 75-ms relaxation time. Binding of the TSI was essentially stoichiometric at NMR concentrations consistent with the published submicromolar IC50 of the inhibitor (57).

1H CPMG Analysis.

Analysis of methyl-TROSY 1H SQ CPMG (33) datasets was performed using ChemEx. Notably, ChemEx accounts for relaxation during the compensation pulses present in the relaxation period of this pulse sequence, making this the most accurate approach for quantitative analysis of these data. For a complete analysis of µs-ms dynamics, full datasets were collected at two magnetic field strengths (850/600 MHz for TrpCM, 850/700 MHz for apoCM). For Rex calculations (Fig. 3), datasets containing the 50 and 2,000 Hz planes were collected at a single field (850 MHz for TyrCM and sRCM). Initial CPMG measurements at 30 °C yielded fits with exchange that was too fast to extract reliable Δω values (60), so we lowered the temperature of acquisition to 15 °C. See SI Appendix, Table S4 for details of data collection. Global fits of full datasets at two fields were first performed on individual methyl groups using a two-state model. These fits faithfully recapitulated the experimental data for all but one methyl group, indicating that more complex kinetic models were not warranted. Methyl groups yielding similar kex values (and pb if well defined) were subsequently fit together. Fit parameter values can be found in SI Appendix, Table S1.

13C CEST Analysis.

Analysis of CHD2 methyl 13C CEST (34) datasets was performed using in-house MATLAB scripts. The acquisition temperature was set to 15 °C to match the CPMG data and provide the added benefit of sharper, more well defined CEST dips (61), translating to more reliable Δω determination. For both apo and Trp, a single dataset was collected at 600 MHz with a 25-Hz spin lock. See SI Appendix, Table S4 for details of data collection. CEST datasets were analyzed independently from the CPMG datasets; however, the methyl groups comprising the concerted motion was carried over from the CPMG analysis to the CEST. The reason for this is that while some of the methyl groups showed large enough 13C Δω’s for the exchange parameters to be well defined from the CEST data alone, many methyl groups did not. Consequently, in the CEST analysis a true global fit was performed only for methyl groups, which gave well-defined exchange parameters in a local fit of the CEST data. For the remainder of the methyl groups which participated in the concerted motion as determined by the 1H CPMG, local fits were performed in which the kex and pb values were fixed to those obtained in the CEST global fit (see SI Appendix, Table S1 for fit parameter values).

CM Activity Assay.

Activity in the presence and absence of 40 uM tryptophan was carried out using the continuous assay in H2O NMR buffer, pH 7.5 (Fig. 7) or pH 6.5 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) 22 °C as described (27).

Molecular Dynamics Simulations.

Molecular dynamics simulations were run in the GROMACS 2020.3 package (62, 63) using the CHARMM3.6 (64) force field. Rotamer chi angles were calculated using the gmx chi command and interhelical angles were calculated using custom python scripts in PyMOL. Principal component analysis was done in the python scikit-learn package with n_components=2. Additional simulation details are presented in SI Appendix, Table S3.
