Evaluation of a flipped classroom approach based on micro-videos in the standardized gastroenterological resident training
Authors: Jing Hu, Qiao Mei, Juan Wu, Qiuyuan Liu, Peipei Zhang, Hao Ding, Lei Jie, Renyu Fan, Wei Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional teaching methods in standardized training for gastroenterology residents face challenges in developing independent clinical abilities and self-learning enthusiasm. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a flipped classroom approach based on micro-videos compared to that of traditional classroom teaching in the training of gastroenterology residents.MethodsThis study included 112 residents at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from January to December 2024. The participants were randomly assigned to the flipped classroom (FC, n = 56) or traditional classroom (TC, n = 56) group. The FC group received micro-video-based pre-class materials and participated in interactive in-class discussions, whereas the TC group received traditional lectures. After 4 weeks, both groups were evaluated via theoretical examinations, clinical skills assessments, and perceived teaching effectiveness questionnaires. The FC group additionally completed a perception survey.ResultsCompared with the TC group, the FC group showed higher scores on case analysis questions (33.61 ± 2.61 vs. 31.43 ± 3.17, p = 0.010) and clinical practice skills (89.23 ± 2.71 vs. 86.11 ± 3.59, p = 0.001). The flipped classroom approach demonstrated superior outcomes in improving clinical practice skills (p = 0.028), learning interest (p = 0.015), self-learning ability (p = 0.023), problem-solving proficiency (p = 0.002), and team collaboration (p < 0.001). Moreover, in excess of 96% of the flipped classroom participants expressed satisfaction with the teaching method, and 94.6% reported improved understanding of the curriculum. However, 32.1% reported increased time demands, and 28.6% reported greater pressure in self-presentation.ConclusionsThe flipped classroom approach based on micro-videos is an effective teaching method for standardized gastroenterology resident training, and is also advantageous for developing clinical skills, self-learning abilities, and team collaboration. Despite some challenges in time management and presentation pressure, the method was well-received by residents and could be considered for broader implementation in medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09803-6.Keywords: Flipped classroom, Micro-videos, Gastroenterology, Standardized resident training, Teaching effectiveness

Background

The complexity of gastroenterological training stems from its requisite comprehensive mastery of multiple disciplines, including anatomical knowledge, physiological mechanisms, pathological processes, specialized gastroenterology, and diagnostic imaging modalities [1]. The multifaceted nature of the digestive system engenders a diverse spectrum of pathologies. The considerable overlap in symptomatology among various gastrointestinal disorders presents significant diagnostic challenges. Furthermore, the field necessitates proficiency in high-precision diagnostic methodologies, including endoscopic procedures, ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Gastroenterologists must be proficient in these techniques and be able to identify the most suitable examination method on the basis of the specific situation of the patients. Therefore, gastroenterologists are expected to have solid medical theoretical knowledge, excellent clinical practice skills, critical thinking abilities, clinical decision-making and problem-solving abilities in the process of diagnosing and treating digestive system diseases [2]. These competencies are developed predominantly through structured residency training programs.

The Chinese standardized residency training framework represents a systematic educational initiative designed to enhance clinical practitioners’ professional competencies and practical expertise [3]. Through the implementation of standardized clinical curricula, residents can acquire the requisite multidisciplinary competencies for junior professional qualifications [4]. However, traditional standardized training methods for residents are mainly based on a teacher-centred model, which leads to a lack of independent clinical abilities and low enthusiasm for self-learning. Moreover, the intricate and critical nature of gastrointestinal pathologies demands enhanced self-directed learning capabilities, comprehensive knowledge acquisition, and acute clinical response competencies [5]. To address these pedagogical challenges, medical educators have explored innovative instructional methodologies that integrate active learning principles, notably the flipped classroom paradigm.

The flipped classroom represents a learner-centred pedagogical model characterized by the inversion of traditional classroom and independent study activities [6]. In contrast to conventional passive learning modalities, this approach emphasizes pre-class autonomous learning, followed by in-class activities focused on higher-order cognitive engagement and collaborative discourse regarding fundamental concepts [7]. A crucial component of this methodology is the pre-class instructional content, which is typically disseminated through various multimedia formats, including narrated presentations, audio podcasts, and micro-video modules. Micro-videos constitute concise instructional segments, typically ranging from several seconds to minutes in duration, designed to optimize learner engagement and information retention. These brief, focused educational units have gained prominence in digital learning environments, where cognitive load management and content accessibility are paramount. The format emphasizes singular conceptual frameworks and associated knowledge elements, enhancing its efficacy in educational contexts.

Prior research has validated the flipped classroom methodology across various medical specialties, including neurosurgery, rheumatology, ophthalmology, and dermatology [8–11]. However, empirical investigations of the implementation of micro-video-based flipped classroom in standardized gastroenterology residency training remain limited. This study aimed to evaluate the pedagogical efficacy of a micro-video-enhanced flipped classroom approach in gastroenterological residency education. Additionally, the investigation sought to assess resident perceptions and attitudes toward this innovative instructional methodology.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as a randomized-controlled, monocentric trial that compared a micro-video-based flipped classroom (FC) approach with traditional didactic teaching in gastroenterology resident training. The trial was conducted at the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University, a nationally accredited residency training centre in China. The study population comprised 112 gastroenterology residents enrolled in standardized training between January and December 2024. Participation was voluntary, with informed consent obtained from all subjects. Baseline demographic and educational characteristics, including age, gender, years of standardized training, and clinical rotation assignments, were documented. Participants were randomized to either the flipped classroom (FC) intervention group or the traditional classroom (TC) control group using computer-generated random number sequences. A complete randomization sequence was created with a 1:1 allocation ratio. An independent research assistant assigned participants using sequential numbers. The FC cohort received instruction through a micro-video-based flipped classroom methodology, whereas the TC cohort received conventional didactic instruction. After 4 weeks of standardized training, the residents were evaluated to compare the effectiveness of the two teaching methods. The study protocol and participant flow are illustrated in Fig. 1.

Faculty composition

The teaching team consisted of a department chair, six teachers of gastroenterology with at least 5 years of training experience, and two teaching secretaries. All members of the teaching staff had valid certification in standardized resident training. The department chair developed the curricular framework of gastroenterology and supervised the implementation of the entire teaching process. The gastroenterology teachers responsible for teaching implementation were the same for both the FC and the TC groups to ensure the consistency of the teaching content and style. After the training, two teaching secretaries administered the summative assessments and evaluations of the residents.

Implementation of teaching

The curriculum aligned with standardized residency training requirements, encompassing both theoretical knowledge and clinical competencies. The flipped classroom method based on micro-videos was used for teaching of the FC group. The teachers of the flipped classroom were responsible for providing micro-videos that included theoretical knowledge and clinical practice skills relevant to gastroenterology. The final versions were approved by the department chair to guarantee alignment with institutional training standards. These micro-videos ranged in length from 5 to 10 min and covered the following contents (Table 1) : (1) Theoretical knowledge of gastroenterology: pathophysiology, diagnosis, and management of common gastrointestinal disorders, including gastroesophageal reflux disease, esophageal cancer, chronic gastritis, peptic ulcer, gastric cancer, colorectal carcinoma, ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, acute pancreatitis, liver cirrhosis, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, and primary liver cancer. (2) Clinical practice skills of gastroenterology: abdominal paracentesis, nasogastric intubation, application of Sengstaken-Blakemore tube, analysis of laboratory results, imaging interpretation of typical digestive system diseases, and endoscopic image diagnosis of typical gastrointestinal diseases.

Micro-videos for the standardized training of gastroenterological residents in the flipped classroom teaching method

Videos were distributed electronically to the FC group one week before the in-person sessions. The residents were then required to view and learn from these micro-videos and make preparations before the in-person session of flipped classroom lesson. During the in-person session of the flipped classroom, the residents were encouraged to summarize key knowledge points which they had learned from the micro-videos. Then, they were given a series of questions, exercises, and case studies related to the contents of micro-videos. The residents in the FC group discussed these issues with their team members. Finally, the teachers communicated with the residents, answered their questions, and guided them to understand the knowledge content correctly (Fig. 2).

The FC protocol and flowchart. FC, flipped classroom

Traditional teaching methods were employed with the TC group, which means the teachers used traditional classroom lectures for teaching. Residents assigned to the TC group received a lecture-based teaching model with PowerPoint presentations and discussion in the classroom. The knowledge contents provided to the residents in the TC group was the same as that in the FC group.

In addition to regular classroom teaching, both cohorts participated in clinical activities, which included history-taking, physical examinations, medical documentations, and procedural skills, under the guidance of teachers during the rotation and training period.

Assessment methods

Objective evaluation: examinations

After four weeks of training in the Department of Gastroenterology, residents in both the FC and TC groups were required to complete standardized assessments as a part of the evaluation. The assessments included examinations of theoretical knowledge and clinical practice skills, each scored on a 100-point scale.

The theoretical examination, drawn from the institution’s validated question bank, included multiple-choice items (60%) and case-based problems (40%). The theoretical assessment included four case-based analysis modules, each consisting of five questions (each question scored on a 2-point scale) to evaluate diagnostic reasoning, evidence application, and clinical decision-making. The answers were written, and the scoring was performed without revealing the group. Clinical skills assessment evaluated core procedural competencies in gastroenterology through structured observation. Finally, all assessments were proctored by faculty members, and the scores were recorded by the teaching secretaries.

Perceptions of teaching effectiveness

After completing the objective examinations, residents in both the FC and TC groups evaluated the effectiveness of the teaching methods received by each group. The evaluation was conducted through anonymous questionnaires across multiple domains: theoretical knowledge acquisition, clinical skill development, learning engagement, self-directed learning capacity, problem-solving ability, and team collaboration. Each item was assessed using a three-point scale, in which the classifications “unfavorable”, “favorable”, and “more favorable” denoted different levels of potential evaluations [12]. A higher score of the questionnaire represented a better evaluation of perceived teaching effectiveness.

Evaluation of perceptions and satisfaction in the FC group

The residents in the FC group were required to complete an anonymous questionnaire to survey their perceptions and satisfactions with the flipped classroom teaching method. The questionnaire comprised ten items rated on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree), which focused on respondents’ opinions regarding the flipped classroom, improvement of abilities through training, and expectations regarding the teaching method [10]. Higher scores indicated more positive perceptions and higher levels of satisfaction with respect to the flipped classroom.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (Version 23.0, IBM, Armonk, NY, United States). Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as means (M) ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using independent t-tests. Chi-Square test was performed to compare categorical data between groups, while ranked data were compared with Wilcoxon rank sum test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethics

The study protocol adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (Approval number: PJ2023-11-56).

The FC cohort demonstrated higher overall theoretical examination scores (Mean = 90.98, SD = 2.14) compared to the TC cohort (Mean = 89.16, SD = 1.61, p = 0.002). Analysis of specific assessment components revealed comparable performance in multiple-choice questions assessing knowledge retention (FC: Mean = 52.29, SD = 4.73; TC: Mean = 51.89, SD = 1.43; p = 0.651). In general, none of the residents disagreed or strongly disagreed with the statement that the flipped classroom was a good teaching method. In addition, the methodology received unanimous positive endorsement, with 96.4% of participants expressing satisfaction (76.8% strongly agreed, 19.6% agreed). High levels of engagement were also reported, with 82.1% of the participants stating that the flipped classroom teaching method was interesting (62.5% strongly agreed, 19.6% agreed). The majority of FC group residents agreed that the content of flipped classroom was well organized (58.9% strongly agreed, 25.0% agreed) and this new teaching method deepened their understanding of curriculum (82.1% strongly agreed, 12.5% agreed). More than 90% of the residents agreed or strongly agreed that the flipped classroom provided more self-learning opportunities (85.6% strongly agreed, 5.4% agreed) and enhanced communication skills (69.6% strongly agreed, 21.4% agreed).

With respect to implementation challenges, 83.9% (55.3% strongly agreed, 28.6% agreed) of participants appreciated the spatial and temporal flexibility of the flipped classroom approach. However, the degree of satisfaction decreased when the participants reported that the flipped classroom occupied too much of their spare time (10.7% strongly agreed, 21.4% agreed). Similarly, 28.6% (12.5% strongly agreed, 16.1% agreed) of these residents perceived that the flipped classroom method led to more pressure in self presentation and team discussions.

Discussion

The standardized residency training program represents a crucial component in the development of competent medical specialists in China. The field of gastroenterology presents particular pedagogical challenges due to its complex pathologies, frequent emergency situations, and sophisticated diagnostic methodologies. Therefore, new teaching methods need to be explored according to the stringent training requirements for gastroenterology residents. This study evaluated the effectiveness of a micro-video-based flipped classroom model in the standardized training of gastroenterology residency. The findings demonstrated that this teaching method is an effective and well received modality for medical education in gastroenterology.

Quantitative analysis revealed superior performances in both theoretical knowledge and clinical competency assessments among FC participants compared to those in the TC cohort. These findings align with the literature on flipped classroom implementation in medical education. The study from Sierra et al. suggests that, compared with traditional teaching methods, the flipped classroom method in the cardiology residency training curriculum exhibited improved assessment scores among the trainees [13]. Similarly, study integrating massive open online courses (MOOCs) with FC approaches in radiation oncology has demonstrated statistically significant improvements in both theoretical comprehension and procedural competencies [12]. Yang Li and others explored the effects of a micro-video-based flipped classroom teaching model on the standardized training of residents in dermatology and found that the scores of the theoretical knowledge and clinical practice skills tests of the FC group were significantly higher than those of the TC group [11]. However, our study presented comparable scores of the two groups in the multiple-choice questions, which focused mainly on the memorization of knowledge points. Previous investigations have indicated that some students experiencing in traditional teaching methods do not actively seek information for themselves. Instead, they focus on learning to pass exams and achieve high grades [14]. The mean score of the FC group was higher than that of the TC group in term of the case analysis questions that required problem-solving skills. This is consistent with studies that have suggested that the flipped classroom had the abilities to improve academic performance in courses that focus on problem-solving skills, such as medicine and pharmacy [15, 16]. This differential performance pattern suggests that the flipped classroom methodology particularly enhances higher-order cognitive skills that are essential for clinical practice. In this study, residents in the FC group performed better in the clinical practice skill assessments than those in the TC group. A review revealed that the skill test scores of the flipped classroom students were generally higher than those of the traditional classroom students. These results suggest that the positive learning outcomes achieved using the flipped classroom model have the potential to translate into clinical practice skill enhancement [17]. Our findings contribute to the literature by specifying the potential advantages of the micro-video-based FC approach. While the overall effect on knowledge acquisition was modest, the FC method demonstrated a more pronounced advantage in clinical skills assessments. This suggests that the FC approach may be particularly beneficial for transferring declarative knowledge into procedural competencies, which is a key gap in traditional gastroenterology training. Future implementation could therefore focus on aligning FC design with the intended learning outcomes, particularly for procedural and applied clinical tasks. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the four-week course duration and immediate post-course assessment may not fully capture the potential long-term benefits of the flipped classroom approach, as deep learning typically requires extended time to develop and consolidate. Accordingly, future studies should employ longitudinal assessments of knowledge retention, direct patient outcome measures and cost-benefit analyses of faculty time investment to determine the superiority of this teaching method.

The assessment of the educational outcomes revealed that while both pedagogical approaches effectively conveyed theoretical knowledge, the flipped classroom model demonstrated superior results with respect to developing clinical competencies, fostering learner engagement, promoting self-directed learning, enhancing problem-solving capabilities, and facilitating team collaboration. These findings corroborate previous research highlighting the efficacy of the FC methodology in developing critical thinking skills and autonomous learning capabilities among medical residents. De-Yang Y et al. reported that the flipped classroom teaching method based on massive open online courses significantly enhanced the learning ability, problem-solving capability, and teamwork communication skills of oncology radiology residents [12]. Juan Zhang et al. reported that when a flipped classroom approach based on ADDIE (analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation) model was used, the critical thinking capability and self-learning ability of residents increased [18]. These encouraging outcomes may be attributed to various factors. For instance, flipped classroom combined with micro-videos was a student-centred approach and provided opportunities for residents to study independently with flexible learning time. Furthermore, collaborative discussions with team members and teachers improved problem-solving ability and team collaboration among the participants [19, 20]. The between-group differences varied across domains, with the smallest effect observed in theoretical knowledge and the largest in team collaboration. In clinical training, even modest gains may matter. A small knowledge increase could still improve clinical decision-making, while the substantial gain in collaboration is particularly meaningful. However, the modest knowledge gain alone may not justify FC preparation time. Future studies should combine FC with other strategies to boost knowledge outcomes.

In addition, the results of the survey of perception confirm the overall satisfaction of participants trained via the flipped classroom methodology. The majority of the residents in this study agreed that the flipped classroom provided more opportunities for self-learning and offered more flexibility with respect to time and space for learning. This result highlights the particularly noteworthy educational potential of the flipped classroom method. This approach complies with the principles of learning, which allows learners to self manage and flexibly utilize their time or space for learning. Similar to the findings in our study, many students also reported that there were challenges brought by the flipped classroom, including occupying too much spare time, and leading to more pressure in self-presentation and team discussions [8, 21]. This may be because the education model in China is usually a traditional teacher-centred preaching style. Hence, some residents who have not been introduced to the flipped-classroom method may experience difficulty adapting to independent study time and expressing themselves in public places. These concerns may reflect the transition from traditional didactic pedagogy prevalent in Chinese medical education to more interactive, learner-centred approaches. Nevertheless, the attitudes of these residents were positive with the majority of the participants stating that this teaching method was interesting and the content of the flipped classroom was well organized. Additionally, most residents in the FC group felt that the use of a flipped classroom approach resulted in a more in-depth understanding of the curriculum. Accordingly, they agreed that more courses should adopt the flipped classroom model. These positive attitudes indicate that the flipped classroom teaching method was well-received by the residents in gastroenterology. Furthermore, this teaching model may be applicable to other standardized medical training programs.

Limitations

Although this study demonstrated positive results of the flipped classroom method in the standardized training of gastroenterology residents, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively small sample size from a single institution may limit the statistical power of our findings and their generalizability to other training settings. Second, while we compared post-intervention scores between the intervention and control groups, the lack of baseline assessments prevented us from evaluating individual trainees’ improvement over time. This missing pre-course evaluation represents a significant constraint in interpreting the educational outcomes. Although residents were balanced by training year in both groups, the absence of a standardized pre-test skill assessment represents a study limitation. Future studies would benefit from pre-intervention skill assessments to better control for baseline competencies and examine differential effects by training level. Furthermore, the evaluation of perceived teaching effectiveness was partially based on self-reported perceptions. Although these data are invaluable for assessing learner satisfaction and engagement, they cannot serve as a direct indicator for objective outcomes such as the achievement of deeper cognitive learning or the development of clinical skills. Specifically, domains including theoretical knowledge acquisition, clinical skill development, problem-solving ability, and team collaboration are better assessed through objective measures rather than learner self-reports. Accordingly, future research should aim to overcome the current limitations by employing more comprehensive evaluation frameworks. A further limitation is that the “learner presentation” activity in the FC protocol may independently enhance learning outcomes. Since this activity is not unique to the FC approach, the observed benefits likely reflect a combination of FC instruction and the presentation activity. Future studies should control for this by including comparable active learning tasks in both FC and TC groups.

Conclusion

This investigation provides empirical evidence supporting the effectiveness of micro-video-based flipped classroom methodology in gastroenterology residency education. Compared with traditional pedagogical methods, the approach demonstrated consistent improvements in both objective assessments and learner perception measures. While acknowledging the study’s limitations, these findings may indicate the possible benefits of broader implementation of the flipped classroom methodology in medical residency education. Future multi-center studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings and explore optimal implementation strategies.
