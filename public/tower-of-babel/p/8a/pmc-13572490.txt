Gene overexpression reduces inhibitory metabolites to enhance CHO cell growth and IgG1 production
Authors: Duc Hoang, Bingyu Kuang, Zhao Wang, George Liang, Seongkyu Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractControlling the generation of toxic by-products in mammalian bioprocess to maximize therapeutic protein production and glycosylation patterns is a challenge. Intracellular metabolism is often not well-regulated and known to secrete toxic intermediate by-products which hampers cellular performance and negatively impacts critical quality attributes (CQA) of cells. Previous studies have identified trigonelline (TRI), n-acetyl putrescine (NAP), aconitic acid (AA), and cytidine monophosphate (CMP) generated through CHO cell metabolism and verified their negative impacts on growth and antibody production. In this approach, a genetic engineering strategy was developed to control downstream accumulation of inhibitory metabolites. The study successfully identified four different metabolic genes in CHO cells, including Cat (nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism) to control the generation of TRI, Got1 and Hoga1 (proline metabolism) to control the generation of NAP, Got1 (TCA cycle) to control the generation of AA, and Slc35a1 (n-glycan biosynthesis) to control the generation of CMP. Each target gene-of-interest (GOI) was cloned from CHO genomic library, inserted into linearized vector plasmid, and subsequently transfected into cells. CQA of the bioprocess realized 22–30% increase in peak cell density, 16–22% increase overall IVCD, with an improving growth rate during cellular expansion phase when comparing engineered cells against control cells. The study also conducted a follow-up quadruple transfection study where all four GOIs were co-transfected into cells at ¼ of the total DNA concentration per GOI. An increase in cellular performance was also realized, as increases in peak VCD (17% increase), cumulative IVCD (17% increase), and growth rate were achieved. Both studies also found higher IgG1 antibody synthesis when cell metabolism was better regulated, as the studies measured 4% to 40% titer increase across all engineered cells when compared against control cells. The study also measured higher levels of G1F and G2F glycans with decreased level of G0F across all transfected cells, further indicating improvement in bioprocess, as cells were able to produce a higher fraction of semi-complex and complex versus simple glycoforms. Further investigation revealed that Cat and Slc35a1 exhibited comparable expression levels in the MG condition to their single-gene conditions (within 1% and 10% difference, respectively), corresponding to modest titer improvements closest to the control. These findings suggest that when all four genes are co-expressed, Cat and Got1 may act as rate-limiting factors influencing both cellular phenotypes and titer production. In both studies, the concentrations of downstream metabolic inhibitors were measured to be significantly decreased when comparing engineered cells against control cells, further demonstrating that overexpression of genes to re-allocate metabolic fluxes away from synthesizing toxic by-products can significantly improve cellular growth and protein synthesis.Subject terms: Biological techniques, Biotechnology, Cell biology, Molecular biology

Introduction

The production of therapeutic proteins in biomanufacturing heavily relies on cell-based expression systems1. Of these, Chinese Hamster ovary (CHO) cells are the leading mammalian expression system for many recently approved biotherapeutic products2. CHO cells are characterized by their ability to be genetically engineered with recombinant DNA3, can be grown at large scale in industrial bio-culture setting4, and are able to perform complex post-translational modification for monoclonal antibodies (mABs) production5,6. Improvements in bioprocessing and cell line development efforts have led to significant enhancements, achieving CHO cell concentrations over 20 million cells per milliliter in both fed-batch and perfusion culture systems. However, higher cell densities come with adverse effects on critical quality attributes (CQA) such as titer yield and quality, posing a challenge for biomanufacturing processes to lift titer productivity to a higher ceiling6. In addition, detailed knowledge on cell physiology and particularly at the level of cellular metabolism is often lacking due to inability to predict CHO cellular metabolism during various stages of cell culture7.

CHO cell metabolism, characterized by high uptake rates of supplemented nutrients of carbon and nitrogen-derived substrates, is inefficient and suboptimal. Cells grown in fed batch processes consume many nutrients that do not support titer production. Recent work has shown that cells often do not efficiently utilize fed nutrients towards expansion and protein synthesis. Instead, a significant fraction of fed glucose and amino acids are diverted into toxic metabolite pathways and adversely pose a risk of compromised growth and titer production8,9. Traditionally, lactate and ammonia have been reported through literature to be the most dominant forms of by-products generated from glycolysis and other cellular metabolic cycles9–11. Studies have shown that accumulation of these by-products can inhibit cellular growth and suppress peak viable cell density which can negatively impact glycosylation profile12–14. Owing to this, liquid chromatography-mass spectroscopy (LC-MS) has emerged as one of the most powerful set of tools for various metabolomic and modeling studies due to its capability of analyzing countless metabolites and medium additives from a single sample15–18. Recent advances in metabolomic technologies have enabled identification of additional cell-derived metabolic inhibitors, other than lactate and ammonia, and characterized their negative impacts to cell overall physiologies6,19–22.

In our most recent work, it has been shown that trigonelline (TRI), n-acetyl putrescine (NAP), aconitic acid (AA), and cytidine monophosphate (CMP), all of which were generated through CHO metabolism and shown to be toxic to cells23,24. Though the study successfully addressed how accrual of these waste by-products can halt growth, suppress titer production, and alter the glycosylation profile of recombinant proteins, however, insight into their cellular metabolism, along with the appropriate control strategy to mitigate generation of such metabolic waste, remains unexplored. Past studies suggested that multi omics study and genetic engineering of recombinant genes can be employed to develop the appropriate control strategy for mitigation of metabolic waste25–29. Although pathway identification and their related metabolic functionality have been previously studied in other mammalian systems, the extent to which genes are expressed, along with the impact of the generated intermediates on cellular activities including growth and protein production in CHO systems are not currently well studied.

With recombinant mAbs representing the largest group of recently approved therapeutic proteins, protein glycosylation plays a major role in many biological processes. Glycans can be found on both cell surfaces and cell-expressed proteins30, allowing them to participate in molecular recognition processes that typically occur during viral infection, protein sorting, immune and receptor recognition, inflammation, pathogenicity, or tumor metastasis. Properties such as safety, efficacy, or serum half-life of therapeutic antibody can also be closely linked to final glycosylation pattern which play a critical role in complement activation and receptor affinity31,32. Correct glycosylation patterns highly influence efficacy of immunoglobulins G (IgGs)33, and are met with constant on-going research efforts to ensure critical standards can be achieved from the final end products34.

Owing to this, this present work therefore sought to develop a control strategy through genetic modification to modulate the expression of recombinant genes towards better regulating CHO metabolism, with the ultimate goal to improve growth, increase IgG1 antibody synthesis, and generate higher degree of complex glycoproteins. The study successfully identified four different metabolic genes in CHO cells, including Cat (nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism) to control the generation of TRI, Got1 and Hoga1 (proline metabolism) to control the generation of NAP, Got1 (TCA cycle) to control the generation of AA, and Slc35a1 (n-glycan biosynthesis) to control the generation of CMP. Through titer measurement and off-line LC-MS analysis, the study showed that improvements in CQA such as growth and protein synthesis, were realized while effectively decreasing the accumulation levels of toxic metabolites secreted to the extracellular environment when comparing engineered cells against control cells. The study also found a higher fraction of mature glycoproteins, such as semi-complex mono-galactosylated (G1F) and complex bi-galactosylated (G2F) biantennary from transfected cells, while also effectively showing decreased levels of simple a-galactosylated biantennary (G0F). This work successfully demonstrated that overexpression of genes to re-allocate metabolic fluxes away from synthesizing toxic by-products can significantly improve cellular growth and protein synthesis.

Results

Pathway analysis and genetic engineering strategy targeting the accumulation of metabolites

During the growth and production phases, CHO cells generate toxic metabolites as by-products from various metabolic pathways. In this study, a genetic engineering pipeline was developed targeting the metabolites CMP, NAP, AA, and TRI. In CHO cells, the metabolic pathways involving GOT1 (glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 1) and HOGA1 (4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate aldolase 1) play critical roles in regulating nitrogen balance, amino acid flux, and polyamine synthesis (see Fig. 1B). GOT1 catalyzes the transamination of glutamate and oxaloacetate to form aspartate and α-ketoglutarate (αKG)35,36, with glutamate serving as a precursor for ornithine and subsequently putrescine37. Increased GOT1 activity generally shifts this reaction toward the production of aspartate and αKG, redirecting glutamate away from ornithine synthesis and impacting downstream polyamine production38,39 and reducing the substrate available for ornithine and putrescine synthesis. On the other hand, HOGA1 plays a key role in the hydroxyproline degradation pathway, where it catalyzes the conversion of 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate into pyruvate and glyoxylate27,40. Producing pyruvate further feeds into the TCA cycle, enhancing metabolic flux and increasing the consumption of intermediates like αKG41,42. This elevated TCA cycle activity can be expected to further reduce the pool of αKG and nitrogen available for amino acid and polyamine biosynthesis, indirectly affecting the availability of glutamate and ornithine. Simultaneously, increased HOGA1 expression channels more pyruvate into the TCA cycle11, depleting resources like αKG, which are essential for amino acid and polyamine production. This combined upregulation effectively decreases NAP levels, potentially mitigating the toxic effects of excess NAP accumulation in CHO cells. Thus, by upregulating GOT1 and HOGA1, cellular metabolism can be expected to shift away from pathways that support polyamine synthesis, specifically putrescine and its acetylated form NAP.

N-glycan biosynthesis is an important step in post-translational modification of proteins. The attached glycans can provide proteins with important structural and functional properties which play essential roles in many key biological processes. Biosynthesis of sialic acid n-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) begins with uridine diphosphate in the cytosol, which is a downstream product from glucose (see Fig. 1C). Synthesized cytosolic Neu5Ac is transferred into the nucleus and activated by N-acylneuraminate cytidylyltransferase (CMAS) to form cytidine 5′-monophosphate N-acetylneuraminic acid (CMP-Neu5Ac). Complex CMP-Neu5Ac is further transported into Golgi vesicles by binding to transporter SLC35A1 where various glycotransferases function, a process typically known as n-glycan biosynthesis. Transporter enzyme SLC35A1 functions as an antiporter and can bind to both complex CMP-Neu5Ac and individual CMP, with a higher affinity towards free CMP. In our previous work, it has been shown that a high amount of CMP was found to accumulate in CHO cultures, and was highly toxic to cells, affecting growth, productivity, and glycan profile. In this approach, cytosolic antiporter SLC35A1 was up-regulated to bind excess CMP generated from various metabolic pathways (glycan and nucleotide synthesis) as by-products during growth and production.

Tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle is another important metabolic pathway commonly shared among many organisms, including CHO cells. Combining with the glycolysis pathway, the TCA cycle serves as the main source of energy for cells – producing net 30 ATP per one molecule of glucose. The TCA cycle initiates with an aldol condensation step where oxaloacetate is converted to citrate catalyzed by citrate synthase (see Fig. 1 D). Next, a dehydration step occurs, where citrate is converted to aconitic acid (AA), followed by a hydration step where AA is converted to isocitrate, both catalyzed by aconitase. Isocitrate further decarboxylates by isocitrate dehydrogenase to yield alpha-ketoglutarate (αKG). αKG further undergoes a decarboxylation step where αKG is turned to succinyl-CoA, followed by a phosphorylation step catalyzed by succinyl-CoA synthetase to generate succinate. Succinate is then oxidized by succinate dehydrogenase to yield fumarate, which is then converted to oxaloacetate in an oxidation reaction catalyzed by malate dehydrogenase. Finally, oxaloacetate then converts to citrate by citrate synthase in an aldol condensation reaction, thus closing the TCA cycle. In mitochondria, aspartate can be metabolized to produce oxaloacetate by glutamic-oxaloacetic transaminase 2 (GOT2) or transported from the mitochondria into the cytosol where aspartate is then converted to oxaloacetate by GOT1 enzyme. In this approach, metabolic flux from excess aspartate was re-directed towards cytosolic Oac through upregulation of GOT1, effectively aiming towards reducing accumulation of AA which can be generated through recycling of Cit.

Finally, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) is an important coenzyme that is involved in hundreds of redox reactions acting as an electron carrier in CHO cells. Nicotinate, also known as vitamin B3, can be converted to nicotinate mononucleotide (NaMN) catalyzed by nicotinate phosphoribosyltransferase (see Fig. 1E). NaMN is ultimately converted to NAD+, an adenylation reaction catalyzed by enzyme nicotinamide mononucleotide adenylyltransferase. TRI, a by-product from the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism previously shown to negatively impact growth and productivity of CHO cells, can be converted from nicotinate by N-methyltransferase when nicotinate is produced in excess. In mammalian cells, quinolinic acid (QA) is the first intermediate in the de novo pathway of NAD+ biosynthesis and mainly produced by the degradation of tryptophan. In this approach, metabolic resources were diverted away from nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism to prevent excess accumulation of nicotinate which can lead to generation of TRI. Specifically, HAA can be converted into cinnavalininate (CVA) through the action of the catalase (CAT) enzyme.

Vector construction with restriction enzyme and genes cloning

A transient transfection strategy was implemented to regulate metabolite accumulation by integrating pathway analysis and genetic engineering (see Fig. 2A). CHO-K1 cells were cultured in a fed-batch process and harvested between Day 4 and Day 6 for mRNA isolation. The isolated mRNA was used to synthesize cDNA, from which GOIs were amplified using PCR. The amplified GOIs were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector backbone through a ligation process using DNA ligase. Figure 2B presents the final set of genes whose upregulation enhances growth-promoting enzymatic activity, along with their associated metabolic pathways and input metabolites.

Four expression vectors were constructed, each featuring a unique GOI. To ensure optimal expression in the CHO-K1 host system, Kozak sequences were included in the primers, and the primers were designed to maintain in-frame coding for proper protein expression. Spacer sequences were added at the 5′ ends of primers to improve template binding and digestion during PCR amplification. Restriction enzymes were carefully selected to avoid cutting within the GOI sequence, ensuring precise cutting sites at the ends of the expression cassette. The resulting complementary overhangs enabled seamless ligation of the GOIs into the linearized vector.

Purity of the plasmid constructs was verified through absorbance measurements, with acceptable ranges of A260/A280 (1.8–2.0) and A260/A230 (2.0–2.2). To confirm successful insertion of GOIs, plasmids were treated with the same restriction enzymes used during cloning. Gel electrophoresis revealed three distinct bands: the smallest band (<3 kb) corresponding to the cut GOI, a middle-sized band (~5.4 kb) representing the linearized plasmid backbone, and a larger band (>5.5 kb, depending on the inserted GOI) indicating intact plasmids that were not fully digested. The restriction enzyme digestion and gel electrophoresis results confirmed successful assembly, showing three distinct bands: the GOI fragment (<3 kb), the linearized plasmid backbone (~5.4 kb), and intact plasmids (>5.5 kb). These results validated the construction platform and ensured its reliability for downstream applications (see Fig. 2C).

Design of single-gene transfection study, assessment of gene expression, and phenotype characteristic results

After assessing the quality and integrity of fully constructed vectors, the study next sought to validate gene functionality towards growth and production in CHO cells. Schematic diagram representing the design of the transient transfection experiments of target metabolic genes is shown in Fig. 3A. Prior to the experiment, a preliminary study employing a GFP reporter gene was performed to assess expression activity and quantify transfection efficiency (see Supplementary Fig. 2). To study metabolic gene functionality towards growth and protein synthesis, the study was divided into five unique clones. Cells from each clone were derived from the same parental culture to eliminate variation in growth cycle and other culture conditions. Prior to the experiments, stock plasmid solutions containing different GOIs were prepared in high concentration. Batch cultures of CHO cells were then conducted, where the cells were grown to confluency (Day 3) and were transfected with plasmids containing different GOI depending on each condition. Each clone was transiently overexpressed with its respective GOI using a plasmid stock at 40 µg/µL. This corresponds to 40 µg of DNA per 30 mL vessel (approximately 3.65 × 10−⁴ pg DNA per cell), delivered at a 1:4 DNA:PEI mass ratio. The cells were cultured for four additional days until cell viabilities dropped below 80%. The expression levels of engineered metabolic genes in CHO cells were quantified via qRT-PCR and normalized against the control, as shown in Fig. 3B–E. Overall, the quantified expression levels through qRT-PCR of all transfected genes from all engineered conditions were found to be higher when compared against the control. Future study in stable expression systems can be leveraged to confirm stable translation activity.

To study metabolic functionality towards growth and production, critical quality attributes (CQA) in terms of cellular phenotypic traits such as VCD profile, IVCD profile and growth rate obtained from the conducted batch process were systematically evaluated to assess the effect of gene upregulation across all tested conditions (see Fig. 3F–H). Upregulating metabolic genes all realized in VCD increase going into production phase (Day 5) when compared against the control: 22% increase (Cat), 30% increase (Got1), 27% increase (Hoga1), and 22% increase (Slc35a1). This overall increase in peak cellular VCD further translated to better overall cumulative IVCD profile obtained at harvest day, as engineered cultures allowed an additional increase of 16% to 22% overall IVCD, specifically 4.6 × 106 cells/mL for Cat, 6.2 × 106 cells/mL for Got1, 6.6 × 106 cells/mL for Hoga1, and 4.8 × 106 cells/mL for Slc35a1. Cellular performance per day was also improved, as cells on average exhibited better growth rate during growth phase when compared against the control.

Inhibitory metabolite accumulation profiles from single-gene transfection study

The study next sought to investigate the modulation effect due to upregulating metabolic genes towards downstream inhibitory metabolites generation from the transfected subclones, including lactate and ammonia (see Fig. 4A–I). The accumulation profile from each metabolite-gene pair—previously explored in pathway study as shown in Fig. 1—was studied to determine whether genetic intervention could alleviate metabolic fluxes diverting towards toxic by-product generation. Overall, the study observed a decrease in inhibitory metabolite accumulation across all transfected conditions. In a previous work, inhibitory metabolites data were reported from a 14-days fed-batch accumulation23. In this present work, the data were obtained from a 7-days batch process. Unsurprisingly, the mitigating effect was found to be more significant in the case of genes specifically engineered to control a target metabolite. For instance, TRI – a metabolite previously shown to derive from the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism and directly correlated with metabolic enzyme CAT – was shown to be effectively controlled through upregulation of Cat (see Fig. 4A). Besides TRI-Cat, other gene-metabolite pairs, such as NAP-Got1, NAP-Hoga1, AA-Got1, or CMP-Slc35a1, were also found to follow a similar trend, further suggesting the strong relationship between gene overexpression towards controlling downstream inhibitors accumulation. Although not originally intended as a metabolite to be controlled, the accumulation profile of GMP was also found to be decreased and followed closely to CMP (see Fig. 4G). This was rather not surprising, as CMP and GMP both participated in pyrimidine and purine metabolism which are closely related and highly intertwined metabolic pathways. In terms of upstream precursors, CMP and GMP are downstream metabolites generated by conversion of uridine monophosphate and guanosine monophosphate, which are both generated from the same precursors, including glucose, aspartate, glutamine, and arginine. Given the close relationship between CMP and GMP, it was expected to observe a similar accumulation trend of CMP and GMP across the transfected subclones. Although the expression of Slc35a1 was not statistically different from the control, a decrease in accumulation of CMP was observed. This could be due to secondary interaction between genes and metabolites, as was also observed from the single gene study. For example, in Fig. 4D, besides Slc35a1 which was the intended target gene for metabolite CMP, the study also observed decreased level of CMP in the case of overexpressing Hoga1, Cat, and Got1, suggesting that these genes can interact with CMP albeit not being the original control target gene.

In addition, the study also found surprising findings. In the case of AA, for instance, the reduction achieved by cell engineering is rather modest (see Fig. 4C). Further optimization studies, such as optimizing the vector construct to improve gene expression, could be conducted to better improve the results. On the other hand, the study also found a decrease in the measured concentration of toxic metabolites in the case of upregulating genes not intended to target such metabolites (not directly involved in their metabolism pathways), such as HICA with respect to Slc35a1; or MSA with respect to Hoga1, Slc35a1, and Got1; or GMP with respect to Hoga1, Slc35a1, Got1, and Cat. The study shows that reducing toxic metabolite synthesis in cells can be achieved not only by upregulating genes directly involved in the metabolic pathways of these metabolites but also by targeting secondary genes. These secondary genes, while not directly part of the primary metabolic pathways, interact with these metabolites through less understood and understudied mechanisms. This suggests that targeting such “secondhand” genes can indirectly influence the synthesis or accumulation of toxic metabolites, providing an alternative strategy for process optimization. Possible explanations for these outcomes include the following. As Slc35a1 is responsible for transporting CMP-sialic acid, increased activity of Slc35a1 may deplete cytosolic CMP pools to fuel glycosylation pathways43. This could create competition for shared metabolic precursors, such as ATP or glutamine which are required for nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis. Perhaps, reduced availability of key precursors like leucine or oxaloacetate, linking amino acid metabolism to the TCA cycle. Increased Got1 activity may deplete glutamine due to increased glycosylation activity may lower the production of HICA as the BCAA degradation pathway competes for these resources44. On the other hand, increased expression of Hoga1 and Got1 enhances the availability of TCA cycle intermediates, such as pyruvate and αKG. This increased metabolic flux through the TCA cycle may redirect precursors like succinyl-CoA away from the methylmalonyl-CoA pathway, thereby reducing MSA synthesis45. Similarly, increased activity of Slc35a1 may compete for nucleotide precursors, further restricting resources available for MSA production. Altogether, these changes may reduce the availability of methylsuccinic acid as metabolic flux shifts away from pathways contributing to its synthesis46. As for GMP, Hoga1 catalyzes the conversion of hydroxyproline into pyruvate and glyoxylate, feeding intermediates into the TCA cycle. Increase in Hoga1 activity may decrease the availability of precursors like glutamine and aspartate which are essential for purine synthesis (such as GMP), thereby reducing the amount of GMP produced. On the other hand, Got1 catalyzes the reversible transamination of glutamate and cytosolic glutamate levels, reducing its availability for nucleotide precursor pathways, thereby indirectly affecting GMP synthesis47. In addition, the αKG produced by this reaction is consumed in the TCA cycle, reducing its availability for anabolic processes, such as nucleotide synthesis48, further redirecting resources away from GMP production. Interestingly, the study found Slc35a1 to be the least effective gene, relatively speaking, in reducing CMP levels, while being the most effective in decreasing HICA. This observation can be explained by the specific role of Slc35a1 as a nucleotide sugar transporter that facilitates the transport of CMP-sialic acid into the Golgi apparatus for glycosylation processes. Rather than directly modulating CMP synthesis or degradation, Slc35a1 primarily influences the availability of CMP-sialic acid for glycosylation, making its impact on overall CMP levels more indirect. In contrast, Got1 and Hoga1 are more directly linked to central metabolic pathways, including amino acid metabolism and nucleotide synthesis such as TCA cycle and polyamine synthesis, and thus are more effective in terms of directly regulating CMP.

Design of multi-genes quadruple transfection follow-up study, assessment of gene expression, and phenotype characteristic results

After verifying the positive contribution of upregulating individual genes in CHO cultures, the study next sought to determine whether upregulating all four genes into a single culture would yield further improvements; a follow-up study was conducted with a gene mix condition, where each GOI was transfected to cells at ¼ of the total plasmid concentration. Schematic representation of the multi-genes quadruple transfection follow-up experiment is shown in Fig. 5A. The gene expression values of each GOI as expressed from the sampling transfected cells normalized against the control are also shown, further confirming proper expression of all target GOI. Phenotype characteristic profiles of cells are shown in Fig. 5C–E. Overall, an increase in performance of cells was also realized across all CQA, as improvement was observed in terms of peak VCD (17% increase), cumulative IVCD (17% increase), and growth rate. Interestingly, when comparing the phenotype results of quadruple transfected cells to the single transfected cells from the previous study, the increase in peak VCD performance of cells going into production phase from both cases (for example, 17% increase in the case of MG transfected genes versus 22% increase for Cat as shown in previous study) was not found to be significantly different. This outcome is likely attributable to the reduced plasmid dosage per gene in the MG condition (10 µg/µL per gene versus 40 µg/µL in single-gene transfections), which resulted in lower expression levels of each individual gene. To validate this observation, further study can be conducted with higher DNA dosage concentrations under the MG condition to determine whether increased expression of all four genes yields additive or synergistic improvements.

Inhibitory metabolite profiles of multi-genes transfection study

Inhibitory metabolite profiles obtained at harvest day from quadruple transfected cells are shown in Fig. 6. Besides TRI, the measured concentrations of all other toxic metabolites including NAP, AA, CMP, HICA, MSA, and GMP are all shown to decrease. Although better control of toxic metabolites was achieved, the study found no apparent improvement gained from quadruple transfected cells when comparing against single transfected cells which was consistent with the phenotype results as found in previous study. This finding is consistent with the reduced plasmid dosage per gene in the MG condition, which likely limited the expression levels achievable for each GOI. Interestingly, when investigating the classically known and well-established process inhibitors such as lactate and ammonia, there was no significant difference in terms of the accumulation profile between transfected cells and the control. Regardless, cellular phenotype improvements in terms of both peak VCD and growth rate increase on day 5 were still observed (see Fig. 5C–E), suggesting that overexpression of key metabolic genes can effectively control metabolic resources diverting towards non-optimized pathways, further improving overall process performance. The results from both the single transfection and quadruple transfection studies altogether suggested that besides optimizing the amount of key supplemented amino acid nutrients to improve cell culture bioprocess29, upregulation of key metabolic genes can also positively contribute towards VCD and growth rate.

IgG1 antibody and glycosylation profile of single-gene and multi-genes transfected cells

IgG1 antibody profiles of CHO cells obtained from both transfected groups are also shown in Fig. 7A, B. Overall, the study found that overexpressing metabolic genes resulted in higher titer profiles across all engineered cells. Particularly for the case of single transfected cells, the study measured a titer increase of 12% for Cat, 14% increase for Got1, 40% increase for Hoga1, and 4% increase for Slc35a1 across all engineered cells when compared against the control. For the case of MG transfected cells, improvement in titer profile was also realized at 4% increase over control cells obtained at harvest day. The improvement in the titer profile, combined with the previously demonstrated reduction in toxic metabolites, demonstrated the effectiveness of gene modulation in enhancing process performance. These findings highlight the directionality of the study, and further optimization, such as developing better-designed vectors to achieve higher expression, could potentially lead to even better titer improvements. When comparing cells from the MG transfection group against the single-gene transfection group, the comparable rather than additive titer increases suggest that total plasmid dosage per gene plays a more critical role in determining cellular performance. Notably, in the MG condition, each gene received 25% of the plasmid concentration used in single-gene transfections, resulting in correspondingly lower expression levels. For instance, Cat and Slc35a1 exhibited expression levels in the MG condition that differed by only 1% and 10%, respectively, compared to single-gene transfections (see Fig. 3B–E), which aligns with their modest titer improvements (12% and 4% for single transfection versus 4% for MG). These observations underscore that achieving higher expression levels through optimized plasmid dosing or permanent integration may be necessary to observe better improvements from the multi-gene co-expression strategies.

Interestingly, when comparing cells from the MG transfection group against single-gene transfection group, there was no observable difference in terms of titer increase, once again suggesting that total transfected DNA concentration played a more critical role in improving cellular performance than the number of co-transfected genes. When comparing cells from the MG to the single-gene transfection group, no observable difference in titer increase was noted. Interestingly, examining the gene expression levels in the MG condition relative to individual gene transfection revealed that Cat and Slc35a1 exhibited comparable expression levels in both conditions, differing by only 1% for Cat and 10% for Slc35a1 (see Fig. 3B–E). This aligns with the modest titer improvements observed for Cat and Slc35a1, which were closest to the control, showing increases of 12% and 4%, respectively, compared to 4% for MG. These observations suggest that when all four genes are co-expressed, the expression levels of Cat and Got1 may act as rate-limiting factors, influencing both cellular phenotypes and titer production. This highlights the potential interplay between individual gene contributions and co-expression dynamics in determining the overall cellular output.

To further investigate the relationship between cell growth and antibody production, specific productivity (qP) was calculated and analyzed across all transfected groups (see Fig. 7A, B). While both VCD and titer increased in the engineered cell lines compared to control, the magnitude of improvement in VCD was proportionally greater than that observed for titer. This indicates a partial decoupling between cell growth and productivity, where qP values were modestly reduced despite overall improvements in volumetric titer. This phenomenon suggests that the metabolic engineering strategies employed in this study primarily enhanced cell proliferation and viability, with productivity gains arising largely from the increased biomass rather than from elevated per-cell secretion rates. This suggested as metabolic modifications that reduce toxic byproduct accumulation, improvement in cellular energetics may preferentially support growth over recombinant protein synthesis. Nonetheless, despite the reduction in qP, the net outcome remained favorable, with the increased VCD likely contributing to the improved final titers observed across all engineered subclones.

Next, the study also sought to investigate the glycosylation patterns of IgG1 secreted by engineered CHO cells. Comparison of glycoform profiles obtained from purified IgG1 from both transfected cell groups are shown in Fig. 7. In this study, three different types of protein glycoform were investigated, including simple glycan G0F, semi-complex glycan G1F, and complex glycan G2F (see Fig. 7 C). A higher degree of G1F and G2F formation indicates improved bioprocess performance, as cells produce a higher fraction of semi-complex and complex glycans versus simple glycoforms. High levels of G1F and G2F glycans with decreased levels of G0F were observed across all transfected cells. Interestingly, upregulating Got1 significantly improved the G1F and G2F profile (see Fig. 7F, G), suggesting that modulating aspartate aminotransferase activity may positively influence the N-glycan biosynthesis pathway.

Discussion

The observed improvement in VCD through overexpression of Cat, Got1, Hoga1, and Slc35a1 suggested underlying interconnected metabolic mechanisms. Previous studies have demonstrated that overexpression strategies targeting central metabolic enzymes can reduce toxic byproduct accumulation and improve integral viable cell number in CHO cultures49,50. GOT1 overexpression has been reported to enhance anaplerotic refilling of TCA cycle intermediates by catalyzing the transfer of amino groups from glutamate to oxaloacetate, generating aspartate and α-ketoglutarate51. The increase in TCA cycle flux could redirect metabolic precursors away from byproduct-generating pathways. Similarly, HOGA1 catalyzes the cleavage of 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate into pyruvate and glyoxylate27,40, with pyruvate potentially feeding directly into the TCA cycle to enhance metabolic flux. Given that TCA cycle intermediates serve as precursors for nucleotide, amino acid, and lipid biosynthesis52, the enhanced flux through Got1 and Hoga1 may deplete glutamine and aspartate pools required for de novo purine synthesis, which could explain the observed reduction in GMP and CMP accumulation. Collectively, these engineering strategies may reduce metabolic burden while optimizing resource allocation toward productive biosynthesis pathways. Future studies measuring intracellular ATP levels, NAD + /NADH ratios, and metabolic flux analysis using labeled substrates could help further understand the underlying mechanisms.

Residual lactate concentrations remained comparable across engineered cell conditions, despite achieving significantly higher viable cell densities, indicating perhaps a reduction in the specific lactate production rate. Got1 overexpression likely enhanced malate-aspartate shuttle activity, facilitating more efficient transfer of cytosolic NADH into mitochondria for oxidative phosphorylation. This is consistent with previous work demonstrating that overexpression of malate-aspartate shuttle components such as Slc25a12 in CHO cells promotes a metabolic switch toward lactate consumption49,53. Since lactate dehydrogenase requires NADH to convert pyruvate to lactate, reduced cytosolic NADH availability would perhaps favor pyruvate channeling into the TCA cycle rather than lactate fermentation11. Furthermore, Cat overexpression may have contributed to improved mitochondrial integrity by mitigating oxidative stress from reactive oxygen species accumulation, thereby maintaining efficient aerobic metabolism even at elevated cell densities54,55. HOGA1, which generates pyruvate from 4-hydroxy-2-oxoglutarate during hydroxyproline catabolism27, may have contributed additional pyruvate that could be efficiently oxidized given the enhanced mitochondrial capacity. Overall, these metabolic modifications may have shifted cellular metabolism away from the overflow metabolism typically observed in rapidly proliferating CHO cells toward a more oxidative phenotype, resulting in improved metabolic efficiency characterized by sustained glucose utilization with reduced specific lactate accumulation.

Prior study applied global metabolomic analysis across various CHO bioprocess modes, identifying eight growth by-products that accumulated during cell culture23. While these metabolites have been confirmed to inhibit growth when present in the extracellular environment, the specific pathways and degree to which their nutrient precursors are metabolized remain unclear. This knowledge gap makes it challenging to develop strategies for reducing metabolic inhibitor accumulation. To address this, a systematic approach was developed combining pathway analysis with genome-scale metabolic modeling (GEM) to control the buildup of growth-inhibiting metabolites in CHO bioprocesses. Using GEM, the metabolic pathways associated with each metabolite were analyzed and integrated into a comprehensive regulatory network. All metabolic enzymes capable of either directly or indirectly breaking down toxic metabolites, or redirecting metabolic flux toward alternative pathways that do not produce inhibitory by-products, were identified. This approach aimed to reduce inhibitor accumulation by increasing the expression of candidate genes that regulate these enzymes. The gene list was then validated through genetic engineering experiments to evaluate the effects on cell growth.

While the present data demonstrate a correlation between metabolic gene overexpression, reduced inhibitory metabolite accumulation, and improved culture performance, it is also equally plausible that overexpression of key enzymes such as Got1 and Hoga1 may directly strengthen central carbon and nitrogen metabolism, thereby enhancing overall cellular fitness and metabolic robustness. GOT1, for instance, catalyzes reversible transamination linking glutamate metabolism to the TCA cycle, and has been suggested to enable anaplerotic refilling of TCA cycle intermediates, potentially sustaining cellular energetics and enhancing metabolic flux capacity51. Similarly, metabolic engineering studies in CHO cells have indicated that enhancing TCA cycle flux through overexpression of key enzymes such as pyruvate carboxylase may lead to more efficient central carbon metabolism, with reduced lactate accumulation potentially occurring as a consequence of improved metabolic efficiency rather than as the primary driver of cellular improvement56,57. The observed reduction in inhibitory byproducts in the present study could represent a consequence, rather than the primary cause, of enhanced metabolic robustness. Targeted experiments, such as direct supplementation of inhibitory metabolites to high-performing clones, metabolic flux analysis using isotopic tracers, or conditional knockdown studies can help to distinguish whether reduced toxic metabolites can directly drive performance improvements or enhanced metabolic flux capacity would lead to both improved performance and reduced byproduct accumulation as parallel outcomes. Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that genetic intervention targeting these metabolic nodes may offer a promising strategy for bioprocess optimization.

The mechanistic link between Got1 overexpression and improved glycan maturation likely involves enhanced flux through both the TCA cycle and the hexosamine biosynthetic pathway (HBP). The HBP generates UDP-N-acetylglucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), an essential nucleotide sugar donor for N-glycan biosynthesis, using substrates derived from multiple metabolic pathways such as fructose-6-phosphate from glycolysis, glutamine, acetyl-CoA, and UTP58. GOT1 catalyzes the reversible transamination between glutamate and oxaloacetate to produce α-ketoglutarate and aspartate, thereby connecting amino acid metabolism with TCA cycle anaplerosis59. Enhanced GOT1 activity may increase α-ketoglutarate availability for the TCA cycle while simultaneously regenerating glutamate pools for HBP flux through the rate-limiting enzyme glutamine-fructose-6-phosphate amidotransferase. Furthermore, increased TCA cycle activity promotes citrate production, which can be exported to the cytosol and converted to acetyl-CoA by ATP-citrate lyase, providing another critical substrate for UDP-GlcNAc synthesis60,61. Previous studies in CHO cells have demonstrated that intracellular UDP-GlcNAc levels directly correlate with glycan complexity and that both glutamine availability and glycolytic flux significantly impact nucleotide sugar pools and N-glycan profiles62–64. The observed improvements in galactosylation (G1F and G2F) may also reflect overall enhanced nucleotide sugar metabolism, as UDP-galactose shares biosynthetic precursors with UDP-GlcNAc and responds similarly to improved metabolic flux65.

Although the difference in Slc35a1 expression between transfected cells and the control did not reach statistical significance, an increase in magnitude was still observed, which may contribute to the altered N-glycan isoform distribution (see Fig. 7). Glycosylation machinery can be sensitive to even modest changes in transporter levels. Perhaps, the observed reduction in toxic metabolites likely created a more favorable environment for glycosylation, as N-glycosylation in CHO cells responds to overall metabolic state, and not solely to individual gene expression66,67. Ammonia accumulation, in particular, has been shown to adversely affect galactosylation by altering Golgi pH and inhibiting galactosyltransferase activity14. CMP-sialic acid availability in the Golgi also depends on cytosolic biosynthesis and competing pathways, and thus improved cellular metabolism could enhance substrate flux through existing and modestly elevated transporters61. Future studies, such as 13C-aided metabolic flux analysis into glycoproteins formation from changing key enzymes expression, can perhaps offer a better understanding into the interplay between CHO cell metabolism and glycosylation profile in a culture process of mammalian cells.

Overall, this research developed a genetic engineering strategy to control downstream accumulation of inhibitory metabolites. The study successfully identified four different metabolic genes in CHO cells, including Cat derived from the nicotinate and nicotinamide metabolism to control TRI, Got1 and Hoga1 derived from the proline metabolism to control NAP, Got1 from the TCA cycle to control AA, and Slc35a1 from the n-glycan biosynthesis to control CMP. Performance of cells realized 22% to 30% increase in peak cell density, 16% to 22% increase overall IVCD, with an improving growth rate during cellular expansion phase. The study also presented a follow-up quadruple transfection study where all four genes were transfected at ¼ of the total DNA concentration per gene. An increase in performance of cells was also realized across all CQA, as improvements in terms of peak VCD (17% increase), cumulative IVCD (17% increase), and better growth rate were observed. Antibody IgG1 titer profile of engineered cells from both single and quadruple transfection cell groups also showed increased protein synthesis, ranging from 4 to 40% increase across all engineered cells when compared against control cells. When comparing the phenotypes obtained from the quadruple transfected cells to single transfected cells, no significant difference in cellular performance was observed. Further investigation revealed that Cat and Slc35a1 exhibited comparable expression levels in the MG condition to their single-gene conditions (within 1% and 10% difference, respectively), corresponding to modest titer improvements closest to the control. These findings suggest that when all four genes are co-expressed, Cat and Got1 may act as rate-limiting factors influencing both cellular phenotypes and titer production. In both studies, the concentrations of downstream metabolic inhibitors were measured to be significantly decreased when compared against the control, further demonstrating that overexpression of genes capable of re-allocating metabolic fluxes away from synthesizing toxic by-products. Besides this, we also reported surprising findings, such as Got1 and Hoga1 controlling NAP although not being the original target gene, which suggests deeper level of metabolites/genes interaction not originally intended. Such observation can be undertaken by future study to evaluate unknown types of gene/metabolite interaction.

Methods

Construction of expression vector

For screening of metabolic genes from CHO host, CHO cells were cultivated in fed-batch mode, of which cell pellets were collected between Day 4 and Day 6 of the culture. Cellular mRNA was extracted and purified using RNeasy Mini Kit from Qiagen USA (Germantown, MD, USA). Quantification of purified mRNA was conducted on a NanoDrop OneC instrument from Thermo Fischer Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). CHO-K1 cDNA library was constructed utilizing SS3 superscript kit from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). PCR amplification using Q5® High-Fidelity DNA Polymerases from New England Biolabs (Ipswich, MA, USA) was conducted on the previously extracted and purified cDNA library. For each target GOI, DNA sequences were reviewed from NCBI database, of which specific primers containing restriction enzymes (RE) were designed with appropriate features to ensure proper amplification and expression. For separation of amplified PCR products, gel electrophoresis was applied for 80 min at 100 V. Purification of cut gel containing target DNA bands were conducted using QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit from Qiagen USA. Each GOI and pcDNA3.1/Zeo(+) plasmid from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA) were further digested with corresponding pair restriction enzymes from New England Biolabs. Phosphorylation of plasmids 5′ ends was conducted using Quick CIP also from New England Biolabs, afterwards ligation of GOI and plasmids was performed using T4 DNA Ligase from New England Biolabs.

Plasmid transformation and selection

For transformation, 5 µL of ligated plasmids were mixed with 50 µL of NEB 5-alpha competent E. coli cells obtained from New England Biolabs. Cells and plasmids were gently mixed by light tapping on tubes, after which mixture was incubated on ice for 30 min. Cells were then heat shocked using a dry bath from Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated on ice for 5 min. Cells were added to a total volume of 10 mL of SOC Outgrowth Medium from New England Biolabs and incubated over-night. Tubes were slightly capped to allow cell respiration and incubated for ninety min in a humidified, shaking incubator from Queue Systems Inc (Columbia, SC, USA) operated at 250 RPM and 37 °C. For selection of plasmids-containing cells, approximately 100 µL of cells were uniformly spread using a disposable inoculating loop from VWR (Radnor, PA, USA) onto a LB agar ampicillin plate from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) previously warmed to 37 °C. Cells were incubated overnight at 37 °C.

Plasmid propagation

Colonies (approximately three to four) were selected after the incubation period and supplemented with LB broth medium from Sigma Aldrich to a final volume of 5 mL along with 5 µL of ampicillin. Tubes were slightly capped to allow cell respiration and incubated for 12 h in a humidified, shaking incubator from Queue Systems Inc (Columbia, SC, USA) operated at 250 RPM and 32 °C. Plasmid extraction was performed using ZymoPURE II Plasmid Midiprep Kit from Zymo Research (Irvine, CA, USA) after 12 h of incubation. Cell pellets were isolated by centrifuge model 5425 R from Eppendorf North America (Framingham, MA, USA). Final purified plasmid concentration (in the range of ng/µL) was measured using NanoDrop OneC instrument. For GOI validation process, restriction enzymes were used to cut the same constructed plasmid into individual fragment of GOI and empty linearized vector, of which validation constructed plasmid was further confirmed by gel electrophoresis.

Cells, media, and supplements

All data in this study were obtained in batch mode. Cloning studies of metabolic genes were conducted on a CHO-K1 cell line expressing IgG antibody obtained from NIH (National Institute of Health) using a proprietary basal medium (medium A) obtained from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and glutamine at 8 mM obtained from Corning (Corning, NY, USA), supplemented to cell culture on Day 0. Cells were cultivated in sterile 125 mL shake flask obtained from Fisher Scientific with a working volume of 30 mL in a humidified and shaking incubator model AJ125 obtained from ATR Biotech (Laurel, MD, USA) operated at 125 RPM, 36.5 °C, and 5.2% carbon dioxide. Cells were inoculated at low viable cell densities (VCD) (approximately 0.7 × 106 cells/mL) in basal medium and harvested when cell viability dropped below 80%. Sub-culturing was done every 2–3 days when cells reached VCD greater than 3 × 106 cells/mL. Cell viability was maintained above 90% throughout the inoculation phase. Stock solution of each candidate metabolite was prepared by dissolving pure metabolite standards obtained from Sigma Aldrich into ultrapure water from a MilliQ water purifier obtained from MilliporeSigma (Burlington, MA, USA), and were supplemented in the basal media on Day 0 for both batch and fed-batch processes. For all stages of the study, batch process of CHO cells was conducted in medium A for 7-days.

Assessment of growth and productivity

VCD and viability were measured daily with a cell counter device (CeDex HiRes) obtained from Roche (Branchburg, NJ, USA). Glucose, lactate, and ammonia concentration data from each culture condition were measured using an analyzer (Bioprofile FLEX) obtained from Nova Biomedical (Waltham, MA, USA). Titer analysis was performed using high-pressure liquid chromatography (HPLC) model Agilent 1100 series HPLC obtained from Agilent Technologies (Santa Clara, CA, USA) with a protein A column (Poros A, 2 µm, 2.1 × 30 mm) obtained from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Cell culture supernatant samples were collected at different days for metabolomics analysis and titer was analyzed on the harvest day. The integral viable cell density (IVCD) at day n was defined as the summation of VCD for each day during CHO cell culture up until day n. To compare and evaluate the performance of each culture condition, the productivity at day n was calculated—defined as the overall measured titer normalized against IVCD at day n.

LC-MS samples preparation

LC-MS grade chemical solvents and reagents from MilliporeSigma were used as buffers for all LC-MS analysis. A standard solution containing 20 mM trans-aconitic acid, 320 µM leucinic acid, 64 µM methylsuccinic acid, 16 µM indole-3-carboxylic acid, 64 µM cytidine monophosphate, 64 µM guanosine monophosphate, and 3.2 µM N-acetyl-putrescine in water was prepared. Serial dilutions were performed with LC-MS grade water to produce working standard solutions. An internal standard (IS) solution containing 500 µM 13C6-trans-aconitic acid, 200 µM 2H3-leucinic acid, 20 µM 2H6-methylsuccinic acid, 50 µM 2H5-indole-3-carboxylic acid, 50 µM 15N3-cytidine monophosphate, 50 µM 15N5-guanosine monophosphate, and 5 µM 2H3-N-acetylputrescinium was prepared in water. Each sample was prepared by mixing 10 µL of sample with a matrix containing 10 µL culture medium, 10 µL IS and 30 µL acetonitrile. Samples were vortexed for 30 s and centrifuged for 20 min at 16,000 g. The supernatant was then collected for LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis methods

Analysis of cell culture samples was performed using 2 µL sample injected to an ACQUITY UPLC instrument from Waters (Milford, MA, USA) coupled with a quadrupole time-of-flight Xevo G2-XS mass spectrometer also from Waters. For inhibitory metabolites analysis, LC separation was performed on a PEEK-coated column (SeQuant ZIC-cHILIC, 3 µm, 100 × 2.1 mm) with a guard kit (SeQuant ZIC-cHILIC, 5 µm, 20 × 2.1 mm), both obtained from Millipore Sigma. The elution program was set at: 10% A (0–0.5 min), 30% A (2.5 min to 5.5 min), 45% A (5.5 min to 7.0 min), 65% A (7.0 min to 10.0 min), 10% A (10.1 min to 14.0 min). For MS analysis, mass range was set to 50–1200 Da with scan time of 0.5 s in centroid mode. Mass detection was performed in both positive and negative polarity under sensitivity mode. Leucine-enkephalin (leu-enk) was applied as lock mass reference for accurate mass calibration to counteract the potential effect of calibration drift during the long analytical sequences run time. For leu-enk lockspray setup, the following parameters were applied: positive mass 556.2771 Da, negative mass 554.2615 Da, scan time 0.5 s, scan interval 30 s, mass window ± 0.5 Da. For MS settings, the following parameters were used for electrospray ionization (ESI): capillary voltage 3.0 kV, sampling cone 40 V, source offset 80 V, source temperature 100 °C, desolvation temperature 20 °C, cone gas 40 L/hr, and desolvation gas 600 L/hr.

Materials for glycan profiling

Human serum IgG reference standard, 2-aminobenzamide (2AB), 2-picoline borane complex (2PB), ammonium formate and acetonitrile (HPLC-grade) were obtained from Sigma Aldrich. Magnetic beads coated with protein A and protein G were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. A peptide:N-glycosidase F (PNGase F) enzyme kit containing 10X denaturing buffer, NP-40 non-ion surfactant detergent and 10X reaction buffer was purchased from New England Biolabs and stored as instructed by the manufacturer. Diol solid phase extraction cartridges HyperSep™ Diol SPE were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Glycoclean™ S cartridges were purchased from Prozyme (Hayward, CA, USA). An inline filter (0.2 µm) was purchased from Restek Corporation (Bellefonte, PA, USA) and a BEH amide column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm) was purchased from Waters.

Protein purification from cell culture supernatant

The method for glycan analysis was followed closely to a previously established protocol68. Simply put, cell culture samples were collected to contain approximately 0.1 to 0.5 g/L titer (about 100 to 500 µL supernatant) into a 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, after which samples were spun down for 5 min at 1000 rpm. The supernatant which contained IgG was collected into a microcentrifuge tube, into which an aliquot of 10 µL magnetic bead slurry was added. The beads were washed twice, each time with 250 µL buffer. A magnetic stand was incorporated into each wash to separate the beads from the buffer, after which the washing eluent was discarded. A volume of culture supernatant calculated to contain approximately 20 to 50 µg mAb was then added to 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube, after which the tube was vortexed gently and incubated on a shaker for 15 min. The tube was placed on the magnetic stand to separate the beads-IgG complex from the supernatant, after which the supernatant was removed. The beads were washed by two consecutive washes, with the first wash using 250 µL of binding buffer following by a second wash of 250 µL water. The beads were further suspended into 25 μL of elution buffer and incubated for 10 min. The tube was then placed on the magnetic stand and the supernatant (free of magnetic beads) was transferred to a new microcentrifuge tube which was pre-loaded with 8 μL neutralization buffer.

Enzymatic digestion of N-linked glycan from glycoprotein

Purified standard/mAbs solution was denatured by adding 4 µL of 10X denaturing buffer purchased from New England Biolabs and 6 µL of water. The mixture was then heated at 100 °C for 10 min. After cooling for 15 min, the mixture was added with 5 µL of 10X PNGase F reaction buffer and 5 µL of NP-40 in water. Water was added to the sample to reach a final 50 µL reaction volume. The PNGase F enzyme was added at 1 µL (500,000 units/mL), after which the digestion was carried out at 37 °C for 20 min.

Glycan labeling and cleanup procedure

The labeling solution was prepared to a final composition containing 0.35 M of 2AB and 1 M of 2PB in 30/70% volume/volume (v/v) of acetic acid/DMSO. Labeling solution was added at 50 µL to 50 µL of the digested protein solution, after which the mixture was covered with aluminum foil and incubated at 65 °C for 1 h.

For 2AB cleanup, the methodology presented here utilized Diol SPE cartridge as an economical substitute to HILIC cartridge as previous study has reported a similar and comparable final results68. To use Diol SPE for sample cleanup, the cartridges were attached to a vacuum manifold equipped with a vacuum pump. The cartridges were first washed with 1 mL of water, followed by four consecutive washes of 1 mL ACN each wash. Labeled glycans were diluted in ACN at a ratio of 10/90% v/v. Samples were then loaded and pumped through the cartridge, after which two washes of 500 µL ACN each wash was added to remove unbound compounds including excess 2-AB. Finally, two consecutive aliquots of 100 µL water were added to elute the labeled glycans into fresh 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The total volume of the eluent was 200 µL.

HPLC method for glycan analysis

HPLC runs are carried out using the Agilent 1100 series HPLC from Agilent Technologies coupled with a Model 1329 A fluorescence detector. A calibration standard of labeled glycans from human serum IgG was prepared and used to verify stable HPLC performance. Separation was accomplished with a Waters BEH amide column (1.7 µm, 2.1 × 50 mm). A 0.2 μm Ultrashield precolumn filter model 25809 obtained from Restek (Bellefonte, PA, USA) was installed at the front of the column to prevent clogging due to impurities. Mobile phase A consisted of 100 mM ammonium formate at pH 4.5. Mobile phase B consisted of 100% ACN. Analytical conditions were described as follows: 25–35% A (0–20 min), 35%-40% A (20–25 min), 40–25% A (25–26 min), and 25% A (26–32 min). The total run time of each run was 32 min. Column flow rate was set at 0.4 mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 50 °C. For fluorescence detector analysis, the following settings were used: excitation 350 nm, emission 420 nm, PMT gain = 12. For each run, an injection volume of 1.5 mL was employed.
