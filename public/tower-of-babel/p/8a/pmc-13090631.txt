Protocol for deriving proliferating primary fibroblast cultures from Malabar red snapper larvae for cytogenetic analysis
Authors: Kathiresan Purushothaman, Muhammad Hazim bin Mohamed, Saraphina Dianne Tneo Rwei Qing, Nguyen Thanh Vu, Bing Liang, Harmeet Singh, Tan Shixiong, Grace Loo, Saraswathy Bisa, Dean R. Jerry, Jose A. Domingos, Shubha Vij
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we present a protocol for deriving primary fibroblast cultures from early-stage Malabar red snapper (Lutjanus malabaricus) larvae for cytogenetic analysis. We describe steps for dissociating larval tissues using trypsin and culturing them to establish proliferating fibroblast cells. We then detail procedures for arresting cells at metaphase by colchicine treatment, followed by hypotonic treatment, methanol-acetic acid fixation, and Giemsa staining to visualize chromosomes. This protocol yields clear metaphase spreads suitable for karyotype analysis and downstream cytogenetic applications.

Before you begin

Innovation

This protocol focuses on the derivation and optimization of proliferating primary fibroblast cultures from early-stage teleost larvae for cytogenetic analysis. The primary innovation lies in establishing reliable larval-derived cell cultures that consistently yield metaphase chromosomes from minimally invasive material commonly available in breeding programs. Chromosome fixation and staining follow established cytogenetic procedures. The workflow has also been applied to other teleost species (arowana and seabass), supporting broader applicability. Although larval cell cultures have been described in other teleost studies, this protocol provides a simplified and reproducible workflow optimized specifically for early-stage larvae to maximize metaphase recovery under aquaculture laboratory conditions.

Aquaculture is vital for global seafood supply, and genomic data now underpin selective breeding and stock management.1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8 This protocol outlines the steps for culturing cells and analyzing chromosomes from Malabar red snapper (Lutjanus malabaricus), an emerging species in tropical aquaculture,9,10,11,12,13 focusing on challenges in obtaining high-quality metaphase spreads from fish larvae. The protocol uses optimized cell culture conditions, colchicine treatment for mitotic arrest, and Giemsa staining to ensure reliable chromosome preparation for accurate analysis. The main objective of this protocol is to establish a practical and reproducible approach for deriving proliferating larval fibroblast cultures suitable for cytogenetic analysis, rather than to introduce new chromosome staining techniques.

Conventional fish cytogenetic workflows have traditionally relied on direct chromosome preparations from solid tissues or field-adapted air-drying methods. While these approaches are effective, they are not always optimal when only early life stages are available or when consistent metaphase recovery is required from limited material.14,15 Chromosome preparation from fish larvae has also been reported, with studies showing that factors such as colchicine exposure, hypotonic treatment, and larval age strongly influence metaphase quality and yield.16 In parallel, embryo-derived culture approaches have been described in teleost models, including simplified methods for generating fibroblast-like cultures from single embryos, demonstrating the feasibility of early-stage cell derivation for downstream applications.17 Compared with previous larval chromosome preparation approaches that rely mainly on direct metaphase collection from larvae,16 the present protocol emphasizes the establishment of short-term primary fibroblast cultures that provide a more stable and reproducible source of dividing cells for cytogenetic analysis. Furthermore, while embryo-derived culture systems have been described primarily for model species such as zebrafish,17 the workflow presented here is optimized for aquaculture species and practical laboratory conditions, incorporating simplified tissue dissociation, minimal manipulation of larval material, and culture parameters that support consistent metaphase recovery from small numbers of larvae.

Building on this prior work, the present protocol provides a simplified and stage-appropriate workflow for establishing proliferating primary fibroblast cultures from early-stage Malabar red snapper larvae under aquaculture laboratory conditions, enabling reproducible and consistent metaphase recovery using established cytogenetic procedures. Compared with previously published larval or embryo-derived culture approaches, this protocol emphasizes a simplified workflow using small numbers of larvae and optimized culture conditions suitable for aquaculture laboratories, enabling reliable recovery of metaphase chromosomes for cytogenetic analysis.

Malabar red snapper plays a key role in sustainable aquaculture, making genetic studies essential for improving breeding programs and species conservation.18 This karyotyping protocol enables reliable determination of chromosome number and morphology, providing a cytogenetic baseline for genome characterization, breeding programs, and comparative studies in red snapper. It can also be adapted for other aquaculture species requiring similar chromosomal analysis for breeding and genetic research. Conventional karyotyping remains a fundamental tool for establishing baseline chromosomal architecture, confirming diploid chromosome number, identifying large-scale chromosomal rearrangements, and supporting genome assembly validation and selective breeding programs in teleost fishes, including Lutjanus malabaricus.

Arresting cell division to obtain chromosomes

Timing: ∼2.5 h

Timing: ∼2.5 h

In this step, cell division at metaphase will be arrested to obtain the chromosomes. The use of colchicine disrupts microtubule formation, halting the cells at the metaphase stage, which is crucial for chromosome preparation.1.Arresting cell division:a.Ensure cells are 80% confluent.b.Add colchicine to a final concentration of 0.1 μg/mL.c.Incubate at 22°C for 2 h to arrest cells at metaphase.d.Gently remove serum.e.Add 1 mL of trypsin and incubate at 22°C for 2 min.f.Stop trypsinization by adding 3 mL of growth media.g.Collect cells in a 15 mL tube and centrifuge at 1000 × g for 5 min.h.Discard the supernatant without disturbing the pellet.CRITICAL: Ensure that cells are not exposed to colchicine for longer than the recommended 2 h to prevent unwanted effects on cell health.Note: After trypsinization check the cells under a microscope to ensure that majority cells are detached.

Expected outcomes

The primary expected outcome of this protocol is the successful establishment of proliferating larval-derived fibroblast cultures capable of producing sufficient metaphase spreads for cytogenetic analysis. Once cultures reach approximately 70%–80% confluency, colchicine treatment enables recovery of condensed metaphase chromosomes suitable for enumeration and morphological assessment using standard cytogenetic procedures.

Following fixation and Giemsa staining, chromosomes can be examined for overall morphology, relative size, and centromere position, enabling their arrangement into homologous pairs.

Using this protocol, an average of 10–20 analyzable clear metaphase spreads were obtained per slide from pooled larval samples across repeated experiments.

This technique enables accurate determination of chromosomal composition and supports cytogenetic characterization using conventional karyotyping approaches. While optimized here for Lutjanus malabaricus, this protocol provides a practical framework that can be adapted to other teleost species with minor lineage-specific adjustments.

Limitations

This protocol may not be universally applicable to all cell types, as different cells can have varying chromosomal structures, and preparation requirements. As a result, the protocol often requires optimization for each specific cell type or biological material to ensure optimal cell growth conditions and proper chromosome spreading, as determined by colchicine treatment, leading to suboptimal chromosome arrest or damage. Primary fibroblast cultures have a limited number of passages that maintain a high mitotic index. Cultivation temperature of primary fibroblasts cell culture of various fish species can be slightly different and range between 22°C–27°C. The quality of the metaphase spread can also vary between samples, influenced by factors such as cell density, age, or overall health of the cells. Furthermore, certain types of chromosomal abnormalities or structural variations may not be easily detected with Giemsa staining alone, requiring supplementary techniques for more accurate detection.

Problem 2

Colchicine is a key agent used to arrest cells at the metaphase stage of the cell cycle for karyotyping. Its effectiveness can be compromised if the concentration is too low, or the exposure time is too brief. The optimal concentration of colchicine must be carefully adjusted for the specific cell line, as different cell types may require varying doses to achieve proper metaphase arrest. Insufficient colchicine concentration or inadequate exposure time may result in incomplete metaphase arrest, reducing the number of viable cells available for chromosome analysis. Conversely, prolonged colchicine exposure can induce toxicity, leading to cell death or abnormalities such as aneuploidy, where cells exhibit an abnormal number of chromosomes due to improper division.

Potential solutions

•Optimize Colchicine Concentration: Adjust the colchicine concentration to the specific requirements of the cell line being used. While concentrations between 0.05 μg/mL and 0.1 μg/mL are typically effective, adjustments may be needed based on the sensitivity of the cells.•Limit Exposure Time: To avoid cellular toxicity, limit colchicine exposure to 1–2 h. Prolonged exposure can cause excessive toxicity, leading to cell death or chromosomal abnormalities.•Monitor for Toxicity: Carefully observe cells during and after colchicine treatment for signs of toxicity, such as detachment, clumping, or morphological changes. Discontinue treatment immediately if these symptoms occur.

Problem 3

Hypotonic treatment using potassium chloride (KCl) can present several challenges during chromosome preparation. One potential problem is poor cell swelling, which is essential for spreading chromosomes to analyses. If the KCl solution is improperly prepared or used at an incorrect concentration, it may fail to induce adequate cell swelling. Similarly, insufficient incubation time can result in incomplete swelling, making chromosome analysis more difficult due to inadequate spreading on the slide.

Another concern is cell lysis, which can occur if cells are exposed to the hypotonic solution for too long or if the KCl concentration is too low. This can cause the cell membrane to rupture, releasing cellular contents and disrupting chromosome morphology. Excessive lysis can lead to inadequate chromosome preparation and potentially ruin the sample.

To avoid these issues, it is essential to use the correct hypotonic concentration, optimize the solution temperature and incubation time. Careful monitoring of the cells during treatment helps ensure proper cell swelling while minimizing excessive cell lysis, leading to successful chromosome preparation.

Potential solutions

•Use Correct Hypotonic Solution Concentration: Ensure that the hypotonic solution is prepared at the appropriate concentration (e.g., 0.075 M KCl) for the specific cell line. An overly concentrated or diluted solution can cause incomplete swelling, compromising chromosome preparation.•Limit Exposure Time: To minimize the risk of excessive cell lysis, optimize the incubation period or temperature. Typically, an exposure time of on ice no more than 30 min is recommended.•Optimize Hypotonic Treatment Conditions: Minor empirical adjustments in hypotonic exposure time or solution strength around near-isotonic conditions may be required depending on larval tissue sensitivity, rather than substantial dilution of the hypotonic solution.

Problem 4

Fixation and slide preparation are critical steps in chromosome analysis, and errors during these processes can significantly affect the quality of results. One common issue is inconsistent fixation. If the fixative solution is improperly prepared, either too concentrated or inadequately mixed, chromosomes may not be preserved correctly. Additionally, incorrect fixation time or temperature can result in poorly preserved chromosomes, leading to weak or fragmented staining patterns.

Excessive fixation, caused by prolonged exposure to fixatives, can make chromosomes too rigid or cause them to clump together, complicating their spread on slides. Over-fixation may also result in chromosome breakage or loss of structural integrity, further hindering analysis.

Problem 5

Inadequate slide preparation can significantly complicate chromosome analysis. One common issue is uneven distribution of cells on the slides, often resulting from improperly concentrated cell suspension. A suspension that is too concentrated can lead to cell clumping, while a too diluted suspension may result in uneven spreading of cells, both of which hinder effective chromosome analysis.

Additionally, chromosome clumping, cell and contaminations debris or poor spread can obscure chromosome structure and number, leading to inaccurate analysis. Ensuring the correct cell suspension and using proper slide preparation techniques are crucial for achieving optimal chromosome spreads and reliable analytical results.

Problem 6

Uneven or faint staining is a common problem in the karyotyping process, often caused by improper stain preparation. If the staining solution is too diluted, not mixed thoroughly, or if the slide isn’t exposed to the stain for sufficient time, inconsistent or faint staining may occur. Additionally, incorrect timing during the staining procedure can result in either insufficient or excessive staining, complicating chromosome visualization. Poor banding patterns may also stem from inadequate staining or even uneven chromosome spreading. If chromosomes are not properly exposed to the correct conditions during the banding process, the resolution may be too low, making it difficult to distinguish individual chromosomes.

In general fish chromosomes are small and high condensed, at this reason quite difficult to obtain high-quality banding patterns with conventional staining method.22

Culture optimization and adaptation for other teleost species

Successful establishment of larval fibroblast cultures depends on seeding density, temperature stability, and media composition. Cultures should reach approximately 70%–80% confluency before colchicine treatment to maximize metaphase yield. Low seeding density may reduce proliferation, whereas excessive confluency may decrease mitotic index. Temperature ranges between 22°C–28°C and serum concentrations between 10%–20% may require adjustment depending on species. Minor optimization of these parameters may improve culture stability and metaphase recovery in other teleost species.
