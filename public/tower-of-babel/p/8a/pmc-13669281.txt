Mitochondrial stress-induced cuproptosis: a metabolic bridge to reprogramming the GBM immune microenvironment
Authors: Wenyang Li, Yaqing Lv, Guanrong Wang, Xiaolei Lan, Leina Ren, Hai Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlioblastoma (GBM) remains the most lethal primary brain malignancy, characterized by profound metabolic heterogeneity and an immunosuppressive tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) that severely limits the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade. While cuproptosis has recently been defined as a distinct form of regulated cell death driven by copper-induced mitochondrial proteotoxicity, its non-cell-autonomous roles in remodeling the immune landscape remain poorly understood. This review synthesizes emerging evidence to position cuproptosis not merely as a metabolic collapse, but as a potent driver of immunogenic cell death (ICD). We propose a potential “metabolic-immune” signaling axis wherein copper-triggered aggregation of lipoylated TCA cycle enzymes leads to mitochondrial membrane rupture and the subsequent leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol. This danger signal is sensed by the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), activating the STING pathway to stimulate type I interferon production. We discuss how this cascade orchestrates a systemic immune response, including the recruitment of cytotoxic CD8 + T cells and the repolarization of tumor-associated macrophages from a pro-tumor M2 to an anti-tumor M1 phenotype. Furthermore, we highlight the translational potential of copper ionophores and bioengineered nanomedicines as next generation immunomodulators. By integrating copper metabolism with innate immunity, this review provides a strategic roadmap for exploiting mitochondrial stress to reverse immune exclusion and overcome therapy resistance in GBM.Keywords: GBM, Cuproptosis, Mitochondrial Stress, cGAS-STING, Tumor Immune Microenvironment, Immunotherapy

Introduction

GBM remains the most recalcitrant primary brain malignancy in adults, characterized by inevitable recurrence and a median survival of less than 15 months despite aggressive multimodal treatments [1]. The failure of standard-of-care protocols—surgical resection followed by radiotherapy and temozolomide (TMZ)—is largely attributed to the profound intratumoral heterogeneity and the blood-brain barrier (BBB) that limits drug delivery [2]. More critically, the advent of immunotherapy, specifically immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has failed to replicate its systemic success in GBM due to the profound immunosuppressive nature of the TIME [3]. While historically classified as an ‘immune-cold’ phenotype due to the profound sequestration and exhaustion of T cells, this designation oversimplifies the highly dynamic and heterogeneous nature of the GBM TIME [4, 5]. The GBM microenvironment is heavily infiltrated by myeloid cells, which can constitute up to 50% of the tumor mass. Crucially, this myeloid compartment is ontogenetically diverse, comprising both yolk-sac-derived, brain-resident microglia and peripherally recruited bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) [6, 7]. These distinct populations adopt converging, yet transcriptionally and metabolically unique, immunosuppressive states that actively orchestrate T-cell exclusion [8, 9]. Therefore, developing novel strategies to ignite immunogenic cell death (ICD) and reverse this “cold” status is the holy grail of neuro-oncology [10].

Metabolic reprogramming is a recognized hallmark of cancer, yet the role of trace metal homeostasis in tumorigenesis has only recently gained prominence [11]. Copper, an essential cofactor for mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, is paradoxically upregulated in GBM to sustain high bioenergetic demands [12–14]. In 2022, Tsvetkov et al. defined “cuproptosis,” a distinct form of regulated cell death triggered by the direct binding of copper to lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [15]. Unlike apoptosis or ferroptosis, cuproptosis is uniquely characterized by mitochondrial proteotoxic stress and membrane rupture [16–18].

Herein lies a critical translational gap: while the cytotoxic mechanisms of cuproptosis are established, its non-cell-autonomous effects on the immune system remain unexplored. We propose that the mitochondrial collapse inherent to cuproptosis acts as a potent “danger signal” generator. specifically releasing mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) to activate the cytosolic cGAS-STING pathway [19]. This review synthesizes emerging evidence to posit cuproptosis as a metabolic bridge to immunity [20]. By elucidating how copper-induced mitochondrial stress reprograms the GBM microenvironment, we provide a roadmap for combining copper ionophores with immunotherapies to overcome therapeutic resistance [21, 22] (Fig. 1). It should be noted that while the metabolic framework of cuproptosis is established, its immunogenic implications in GBM remain largely a subject of informed hypothesis based on generalized immune signaling.

The metabolic vulnerability: rewiring copper homeostasis in GBM

Transition metals serve as double-edged swords in cellular physiology; while indispensable as catalytic cofactors for enzymes governing oxidative phosphorylation and detoxification, their excess precipitates lethal cytotoxicity via oxidative damage and proteostatic collapse [23]. In the context of GBM, copper homeostasis is profoundly dysregulated to meet the bioenergetic and signaling demands of rapid tumor expansion [24]. Clinical data consistently reveal elevated serum and intratumoral copper levels in GBM patients compared to healthy controls, correlating with tumor grade and poor prognosis [25]. This “copper addiction” is mechanically underpinned by the overexpression of the high-affinity copper importer SLC31A1 (CTR1) and intracellular chaperones such as ATOX1, which are hijacked to drive angiogenesis and MEK/ERK signaling [26]. While this avid uptake fuels tumorigenesis, it simultaneously exposes a metabolic Achilles’ heel: the susceptibility to copper toxicity.

However, intracellular copper accumulation alone is insufficient to trigger cuproptosis; the metabolic state of the cell strictly dictates its vulnerability [27]. Unlike the “Warburg effect” phenotype where cells rely predominantly on aerobic glycolysis, cuproptosis is inextricably linked to mitochondrial respiration. Tsvetkov et al. demonstrated that cells relying on the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle are hypersensitive to copper-induced death, whereas basal glycolysis confers resistance [28]. This is particularly relevant to GBM, which is characterized by metabolic heterogeneity; specifically, glioma stem-like cells (GSCs) and cells at the invasive front often exhibit a dependence on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), rendering them primary targets for cuproptosis induction [29]. Importantly, acknowledging this OXPHOS dependency resolves a critical spatial paradox within the GBM microenvironment. The bulk of the GBM core is notoriously hypoxic and heavily reliant on HIF-1α-driven glycolysis—a “Warburg-dominant” state that is intrinsically refractory to copper-induced proteotoxicity. Therefore, cuproptosis should not be viewed as a blunt instrument for bulk tumor debulking, but rather as a precision metabolic weapon. Its lethality is anatomically restricted to the well-oxygenated perivascular niches and the highly invasive tumor margins where glioma stem-like cells (GSCs) preferentially reside and exploit mitochondrial respiration. By specifically eradicating these OXPHOS-dependent populations at the invasive edge, cuproptosis therapeutically complements traditional hypoxia-targeting or anti-angiogenic modalities, dismantling the tumor’s capacity for recurrence and parenchymal invasion.

The molecular execution of cuproptosis hinges on the mitochondrial matrix enzyme Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) [30]. As an upstream regulator, FDX1 functions as a reductase that converts relatively non-toxic Cu2 + into highly reactive Cu1+ [31, 32]. This monovalent copper binds with high affinity to lipoylated proteins, specifically the dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT), the E2 component of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex [32]. This binding event triggers the aberrant oligomerization of DLAT and the loss of iron-sulfur (Fe-S) cluster proteins, precipitating acute proteotoxic stress within the mitochondrial matrix [33]. Consequently, targeting the FDX1-lipoylation axis transforms the GBM cell’s metabolic dependence on the TCA cycle into a mechanism of regulated suicide [33] (Fig. 2).

The mechanistic bridge: translating mitochondrial stress into immunogenicity

The canonical understanding of cuproptosis describes a cell-autonomous demise driven by metabolic collapse; however, the profound immunologic consequences of this process stem from the catastrophic failure of mitochondrial integrity [34–36]. Unlike the organized disassembly seen in apoptosis, copper-induced death precipitates a form of mitochondrial catastrophe that converts the organelle from an energy power plant into a potent signaling hub for innate immunity [34–36]. The initiation of this cascade begins with the aberrant oligomerization of lipoylated DLAT, which induces severe proteotoxic stress within the mitochondrial matrix exceeding the folding capacity of resident chaperones such as HSP60 and HSP70 [34–36]. This unresolvable protein aggregation triggers the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and the stochastic rupture of the mitochondrial membranes, a biophysical event distinct from the BAX/BAK-mediated pore formation characteristic of intrinsic apoptosis [37, 38]. The physical breach of the mitochondrial envelope results in the indiscriminate release of matrix components into the cytosol, including reactive oxygen species (ROS), ATP, and, most critically, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [39].

Extrapolating from generalized immunological principles, the release of mtDNA is hypothesized to represent a critical ‘danger signal’ that could bridge cuproptosis to immune surveillance in the GBM context. Evolutionarily derived from alpha-proteobacteria, mtDNA retains hypomethylated CpG motifs that are distinct from nuclear DNA and are recognized by the host innate immune system as “foreign” or pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) [40–42]. Under homeostatic conditions, mtDNA is compacted by transcription factor A, mitochondrial (TFAM) and sequestered within the matrix; however, upon cuproptosis-induced membrane rupture, this genetic material is expelled into the cytoplasm [40–42]. Once in the cytosol, dsDNA is detected by cGAS, a pivotal nucleotidyltransferase that functions as a cytosolic DNA sensor [43, 44]. Upon binding to the sugar-phosphate backbone of the leaked mtDNA, cGAS undergoes a conformational change that catalyzes the synthesis of the non-canonical secondary messenger 2’3’-cyclic GMP-AMP (cGAMP) from ATP and GTP [45–47].

The synthesis of cGAMP initiates a high-fidelity signaling cascade by binding to the Stimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) adaptor protein anchored in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane [45–47]. This binding event induces a conformational shift in STING, triggering its translocation from the ER to the Golgi apparatus, where it recruits and activates TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) [48, 49]. Activated TBK1 subsequently phosphorylates the transcription factor Interferon Regulatory Factor 3 (IRF3), promoting its dimerization and nuclear translocation [48, 49]. Within the nucleus, IRF3 orchestrates the transcriptional upregulation of Type I interferons and a spectrum of pro-inflammatory cytokines, establishing a “hot” cytokine milieu essential for priming anti-tumor immunity [50]. Furthermore, the activation of this pathway upregulates the expression of MHC-I molecules, thereby enhancing the visibility of tumor antigens to cytotoxic T cells [50].

A critical mechanistic distinction must be made regarding why cuproptosis is superior to apoptosis in eliciting this immunogenic response. In classical apoptosis, the activation of effector caspases (caspase-3 and − 7) leads to the proteolytic cleavage and inactivation of critical immune signaling proteins, including cGAS, MAVS, and IRF3, effectively enforcing “immunological silence” to prevent autoimmunity during physiological cell turnover [50]. In stark contrast, cuproptosis is a caspase-independent form of cell death; consequently, the cGAS-STING signaling axis remains intact and functional throughout the dying process [50]. This preservation of the sensing machinery allows for a sustained and amplified production of interferons and chemokines, positioning cuproptosis as a uniquely immunogenic modality capable of overcoming the immunosuppressive barriers of the GBM microenvironment [39]. Thus, the copper-mitochondria-cGAS axis represents a fundamental rewiring of cellular stress into a systemic call for immune elimination [51].

Comparative immunogenicity: cuproptosis in the context of other RCD pathways

Unlike ferroptosis, which relies on iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and exhibits highly context-dependent immunogenicity, cuproptosis is driven by mitochondrial proteotoxic stress. While ferroptosis is generally considered immunogenic, it can also create metabolically suppressive environments depending on the degree of glutathione depletion or the release of anti-inflammatory lipid mediators in certain tumor types. Cuproptosis, resulting from definitive mitochondrial aggregation of lipoylated proteins, may offer a more reliable and less context-dependent ICD signal in the metabolically robust invasive margin of GBM [13, 52, 53].

Perhaps most critically, cuproptosis exhibits a unique anatomical specificity in its DAMPs release that distinguishes it from the plasma-membrane explosive ICD modes: pyroptosis and necroptosis. Both pyroptosis and necroptosis culminate in the massive permeabilization of the plasma membrane, leading to an ‘explosive’ release of cytosolic content and pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β [54]. While highly immunogenic, this can sometimes lead to excessive, localized inflammation.

In contrast, we hypothesize that cuproptosis initiates a unique sub-lethal mitochondrial-driven ICD. Because cuproptosis is driven by FDX1-mediated mitochondrial matrix stress and membrane rupture, it may occur without the immediate, catastrophic rupture of the plasma membrane [55]. Crucially, as a caspase-independent process, cuproptosis preserves the cytosolic DNA sensing machinery, avoiding its caspase-mediated cleavage which typically occurs during tolerogenic apoptosis. This unique preservation allows cuproptosis to convert the organelle into a dedicated danger signal generator while keeping the cytosolic sensor pathways intact for sustained interferon production. This places cuproptosis in a distinctive niche among the ICD modalities, characterized by conserved cytosolic integrity coupled with definitive mitochondrial catastrophe.

Remodeling the TIME: from “cold” to “hot”

The culmination of the mitochondrial stress-cGAS-STING signaling axis is not merely the demise of the individual glioma cell, but the radical reprogramming of the surrounding TIME. GBM is notoriously characterized as an immune “desert” or “cold” tumor, dominated by a paucity of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and an abundance of immunosuppressive myeloid cells [51]. Cuproptosis is positioned as a candidate trigger to facilitate a transition toward an inflamed, ‘hot’ phenotype, potentially via a coordinated engagement of both innate and adaptive immune arms.

Spatiotemporal coordination of DAMPs and the licensure of Batf3 + dendritic cells

Initially, the collapse of mitochondrial ATP synthase during cuproptosis leads to the active efflux of ATP into the extracellular space. Brain-resident microglia, acting as the primary immunological sentinels of the CNS, are exceptionally sensitive to these early purinergic signals. Extracellular ATP binds to ionotropic P2Y2 and P2 × 7 receptors densely expressed on these resident microglia, initiating the early ‘find-me’ cascade. This microglial activation is a prerequisite for the subsequent recruitment of peripheral immune cells and the maturation of infiltrating dendritic cells [53].

The efficiency of antigen presentation in the CNS is historically limited by the scarcity of professional antigen-presenting cells (APCs). Cuproptosis overcomes this barrier by orchestrating a “Find-me” and “Eat-me” signaling complex that precisely licenses Batf3-dependent type 1 conventional dendritic cells (cDC1s), the lineage essential for cross-priming CD8 + T cells [51]. Unlike the passive leakage seen in necrosis, the release of Damage-Associated Molecular Patterns (DAMPs) during cuproptosis follows a specific spatiotemporal hierarchy [51]. Initially, the collapse of mitochondrial ATP synthase leads to the active efflux of ATP into the extracellular space. This extracellular ATP acts as a potent chemoattractant, binding to ionotropic P2Y2 and P2 × 7 purinergic receptors on resident microglia and infiltrating DCs. Activation of P2 × 7 induces K+ efflux and Ca2 + influx, triggering the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome and the processing of pro-IL-1β into its bioactive form, which is crucial for the initial recruitment of APCs. Subsequently, the release of High-Mobility Group Box 1 (HMGB1) from the nucleus occurs in a hyper-acetylated state, preventing its oxidation and maintaining its capacity to bind TLR4 and RAGE receptors on DCs [56]. This ligation activates the MyD88-IRAK4-NF-κB axis, providing the necessary “Signal 2” (co-stimulation) for DC maturation [56]. Crucially, the IFN-β secreted downstream of the tumor-intrinsic cGAS-STING pathway function in an autocrine and paracrine loop to upregulate the expression of the transporter associated with antigen processing and tapasin. This enhances the peptide-loading complex’s efficiency, facilitating the processing of cuproptosis-derived neoantigens onto MHC Class I molecules. Consequently, this multi-step activation protocol ensures that cDC1s are not merely recruited but are functionally “licensed” to migrate to deep cervical lymph nodes and prime naïve T cells, bridging the gap between innate sensing and adaptive execution [56].

The physical egress of these licensed cDC1s from the immune-privileged central nervous system to peripheral lymphoid organs represents a major anatomical bottleneck. Emerging neuroimmunological paradigms reveal that this migration is not a diffuse process but highly directed, utilizing newly characterized anatomical corridors. Specifically, cuproptosis-licensed DCs and tumor neoantigens exploit the glymphatic system to reach the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), subsequently draining through the meningeal lymphatic vessels (mLVs). Furthermore, the ocular-brain lymphatic route has recently been identified as a critical gateway for CNS antigen clearance. By tracking along the optic nerve sheaths and dural lymphatics, these specific pathways provide the essential anatomical conduits that allow cuproptosis-derived DAMPs and matured APCs to breach the CNS barriers and successfully arrive at the deep cervical lymph nodes for robust T cell cross-priming (Fig. 3).

Metabolic-epigenetic reprogramming of GAMs

GAMs typically adopt an immunosuppressive M2-like phenotype, sustained by OXPHOS and fatty acid oxidation [57]. Cuproptosis disrupts this protumorigenic status not only through cytokine signaling but by enforcing a profound immunometabolic and epigenetic reprogramming [58]. The pivotal mechanism involves the cGAS-STING-IFN-β axis, which fundamentally alters the transcriptional landscape of GAMs [59]. Upon IFNAR ligation, the JAK1-TYK2-STAT1 signaling pathway is activated. Phosphorylated STAT1 (p-STAT1) homodimers translocate to the nucleus, where they compete with STAT3 and STAT6 for binding to the promoters of inflammatory genes [60].

More critically, recent evidence suggests that the copper released from dying tumor cells is taken up by surrounding macrophages via CTR1, where it acts as a cofactor for superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) and cytochrome c oxidase (COX) [61–63]. This acute intracellular copper flux, combined with STING activation, triggers a “Warburg-like” metabolic switch in GAMs, shifting their metabolism from OXPHOS to aerobic glycolysis [64]. This metabolic shift causes the accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates, specifically succinate, which inhibits prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) enzymes. PHD inhibition leads to the stabilization of HIF-1α, a transcription factor that directly drives the expression of the M1 marker inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) and the inflammatory cytokine IL-12 [65]. Furthermore, the lactate-depleted microenvironment reduces the histone lactylation (H3K18la) levels in macrophages, an epigenetic mark previously shown to support M2 polarization. Thus, cuproptosis dismantles the M2 phenotype through a dual mechanism: direct transcriptional overrides via STAT1 and an indirect epigenetic rewiring driven by metabolic substrate availability (Fig. 4).

Importantly, this immunometabolic remodeling is unlikely to impact all myeloid lineages uniformly. The GAM population is ontogenetically heterogeneous, comprising both yolk-sac-derived tissue-resident microglia at the tumor periphery and bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) recruited to the hypoxic core and perivascular niches—the precise anatomical sites where copper-induced proteotoxicity and DAMP release are most concentrated [66]. BMDMs exhibit a remarkably higher degree of metabolic and epigenetic plasticity compared to their microglial counterparts. Consequently, we hypothesize that the cuproptosis-induced “Warburg-like switch” and the subsequent erasure of M2-supportive epigenetic marks—such as the reduction in H3K18la due to altered lactate flux—may predominantly and more robustly reprogram the BMDM compartment. By converting these deeply infiltrating, highly suppressive peripheral monocytes into pro-inflammatory effectors, cuproptosis specifically targets the most recalcitrant component of the GBM stroma (Fig. 4).

Endothelial activation and the CTL cytotoxic loop

The final and most critical step in TIME remodeling is the infiltration and functional execution of cytotoxic CD8 + T lymphocytes. The BBB typically excludes T cells via the downregulation of adhesion molecules on the brain endothelial cells (BECs). It is critical to emphasize that the proposed effects of cuproptosis on BBB permeability and endothelial activation represent a strictly unverified hypothesis. Currently, there is a lack of robust, direct experimental evidence in the literature to support this specific cuproptosis-driven cascade. Drawing extrapolations from broader ICD and cGAS-STING literature, we hypothesize that the inflammatory cascade initiated by cuproptosis might theoretically influence the neurovascular unit. In this proposed model, the robust production of CXCL9 and CXCL10 by STING-activated tumor cells and repolarized M1-like macrophages could establish a chemotactic gradient that diffuses toward the perivascular space. Furthermore, because sustained Type I IFN signaling is generally known to upregulate ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 on the luminal surface of BECs [67], we speculate that localized IFN production following cuproptosis might permit the firm arrest and diapedesis of CXCR3 + CD8+ T cells and NK cells. However, whether cuproptosis-derived signals can achieve sufficient amplitude to functionally reverse endothelial anergy in the GBM microenvironment remains an open question requiring rigorous in vivo validation. If such T-cell infiltration occurs, we speculate that their effector function could be amplified by the unique inflammatory milieu created by cuproptosis. Unlike the nutrient-deprived environment of a proliferating tumor, the theoretical post-cuproptotic niche would be rich in ROS and inflammatory cytokines but depleted of immunosuppressive metabolites like adenosine. This environment could hypothetically sustain the effector function of T cells, preventing the rapid onset of exhaustion characterized by TOX and PD-1 high expression. Upon recognizing peptide-MHC complexes, CTLs release perforin and granzyme B. Interestingly, because granzyme B can cleave components of the electron transport chain, it might further exacerbate mitochondrial stress in target cells, thereby creating a theoretical feed-forward loop of mitochondrial-dependent cell death to eliminate residual disease and promote epitope spreading.

Clinical translation and therapeutic strategies: from bench to bedside

The elucidation of the cuproptosis-cGAS-STING axis provides a compelling biological rationale for therapeutic intervention; however, the translation of these molecular insights into clinical efficacy for GBM requires overcoming significant physiological and pharmacological barriers. To transition from proof-of-concept to standard-of-care, three critical pillars must be addressed: precision patient stratification, BBB penetrance, and the rational design of combinatorial regimens to counteract adaptive resistance.

Precision oncology: the biomarker paradox of FDX1

A fundamental tenet of modern oncology is that metabolic therapies are rarely universally effective. The efficacy of cuproptosis induction is strictly contingent upon the specific metabolic wiring of the tumor, necessitating robust predictive biomarkers for patient stratification. Ferredoxin 1 (FDX1) has emerged as the leading candidate, as its expression directly correlates with susceptibility to copper-induced mitochondrial proteotoxicity [68]. However, we position FDX1 as a critically underdeveloped biomarker that currently lacks the fidelity required for clinical deployment.

A significant challenge lies in the spatial and metabolic heterogeneity inherent to GBM. FDX1 expression is intrinsically linked to the cell’s reliance on the TCA cycle and OXPHOS. Consequently, FDX1 may be restricted to glioma stem-like cells (GSCs) in well-oxygenated perivascular niches, while being virtually absent in the ‘Warburg-dominant’ cells of the hypoxic tumor core. This implies that a standard immunohistochemical (IHC) score derived from a single bulk biopsy may significantly misrepresent the overall cuproptosis-permissive landscape of the tumor [69].

Furthermore, a potential functional disconnect exists between FDX1-associated cytotoxicity and actual immunogenic outcomes. While high FDX1 levels ensure rapid cell death in vitro, an ‘over-threshold’ activation might precipitate uncontrolled, stochastic necrosis. Such explosive death could lead to the premature release of suppressive factors or the recruitment of pro-tumorigenic GAMs, rather than the targeted mitochondrial membrane rupture required to activate the cGAS-STING-IFN-I axis [70]. Therefore, FDX1 must be validated not merely as a marker of ‘tumor kill,’ but as a robust predictor of sustained immune-cold phenotype reversal and clinical durability in prospective GBM trials.

Nanotechnology: breaching the blood-brain barrier

The BBB represents the single greatest attrition factor in neuro-oncology drug development. While small molecule copper ionophores like Elesclomol and Disulfiram (DSF) have shown promise in vitro, their clinical utility in GBM is hampered by poor solubility, rapid hepatic metabolism, and non-specific systemic distribution. To achieve therapeutic concentrations within the intracranial tumor site without inducing hepato- or neurotoxicity, advanced nanomedicine delivery systems are essential [18].

Current translational efforts focus on biomimetic nanocarriers. For instance, encapsulating copper-diethyldithiocarbamate complexes within liposomes or polymeric nanoparticles surface-modified with Angiopep-2 or Transferrin allows for receptor-mediated transcytosis across the brain endothelial cells via LRP1 or TfR, respectively [71]. Once inside the parenchyma, these nanoparticles can be engineered to be “stimuli responsive.” Exploiting the unique pathophysiology of the GBM microenvironment—specifically its acidity (pH < 6.5) and high levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs)—allows for the triggered release of the copper payload exclusively within the tumor bed [72].

Moreover, “Metal-Organic Frameworks” (MOFs) represent a cutting-edge approach. Cu-MOFs can be designed to co-deliver copper ions and FDX1-agonists or GSH-depleting agents. By simultaneously supplying the “bullet” (copper) and removing the “armor”, these nanosystems can dramatically lower the threshold for cuproptosis induction, ensuring robust mitochondrial collapse even in metabolically resilient glioma stem cells [73].

Synergistic integration: overcoming adaptive immune resistance and combinatorial regimens

While the induction of cuproptosis successfully orchestrates the recruitment of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) into the GBM parenchyma, monotherapeutic approaches are historically prone to failure due to the rapid onset of adaptive immune resistance. This phenomenon represents a compensatory feedback mechanism wherein the tumor microenvironment actively suppresses the newly elicited immune response to re-establish homeostasis. Specifically, the infiltration of functional CD8 + T cells and their subsequent secretion of IFN-γ paradoxically triggers the upregulation of inhibitory checkpoints on surviving tumor cells and myeloid populations. Mechanistically, IFN-γ signaling engages the JAK-STAT pathway to induce the expression of Interferon Regulatory Factor 1 (IRF1), which directly binds to the promoters of CD274 [74]. Consequently, the very cytokine responsible for cytotoxic execution simultaneously acts as a molecular rheostat, initiating T-cell exhaustion and metabolic starvation via tryptophan depletion. Therefore, the strategic integration of cuproptosis inducers with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, is not merely additive but biologically synergistic. In this combinatorial model, cuproptosis serves as the critical “priming” event that transforms the immunologically “cold” tumor into a “hot” phenotype, thereby generating the requisite substrate—infiltrating T cells—for ICIs to act upon. By blocking the PD-1 axis, ICIs prevent the terminal differentiation of these recruited effector cells into an exhausted state, ensuring a durable and self-sustaining anti-tumor response [75].

Furthermore, the molecular convergence of cuproptosis with standard-of-care radiotherapy offers another avenue for therapeutic amplification. Ionizing radiation primarily exerts its cytotoxicity through nuclear DNA damage and the generation of micronuclei, which are known triggers of the cGAS-STING pathway. However, GBM cells often exhibit robust DNA repair mechanisms and high thresholds for cytosolic DNA sensing [76]. Cuproptosis, by specifically causing the rupture of mitochondrial membranes and the release of mtDNA, provides a distinct and parallel source of cytosolic DNA. The concurrent administration of copper ionophores with radiotherapy could theoretically saturate the cytosolic nucleases (such as TREX1) that typically degrade rogue DNA, thereby lowering the threshold for cGAS activation [77]. This “dual-source” DNA sensing strategy—combining nuclear DNA damage from RT and mitochondrial DNA leakage from cuproptosis—would likely result in a suprathreshold production of Type I interferons, potentially amplifying the abscopal effect where localized treatment hypothetically leads to systemic immune-mediated tumor regression. Such multimodal regimens, which attack the tumor’s metabolic vulnerabilities while simultaneously dismantling its immunological defenses, represent the most promising frontier for overcoming the therapeutic recalcitrance of GBM [78] (Fig. 5).

Challenges and future perspectives

While the therapeutic exploitation of cuproptosis presents a paradigm-shifting opportunity for GBM management, several critical scientific and clinical hurdles must be addressed to ensure its successful translation. The primary challenge lies in the delicate balance of systemic copper homeostasis, particularly within the central nervous system. The brain is uniquely susceptible to copper dysregulation, as exemplified by the neurodegeneration observed in Wilson’s disease, where ATP7B mutations lead to toxic copper accumulation in the basal ganglia [79]. Consequently, the indiscriminate administration of systemic copper ionophores carries a substantial risk of off-target neurotoxicity, potentially damaging healthy neurons and astrocytes that also rely on mitochondrial respiration. Although nanomedicine offers a targeting solution, future research must rigorously define the “therapeutic window”—the specific concentration range that induces lethality in FDX1-high glioma cells while sparing normal neural tissue. Furthermore, long-term monitoring of neurocognitive function in preclinical models is imperative to rule out subtle, cumulative metal toxicity that traditional toxicology screens might miss [80].

A second formidable challenge is the anticipated emergence of adaptive resistance mechanisms. Cancer cells are evolutionarily agile, and under the selective pressure of cuproptosis induction, glioma cells may acquire resistance phenotypes. The most obvious escape route involves the downregulation or genetic loss of FDX1, effectively dismantling the machinery required to convert Cu2 + into its toxic state [81]. Additionally, the upregulation of intracellular antioxidant defense systems represents a potent counter-measure. The Nrf2-KEAP1 pathway, frequently hyperactivated in GBM, drives the expression of genes involved in glutathione (GSH) synthesis (e.g., GCLC, GCLM) and the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11 [82]. Since GSH is a potent copper chelator that sequesters cuprous ions before they can bind to lipoylated proteins, an elevated GSH/GSSG ratio would render cells refractory to cuproptosis. Future studies must therefore investigate the efficacy of combination strategies that preemptively block these escape routes, such as the co-administration of GSH synthesis inhibitors or proteasome inhibitors that prevent FDX1 degradation.

Finally, the complexity of the human immune system poses a translational gap that current animal models struggle to bridge. Most cuproptosis studies rely on syngeneic murine models (e.g., GL261 or CT-2 A), which, while valuable, do not fully recapitulate the profound immunological heterogeneity and the specific immunosuppressive architecture of human GBM [83]. For instance, the expression patterns of cGAS and STING can vary significantly between species and even between different patient molecular subtypes. Moreover, there is a theoretical risk that chronic, unresolved activation of the cGAS-STING pathway could promote a chronic inflammatory state that facilitates tumor angiogenesis rather than rejection, a phenomenon known as inflammation-induced tumorigenesis [84]. This dichotomy is dictated by the signal amplitude and temporal dynamics of mtDNA leakage. Acute, high-amplitude mitochondrial rupture during therapeutic cuproptosis robustly activates the canonical TBK1-IRF3 axis, culminating in the secretion of Type I IFNs necessary for tumor rejection. Conversely, sub-lethal copper stress or incomplete cuproptosis may result in chronic, low-level mtDNA sensing. This persistent signal shifts the STING machinery toward non-canonical NF-κB activation, driving the secretion of pro-angiogenic factors (e.g., VEGF) and immunosuppressive cytokines like IL-6. This chronic inflammatory milieu inadvertently recruits myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and upregulates indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), paradoxically enforcing immune evasion. Therefore, achieving a definitive, supra-threshold induction of cuproptosis is not merely a matter of maximizing cytotoxicity, but an immunological prerequisite to prevent the TME from slipping into a pro-tumorigenic inflammatory state.

Therefore, the development of humanized mouse models and the utilization of patient-derived organoids (PDOs) co-cultured with autologous immune cells will be essential tools to accurately predict the immunogenic efficacy of cuproptosis in the clinical setting [85] (Fig. 6).

Conclusion

GBM remains a fortress of therapeutic resistance, guarded by a restrictive blood-brain barrier, a heterogenous metabolic landscape, and a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment. This review outlines a novel strategic framework that turns the tumor’s metabolic “addiction” to copper and mitochondrial respiration into its ultimate vulnerability. We posit that cuproptosis is not merely an alternative mode of regulated cell death but functions as a critical “metabolic bridge” to innate immunity. By triggering mitochondrial proteotoxicity and the subsequent rupture of the mitochondrial membrane, cuproptosis releases mitochondrial DNA to activate the cGAS-STING signaling axis, thereby breaking the immunological silence that characterizes GBM. This cascade orchestrates a systemic immune response, repolarizing macrophages and recruiting cytotoxic T cells to dismantle the tumor from within. As we move forward, the integration of precision biomarkers, blood-brain barrier-penetrating nanomedicines, and synergistic immunotherapies will be pivotal. Ultimately, mastering the molecular language of copper signaling offers the potential to transform GBM from an incurable fatality into a treatable, immunologically vulnerable disease.
