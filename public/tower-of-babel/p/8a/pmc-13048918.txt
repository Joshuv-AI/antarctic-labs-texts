TIM-3 in AML: pathogenic roles and therapeutic targetability
Authors: Liyan Zhong, Hamed Soleimani Samarkhazan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) remains a therapeutic challenge due to immune evasion and relapse. T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin domain-containing-3 (TIM-3) is an immune checkpoint receptor aberrantly expressed on immune cells and leukemic stem cells (LSCs) in AML. Biologically, TIM-3 predominantly mediates deleterious effects, promoting T-cell/NK dysfunction and supporting LSC self-renewal, but this pathogenic expression also makes TIM-3 a therapeutically actionable target. This narrative review synthesizes current preclinical and clinical evidence on the role of TIM-3 in AML. We examined its structure, signaling pathways, and dual functions in promoting immune suppression and LSC self-renewal. A comprehensive analysis of ongoing therapeutic strategies, including monoclonal antibodies and cellular therapies, was conducted. Relevant literature was identified through searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, with a focus on recently published studies. TIM-3 contributes to immune dysregulation by inducing T-cell exhaustion, impairing NK cell cytotoxicity, and enhancing immunosuppressive myeloid cells. Concurrently, its expression on LSCs drives leukemogenesis through autocrine signaling loops involving Galectin-9 and the β-catenin pathway. Preclinical studies show that TIM-3 blockade reduces leukemic stem-cell frequency and impairs LSC reconstitution in xenograft models, and can reinvigorate anti-leukemic immunity in experimental systems; however, these findings are preclinical and have not yet translated into consistent, randomized clinical benefit in human trials, underscoring the need for biomarker-guided and combination approaches in clinical development (Kikushige et al. Cell Stem Cell 17(3):341–352, 2015; Kikushige et al. Cell Stem Cell7(6):708–717, 2010; Zeidan et al. Lancet Haematol11(1):e38–e50, 2024). Clinically, the anti-TIM-3 antibody sabatolimab showed a tolerable safety profile and preliminary signals of activity when combined with hypomethylating agents; however, randomized phase II data did not demonstrate statistically significant improvements in the primary endpoints (complete response rate and progression-free survival), and the development program has since been re-evaluated in light of these results. TIM-3 also shows promise as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. TIM-3 plays an important and multifaceted role in AML pathogenesis, with evidence supporting both immune-regulatory functions and roles in leukemic stem cell biology; however, definitive proof that TIM-3 is essential for LSC maintenance across all AML subtypes requires additional genetic and functional validation. Targeting TIM-3 remains a biologically compelling strategy to address immune suppression and LSC biology in AML, but definitive clinical benefit has not been established in randomized studies to date; further investigation, especially in biomarker-enriched, low-tumor-burden settings and rational combinations, is required to define its clinical role.Keywords: TIM-3, Acute myeloid leukemia, Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Anti-TIM-3

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is one of the malignant disorders that arises from a subpopulation of stem cells called leukemic stem cells (LSC), which are characterized by clonal expansion. Despite advances in chemotherapy, transplant, and targeted therapy, AML prognosis remains poor, especially in relapsed or refractory cases [10]. There is growing interest in harnessing the immune system against AML, inspired by the success of immune checkpoint blockade in solid tumors [11, 12]. However, conventional checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., anti-PD-1/PD-L1) have shown limited efficacy in AML, indicating that more research is needed to fully understand the immune evasion mechanisms involved [13, 14]. TIM-3 is aberrantly expressed on AML and leukemic blasts, a finding demonstrated in primary patient samples and functional xenotransplant assays [8, 15]. In myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), elevated TIM-3 expression has been observed on TH1 cells, regulatory T cells, CD8 + T cells, and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), which contribute to blast proliferation and the facilitation of immune evasion [16–20]. Numerous studies in AML have documented the overexpression of TIM-3 on LSCs and on CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, resulting in impaired functionality in AML [8, 15, 20–22]. Relapse and poor therapeutic response may be associated with the overexpression of TIM-3 on exhausted CD4 + and CD8 + T cells, as well as leukemic cells, in patients with CML, ALL, and CLL [23–26].

One such mechanism involves TIM-3, an immune checkpoint receptor originally identified on T helper cells (Th1 cells) and Cytotoxic CD8 + T cells (CTLs) as a negative regulator of immune responses. TIM-3 is now known to be expressed on a broad range of immune cells, including T regulatory cells, dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes/macrophages, and others, where it generally contributes to immune tolerance and exhaustion [27, 28]. Notably, TIM-3 is also aberrantly expressed in the leukemic compartment: it is found on AML blasts and is highly enriched on LSCs, while being absent or low on normal HSCs [8]. This dual expression pattern indicates two distinct, related aspects of TIM-3 in AML: (1) a pathogenic biological role, mediating immune evasion by dampening anti-leukemic immunity and directly promoting leukemic stem cell survival, and (2) a therapeutic opportunity, since TIM-3’s selective expression on leukemic cells/LSCs makes it a promising target for interventions aimed at eradicating LSCs or reversing immune suppression [7, 29]. Recent studies have shown that TIM-3 signaling in AML can both suppress anti-tumor T cell function and drive intrinsic pathways in LSCs that enhance leukemia progression, which is associated with a poor prognosis [30, 31]. These findings show TIM-3 as an attractive therapeutic target in AML immunotherapy, so this narrative review will examine the dual roles of TIM-3 in AML, focusing on its immunoregulatory functions and therapeutic potential. This narrative review was based on a comprehensive search of published literature in PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science (2005–2025) using keywords including ‘TIM-3’, ‘acute myeloid leukemia’, ‘leukemia stem cells’, ‘immune checkpoint’, and ‘immunotherapy’. Studies were selected based on relevance to TIM-3 biology, expression, function, and therapeutic targeting in AML. Also, no systematic review protocol was followed, but all included studies were peer-reviewed and relevant to the scope.

TIM-3 structure, ligands, and signaling pathways

TIM-3, which is part of the TIM gene family, has been recognized as an immune checkpoint that contributes to biological responses in human immune cells [32]. TIM-3 is encoded by the human gene HAVCR2, which is located on chromosome 5 at q33.2 [33]. Its structural components include an immunoglobulin V (IgV) domain at the N-terminus, a mucin domain in the middle, a transmembrane region, and a cytoplasmic tail at the C-terminus (Fig. 1). This tail does not contain any recognized inhibitory signaling motifs; however, it does include six tyrosines, two of which, Y256 and Y263, become phosphorylated when the protein interacts with its ligands [28, 33].

To date, TIM-3 has been found to be expressed on T cells (except for Th2 cells) and other immune cells, including NKs, monocytes, macrophages, DCs, and mast cells [29, 34]. When TIM-3 interacts with a ligand, the maturation and activation of innate or adaptive immune cells are diminished, which favors tumor cell proliferation and survival. TIM-3 has multiple known ligands that bind to distinct sites on its IgV domain. The first and most extensively characterized ligand is galectin-9 (Gal-9), followed by high-mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1), phosphatidylserine (PtdSer), and carcinoembryonic antigen cell adhesion molecule 1 (CEACAM-1). Gal-9, which is released by a variety of hematopoietic cells, was the initial TIM-3 ligand to be discovered. Gal-9 ligation to the IgV domain of TIM-3 induces intracellular calcium fluxes and apoptosis of Th1 cells, thereby limiting Th1-mediated immune responses [35]. In addition, several studies have shown that Gal-9 suppresses differentiation/expansion of IL-17–producing Th17 cells and promotes regulatory T cell (Treg) induction in autoimmune/inflammatory models [36, 37]. PtdSer, exposed on apoptotic cells, serves as the second ligand for TIM-3, facilitating apoptotic body clearance and antigen cross-presentation by DCs through binding to a pocket formed by the FG and CC’ loops in TIM-3’s IgV domain [38–40]. HMGB1 is known as an alarmin released by stressed or dying cells [41, 42]. TIM-3 + tumor-infiltrating DCs through TIM-3/HMGB1 interaction can attenuate the innate immune response to nucleic acids. In contrast to other ligands, the binding site of the TIM-3/HMGB1 interaction has not been identified [42]. The fourth described ligand is CEACAM1, which is expressed on hematopoietic cells and can bind to the CC’ and FG loops of TIM-3 [43]. The TIM-3/CEACAM1 interaction has been found to regulate autoimmunity and antitumor immunity (Fig. 1) [43, 44].

The absence of conventional inhibitory signaling motifs in TIM-3 and an incomplete understanding of the intracellular signaling pathway have presented challenges to understanding its mechanism of action. While there is still much to discover, research has demonstrated that when Gal-9 and/or CEACAM1 engage TIM-3, it causes the phosphorylation of two cytoplasmic tyrosines, Y256 and Y263, which bind to BAT3, an HLA-B-associated gene [43, 45, 46]. The phosphorylation of Y256 and Y263 causes BAT3 to be released from TIM-3’s cytoplasmic tail, allowing for TIM-3-mediated inhibition. Subsequently, the src-kinase FYN, which is involved in anergy induction, can also bind in the same region [47]. Thus, a BAT3-FYN switch may determine whether TIM-3 permits or inhibits TCR signaling. Additionally, In CD8⁺ effector T cells, binding of Gal-9 to TIM-3 promotes the co-localization of TIM-3 with the receptor phosphatases CD45 and CD148, resulting in inhibition of TCR signaling [44, 46, 48]. Thus, the suppressive effect of TIM-3 on T cells is exerted through multiple molecular mechanisms, and more research is needed to understand TIM-3 signaling in other immune cells. Structurally, TIM-3’s distinct extracellular IgV fold, including the FG–CC’ ligand-binding cleft and non-canonical disulfide pattern, together with a heavily glycosylated mucin domain that facilitates cis/trans interactions (for example with CEACAM1), explain its ability to bind multiple ligands (Gal-9, PtdSer, HMGB1, CEACAM1) and thereby mediate immune-suppressive effects; concurrently, the cytoplasmic tail’s phosphorylatable tyrosines (Y256/Y263) that regulate BAT3/FYN recruitment provide a direct molecular entry point for signalling cascades (e.g., NF-κB and the β-catenin axis) that drive leukemic stem-cell self-renewal. These structural features therefore mechanistically link TIM-3’s extracellular ligand promiscuity and cis-partnering to immune modulation with its intracellular signalling capacity that supports LSC-intrinsic pro-leukemic programs in AML [7, 39, 44, 46, 49].

Immune regulatory role of TIM-3 in AML

The term ‘dual’ in many prior descriptions refers to two roles, (i) TIM-3’s pathogenic biological effects that impair anti-leukemic immunity and support LSCs, and (ii) the therapeutic advantage that arises because those same properties make TIM-3 targetable. There is little evidence that TIM-3 produces a net beneficial biological effect on host anti-AML immunity; rather, its ‘positive’ aspect is clinical, targetability by antibodies or cell therapies [7, 29].

TIM-3 as a T cell checkpoint

This subsection focuses on TIM-3’s role on immune cells, particularly T cells; TIM-3 expression on leukemic blasts and LSCs, and their cell-intrinsic TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine loop, are discussed separately in Sect. 4. Across preclinical models and clinical studies, TIM-3 commonly co-expresses with PD-1 on antigen-experienced CD8⁺ (and in some reports CD4⁺) T cells, and this co-expression defines a functionally exhausted phenotype characterized not only by reduced cytokine secretion (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-2) but also by impaired proliferation, decreased cytotoxicity and altered differentiation state. In murine AML models, TIM-3/PD-1 co-expression increases with disease progression and identifies CD8⁺ T cells with severely diminished effector function; combined blockade of TIM-3 and PD-1 more effectively restores anti-leukemic activity than blocking either pathway alone [50, 51]. Human studies corroborate the clinical relevance of this signature: elevated PD-1hi TIM-3+ T cell frequencies in peripheral blood or bone marrow have been associated with relapse after allo-HSCT and with other adverse outcomes in AML cohorts [52]. Mechanistically, TIM-3 engagement (e.g., via Gal-9 or CEACAM1) contributes to inhibitory signaling at the immune synapse and to recruitment of phosphatases that blunt TCR signaling, providing a molecular rationale for combination checkpoint blockade in AML [28, 50]. Finally, studies of unconventional T-cell subsets (for example Vδ2 γδ T cells) suggest that PD-1 upregulation alone does not always equal dysfunction, TIM-3 marks a subset more refractory to PD-1 blockade and in some cohorts requires TIM-3 inhibition to restore cytokine production [53].

Myeloid and innate immune cells

NK cells: TIM-3 frequently marks activated/mature NK subsets that can produce IFN-γ and degranulate robustly, yet the functional consequence of TIM-3 expression depends on how the receptor is engaged and the clinical/sample context. Several human studies show TIM-3 is enriched on the most responsive CD56bright/CD56dim NK subsets and can function as a co-receptor to enhance IFN-γ and cytokine responses [3, 5]. However, experimental cross-linking of TIM-3 or exposure to TIM-3 ligands in specific contexts can blunt NK cytotoxicity, indicating that ligand engagement (e.g., Gal-9 on target cells) or sustained exposure may convert a marker of activation into a suppressive signal [5]. Taken together these data are not contradictory when parsed mechanistically: TIM-3 upregulation often reflects prior activation or maturation (so TIM-3high NKs can be ‘responsive’ ex vivo), but the net effect in vivo will depend on (i) whether TIM-3 is cross-linked by ligands on targets (Gal-9/CEACAM1), (ii) tissue source (peripheral blood vs. bone marrow), (iii) disease stage or recent therapies (post-transplant or active disease), (iv) co-expression of other receptors and activation stimuli, and (v) the assay used (antibody cross-linking vs. ligand exposure vs. functional killing assays). This mechanistic frame explains why some AML cohorts report preserved/enhanced cytokine or degranulation readouts while others observe impaired killing when TIM-3 is engaged in situ; harmonized reporting of tissue source, ligand levels and functional assay conditions will resolve many apparent discrepancies [6, 54].

Dendritic cells (DCs): TIM-3 on tumor-associated DCs limits nucleic acid sensing by sequestering HMGB1, thereby reducing endosomal activation and innate responses. This mechanism was shown in murine and human tumor DCs. In other models, TIM-3 ligation or agonists can promote DC maturation and chemokine (e.g., CXCL9) production, improving T cell priming. Thus, DC outcomes are context dependent: TIM-3 may suppress DC-driven nucleic acid sensing in the tumor microenvironment yet can also modulate maturation under defined stimuli. Importantly, whether these DC mechanisms operate identically in AML bone marrow has not been directly established and should be stated as a hypothesis [2, 42].

Monocytes/macrophages : TIM-3 contributes to apoptotic cell clearance through PtdSer recognition and can temper macrophage TLR responses. Experimental studies show TIM-3 signaling reduces macrophage activation, whereas TIM-3 blockade increases phagocytosis and pro-inflammatory responses. TIM-3 signaling has also been associated with M2-like features in some tumor models, which could foster an immunosuppressive niche. Direct functional data in AML macrophages are limited and further investigation is required [1, 40].

Myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs): Several studies indicate the TIM-3/Gal-9 and TIM-3/CEACAM1 axes promote myeloid immunosuppressive phenotypes and are associated with reduced CD8^+ T-cell function. In AML bone marrow, expansion of suppressive myeloid populations is a recognized mechanism of immune evasion; TIM-3–linked pathways are a plausible contributor but mechanistic confirmation in AML cohorts is still emerging [4, 55–57].

In summary, TIM-3 directly suppresses the anti-tumor efficacy of T and NK cells, enhances the immunosuppressive capabilities of MDSC and M2 macrophages, and modifies the immune-regulatory functions of DC cells. All of these things come together to create a positive feedback loop in the “tumor-immune microenvironment” that pushes the disease forward. Consequently, treatment approaches designed to inhibit TIM-3 may not only directly suppress tumor proliferation but also modify the immunological setting and enhance the immunoregulatory functions of immune cells (Fig. 2). Importantly, recent work emphasizes that TIM-3 biology in AML is not strictly separable into “immune” versus “leukemia-intrinsic” compartments but reflects direct crosstalk between AML blasts/LSCs and immune cells within the bone-marrow niche. AML cells and LSCs can be a source of Gal-9 that engages TIM-3 on neighboring T cells and NK cells, driving local exhaustion and impaired cytotoxicity, while the same Gal-9–TIM-3 axis operates in an autocrine/paracrine manner on TIM-3+ LSCs to activate NF-κB and β-catenin (Wnt) programs that promote self-renewal and chemoresistance. This dual action, suppression of anti-leukemic immunity and simultaneous reinforcement of LSC stemness, creates a feed-forward loop in the marrow niche that favors persistence and relapse. The original demonstration of a TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine stimulatory loop in LSCs and elevated circulating Gal-9 in AML patients provides direct mechanistic evidence for this model [7]. Clinically, cohorts have correlated high Gal-9 / TIM-3 axis expression with induction failure and with higher frequencies of exhausted PD-1hiTIM-3+ T cells in the marrow, supporting the biological relevance of this niche crosstalk to treatment resistance [19, 58].

TIM-3 in leukemic blasts and LSCs

Aside from its immunological effects, TIM-3 has critical cell-intrinsic roles in AML. Importantly, these intrinsic LSC signals are functionally connected to the immune compartment in the marrow: AML blasts/LSCs secrete Galectin-9 and other modulators that engage TIM-3 on both leukemic and immune cells, thereby simultaneously reinforcing LSC self-renewal and inducing local immune exhaustion, a reciprocal crosstalk that underpins persistence and relapse [7, 58]. It is highly expressed on leukemic progenitor cells in most AML patients. In a landmark study, Kikushige et al. identified TIM-3 as a surface marker that distinguishes LSCs from normal HSCs [8, 49]. This finding has been confirmed in larger cohorts, as examined by Haubner et al., who found that 78.5% of AML patient samples contained TIM-3 + LSCs at initial diagnosis [59]. Functionally, TIM-3 engages a TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine loop in LSCs that activates NF-κB and β-catenin signaling to promote self-renewal; neutralization of Gal-9 or TIM-3 disrupts this loop and reduces LSC reconstitution in xenograft models [7]. In contrast, TIM-3 is either absent or expressed at modest levels on normal myeloid progenitors and other hematopoietic cells, making it a reasonably specific antigen for leukemia. It appears that TIM-3 + LSCs play a crucial role in the progression of leukemia. Specifically, when AML cells from patients are transplanted into immunodeficient mice, the fraction that is CD34 + CD38-Tim-3 + is the only one that successfully engrafts and develops leukemia [15]. These xenotransplant and functional studies support a role for TIM-3 in LSC biology, TIM-3⁺ fractions preferentially engraft in immunodeficient mice and TIM-3/Gal-9 neutralization reduces xenograft reconstitution, but xenograft activity does not prove that complete LSC eradication is achievable in patients; clinical translation requires prospective biomarker assessments and randomized evaluation [7, 8]. However, engraftment and antibody/ligand neutralization experiments establish a functional role rather than absolute proof that TIM-3 is universally essential for LSC maintenance, definitive causality across AML subtypes would be strengthened by orthogonal loss-of-function/rescue genetic experiments and broader validation in primary patient cohorts [7, 8].

A pivotal finding by Takayanagi et al. revealed that ligation of TIM-3 by Gal-9 forms an autocrine signaling loop in AML LSCs [7]. This autocrine signal enhances the self-renewal of TIM-3-positive LSC by activating the NF-kB and β-catenin signaling pathways, which are recognized for promoting LSC self-renewal [7]. A neutralizing anti-Gal-9 antibody treatment of AML cells interrupted the TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine loop, which in turn reduced the leukemia reconstitution capability of AML LSCs in xenotransplantation trials, demonstrating the significant effects of this autocrine loop [7]. Normal HSC formation requires dose-dependent Wnt/β-catenin signaling [60]. Increased β-catenin expression and reduced signaling activity have been linked to hematological diseases, including AML [61]. Increased β-catenin expression and Wnt signaling activity support LSC self-renewal [62]. Additionally, a recent study by Sakoda et al. found that the β-catenin pathway is activated by the TIM-3/HCK/p120-catenin axis, which mediates the self-renewal and maintenance of AML LSC [49]. The results of these investigations point to the involvement of unregulated Wnt/β-catenin in the development, survival, and resistance to drugs of AML LSC. Hence, therapeutic strategies targeting the TIM-3/Gal-9 autocrine loop warrant further investigation.

Studies of TIM-3 and clinical outcome in AML have produced heterogeneous results and are best interpreted in their biological/genetic context. Briefly, some cohorts link high TIM-3 to adverse features and worse survival (e.g., associations with FLT3-ITD and poor-risk groups), while other studies find no adverse association or even increased chemotherapy sensitivity in specific genetic subsets (for example, CBF-AML). For detailed study results and cohort descriptions see Sect. 4 (TIM-3 in leukemic blasts and LSCs), which summarizes the primary reports. Thus, TIM-3’s prognostic value is context-dependent (cell type assessed, AML genetic subtype, and microenvironment), and should not be used as a standalone prognostic marker without integrated molecular/immune profiling.

A different aspect of TIM-3 in leukemia biology is its association with relapse following chemotherapy or allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT). LSCs exhibiting elevated TIM-3 levels are recognized for their relative chemoresistance, resisting induction therapy and resulting in measurable residual disease (MRD). Following allo-HSCT in AML, recurrence is believed to originate from LSCs. Sakoda et al. (2024) demonstrated that CD34 + CD38- bone marrow cells from AML patients post-engraftment comprised TIM-3 + cells possessing the patient’s leukemic mutations, alongside TIM-3- cells originating from donor HSCs [63]. The finding validated that TIM-3 + CD34+CD38- cells represent the detectable residual leukemic stem cells (MR-LSCs) post-transplantation. Patients exhibiting TIM-3 + MR-LSClow status (< 60%) post-HSCT showed remarkable outcomes, with a modest 22.2% relapse rate and a median event-free survival (EFS) of 1581 days. In contrast, those with TIM-3 + MR-LSCint/high status (≥ 60%) experienced numerous relapses (87.5% of cases) and had a median EFS of just 140.5 days [63]. Moreover, Dama et al. indicated that AML patients who did not respond to induction therapy had overexpression of the TIM-3/Gal-9 axis [58]. Furthermore, in a clinical trial that combined Selinexor with high-dose cytarabine and mitoxantrone, non-responders had elevated Gal-9 expression on leukemia cells and increased TIM-3 expression on BM T cells compared to responders [58]. The overexpression of TIM-3/Gal-9 was associated with induction failure, suggesting that TIM-3 signaling contributes to immune evasion and chemoresistance. Collectively, TIM-3 promotes AML by driving LSC self-renewal, immunosuppression, and chemoresistance, making it a compelling therapeutic target.

TIM-3 as a biomarker in AML

The unique expression pattern and functional significance of TIM-3 in AML extend beyond its biological roles, positioning it as a promising biomarker with potential applications in diagnosis, prognosis, and prediction of therapeutic responses. Nevertheless, clinical data are heterogeneous and standardized assay/threshold definitions are lacking; thus TIM-3 should currently be considered a candidate biomarker that requires prospective validation and assay harmonization before being described as essential or definitive for clinical decision-making [9, 59].

Diagnostic biomarker

A key diagnostic utility of TIM-3 lies in its ability to distinguish LSCs from normal HSCs. As established in seminal studies, TIM-3 is highly expressed on CD34 + CD38- LSCs in a majority of AML patients, while it is absent or expressed at very low levels on their normal counterparts [8, 49, 59]. This differential expression makes TIM-3 a valuable surface marker for identifying and isolating the LSC population via flow cytometry, which is crucial for MRD monitoring and for studying the biology of the disease-initiating cells [15]. The specificity of TIM-3 for leukemic blasts, particularly LSCs, offers a significant advantage over more broadly expressed markers, providing a clearer window into the leukemic compartment.

Prognostic biomarker

The prognostic value of TIM-3 expression in AML is complex and appears to be context-dependent, leading to some controversy. Several studies have associated high TIM-3 expression on either leukemic blasts or immune cells with worse clinical outcomes. For instance, elevated TIM-3 on leukemic cells has been correlated with inferior overall survival and poor-risk genetic features like FLT3-ITD mutations [21, 30, 31]. High TIM-3 expression on T cells, marking exhaustion, has also been linked to increased relapse rates post-allo-HSCT [52].

Conversely, other studies report conflicting findings: Hong et al. linked high TIM-3 on blasts with core-binding factor (CBF) translocations rather than adverse outcomes, while Xu et al. reported that high TIM-3 on blasts associated with better initial chemotherapy responses [64, 65]. These opposing observations can be really when three domain-specific variables are considered: (1) cell population assessed: TIM-3 on blasts/LSCs reflects tumor-intrinsic biology whereas TIM-3 on T cells signals immune exhaustion (different prognostic implications); (2) AML genotype and clinical stage: TIM-3 expression associates with differing genetic subtypes (e.g., FLT3-ITD is reported with poor outcome while CBF-AML may associate with higher TIM-3 but better chemo-sensitivity), so genotype confounds prognostic interpretation; and (3) assay/tissue/ligand context: bone-marrow versus peripheral blood sampling, antibody clone/gating thresholds, and the local abundance of ligands (Gal-9) or co-inhibitory receptors (PD-1/LAG-3) can shift TIM-3 from a neutral/activation marker to a functional mediator of immune suppression [5, 64, 65]. In short, TIM-3’s prognostic association is conditional: a single ‘high TIM-3’ readout without specifying the cell type, genotype, sampling timepoint and assay methodology is likely to produce inconsistent results. Future prognostic studies should therefore pre-specify the cell population measured (blast vs. LSC vs. T cell), report co-expression of key checkpoints and Gal-9 levels, stratify by major genetic subgroups (e.g., CBF vs. FLT3-ITD) and harmonize cut-offs to produce reproducible prognostic data [64, 65].

Briefly, several methodological and biological factors explain why studies report conflicting prognostic associations for TIM-3 in AML. Methodological sources of variability include differences in assay platform (flow cytometry panels and gating strategies versus immunohistochemistry or ELISA for soluble TIM-3), specimen source (bone marrow vs. peripheral blood), sample processing (fresh vs. cryopreserved cells), and cut-off definitions for “high” versus “low” expression, all of which can produce systematic differences in measured TIM-3 frequency or intensity. Analytical validation and antibody specificity for IHC/flow assays also vary across groups and can alter results [66, 67].

Biological sources of discordance include AML’s extensive genetic and clonal heterogeneity, different cytogenetic/molecular subtypes and subclonal architectures can associate TIM-3 expression with distinct biology (for example, associations with FLT3-ITD or CBF-AML produce opposite prognostic signals in some cohorts). Timing of sampling (at diagnosis, after induction, or at MRD/post-transplant) and the specific cell type assessed (blast/LSC vs. exhausted T cell subsets) also change the prognostic meaning of TIM-3. Finally, many published studies are small or retrospective and use different clinical endpoints (OS, CRR, relapse, MRD), which reduces power and increases the chance of contradictory findings [64, 68, 69].

Taken together, these methodological and biological sources argue that future prognostic studies should pre-specify assay methods, report the cell population measured and sampling time, and ideally integrate TIM-3 status with genetic subtype and MRD readouts to produce reproducible, clinically meaningful prognostic data [66, 69].

Predictive biomarker

The most promising emerging role for TIM-3 is as a predictive biomarker, capable of identifying patients most likely to benefit from TIM-3-targeted therapies. The presence of TIM-3 + LSCs or exhausted TIM-3 + PD-1 + T cells could stratify patients for clinical trials involving anti-TIM-3 monoclonal antibodies like sabatolimab or combination therapies [70, 71]. For example, patients with MRD positive for TIM-3 + cells post-transplant represent a population where TIM-3 blockade could be specifically effective in eliminating persistent, treatment-resistant LSCs [70].

Furthermore, the level of TIM-3 expression, along with the co-expression of other immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-1, LAG-3), may predict response to immunotherapy. Patients with a high “immune exhaustion signature” might derive greater benefit from combination checkpoint blockade [50, 72, 73]. As clinical trials progress, validating TIM-3 surface density or soluble TIM-3 levels as predictive biomarkers will be essential for personalizing immunotherapy in AML.

Assay methods for TIM-3 detection

The primary method for assessing TIM-3 biomarker status is multiparameter flow cytometry on bone marrow or peripheral blood samples. This technique allows for the simultaneous detection of TIM-3 on specific cell populations (blasts, LSCs, T cell subsets, NK cells) using panels that include markers like CD34, CD38, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD56, and PD-1 [8, 21, 52]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) on bone marrow biopsies is also used to evaluate TIM-3 protein expression and spatial distribution within the marrow niche [30]. Additionally, measuring levels of soluble TIM-3 (sTIM-3) in plasma by ELISA is being explored as a less invasive, serial monitoring tool, with some studies suggesting a correlation with disease burden [74]. Standardization of these assays and defining cut-off values for positivity are critical next steps for integrating TIM-3 into routine clinical biomarker testing. Therapeutic targeting of TIM-3 in AML

Due to TIM-3’s dual function in inhibiting immune responses and supporting leukemia cells, it has become a significant therapeutic target. The objective of TIM-3–targeted therapy is double: (1) to “release the brakes” on the immune system, therefore revitalizing T cells and NK cells to combat AML, and (2) to directly eradicate TIM-3–expressing leukemic cells, particularly LSCs. Various treatment options are being investigated, including cellular therapies and monoclonal antibodies that inhibit TIM-3 ligand interactions. This paper summarizes key preclinical data and clinical trial findings.

Preclinical studies

The preliminary evidence for the therapeutic potential of TIM-3 targeting originated from in vivo models. Kikushige et al. revealed that an anti–TIM-3 mAb (ATIK2a) can selectively eradicate AML LSCs while preserving normal HSCs [8]. Xenograft experiments demonstrated that administering ATIK2a to AML-engrafted mice resulted in a decreased frequency of human LSCs and eradicated functional LSCs in primary recipients [8]. Mechanistically, the original research antibody used to validate TIM-3 as an LSC target (e.g., ATIK2a) eradicated TIM-3⁺ cells primarily via Fc-effector mechanisms, complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), in xenograft models; by contrast, clinical-stage TIM-3 antibodies use different strategies depending on Fc backbone and engineering: sabatolimab (MBG453) is a humanized IgG4(S228P) that blocks TIM-3 ligand binding and downstream signaling while also showing Fc-dependent activity (notably antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis/ADCP and Fc-dependent enhancement of T-cell killing in vitro), whereas other candidates (for example INCAGN02390) have been engineered with Fc-silencing mutations (N297A) to minimize ADCC/CDC and act predominantly as signaling-blocking antibodies [8, 76]. There is immunologic evidence that inhibiting TIM-3 works synergistically with other checkpoint inhibitors. In preclinical models, concurrent inhibition of TIM-3 and PD-1 enhances antitumor immunity and facilitates tumor remission [50, 72].

Furthermore, TIM-3 “switch receptor” approaches are being investigated in the chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR T-cell) field [77–79]. A unique design involves a chimeric receptor that combines the extracellular domain of TIM-3 with the intracellular signaling domain of CD28 [80]. Fusion proteins combining TIM-3 and CD28 have been engineered to convert inhibitory signals from TIM-3 activation into stimulatory signals via CD28. Upon stimulation with anti-CD3 antibodies, these fusion proteins demonstrate the highest response rates. These proteins have a crucial role in enhancing the cytotoxic ability, activation, and proliferation of regular anti-CD19 CAR T-cells [80]. Another cellular treatment strategy involves TIM-3–targeted CAR T-cell. A second-generation anti-TIM-3 CAR T-cell, developed by Lee et al., has shown strong anti-AML activity, including against primary LSCs [81]. Notably, CAR-T cells directed against TIM-3 may efficiently eliminate primary LSCs obtained from patients, presenting a potential approach for eradicating LSCs in minimal residual disease [81]. Furthermore, He et al. demonstrated that bispecific CAR T-cells targeting CD13 and TIM-3 effectively eliminate AML stem cells while minimizing damage to normal HSCs and other normal tissues in the reported preclinical models. However, targeting myeloid antigens such as CD13 raises a well-described risk of on-target/off-tumor hematologic toxicity, because CD13 is expressed at varying levels on normal myeloid progenitors and some non-hematopoietic epithelia; thus CD13-directed single-antigen CARs could, in principle, cause myeloid depletion or myelosuppression in patients. Preclinical statements of ‘minimal toxicity’ therefore require careful qualification and translational safeguards before clinical use [82, 83].

To address these safety concerns, multiple engineering solutions have been explored and were applied in the studies cited above: (1) dual-antigen or logic-gated designs (e.g., CD13×TIM-3 bispecific/split CARs) restrict effector activity to cells co-expressing both antigens, raising the activation threshold for normal cells that express only CD13 at moderate levels [84]. (2) affinity-tuning of the CAR single-chain variable fragment can spare low-antigen-density normal cells while retaining high-avidity killing of AML cells with high antigen density, effectively widening the therapeutic window [85]. (3) transient expression platforms (mRNA CARs) or use of CAR-NK cells limit long-term persistence of effector cells and thereby reduce duration of potential myelotoxicity; CAR-NK approaches additionally carry lower theoretical risk of severe T-cell–mediated toxicities and can be used as ‘bridging’ or controlled therapies [86, 87]. (4) safety switches (e.g., inducible caspase-9) and other pharmacologic control systems permit rapid elimination of CAR effectors if clinically significant cytopenias occur [88].

Taken together, these strategies explain how CD13-directed approaches can show reduced toxicity in controlled preclinical models, but they also emphasize that clinical translation must (a) demonstrate sparing of normal hematopoiesis in validated human hematopoietic models, (b) include formal safety switches or dosing strategies, and (c) consider CAR platforms (affinity-tuned, dual-antigen, CAR-NK, or transient expression) that minimize long-term on-target off-tumor effects before first-in-human studies [89, 90]. To minimize the potential adverse effects of CAR T-cell, including severe cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity, several researchers are working on integrating CAR into NK cells, thereby creating CAR-NK cells. Initial findings indicate that TIM-3-CAR-NK demonstrates superior anti-tumor efficacy in both in vitro and ex vivo studies [91]. Recently, Pe et al. optimized anti-TIM-3 CAR constructs and demonstrated that using a CD8α spacer/transmembrane domain together with TNFR-family-based costimulatory signals significantly improved CAR expression stability, reduced fratricide, and enhanced in vitro and in vivo anti-AML activity [92].

Emerging strategies are being explored to target the TIM-3 pathway. One such approach is anti-Gal-9 therapy, as Gal-9 serves as a primary ligand that activates TIM-3 signaling; hence, neutralizing Gal-9 might produce comparable dual effects [93]. A humanized anti–Gal-9 antibody, known as LYT-200, has commenced early clinical trials in solid tumors and may also be evaluated in AML, especially in combination with TIM-3 or PD-1 inhibitors. Henry et al. investigated the effectiveness of LYT-200 through both in vivo and in vitro studies involving hematological cancers. The results indicate that in mouse models of T-ALL and AML, LYT-200 exhibits potentially greater efficacy compared to traditional treatments. These results facilitated the initiation of clinical trials for TIM-3 inhibitors in patients [94]. In conclusion, preclinical evidence robustly endorses TIM-3 as a viable therapeutic target. Blocking TIM-3 can directly reduce AML LSCs and, when combined with other immunotherapies, can enhance the body’s ability to fight leukemia.

Clinical trial outcomes

Translating the TIM-3 blocking approach into clinical trials has led to the development of numerous monoclonal antibodies. Presently, the TIM-3 inhibitors undergoing clinical studies comprise MBG453 (also referred to as Sabatolimab), TSR-022, BMS-986,258, LY3321367, SYM023, BGB-A425, and SHR-1702 (Table 2). Sabatolimab is the most advanced high-affinity humanized IgG4 antibody targeting TIM-3 [95]. Sabatolimab was initially evaluated in solid tumors and demonstrated a tolerable safety profile [96].

At present, sabatolimab (MBG453) is the most extensively studied anti–TIM-3 antibody in myeloid malignancies, but the largest randomized, controlled data come from higher-risk MDS rather than de-novo AML. In the randomized phase II STIMULUS-MDS1 trial of treatment-naïve higher-risk MDS, the addition of sabatolimab to hypomethylating agents did not significantly improve the co-primary endpoints of complete response rate or progression-free survival (CR 22% vs. 18%; median PFS 11.1 vs. 8.5 months; differences not statistically significant), prompting program re-assessment for frontline MDS indications [9]. By contrast, clinical testing of sabatolimab in AML has so far been limited to early-phase, exploratory settings. For example, the STIMULUS-AML2 program (NCT04623216) is an open-label phase Ib/II study of sabatolimab (alone or with azacitidine) as pre-emptive therapy in AML patients who are in hematologic complete remission but are MRD-positive after allogeneic stem cell transplantation. Preliminary safety/run-in reports from a small initial cohort suggest tolerability and early signals of activity; however, these AML data remain small and exploratory and must not be interpreted as evidence of the randomized MDS results translating to AML patients [70, 97]. Therefore, in the revised manuscript text we now explicitly label trial results as (MDS) or (AML / MRD-post-allo HSCT) wherever they are discussed so that readers can distinguish observations that arise from MDS cohorts (randomized STIMULUS-MDS studies) from preliminary, hypothesis-generating AML cohorts (STIMULUS-AML2 and other early-phase reports).

Clinical translation of TIM-3 blockade has produced mixed results. Early phase Ib studies of sabatolimab combined with hypomethylating agents reported encouraging overall response rates and an acceptable safety profile in single-arm cohorts; however, randomized data from STIMULUS-MDS1 (a double-blind, placebo-controlled Phase II study) did not meet its two primary endpoints of complete response rate and progression-free survival: the CR rate was 22% with sabatolimab + HMA versus 18% with placebo + HMA (p = 0.77), and the observed prolongation in median PFS (11.1 vs. 8.5 months) did not reach statistical significance (HR 0.75, p = 0.10). These randomized results indicate that early single-arm signals require confirmation in adequately controlled trials and temper expectations for broad benefit from TIM-3 blockade as a routine frontline addition to HMAs [9].

Furthermore, follow-on development decisions have reflected these neutral randomized findings: subsequent program updates and later-phase trials were re-assessed and, for some indications, discontinued or deprioritized by the sponsor. This underlines the importance of (1) identifying predictive biomarkers and responsive disease subgroups (e.g., molecularly defined cohorts or MRD-positive post-transplant settings), and (2) pursuing rigorous randomized testing before concluding clinical utility [98]. Challenges and future directions

Although targeting TIM-3 in AML is biologically beneficial, several obstacles have arisen from research and clinical practice.

Despite the promise of TIM-3 blockade, several important limitations and safety considerations require discussion. First, resistance can be either tumor-intrinsic or tumor-extrinsic. Tumor-intrinsic resistance to TIM-3–directed therapy is likely multifactorial and can include selection or enrichment of TIM-3–negative leukemic clones as well as biochemical and cellular mechanisms that reduce target dependence. Pre-existing intratumoral heterogeneity can allow TIM-3–negative subclones to expand under selective pressure from TIM-3 antibodies or TIM-3–directed cellular therapies, producing clinical antigen-negative relapse, a phenomenon well-described for other targeted immunotherapies (e.g., CD19-negative relapse after CD19 CAR-T). Mechanisms that can cause apparent loss of TIM-3 include (i) expansion of pre-existing TIM-3–negative clones, (ii) transcriptional/epigenetic downregulation of TIM-3, (iii) alternative splicing or mutations in the TIM-3 coding sequence that abrogate antibody binding, (iv) epitope masking or trogocytosis following immune engagement, and (v) lineage or phenotypic switching that removes surface TIM-3 expression. These mechanisms have been documented for antigen loss and escape in other hematologic immunotherapies and are biologically plausible for TIM-3 targeting in AML. Importantly, TIM-3–directed pressure may also select for clones that shift their reliance to parallel stemness and immune-evasion pathways (for example, increased Wnt/β-catenin signaling, upregulation of alternative checkpoints such as LAG-3/TIGIT, or increased secretion of immunosuppressive mediators), further limiting single-agent activity. Because direct clinical data on TIM-3-negative relapse are still limited, the field should prioritize serial monitoring (multi-parameter flow cytometry of TIM-3 on blasts/LSCs, single-cell transcriptomics where available, and MRD surveillance) to detect antigen modulation and clonal evolution early. To mitigate antigen-negative escape, strategies include combinatorial antigen targeting (bispecifics or dual CARs), ligand-targeted approaches (for example anti-Gal-9), rational combinations that block compensatory pathways, and clinical trial designs that incorporate serial correlative sampling to detect emergent TIM-3 loss or alternative resistance patterns [99–103]. Tumor-extrinsic or immune-mediated resistance may occur through the adaptive upregulation of alternative immune checkpoints (e.g., PD-1, LAG-3, CTLA-4) or increased immunosuppressive mediators (TGF-β, adenosine), which can blunt the efficacy of single-agent TIM-3 inhibitors and favor the emergence of immune-evasive clones. These adaptive resistance patterns have been described for immune checkpoint blockade more broadly and are plausible contributors to variable responses to TIM-3 targeting [104, 105].

Second, ligand redundancy and sequestration (notably the role of Galectin-9) may limit the benefit of receptor blockade alone: tumors with high Gal-9 expression or with alternative TIM-3 ligands may maintain immunosuppression despite TIM-3 blockade, supporting investigation of combined or ligand-targeting strategies [106].

Third, safety concerns and on-target effects deserve explicit consideration. Although early sabatolimab trials reported a tolerable safety profile, hematologic adverse events, in particular neutropenia, thrombocytopenia and febrile neutropenia, were common in larger randomized data and should be anticipated when combining TIM-3 inhibitors with cytotoxic or myelosuppressive agents. Immune-related adverse events (irAEs) appear infrequent to date, but immune reactivation could theoretically precipitate autoimmunity in susceptible individuals (including those with underlying HAVCR2/TIM-3 variants), and the long-term immune consequences of TIM-3 blockade remain incompletely defined. Importantly, in post-allograft settings the effect on graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) needs continued vigilance despite reassuring early reports [9, 107]. Finally, practical challenges include patient selection and biomarker standardization, potential fratricide or exhaustion risks when TIM-3 is used as a CAR target in cell therapies, and heterogeneity across AML subtypes that may limit generalizability. Together, these considerations argue for (1) developing biomarkers that predict dependence on TIM-3 (for example, TIM-3 + LSC burden, Gal-9 expression, co-inhibitory signature), (2) rational combinatorial approaches to prevent or overcome adaptive resistance, and (3) careful safety monitoring (especially cytopenias and immune toxicities) in future trials [43, 108].

Optimal therapeutic timing is an important, actionable variable when testing TIM-3–directed agents. Lower disease burden settings, notably MRD-positive states after induction or following allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplant (allo-HSCT), offer a biologically plausible window to target residual TIM-3 + leukemic stem cells while immune effector function can be re-invigorated. Early clinical work supports this strategy: a safety/run-in of the STIMULUS-AML2 program evaluated sabatolimab as pre-emptive therapy in patients who were in hematologic CR but MRD+ after allo-HSCT, illustrating the feasibility of post-transplant MRD-directed TIM-3 therapy. Likewise, randomized frontline MDS studies tested TIM-3 blockade added to hypomethylating agents (HMAs), but benefit appeared smaller when disease bulk was higher, reinforcing the hypothesis that MRD/low-burden settings (including early post-induction MRD and post-transplant MRD) may be the most promising clinical contexts to evaluate TIM-3 inhibitors. Clinically, MRD status and immune/chimerism readouts should therefore be prioritized as stratification or enrollment criteria in future trials testing TIM-3–targeted agents [9, 70, 97].

Not all AML/MDS patients derive equivalent benefit from TIM-3 inhibition; the clinical program highlights this heterogeneity. Although early single-arm studies suggested activity, the randomized STIMULUS-MDS1 study failed to meet its co-primary endpoints (CR rate and PFS), and subsequent program decisions reflected the neutral randomized findings. These results caution against over-interpretation of nonrandomized and early-phase signals and indicate three practical implications: (1) biomarker enrichment, future trials should prospectively select molecular or immunologic subgroups most likely to benefit (for example, MRD-positive post-HSCT or specific mutational subsets); (2) rational combinations, TIM-3 inhibitors may be most effective when combined with agents that address complementary resistance pathways, but combinations must be tested in randomized platforms rather than relying on single-arm activity; and (3) endpoints and populations, careful choice of clinically meaningful, randomized endpoints (OS, durable CR and MRD eradication) and appropriately selected patient populations are needed to demonstrate clear benefit. Taken together, these lessons underscore that while TIM-3 remains a biologically compelling target, clinical application will require more targeted, hypothesis-driven randomized studies [9, 98]. While early clinical studies of anti–TIM-3 agents (for example sabatolimab) have reported relatively infrequent severe irAEs to date, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. TIM-3 is expressed not only on exhausted, tumor-specific T cells but also on activated T cells and multiple innate immune populations (including monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and NK cells), and it can regulate both inhibitory and pro-inflammatory pathways. Consequently, TIM-3 blockade has the theoretical potential to induce broader immune activation beyond reversal of T-cell exhaustion, with attendant risks for immune-related toxicity, particularly when used earlier in disease, in combination with other immune-modulatory agents, or in the post-transplant setting. Clinical vigilance and systematic collection of immune-toxicity data (including prospective assessment of autoimmunity and graft-versus-host disease in allo-HSCT recipients) are therefore warranted as TIM-3 inhibitors are developed [1, 28, 29]. But as TIM-3 treatments are used earlier in the disease or in combination with other treatments, more caution is needed.

Finally, understanding the function of TIM-3 in pre-leukemic states (such as clonal hematopoiesis or MDS) may facilitate earlier intervention. TIM-3 is associated with the progression from MDS to AML, since elevated TIM-3 levels on MDS stem cells predict transformation to AML and reduced survival rates [19, 109]. This brings up the question of whether intervention at the MDS stage with TIM-3 blockade could delay or prevent evolution to AML.

Conclusion

In conclusion, TIM-3 in AML acts both as a pathogenic mediator, promoting immune evasion and LSC self-renewal, and as a therapeutic vulnerability because of experssion on leukemic cells and exhausted immune populations. Translational and clinical data to date suggest that TIM-3 blockade will most likely succeed as a component of rational combinations and in biomarker-enriched, low-burden settings rather than as a universal single-agent therapy. Logical combinations to prioritize include TIM-3 inhibitors plus hypomethylating agents (supported by early-phase signals and the STIMULUS program) and approaches that pair TIM-3 blockade with therapies that enhance innate immune clearance such as CD47-SIRPα blockade, the latter class (e.g., anti-CD47 antibodies) has demonstrated synergy with azacitidine in AML/MDS models and early clinical work, providing a rationale to test dual TIM-3/CD47 strategies that simultaneously reinvigorate adaptive immunity and promote macrophage-mediated phagocytosis. Importantly, the optimal clinical niche for TIM-3 combinations may be MRD-positive or other low-tumor-burden settings (for example, pre-emptive post-allo-HSCT or consolidation/MRD eradication), where LSC depletion and immune restoration are both plausible and measurable goals. Moving forward, clinical development should prioritize biomarker-driven trials (TIM-3 + LSC burden, Gal-9 expression, exhaustion signatures, and MRD status), randomized comparison arms, and endpoints that capture durable MRD eradication and survival benefit.
