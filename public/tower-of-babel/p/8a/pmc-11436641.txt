Impact of salt strength on in vitro propagation and rebaudioside A content in Stevia rebaudiana under semi-solid and liquid MS media
Authors: Pritom Biswas, Ankita Kumari, Nitish Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Stevia rebaudiana (Bertoni), commonly known as stevia, is a sought-after natural sweetener, but its conventional propagation methods are slow and inefficient. This study aims to enhance the in vitro culture for stevia by investigating the impact of different Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium salt strengths and plant growth hormones on growth and rebaudioside A content. Apical bud-containing shoot segments were used as explants and cultured on various semi-solid and liquid MS media formulations, incorporating cytokinins (BAP and Kin), auxins (NAA and IAA), and different MS major salt concentrations (MS full, ½ MS, and ¼ MS). Assessments of shoot growth parameters, root formation, and HPLC analysis for rebaudioside A content were conducted. The optimal conditions for in vitro growth was found to be in the ¼ MS + Kin 3 mg/L + NAA 0.1 mg/L (semi-solid) medium, resulting in significantly improved shoot growth and enhanced 30.04% rebaudioside A content. Genetic fidelity of regenerated plants was confirmed using RAPD and ISSR markers. These findings offer valuable insights for optimizing in vitro propagation of stevia and potentially enhancing rebaudioside A content.

Introduction

Stevia rebaudiana (Bertoni), commonly known as stevia, is a perennial herb native to South America and belonging to the Asteraceae family (2n = 2x = 22), is famous for its leaves containing natural sweeteners called steviol glycosides (SG). These SG compounds are noted to be around 250–300 times sweeter than sugar, yet they do not contribute the same caloric content or affect blood sugar levels1,2. As a result, stevia has become a popular sugar substitute, especially among those aiming to reduce calorie intake and manage blood sugar levels. Despite being pH stable3, SG have been extensively studied and found to have no known adverse effects on blood sugar and insulin levels. Additionally, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) endorsed its safety for human consumption in 2008. Consequently, this plant is widely cultivated commercially and integrated into various products, including food, beverages, and dietary supplements. Furthermore, some reports highlight its medicinal properties, such as anti-inflammatory, antihypertensive, vasodilator and hypoglycemic effects4–6. Stevia contains over 35 different SG, with stevioside and rebaudioside A being the most prevalent ones7,8.

Rebaudioside A stands out for its exceptional water solubility and flavor-enhancing properties9,10. With a sweetness approximately 240 times that of regular table sugar, rebaudioside A possesses favorable physicochemical and organoleptic characteristics. In contrast, while approximately 140 times sweeter than sucrose, stevioside exhibits suboptimal palatability and a lingering bitter aftertaste, as demonstrated by Palmer et al.11. The superior sweetness and taste of rebaudioside A can be attributed to an additional glucose unit at position C-13 in its chemical structure12. Consequently, rebaudioside A has surpassed stevioside in prominence within the global pharmaceutical industry despite stevioside being the predominant SG in the leaves of stevia. This has led to a surge in research efforts focused on enhancing rebaudioside A production in plants, as evidenced by recent studies13. Moreover, some studies have reported that in vitro production of rebaudioside A outpaces that of stevioside14,15. Some research has indicated that certain cytokinins, such as 6-Benzylaminopurine (BAP) and Kinetin (Kin); along with auxins like naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA) can affect plant growth and the synthesis of rebaudioside A13,16–19.

Conventional approaches to propagate stevia, such as employing seeds or stem cuttings, do not consistently yield reliable results, leading to variations in crop quality20. Additionally, these methods can be labor-intensive and time-consuming, thereby increasing costs for cultivators. Traditional propagation techniques also come with inherent challenges, including low germination rates, issues of seed incompatibility, and infrequent rooting in stem cuttings21. Due to the challenges of traditional propagation methods, tissue culture is becoming a more rational as well as feasible option for cultivating stevia. This approach facilitates the rapid multiplication of plant material under controlled conditions, resulting in uniform and high-quality plants. While numerous studies have addressed the in vitro cultivation of stevia, as demonstrated by various reports22–27, recent scientific efforts have placed increased emphasis on the development of more effective strategies for in vitro cultivation with the aim of enhancing the growth of stevia plants and improving their SG profile18,28–31.

To develop a consistent and effective in vitro regeneration procedure that can provide greater biomass and a higher concentration of desired SG, further study is still required. Tissue culture could be a highly effective way to increase the levels of SG in leaves, and also promote plant growth and multiplication. Consequently, it is important to look into how stevia plant development and SG biosynthesis relate to each other.

The existing literature on the in vitro growth of stevia using Murashige and Skoog (MS) medium presents a notable gap in research. The composition of MS medium is reported by many studies32. To the best of our knowledge, very few studies have explored the influence of MS medium strength on stevia27,33,34. Similarly, there are reports on influence of MS media strength on other plant species as well, e.g.—Pogostemon cablin and Mentha spicata35,36. However, the specific effects of MS medium strength and culture system (solid or liquid) on Stevia, alongside the impact of different plant growth regulators (PGRs), remain largely unexplored. Previous research has highlighted the potential influence of MS medium strength and culture system on shoot growth, rooting, and nutrient uptake in various plant species, like Pogostemon cablin, Mentha spicata and Boswellia serrata Roxb35–37. However, these factors have not been systematically investigated in the context of Stevia rebaudiana.

In the present study, we aimed to address this research gap by examining the influence of diverse PGRs (BAP, Kin, NAA), in conjunction with varying concentrations (full MS, ½ MS, and ¼ MS), and formulations (semi-solid or liquid) of MS media on the in vitro growth and rebaudioside A content of stevia. To evaluate whether the in vitro regenerants were genetically identical to the original plant, two molecular marker techniques were employed: Random Amplified Polymorphic DNA (RAPD) and Inter Simple Sequence Repeat (ISSR). These techniques analyze specific regions of the DNA to identify variations in the base sequence, which can serve as genetic fingerprints. By comparing the RAPD and ISSR profiles of the in vitro regenerants to those of the original plant, genetic fidelity can be determined. By elucidating these previously unexplored aspects of Stevia in vitro culture, our research contributes novel insights into optimizing growth conditions and enhancing rebaudioside A content, thus addressing a significant gap in the existing literature. The establishment of an effective tissue culture protocol holds the potential to yield significant advantages, including enhanced agricultural production, increased economic returns, and the provision of a sustainable source of natural sweeteners.

Material and methods

Selection and disinfection of explant

Shoot segments with apical buds (approximately 2.5–3 cm in length) were carefully selected from the garden of Central University of South Bihar, Gaya, India. During the collection of these explants, we ensured full compliance with pertinent institutional, national and international guidelines and regulations. To eliminate surface contaminants, the explants underwent a thorough disinfection protocol. They were first rinsed under running tap water for 15–20 min, followed by a 5-min soak in 2 mL/L Tween-20 (Polyoxyethylene Sorbitan Monolaurate) and three rinses with distilled water. The explants were then immersed in carbendazim (HiMedia) (1000 mg/L) for 10 min with continuous agitation to remove dust particles and spores of bacterial and fungal origin. After thoroughly cleaning the explants to remove any surface contaminants, they were subjected to a disinfection procedure to eliminate any microorganisms that could potentially harm the cultures. This involved immersing the explants in a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution for two minutes, followed by three thorough rinses with sterile distilled water to remove any residual bleach. Next, the explants were disinfected under a laminar airflow (LAF) hood by immersing them in 0.1% mercuric chloride for 40–45 s. This was immediately followed by thorough rinsing with autoclaved milli-Q water, repeated at least 4–5 times. Nodal segments bearing a single axillary bud on either one or both sides were aseptically excised (approximately 0.5–0.8 cm) and vertically inoculated onto MS medium. In the case of liquid cultures, the explant was introduced into the medium using a supporting matrix of blotting paper. Observations were meticulously recorded, and data was collected after a period of 3–4 weeks.

Establishment of primary culture

MS medium was prepared by combining MS media (HiMedia), 3% sucrose, various concentrations of BAP and NAA (Table 1), and 0.8% Agar agar (Type I, HiMedia). All the PGRs were procured from HiMedia. The pH was regulated to 5.7. Subsequently, the medium underwent autoclaving at a pressure of 1.06 kg cm-2 for a duration of 20 min at 121 °C. The nodal explants were cultivated in both control and treatment sets (Table 1). For optimal growth and development, the explants were cultivated in a meticulously controlled growth chamber with precise environmental conditions. The photoperiod was set to 16 h of light and 8 h of darkness, with an irradiance of 35 mmol m−1 s−1 provided by white fluorescent lighting. The temperature was maintained at a constant 25 ± 1 °C, and the relative humidity was kept within a range of 55–60%. Semi solid media

A culture medium containing 3% sucrose was meticulously prepared, and various concentrations of PGRs were introduced into this medium as documented in Table 2. The pH was meticulously adjusted to 5.7 for optimal conditions. To solidify the medium, 0.8% agar agar (Type I) was employed. In the control groups, solely MS zero was utilized, devoid of any PGRs. In the case of ½ MS, the concentration of the major salts was halved, while the minor salts and vitamins maintained their original concentration. Likewise, with ¼ MS, the major salts were decreased fourfold while the other components remained unaltered.

Liquid media

For liquid culture, MS medium with 3% sucrose was carefully prepared and different PGR concentrations were added (Table 3). The pH was adjusted to 5.7 for optimal conditions. In the control groups, only MS zero (without PGRs) was used. In ½ MS, the major salts concentration was reduced by half, while the minor salts and vitamins remained unchanged. Similarly, in ¼ MS, the major salts were reduced fourfold, and the other components were not altered. The explant was introduced using blotting paper as a supporting matrix.

Rooting, hardening and acclimatization

Vigorous and healthy shoots, approximately 3 cm in length, were carefully chosen for the rooting process. These selected shoots were subjected to culture conditions with ½ MS zero and ¼ MS + 0.2 mg/L IAA for root induction, as detailed in Table 4. Following a four-week incubation period, the shoots were assessed for the presence of roots, and the rooting percentage was calculated. Shoots displaying root development were gently removed from the growth medium, rinsed thoroughly with autoclaved milli-Q water to eliminate any residual medium traces from their roots. Afterward, they were relocated to containers containing a sterilized blend of soil and sand in a 1:3 ratio. Transparent plastic bags were used to cover them in order to retain the ideal level of humidity. The plants were then placed in a controlled environment with a temperature of 25 °C ± 1 °C and 16 h of illumination from cool white fluorescent lights at an irradiance of 35 µmol m−1 s−1. Determination of rebaudioside A concentration by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC)

To evaluate the amount of rebaudioside A in stevia, leaf samples were obtained from two distinct sources: ex vitro and in vitro plants. The in vitro plants were further divided into two groups, namely the control set (derived from plants grown in MS zero) and the group cultivated in the most effective growth medium (comprising ¼ MS + Kin 3 mg/L + NAA 0.1 mg/L). Subsequently, an HPLC analysis was performed, following a protocol closely aligned with the method outlined by Javed et al.38, with minor adjustments. To prepare the leaf samples for HPLC analysis, the collected leaves were subjected to a drying process at 45 °C for approximately 48 h. This was followed by a thorough pulverization step to obtain a fine powder. Subsequently, 20 mg of the powdered leaf material was carefully combined with 1 mL of high-purity methanol (HPLC grade). The resulting mixture was vortexed for 20 s to ensure complete homogenization. The mixture was then subjected to a water bath treatment at 55 °C for a duration of 2 h. Following this step, the samples were subjected to sonication for 20 min. To separate the extract from the insoluble components of the leaf powder, the samples were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. Finally, to ensure the removal of any remaining particulate matter, the supernatant was meticulously filtered through a 0.22 µm syringe filter before proceeding to the HPLC analysis.

To prepare the standard solution of rebaudioside A, which was procured from Sigma-Aldrich, USA, 1 mg of the compound was dissolved in 1 mL of HPLC grade methanol. The analysis was carried out using a ThermoFisher Vanquish HPLC system, equipped with a C-18 Accucore column (4.6*150 mm, 2.6 μm). The rebaudioside A concentration was determined by employing a variable wavelength detector set to 210 nm. The mobile phase employed for the HPLC analysis consisted of a mixture of acetonitrile and water in an 80:20 (v/v) ratio. An isocratic flow rate of 1 mL/min was maintained throughout the analysis, ensuring consistent and reproducible elution of the target compound. The sample injection volume was set to 20 μL, allowing for the introduction of an adequate amount of extract while maintaining optimal chromatographic conditions. The entire HPLC analysis was consistently conducted over a 10-min duration, providing sufficient time for the resolution and detection of rebaudioside A.

Analysis of genetic fidelity utilizing RAPD and ISSR markers

The CTAB method39, was employed to extract DNA from the leaves of six randomly selected plants regenerated by in vitro culture and a mother plant, serving as the control. Subsequently, the extracted DNA underwent quantification and quality evaluation utilizing a spectrophotometer (Nanodrop, Thermo). The obtained DNA samples were appropriately diluted and adjusted to a concentration of 100 ng/µL, utilizing Tris–EDTA (TE) buffer, and were then stored at a temperature of 4 °C.

RAPD amplification was carried out using a reaction volume of 25 µL. This comprised 1 µL of genomic DNA (100 ng), 12.5 µL of master mix (GCC Biotech Pvt. Ltd., Kolkata, India), 1 µL of a 10 pmol 10-mer deoxynucleotide primer (G Bioscience, India), and the remaining volume was adjusted with 10.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The protocol consisted of an initial denaturation step at 94 °C for 5 min to separate the double-stranded DNA into single strands. This was followed by 30 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension. During denaturation, the DNA was heated to 94 °C again for 30 s to melt the single strands. Annealing was performed at 50 °C for 1 min, allowing the primer to bind to its complementary sequence on the DNA template. Finally, extension was carried out at 72 °C for 1 min, enabling the DNA polymerase to extend the primer, generating amplified DNA fragments. The amplification cycle was concluded with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min to ensure complete synthesis of the amplified DNA fragments.

ISSR amplifications were carried out using a reaction volume of 25 µL. The reaction mixture contained 1 µL of genomic DNA (100 ng), 12.5 µL of master mix (GCC Biotech Pvt. Ltd., Kolkata, India), 1 µL of a 10 pmol 17-mer deoxynucleotide primer from G Bioscience, India, and the remaining volume was adjusted with 10.5 µL of nuclease-free water. The amplification procedure began with an initial denaturation stage lasting 5 min at 94 °C. Subsequently, 30 cycles were carried out, involving denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, then annealing at 50 °C for 1 min, and lastly extension at 72 °C for 1 min. The amplification process concluded with a final extension step lasting 10 min at 72 °C.

PCR amplifications were executed utilizing the ProFlex thermocycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Each sample was subjected to a minimum of two independent PCR amplifications, employing RAPD and ISSR primers. After the amplification process was complete, the amplified products were separated using gel electrophoresis. This involved running the amplified DNA samples through a 1.5% agarose gel submerged in 1 × Tris–borate-EDTA (TBE) buffer. The DNA bands were then visualized by staining the gel with ethidium bromide (EtBr). To visualize the gel electrophoresis results, a Bio-Rad gel documentation system was utilized, and the amplicon sizes were determined by comparing them to a 100 bp DNA ladder (G bioscience).

Data collection and statistical analysis

The experiments followed a completely randomized design, with each treatment replicated three times and 10 explants per treatment. Observations were made at every 5-day intervals and data was collected accordingly for statistical analysis. Analysis was conducted using one-way ANOVA, and means were compared using Tukey's test (P ≤ 0.05) with Graph Pad Prism version 8.0.2. Results are presented as mean ± SE from three independent experiments. Rebaudioside A concentration was measured using a ThermoFisher Vanquish HPLC system and Chromaleon version 7.3 software. Gel images were captured using a Bio-Rad gel documentation system and analyzed with ImageLab software. The scale bars in the images were made using ImajeJ software.

Results and discussions

Initiation of sterile cultures, emergence of auxillary buds, and multiplication of shoots

The explants underwent a successful surface disinfection process with no signs of burning or damage, and only a minor contamination of about 8–10% was noted in the initial culture. Within five days of inoculating the explants, shoots started to emerge from the lateral buds of the nodes. The growth of these shoots exhibited significant variation, primarily depending on the dosage of BAP and NAA in the MS medium, which included a control group (MS 0) (see Table 1, Fig. 1A–E). Optimal results in terms of growth rate (91.25 ± 1.49%) and the production of vigorous, healthy plants with four nodes were documented when shoot tip explants were cultured on the MS medium supplemented with 2 mg/L of BAP and 0.1 mg/L of NAA (Fig. 1B). A comparison of the results from the semi-solid medium and the liquid medium experiments reveals some notable differences. In the liquid medium, the overall growth of the plants was generally reduced compared to the semi-solid medium (Fig. 3), which supports the results of Refs.41,42. This suggests that the physical state of the medium can influence plant responses to PGRs, supporting the findings of Xu et al.43. The comparison of overall growth parameters between semi-solid and liquid medium is crucial in understanding how the physical state of the medium influences the growth, development and maturation of the target plant species.

Cytokinins are known to play an important role in shoot proliferation; therefore, we cultured the in vitro regenerated shoots as an explant on MS medium supplied with BAP and Kin. Our results were supported by other studies in which adding additional cytokinin (both BAP and Kin) to the MS medium containing a small amount of NAA showed a positive impact on shoot organogenesis. Kin improves plant physiological activities, leaf area, and photosynthetical characteristics44. Also, it was reported by Shaafi et al.45 that Kin in combination with NAA boosted better shoot growth than the combination of BAP + NAA, which has been further supported by our findings.

The comparison of the effects of medium strength and PGRs (BAP and Kin) in both semi-solid and liquid media revealed some notable differences in plant growth responses. In the semi-solid growth medium, when the major salts in the MS medium were reduced by half, it resulted in improved growth and development for plant tissues treated with BAP. However, the opposite effect was observed when Kin was used. Kin demonstrated better growth when the strength of major salts in the MS medium was reduced to one-fourth. One possible hypothesis to explain this phenomenon is that BAP and Kin may have distinct interactions with the specific salts present in the medium, leading to these contrasting responses. Therefore, the selection of the medium type (either semi-solid or liquid) and its particular attributes, including the concentration of MS salts, can significantly influence how plants respond to PGRs. This assertion finds support in the findings of Rezali et al.41, who observed enhanced growth of T. flagelliforme with an increased strength of MS in liquid media. Conversely, T. flagelliforme exhibited improved growth when the MS strength was reduced in solid media.

MS major salts comprises of macronutrients, namely, nitrogen (N), potassium (K), magnesium (Mg), sulfur (S), phosphorus (P), and calcium (Ca)46. These elements are imperative for the growth and developmental processes in plants. BAP might be more effective in promoting growth and differentiation when the concentration of certain salts is reduced by half, which was also reported by Rasool et al.47. It is possible that these salts could have an inhibitory effect on BAP activity, and decreasing their concentration enhances BAP’s effectiveness. Conversely, a one-fourth concentration of the salts might provide the ideal conditions for Kin’s activity, while a higher concentration could be inhibitory or disruptive to its effects. It is also possible that the reduced salt concentration in semi-solid medium affects the metabolism of BAP and Kin within plant cells. BAP is metabolized more quickly than Kin. It is possible that the reduced salt concentration slows down the metabolism of BAP, making it more effective at promoting shoot regeneration.

When the strength of MS major salts is decreased from full to half strength in liquid medium, the response rates of both BAP and Kin improve, with Kin showing a better response than BAP in both full and half strength. This contradicts the reports of Hossain et al.48 which states that BAP yields better results than Kin. However, the trend changes when the major salt concentration is reduced to one fourth, with BAP showing a better response than Kin. One possible hypothesis for this phenomenon is that the lower concentration of major salts in liquid medium reduces the osmotic stress on the plant cells, making it easier for them to absorb cytokinin.

However, it is crucial to consider that the specific salts present in the MS medium have distinct roles and can interact with the PGRs differently. The varying responses to altering salt concentrations could be due to these complex interactions and the specific requirements of each PGR for their activity. Further research and experimentation would be needed to validate and expand upon this hypothesis and to uncover the precise mechanisms underlying these observations. Researchers and tissue culture practitioners should consider these differences when selecting the appropriate culture system for their specific objectives. The semi-solid medium might be more suitable for enhancing plant growth, elongation, and branching, whereas the liquid medium may be preferred for specific applications where ease of manipulation and uniform nutrient distribution are essential.

Rooting, hardening and acclimatization

After being removed from the culture medium containing ¼ MS + 3 mg/L Kin + 0.1 mg/L NAA, the shoots were subcultured on different rooting media (Table 4). After approximately 30–32 days of cultivation, root formation took place, and relevant data were collected. Notably, the initial emergence of roots directly from the base of the shoots occurred 19 days into the culture period without the formation of any intermediary callus tissue. The most remarkable percentage of root induction (90%) was noted upon inoculation on ¼ MS + 0.2 mg/L IAA, resulting in an average of 11.20 ± 0.37 roots per shoot. In contrast to previous studies that required higher auxin concentrations for rooting49, this study demonstrated that rooting can occur in low-strength MS media (¼ and ½ MS) without the addition of plant growth regulators (PGRs). This suggests that lower auxin concentrations may be sufficient for stevia rooting, potentially reducing the cost of in vitro propagation process.

Plants form roots to gather nutrients and if the availability of the nutrients in the media could be reduced, plants have to form roots to search for and obtain the required nutrients. So, the strength of MS medium was reduced to half and one fourth. Also, as per the existing reports, for the in vitro rooting, better results were achieved with IAA on ½ MS rather than full MS19,50–52. So, ½ MS and ¼ MS was employed in our study. Contrarily, Thiyagarajan and Venkatachalam49 found that rooting respond well on NAA.

After being maintained in a controlled environment for 18–20 days within pots (Fig. 4C), the ziplock bags were gradually withdrawn to enable the plantlets to adapt to greenhouse conditions. Following this transitional phase, the plantlets were transferred to open-field conditions (Fig. 4D), yielding an impressive survival rate of approximately 77%. The regenerated plants exhibited vigorous growth and closely resembled the parental stock in terms of their phenotypic characteristics.

Determination of rebaudioside A concentration by HPLC

The concentration of rebaudioside A was measured using a standard curve generated with an HPLC-grade reference compound (rebaudioside A, procured from Sigma Aldrich). The retention time for rebaudioside A was found to be 4.39 min (Fig. 5a–c). The rebaudioside A content was found to be 229.02 ± 1.02 mg/L in ex vitro plants, 201.7 ± 0.76 mg/L in the control set of in vitro propagated plants, while 273 ± 1.53 mg/L in in vitro cultured plants in the treatment sets (Fig. 6). Hence, the rebaudioside A was found to be highest in the treatment set, 30.04% higher than the control and even 17.52% higher than the ex vitro plants. These findings align with the report of Ashrafi et al.19 which demonstrated that the use of Kin in specific combinations influences the content of rebaudioside-A, with varying effects on glandular trichome density and the overall production of SG in stevia. Analysis of somaclonal variations

To evaluate the genetic consistency of micropropagated stevia plants, as illustrated in Figs. 7 and 8, two PCR-based molecular techniques, namely RAPD and ISSR, were applied to six randomly chosen micropropagated plants and a mother plant (control). Initially, 80 RAPD primers were screened, and subsequently, twenty-four primers that exhibited five or more distinct bands were chosen for more detailed examination. These selected primers resulted in a total of 847 amplicons, ranging in size from 300 to 1600 bp. Twenty primers were screened for ISSR, and twelve primers that produced six or more scorable bands were chosen to undergo further examination. There were no instances of polymorphic bands observed. A representative profile, using a single primer from each set, OPA 7 for RAPD and 810 for ISSR, is depicted in Figs. 7 and 8, respectively. The uniform banding patterns observed in RAPD and ISSR analyses indicate the genetic stability of micropropagated stevia plants, confirming their similarity to the mother plant. The absence of polymorphic bands in both analyses further validates the genetic integrity of the regenerated plants, highlighting their genetic uniformity54–56. Ultimately, the consistent banding patterns affirm the genetic stability and integrity of micropropagated stevia plants, making them suitable for further research and applications.

Conclusion

The present study successfully optimized the in vitro growth conditions for S. rebaudiana using shoots with apical buds as explants. The combination of ¼ MS medium with Kin and NAA in semi-solid form produced the best growth parameters—shoot length, number of nodes, and leaves. Additionally, the ¼ MS medium with IAA was found to be the most effective for rooting. Although comparatively, liquid culture proved to yield better results and produced desirable quality of plants. So, effect was clearly visible on the morphological parameters of the plants, once the strength of the major salts was manipulated. Hence, it can be concluded that irrespective of the culture conditions (be it semi-solid or liquid culture), the major salts of MS medium have the potential to influence the growth and elongation of the shoots in in vitro conditions. The HPLC analysis revealed a notable rise in the production of rebaudioside A in the in vitro regenerated plants that were treated with PGRs and their genetic consistency was confirmed with RAPD and ISSR markers. These findings can be useful for the mass production of stevia and provide a foundation for further research on the molecular mechanisms underlying the plant’s growth and production of SG.
