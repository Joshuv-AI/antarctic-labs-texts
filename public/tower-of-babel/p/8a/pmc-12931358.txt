Exploring the relationships among weight stigma, weight bias internalisation, feeling fat, and self-esteem across preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum
Authors: Michelle Dever, Angela C. Incollingo Rodriguez, Helen Skouteris, Briony Hill
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWeight stigma permeates women's reproductive experiences with significant psychological consequences, yet remains understudied across different reproductive stages and country contexts. This study addresses this gap by investigating weight stigma experiences and their associations with weight bias internalisation (WBI), feeling fat, and self-esteem across preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum (PPP) stages in Western and non-Western contexts.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, a convenience sample of women aged 18–45 years from five countries completed an anonymous online survey assessing weight stigma experiences, WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem. For exploratory comparison, countries were grouped into Western (Australia, United Kingdom, United States) and non-Western (Singapore, Philippines) contexts. Hierarchical regressions and mediation analyses examined relationships between variables.ResultsIn total, 511 women completed the survey (preconception n = 216, pregnant n = 125, postpartum n = 170). Overall, 62.6% of women reported experiencing weight stigma, with sources varying by PPP stage. Weight stigma from the media was most prevalent during preconception, workplace during pregnancy, and family in postpartum. Women residing in Western countries reported more healthcare-related stigma (p = .01), while non-Western women reported more stigma from friends (p < .001) and workplace (p = .001). Weight bias internalisation, feeling fat, and self-esteem levels were similar across countries. Weight bias internalisation consistently mediated the relationship between weight stigma experiences and both feeling fat and self-esteem across all PPP stages; weight stigma promoted greater WBI, which in turn promoted higher scores on feeling fat and lower scores on self-esteem.ConclusionWhile sources of weight stigma differed by reproductive stage and between Western and non-Western contexts, WBI consistently mediated how these experiences related to both feeling fat and self-esteem. Interventions should target stage-specific stigma sources while addressing WBI as a key mediating mechanism to improve health and wellbeing outcomes for PPP women.

Introduction

Body image concerns take on heightened significance during the reproductive years, a time when women's bodies undergo natural changes that typically attract increased scrutiny (Hill & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2020). Weight stigma, which refers to the social devaluation and negative stereotyping of individuals based on body size (Nutter et al., 2024) is a key factor shaping women's body image experiences during this period. Nearly two-thirds of pregnant and postpartum women—across all body sizes—report experiencing some form of weight stigma (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). These experiences of weight stigma are associated with adverse outcomes, including increased stress, depression, and maladaptive dieting behaviours (Dever et al., 2025). Additionally, weight stigma has been directly associated with gestational weight gain above recommendations, postpartum weight retention, body dissatisfaction, and increased risk for gestational diabetes, which are also risk factors for further weight stigmatisation (Hill et al., 2023; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2019; Nagpal et al., 2021).

Despite growing recognition of weight stigma’s harmful effects during pregnancy and postpartum, three critical gaps remain in the research. First, how weight stigma is experienced across different reproductive stages and how these experiences may contribute to the aforementioned harmful outcomes, have not been comprehensively explored, particularly during the preconception phase, where, to our knowledge, no studies exist. Preconception is an especially important period to consider, as many adverse psychosocial wellbeing factors are shaped by weight-related attitudes and experiences that occur prior to pregnancy (Hill et al., 2023). Therefore, examining weight stigma across the preconception, pregnancy and postpartum (PPP) stages may help identify optimal intervention timing and inform stage-specific strategies.

Second, existing studies predominantly examine healthcare-based weight stigma in Western contexts (Dever et al., 2025), which provides only a partial understanding of women's experiences. This narrow focus overlooks other significant sources in everyday settings where exposure may be equally or more prevalent. Additionally, comparative research examining weight stigma across different country contexts is limited, despite considerable variation in body size norms (Abdoli et al., 2024), including before, during, and after pregnancy. Most existing studies have focused on Western countries such as Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States, where thinness is commonly idealised and explicit weight bias toward PPP women tends to be more pronounced (Abdoli et al., 2024; Hailu et al., 2025). This overrepresentation significantly limits our understanding of weight stigma experiences in non-Western countries, particularly where societal attitudes toward body size and reproductive weight may differ. As weight stigma is increasingly recognised as a global health challenge (Brewis et al., 2018), and obesity rates continue to rise in non-Western countries (Tee & Voon, 2024), there is a clear need to examine these experiences across more diverse settings.

Third, the relationship between weight stigma and key psychosocial constructs—weight bias internalisation (WBI), feeling fat (a construct of body image), and self-esteem—has not been examined collectively in reproductive contexts. This presents a novel opportunity to understand how these factors interrelate during these significant life transitions. Weight bias internalisation, where individuals accept and apply negative weight-based stereotypes to themselves, is a significant yet understudied consequence of weight stigma (Nagpal et al., 2022). Emerging evidence suggests this internalisation process is associated with greater healthcare avoidance and lower provider trust during pregnancy (Byrd et al., 2023). Additionally, WBI has been shown to mediate the relationship between postpartum weight retention and depressive symptoms, Sharp et al. (2023) suggesting that internalised weight bias may be a key mechanism linking weight stigma to adverse psychological outcomes across reproductive stages.

Among the various dimensions of body image, 'feeling fat' represents a common and often distressing experience for many women (Striegel-Moore et al., 1986). This construct captures the subjective, emotional aspect of body dissatisfaction (Tiggemann, 1996) that, unlike cognitive body image measures, manifests as a deeply felt, embodied state women may report, Mehak and Racine (2021) especially during periods of anticipated or actual weight changes like reproductive transitions. Feeling fat is strongly associated with restrictive eating, binge eating, eating in response to external stimuli, and fear of weight-gain in Western populations (Mehak & Racine, 2021; Ralph-Nearman et al., 2024; Tiggemann, 1996). Recent findings suggest that feeling fat predicts binge eating through negative moral emotions such as disgust, guilt and shame, indicating that these emotions may reinforce disordered eating behaviours (Anderson et al., 2022). In the context of PPP, where women may face heightened societal scrutiny and internalised pressures regarding their bodies, the associations between feeling fat, WBI, and weight stigma experiences have important implications for maternal mental health and eating behaviours, warranting further investigation.

While feeling fat represents a body-specific experience, weight stigma may also be associated with broader psychological functioning through self-esteem—an individual's overall sense of self-worth (Rosenberg, 1965). When self-worth is contingent on physical appearance, women tend to report lower self-esteem (Adams et al., 2017). Existing research links weight stigma to reduced self-esteem in non-reproductive populations (Mellor et al., 2010; Wu & Berry, 2018), but these relationships remain largely unexplored during reproductive transitions, where normative weight changes and heightened body scrutiny are prevalent. The unique contexts of PPP may shape how these factors interact, particularly across countries with varying body ideals and social norms. Based on the available evidence, we hypothesise that weight stigma experiences will be associated with WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem, with WBI mediating these relationships similarly across reproductive stages and between countries. These associations are critical to understand because reproductive body changes, while biologically necessary, often become targets for weight stigma, reinforcing tensions between physiological processes and societal expectations (Hodgkinson et al., 2014).

Given these gaps in understanding weight stigma across reproductive stages and country contexts, we aimed to examine its sources, associations with WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem, and the mediating role of WBI in PPP women. Specifically, the study objectives were to: (1) characterise weight stigma experiences (from key sources), WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem across PPP stages, and between Western and non-Western countries; (2) investigate the associations between weight stigma experiences and WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem; and (3) determine whether WBI mediates the relationship between weight stigma experiences and feeling fat and self-esteem. By examining the experiences of weight stigma and their psychosocial correlates among PPP women from Western and non-Western countries we contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of weight stigma across the reproductive lifespan.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an online cross-sectional study following Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (von Elm et al., 2007) (see Supplementary Table S1).

Participants

Participants were preconception, pregnant, or postpartum women aged 18 to 45, able to read English and with internet access. The age range of 18–45 years was selected to align with the typical reproductive age span commonly used in reproductive health research (Temmesen et al., 2023). Preconception was defined as actively trying to conceive with deliberate pregnancy planning (Hill et al., 2020), pregnancy as carrying a developing foetus (National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, 2024), and postpartum as having given birth within the past 24-months preceding participation (Lipsky et al., 2012).

Procedure

The study was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee (ID: 38307) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In August 2023, 511 preconception, pregnant, and postpartum women were recruited through a Qualtrics research panel (Qualtrics, Provo, UT) using a quota-based convenience sample. Country selection was informed by panel availability, with the goal of achieving geographical diversity. Recruitment was conducted from Australia, the United Kingdom, and the United States (Western contexts where thinness is commonly idealised and explicit weight bias toward PPP women tends to be more pronounced (Abdoli et al., 2024; Hailu et al., 2025), and Singapore and the Philippines (non-Western contexts with rising obesity rates (Tee & Voon, 2024) and limited research on reproductive weight stigma). These countries were grouped into Western and non-Western contexts for exploratory comparison purposes. Participants were presented with an explanatory statement at the beginning of the 30-minute anonymous online survey. Continuation into the survey after reading this statement constituted consent to participate. Based on their reproductive status, participants were directed to stage-specific questionnaires, which assessed demographics, sources and frequency of weight-stigmatising experiences, WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem. To ensure data integrity, we monitored IP addresses and embedded attention cheques. Respondents who completed the survey received remuneration according to Qualtrics' panel standard rates and procedures.

Measures

Demographics

Participants reported their age, PPP status (preconception, pregnant, or postpartum), parity, gravidity, Body Mass Index (BMI), marital status, gender identity, country of residence, ethnicity, education, and body self-perception.

Sources and frequency of weight stigma experiences

Sources and frequency of weight-stigmatising experiences were assessed using a 10-item checklist based on the Stigmatising Situations Inventory (SSI), (Myers & Rosen, 1999) adapted by Vartanian et al. (2014, 2018) and subsequently by Incollingo Rodriguez et al. (2019, 2020), Nagpal and Incollingo Rodriguez (2023) for pregnant and postpartum populations. The scale captures whether and to what extent women have been treated differently or made to feel uncomfortable because of their weight. To extend its applicability to preconception women, we adapted item wording (e.g. ‘Since becoming pregnant…’ was changed to ‘Since starting to think about getting pregnant…’) and pilot tested the revised version with 10 preconception participants. Minor modifications were made based on feedback and no items were removed. The final survey included a 10-item list of potential stigma sources: workplace (employers/co-workers), family (immediate and extended), friends, partners, healthcare providers (physician/nurse/midwife/office staff), strangers, the media (television/news/social media), pregnant women or new mothers, and society in general (societal expectations). Each source was coded dichotomously (0 = No, 1 = Yes) to indicate presence of stigma, and for endorsed sources, participants rated frequency of experiences from less than once a month to one or more times a day. This inventory demonstrates good internal consistency across reproductive studies (Cronbach's α = .71–.79), (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2019; Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020; Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2023) and in the current study (α = .74; (Supplementary Table S2).

Weight bias internalisation (WBI)

Weight bias internalisation was measured using the Modified Weight Bias Internalisation Scale (WBIS-M), originally developed by Pearl and Puhl (2014). The WBIS-M was later adapted for use in pregnant and postpartum populations (Hailu et al., 2025), and we further adapted and piloted this version for preconception women. All original items were retained, with minor wording adjustments to suit the preconception context. The 18-item scale assesses the extent to which individuals accept and apply negative weight-based stereotypes to themselves. Participants rated items (e.g. ‘I feel anxious about my body weight because of what other people might think of me’) on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores reflecting greater internalisation. The WBIS-M demonstrates excellent internal consistency across reproductive studies (Cronbach's α = .91) (Hailu et al., 2025) and in the current study (α = .94).

Feeling fat

The Feeling Fat subscale from the Ben-Tovim Walker Body Attitudes Questionnaire (BAQ) was used to assess subjective feelings of fatness (Ben-Tovim & Walker, 1991). Originally developed for non-pregnant populations, the subscale has since been applied in research with pregnant and postpartum women (Clark et al., 2009; Fuller-Tyszkiewicz et al., 2012; Skouteris et al., 2005). The seven-item subscale uses a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater feelings of fatness. An example item is: ‘I get so worried about my shape that I feel like I ought to diet.’ The subscale has demonstrated good to excellent internal consistency across non-pregnant, pregnant, and postpartum populations (Cronbach’s α = .62–.94) (Clark et al., 2009; Fuller-Tyszkiewicz et al., 2012; Skouteris et al., 2005) supporting its applicability across the PPP period. In the current study, internal consistency was excellent (α = .91; see Supplementary Table S2).

Self-esteem

Self-esteem was measured using the Rosenberg Self-Esteem Scale (RSES) (Rosenberg, 1965). Participants indicated their agreement with statements such as, ‘On the whole I am satisfied with myself’ on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). Higher overall scores indicated greater self-esteem. The RSES demonstrates good to excellent internal consistency across pregnancy and postpartum populations (Cronbach's α = .76–.92) (Maçola et al., 2010; Raudasoja et al., 2022; Raudasoja et al., 2024), and excellent consistency in the current study (α = .86; Supplementary Table S2). Internal consistency for all self-report measures; weight stigma experiences, weight bias internalisation, feeling fat, and self-esteem, was high to excellent across the preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum stages (see Supplementary Table S2 for Cronbach’s α values by stage).

Sample size calculation and data analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS version 29 (IBM Corporation, Armonk NY; U.S.A.). A priori power analysis determined that 477 participants were required to detect a small effect size (f = 0.10) with 80% power (α = .05) for analyses with up to 20 predictor variables (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). This calculation was designed for the primary regression analyses examining associations between weight stigma sources and psychosocial outcomes across reproductive stages. No variable had more than 5% missing data. For variables with missing values, mean substitution was used (Roderick & Little, 1998). We further excluded participants with biologically implausible BMI values (n = 20) to maintain data integrity (Flegal et al., 2019).

To address objective 1, we used descriptive statistics to summarise sample characteristics and key psychosocial outcomes (WBI, feeling fat, self-esteem) across PPP stages and countries. Chi-square tests examined differences in demographics and weight stigma sources across PPP stages, while one-way ANOVAs with Bonferroni corrections identified between group differences. Correlation matrices explored relationships among psychosocial variables. Independent t-tests compared these variables between Western and non-Western participants.

For objectives 2 and 3, we established a priori criteria for combined versus separate analyses by country based on objective 1 results: separate analyses would be conducted only if significant differences (p < .05) with medium effect sizes (Cohen's d ≥ 0.5) were found between Western and non-Western countries for all key outcomes. For objective 2, we first conducted univariate linear regressions to examine associations between demographic factors and weight stigma sources (predictors) and our psychosocial outcomes (WBI, feeling fat, self-esteem). Significant demographic variables (p < .05) were then included as covariates in multivariable regression models. For objective 3, we used the PROCESS macro (model 4) with 5,000 bootstrap resamples (Hayes, 2013) to test whether WBI mediated relationships between significant weight stigma sources and both feeling fat and self-esteem, controlling for significant demographic predictors as confounders.

Objective 1: characterisation of weight stigma experiences, weight bias internalisation, and feeling fat across PPP stages and countries

Weight stigma sources and frequency

Nearly two-thirds (62.6%) of participants reported experiencing weight stigma, with similar prevalence across reproductive stages (preconception: 62.5%, pregnant: 59.2%, postpartum: 65.3%). The most common sources among the overall sample were immediate family, friends, extended family, and the workplace (See Table 2). Significant stage-specific differences were apparent for the media, pregnant women/new mothers, and society in general (Table 3). Weight stigma was typically experienced a few times a month, with the number of sources positively correlating with frequency (r = .21, p < .001).

Cross-country comparison

Women from non-Western countries reported higher levels of weight stigma from friends, the workplace, pregnant women/new mothers, and strangers, while women from Western countries reported higher levels of healthcare-related weight stigma (all p < .05). No cross-country differences were observed for WBI, feeling fat, or self-esteem. Effect sizes for the observed differences ranged from negligible to small-to-moderate (Cohen's d = .01 to 0.43). See Supplementary Table S4 for detailed comparisons.

Objective 2: associations between weight stigma experiences and weight bias internalisation, feeling fat, and self-esteem

Overall

Given the limited cross-country differences in key psychosocial outcomes (WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem), subsequent analyses combined the full sample. In the adjusted multivariable analysis, women who experienced weight stigma from the workplace (p < .001), immediate family (p < .001), the media (p = .009) and friends (p = .02) reported higher WBI. For feeling fat, weight stigma from immediate family (p < .001), the workplace (p = .004), and the media (p = .03) were associated with higher feelings of fatness. For self-esteem, weight stigma from the workplace (p < .001), immediate family (p < .001), and society in general (p = .004) were associated with lower self-esteem. Body weight self-perception was consistently associated with both higher WBI and feeling fat (both p < .001) across all analyses (Full regression results are presented in Supplementary Tables S5–S7).

Stage-specific patterns of weight stigma sources

The association between specific weight stigma sources and WBI varied across reproductive stages. During preconception, media-related weight stigma was associated with higher WBI (p < .001), higher feeling fat (p = .01), and lower self-esteem (p = .003). During pregnancy, workplace stigma was associated with higher WBI (p < .001), higher feeling fat (p = .003), and lower self-esteem (p = .005). In the postpartum period, immediate family stigma showed consistent associations with WBI (p < .001), feeling fat (p = .01), and self-esteem (p = .02) (see Tables S5–S7 for full results).

Objective 3: weight bias internalisation as a mediator between weight stigma experiences and feeling fat and self-esteem

Mediation analyses examined whether WBI explained the relationships between weight stigma sources and psychological outcomes. In the overall sample, mediation analyses revealed that WBI significantly mediated the relationships between key weight stigma sources and psychological outcomes. For feeling fat, significant indirect effects through WBI were found for weight stigma in the workplace (indirect: B = 3.35, 95% CI: 2.37, 4.36; direct B =–0.37, p = .48), immediate family (indirect: B = 2.87, 95% CI: 2.03, 3.70; direct B = 0.57, p = .24), and the media (indirect: B = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.89, 3.77, direct: B = 1.09, p = .09). For self-esteem, significant indirect effects through WBI were found for weight stigma from the workplace (indirect: B = –2.48, 95% CI: –3.24, –1.76; direct B = –5.33, p = .31), immediate family (indirect: B = –2.11, 95% CI: –2.82, –1.50; direct B = –1.07, p = .03), and partners (indirect: B = –1.16, 95% CI: –1.94, –0.42; direct: B = –1.33, p = .04).

Distinct mediation patterns emerged that were different across reproductive stages. In preconception women, media-related weight stigma showed indirect effects through WBI on both feeling fat (indirect: B = 3.50, 95% CI: 2.29, 4.87; direct: B = 1.51, p = .10) and self-esteem (indirect: B = –2.64, 95% CI: –3.72, –1.68; direct: B = –1.68, p = .50). In pregnant women, workplace weight stigma demonstrated indirect effects through WBI on feeling fat (indirect: B = 5.74, 95% CI: 4.01, 7.63; direct: B = -0.53, p = .60) and self-esteem (indirect: B = –3.62, 95% CI: –5.51, –2.09; direct: B = –0.47, p = .66). In postpartum women, immediate family weight stigma showed indirect effects through WBI on both feeling fat (indirect: B = 2.17, 95% CI: 0.81, 3.56; direct: B = 1.35, p = .10) and self-esteem (indirect: B = –1.82, 95% CI: –3.04, –0.8; direct: B = –1.37, p = .10; See Supplementary Tables S8 and S9).

Discussion

Our study examined weight stigma across preconception, pregnancy, and postpartum (PPP) in Western and non-Western countries, focusing on its associations with WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem. To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine these relationships across all three reproductive stages. We found that nearly two-thirds of women experienced weight stigma, with sources varying by reproductive stage and between countries. Importantly, our findings supported our hypothesis that weight bias internalisation mediated the relationship between weight stigma experiences and both feeling fat and self-esteem, despite variations in stigma sources.

The reported prevalence of weight stigma experiences in our sample (62.6%) aligns with previous research in pregnancy and postpartum populations (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020), while extending these findings to the preconception stage. Although reported frequencies were relatively low (i.e. less than monthly to a few times a month), these experiences were reported across women from all BMI ranges, challenging the assumption that weight stigma primarily affects women with higher body weights and reinforcing earlier findings of weight stigma's prevalence across diverse body sizes (Incollingo Rodriguez et al., 2020). Close relationships and the workplace emerged as the most common sources overall, highlighting weight stigma's pervasiveness in both personal and professional contexts. This is important as, while previous research has focused on close relationships as sources of stigma (Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2023; Nagpal et al., 2023), the workplace has received relatively less attention. However, weight stigma sources varied across reproductive stages, with the media most commonly reported in preconception, workplace in pregnancy, and family in postpartum.

Cross-country comparisons revealed differences in the predominant sources of stigma. Women from non-Western countries tended to report more weight stigma from friends and the workplace, while Western women experienced more healthcare-related stigma. These preliminary findings likely reflect differences in healthcare approaches, with Western systems often placing greater emphasis on weight management regardless of the presenting issue (Tylka et al., 2014). However, despite these source differences, levels of WBI, feeling fat, and self-esteem were similar across country contexts. This challenges the notion that body image concerns are primarily Western phenomena and supports research suggesting that globalisation has contributed to standardised body ideals across diverse settings, particularly through increased exposure to Western media (Al Riyami et al., 2024; Khaled et al., 2018; Lang & Ye, 2024; Nagar & Virk, 2017; Thompson et al., 2020).

We further identified clear associations between weight stigma experiences and psychosocial outcomes across all PPP stages including increased WBI, greater feelings of fatness and lower self-esteem. The sources of stigma linked to these outcomes varied according to reproductive stage. In preconception women, media-related weight stigma correlated with negative psychosocial outcomes, suggesting that women anticipating motherhood engage in more frequent upward social comparisons with idealised media images (Festinger, 1954). This finding indicates that weight stigma experiences begin well before pregnancy and may shape body image concerns throughout the reproductive journey. This highlights the need for early interventions targeting media influences to address the harmful body-related messaging women may carry into pregnancy.

Comparing the pregnancy period to preconception, workplace weight stigma demonstrated notable associations with the same negative outcomes, likely reflecting the challenges of navigating professional environments while pregnant. It suggests that pregnant women may face a dual burden: adjusting to physical changes while contending with workplace cultures that may perceive pregnancy as incompatible with professional success, or burdensome to the organisation (Jones et al., 2016). Additionally, these experiences can intersect with broader pregnancy-related discrimination (Skorinko et al., 2020), reinforcing the need for workplace policies that explicitly address weight-related bias and strengthen protections for pregnant employees.

During postpartum, family was a significant source of weight-related pressure, underscoring the complex dynamics of close relationships. Although family support is essential for new mothers, our findings suggest that family members may inadvertently perpetuate stigma surrounding body attitudes and weight. This suggests a paradox: the intimate support system intended to nurture new mothers is also a source of body-related distress. Notably, family-based weight stigma has been linked to diminished relationship quality (Nagpal & Incollingo Rodriguez, 2023). Our findings highlight the influence of family on postpartum weight experiences and downstream body image evolution, underscoring the need to address familial attitudes during this important period.

A key finding of this study is the consistent mediation by WBI in the relationship between weight stigma sources (media, workplace, and family) and both feeling fat and self-esteem. Despite differences in stigma sources across reproductive stages, WBI remained comparable in level and consistently mediated these associations, suggesting it may serve as a common psychological pathway connecting stigma experiences to psychological outcomes across different reproductive stages and countries.

The relationship between WBI and feeling fat warrants attention, particularly given the natural body changes during reproductive phases. Our results indicate that these constructs are closely intertwined, reinforcing the role of internalised stigma in shaping women’s embodied experiences during this time. This is especially important given previous research demonstrating associations between weight stigma and maladaptive dieting (Dever et al., 2025), and between feeling fat and both restrictive and binge eating behaviours (Byrd et al., 2023; Nagpalet al., 2022; Sharp et al., 2023; Striegel-Moore et al., 1986; Tiggemann, 1996). Although eating behaviours were not directly assessed in this study, the consistent mediating role of WBI may help explain how weight stigma contributes to disordered eating patterns documented in prior research (Dever et al., 2025).

Our findings have important implications for health psychology research and practice. The consistent role of WBI as a mediator suggests that psychological interventions targeting WBI may be effective across PPP stages. Cognitive-behavioural approaches that directly address self-stigma, combined with self-compassion interventions and body appreciation practices (Alleva & Tylka, 2021; Geller et al., 2015; Pearl et al., 2023), may help disrupt the internalisation pathway through which external stigma contributes to psychological distress. Screening tools could also be developed by health psychologists to identify women at risk of WBI and support early intervention to promote positive body image and self-esteem across the PPP period. Consistent with ecological models in health psychology, approaches that address environmental influences (e.g. media literacy counselling during preconception, psychological support for managing workplace stressors during pregnancy, and family-based communication interventions postpartum) may complement individual therapy by reducing exposure to weight-stigmatising messages and supporting body image resilience across reproductive stages. Hence, while individual-level interventions may provide immediate support, they must be complemented by broader structural changes addressing the underlying sources of weight stigma that drive these experiences. Without systemic shifts in healthcare, workplace, media, and family contexts, weight stigma will continue to shape women's reproductive experiences, reinforcing harmful norms that perpetuate internalised bias and body dissatisfaction.

A key strength of this study is our geographically diverse sample enhancing generalisability beyond single-country studies. Additionally, we incorporated the preconception phase to assess the full reproductive continuum, as previous research has not done. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Second, while we sought geographical diversity, our sample remains a convenience sample recruited through quota-based panels and findings are not nationally representative of any country. Third, our Western/non-Western grouping, while commonly used in research literature, is a broad classification that does not capture the full sociocultural complexity or diversity that exists between and within countries. This categorisation was used for analytical comparison purposes and does not assume cultural homogeneity. We acknowledge that this framework provides a preliminary basis for examining cross-national differences and cannot capture migration-related complexity or nuanced cultural factors within each setting. Fourth, our sample sizes within individual countries limit the power to detect country-specific effects. Thus, our cross-country comparisons represent preliminary findings requiring replication in larger, nationally representative samples. Future research using longitudinal designs is needed to examine how weight stigma experiences evolve throughout women's reproductive journeys and whether stigma at one stage affects subsequent stages. Additionally, investigating how the mediating pathways we identified longitudinally influence eating behaviours represents an important direction for future research, given the established links between weight stigma, feeling fat, and disordered eating patterns.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that while weight stigma sources vary across reproductive stages—media during preconception, workplace during pregnancy, and family during postpartum—WBI consistently mediates the relationship between these experiences and both feeling fat and self-esteem, such that weight stigma is related to higher internalisation, which in turn is related to greater reports of feeling fat and lower reports of self-esteem. This suggests a common psychological pathway connecting external stigma experiences to internalised attitudes and outcomes, regardless of country context or reproductive stage. Our findings identify WBI as a critical intervention target, indicating that addressing WBI could enhance the effectiveness of stage-specific strategies aimed at external stigma sources. A dual approach that targets both external stigma and its internalisation may provide a more comprehensive framework for supporting women's psychological wellbeing across the reproductive journey.
