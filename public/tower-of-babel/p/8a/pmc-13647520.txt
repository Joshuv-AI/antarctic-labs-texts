Kinesiophobia and its impact on shoulder function in patients following arthroscopic rotator cuff repair of the supraspinatus tendon: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Kai-li Hu, Dan Xie, Hua-zhen Rui, Qing-lian Wu, Yi Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRotator cuff tears are common and debilitating, with arthroscopic repair being the preferred treatment. However, the relationship between kinesiophobia and shoulder function remains insufficiently explored.ObjectivesThis study aims to assess shoulder function and kinesiophobia levels in patients six weeks after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) of the supraspinatus tendon, and to explore their interrelationships to provide insights for improving recovery and patient outcomes.MethodsThe study involved 129 patients who had undergone ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon at a tertiary hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, between March 2023 and May 2024. A general information questionnaire, the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK), the Constant-Murley Score (CMS), the Self-Efficacy for Exercise (SEE) scale, the Groningen Orthopaedic Social Support Scale (GO-SSS), the Numeric Pain rating scale(NRPS), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 (GAD-2) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2) were used to survey. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and regression models via SPSS 29.0 software. The STROBE checklist guides the reporting of the manuscript.ResultsThe average TSK score was 46.90 ± 5.40, with 96.12% of patients scoring above the threshold for high kinesiophobia. The mean score for the CMS was 37.93 ± 16.04. Analysis revealed that TSK score (β = -0.149, p = 0.027), age (β = -0.189, p = 0.005), and NRPS score (β = -0.658, p < 0.001) significantly influenced CMS scores, accounting for 52.7% of the variance in shoulder function.ConclusionsKinesiophobia is at a high level among patients 6 weeks after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon. Higher levels of kinesiophobia are associated with poorer shoulder function following the surgery.Relevance to clinical practiceFuture clinical practice may consider assessing kinesiophobia during early postoperative care to identify patients after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon with fear of movement, which could be considered when planning individualized rehabilitation strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10144-0.

Background

Rotator cuff repair (RCR) is a common treatment for rotator cuff (RC) tears, with arthroscopic repair (ARCR) now accounting for over 95% of all RCR procedures [1]. Compared with traditional open or mini-open repair, ARCR offers advantages including smaller incisions, less soft tissue disruption, reduced immediate postoperative pain, less deltoid morbidity, and decreased postoperative shoulder stiffness [2]. Furthermore, a network meta-analysis demonstrated that ARCR ranked highest in shoulder function scores among various arthroscopic and non-surgical interventions, indicating superior functional improvement [3]. ARCR also enables better mobilization of the rotator cuff and provides improved visualization during surgery [4].

Despite the advantages of ARCR, postoperative stiffness remains a concern, potentially affecting shoulder function and quality of life [5]. High levels of fear-avoidance have been found in up to 56% of patients with chronic musculoskeletal pain, and is associated with greater disability, greater pain intensity and lower quality of life in this population [6]. Effective rehabilitation is essential to optimize patient outcomes, with the primary goals being to restore shoulder function, promote tendon healing, alleviate pain, and reduce the risk of retear [7, 8]. Factors that may adversely affect shoulder function and delay recovery should therefore be carefully addressed during rehabilitation.

Kinesiophobia is defined as an irrational and excessive fear of physical activity due to increased sensitivity to pain and fear that secondary injury from the activity will be detrimental to recovery [9]. This fear often leads to heightened vigilance towards movement, work, and daily activities, exacerbating disability and increasing the likelihood of chronic pain [10]. The fear-avoidance model provides a framework for understanding how fear of pain or reinjury can restrict movement in patients following ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon [9]. It proposes two potential responses: when the context is perceived as safe, the individual engages in active coping; when it is perceived as threatening, a self-reinforcing cycle of fear and avoidance may emerge [11]. This model is especially relevant for shoulder surgery patients, as their perception of pain and reinjury risks significantly impacts their rehabilitation and long-term shoulder function. Recognizing how kinesiophobia influences patients’ perceptions of disability is key to helping clinicians adjust their care strategies and reduce the fear that slows recovery [12]. By including the management of kinesiophobia in rehabilitation goals, clinicians and physiotherapists can implement targeted interventions to support patient progress throughout the treatment process.

Currently, kinesiophobia has been extensively studied in patients with low back pain [10], neck pain [13], and knee injuries [14], but evidence specifically addressing the relationship between kinesiophobia and shoulder function in patients after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon remains limited. Although some studies have reported a high prevalence of kinesiophobia in the early postoperative period following ARCR [15], and a negative association between kinesiophobia and shoulder function has been observed in patients undergoing mini open repair [16], However, few studies have directly examined this relationship in the context of purely arthroscopic repair. Since ARCR is now the dominant treatment approach and shoulder function is essential for daily activities such as driving, dressing, and reaching [17], understanding how kinesiophobia influences recovery is critical. Further research is therefore needed to clarify its role and inform postoperative rehabilitation strategies.

This cross-sectional study aims to assess shoulder function and kinesiophobia at 6 weeks after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) of the supraspinatus tendon, and to explore their association while considering other potential influencing factors. The 6-week time point was selected based on the American Society of Shoulder and Elbow Therapists’ guidelines, which define this period as the first phase of post-operative rehabilitation [7]. The hypothesis of this study is: Kinesiophobia is negatively associated with shoulder function in patients following ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon.

Methods

Participants and design

A convenience sampling method was used to select patients who had undergone ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon at a tertiary hospital in Wuhan, Hubei Province, China, between March 2023 and May 2024, with data collected 6 weeks after surgery. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Research Ethics Committee of Tongji Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval Code: TJ-IRB 202411081).

Eligibility criteria: The inclusion criteria for this study were patients who underwent unilateral arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) surgery due to partial or small to massive nontraumatic tears of the supraspinatus tendon, diagnosed by MRI, with a tear size of less than 5 cm. Exclusion criteria included the presence of a stiff shoulder or arthropathy, history of ipsilateral shoulder surgery, shoulder instability, lesions in rotator cuff tendons other than the supraspinatus, non-repairable rotator cuff ruptures, and significant comorbidities such as neurological disorders or rheumatoid arthritis. All surgery procedures were performed by the same surgeon. The STROBE checklist guided the reporting [18]. The minimum sample size required was estimated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.6). A multiple linear regression analysis was planned with shoulder function (Constant-Murley Score) as the dependent variable, an effect size of 0.15, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power (1-β) of 0.80, following the sample size calculation approach used in previous studies of similar populations [19]. Considering a potential dropout rate of 20% [20], the required sample size for the study was calculated to be 118 cases.

Data collection

Before the screening, eligible patients received detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures, potential risks, and benefits during their hospitalization. Potential risks included discomfort from answering personal questions, privacy concerns, and emotional distress from recalling their surgery. Benefits included contributing to research that may improve future care, gaining insight into their recovery, and receiving extra support if needed. After surgery, all patients follow a standardized rehabilitation program. The hospital has developed videos to guide the post-operative recovery process, with each stage demonstrating key exercises and providing clear instructions. Patients can access the videos at any time via a QR code or online platform to review and follow the exercises after discharge. The QR codes are provided in Supplementary file (1) For continuous support, patients may contact the ward by phone or schedule follow-up appointments at the outpatient clinic at any time post-surgery. This allows the healthcare team to monitor progress and make necessary adjustments to the rehabilitation plan based on individual recovery. If they agreed to join, they were required to sign two informed consent forms—one for the research team and one for their own records. The survey was conducted by telephone, starting with an explanation of the study’s aim and obtaining consent from all participants before proceeding with any questions. The questionnaire was shown in Supplementary file (2) Data were collected using an online survey hosted on the Wenjuanxing platform. Two trained researchers conducted the survey by phone, completing the online questionnaire after speaking with the patients. To ensure consistency, both researchers received standardized training, followed identical procedures for administering the questionnaire, and regularly cross-checked their data. The survey was administered six weeks post-surgery, with participants required to answer all questions. It took approximately 20 min to complete, and each questionnaire item included an explanation to assist the researchers in clarifying any unclear questions for the participants. The researchers would also refer to medical information system to verify the accuracy of information, such as age, education, and payment method.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

Patient characteristics were collected using a sociodemographic questionnaire developed by the research team. The questionnaire covered information such as age, body mass index (BMI), gender, place of residence, education, marital status, employment, payment method, monthly income, and the affected limb.

Kinesiophobia

Kinesiophobia, or the fear of movement, was evaluated using the Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK). This scale consists of 17 questions, each rated on a 4-point scale from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree” [21]. Four questions (4, 8, 12, and 16) are scored inversely. The total score ranges from 17 to 68, and a score of 37 or higher indicates high levels of kinesiophobia [22]. The Chinese version of the TSK used in this study has been validated for the Chinese population by Hu [22]. This validation ensures that the scale is culturally appropriate and reliable for our study population.

Shoulder function

The Constant-Murley Score (CMS) was employed to assess objective shoulder function levels [23]. The CMS is a 100-point scale that evaluates four areas: pain (15 points), activities of daily living (20 points), strength (25 points), and range of motion (ROM) including elevation, abduction, external, and internal rotation (40 points). A higher score indicates better shoulder function [24]. The Chinese version of the CMS has been validated for its reproducibility, reliability, and construct validity [25].

Self-efficacy for exercise

The Self-Efficacy for Exercise (SEE) scale assesses participants’ confidence in their ability to exercise. This scale has been thoroughly validated and is known for its reliability and validity, particularly among older adults [26]. The SEE scale was first developed by Resnick and Jenkins and includes nine items rated from 0 to 10. The total score can range from 0 to 90, with higher scores indicating greater confidence in one’s ability to exercise [27]. The scale has been adapted and validated for use in China by Lee et al. [28].

Social support

The Groningen Orthopaedic Social Support Scale (GO-SSS) was used to evaluate participants’ social support. This scale consists of 12 questions divided into two subscales: perceived social support (7 items) and instrumental support (5 items) [29]. Respondents select from four options on a Likert scale: never or rarely, occasionally, regularly, and often. Higher scores reflect a greater perception of social support. The GO-SSS has demonstrated high reliability and validity. It has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89 overall and an internal consistency of 0.86 for the perceived social support subscale [30].

Pain

The Numeric Pain Rating Scale (NRPS), a basic self-reported measure, was used for assessing pain intensity. Patients rate their pain on the NRPS, ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (unbearable pain), typically assessing their pain at its worst [31]. High scores indicate increased pain. The categorization of pain intensity based on the NRPS score is as follows: 0–3 represents mild pain, 4–6 represents moderate pain, and 7–10 represents severe pain.

Mental health

Anxiety and depression were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-2 scale (GAD-2) and the Patient Health Questionnaire-2 (PHQ-2), respectively. Both scales are validated tools for measuring core symptoms of anxiety and depression [32, 33]. The GAD-2 measures the core features of anxiety, specifically feelings of nervousness and uncontrollable worrying [32]. The PHQ-2 evaluates the two primary features of depression: depressed mood or hopelessness and a loss of interest or pleasure in most activities [34]. Respondents indicate how often they experienced these symptoms over the past two weeks [35]. Scores for both the GAD-2 and PHQ-2 are summed, with a range from 0 to 6, where higher scores indicate poorer mental health.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) Version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Data analysis was performed by two researchers working collaboratively. One researcher was primarily responsible for conducting the analysis, while the second researcher was tasked with reviewing and verifying the results to ensure accuracy and consistency. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of the sample. To assess the normality of the data, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed. For measurement data, results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (M ± SD). A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess between-group differences. Count data were reported as absolute and relative frequencies, with the chi-square test applied for between-group comparisons. Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the correlation between shoulder function and factors such as kinesiophobia, pain, anxiety, depression, social support, and self-efficacy for exercise, as measured by the CMS, TSK, NRPS, GAD-2, PHQ-2, GO-SSS and SEE. Multiple linear regression analysis was employed to investigate the influence of kinesiophobia on shoulder function, with kinesiophobia assessed using the TSK and shoulder function evaluated by the CMS. Variables with statistically significant results(p < 0.05) from the ANOVA, chi-square tests, and Pearson correlation analysis were included in the multiple regression model using the stepwise method. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported for the estimated coefficients in the regression analysis.

Results

Characteristics of participants

A total of 140 questionnaires were initially distributed, but 11 participants were excluded due to incomplete data resulting from either follow-up interruptions or inability to contact the participants. Consequently, the final sample comprised 129 patients who had undergone ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon and were included in this study. The average age of the participants was 55.43 ± 10.93 years, and the average BMI of 24.11 ± 2.74 kg/m². Detailed demographic information is provided in Table 1. Among the participants, 56.59% were female, and 93.80% were married. The residential distribution as follows: 37.98% from urban areas, 27.91% from towns, and 34.11% from rural areas. Most participants had medical insurance (89.15%), although their monthly incomes varied. Employment status varied as well, with a majority being self-employed or in other employment categories. Additionally, 74.42% of participants underwent surgical repair of the RC in their right limb. The scores for pain (measured by NRPS), self-efficacy (measured by SEE), social support (measured by GO-SSS), mental health (measured by GAD-2 and PHQ-2), kinesiophobia (measured by TSK), and shoulder function (measured by CMS) are shown in Table 1. Among these, 124 patients (96.12%) had a TSK score exceeding 37.

Correlation analysis between kinesiophobia (TSK) and shoulder function (CMS)

The correlation analysis revealed significant negative associations between shoulder function (as measured by CMS) and pain (NRPS), anxiety (GAD-2), and fear of movement (TSK) in patients following ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon, as detailed in Table 2. Specifically, poorer shoulder function was significantly associated with higher pain levels (NRPS) (r = -0.690, p < 0.001), increased anxiety (GAD-2) (r = -0.220, p = 0.012), and greater fear of movement (TSK) (r = -0.330, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the analysis found significant positive correlations between patients’ pain (NRPS) (r = 0.207, p = 0.004), anxiety (GAD-2) (r = 0.267, p < 0.001), and depression (PHQ-2) (r = 0.250, p < 0.001) with their fear of exercise (TSK). These correlations were all statistically significant, as shown in Table 2.

Factors influencing shoulder function (CMS) in patients after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon: the role of kinesiophobia

Through ANOVA, chi-square tests, and Pearson correlation analysis, it was found that factors such as education level, payment method, pain (NRPS), kinesiophobia (TSK), anxiety (GAD-2), and age were statistically significantly associated with differences in shoulder function (CMS). The variables with statistically significant results from the univariate analyses were subsequently included in a multiple linear regression for further analysis. As shown in Table 3, CMS scores were significantly influenced by age(β=-0.189, p = 0.005), NRPS score(β=-0.658, p < 0.001), and TSK score(β=-0.149, p = 0.027), accounting for 52.7% of the variance. Specifically, older age, higher pain levels as indicated by the NRPS, and greater fear of movement (TSK) were all associated with poorer shoulder function.

Discussions

This study identified several key findings regarding kinesiophobia and its impact on shoulder function in patients after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon. Kinesiophobia was found to be prevalent in this patient population, with a mean Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK) score significantly exceeding the threshold indicating concern. This heightened fear of movement was closely associated with poorer shoulder function, as measured by the Constant-Murley Score (CMS), and influenced by factors such as pain intensity and age. Specifically, older age, higher pain levels, and greater fear of movement were identified as key factors linked to poorer shoulder function following surgery.

The study found an average Constant-Murley Score (CMS) of 37.93 ± 16.04, indicating significant shoulder dysfunction. This is consistent with a previous study of ARCR patients six weeks post-surgery, which reported a CMS of 41 [36]. These findings highlight the challenges patients face in regaining full shoulder function, especially in the early stages of recovery. While significant improvements in function (CMS) and strength are typically observed at six months post-surgery, some patients may experience a temporary decline before further progress is made [37]. The low CMS may indicate potential concerns, underscoring the need for careful management and personalized rehabilitation in the early postoperative phase. According to the consensus statement from American Society of Shoulder and Elbow Therapists, most patients only need to complete the first three phases of rehabilitation [7]. Phase 1, lasting six weeks after surgery, primarily involves passive exercises aimed at minimizing stress on the repaired tendon [7]. In addition to physical exercises, effective rehabilitation includes education and cognitive restructuring to help patients reframe their understanding of pain [38]. This approach aims to disrupt the cycle where pain leads to reduced activity, which then exacerbates both pain and disability. By addressing both functional and behavioral aspects of recovery, rehabilitation can improve overall function and well-being.

The findings indicated a high prevalence of kinesiophobia among patients at 6 weeks after ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon, with 96.12% scoring above the TSK threshold of 37. This high rate may be associated with postoperative factors such as pain, limited mobility, and concerns about re-injury, as the assessment was conducted at 6 weeks post-surgery, when patients are still in the early phase of recovery. Consistent with this finding, Wang et al. [16] reported that elevated levels of kinesiophobia persist in many patients after RCR. These findings suggest that although kinesiophobia is common after ARCR, the relatively high prevalence observed in this study warrants attention. Kinesiophobia can significantly affect daily activities and overall quality of life by creating a fear of movement [17]. According to the fear-avoidance (FA) model, this fear encourages avoidance behaviors, which can lead to pain-related disability, emotional distress, and physical decline [39]. Over time, heightened awareness of pain and continued avoidance of movement may prolong recovery and contribute to chronic pain [10]. It is important to note that kinesiophobia does not necessarily imply avoidance of exercise. While patients may feel anxious about movement, many still engage in physical activity, albeit with caution. Therefore, clinicians should first assess patients’ attitudes toward exercise and physical activity. The Avoidance of Daily Activities Photo Scale (ADAPS), a reliable and valid tool, is particularly useful in evaluating avoidance behaviors in adults with arthroscopic rotator cuff injuries, especially those who are not athletes [40]. Using such tools allows clinicians to better understand how kinesiophobia affects patients’ daily functioning. Once kinesiophobia is identified, clinicians can educate patients on the benefits of movement, create personalized and safe exercise plans, and select appropriate methods and intensities to support recovery. This approach helps reduce kinesiophobia while also boosting motivation for rehabilitation.

As showed in Table 3, this study identified age, pain intensity, and kinesiophobia as key factors affecting shoulder function, accounting for 52.7% of the variance in Constant-Murley Scores (CMS). Among these, pain intensity had the greatest impact on shoulder function, with higher pain levels being associated with poorer shoulder function. Older patients and those with greater kinesiophobia also demonstrated worse functional outcomes. Consistent with a previous study, the results showed that lower postoperative CMS scores were associated with older age in patients undergoing ARCR [41].

Table 3 reveals kinesiophobia was identified as a significant variable associated with shoulder function in patients who have undergone ARCR of the supraspinatus tendon. It is noteworthy that the scores on the TSK were relatively high for these patients, indicating that fear of movement is common and may impact their recovery and functional outcomes. Previous research has indicated that periodic kinesiophobia assessments can pinpoint areas of rehabilitation concern [42]. This requires healthcare providers to tailor rehabilitation programs more effectively and, consequently, improve recovery outcomes.

Regarding the impact of pain on shoulder joint function, it is possible that the greater sensation of pain in early rehabilitation limits range of motion (ROM) progression [43]. A higher degree of acute postoperative pain should be expected in patients undergoing arthroscopic rotator cuff tear repair compared to other arthroscopic shoulder procedures [44]. Furthermore, Yaari et al. ’s [43] study found that an individual’s pain sensitivity was an important factor influencing the surgical outcomes for RCR, regardless of the surgical technique used. Patients undergoing RCR who had high pain sensitivity experienced less improvement in ROM and worse patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) after surgery compared with patients who had normal pain sensitivity. Notably, preoperative pain sensitivity questionnaires (PSQ) may serve as indicators of postoperative pain improvement. Effective pain management and strategies may be crucial for improving functional outcomes in these patients. However, it is important to note that, as shown in Tables 2 and 3, while significant correlations exist between CMS and NRPS, TSK, the magnitude of these correlations is relatively low. This suggests that other unaccounted variables may also influence the recovery and function of the shoulder. Therefore, future research should explore the more complex relationships between these factors and investigate how multidimensional interventions could be used to enhance shoulder function recovery.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered to prevent overgeneralization of the findings. First, due to time and resource constraints, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary hospital ward using convenience sampling. This method may introduce selection bias and limit the ability to generalize the results to a broader population. Second, data were collected via phone rather than paper or online questionnaires. This approach may have limitations related to data accuracy. In addition, this study selected 6 weeks post-surgery as the endpoint, and patients are still in the early phase of recovery, which may limit the interpretation of functional outcomes. Future research should include a larger, more diverse sample and use random sampling techniques to reduce selection bias. In-person data collection via paper or online questionnaires could improve data quality and protect patient privacy. Furthermore, assessing the correlation between kinesiophobia at 6 weeks and functional outcomes at 12 months would provide more meaningful insights, as shoulder function at 6 weeks remains limited in this patient population.

Conclusions

This study examined the impact of kinesiophobia on shoulder function in patients after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) of the supraspinatus tendon. The results indicate that kinesiophobia levels are notably high among patients 6 weeks after the procedure, surpassing the threshold of concern. A significant association was found between higher levels of kinesiophobia and poorer shoulder function. As assessments were conducted only at 6 weeks, when patients are still in early recovery, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should include larger, more diverse samples and investigate how kinesiophobia at 6 weeks relates to longer-term functional outcomes, providing guidance for optimizing postoperative rehabilitation strategies.
