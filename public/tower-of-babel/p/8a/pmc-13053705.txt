Mechanistic insights into cartilage-sensory nerve crosstalk in osteoarthritis progression
Authors: Huan Meng, Junxuan Ma, Line Kawtharany, Rui Yue, Chunyi Wen, Sibylle Grad, Olivier Chassande, Zhen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) pain arises from dynamic crosstalk between degraded cartilage and sensitized sensory nerves. Cartilage-derived signals, including nerve growth factor (NGF), pro-inflammatory cytokines, and matrix-degrading enzymes, promote nerve sprouting and hyperexcitability. While sensory afferents release neuropeptides that further amplify inflammation and cartilage degeneration. Altered joint mechanics additionally activate mechanosensitive ion channels, linking biomechanical stress to nociceptive signaling. This review summarizes current knowledge on cartilage–sensory nerve interactions in OA and their contribution to pain progression. We discuss key molecular mediators, biomarkers, and therapeutic targets, and provide an overview of in vivo, in vitro, and ex vivo model platforms for studying cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk, highlighting emerging predictive systems for mechanistic and translational research. Mechanistic insights into neuroinflammatory and mechanosensitive pathways support the development of biomarkers for pain phenotyping and patient stratification. These insights have direct implications for clinical trial design and interpretation, particularly in addressing discordance between structural and symptomatic outcomes.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a leading cause of pain and disability in aging populations. While traditionally viewed as a disease of cartilage degeneration, OA pain often does not correlate well with structural severity, highlighting the need to explore its underlying molecular and neurobiological drivers.

One emerging concept is the bidirectional crosstalk between degraded cartilage and sensitized sensory nerves. Although articular cartilage is inherently avascular and aneural, joint damage appears to trigger the release of cytokines, neurotrophic factors, and lipid mediators from cartilage and synovium. These molecules may promote nerve sprouting and hyperexcitability in the subchondral bone and periarticular soft tissues. In turn, activated sensory afferents release neuropeptides and inflammatory mediators that can further enhance cartilage degradation and inflammation—suggesting a self-amplifying feedback loop that may contribute to pain persistence and disease progression.

This neuroinflammatory axis is increasingly recognized as a contributor to mechanical hypersensitivity and chronic OA pain, and may represent a valuable source of mechanism-based therapeutic targets. In this review, we examine the current understanding of cartilage–sensory nerve interactions, evaluate key findings from in vivo animal models, in vitro co-culture systems, and human tissue studies, and propose translational strategies for improving biomarker-based patient stratification, mechanistic target validation, and the development of predictive neural-cartilage model systems.

Understanding cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk also requires experimental model systems that can capture neural–cartilage interactions under inflammatory and mechanical conditions. While in vivo OA pain models remain essential for studying integrated joint pathology, their translational resolution to OA pain mechanism is limited. Recent advances in in vitro and ex vivo platforms provide complementary approaches to dissect cartilage–sensory nerve signaling with greater mechanistic control, motivating a dedicated discussion of current and emerging model systems in this review.

Previous reviews have addressed OA pain from inflammatory, structural, or neural perspectives, but these processes are often discussed in isolation. In contrast, the present review centers specifically on cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk as a unifying mechanism linking joint degeneration to pain. Our novel contribution lies in integrating sensory neuropeptides, inflammatory mediators, and mechanosensitive ion channels into a single translational framework that connects molecular mechanisms with pain phenotypes and clinical decision-making. By systematically aligning evidence across experimental contexts, we propose a biologically grounded pain endotyping concept with direct relevance for patient stratification and mechanism-informed therapeutic development.

To ensure transparency while retaining the scope of a narrative review, we performed a structured literature search to identify studies relevant to cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk in OA. The database PubMed was searched for articles published between January 2000 and December 2025. Core search terms included combinations of osteoarthritis, cartilage, sensory nerve or sensory neuron, pain, neuropeptide, nerve growth factor, mechanotransduction, and inflammation. Studies were included if they investigated (i) molecular or cellular mechanisms linking cartilage and sensory nerves, (ii) neurogenic or inflammatory mediators relevant to OA pain, or (iii) experimental or clinical evidence supporting translational relevance to OA pain phenotypes. Both preclinical (in vitro and in vivo) and human studies were considered. Exclusion criteria included studies unrelated to OA, investigations focused exclusively on motor neurons, or reports lacking mechanistic or pain-relevant outcomes (Figure 1). Given the aim of integrating mechanistic and translational evidence rather than performing a systematic meta-analysis, no formal risk-of-bias scoring was applied. Instead, evidence is explicitly contextualized according to experimental level. The role of sensory nerves and neuropeptides in regulating cartilage degradation in OA

In this review, ‘sensory neurons’ refers to the neuronal cell bodies, primarily located in dorsal root ganglia, whereas ‘sensory nerves’ or ‘sensory fibers’ denotes their peripheral axons and terminal endings within joint tissues. Although the terms differ based on context, they represent components of the same sensory pathway involved in OA pain mechanisms.

In OA, sensory nerves in joint tissues become hyperactive, contributing to pain and promoting cartilage degradation through the release of pro-inflammatory neuropeptides. Understanding sensory innervation and its mechanisms is crucial for developing neural-targeted therapies. This section explores the role of sensory nerves in cartilage degradation and OA pathophysiology.

Sensory innervation in OA

Sensory nerves play active roles in regulating inflammation. During acute inflammation, the rapid neural response takes place as a fast “alarm system”, this is marked with increased sympathetic and sensory but decreased parasympathetic growth and activity. These neural responses mobilize energy for immune activation and tissue repair. However, in chronic conditions like OA, sustained peripheral nerve activation, including changes in the innervation patterns and increased expression of nociceptive molecules in joint tissue, may further promote ongoing inflammation, tissue remodeling, and structural damage of OA joints [24].

Sensory innervation in subchondral bone

Early studies questioned whether sensory fiber density differs between healthy and OA joints (for review see [25]), however, latest evidence suggests that the sensory innervation and the nerve activity is elevated in subchondral bone, in both rodent models [26,27] and human tissue samples [28]. K. Aso et al. [28] reported an increase of calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) immunoreactive sensory nerves in osteochondral channels in human symptomatic OA than in asymptomatic cases, indicating the increased innervation in osteochondral junction is related to OA pain. In addition, the authors further confirmed these findings in meniscal transection rat OA models, that significantly increased CGRP positive sensory nerves were found in subchondral bone of OA rats. In another late-stage OA rat model, electrophysiological studies report increased activity and sensitivity of Aδ and C sensory nerve fibers in subchondral bone [26]. In addition, in a mice OA model, osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling has been shown to promote sensory nerve invasion [27], linking bone turnover to pain generation. These findings suggest that subchondral innervation may contribute to OA pain and represent a potential therapeutic target.

Sensory innervation in joint soft tissues

Altered innervation has also been observed in the synovium and joint capsule. G. Saxler et al. [29] found increased Substance P (SP)- and CGRP-positive fibers in the synovium and fossa acetabuli of painful hip OA patients. Rodent models show elevated CGRP- and Netrin-1-positive nerve fibers in inflamed synovium [30,31]. However, this trend is not consistent across all animal models. In a collagenase-induced acute mice OA model, CGRP-positive nerve density in synovium decreased markedly within one week [32]. Another study reported a reduced synovial innervation correlating with disease severity in a naturally occurring equine OA model [33]. These discrepancies suggest that synovial sensory innervation is dynamic, and may depend on disease stage, model type, and timing of assessment. Standardized, time-resolved models are needed to accurately quantify synovial nerve changes during OA progression.

Sensory fibers are also present in other joint soft tissues. In human knee OA, S. Ashraf et al. [34] reported that medial meniscus vascularization and sensory innervation increased with cartilage degeneration. Similarly, S. M. Ikeuchi et al. [35] found persistent nociceptive innervation in posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) in OA patients, suggesting it as another contributor to OA-related pain.

Sensory neuropeptides and cartilage degradation in OA

Beyond structural changes such as aberrant sensory innervation, activation of sensory nerves in OA leads to the release of neuropeptides and neurotransmitters that actively regulate joint inflammation and cartilage homeostasis, a process commonly referred to as neurogenic inflammation [36]. Among these mediators, substance P (SP), calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP), neuropeptide Y (NPY), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) have emerged as key molecular links between sensory nerves and cartilage degradation. Substance P (SP)

SP, a member of the tachykinin neuropeptide family, binds to the Neurokinin-1 Receptor (NK1R) to initiate downstream signaling cascades. SP is widely expressed in various immune cells and is actively involved in immunoregulation [37]. Activation of NK1R initiates downstream signaling cascades involving mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), phospholipase C (PLC), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). In chondrocytes, SP stimulation enhances the release of pro-inflammatory factors, such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), reactive oxygen species (ROS), interleukin-1 (IL-1), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), further exacerbating cartilage damage [39]. Interestingly, SP differentially affects chondrocytes from healthy and OA cartilage: while it has mild effects on healthy cells, it significantly upregulates inflammatory markers, inhibits chondrogenic markers, and promotes apoptosis and senescence in OA chondrocytes [1]. Collectively, SP–NK1R signaling amplifies inflammatory and catabolic responses within cartilage, reinforcing neurogenic inflammation and matrix breakdown.

Calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP)

CGRP is a potent vasoactive neuropeptide released from peptidergic sensory nerve fibers and signals through a heteromeric receptor complex composed of the calcitonin receptor-like receptor (CLR) and receptor activity-modifying protein 1 (RAMP1). CGRP receptor activation primarily engages cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)–dependent signaling, including protein kinase A (PKA), while also interacting with MAPK and inflammatory signaling pathways in a context-dependent manner (Figure 3) [40].

In joint tissues, CGRP enhances inflammatory mediator production and promotes angiogenic responses, both of which are implicated in OA progression [41,42]. In chondrocytes, CGRP signaling suppresses expression of anabolic cartilage markers while augmenting inflammatory and catabolic gene expressions [1,43]. Preclinical studies showed promising results that the CGRP knockout in mice prevents overexpression of inflammation markers in OA-affected knee joints and reduces catabolic cartilage turnover markers [44]. Furthermore, inhibition of CGRP activity by a CGRP receptor antagonist, Rimegepant, could alleviate OA progression by inhibiting cartilage catabolic gene expression in mice after destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) [3]. Together, these results suggest that CGRP signaling may contribute to cartilage degradation and represents a potential therapeutic target of OA.

Neuropeptide Y (NPY)

NPY is widely expressed in both central and peripheral nervous systems and signals through multiple G protein–coupled receptors, among which the Y1, Y2, and Y5 receptors are most relevant in joint tissues. In the context of cartilage biology, the Y2 receptor has been implicated as a dominant mediator of NPY-induced catabolic effects.

Activation of NPY receptors modulates intracellular pathways including phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)–AKT and mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) signaling [45]. In chondrocytes, NPY–Y2 receptor signaling promotes metabolic reprogramming and shifts the balance toward catabolic activity, contributing to extracellular matrix degradation [7]. These findings suggest that NPY acts as a metabolic and inflammatory regulator at the interface of sensory nerve signaling and cartilage homeostasis.

Vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP)

In contrast to SP and CGRP, which predominantly show catabolic effects, VIP generally exerts anti-inflammatory and cartilage-protective effects. VIP signals through VPAC1 and VPAC2 receptors, both of which are G protein–coupled receptors that robustly activate adenylate cyclase and cAMP–PKA signaling pathways. This signaling axis is known to antagonize pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs.

VIP levels measured in cartilage and synovial fluid were found to be negatively correlated with both Kellgren–Lawrence (K-L) grade and Mankin score, suggesting a link to disease severity [4]. In chondrocytes and synovial cells, VIP suppresses NF-κB activation and downregulates expression of inflammatory cytokines and matrix-degrading enzymes [5,46]. Proteomic and transcriptional analyses further demonstrate that VIP signaling attenuates inflammatory and extracellular matrix catabolic pathways while supporting cartilage homeostasis [47]. Collectively, VIP represents a counter-regulatory neuropeptide that may restrain neurogenic inflammation and cartilage degradation under physiological conditions, with loss of VIP signaling potentially contributing to OA progression.

Together, these sensory neuropeptides engage distinct receptor-specific signaling pathways in cartilage and joint-resident cells, resulting in either amplification (SP, CGRP, NPY) or attenuation (VIP) of inflammatory and catabolic processes. By acting at the interface of sensory nerve activity and cartilage biology, these pathways establish a mechanistic foundation for cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk in OA and provide a molecular basis for subsequent discussion of evidence integration, biomarker potential, and translational targeting.

The role of cartilage degradation and inflammation in regulating sensory nerve activity in OA

Under OA conditions, cartilage-derived factors such as nerve growth factor (NGF), pro-inflammatory cytokines, and other inflammatory mediators have been shown to regulate sensory nerve growth, sensitivity, and pain-related gene expression, thereby amplifying nociceptive signaling (Figure 2).

This interaction may constitute a feedback loop between cartilage degradation and neural sensitization, potentially contributing to symptom persistence and disease progression. This section reviews cartilage-derived factors that regulate sensory nerve activity (Table 1).

Cartilage-derived NGF promoting sensory neural activity

NGF exerts its biological effects through two receptors: the high-affinity TrkA and the low-affinity p75NTR. Both receptors, along with NGF itself, are expressed in joint tissues, including chondrocytes [48]. Elevated NGF levels are detected in OA cartilage [9], synovial fluid and serum of patients [8], associated with disease severity. In in vitro and ex vivo studies, chondrocytes respond to inflammatory stimuli such as IL-1β and mechanical stress by upregulating NGF production, indicating a direct link between cartilage damage and nerve sensitization [10]. Synovial fibroblasts and macrophages also contribute to increased NGF production in response to TNF-α and IL-1β [49], indicating that degraded cartilage and synovium are major NGF sources in OA joints.

NGF is well studied to promote sensory nerve remodeling by inducing axonal sprouting in vitro [50] and by modulating nociceptive ion channels and neuropeptides (for review see [51]). Elevated NGF levels are reported to stimulate peripheral nerve terminals to sprout aberrantly in a mice model, along with OA related pathological changes such as knee swelling, synovitis and subchondral remodeling [52]. Functionally, NGF-TrkA signaling regulates ion channels like transient receptor potential vanilloid 1 (TRPV1), and neuropeptides such as CGRP and SP, amplifying nociceptive signaling [51]. Prolonged NGF exposure promotes long-term changes in neural circuits, reinforcing pain pathways and contributing to the chronic, refractory pain often seen in late-stage OA [53]. These findings provide the mechanistic basis for anti-NGF therapy currently being evaluated in clinical trials for OA pain management [54].

Osteoarthritic cartilage-derived inflammatory factors affecting sensory nerve activity

Chronic inflammation in OA joints significantly affects peripheral nerve structure and function. Unlike acute inflammation, where nerve regeneration supports tissue repair, persistent inflammation promotes aberrant nerve growth, sensitization, and chronic pain. Cartilage-derived proinflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 influence sensory nerves directly and indirectly by amplifying the inflammatory cascade.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines

IL-1β is a central pro-inflammatory cytokine in OA pathophysiology. It promotes sensory nerve growth, as shown in nerve injury animal models where IL-1β enhanced axonal regeneration and neurite outgrowth [11]. IL-1β also modulate sensory nerve sensitization by upregulating TRPV1 expression in a rat model [55], and by increasing action potential amplitude and sodium current in vitro DRG culture [12]. IL-1β also drives the expression and release of neuropeptides such as SP and CGRP (for review see [56]), reinforcing nociceptive signaling.

TNF-α, another key cytokine elevated in OA, acts directly on sensory nerves through TNF receptors [13]. It modulating voltage-gated sodium channels and TRP channels like TRPV1 and TRPV4 in vitro [57]. Intra-articular TNF-α injection in rats progressively activated nociceptive fibers, an effect reversed by TNF-α neutralization [13]. Additionally, in OA joints, TNF-α is shown to activate NF-κB pathways, upregulating secondary cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, Receptor Activator of Nuclear factor-kappa-B(RANK)) (for review see [57]), thereby creating a self-perpetuating pro-inflammatory loop impacting nerve activity.

IL-6, which is often induced downstream of IL-1β and TNF-α, also modulates sensory nerves. IL-6 receptors are expressed on sensory neurons [58], supporting their survival and sensitization [59]. In in vivo experiments intra-articular IL-6 administration heightened C-fiber mechanical sensitivity, which was reversible by IL-6 antagonists [14]. IL-6 further upregulates nociceptive neuropeptide SP in vitro [15], enhancing pain responses.

Inflammatory mediators

Persistent cytokine activation triggers downstream inflammatory mediators, notably PGE2 and Nitric Oxide (NO), all of which further influence sensory nerve activity.

PGE2, synthesized via cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) activation under NF-κB signaling, promotes nociception by activating prostanoid receptors (EP) 1-4 and sensitizing Ca2+ and TRPV1 channels [60]. Clinically, the nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) COX/COX-2 inhibitors relieve OA pain partly by dampening PGE2 pathways. PGE2 also stimulates IL-6 and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) expression [61], linking inflammatory pain to cartilage degradation.

Produced by inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in OA chondrocytes, NO correlates with pain severity in OA patients [62]. Inhibiting NO production alleviates OA pain in animal models [62]. At the cellular level, NO modulates neural excitability by altering the activity of ion channels including KCNQ, K+ATP, TRP, and calcium channels [17].

Together, these cytokines and inflammatory mediators help restructure the sensory neurochemical environment in OA. Their combined actions may promote nerve sprouting, sensitization, and chronic pain states, highlighting them as potential targets for disease-modifying and pain-relieving interventions.

Altered mechanical environment in OA affecting neuron structure and function

Mechanical environment changes of cartilage and subchondral bone in OA

Healthy articular cartilage provides a smooth, lubricated surface with lower stiffness than bone, protecting subchondral structures from excessive mechanical load. Cartilage ECM, composed of a collagen network, proteoglycans, and interstitial water, ensures compressive resilience. In OA, ECM synthesis is suppressed and degradation accelerated, disrupting the collagen type II and proteoglycan architecture. Consequently, cartilage stiffness decreases by 20–80% [63], and relaxation modulus drops by up to 40% [64], compromising load-bearing capacity. As cartilage deteriorates, abnormal stress transmission to subchondral bone induces significant structural remodeling. Early changes include bone marrow lesions (BMLs), detectable via MRI and associated with disease progression [65]. Subchondral bone shows a loss of rod-like trabeculae, thickened plate-like trabeculae, and expansion of calcified cartilage invading the overlying hyaline cartilage, causing tidemark duplication or disruption [66]. These mechanical alterations may create a profoundly changed microenvironment for sensory nerves, likely affecting their mechanotransduction properties and contributing to OA pain.

Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β) derived from chondrocytes

In articular cartilage, Transforming Growth Factor-β (TGF-β) is secreted by chondrocytes in an inactive form and deposited into the matrix. Upon mechanical and biochemical stimulation, latent TGF-β is activated and binds to its receptors on chondrocyte membranes, triggering downstream signaling pathways [67]. Although TGF-β is traditionally regarded as an anti-inflammatory factor, its role differs markedly between healthy and OA joints. In normal cartilage, TGF-β maintains chondrocyte differentiation and tissue homeostasis, whereas in OA, persistently elevated TGF-β levels contribute to cartilage degradation and pathological remodeling [68].

Beyond cartilage, TGF-β plays an important role in regulating peripheral nerve function. It promotes nerve regeneration after injury and enhances neurite outgrowth, as shown in cultured sensory neurons [18]. TGF-β has the potential to regulate the functions of sensory nerves. In vitro exposure to TGFβ1 enhanced sensory neuronal excitability, reduced voltage-gated A-type K+ currents (IA), and downregulated Kv1.4 (KCNA4) gene expression [69]. TGF-β also promotes NGF expression in human OA synovial tissue, indicating a TGF-β dependent NGF regulation of downstream nerve remodeling in OA [70]. In addition, CGRP and SP, major pro-inflammatory neuropeptides, are found regulated by TGF-β in vitro [71].

Sensory nerves are regulated by mechanical stimuli

Sensory nerves are highly responsive to mechanical cues such as tensile stress and matrix stiffness changes. Stretching within an optimal range promotes neurite extension, as shown in animal models and nerve repair studies [70]. Likewise, substrate stiffness modulates outgrowth: soft matrices enhance neurite branching and network formation, while rigid matrices inhibit neuronal extension [72]. These findings suggest that OA-induced changes in cartilage and bone mechanics may drive sensory neuroplasticity and increase pain signaling.

Mechanosensitive ion channels play critical roles in sensory nerve responses to mechanical stimuli. Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 4 (TRPV4), expressed in dorsal root ganglion (DRG) neurons, mediates nociceptive responses to mechanical stress and inflammation [73]. Activation of TRPV4 promotes CGRP release, while TRPV4 silencing reduces sensory neurons sensitization in vitro [74]. Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 2 (PIEZO2), another mechanosensitive ion channel expressed in sensory nerves, regulates mechanical pain sensitivity [75]. In OA animal models, PIEZO2 expression in intra-articular nerves contributes to joint pain, and PIEZO2 deletion alleviates mechanical hypersensitivity [22]. Thus, altered cartilage and bone mechanics in OA activate mechanosensitive pathways in sensory nerves, driving pain signaling.

In OA, alterations in cartilage stiffness and subchondral bone architecture may restructure the local mechanical microenvironment that sensory nerves depend on, including TGF-β produced and released by cartilage. Sensory nerves are highly responsive to mechanical cues such as tensile strain and matrix stiffness; therefore these changes can modulate nerve activity. Mechanosensitive ion channels like TRPV4 and PIEZO2 mediate this response, linking joint tissue mechanics to nociceptive signaling and pain perception in OA.

Current models studying cartilage – sensory nerve crosstalk in OA

In vivo OA pain models

In vivo OA pain models are typically based on chemical induction or surgical destabilization. These models remain the cornerstone of preclinical osteoarthritis research due to their reproducibility, whole-joint pathology, and translational relevance to OA. These models simulate key features of human disease progression, including cartilage degradation, synovitis, subchondral bone remodeling, and behavioral manifestations of pain [76]. Validated behavioral assays such as weight-bearing asymmetry, gait analysis, mechanical allodynia thresholds, and spontaneous activity changes have enabled researchers to quantify nociceptive responses and evaluate therapeutic effects over time. These assessments capture integrated outputs of joint pathology, peripheral nerve sensitization, and central modulation.

However, in vivo models also have critical limitations, particularly when used to study specific and isolated pain mechanisms, like neuro activity. Pain readouts in animals are indirect, influenced by species, sex, and behavioral variability. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of joint pathology affecting cartilage, bone, synovium, and ligaments simultaneously makes it challenging to isolate cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk as a discrete driver of pain. Additionally, standard in vivo OA models do not permit precise control over microenvironmental variables such as mechanical loading, timing of cytokine exposure, or axonal–chondrocyte interactions. These constraints hinder mechanistic dissection of specific molecular targets and can obscure on-target effects during drug screening.

Thus, while in vivo models remain indispensable for capturing whole-joint biology and systemic influences on pain, they will be best complemented by targeted in vitro and ex vivo systems that enable controlled, reductionist exploration of cartilage–nerve interactions, particularly for early-stage target validation and mechanistic screening.

Models focusing on cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk

To address the limitations of conventional OA models in studying discrete pain mechanisms, several targeted in vitro and ex vivo systems have been developed to investigate cartilage–sensory nerve interactions directly. These models enable mechanistic dissection of bidirectional signaling between chondrocytes and nociceptors under controlled conditions.

A notable example is the organ-on-chip system by Kahraman et al. [77] cocultured mouse DRG neurons with human OA-like cartilage, demonstrating sensory axon innervation in response to macrophage-derived factors. Although promising, this model lacked mechanical loading inputs critical for mimicking the OA environment.

Similar in vitro platforms examining nerve interactions with bone [78] highlight the broader potential of sensory nerve–tissue models. These systems allow quantitative assessment of nerve sprouting, density, and pain-related marker expression using advanced imaging and electrophysiology, offering mechanistic insights into OA pain pathways and potential therapeutic strategies.

Predictive in vitro and ex vivo model platforms focusing on cartilage – neuro crosstalk in OA pain research

To advance the discovery and validation of mechanism-based OA pain therapeutics, there is an urgent need for predictive, mechanistically informative in vitro and ex vivo platforms that can accurately recapitulate cartilage–sensory neuron crosstalk under OA-relevant conditions. Current in vitro or ex vivo models of OA often address overall cartilage-subchondral pathology [79] but rarely reflect this cartilage–sensory neuron bidirectional interaction, limiting their utility for dissecting target biology or predicting human efficacy.

With iterative development and validation, such mechanism-anchored platforms could transform OA pain research from empiricism to predictive, precise management.

However, several translational challenges remain. First, current in vitro systems often lack the full immune and vascular context of the OA joint. Second, nociceptive signaling in vitro is inferred through proxy molecular and electrophysiological markers. These measurements could not capture the subjective, central integration of pain that defines clinical outcomes. Third, most platforms do not yet recapitulate the time-resolved, progressive nature of OA, where pain mechanisms shift over time from inflammatory to neurogenic to mechanical.

Therefore, while these platforms provide unprecedented mechanistic resolution, their outputs must be carefully interpreted and ideally validated against clinical human pain data (e.g., CGRP/SP expression in joint tissues, sensory innervation in arthroplasty samples).

Discussion: translational significance and future directions

Understanding OA pain mechanisms: beyond joint pathological progression

OA pain cannot be fully explained by structural joint degeneration alone. Emerging evidence highlights that OA pain is a complex, multifactorial process involving inflammatory, neurogenic, and mechanical components. Rather than being purely inflammatory or purely neuropathic, OA pain arises from a dynamic interplay between degraded cartilage-derived factors, sensitization and remodeling of sensory nerves, and altered mechanical loading within the joint microenvironment. The integrity of cartilage is maintained by anti-neural factors such as Semaphorin 3A (Sema3A) [81],which inhibit neurite ingrowth and maintain an aneural environment. However, under OA conditions, this barrier is compromised. Degraded cartilage becomes a source of neuroinflammatory signals, releasing NGF, pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β, TNF-α), and inflammatory mediators such as PGE2 and NO. These molecules promote axonal sprouting and neuronal hyperexcitability. Sensory nerves, in turn, release neuropeptides such as SP and CGRP, exacerbating joint inflammation and matrix degradation (Figure 2). In parallel, the mechanical environment of the joint is profoundly altered. Cartilage softening and subchondral bone remodeling change the way mechanical loads are distributed, activating mechanosensitive ion channels such as TRPV4 and PIEZO2 expressed on sensory neurons. Their activation promotes CGRP release and nociceptive firing, linking mechanical stress to pain signaling.

Together, these inflammatory, neural, and mechanical signals reflect a breakdown of the normal joint homeostasis. In healthy joints, the absence of significant innervation, stable extracellular matrix composition, and balanced biomechanical forces preserve function and quiescence. In OA, this equilibrium collapses. Sensory nerves infiltrate previously aneural compartments, neuropeptides sustain inflammation, and abnormal mechanical cues exacerbate nociceptive sensitivity, so that all contributing to persistent pain and joint degeneration (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

Neuroinflammatory and mechanosensitive biomarkers supporting OA pain phenotyping and stratification

Increasing recognition of bidirectional crosstalk between cartilage and sensory nerves in OA highlights the need for biomarkers that reflect underlying pain mechanisms rather than structural severity alone. Conventional inflammatory markers molecules such as interleukin-17 (IL-17), C-reactive protein (CRP) [82] and radiographic measures such as BML measurements show limited ability to explain inter-individual variability in pain, underscoring the importance of identifying mechanism-linked readouts that capture inflammatory, neural, and mechanical contributors to OA pain. Biomarkers associated with cartilage–sensory nerve interactions therefore offer an opportunity to support biologically informed pain phenotyping and patient stratification.

Neural axis biomarkers

Neurogenic mediators represent a core biomarker class reflecting sensory nerve activation and sensitization within the osteoarthritic joint. Several neuropeptides implicated in cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk are detectable in synovial fluid and/or circulation and show associations with pain-related outcomes.

NGF [8] has emerged as a central neural biomarker in OA, reflecting enhanced nociceptor sensitization and axonal remodeling. Elevated NGF levels have been reported in synovial fluid and joint tissues, with positive associations to pain intensity. Other sensory neuropeptides, including SP [21], CGRP [42], and NPY [23], are similarly increased in OA joint compartments and correlate with painful phenotypes, while VIP levels showed reversely related to OA pain and pathological progression [4]. These mediators integrate nociceptive signaling with inflammatory amplification, making them attractive indicators of neural-dominant pain mechanisms.

Beyond molecular measurements, functional assessments and quantitative sensory testing, including pressure pain thresholds and measures of central sensitization, may further support identification of neural sensitization endotypes, particularly when combined with neuropeptide profiling.

Inflammatory axis biomarkers

Inflammatory mediators released from cartilage and synovial tissues form a second biomarker axis closely linked to OA pain and disease activity. Pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are consistently elevated in synovial fluid and serum of OA patients and correlate with synovitis severity and pain scores [83].

Importantly, inflammatory and neural biomarker axes are mechanistically intertwined. Inflammatory cytokines stimulate NGF production in chondrocytes and synovial fibroblasts [9], thereby coupling inflammatory burden to neural sensitization. Lipid mediators such as PGE2 and reactive nitrogen species derived from NO pathways further amplify nociceptive signaling and inflammatory feedback loops [38]. Together, these markers reflect an inflammation-driven pain endotype characterized by synovitis, heightened cytokine activity, and secondary neural sensitization.

Mechanical and mechanotransduction-associated biomarkers

A third biomarker axis reflects altered joint biomechanics and mechanotransduction, which contribute to load-dependent pain phenotypes. Structural imaging features, particularly bone marrow lesions (BMLs) detected by magnetic resonance imaging, are robustly associated with pain severity and mechanical symptoms in OA [84]. At the molecular level, mechanosensitive ion channels such as PIEZO2 have been implicated in translating abnormal mechanical forces into nociceptive signals. Upregulation of PIEZO2 expression in joint tissues and sensory neurons has been reported in OA models [22], and associations between PIEZO2 expression in infrapatellar fat pad–synovial membrane complexes and pain severity support its relevance to load-driven pain mechanisms [21]. Similarly, increased TRPV4 activity has been reported in experimental OA models [23], and suppression of TRPV4 improved pain behavior in a rat MIA models. Although still emerging, mechanotransduction-linked biomarkers provide a molecular complement to imaging-based measures of mechanical stress.

Integrating biomarker axes for OA pain stratification

Individually, neural, inflammatory, and mechanical biomarkers offer partial insight into OA pain mechanisms. Their integration, however, enables definition of biologically distinct pain endotypes that transcend radiographic staging. For example, concurrent elevation of IL-1β and NGF may reflect an inflammation-driven neural sensitization phenotype, potentially responsive to a combined anti-inflammatory and anti-NGF therapy. Upregulation of PIEZO2 and NGF in cartilage/synovium may indicate a mechanical overloading phenotype, suggesting benefit from joint-load reduction and NGF blockade. Validation of integrated biomarker signatures in longitudinal cohorts and interventional studies will be essential to translate pain endotyping into clinical trial design and, ultimately, personalized OA pain management.

Translational targeting of cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk: trial-readiness and safety considerations

The mechanistic and biomarker evidence reviewed above identifies cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk as a central driver of OA pain and highlights several molecular nodes with translational potential. However, successful clinical translation requires not only target engagement but also careful alignment between pain endotypes, therapeutic strategy, and safety monitoring. Below, we outline trial-readiness considerations for key therapeutic classes, with explicit attention to patient selection, endpoints, and risk boundaries.

Anti-NGF and neuropeptide-targeted strategies: benefit–risk stratification

NGF represents the most clinically advanced approach targeting neural sensitization in OA. Monoclonal antibodies against NGF have demonstrated robust analgesic efficacy in patients with moderate-to-severe OA pain, supporting the central role of NGF-dependent nociceptor sensitization [85]. However, the emergence of rapidly progressive osteoarthritis (RPOA) in a subset of treated patients underscores the need for biologically informed patient selection and safety frameworks [54].

Patient enrichment and inclusion features: Anti-NGF or neuropeptide-directed interventions are most likely to benefit patients with evidence of a neural-dominant or inflammation-driven neural sensitization endotype, characterized by elevated neurogenic mediators (e.g., NGF, CGRP, SP), heightened pain sensitivity, or features of peripheral sensitization. Inclusion of patients with preserved joint stability and without end-stage structural collapse may improve interpretability of analgesic benefit while reducing structural risk.

Exclusion and caution criteria: Patients with pronounced mechanical instability, severe malalignment, or advanced structural deterioration may be at increased risk of adverse structural outcomes when pain perception is suppressed. In such settings, analgesia without adequate mechanical protection could exacerbate joint loading and accelerate damage. These features should therefore be considered exclusion criteria or trigger enhanced monitoring in trials targeting neural pathways.

Composite efficacy endpoints and safety monitoring: To balance analgesic benefit with disease safety, composite endpoints integrating pain (e.g., Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC) pain score), function (e.g., WOMAC function score), and structure (radiographic joint space width and/or MRI-based structural features) are recommended. Structural monitoring at predefined intervals is essential to detect early signals of RPOA. Within an endotype-guided framework, these safeguards allow evaluation of benefit while minimizing harm.

CGRP- and SP-pathway interventions, including receptor antagonists, represent related but less clinically mature approaches. CGRP receptor antagonists, already approved for migraine (e.g., Rimegepant), show promise in preclinical OA models by reducing nociceptive signaling and cartilage degradation [44], Similarly, NK1R antagonists targeting SP-mediated inflammation and neurogenic sensitization are under investigation [6], although translational data remain limited. Given their roles in nociceptive amplification and local inflammation, these strategies may be particularly suitable for patients with neurogenic pain-dominant phenotypes and limited structural disease. However, their broader safety and disease-modifying implications remain to be defined, warranting cautious, biomarker-guided clinical exploration.

Mechanotransduction targets (TRPV4, PIEZO2): early proof-of-concept strategies

Mechanosensitive ion channels such as TRPV4 and PIEZO2 provide a mechanistic bridge between abnormal joint loading and nociceptor activation, making them attractive targets for mechanical overload-driven OA pain endotypes. These channels are upregulated in OA-affected joint tissues and DRG neurons, and their blockade has been shown to reduce mechanical allodynia and CGRP release in animal models [86]. However, translation of these targets into clinical testing requires careful attention to patient selection and outcome sensitivity.

Patient selection and entry criteria: Patients exhibiting load-dependent pain patterns, malalignment, or imaging features indicative of focal mechanical stress—particularly BMLs—may represent an enriched population for mechanotransduction-targeted interventions. Molecular evidence of mechanotransduction pathway activation, where available (e.g., PIEZO2 expression in synovial tissue, TRPV4 activity in synovial tissue), may further support inclusion.

Early proof-of-concept trial design: Initial clinical studies targeting TRPV4 or PIEZO2 should prioritize mechanistic signal detection over broad efficacy. Short-term, randomized, placebo-controlled proof-of-concept designs may use pain on provocation, pressure pain thresholds, or dynamic pain measures as primary outcomes, with secondary assessment of function and imaging-based stress markers. Follow-up should include both early mechanistic time points and later structural assessments to capture potential unintended effects on joint integrity.

Interpretation and safety considerations: Because mechanosensitive channels contribute to physiological load detection, excessive blockades could theoretically impair protective mechanosensation. Careful dose selection, monitoring of activity levels, and structural imaging are therefore essential to define therapeutic windows that reduce pathological pain without compromising joint safety.

Toward endotype-guided OA pain trials

Across therapeutic classes, a consistent theme emerges: efficacy and safety are likely to depend on matching interventions to underlying pain mechanisms rather than radiographic severity alone. Integration of biomarker-defined neural, inflammatory, and mechanical axes provides a framework for endotype-guided trial design, enabling enrichment of responsive populations while reducing heterogeneity and risk.

Ultimately, translation of cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk into clinical benefit will require iterative validation of biomarker signatures, careful endpoint selection, and explicit safety boundaries. By embedding mechanistic understanding into trial design, future studies may achieve more reliable analgesic outcomes and clearer interpretation of disease-modifying potential in OA.

Limitations

This review focuses on bidirectional cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk as a core mechanism underlying osteoarthritis pain; however, OA is a complex joint disease involving multiple interacting tissues, including subchondral bone, synovium, vasculature, and different nerve populations. These components can modulate neural sensitization and pain through inflammatory, vascular, and biomechanical pathways. While emerging studies [24] highlight neurovascular and multi-tissue contributions to OA pain, a comprehensive integration of all joint compartments was beyond the scope of this review. Future work should aim to embed cartilage–sensory nerve interactions within broader, multi-tissue models of the osteoarthritic joint microenvironment.

In addition, the mechanistic concepts discussed here are primarily derived from studies of load-bearing joints, particularly knee osteoarthritis, where pain-related outcomes and biomarker associations are best characterised. Other joints, such as the temporomandibular joint [87] or small hand joints [88], differ in biomechanical loading, innervation patterns, and disease presentation, which may influence the relevance of cartilage–sensory nerve crosstalk. Comparative studies across joint types, together with longitudinal human investigations and predictive in vitro and ex vivo model platforms, will be beneficial to determine joint-specific features and to advance mechanism-based pain stratification in osteoarthritis.
