Image segmentation of tunnel water leakage defects in complex environments using an improved Unet model
Authors: Pinglun Wang, Guigang Shi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Computer vision technology provides an intelligent means for detecting tunnel water leakage areas. However, the accuracy of defect feature extraction and segmentation is limited by factors such as insufficient lighting and environmental interference inside tunnels. To address the problem, this paper proposes a tunnel water leakage area segmentation network model called Customized Side Guided-Unet (CSG-Unet), using Unet as the baseline model. The main contributions are: (1) To improve the accuracy of water leakage area extraction, a customized side guided term is introduced to direct the net’s attention to the changes in light and shade within the image. A parallel attention network module is designed to extract internal information from the guided term. Subsequently, a strengthened channel attention module aggregates the guided term and the original information to achieve accurate segmentation of water leakage areas; (2) To address the scarcity of tunnel water leakage area datasets, a basic dataset is constructed by collecting data from open-source datasets and manually gathered data in tunnels. On this basis, perspective transformation is used to change the camera viewpoint, gaussian noise is randomly added to the images in the dataset to simulate images taken in dimly lit scenes, thereby expanding the dataset and enhancing the network’s generalization. The CSG-Unet network was trained using the constructed training set, achieving a mean Intersection over Union (mi IoU) of 85.54%, a mean Dice coefficient (mi Dice) of 85.26%, and a mean Pixel Accuracy (mi PA) of 90.85%. Compared to its baseline network, U-Net (tiny), these metrics show an improvement of over 3.2% in each indicator. Finally, a visual comparison between the improved network and the baseline network further confirms that the proposed model can effectively adapt to the segmentation of water leakage areas in complex environments.

Introduction

Tunnel water leakage is a pervasive and severe issue in tunnel engineering, occurring in construction and operation phases. During construction, the complexity of geological conditions and inadequacies in construction techniques, such as improper waterproof layer installation or incomplete coverage by shotcrete, may lead to groundwater infiltration. In the operation phase, factors like earthquakes, vehicle vibrations, and environmental changes may gradually cause the waterproofing system to fail, resulting in water leakage1. This leakage not only weakens the load-bearing capacity and safety performance of the lining, threatening the stability of the tunnel structure but also can make the road surface slippery, increasing the risk of accidents. Additionally, it may affect the operation of electrical equipment, posing a threat to personnel safety. Therefore, timely and accurate detection and management of water leakage issues are crucial.

To ensure the stability and operational safety of tunnel structures, regular inspections of tunnel linings are essential. Traditionally, manual visual inspection has been the primary method, but it has numerous limitations, including uncertainty, subjectivity, danger, and inefficiency. In recent years, with the development of artificial intelligence technology, machine vision-based methods for tunnel lining defect detection have become a research focus2–5. The authors collect images of tunnel linings and use image recognition technology to identify surface defects. After the image acquisition phase, the key challenge lies in achieving high-precision and high-efficiency defect recognition. Breakthroughs in computer vision, particularly the application of convolutional neural networks (CNNs), have brought new research opportunities for tunnel lining defect recognition. CNN-assisted defect recognition technology, with its high recognition rate and level of automation, has become an effective means to improve the efficiency and accuracy of tunnel lining inspections6–8.

Compared to the time-consuming and labor-intensive manual visual inspections, computer vision-based detection methods have garnered significant attention and application in the detection of apparent defects in tunnel structures. Computer vision offers a new opportunity to overcome the limitations of manual inspections and achieve automated detection of water leakage areas. In addressing the issues of low detection accuracy, poor anti-interference capability, and slow detection speed in traditional tunnel lining defect detection methods, Zhou et al.9 proposed a deep learning-based model, YOLOv4-ED, for tunnel lining defect detection. This model employs EfficientNet and Depthwise Separable Convolution (DSC) technologies. In YOLOv4-ED, EfficientNet is used as the backbone to enhance the recognition accuracy of defect targets that are difficult to distinguish in complex tunnel backgrounds and varying lighting conditions. Additionally, the introduction of DSC blocks reduces the model’s storage space and improves detection efficiency. Experimental results demonstrate that YOLOv4-ED outperforms comparative models in both detection accuracy and efficiency. To quickly, accurately, and automatically identify various defects (such as leaks, cracks, and spalling) in a large amount of metro tunnel lining image data, Feng et al.10 proposed a two-step recognition framework based on deep learning. First, they introduced an automatic real-time image classification model, TLCNet, to separate and save defective images from a large set of tunnel lining images. In the second step, they developed an accurate target detection model, TDDNet, to identify major defects on the lining surface. Extensive comparative, ablation, and generalization experiments demonstrated the superiority of TLCNet and TDDNet. Based on this, an intelligent recognition platform combining TLCNet and TDDNet was established, achieving multi-defect recognition in practical metro tunnel lining inspection tasks.

With the development and application of models for instance segmentation and semantic segmentation, algorithms such as Mask R-CNN and Unet have provided efficient methods for the segmentation and extraction of water leakage contours11,12. Li et al.13 established a road tunnel water leakage identification model based on deep learning technology, along with a water leakage area extraction model based on image processing technology, ultimately achieving accurate detection of tunnel road surface water leakage. Man et al.14 used ResNet combined with transfer learning for tunnel leakage and crack detection, achieving accuracies of 96.30% and 91.29% with ResNet50 and ResNet34, respectively, both higher than the detection accuracies of the models without transfer learning. Zhao et al.15 proposed a model consisting of a multi-level transformer encoder and an adaptive multi-task decoder, achieving final segmentation results for tunnel leakage based on the Unet network. The experimental results showed that the model’s Dice coefficient and mIoU reached 95.1% and 90.4%, respectively, indicating higher accuracy and generalization ability compared to other related models. Qiu et al.16 introduced a lightweight automatic water leakage segmentation method based on hybrid supervised deep learning technology. This method combines weakly supervised learning and fully supervised learning. Compared to other end-to-end semantic segmentation networks, the hybrid supervised learning method can more completely segment water leakage areas in complex environments. The proposed hybrid supervised deep learning method achieved the highest mIoU of 82.8% on the experimental dataset, significantly outperforming other end-to-end deep learning methods. Wang et al.17 also utilized deep learning for shape segmentation of tunnel leakage areas. They used Mask R-CNN as the baseline model and introduced a mask cascading strategy to improve the quality of positive samples. Additionally, they replaced the backbone network with RegNetX and incorporated MDConv to enhance the model’s feature extraction capability in edge receptive fields. Their resulting model achieved significant improvements over existing models.

In recent years, Transformer-based networks have also demonstrated outstanding performance in image segmentation18–21. Qin et al.7 proposed a tunnel defect segmentation model based on Vision Transformer, introducing new adapters and decoding heads. This model achieved more than 10% higher average accuracy compared to convolutional neural networks under the same training configuration. Huang et al.22integrated technologies such as Swin Transformer and CARAFE upsampling to propose BridgeNet, which surpassed the original Mask R-CNN by over 33% in mAP and over 26% in mIoU metrics. Lei et al.23 enhanced Mask R-CNN by incorporating Transformer attention mechanisms, resulting in the Mask R-CNN-TD network, which outperformed the Mask R-CNN series in terms of accuracy. Although Transformer-based networks have achieved significant success, they generally come with higher computational costs.

Existing methods for water leakage image segmentation face challenges in real-time recognition, handling voids, and addressing discontinuous edges of leakage areas. Tan et al.24 introduced the PP-LiteSeg-Attn model, based on the real-time semantic segmentation model PP-LiteSeg-B, and combined it with multi-layer CBAM and CoT attention mechanisms. The PP-LiteSeg-Attn model was trained and validated using public datasets, achieving IoU and F1 scores of 88.18% and 93.72%, respectively, outperforming similar models on these metrics. Extensive experiments demonstrated that the PP-LiteSeg-Attn model achieved a segmentation speed of 112.28 FPS, meeting real-time requirements and effectively resolving issues such as voids, discontinuities, and blurred edges in leakage areas. The PP-LiteSeg-Attn model is better suited for complex tunnel environments and provides a technical reference for real-time diagnosis of tunnel defects. Wang et al.25 proposed a water leakage segmentation model with an encoder-decoder structure. The encoder employs multi-branch convolutional attention for feature fusion, while the decoder uses a lightweight design with only multi-layer perceptrons. Test results indicated that the model robustly detected leakage in highly noisy backgrounds, achieving an IoU of 90.75% under performance-computation trade-offs. This model can effectively replace existing visual inspection techniques, providing an almost automated detection platform for shield tunnels.

Despite significant progress in the field of tunnel water leakage detection, several challenges remain. First, most current research focuses on identifying leakage areas, with relatively little attention given to the precise segmentation and extraction of these areas’ specific shapes. Accurate segmentation of defect areas is a crucial prerequisite for damage assessment and quantification. However, existing technologies still have shortcomings in segmentation accuracy and precision, particularly in complex environments such as tunnels with insufficient lighting. There is substantial room for improvement in detection accuracy under these challenging conditions.

Therefore, to improve the accuracy and reliability of tunnel water leakage detection, there is an urgent need to develop new intelligent algorithms and models. These algorithms and models should be capable of accurately segmenting and extracting the morphological features of water leakage areas on tunnel surfaces, even against dark light background or in complex environmental conditions. This capability would provide a solid foundation for the assessment and quantification of defects and significantly advance the automation and intelligence of tunnel maintenance and safety management. Based on the above background, this paper proposes a Customized Side Guided-Unet (CSG-Unet) model. By incorporating a side-guidance term, the network is prompted to pay attention to brightness variation information in the environment. Additionally, parallel attention modules and enhanced channel attention modules are used to strengthen the network’s learning of the side-guidance term, improving its ability to capture brightness changes and enhancing semantic segmentation performance of leakage areas in complex environments such as low-light conditions.

Methodology

Customized side guided-Unet model

Unet is a convolutional neural network originally designed for medical image segmentation, but it has also demonstrated strong performance in segmenting surface defects in civil engineering structures26–28. For this reason, this study selects Unet as the baseline semantic segmentation model. Unet features a symmetric encoder-decoder architecture, which is the core of its design. In the encoder stage, the network progressively extracts and refines image features through consecutive convolution and pooling operations, simultaneously reducing the spatial resolution of feature maps. This process effectively captures deep semantic information from the images. Subsequently, in the decoder stage, the network uses upsampling and convolution operations to gradually restore the resolution of feature maps, recovering image details. Crucially, Unet employs skip connections between the encoder and decoder, which directly transmit high-resolution feature maps in low-level network to high-level network. This design allows the effective fusion of feature information from different levels, ensuring that detail information in low-level network is retained throughout the segmentation process, thereby enhancing segmentation accuracy. Unet can accurately identify and segment water leakage areas based on the input image features, achieving pixel-level segmentation. Due to its automation capabilities, Unet significantly enhances the efficiency of water leakage area detection.

Assuming a batch size N during network training, where N images are processed simultaneously, each color image has 3 channels, and dimensions H and W for height and width respectively, the initial input image’s multidimensional feature dimension is N×3×H×W. As training progresses, the convolutional modules continually extract information from the initial feature tensor to produce a final output tensor with dimensions N×2×H×W. Here, the 2 channels represent the segmentation categories “background” and “water leakage area”. Ge et al.29 proposed that in the process of deep learning networks acquiring visual knowledge, attributes such as color, shape, and texture of images contribute significantly to recognition tasks. The network acquires color knowledge by extracting channel dimension information from the input tensor and shape and texture knowledge by extracting height and width dimension information. Related research30 indicates that networks lack clarity on which information to prioritize during learning, leading to a lack of focus. To address this, this study leverages Unet as a baseline model and introduces a Customized Side Guided Item to guide the network’s attention towards brightness information in images. Additionally, more channel attention mechanisms are incorporated within the network to enhance its focus on color information across channel dimensions, resulting in the development of Customized Side Guided-Unet (CSG-Unet).

The Customized side guided item is designed to prioritize brightness information, structured as a combination of several single-channel images. Initially, the original images are transformed into grayscale. Subsequently, the Otsu algorithm is applied to convert the grayscale images into binary images. Considering that the threshold determined by the Otsu algorithm may not fully reflect the brightness information of the image, a series of threshold gradients are formed based on this threshold, generating multiple binary images. Finally, each generated binary image is concatenated along the channel dimension to form the ultimate customized side guided item. In this study, during the generation of multiple binary images, thresholds are incremented in steps of 10 around the Otsu threshold, generating four thresholds above and four below the Otsu threshold, resulting in a total of nine additional binary images alongside the one generated by the Otsu algorithm. Therefore, the final Customized Side Guided Item comprises a grayscale image and nine binary images, totaling 10 channels, as illustrated in Fig. 1. The impact of the number of threshold images on network performance is extensively discussed in Sect. Ablative experiment of this paper.

Process of guiding item formation.

Figure 2 illustrates the network architecture of the designed CSG-Unet. The network takes standard RGB images as input, denoted as tensor X. Initially, the customized side guided item, Xcs, is generated using the aforementioned guiding item generation algorithm. This tensor has dimensions of 10×H×W. To align this tensor with the deep network tensors in height and width dimensions, it undergoes 16-fold max pooling, resulting in tensor X16 with dimensions of 10×H/16×W/16. Given its sparse 10 channels compared to the deep network, its influence is limited. To further extract information, the Depthwise Separable Convolution (DS Conv) module is applied, yielding a 32-channel tensor, Xcsi. This tensor is processed through the designed Parallel Attention module and directly concatenated with internal network tensors in the channel dimension.

The mentioned Depthwise Separable Convolution module refers to DS Conv combined with Batch Normalization (BN) and activation function ReLU. The introduced side information serves as auxiliary data and thus has not been overly channelized to prevent excessive impact on the main network. Its introduction not only guides the network to focus on brightness variations but also facilitates skip connections, allowing more shallow-level information to merge into the deep network, maintaining a comprehensive understanding of input data. The Parallel Attention module, which combines channel attention and spatial attention, is detailed in subsequent sections. After the fusion of the side input tensor with deep network tensors, a feature tensor of size 544×H/16×W/16 is formed. To further extract channel dimension information, the Enhanced Channel Attention (Channel Attention X) module is designed for feature extraction. Post-processing with the Channel Attention X module is followed by another DS Conv module, transforming its dimensions to 512×H/16×W/16 within the deep network. Additionally, standard convolutions (Conv) in the baseline Unet network are replaced with DS Conv to reduce network parameters, speeding up calculations.

The designed parallel attention mechanism module is shown in Fig. 4, where the input vector Xcsi passes through both the spatial attention module and the channel attention module. For the spatial attention module (Space Attention), the input vector first undergoes channel average pooling to obtain a feature map with a single channel and the same height and width as the input. Subsequently, it passes through a 3 × 3 DS Conv module and sigmoid activation to obtain spatial attention weights Ws. For the channel attention module (Channel Attention), the input vector first undergoes global average pooling to obtain a feature map with the same number of channels as the input and dimensions of 1 × 1. Then, it sequentially passes through 1 × 1 Conv, independent 1 × 1 Conv, and sigmoid activation to obtain channel attention weights Wc. Ws and Wc are then element-wise multiplied with the input vector Xcsi to obtain feature vectors Xcsi_s and Xcsi_c, respectively. These two vectors are concatenated along the channel dimension in the fusion module and undergo channel dimension transformation through independent 1 × 1 convolutions, resulting in an output vector with the same dimensions as Xcsi. Finally, this output is element-wise added to Xcsi to obtain the output vector Xcsi_out after the parallel attention module.

Fig. 4Principle of Parallel Attention Module.

Principle of Parallel Attention Module.

The enhanced Channel Attention X module is illustrated in Fig. 5, designed to intricately learn information between channels in detail. This channel attention mechanism is more complex compared to the channel attention module in the parallel attention module, incorporating not only the global average pooling branch but also the global max pooling branch. The input feature maps undergo channel attention through both max pooling and average pooling branches. The results are element-wise added together and then element-wise multiplied with the original feature maps to obtain the feature maps after the Channel Attention X module.

Loss function

Binary Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss function is a commonly used loss function in semantic segmentation. This loss function evaluates the similarity between the predicted distribution and the true distribution to determine the accuracy of the predicted distribution. Where N represents the total number of pixels. y(i) denotes the true label at a pixel i, which takes values 0 or 1. In this context, 0 represents the background class and 1 represents the seepage area. When the number of classes is relatively balanced, binary cross-entropy (BCE) loss performs well. However, in the presence of class imbalance, using BCE loss alone can lead to poorer training outcomes. Data collection and dataset pre-processing

The leakage water images used in this study are sourced from open datasets31and from actual field captures inside a tunnel, as shown in Fig. 6. The open-source dataset images have a resolution of 1438 × 1078 pixel. For field capture, the camera used was the Hikvision MV-CA013-21UC, which features a CMOS sensor with a pixel size of 4.8 μm × 4.8 μm and an image resolution of 1280 × 1024 px, with a maximum frame rate of up to 210 fps. Both the open-source dataset and the field-captured images include both horizontal and vertical images. The collected data consists of images taken under low light conditions in tunnels, as illustrated by the first three images in Fig. 6. These images include local highlights caused by flashlight illumination and some partial occlusions; for example, the first and third images in Fig. 6 show line interference, while the second image contains pipeline occlusion. The images in the open-source dataset were taken under well-lit conditions and depict tunnel water leakage. The constructed dataset thoroughly includes images of leakage areas under low light conditions in tunnels, which is beneficial for the model to learn image features in such environments. In the development process of deep learning models, having a rich training dataset is crucial. To enhance the model’s generalization capability to unseen data, features automatically extracted rely on a large and diverse set of samples. Therefore, this research adopted data augmentation strategies to expand the original dataset by incorporating random noise and perspective transformations31.

Random noise is added using a Gaussian noise model. In tunnel environments, due to poor lighting conditions, images often have a low signal-to-noise ratio, increasing the likelihood of noise generation during the photosensitive element and signal quantization processes. Additionally, environmental factors inside tunnels such as vibrations, vehicle emissions, dust, etc., can also affect the performance of imaging devices. Therefore, to enable the model to adapt to tunnels darker than those in experimental conditions, we added random noise to the collected image data to enhance the network’s generalization ability. Gaussian noise is a common type of noise characterized by its intensity (pixel values) following a normal distribution across the image. The image after noise addition can be represented as:4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I_{{gauss\_noisy}}^{{}}(x,y)=I(x,y)+N(x,y),N(x,y)\sim \mathcal{N}(\mu ,\sigma _{{}}^{2})$$\end{document}

Where Igauss_noisy(x,y) represents the image after applying Gaussian noise. I(x,y) represents the original image. N(x,y) represents Gaussian noise, which follows a normal distribution with mean µ and standard deviation σ.

Specifically, this transformation involves converting the image through perspective projection to make it appear as if it were captured from another viewpoint. The image after perspective transformation typically appears as a parallelogram. To make the transformed image suitable for network input, we applied the maximum inscribed rectangle method to crop the image, thus obtaining augmented data.

The dataset transformation process is illustrated in Fig. 7. We assumed the camera rotates along the x, y, and z axes by certain angles, performing perspective transformation and cropping on the images to obtain perspective-transformed images. In practical operations, to avoid significant distortion after image rotation, the rotation angle is kept below 0.5°. Additionally, to introduce more randomness in camera angles and help the network better adapt to angle variations, the rotation angles around the x, y, and z axes are set as random values between 0.1° and 0.5°. This process quadrupled the original image dataset. Subsequently, we added random Gaussian noise with a mean µ = 0 and standard deviation σ = 150 to both the original and perspective-transformed images, further expanding the dataset to eight times its original size. Finally, the training set contains 4480 images, while the validation set comprises 1200 images.

Results analysis of different models

Performance comparison of different models

This section provides a comprehensive comparison of the CSG-Unet model with its baseline models, U-Net (large) and U-Net (tiny). Additionally, the classic PSPNet is included as a comparative network. While Transformer-based models have indeed achieved notable success across a range of applications, they often require significantly more computational resources compared to other architectures. This increased demand poses a critical consideration when assessing their suitability for specific tasks, especially in resource-constrained environments. Consequently, classical segmentation models like U-Net were selected for this study due to their favorable balance between computational resource requirements, efficiency, and accuracy.

Adam optimizer was used in this study to train the deep learning models. When setting the optimizer’s hyper-parameters, aside from the learning rate, all other parameters were kept at their default values: exponential decay rates β=(0.9, 0.999), epsilon for numerical stability ε = 10−8, and weight decay set to 0. For all models discussed in this section, the learning rate was uniformly set to 5 × 10−4. The training period for the models was 150 epochs, with each batch containing 4 samples. The training process was conducted on a computer equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3060 GPU and an Intel Core i9-9900 K CPU @ 3.6 GHz. PyTorch was chosen as the deep learning framework. During training, network parameters were initialized using default initialization method of PyTorch.

During the training process, to prevent image distortion during scaling when inputting into the network, original images were padded with a black background to form square images, which were then resized to 512 × 512 pixels and fed into the network for training. The loss curves for Unet(tiny), Unet(large), PSPNet, and CSG-Unet networks are shown in Fig. 8. From the loss curves, it is observed that Unet(tiny) exhibits significant fluctuations in the validation set during training but shows an overall converging trend, with a slight increase in validation loss towards the end. Unet(large) converges faster with relatively stable validation loss, but shows a tendency of increasing loss with more training epochs, indicating overfitting. Due to its higher complexity and more parameters, PSPNet shows a noticeable increase in validation loss during training, indicating pronounced overfitting. In contrast, CSG-Unet demonstrates fast convergence with lower validation loss compared to the other three networks. Furthermore, CSG-Unet exhibits almost no signs of overfitting. These loss trends align with the design intent of CSG-Unet: fewer network parameters and the inclusion of illumination transformation guidance enhance the model’s ability to effectively learn the essence of segmenting seepage areas-variations in brightness within images-thus mitigating overfitting.Fig. 8 Loss change curve of model training and validation.

This paper uses model evaluation metrics to perform quantitative analysis on the results of leakage water segmentation, calculating various evaluation metrics using the test set samples. The evaluation metrics in this experiment are divided into accuracy metrics and efficiency metrics. Accuracy metrics include: pixel accuracy (PA), Intersection over Union (IoU), and Dice coefficient (Dice). Among these, IoU refers to the ratio of the intersection to the union of predicted and true values of pixels in the leakage area; PA represents the proportion of correctly identified pixels in the leakage area to the total number of pixels; Dice measures the overall similarity between the predicted sample and the true value. The formulas for PA, IoU, and Dice metrics are as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PA=\frac{{P_{{11}}^{{}}}}{{P_{{00}}^{{}}+P_{{01}}^{{}}+P_{{10}}^{{}}+P_{{11}}^{{}}}}$$\end{document}8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$IoU=\frac{{P_{{11}}^{{}}}}{{P_{{11}}^{{}}+P_{{10}}^{{}}+P_{{01}}^{{}}}}$$\end{document}9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Dice=\frac{{2 \times P_{{11}}^{{}}}}{{(P_{{11}}^{{}}+P_{{10}}^{{}})+(P_{{11}}^{{}}+P_{{01}}^{{}})}}$$\end{document}

Here, Pab represents the number of pixels where the actual class is b and the predicted class is a. In this context, 0 represents the background and 1 represents the leakage area. For instance, P10 denotes the number of pixels that are actually background but predicted as leakage area, while P11 denotes the number of pixels that are actually leakage area and predicted as leakage area. PA, IoU, and Dice coefficients are computed separately for each image in the validation set. Finally, the average values across all images are calculated to obtain mi PA, mi IoU, and mi Dice as the accuracy evaluation metrics adopted in this paper. In addition, this paper adopts image processing speed (FPS) as efficiency metrics.

The results of the proposed CSG-Unet model compared to Unet (tiny), Unet (large), and PSPNet models on the validation set are shown in Table 1. It can be observed that the proposed CSG-Unet significantly reduces computation time compared to the baseline model Unet (large), while significantly improving computational accuracy. Specifically, mi PA, mi IoU, and mi Dice show improvements of 1.48%, 1.67%, and 1.25%, respectively. In terms of inference time, efficiency is increased by 11.51 frames per second (f/s). Compared to Unet (tiny), mi PA, mi IoU, and mi Dice improve by 3.21%, 3.82%, and 3.62%, respectively. However, the inference speed decreases by 7.98 f/s. Given the improvement in accuracy, this sacrifice in inference time is acceptable for on-site detection in engineering scenarios. This section explores the differences in training models using different strategies. Table 2 summarizes the training results of the models under different loss functions. The last row represents the loss function configuration adopted in this paper. It can be observed that when using only BCE Loss, the model focuses on improving mi IoU performance, whereas using only Dice Loss emphasizes improving mi Dice performance. By combining both loss functions, they complement each other, improving both mi IoU and mi Dice performances. Compared to using only BCE Loss, mi IoU improved by 0.81%; compared to using only Dice Loss, mi Dice improved by 0.62%.

Table 3 illustrates the influence of guidance channel numbers on network performance. It shows that as the number of guidance channels increases, the network performance gradually improves. Significant improvements in network performance are observed when increasing the channels from 2 to 6: mi PA increases by 1.72%, mi IoU by 1.59%, and mi Dice by 1.41%. This indicates that the network captures crucial information about leakage areas, such as the brightness change from the added guidance channels. When using only 2 channels for input, only the Otsu threshold image is utilized, which cannot accurately reflect the true shape of the leakage area. Increasing the number of channels expands the range of threshold variations, enabling the generated guidance channels to encompass binary images that more accurately reflect the true shape of the leakage area. Furthermore, as the number of channels increases to 6, the network performance approaches saturation. Increasing from 6 to 10 channels results in minimal improvements: mi PA increases by only 0.17%, mi IoU by 0.22%, and mi Dice by 0.16%.

Compared to using only channel attention module, adopting the Parallel Attention mechanism resulted in an increase of 0.77% in mi PA, 0.61% in mi IoU, and 0.63% in mi Dice. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of the Parallel Attention module used in this study.

Conclusions

To accurately extract and segment leakage areas in tunnels under complex conditions such as dark light, this study developed a CSG-Unet semantic segmentation network based on a light-shadow attention mechanism. In dataset construction, real-shot datasets of tunnel leakage areas and open-source datasets were collected. The perspective transformation was applied to expand the dataset by varying image perspectives. Additionally, random noise was added to simulate images captured in dimly lit and complex scenes, enhancing the network’s generalization capability.

In network design, the study used Unet as the baseline and introduced light-shadow transformation as an auxiliary task to guide the network to focus on brightness changes in images. Attention modules were implemented to aggregate guidance features and deep network features, thereby improving the segmentation accuracy of leakage areas. The proposed model achieved metrics with mi PA at 90.85%, mi IoU at 85.54%, and mi Dice at 85.26%, all surpassing baseline and other models. It effectively segments leakage areas in tunnels under complex backgrounds such as obstruction or glare. Furthermore, compared to the baseline model, the proposed model showed faster convergence during training and was less prone to overfitting.

This study focused solely on the accurate segmentation of leakage areas without calculating the relative area of the leakage. Moreover, in balancing accuracy and computational efficiency, this paper did not compare the proposed model with many of the current advanced models. This will be a focus of future research. Additionally, developing more accurate, robust, and lightweight segmentation models to achieve the recognition, localization, and quantification of leakage areas in tunnels is also a valuable direction for further investigation.
