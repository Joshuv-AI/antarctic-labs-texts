Therapeutic Inertia in an Insured Population with Type 2 Diabetes in the United States: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Kristina S. Boye, Maureen J. Lage, Kendra A. Terrell, Vivian T. Thieu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThis study examines the characteristics of adults with type 2 diabetes (T2D) who were not initially treated with an antihyperglycemic agent (AHA).MethodsThe analyses used Optum de-identified Market Clarity data from January 2013 through September 2023. The US study included nonpregnant adults with T2D who were continuously insured from 1 year prior through 5 years post diagnosis and did not fill a prescription for an AHA in the year after their initial T2D diagnosis. Differences between those treated in years 2–5 with an AHA (delayed treatment) and those untreated with an AHA for 5 years post diagnosis (untreated) were examined descriptively and using multivariable analyses.ResultsOut of 186,259 adults with T2D, 56.7% (N = 105,533) did not fill a prescription for an AHA in the year after diagnosis and were included in the study. Of these 105,533 adults (mean age 59.6 years; 51.4% female), 75.0% were untreated for the entire 5 years post diagnosis. In the delayed treatment group, metformin was the most common first-line therapy (72.9%), and 83.0% of those who initiated monotherapy never received additional classes of AHAs. Compared to the delayed treatment group, the untreated group had significantly higher rates of incident cardiovascular outcomes and all-cause direct total costs ($118,191 vs $108,687; P < 0.05).ConclusionOver 50% of adults diagnosed with T2D were untreated with an AHA in the first year post diagnosis, and most of those who went untreated the first year remained untreated after 5 years. Among the delayed treatment patients, the majority did not use additional AHA classes besides their index therapy in the post-period. These findings suggest that therapeutic inertia affects a significant percentage of individuals with T2D. Given the untreated group’s significantly worse cardiovascular outcomes and higher medical costs, these findings highlight a potential unmet need in the years immediately following T2D diagnosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-025-01793-w.

Introduction

Diagnosis of type 2 diabetes (T2D) may occur years after its onset [1, 2]. By the time of diagnosis, up to 50% of patients may have clinical or preclinical manifestations of micro or macrovascular complications [3] and up to 50% of β-cell function may have been lost [4]. This undiagnosed period represents a missed opportunity for early interventions, including drug treatment, diet and exercise changes, weight control, and management of cardiovascular risk factors [5]. Once diagnosed, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) Standards of Care recommend that “Pharmacotherapy should be started at the time type 2 diabetes is diagnosed, without delay, unless there are contraindications” [6].

Retrospective research has found that treatment earlier in the course of the disease is associated with improved glycemic control and more weight loss [7]. Furthermore, computer simulation research has shown that early detection and treatment of T2D reduces cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [8]. Even mild diabetic complications have been shown to adversely affect health-related quality of life [9], creating a clinical imperative for early interventions that prevent the onset of complications. Despite the known benefits of early treatment, therapeutic inertia, defined as “the failure to initiate or intensify therapy in a timely manner according to evidence-based guidelines” [10] has been found to be an important factor in explaining why people with diabetes do not achieve treatment targets [11].

Given this evidence in favor of early therapeutic intervention, the present research used real-world data to quantify and characterize US adults with T2D who were not treated with pharmacotherapy for at least 1 year post diagnosis. In addition, the study examined subsequent treatment with antihyperglycemic agents (AHAs) in years 2–5 post diagnosis and compared outcomes of patients who were treated with an AHA in years 2–5 (delayed treatment) to the outcomes of untreated patients. Results from this research may be used to increase understanding of the characteristics of individuals who may benefit from earlier therapeutic interventions with pharmacologic agents and to quantify costs associated with therapeutic inertia.

Methods

The data used for this study came from Optum’s de-identified Market Clarity Data (Market Clarity). Captured from US insurance claims and clinical records, the data covers 90 million patients and provides patient characteristics, diagnoses, and procedures, as well as charges for medical services, supplies, and encounters. While generally representative of the USA, the data does slightly overrepresent individuals from the Midwest and those age 65 or older. The complete time horizon for the data used in this study extended from January 1, 2013 through September 30, 2023. All data were compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA). Given the use of retrospective, de-identified data, no institutional review board approval was required. Permission was received from Optum to utilize the data.

Consistent with previous research which has utilized Optum data, individuals were required to have received two or more ICD-9 or ICD-10 diagnoses of T2D and no more than one diagnosis of type 1 diabetes, with the date of the first diagnosis with T2D identified as the diagnosis date [12]. Furthermore, the criteria are consistent with research that has shown that similar algorithms result in adequate sensitivity (≥ 70%), high specificity (≥ 97.5%), and robustness (kappa ≥ 80%) [13, 14]. Individuals were excluded from the study if they were diagnosed with pregnancy or gestational diabetes at any time from 1 year before the diagnosis date (the pre-period) through 5 years after diagnosis (the post-period). Patients were also excluded if they were younger than age 18 as of the diagnosis date, or if they were uninsured at any time from the start of the pre-period through the end of the post-period. Use of AHAs in the first 5 years post diagnosis and timing of such use was examined using this cohort of 186,259 individuals. Finally, individuals were also excluded if they were treated with an AHA in the first year pre or post diagnosis date. These inclusion and exclusion criteria resulted in a study population of 105,533 insured adults. Medication use, costs, incident cardiovascular events, and incident non-cardiovascular complications and comorbidities were examined in this cohort. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates how each of the study inclusion and exclusion criteria affected the sample size.

This research characterized adults identified with T2D (N = 186,259). In addition, the study examined the AHA usage patterns of the subgroup (N = 105,533) who were not treated with an AHA in the first year post diagnosis. Among this subgroup, outcomes were compared between individuals who received treatment with an AHA in years 2–5 post diagnosis (the delayed treatment group) (N = 26,429) and of individuals who remained untreated throughout the study period (the untreated cohort) (N = 79,104). The outcomes of interest included incident cardiovascular and non-cardiovascular complications and comorbidities as well as direct medical costs. Consistent with prior research which used Market Clarity data [15], costs were defined as total standardized gross payments, including those made by insurance and those made by patients in the form of copayments, deductibles, and coinsurance. Costs were subcategorized as acute care, drug, and outpatient costs. All costs were converted to 2023 dollars using the medical component of the US Consumer Price Index (CPI) [16]. All comorbidities and complications were identified by ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes.

Patient characteristics and AHA use were examined descriptively. Differences in patient characteristics between the delayed treatment and untreated cohorts were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and t tests for continuous variables. Costs were examined using generalized linear models with a gamma distribution and log link, while incident comorbidities and complications were examined using Poisson regressions. All multivariable analyses controlled for patient demographics and pre-period general health. Patient demographic characteristics included age, sex, race, ethnicity, region of residence, and insurance type. In addition, pre-period smoking status and alcohol use status were identified using both ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes and observational data which surveyed alcohol use, smoking, and use of smoking cessation programs, and pre-period substance abuse status was identified using diagnostic codes and included as covariates. Patient general health and comorbidities, also examined in the pre-period, were measured using the Diabetes Complications Severity Index (DCSI) and the adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [17, 18]. The DCSI is scored on a scale of 0–13, with higher scores assigned to patients with a larger number and/or more severe levels of the following diagnoses: retinopathy, neuropathy, nephropathy, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, and metabolic disease [17]. Meanwhile, the CCI creates a composite morbidity score that reflects mortality risk based upon the presence of any of 19 comorbidities, with individual comorbidities given a score between 1 and 6 [18]. In this study, myocardial infarction, peripheral vascular disease, and diabetes with or without complications were omitted from the calculation of the adjusted CCI either because they applied to all individuals (e.g., diabetes) or because they were included in the DCSI. Finally, all multivariable analyses controlled for duration of AHA use. All analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (Cary, NC). A p value < 0.05 was considered, a priori, to be statistically significant.

Results

AHA Use

Figure 1 illustrates that of the 186,259 adults with T2D who met all other study criteria, 105,533 (56.7%) were not treated with an AHA in the first year post diagnosis. Figure 1 also shows that of the 105,533 included in the final study cohort, 79,104 (75.0%) did not fill any prescriptions for an AHA at any time during the 5-year post-period, while 26,429 (25.0%) were treated with an AHA at some time during years 2–5. Among the 25.0% who were treated with an AHA, initiation on an AHA declined in years 2–5 of the post-period. Figure 2 illustrates which drugs were used to treat the 26,429 individuals diagnosed with T2D who filled at least one prescription for an AHA in years 2–5 post diagnosis (the delayed treatment group). Results revealed that most individuals were initially treated with metformin (MET; 72.9%). After MET, the most commonly prescribed first-line therapies were sulfonylureas (SFU) (5.2%), glucagon-like peptide 1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RA) (4.2%), and insulin (3.8%). Sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) were most commonly prescribed as monotherapy (1.9%) or in combination with dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors (DPP4i) (1.0%). In addition to illustrating first-line therapy, the figure also shows subsequent changes in therapy. An examination of these mutually exclusive categories revealed that the most common change in initial treatment was to restart therapy (28.6%), where a restart was defined by a gap of more than 45 days between the time a prescription ran out and the time it was refilled [19]. Slightly less common was discontinuation of initial therapy, defined as no additional use of initial therapy, which affected 26.3% of individuals eventually treated with an AHA, while 24.6% continued their initial therapy with no gap of more than 45 days between prescriptions. Note that for 79.5% of patients (continuers, discontinuers, and restarters), the first AHA treatment was the only AHA therapy used in years 2–5 of the post-period. By definition, Fig. 3 omits individuals who had no first-line therapy, who continued their first-line therapy throughout the post-period, or who discontinued all AHA use after first-line therapy. Outcomes

Figure 4 compares incident rates of cardiovascular outcomes between the delayed treatment and untreated subgroups based upon the results of multivariable analyses. Results revealed that the untreated cohort, compared to the delayed treatment cohort, had significantly higher incident rates per 1000 patient years of cardiovascular disease (119.0 vs 102.4), stroke (44.3 vs 31.6), coronary heart disease (57.3 vs 48.0), heart failure (27.6 vs 21.6), myocardial infarction (11.7 vs 10.2), and unstable angina (10.3 vs 8.5) (all P < 0.05). Table 2 shows differences in incident rates per 1000 patient years of non-cardiovascular complications and comorbidities. Results illustrate that untreated patients had significantly higher rates of Alzheimer’s disease, chronic kidney disease, foot ulcer, malignancy, osteoarthritis, osteoarthritis of the knee, renal failure, rheumatoid arthritis, and severe vision loss. Figure 5 illustrates the results of the multivariable analyses which examined all-cause costs over the 5-year post-period. Results revealed that all-cause total costs were significantly higher for the untreated T2D cohort compared to the delayed treatment group ($118,191 vs $108,687; P < 0.05). Consistent with the finding of significantly higher all-cause total costs for the untreated patients, their outpatient ($71,359 vs $63,179) and acute care costs ($35,142 vs $29,941) were also significantly higher for the untreated group (both P < 0.05). In contrast, and as expected, all-cause drug costs were significantly lower for patients untreated with any AHA in the 5-year post-period ($22,432 vs $25,309; P < 0.05). The higher all-cause total costs of the untreated group (Fig. 5) are consistent with the finding that the untreated group, compared to the delayed treatment group, used significantly more acute care. Specifically, the untreated group was significantly more likely to be hospitalized (32.2% vs 30.3%) and had significantly longer post-period days of hospitalization (6.9 vs 4.9) and significantly more emergency room (ER) visits (3.2 vs 3.0) (all P < 0.05).Fig. 55-year all-cause medical costs: treated with AHA in years 2–5 post T2D diagnosis vs untreated

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that, at least initially, a relatively large proportion of adults with T2D are not being treated with an AHA. Specifically, the results indicated that 56.7% of our eligible population were not treated with an AHA in the first year post diagnosis and, among those not treated in the first year, 75.0% were not treated within the first 5 years. Even among the 25.0% of adults who were eventually treated with an AHA in years 2–5 of the post-period, 79.5% did not change AHA class in the 5 years post diagnosis, either discontinuing all AHA therapy (26.3%), continuing initial AHA therapy (24.6%), or restarting the same therapy after a gap of more than 45 days (28.6%). The findings of a high rate of initial lack of treatment as well as the large percentage of individuals who did not change initial treatment class in years 2–5 post diagnosis appear to be inconsistent with the ADA Standards of Care, which suggest that most individuals should initiate therapy with an AHA at the time of diagnosis of T2D and that treatment should be reassessed and potentially modified regularly (every 3–6 months) to avoid therapeutic inertia [6].

Previous research has also found that there may be a lag between diagnosis and initial AHA therapy. For example, one prior study indicated that a large proportion of people are not prescribed an AHA in the first year post diagnosis with T2D and that those who go untreated in the first year are unlikely to be started on an AHA in the second year [20]. A more recent study reported a 51% rate of patients untreated in the first year post diagnosis with T2D [21], a finding similar to the 56.7% 1-year nontreatment rate found in the present study. Among those treated with an AHA, over 90% initiated monotherapy, and 83.0% of these patients did not use additional AHA classes besides their index therapy in the post-period. Furthermore, 87.0% of the 91.3% who started monotherapy were never intensified to combination therapy over the entire post-period. While the ADA recommends “a person-centered, shared decision making approach” for choosing AHA medications [6], previous clinical trial research has found that early combination therapy is beneficial in the treatment of T2D. For example, a meta-analysis of 15 clinical trials that examined differences in outcomes between initiation on MET or MET in combination with an additional AHA found that combination therapy was associated with significant reductions in HbA1c and a greater percentage of individuals achieving HbA1c target of < 7% [22].

The results of the present study also indicated that nontreatment with an AHA in the 5-year post-period, compared to delayed treatment with an AHA in years 2–5 post diagnosis, was associated with significantly higher incident rates of cardiovascular outcomes and chronic kidney disease/diabetic kidney disease. These results are consistent with research showing that some AHA therapies are associated with a lowered risk of cardiovascular and renal complications [23]. Conversely, it should be noted that there were higher rates of macular edema and depression in the delayed treatment cohort. Given that those in the delayed treatment group were predominantly male and treated with metformin, the higher rate of depression found among this group was somewhat unexpected. Previous research has indicated that men and people treated with metformin have lower rates of depression [24, 25]. However, the higher rate of depression in the delayed treatment cohort is consistent with the group’s higher rates of diagnosed obesity and obstructive sleep apnea. Previous research has shown that obesity is correlated with mood disorders, including depression [26, 27]. Obstructive sleep disturbances have also been shown to be associated with obesity and depression [28, 29].

As well as having higher rates of incident cardiovascular outcomes, the untreated cohort also was associated with significantly higher all-cause total direct medical costs and significantly higher all-cause acute care costs and outpatient costs. Note that all-cause total costs were higher for the untreated group even though this group spent $0 on AHAs in the post-period. Given our sample of 79,104 untreated patients and the average estimated post-period increase in all-cause total costs of $9504 per person for this group, the total additional costs associated with no AHA therapy over the 5-year post-period were estimated to be $751,804,416 (average of $150,360,883 annually). The higher costs and higher rates of cardiovascular events are consistent with research which has shown that complications are a major driver of T2D medical costs [30]. Specifically, prior research has found that, among adults with T2D, the costliest complications included cardiovascular disease and renal failure [30], both of which were at increased incidence in our untreated group. Additional research has found that the presence of cardiovascular disease among patients with T2D is associated with a 16% increase in per-patient per-month healthcare costs [31].

While this study examined the impact of therapeutic inertia, it was unable to determine the causes. Therapeutic inertia may be driven by providers, patients, and systemic barriers [32]. For providers, time constraints, lack of knowledge, multiple and/or frequently updated guidelines, and concern regarding side effects have been shown to drive therapeutic inertia [33]. At the patient level, male sex, older age, multiple comorbidities, and a lower life expectancy have all be found to be associated with therapeutic inertia [34]. Systemically, therapeutic inertia may be due to costs and copayments as well as differences in healthcare settings and support provided [35]. Furthermore, the analyses focused on untreated patients and comparing delayed treatment to non-treatment. As such, the study did not compare outcomes between untreated patients and those treated with an AHA in the first year post diagnosis. Additional analyses comparing treated and untreated patients with T2D are warranted to investigate the impact of early treatment.

The study findings should be interpreted within the context of its limitations. One limitation was that the retrospective data focused on well-insured individuals. This limitation restricts the generalizability of the results. For example, uninsured individuals have found to be, in general, younger than the insured population and disproportionately Hispanic and African American [36]. Furthermore, research has shown that inadequate insurance coverage is one of the most mentioned systemic barriers to optimal treatment of T2D [37]. Another limitation was the reliance on diagnostic codes versus formal tests to identify our cohort of adults with T2D as well as patient comorbidities and complications. In addition, claims data was unable to capture drug use outside of the insurance system. It is possible that individuals filled prescription for AHAs such as metformin without going through their insurance company. However, a comparison of AHA prescriptions filled with associated claims and patient self-reports of medications prescribed revealed that more than 99.7% of oral AHA use was captured in the claims data. This result suggests that the claims data captured the vast majority of patient prescriptions. To the extent that such information is missing, the current study would overestimate therapeutic inertia.

Finally, it should be noted that while the study utilized multivariable analyses to examine the association between AHA treatment and patient outcomes, results focused on statistical rather than clinical significance and associations rather than causations. Consistent with the retrospective and observational nature of the data, the study was unable to capture all potential confounders. For example, analyses were unable to control for patient socioeconomic characteristics or index HbA1c. The lack of consistently collected HbA1c data precluded examining how increased levels of HbA1c were associated with therapeutic inertia or how treatment decisions impacted glycemic control. Furthermore, the data used in this study did not allow identification of cases when a physician determined that patient HbA1c did not warrant initiation on an AHA or the patient had a contraindication for AHA initiation. The strengths of this study included a large dataset that covered an extended time horizon and consideration of a wide range of outcomes.

Conclusions

The results of this study indicate that therapeutic inertia potentially impacts a large proportion of newly diagnosed adults with T2D. In this study, 56.7% of nonpregnant, continuously insured patients were untreated with an AHA within the first year post diagnosis. Moreover, 75.0% of those untreated in the first year remained untreated for 5 years post diagnosis. Patients untreated with an AHA, compared to those treated in years 2–5 post diagnosis, had significantly higher rates of cardiovascular outcomes and significantly higher all-cause, direct, total medical costs, as well as higher costs attributed to acute care and outpatient care. These findings suggest a potential unmet need in the treatment of T2D.
