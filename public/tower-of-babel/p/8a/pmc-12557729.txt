Lost in translation? Comprehension of care for English-speaking vs. Spanish-speaking trauma patients
Authors: Souma Kundu, Steven Arias, Rolando Arreola, Todd W Costantini, Jay J Doucet, Allison E Berndtson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCulturally responsive care is a core recommendation to reduce health disparities. Language barriers contribute to misunderstandings, dissatisfaction, and worse outcomes. This is exacerbated in trauma when communication is constrained by time, complexity, and competing priorities. We hypothesized that Spanish-speaking trauma patients (SSP) would have less accurate comprehension of care (CC) and discharge instructions compared with English-speaking patients (ENG).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed discharges from a level 1 trauma center (October 2021 to March 2022) who were aged ≥18, primarily ENG or SSP, discharge Glasgow Coma Scale score ≥14, and without memory loss. Patients were surveyed <48 hours from discharge to assess CC. Patients self-rated CC on a Likert scale and answered open-ended questions on CC and discharge instructions. Charts were reviewed to assess and rate concordance of CC with actual care and ENG and SSP cohorts compared.ResultsWe included 46 patients (21 SSP, 25 ENG). Mean age was 48.3 years SSP, 43.3 years ENG; 47% SSP were female versus 32% ENG (p=0.28). 56% SSP had ≥high school diploma versus 72% ENG (p=0.34). Self-rated CC was similar, with both groups rating high understanding of their care and follow-up. SSP were less likely to accurately report diagnoses, home medications, and follow-up appointments than ENG. When corrected for health literacy, differences remained between groups in accuracy of diagnoses despite having high confidence in their CC.ConclusionsThough both SSP and ENG self-rated their comprehension of their care highly, there were significant differences between groups’ accuracy. Increased use of certified medical interpreters throughout hospitalization may improve language disparities in patient comprehension.Level of evidenceTherapeutic/care management, level IV.

Introduction

Culturally responsive and appropriate care has been identified as a core area needing improvement by the Institute of Medicine.1 For the approximately 25 million people in the USA with limited English proficiency (LEP), this core need includes access to linguistically appropriate care.2 Existing literature on LEP status has shown linkages between language barriers and greater patient dissatisfaction, decreased patient understanding of medical situations, and ultimately poorer clinical outcomes.39 Yet, despite these findings, and federal policy mandates requiring availability of professional certified medical interpretation free of cost to patients, use of professional interpretation often remains underutilized.10 Instead, communication with LEP patients frequently occurs through informal means using untrained individuals such as hospital staff or friends and family members of the patient.1014

In emergency and acute care settings, communication with LEP patients may be further hindered by both the need for rapid assessment and treatment of complex injuries, as well as patient conditions including altered mental status, traumatic brain injury, or emotional distress. Prior studies on the effect of LEP status in these acute clinical settings have focused largely on mortality, end-of-life discussions, and patient satisfaction scores, but the impact of a language barrier on patient comprehension of a trauma-related hospitalization after they have been discharged is not well understood.1517

The objectives of this study are to identify potential differences and patterns in English-proficient and LEP patients’ understanding of their hospital course and postdischarge instructions. We hypothesized that Spanish-speaking patients (SSP) with LEP would have less accurate comprehension of their care and postdischarge instructions compared with English-proficient patients and seek to better understand these potential differences.

Methods

We performed a prospective study involving patients admitted to the trauma service of an American College of Surgeons-verified level 1 academic trauma center from October 2021 to March 2022. Inclusion criteria were English-speaking patients (ENG) or SSP, at least 18 years of age at the time of admission, appropriate mental status defined as a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 14 or 15 at the time of discharge, and discharge to home or with family. Patients were excluded if they were less than 18 years of age, pregnant, incarcerated at discharge, in custody of immigration enforcement, had a history of dementia or cognitive impairment, discharged to home with 24/7 supervision or home care, discharged to a rehabilitation or skilled nursing facility, or were admitted with injuries or examination findings consistent with short-term memory loss. Patients were sequentially approached to participate by phone within the first 48 hours after discharge.

Survey

All patients were contacted in their preferred language, based on hospital documentation during their admission. Patients were read a standardized memo about the research study and told that they could withdraw consent to participate or discontinue participation at any time. Patients who consented to participate were asked to verbally respond to survey questions over the phone. Researchers did not review patient charts prior to administering the survey, other than to confirm study eligibility, to prevent prompting toward correct answers. The survey instrument and recruitment strategy were adapted from a study of patients discharged from the University of Michigan.18 The survey form was divided into two sections. The first section (Part I) asked patients to rate how well they understand their diagnosis, treatment, discharge instructions, and return precautions using a 5-point Likert scale for each item. The second section (Part II) asked patients to describe the five aspects of care in their own words: what diagnoses they were given, what tests and imaging were performed during their hospital stay, what treatment they were given, their post-discharge instructions, and their return precautions. A copy of the full survey can be found in online supplemental appendix A.

Prior to survey administration, we tested the English version of the survey with five test patients to ensure standardized interpretation of the questions. After this validation step, we translated the English version of the survey to Spanish with assistance from author SA, a native Spanish speaker and certified medical interpreter, and back-translated the document to English with assistance of author RA, also a native Spanish speaker. The entire survey, including introduction and consenting process, was translated to ensure standardized administration. The survey was administered by author SK for ENG and by authors SA and RA for SSP.

Data coding

Part I of the survey, regarding patients’ perception of their own understanding of their care (‘self-rating’), was analyzed for statistically significant differences between the two cohorts. Part II of the survey was analyzed by comparing patients’ free responses with their electronic medical record After Visit Summary documents and discharge summary notes to evaluate the comprehensiveness and accuracy of their responses (‘accuracy’). For each of the five categories, patient responses were scored for the level of concordance with their medical record as: complete concordance, near concordance, partial concordance, minimal concordance, or no concordance. We followed the detailed guide for scoring provided in the original study that this survey was adapted from.18

To ensure standardized application of the scoring guidelines, five practice surveys were analyzed by both authors SK and AEB, with an inter-rater reliability kappa coefficient of 0.744. Discordant categories were reviewed and additional detail was developed for the scoring guideline for clarity. Another five surveys were compared by the two researchers, achieving an inter-rater reliability kappa coefficient of 0.915. The remainder of all surveys, both ENG and SSP, was analyzed independently by both authors SK and AEB.

A detailed copy of the scoring guideline and description of the analysis process can be found in online supplemental appendix B.

To account for potential differences in health literacy, participants were also administered a validated health literacy questionnaire. The screening tool consisted of three questions graded across 5-point Likert scales: (1) How would you rate your ability to read? (2) How confident are you filling out medical forms by yourself? (3) How often do you have someone help you read hospital materials? These three single-item questions have previously been validated for adults aged 18–70 in English-speaking and Spanish-speaking populations against the Short Test of Functional Health Literacy in Adults.18 19 Participants were also asked to report their highest level of education.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using StatCalc for Epi Info V.7.2. Categorical outcomes for the self-reported comprehension (Part I of the study) were converted to bivariate form for analysis, grouping ‘strongly agree and agree’ and ‘neutral, disagree, and strongly disagree’. Categorical outcomes for accuracy of comprehension were converted to bivariate form for analysis by grouping concordance scores of ‘complete and near concordance’ and scores of ‘partial, minimal, and no concordance’. In each cohort, categories of self-rated understanding were compared with the accuracy score via the Mann-Whitney U test. The Mantel-Haenszel common OR test was used to assess whether education level or health literacy as a bivariate variable (minimum completion of general education diploma (GED) or equivalent vs. less than GED, and average health literacy rating of less than good vs. good and very good) was associated with the accuracy of comprehension. In all analyses, p values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

We approached 167 patients and included 46 patients in the final analysis; 25 ENG and 21 SSP (figure 1). Of the 112 SSP reviewed, 21 were included, and nine (8.0%) directly declined to participate. The remaining 82 patients were unable to be reached due to contact phone numbers belonging to relatives or friends, disconnected numbers, or five unsuccessful attempts to connect. Of the 55 ENG reviewed, 25 were included, and nine (16.4%) declined to participate. The remaining 21 patients were deemed unable to be reached after five unsuccessful attempts to connect. Overall participation rate in the SSP cohort was 18.8% versus % in the ENG cohort.

Demographics, education, and health literacy

The mean age of participants was 48±16.8 years in the SSP cohort versus 43±14.4 in the ENG cohort (p=0.280) (table 1), while 47.6% of SSP were female versus 32.0% ENG (p=0.280). There were no significant differences in injury severity or length of stay. Regarding education obtained, 52.9% of SSP had a GED or equivalent versus 66.6% among the ENG, though this difference was not significant (p=0.341). There was, however, a difference in the three single-item health literacy questions between the ENG and SSP cohorts (table 2). Patients in the SSP cohort were more likely to report a limited ability to read (p=0.033), a higher likelihood of asking someone to help them read hospital materials (p=0.015), and a lower confidence filling out medical forms by themselves (p=0.006).

Accuracy of comprehension of care

We found differences in the accuracy of comprehension between the ENG and SSP cohorts for the understanding of diagnoses, home medications, and follow-up appointments (p=0.013, p=0.044, and p=0.043, respectively) when patient reports were compared with discharge documentation (table 4). Accuracy scores for the other categories (inpatient testing, inpatient treatment, non-medication home care instructions, and return precautions) were not significantly different. After adjusting for health literacy level in both cohorts, the difference in accuracy of understanding diagnoses remained significant between ENG and SSP groups (p=0.045), but the understanding of home medications and follow-up appointments lost statistical significance (p=0.153 and p=0.164, respectively), suggesting a degree of effect modification by health literacy.

Follow-up

The percent of follow-up appointments successfully scheduled and attended was higher in the ENG cohort than the SSP cohort (84.0% vs. 62.5%), though this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.180). There remained no statistically significant difference in the follow-up rate when adjusted for comprehension level, health literacy level, or education level in both cohorts (p=0.077, p=0.34, and p=0.15, respectively).

Discussion

In this survey of injured patients discharged from a level 1 trauma center, we identified that while both English-speaking and SSP felt they understood their care well, ENG were much more accurate when recounting their diagnoses, home medications, and plans for follow-up, as we hypothesized. SSP were more likely to overestimate their understanding of their care, which may have negative implications such as patient-physician misunderstandings and poor postdischarge follow-up.

The existing literature on patient self-rated comprehension shows mixed findings. One previous study of English-proficient trauma patients found that more than 90% of patients self-reported being satisfied with their level of understanding, though the measured accuracy of their recall was significantly lower.20 Another study of English-proficient patients discharged from the emergency department found the majority of their participants failed to perceive their own comprehension deficits.18 We identified similar concerns, with low accuracy for ENG particularly regarding follow-up appointments and return precautions. A third study, evaluating the impact of language on patient self-reported comprehension difficulties, found LEP patients to be significantly more likely to report problems understanding a medical situation, or report a bad reaction to medication due to problems understanding instructions, compared with English-proficient patients. This persisted even after adjusting for potential confounders such as sex, education, income, insurance status and other variables.9 Due to the nature of our small sample size, we were not able to stratify our data by the aforementioned characteristics. Our findings did not reproduce this disparity between SSP and ENG; in fact, our LEP SSP self-rated their comprehension higher in every category, though the differences were not statistically significant.

Previous studies on patient accuracy of comprehension vary widely in how well patients perform—ranging from 8% to 94% accurate recall.21 Our study was inspired by previous work by Engel and her team at the University of Michigan studying comprehension of care among English-proficient patients discharged from the emergency department.18 22 We adapted their survey to a trauma setting and added the Spanish-speaking arm to assess whether a patient’s language impacted comprehension of care. In alignment with the original study’s findings, that patient knowledge deficits were greatest in the categories of home care instructions and return instructions, knowledge deficits among our participants were most pronounced for return instructions for both cohorts of patients.

The second greatest knowledge deficit for both cohorts was of follow-up appointments, significantly more so among SSP than ENG. We also identified a real impact of this miscomprehension, with SSP having a lower rate of attendance to follow-up appointments compared with ENG, though it merits further research to describe the possible multiple factors responsible for this difference. For two SSP, retrospective chart review revealed that an appointment could not be scheduled due to the patient stating they live too far from possible appointment sites. Another SSP, who had multiple follow-up appointments scheduled with multiple specialties, missed an appointment due to confusion about which appointment to attend that day, and showing up at the wrong clinic. The potential consequences of these deficits, particularly among SSP, are well understood. Patients with poorer comprehension may be unable to adhere to postdischarge instructions and be lost to follow-up, leading to improper or inadequate treatment and preventable sequelae such as prolonged recovery time, avoidable complications, unplanned readmissions, or even death. Though we did not find studies that have established which categories of knowledge deficit pose the highest risk for poor outcomes, it is logical to consider poorly understood return precautions or postdischarge instructions as a significant risk.

Interestingly, whereas Engel’s study found patients were generally less likely to have a knowledge deficit in their comprehension of diagnoses, we found a significant knowledge deficit for only SSP comprehension of their diagnosis, even after adjusting for health literacy level; ENG had no such deficit with or without adjustment for health literacy. Notably, when we adjusted for health literacy level for SSP, we did lose the significance of difference in comprehension of home medications and follow-up appointments, suggesting a degree of effect modification. A possible explanation for this difference is that SSP may have a less comprehensive understanding of their injuries from admission onward due to underused certified medical interpretation throughout their hospital stay. On the other hand, the home medications and follow-up appointments are more clearly identifiable on the after-visit paperwork, and patients who reported confidence in their ability to read and understand hospital materials would be more easily able to find and follow these instructions. Additionally, while it is an institutional policy at the study site for bedside nursing to use a certified medical interpreter to review discharge instructions at the time of discharge, we do not have data on the compliance rate with this policy. Future research on whether consistent use of medical interpretation improves these findings may be warranted.

These findings may also be due, at least in part, to the differences in health literacy and education between our Spanish-speaking and English-speaking cohorts. Previous studies on accuracy of patient comprehension do not decisively establish a relationship between health literacy, education level, and accuracy; some have found it to correlate, whereas others have not.2325 While these differences in our study population may limit our ability to attribute differences in comprehension accuracy solely to primary language, it is important to note that lower health literacy and lower education are commonly co-occurring vulnerabilities for limited English-proficient patients. In addition, limited English-proficient SSP are also less likely to be insured, have lower incomes, and less likely to have a primary care provider compared with their English-proficient counterparts.26 Our study captures the reality of intersecting disadvantages that likely compound on each other to explain the differences in comprehension. Low health literacy is especially common in trauma patient populations, with a frequency as high as one in four patients.24 While providers may be unlikely to ask their patient their highest level of education or complete a health literacy screen during a clinical encounter, providers may consider patients’ English proficiency as an indicator of a potentially vulnerable patient, while exercising appropriate caution to avoid stereotyping LEP patients.

Previous literature comparing patients’ self-rating and accuracy of comprehension has shown that patients are not reliable at predicting how well they actually understood their care or instructions, a finding we confirmed here. Lin et al25 found that the correlation between self-rating and accuracy was impacted by both patient gender—males were more likely to overestimate their comprehension—and education level—patients with higher education levels had less accurate self-ratings. Similarly, our study demonstrated a relationship between patients’ primary language and their accuracy of self-rating—the SSP cohort’s self-ratings significantly overestimated their level of comprehension when compared with their accuracy. The implication of this finding is that SSP may not know that they do not know or may not be willing to admit what they do not know. Generally, lower accuracy raises concern for a patient’s ability to appropriately care for themselves by following discharge instructions and seeking appropriate follow-up. Unlike a patient who is aware they have not understood their instructions well or has questions, a patient who believes they understood well may be less likely to reach out to providers for clarification or help, missing an opportunity to fill in their gaps in knowledge. This finding, that Spanish-speaking LEP patients may overestimate their understanding, suggests that asking patients ‘did you understand the instructions?’ or ‘did that make sense?’ may not be a sufficient barometer of their comprehension. Instead, literature recommends usage of teach-back or closed-loop method, interactions that ‘test’ patients’ accuracy to improve comprehension.2729

There is also ample literature that demonstrates the added value of language-concordant care, or providers caring for patients in their native language, including: improved adherence to treatment, higher satisfaction ratings, and fewer unplanned post-discharge returns to the emergency department.6 30 31 While these studies did not directly study patient comprehension, it can be postulated from the improvement in patient outcomes that language-concordant providers are better assessing comprehension and addressing incomplete or inaccurate patient understanding. Patients also express higher levels of satisfaction with language-concordant providers, which may decrease the barrier to requesting help or further information.32 While over 42 million SSP live in the USA,2 only 6% of US physicians identify as Hispanic, and only 2% of the US physician workforce speaks Spanish.33 Increasing the number of providers able to provide language-concordant care to patients is another imperative to address the disparities our study has identified.

Our study is not without limitations. This study was conducted at a single center, so results may not be generalizable to all LEP patients, or to SSP with different demographics than ours. Our study also had a small sample size, mainly due to the difficulty recruiting SSP. It is noteworthy that of the 91 SSP we attempted to reach but did not enroll, only nine directly declined to participate. The remaining 82 could not be reached. Additionally, all the ENG were recruited within two phone call attempts, whereas many of the SSP required three or four attempts to complete the survey. This difficulty in communication has additional implications for adequate postdischarge follow-up for these patients. The size of the sample also limits our ability to stratify the data to address possible modifying effects or confounders. While the data are suggestive of relationships between language, health literacy, education level, and comprehension, further investigation with larger samples is necessary to define the impact of these patient factors and control for other effects. Additionally, we did not record demographics of the non-respondents to our survey—if these patients were significantly different from responders, our survey could have some intrinsic responder bias. Lastly, due to the very nature of survey data collection, patients who were very happy with their care or very unhappy with their care may have been more willing to participate in the study than those with less strongly formed opinions.

Conclusions

Inaccurate or incomplete comprehension of care is a challenge that must be addressed for all patients, not only those with LEP. However, as our study demonstrates, patients who speak a non-English primary language may be at greater risk of discharging with poor comprehension. This disparity can lead to inadequate patient follow-up, and as sequelae, poorer outcomes. While this study is a preliminary assessment of the relationship between language and comprehension, additional studies with larger sample sizes may be able to further assess the combined effects of education, health literacy, frequency of inpatient use of interpreters, and other factors on the degree of care comprehension. We aim to pursue institutional improvement efforts for SSP based on the results of our study, including better documentation of a working phone number and alternative contact numbers for patients prior to discharge, translating discharge materials into Spanish, and employing evidence-based teach-back methods in follow-up phone calls.
