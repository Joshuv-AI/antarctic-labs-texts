A novel zinc oxide/schwertmannite composite for efficient remediation of oxytetracycline contamination
Authors: Yingxuan Fan, Jian Zhang, Fenwu Liu, Wenlong Bi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Addressing oxytetracycline (OTC) contamination is essential for controlling agricultural area-source pollution. Developing green remediation materials that can efficiently remove pollutants in a short period holds ecological sig-nificance and research value. This study synthesised a novel zinc oxide/schwertmannite (ZnO/SCH) composite by integrating photocatalytic and adsorptive properties to efficiently remove OTC. Initially, the performance of the ZnO/SCH was evaluated in aqueous systems, which showed excellent OTC removal efficiency. 30 mg/L of OTC was removed by more than 90% under solar light and more than 70% under visible light after irradiation for 4 h at a ZnO/SCH addition of 0.2 g/L, primarily driven by hole (h+) and superoxide anion (·O2–) radicals. Remarkably, the composite maintained over 95% OTC removal across a wide pH range (4–10). Building on these promising results, the study further investigated the application of ZnO/SCH in OTC-contaminated calcareous soil. Environmental factors, including the degree of contamination, temperature, soil moisture content, soil pH, calcium carbonate (CaCO3) content, and organic matter (SOM) levels, significantly influenced OTC adsorption and fixation. Soil OTC fixation and removal was favoured by higher temperatures (30 ℃), lower OTC concentrations (30 mg/kg), and a suitable soil moisture content (18%). Alkaline conditions and high calcium carbonate content reduced electrostatic adsorption and cation exchange, while low organic matter content weakened soil adsorption of OTC. Notably, ZnO/SCH acted as a secondary adsorbent, capturing OTC released from soil under changing conditions and mitigating secondary pollution risks. This study provides a high-performance, cost-effective solution for the remediation of OTC-contaminated water and even soil, offering significant ecological and practical value.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-12591-6.

Introduction

With the rapid development of the livestock and poultry farming industry, excessive use of veterinary drugs, particularly tetracyclines (TCs), has become widespread1. Most tetracyclines not absorbed by livestock enter the environment in their unmetabolised form, contaminating water and soil2. Although TCs have been banned as growth promoters in the European Union, the United States, and China, they can still be detected in livestock and domestic wastewater3,4. When used for irrigation, untreated sewage poses a significant risk of OTC contamination in farmland soils5. In China, 55% of agricultural soils are calcareous, characterised by high pH, calcium carbonate content, and low organic matter levels6. Combined with the widespread application of organic fertilizers, these conditions heighten the risk of OTC contamination. Given the growing concerns for ecological health and food security, the remediation of OTC-polluted water and soil has become extremely urgent.

Numerous remediation techniques were used by researchers for OTC removal, including physical adsorption, Fenton oxidation, persulfate oxidation, electrocatalysis, and photocatalysis. Among them, adsorption was widely used for municipal and industrial wastewater treatment due to its low cost and easy operation. For example, nano-activated carbon7 Fe/N-doped biochar8 and lignin-based carbon9 were used for the removal of OTC from wastewater. However, these conventional adsorbents could not achieve the mineralisation of OTC. Fe-based materials attracted much attention because of their strong adsorption capacity10 and easy regeneration by chemical oxidation11. Nevertheless, the adsorption capacity of Fe-based materials for antibiotics is generally low12,13 and most studies have focused on the adsorption of heavy metals by Fe-based materials and the removal of antibiotics by chemical oxidation of these materials14,15. Moreover, the treatment process of chemical oxidation is complicated and costly. Therefore, it is necessary to find a material that is easy to operate, low-cost and combines the adsorption and oxidation of antibiotics.

Schwertmannite (SCH) is an effective stabiliser with a large specific surface area, numerous pores and a weakly crystalline structure16. As an environmentally friendly material, SCH is widely applied in environmental remediation for pollutant removal17. BC-loaded SCH-based materials improved the coadsorption capacity of ciprofloxacin and roxarsone, and their maximum adsorption was significantly higher than that of other Fe-based adsorbents18. Although the adsorption of OTC by Sch is weak12 it is noteworthy that Fe(II) released from Sch under light irradiation can form a photosensitive complex with OTC and promote the degradation of OTC19,20. However, this process is limited. Moreover, SCH tends to aggregate into large particles, which further limits the exposure of SCH to pollutants21. Zinc oxide (ZnO) is an ideal catalyst for environmental remediation due to its effective radiation absorption, photostability, and low cost. It is also widely used in the treatment of polluted environments22. The electron-hole pairs generated by photocatalytic materials under light irradiation can react with O2, OH− or H2O adsorbed on the surface of photocatalytic materials to generate hydroxyl radical (·OH) and superoxide anion (·O2−) with strong oxidising properties, thus safely and efficiently oxidising and decomposing organic pollutants23. Rajamanickam found that ZnO-assisted photocatalysis degraded 4-nitrophenol efficiently under solar light irradiation, and the adsorption of 4-nitrophenol on ZnO was favourable for this process24. Under solar light irradiation, the removal of ciprofloxacin was 75% and 40% when ZnO or bentonite alone was added, and the removal of ciprofloxacin increased to 95% with the addition of ZnO/bentonite. The addition of bentonite prevented the agglomeration of ZnO nanomaterials and provided more active sites on the composite surface25. Meanwhile, it is also frequently used as an agricultural fertiliser to promote plant growth and enhance crop yields in farmland soils26. In a study by Phung L.D., composted sludge containing ZnO improved the mineral composition of rice grains and enhanced soil fertility27.

Many studies showed that SCH and ZnO had been widely used in the remediation of antibiotic pollution with good results. However, both SCH and ZnO have different degrees of defects in antibiotic pollution remediation due to the differences in material structure and physicochemical properties. Considering the adsorption capacity of SCH and the remarkable electrical and optical properties of ZnO, ZnO loading onto SCH (ZnO/SCH) exhibits various potential advantages.

Currently, there is no literature report on green and environmentally friendly dual-function photocatalytic and adsorption SCH-based materials that can remove OTCs efficiently. In this study, we synthesised ZnO/SCH composite materials by co-precipitation method for the first time, combining photocatalytic water detoxification with soil adsorption passivation technology, targeting the efficient remediation of OTC-contaminated wastewater and soil. The removal efficiency of ZnO/SCH for OTC was evaluated under dark, visible light, and solar light conditions and the effect of pH on its performance was also examined. In addition, the free radicals involved in the photocatalytic degradation of OTC under solar light irradiation and the TOC removal rate were thoroughly investigated. In practical environments, the water environment and soil environment themselves will interact and affect each other. To extend the practical applications of ZnO/SCH, we assessed its performance in removing OTC from calcareous soil. We analysed the influence of environmental factors, including contamination level, temperature, soil moisture, pH, Calcium carbonate, and organic matter content. The adaptability of this study shows the great innovation of ZnO/SCH in the green and efficient removal of OTC from wastewater and soil. This study provides a valuable reference for controlling OTC area-source pollution in OTC-contaminated wastewater or even soil and will also provide theoretical support and technical guidance for the management of OTC-contaminated environments.

Methods

Chemicals

The oxytetracycline hydrochloride (OTC) was supplied by Biomax, China. Chromatographically pure formic acid, methanol and oxalic acid used for the OTC extraction assay were provided by Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Factory, China. The rest of the reagents and chemicals in this study were of analytical grade. The solutions used in the experiments were prepared with ultrapure water (resistivity is 18.2 M-cm).

Synthesis of zno/sch

The SCH were prepared as described below28. Briefly, Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans bacterial solution was activated three times in modified 9 K liquid culture mediums to obtain a suspension of Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans cell suspension. This suspension was added to FeSO4 solution and incubated at constant temperature until complete oxidation of ferrous iron. The resulting reddish-brown precipitate was washed three times with acid and ultrapure water. Finally, SCH was obtained by freeze-drying, grinding and passing through a 100-mesh sieve.

The synthesis of Zno/SCH is described in detail29. Dissolved 2.1 g NaHCO3 in 250 mL of ultrapure water. 1 g of SCH was added, put it on a magnetic stirrer, and stirred vigorously for 30 min so that SCH was dispersed entirely. Triton X100 was slowly dripped into the above dispersion while stirring vigorously to obtain suspension A. Zinc acetate solution was slowly dripped into suspension A by a peristaltic pump at a rate of 1 mL/min while stirring vigorously. The tan precipitate, filtered through a Buchner funnel, was rinsed three times with anhydrous ethanol and ultrapure water, respectively. After freeze-drying, it was calcined at 200 °C for 5 h, ground, and sieved to obtain ZnO/SCH powder. Meanwhile, ZnO without SCH was synthesised using the same method. The resulting ZnO/SCH composite was characterised using various techniques, including X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

OTC degradation experiment in solution

Photocatalytic degradation of OTC

0.01 g of material (SCH, ZnO, ZnO/SCH) was weighed and added to 50 mL of a 30 mg/L OTC solution in a clean quartz tube. Photocatalytic degradation of OTC was conducted under dark, visible light (350 W xenon lamp with a filter having a cutoff wavelength of 420 nm) and solar light (350 W xenon lamp with a 290 nm cutoff filter). Before irradiation, the solution was stirred for 30 min to reach adsorption and desorption equilibrium. After the light source was stabilised for 15 min, the quartz tube was irradiated, and the experiment was started. During the photodegradation process, the solution was stirred continuously, and 1 mL samples were taken at intervals of 0, 5, 10, 15, 30, 60, 120, 180, and 240 min. The samples were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, and the OTC concentration was determined.

Effect of pH

The degradation experiment of OTC was carried out under solar light with six different pH values: 2.00, 4.00, 6.00, 7.00, 8.00, and 10.00. The concentration of ZnO/SCH was 0.2 g/L, and the concentration of OTC was 30 mg/L. The specific experimental procedure was the same as the photocatalytic degradation of OTC. The experiments were repeated three times for each pH value.

Scavenging of free radicals

To investigate the free radicals that play a role in the photocatalytic degradation of OTC, we scavenged the free radicals during the degradation of OTC under solar light. The concentration of ZnO/SCH was 0.2 g/L, and the concentration of OTC was 30 mg/L. Isopropanol was added to trap ·OH, p-benzoquinone was added to trap ·O2–, and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium was added to trap h+. The radical trapping agent was added before the start of the reaction. The samples obtained at 5, 10, 15, 30, 60 and 120 min were passed through a 0.22 μm filter membrane. Finally, the OTC content of the samples was determined.

Microbial growth effect experiment

To investigate the ecological safety of the photocatalytic degradation process of OTC by ZnO/SCH, we further conducted microbial growth influence experiments. We used a common broad-spectrum inorganic salt medium containing concentrations of glucose and yeast extract to cultivate the microorganisms. The medium was prepared by weighing 1.1 g of NH4Cl, 1.9 g of KNO3, 0.17 g of KH2PO4, 0.37 g of MgSO4, 0.44 g of CaCl2, 2 g of glucose, and 0.3 g of peptone dissolved in 1 L of ultrapure water30. 5 g of soil was collected from the campus, mixed with 120 mL of the above medium in a 250 mL conical flask, and incubated for 3 d in a thermostatic shaking incubator at 30 ± 2 °C and 150 rpm/min. After the initial incubation, let the mixture stand for 1–2 h. Take 2 mL of the supernatant into a conical flask containing 100 mL of the above medium and continue to incubate for 3 d. Repeat the above steps until the end of incubation for 3 times. The resultant mixture is the microbial inoculum. The solar photocatalytic OTC degradation experiment procedure is referred to in 3.3.1, without sampling in the middle. At the end of the experiment, 15 mL of the filtrate was mixed with 10 mL of doubled medium, and 0.5 mL of microbial inoculum was added. The mixture continued to be incubated in a constant temperature shaking incubator. Samples were taken at 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h. The OD value of the samples was measured at 600 nm.

OTC contamination remediation experiment in calcareous soil

Remediation of soil contaminated with OTC

To further investigate the remediation effect of ZnO/SCH on OTC-contaminated soil, we conducted a contaminated soil culture simulation experiment. This was done as follows: 210 g of air-dried soil and uniformly mixed with 0, 0.005%, 0.05%, 0.5%, 0.5%, and 2% ZnO/SCH in a small transparent cup (diameter of 5.50 cm and height of 4.50 cm). 63.0 mL of 300 mg/L OTC solution was added, mixed again and placed in the laboratory at 25 ℃ for constant incubation. Weigh and rehydrate during incubation to maintain soil moisture content around 60%. Weighed and labelled. Three parallels were set up for each treatment. Soil samples were collected after stirring and mixing the samples at 0, 1, 3, 7, 14, 21, 28, and 56 days of incubation. The OTC content of the soil samples was determined after freeze-drying. SCH and ZnO remediation of OTC-contaminated soil was carried out as above.

Impact of environmental factors

Changes in the environment will cause changes in the environmental behaviour of soil OTC, which in turn will affect the remediation effect of ZnO/SCH on OTC. In this experiment, the following common environmental variables were set: (1) OTC pollution degree (30, 60, 90 mg/kg); (2) temperature (20 °C, 25 °C, 30 °C); and (3) soil moisture content (5%, 18%, 30%). Three parallels were set for each treatment. The specific experimental procedure was the same as the remediation of soil contaminated with OTC.

Considering the characteristics of calcareous soil, the following variables were set to further investigate the effect of this fraction on soil OTC activity: (1) soil pH (6.59, 7.00, 8.63); (2) soil Ca2+ content (50, 125, 200 mg/kg); and (3) soil organic matter content (15, 25, 35 mg/kg). Three parallels were set for each treatment. The specific experimental procedures were the same as above.

Analytical methods

Antibiotic extraction methods for soil samples: OTC was extracted from soil samples by repeated extraction with Na₂EDTA-McIlvaine buffer (pH = 4) three times. The three extracts were combined thoroughly, and the resulting OTC extract was washed three times with 10 mL of n-hexane. After washing, the extract was concentrated by rotary evaporation to 5 mL at 40 °C and then subjected to solid-phase extraction. The extract was collected, concentrated to less than 1 mL at 40 °C using a nitrogen blow-down apparatus, and then reconstituted to 1 mL using the oxalic acid-methanol mixture. The OTC concentration was then measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, Shimadzu, Japan). The OTC extraction methods for the material system and the mixed system of material and soil were consistent with the soil OTC extraction steps described above.

Antibiotic determination: Separation was done using a C18 column [5 μm, 4.6 × 150 mm (Waters BEH)], with mobile phase A being 0.1% formic acid and mobile phase B being acetonitrile. The flow rates were 0.8 mL/min for mobile phase A and 0.2 mL/min for mobile phase B. A 20 µL sample was injected, and analysis was performed using isocratic elution with detection at 355 nm. The total analysis time was 5 min.

Results and discussion

Morphology characterisations

Surface morphology and EDS analysis

The morphological characteristics of the prepared SCH, ZnO, and ZnO/SCH were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 1a-c illustrate the shapes and surface morphologies of SCH, ZnO, and ZnO/SCH. The biosynthesised SCH material exhibits a spherical, urchin-like morphology with a rough surface and spines (Fig. 1a), which aligns with previously published studies31. Figure 1b clearly shows that the synthesised ZnO particles tend to agglomerate into irregular shapes due to poor dispersion, likely due to the small particle size of ZnO, consistent with the morphology observed in other studies32. In contrast to SCH and ZnO, ZnO/SCH exhibits a distinct morphology with a porous structure (Fig. 1c). With the aid of SCH, the dispersion of ZnO is significantly improved. A similar result was observed in a 2016 study by Tayyebi A33.

Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis confirmed that ZnO/SCH contains ZnO and SCH components (Fig. 1d-f). Compared to SCH, the Zn content on the surface of ZnO/SCH was notably higher, indicating that ZnO has been successfully incorporated into SCH.

Mineral phase analysis

As shown in the XRD spectrum (Fig. 2a), the diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 18.239°, 26.267°, 35.164°, 46.534°, and 61.344° correspond to the standard pattern of SCH (JCPDS card No. 47-1775)34. The diffraction peaks of ZnO at 31.769°, 34.421°, 36.252°, 47.538°, 56.602°, 62.862°, and 67.961° are attributed to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), and (112) crystal planes, matching the standard crystal planes of ZnO (JCPDS card NO. 36-1451) and confirming that the synthesised ZnO has a hexagonal wurtzite structure35. These results indicate that both SCH and ZnO have been successfully synthesised. The diffraction peaks of ZnO/SCH mainly show those of ZnO, which could be due to the close overlap between the standard diffraction peak of SCH at 35.164° and those of ZnO at 34.421° and 36.252°. However, the diffraction peaks of ZnO/SCH are weaker than those of ZnO, suggesting that ZnO is present within ZnO/SCH and encapsulated by SCH. This is further corroborated by the SEM and EDS results.

Specific surface area and pore size distribution

The specific surface area and pore structure of the synthesised materials were analysed through N₂ adsorption-desorption isotherms (Fig. 2b). As shown in the figure, the adsorption-desorption isotherms of SCH, ZnO, and ZnO/SCH are all type IV isotherms, and ZnO and ZnO/SCH exhibit H1-type hysteresis loops. At low P/Po, N2 adsorption by the micropores of the synthesised materials shows an isotherm jump. SCH has fewer pores, while ZnO/SCH is mainly microporous (Fig. S1). As P/Po increases to 0.8, the adsorption isotherm increases sharply, indicating the presence of abundant mesopores and macropores in the synthesised materials. Calculation of the specific surface area and pore volume revealed that SCH has the smallest surface area, 3.89 m2/g. With the addition of ZnO, the specific surface area of ZnO/SCH increases to 109.77 m2/g. SCH has a pore volume of only 0.02 cm3/g, significantly smaller than that of ZnO and ZnO/SCH (Table S1).

Control of OTC in aqueous systems by zno/sch

Dosage optimisation of zno/sch in OTC degradation

Figure 3 reveals the degree of degradation of OTC under solar light irradiation with different additions of ZnO/SCH. The appropriate material additions are crucial for the industrial production cost and obtaining better photocatalytic effects in practical applications. Therefore, different ZnO/SCH additions in the 0.04–0.28 g/L range were selected to determine the optimal catalyst dosage for photocatalytic activity. It can be seen that the removal rate of OTC increased from 85.71 to 100% with the increase of ZnO/SCH addition from 0.04 g/L to 0.28 g/L. However, when the addition amount of ZnO/SCH was more than 0.20 g/L, the OTC removal rate increased insignificantly. The increase of active groups with the increase in catalyst dosage was the main reason for the rise in photocatalytic efficiency. 0.20 g/L of ZnO/SCH had an excellent photocatalytic degradation of OTC (98.71%). This effect was very close to that of 0.28 g/L ZnO/SCH. Therefore, based on production cost considerations, 0.20 g/L was considered the optimal ZnO/SCH addition and was used for subsequent studies.

OTC degradation

The degradation of OTC under different light conditions is shown in Fig. 4. Under dark conditions, for the pure OTC solution (control, CK), only 1.23% of OTC was removed after 4.5 h (Fig. 4a). In the system with only SCH, after 4 h and 30 min, the removal rate of OTC was 21%. During the adsorption equilibrium phase under dark conditions (-30 ~ 0 min), the OTC adsorption rates for SCH and ZnO/SCH were 8.10% and 23.55%, respectively. After 4 h, the removal rates reached 21% and 26%, respectively. This is attributed to ZnO/SCH improving the pore structure of SCH, as confirmed by BET results.

Figure 4b illustrates the degradation of OTC under visible light. In the control group (CK), OTC concentration decreased by only 1.84% after 4 h. When SCH was added, the OTC removal rate increased by only 11.9% after 4 h compared to the dark condition. Notably, after 4 h of visible light irradiation, ZnO/SCH removed 70.22% of OTC, representing a 170% increase in the removal rate compared to dark conditions. Therefore, the synthesised ZnO/SCH overcomes the limitation of poor visible light absorption in traditional ZnO, enhancing light absorption in the visible region and accelerating the degradation rate of OTC36.

The effect of solar light on OTC degradation is shown in Fig. 4c. Under solar light irradiation, the OTC concentration in the pure solution decreased by only 2% after 4 h. Illumination accelerates the degradation of OTC, which is determined by its photodegradation characteristics37. When only SCH was added, after 4 h of reaction, the removal rate of OTC increased by only 15.86% compared to dark conditions. Fe(II) is generated by SCH under photon energy complexes with OTC, forming photosensitised compounds that accelerate the degradation process12. With ZnO/SCH added, the OTC removal rate reached over 90% after 120 min and 98.71% after 4 h. On the one hand, ZnO/SCH enhances light energy utilisation, improving the degradation rate of OTC38; on the other hand, it increases adsorption and reaction sites, improving stability and catalytic activity39. In the experiment, the solar light is a band greater than 290 nm, containing the UV band from 290 to 400 nm. The visible light is the band greater than 420 nm, which does not contain UV light. Numerous studies showed that under UV irradiation, not only is the rate of OTC self-degradation faster40 but ZnO also generates more oxygen vacancies and has stronger catalytic oxidation performance41. Therefore, ZnO/SCH has a stronger photodegradation efficiency than OTC under solar light irradiation. Overall, the removal of OTC by ZnO/SCH after 4 h of light irradiation was very close to that of ZnO (Fig. S2), but the adsorption of OTC by ZnO/SCH was significantly improved (Fig. S3). From an ecological safety perspective, we also conducted microbial growth experiments. The results indicated that ZnO/SCH had minimal effect on microbial growth, suggesting good application prospects (Fig. S4). There are also many studies on OTC degradation experiments. Although In-situ S-doped mesoporous carbons were highly efficient for the activation of peroxydisulfate towards aqueous OTC removal, with 100% removal achieved within 30 min under optimal conditions, this is based on the presence of peroxydisulfate chemistry, which is costly42. The photodegradation rate of ZnO-Pt@CMC composite catalyst for OTC was 85.4% after 3 h under optimal reaction conditions. The wavelength necessary for the photocatalytic reaction of the catalyst lies in the near ultraviolet region (320–400 nm), and the degradation performance of the composite catalyst under visible light irradiation was unsatisfactory43. In terms of photodegradation performance, ecological safety and cost-effectiveness, ZnO/SCH has excellent performance and is a very promising material for environmental remediation.

Effect of pH

pH influences the form in which OTC is present, subsequently affecting its degradation rate under solar light. The photocatalytic degradation of OTC was tested under initial pH conditions ranging from 2 to 10 in polluted water. The results are shown in Fig. 5. Overall, ZnO/SCH demonstrates a wide pH tolerance range. In polluted water with a pH range of 4–10, ZnO/SCH removes more than 95% of OTC.

At pH = 2, OTC is present in its cationic form. In this system, the OTC removal rate is slower. This may be due to the instability of ZnO/SCH in acidic conditions, where the ZnO on the surface dissolves, exposing the internal SCH. In contrast, the pHpzc of biogenic SCH was 7.644. At this point, the surface of SCH carried a large amount of positive charge, which was electrostatically repulsive to the cationic form of OTC. Therefore, during the adsorption equilibrium phase, the OTC adsorption by ZnO/SCH was lowest at pH = 2. Previous BET and photocatalysis experiments support this conjecture. At pH = 10, h+ generated by ZnO/SCH reacts with OH⁻ under light to generate more oxidative ·OH, accelerating the degradation of OTC by ZnO/SCH.

Free radical scavenging experiment

To investigate the types and contributions of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the OTC removal process by ZnO/SCH under solar light, free radical quenching experiments were carried out with p-quinone (p-BQ), isopropanol (IPA), and ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium (EDTA-2Na) as quenchers for ·O2–, ·OH, and h+ (Fig. 6). After 60 min, 76.37% of OTC was removed without any quenchers. With isopropanol added, the removal rate decreased to 59.13%, indicating that ·OH scavenged 17.24% of OTC. In the para-benzoquinone system, the OTC removal rate decreased to 24.19% after 60 min, while in the Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium system, the removal rate was 9.54%. The addition of p-quinone and Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium significantly suppressed OTC removal, while isopropanol caused a slight decrease in the removal rate. These results suggest that h⁺ and ·O₂⁻ are the dominant reactive species responsible for OTC degradation.

TOC removal

TOC content after 90 min of reaction under solar light was measured in different systems, and the results are shown in Fig. 7. In the SCH + OTC system, the residual ratios of OTC and TOC after 90 min were 0.78 and 0.96, respectively. Notably, ZnO/SCH reduced the residual ratio of OTC to 0.15 and achieved a TOC removal rate seven times higher than that of the SCH + OTC system. This shows that ZnO/SCH has excellent mineralisation ability under solar light.

Evaluation of OTC pollution control in calcareous soil

To assess the remediation capacity of ZnO/SCH for OTC (OTC) contamination in calcareous farmland soil, we conducted a long-term (56-day) simulation experiment. OTC extraction experiments were conducted in material, soil, and soil-material mixed systems to improve data reliability, with the results shown in Table S2. Notably, the OTC extraction rate from soil was only 71.97%, and the extraction rate in the soil-material mixed system was even lower (Table S2). This is a plausible result because of the greater adsorption fixation capacity of ZnO/SCH than that of soil. Based on this, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the simulated experiment data (Table S3). The results show that, at the same dosage, ZnO/SCH increased the OTC adsorption capacity compared to SCH and ZnO (Table S4), which is consistent with the equilibrium adsorption results of ZnO/SCH (Fig. S3). This may be attributed to the mesoporous structure and larger surface area of ZnO/SCH.

In this culture system, the main pathways of OTC activity are: (1) adsorption by the soil matrix, with some converting to an unextractable residual form; (2) adsorption by the synthesised material; (3) degradation by soil microorganisms; (4) photodegradation; (5) free. The addition of synthetic materials affects points (1) and (5), influencing the extractable OTC content in the soil (Fig. 8).

Based on the actual OTC removal rate, when a low dose (0.005%) of SCH was added, the OTC removal rate in the soil was 95.62% (Fig. 9). As SCH concentration increased from 0 to 2%, the corresponding kobs removal for OTC rose from 0.05 to 0.07, indicating that adding SCH alleviated microbial stress from OTC, enhanced microbial activity, and promoted OTC removal in the soil (Fig. S5). In contrast, the corresponding Kobs of OTC removal gradually decreased from 0.05 to 0.02 in the presence of progressively increasing amounts of added ZnO/SCH, as ZnO/SCH has a stronger adsorption capacity for OTC. The addition of ZnO had little effect on the OTC removal rate (Fig. S6). Remarkably, Kulshrestha et al. noted that antibiotics adsorbed or encapsulated in soil are slowly released due to changes in environmental factors such as soil moisture, pH, and ionic strength45. Many studies also show that microbial degradation of antibiotics often results in more toxic intermediate products46. Therefore, the soil-ZnO/SCH mixed system, less affected by these factors, demonstrates better ecological safety (Table S4). Additionally, as reported by Xu et al., under light irradiation, the light transmittance of shallow soils in conical flasks, in addition to surface soils, increased, accelerating the degradation of DOX47. In general, ZnO/SCH, with high ecological safety, adsorption capacity, and photosensitivity, is highly effective for OTC adsorption and fixation.

Impact of environmental factors

The adsorption and degradation of OTC in soil are influenced by environmental conditions. The effects of soil OTC contamination, temperature, and moisture content on OTC removal by ZnO/SCH were investigated. At a lower contamination level of 30 mg/kg, OTC removal from soil was 94.93% (Fig. 10a). In contrast, at a higher contamination level of 90 mg/kg, the OTC removal rate was only 82% of that at the lower level. As the contamination level increased, kobs for OTC removal decreased from 0.06 to 0.03 (Fig. S7a). This may be mainly related to changes in microbial activity48. While many studies state that material adsorption increases in more polluted environments49 the limited adsorption sites of ZnO/SCH and soil increase the percentage of free OTC. When OTC concentration exceeds the tolerance of soil microorganisms, OTC toxicity reduces their activity47,50. This may also be due to the bactericidal nature of OTC51. Meanwhile, the accumulating intermediates of OTC degradation may also inhibit the activity of soil OTC-degrading bacteria52. Besides this phenomenon can also be explained by the dilution effect. In the study of Mehmood S et al., it was similarly found that as the initial Cr contamination level increased, the Cr (VI) removal rate decreased from 40.69 to 19.82% after adding Ai-ZnONP to the soil26.

Figure 10b shows the effect of temperature on OTC removal. In the ZnO/SCH remediation of OTC-contaminated soil, the OTC removal rate was slightly inhibited at 20 °C. As the temperature increased, kobs for OTC removal in soil increased from 0.02 to 0.03. At 25 °C and 30 °C, OTC removal rates in soil were 1.07 and 1.29 times higher than at 20 °C, respectively (Fig. S7b). This is likely because increased temperature accelerates the movement of OTC molecules and ZnO/SCH particles, resulting in more effective collisions and enhanced adsorption. This aligns with the particle collision theory. In addition, temperature changes may also affect the growth of soil OTC-degrading bacteria and the activity and stability of enzymes in microorganisms53. For example, Pseudomonas and Burkholderia cepacia, which have an optimal growth temperature of 30 °C, showed favourable OTC degradation in previous studies54,55. Qi W et al. also demonstrated that high temperature promotes the biodegradation of OTC in the range of 25 °C − 40 °C54.

The effect of soil moisture content differences on OTC removal is shown in Fig. 10c. Differences in soil moisture affect the residual OTC in the soil. At the end of the 56-day period, the OTC removal rates in soils with 5%, 18%, and 30% moisture content were 65.99%, 79.63%, and 78.12%, respectively, with its kobs constant increasing from 0.02 to 0.03 (Fig. S7c). In general, The effect of soil moisture content on OTC residues may be related to the fact that changes in soil moisture conditions alter microbial activity and affect the degree of hydrolysis of OTC56. Long-term dry conditions (5%) limited microbial activity and reduced OTC hydrolysis57. When moisture content is sufficient, changes in moisture have less impact on OTC residue but alter the removal mechanism. Moist soil conditions (18%) promoted microbial growth and enhanced OTC biodegradation. Long-term wet conditions (30%) limited microbial activity due to restricted O₂ diffusion but facilitated OTC hydrolysis due to the abundant moisture58. Additionally, sufficient moisture allowed for the movement of free OTC and ZnO/SCH, increasing the contact probability between them. Moreover, the added ZnO/SCH generated free reactive species under light, further enhancing OTC removal.

The physicochemical properties of the antibiotic and soil characteristics, such as pH, calcium carbonate, and organic matter content, influence the adsorption mechanism of OTC in soil59. Using calcareous soil characteristics as an example, we investigated the effects of these factors on OTC removal by ZnO/SCH. The OTC removal under pH conditions ranging from 6.59 to 8.63 is shown in Fig. 11a. Variations in soil pH affect OTC residue in the soil. At the end of the 56-day experiment, OTC residues in soils with pH 6.59, 7.00, and 8.63 were 0.78, 3.74, and 17.87 mg/kg, respectively. At pH 6.59, OTC adsorption by the soil was 1.24 times higher than at pH 8.63. The kobs for OTC removal decreased with increasing pH (Fig. S8a). This is likely related to OTC’s ionic state in different pH environments60. Furthermore, soil pH modulates the surface charge characteristics of soil particles and regulates the cation exchange capacity (CEC) of the soil system61. When the soil pH is below the first pKa value of OTC (pKa1 = 3.57), the OTC predominantly exists in its cationic form, facilitating enhanced cation exchange and adsorption processes62. With increasing pH, electrostatic repulsion between OTC molecules and soil particle surfaces becomes more pronounced, even though cation exchange and adsorption mechanisms persist63. These findings are consistent with previous studies by Wu et al. on OTC adsorption by iron-montmorillonite at different pH levels64. Under such pH conditions, the increased free OTC concentration enhances its adsorption onto ZnO/SCH, which also affects OTC biodegradation51.

Figure 11b illustrates the impact of varying calcium carbonate (CaCO3) concentrations on OTC removal rate. The calcium carbonate content significantly influences OTC’s environmental behaviour and fate in soil systems. After 56 days, the OTC removal rate in soils containing 125 and 200 mg/kg calcium carbonate decreased by 8.74% and 30.26%, respectively, relative to the 50 mg/kg treatment. It is mentioned that as calcium carbonate content increased, OTC removal decreased significantly (Fig. S8b). The post-reaction soil pH ranged from 8.45 to 8.55, causing OTC to primarily exist as an anion. Previous studies indicate that the anionic form of OTC can form a complex with Ca2+, generating OTC-calcium65. While OTC-calcium formation promotes OTC removal, excessive calcium carbonate content induces electrostatic repulsion between anionic OTC and soil particles. Additionally, this process alters soil organic carbon content, subsequently affecting microbial activity and OTC adsorption capacity. Consistent with Guo et al.‘s finding, calcium carbonate amendment effectively neutralises soil acidity, and with increasing calcium carbonate, soil pH rose accordingly66. It may also be due to Ca2+ competing with cationic OTC for adsorption sites on soil minerals. However, the impact is limited since cationic OTC constitutes a small proportion of this system.

Soil organic matter (SOM), a crucial soil component, significantly influences OTC adsorption and its environmental fate through multiple mechanisms. Figure 11c shows the OTC removal under different organic matter conditions. Following 56 days of incubation, the OTC removal rates reached 84.63%, 87.20%, and 91.60% in soils with 15, 25, and 35 mg/kg organic matter additions, respectively. Soils with higher organic matter content showed significantly greater OTC adsorption capacity (Fig. S8c). This phenomenon can be attributed to: (1) soil organic matter has a larger surface area, providing more adsorption sites; (2) functional groups in OTC, such as amino, hydroxyl, and keto groups, exhibit high affinity for organic matter through cation bridging and exchange mechanisms67. Bermudez-Couso also found that when affinity is low, differences in soil adsorption are primarily due to variations in organic matter content68. This agrees with the findings of Ling et al.69. However, contrasting findings exist, as Yang et al. reported enhanced OTC biodegradation rates in organic matter-rich soils, suggesting complex organic matter-OTC dynamics70.

Conclusion

In this study, ZnO/SCH soil remediation material was successfully synthesised and demonstrated excellent photocatalytic degradation and adsorption properties in both aqueous solutions and calcareous soils. The results revealed that the ZnO/SCH composite possesses several advantageous properties: high surface area (109.77 m²/g), excellent light-responsive ability, wide pH tolerance (4–10), and efficient remediation performance. As a soil remediation agent, ZnO/SCH rapidly fixes OTC in the soil. Furthermore, we systematically investigated the influence of key soil environmental factors (contamination level, temperature, soil moisture, pH, Calcium carbonate, and organic matter content) on the activity of OTC in soil. Increases in temperature and soil moisture content directly promoted OTC fixation by ZnO/SCH. In conclusion, soil acts as a natural “pollutant reservoir,” with ZnO/SCH serving as a secondary barrier and a “backup resource.” The ZnO/SCH effectively mitigates secondary pollution risks by rapidly immobilising OTC released from soil under changing environmental conditions. The dual-functional ZnO/SCH demonstrates significant potential for environmental remediation applications, including water treatment, soil decontamination, and paddy field restoration.
