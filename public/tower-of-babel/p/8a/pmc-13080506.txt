Rotational Variability in Arthritic Knee Morphometry Predominantly Arises From the Hip and May Warrant Preoperative Consideration Prior to Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Andrew J. Yang, Joshua J. Woo, Teja S. Polisetty, Yash S. Saboo, Prem N. Ramkumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPreoperative planning in total knee arthroplasty (TKA) has relied on local knee landmarks without considering proximal ones. Although balancing requires triplanar (coronal, sagittal, axial) anatomy, the literature emphasizes 2-dimensional imaging that ignores axial-plane rotation clarified by computed tomography scans. This study of broad arthritic knee anatomy aimed to (1) identify the source of greatest rotational variation between hip and knee, and (2) determine if local knee anatomy morphometrically approximates hip rotation or if the hip independently impacts knee morphometry.MethodsA computer vision pipeline analyzed 1352 TKA candidate computed tomography scans to extract 5 hip and 10 knee angular measurements; among 422 anatomical outliers, variability was quantified via dispersion metrics and principal component analysis, while regression models assessed if knee morphology predicts hip morphology.ResultsHip-related features showed significantly greater variability than knee-related features among anatomical outliers (P < .05), particularly in the axial plane, suggesting that most lower-limb rotational variability originates from the hip. Knee-based measurements failed to predict hip morphometry, identifying hip rotation as an independent factor in lower-limb presentation of arthritic knee anatomy.ConclusionsDespite the dogma of intraoperatively balancing using local knee landmarks, the hip—a major source of lower-limb anatomical heterogeneity not captured by standard knee imaging—contributes greater rotational variability than the knee and independently influences observed variation in arthritic knee anatomy. While specific clinical protocols are not yet defined, the proximal femur may represent an important area for further investigation to determine if its consideration can improve TKA rotational alignment, flexion balancing, and patellofemoral stability.

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) is a widely performed procedure for end-stage knee osteoarthritis, yet 15-30% of patients remain dissatisfied despite technically successful procedures [[1], [2], [3]]. Accurate restoration of limb alignment, traditionally guided by neutral mechanical alignment using knee-centered anatomical landmarks, is central to TKA success [4]. Whether using gap balancing vs measured resection, [5] kinematic vs mechanical alignment, or evaluating local landmarks (transepicondylar axis, [6] anteroposterior axis, [7] dorsal cortical line, [8] posterior condylar axis), all philosophies ignore the upstream influence of the proximal femur. This approach, rooted in the assumption of average knee morphometry, often neglects interindividual anatomical variability. Growing evidence suggests that variability, especially in proximal femoral morphometry, may explain the 31% of anatomic outliers prior to TKA [9].

With increased emphasis on alignment philosophies like kinematic or functional alignment principles attempting to restore premorbid anatomy with patient-specific corrections and minimal biomechanical changes, planning still rely on distal femoral and tibial anatomy [[10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. These knee-based parameters guide implant positioning without routinely incorporating data from the hip. However, studies show the rotational axis of the femur is nonuniform, and proximal femoral features such as neck-shaft angle, torsion, and femoral bowing can introduce systematic deviations in both coronal and axial planes [9,[15], [16], [17], [18]]. TKA patients with variable hip anatomy may be predisposed to patellofemoral or flexion instability—or subpar outcomes—if the rotation deviates greatly from the predisease state despite creating a perceptibly “balanced” knee under anesthesia.

Recent advances in image-based modeling now allow high-resolution, automated evaluation of lower-limb anatomy. Woo et al. applied such a pipeline to a multicenter registry of 1352 TKA candidates, revealing that over 30% exhibited “anatomical outlier” profiles characterized by substantial variation across hip, femur, and tibia [9,19]. Some of the most variable features were in the proximal femur, including femoral version and neck-shaft angle, which are not captured by standard knee imaging or mechanical guides [9]. These findings suggest that hip morphometry may be a major contributor to axial alignment during TKA. Moreover, published classification frameworks like the Coronal Plane Alignment of the Knee [20] have been found to poorly correlate with axial and sagittal plane measurements [21]. Talbot et al. have also reported malalignment of the quadriceps, which originate at the hip and follow the torsion of the femur, to be an independent anatomical consideration that affects rotational balancing at the time of TKA [22,23]. Taken together, these findings challenge the adequacy of knee-centered planning and introduces the concept of accounting for hip-centered rotational metrics when balancing a TKA.

In this study, we analyzed the triplanar angular anatomy of 1352 pre-TKA patients to evaluate whether upstream hip morphometry is more variable than downstream knee morphometry. Then, we investigated the relationship between knee (distal femoral) morphometry and hip (proximal femoral) morphometry to determine the degree to which these joints are related in the context of TKA alignment planning. Specifically, we assessed whether knee measurements correlate with and could serve as proxies for proximal femoral morphometry. If strong predictive relationships exist, it would indicate that surgeons might infer certain hip characteristics from routine knee imaging or exam, enhancing preoperative alignment strategies without always requiring full-length advanced imaging. On the other hand, if knee-based measurements prove poor predictors of hip anatomy, it would affirm the potential need for direct assessment of the hip to evaluate TKA alignment in the axial plane.

Material and methods

Data curation and preparation

This was an institutional review board exempt study. 1352 preoperative TKA hip-knee-ankle computed tomography (CT) scans were collected from a database at a multicenter referral center between 2019 and 2022. These scans were obtained as part of the standard preoperative planning protocol for TKA prior to robotic-assisted or custom. Inclusion criteria were (1) patients with Kellgren-Lawrence grade 4 changes in at least one compartment; (2) patients clinically indicated after failing at least 3 months of nonsurgical management (activity modification, anti-inflammatory medication, physical therapy, injections) and with functional impairment; and (3) patients undergoing primary TKA cases using a cruciate-retaining implant without the need for augments or cones to manage complex conditions necessitating increased constraint or bone loss management. Exclusion criteria were patients with active infection or prior native septic arthritis, previous open surgery (arthroplasty, osteotomy, fracture), inflammatory arthritis, or retained hardware. The severity of osteoarthritis was not based on radiographic parameters to intentionally capture broad anatomic variation among patients clinically indicated for primary TKA and to best reflect clinical practice. No patients underwent ipsilateral total hip arthroplasty.

Each CT scan was processed into standardized 1 × 1 × 1-mm resolution and resized for downstream modeling. A validated automated computer vision pipeline measured lower-limb angular morphometric parameters in the coronal, sagittal, and axial planes [19,24].

The deidentified dataset—a cross-sectional anatomical snapshot of the lower extremity prior to TKA—served as the exclusive source of input data for this study. To reflect their anatomical regions, we grouped variables into hip- and knee-specific categories.•Hip-related morphometry measurements (5): acetabular version, femoral version, femoral neck angle, femoral neck-shaft angle, femoral torsion.•Knee-related morphometry measurements (10): hip-knee-ankle axis angle, joint line convergence angle, lateral distal femoral angle, lateral posterior tibial slope angle, medial posterior tibial slope angle, posterior condylar axis angle, sulcus angle, tibiofemoral angle, transepicondylar axis horizontal angle, and transepicondylar axis-posterior condylar axis angle.

Exploratory and correlation analysis

Summary statistics were computed: continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages. Distributions of all morphometry measurements were visualized using histograms stratified by sex and constitutional alignment.

To characterize pairwise variable relationships, we constructed a matrix of visual and statistical summaries for each variable pair: scatter plots with Pearson correlations for continuous-continuous pairs, box plots with Welch’s t-tests for continuous-binary pairs, and heatmaps with chi-square tests for binary-binary pairs.

Comparison of variance in hip- and knee-related morphometry measurements among anatomical outliers

To investigate differences in variability between hip- and knee-related measurements among anatomical outliers, we conducted a series of dispersion analyses using the subset of 422 outlier individuals (31.2% of the 1352 TKA candidates) identified by Woo et al. Outlier status was assigned to individuals with 9 or more of 27 morphometric measurements falling outside the 15th-85th percentile range. The outlier population served to pinpoint sources of variation within this higher-risk cohort of TKA candidates with atypical anatomy.

From this outlier cohort, we extracted 2 feature sets per subject: (1) all hip and knee morphometric measurements, and (2) a subset limited to axial plane measurements (see Table 1).

For each measurement, we calculated 3 dispersion statistics—variance, interquartile range (IQR), and median absolute deviation (MAD)—separately for hip and knee regions. One-tailed Mann-Whitney U tests were then used to determine whether hip features showed significantly greater dispersion than knee features, with the alternative hypothesis specifying increased variability in the hip.

To examine multivariate variability, we conducted principal component analysis independently on the hip and knee feature matrices after mean-centering within the outlier cohort. We quantified total variance explained by all components, as well as the proportion explained by the first principal component. Permutation testing (1000 iterations) assessed the significance of total variance differences.

Prediction of hip-related morphometry measurements with knee-related morphometry measurements

To evaluate whether knee morphometry can serve as a surrogate for hip morphometry, we designed a predictive modeling pipeline using the full pool of 1352 TKA candidates. A random subset comprising 30% of the dataset was withheld as a test set to evaluate model generalizability. Missing values, which were infrequent (<5% for all morphometry measurements and primarily attributable to imaging artifacts), were imputed using the median for continuous variables and the most common value for categorical variables.

We first used ordinary least squares regression to model each of the 5 hip measurements as a function of the 10 knee measurements, along with age and weight. This modeling framework was designed to test the hypothesis that distal femoral and tibial morphometry, as measured through knee-centered features, does not adequately capture variability in proximal femoral anatomy. For each model, we reported test R-squared values, and we examined regression coefficients and P values to identify statistically significant associations.

Next, to determine whether machine learning models could improve predictive performance, we evaluated ridge regression, lasso regression, elastic net, and random forest regression, with hyperparameters optimized via cross-validation [25]. Model performance on the held-out test set was evaluated using R-squared, mean absolute error, root mean squared error, and Pearson correlation between predicted and actual values.

We next restricted our analysis to axial-plane features. Again, hip-related features showed greater dispersion than their knee counterparts. Differences were significant for IQR (P = .029) and MAD (P = .029) but borderline for variance (P = .057), indicating that hip-related axial features tend to be more variable among outliers.

To assess multivariate variability, we performed principal component analysis separately on the hip and knee feature sets. The hip region showed much higher overall variability compared to the knee (total variance = 113.78), and this difference was even greater for axial features (total variance = 210.61). Permutation testing confirmed that these findings were robust (P = .009 for all features, P = .033 for axial features only), indicating that the hip’s wider range of values represents a distinct source of anatomical heterogeneity rather than a function of feature dimensionality.

Knee morphometry fails to predict hip morphometry

Of particular interest was whether variation in distal femoral and tibial morphometry could account for interindividual differences in proximal femoral structure. All hip-related morphometric measurements were poorly predicted by knee-related measurements, establishing hip rotation as an independent variable that may contribute to the broad variation present in arthritic knee anatomy. Across the 5 hip outcomes, R-squared values were uniformly low (<0.18), suggesting that knee-derived measurements offer limited explanatory power for most aspects of hip morphometry.

To determine whether more complex models could improve predictive performance, we evaluated several machine learning regressors (ridge, lasso, elastic net, random forest) on the same prediction tasks. As shown in Table 5, all machine learning models performed poorly, with no meaningful improvement in predicting hip morphology (R-squared <0.19).

Discussion

This CT-based study of 1352 full-length pre-TKA knees demonstrates 2 key findings in the context of TKA planning: (1) rotational variation at the hip is a major, independent contributor to overall lower-limb anatomical heterogeneity; and (2) hip rotation is an independent variable that cannot be inferred from local knee landmarks but directly contributes to the observed variation in arthritic knee anatomy pending knee replacement. Our findings indicate that among the 31% of patients classified as anatomical outliers, hip-related measurements exhibited significantly greater variability than knee-related measurements. This was evident across multiple dispersion metrics for both the full feature set and the axial plane features relevant to rotational alignment. Principal component analysis further confirmed that hip morphometry accounted for a greater share of total variance in the outlier population, suggesting that the hip is a major driver of lower-limb anatomical heterogeneity. Predictive models showed no relationship between hip and knee morphometry. Taken together, these findings suggest the hip and proximal femur surprisingly warrant preoperative consideration to fully account for the axial plane in rotational alignment during TKA.

While traditional TKA approaches emphasize intraoperative knee-based landmarks, our results suggest that this paradigm may be insufficient precisely in the subset of patients whose anatomy deviates most from average norms (ie, the anatomical outliers). In this group, hip morphometry not only varies more, but does so in ways that cannot be captured by local knee measurements alone. Thus, from an anatomic perspective, the patients most likely to benefit from personalized alignment strategies at the time of TKA are also those for whom knee-based planning is least reliable. Specifically, key rotational parameters such as femoral version and torsion showed minimal to no predictability with intraoperative knee landmarks. This disconnect highlights that the rotational alignment critical to TKA stability and satisfaction cannot be assumed from local knee anatomy and should thus be considered preoperatively. In outlier populations with high rotational variability, these assumptions are particularly precarious and may predispose the joint to rotational malalignment and instability. Whether the rotation of the hip is formally assessed with a full-length CT scan or an examination under anesthesia, it is clear there exists a significant influence of proximal femoral anatomy during balancing in the axial plane at the time of TKA.

These findings challenge long-standing assumptions in TKA planning. Traditional mechanical alignment strategies presuppose consistent femoral geometry and often disregard hip morphometry. Newer techniques like kinematic alignment and functional planning seek to restore the joint to its premorbid state to minimize the effect upon soft tissues and dynamic stabilizers. Such attempts at “personalized arthroplasty” necessitate evaluation of the hip if we are to acknowledge the triplanar nature of the reconstruction. Our data reveal that accounting for the hip may mitigate the phenotypic diversity encountered at the time of TKA, particularly in terms of rotational alignment. This clinical relevance is underscored by Howell et al., who found that patients had significantly better Forgotten Joint Scores when the prosthetic trochlear angle was lateral to the quadriceps vector—a landmark-driven vector originating at the anterior inferior iliac spine of the hip [34]. Historically, this concept is partially broached in discussion of the “q angle,” which accounts for the coronally based phenomenon of patellofemoral tracking and lateral soft tissue tension about the knee. However, we now know that an increased coronal q angle reflects increased femoral anteversion in the axial plane [35]. Talbot et al. have specifically modernized the impact of proximal anatomy on intraoperative TKA balancing in their description of quadriceps malalignment—a surrogate for femoral torsion in our study—and requires additional intraoperative attention [22,23].

Our data demonstrate that among the broad spectrum of arthritic knee anatomy we treat with TKAs, an independent and neglected factor on arthritic knee morphometry is hip rotation. It is intuitive that hip rotation may affect intraoperative TKA elements like flexion gap balancing and patellar tracking, as both femoral rotation and the extensor mechanism originate at the hip, respectively. If the hip and knee were correlated, we could rely solely on knee landmarks to safely infer the upstream rotational profile. Because they are independent, one can infer the hip is a critical and overlooked anatomic landmark necessary to consider prior to knee reconstruction. A surgeon looking only at the knee cannot know if they are operating on a patient with extreme femoral anteversion or retroversion. This “blind spot” is quantitatively supported by our finding that knee-derived measurements failed to predict hip morphometry and is where risk of rotational malalignment lies. These patterns may mirror the “miserable malalignment syndrome,” where proximal torsion influences distal joint mechanics [36]. For a patient with extreme anteversion, for example, an increased lateral flexion gap may be better tolerated than one with retroversion. While ligamentous tension is fixed, the way a patient accommodates load may depend on their unique rotational anatomy. Our data provide the anatomical framework for this heterogeneity, though the specific biomechanical mechanisms of load accommodation require dynamic study. Whether we should adjust component placement remains an open question, similar to the ongoing discussions regarding the pathologic quadriceps. Perhaps in an extremely anteverted patient, relative femoral component internal rotation (ie, 0-3° external rotation to the transepicondylar axis) may be more “natural” than an excessively externally rotated component. These findings suggest that while specific surgical targets are not yet defined, the proximal femur is a critical and overlooked landmark.

Our study is strengthened by its use of a large, multicenter cohort and a validated automatic image analysis pipeline. The combination of statistical dispersion analysis, principal component analysis, and machine learning modeling provides a comprehensive evaluation of the relationship between hip and knee morphometry. However, limitations are inherent to the observational nature of this study. Specifically, while our data highlight the independence of hip rotation, any direct clinical recommendations for how to account for the hip during TKA remain speculative and require further validation. Moreover, accounting for 3-dimensional anatomy presupposes all physicians are acquiring preoperative CT scans prior to TKA; many physicians do not. Finally, while age, sex, and weight were controlled for, this dataset lacked granular data on ethnicity, nutrition, and patient height, all of which may influence femoral development and morphometry. Despite these limitations, the rotational profile from the axial plane at the time of TKA has been largely ignored due to the 2-dimensional nature of perioperative radiographic evaluation. CT-based analysis now elucidates the driving factor arises from the proximal femur and should be accounted for at the time of reconstruction.

Conclusions

Despite the dogma of intraoperatively balancing using local knee landmarks, the hip contributes greater rotational variability than the knee and independently influences the observed variation in arthritic knee anatomy. These findings identify the hip as a major source of lower-limb anatomical heterogeneity that is not captured by standard knee imaging. While specific clinical protocols are not yet defined, this study suggests that the proximal femur represents an important area for further investigation to determine if its consideration can improve TKA rotational alignment, flexion balancing, and patellofemoral stability.
