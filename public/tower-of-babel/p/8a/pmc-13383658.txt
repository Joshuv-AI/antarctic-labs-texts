Medium-fluorination carbonate electrolytes for stable 4.6 V lithium metal batteries
Authors: Wenjun Xie, Yuhang Zhang, Xinchun Song, Qi Yu, Zihan Xu, Yongtao Li, Zhipeng Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fluorination of carbonate electrolytes is an effective strategy for improving the stability of high-voltage lithium metal batteries (LMBs). However, highly fluorinated carbonate solvents often suffer from limited lithium-salt solubility, increased cost, and potential environmental concerns. Herein, we develop medium-fluorination carbonate electrolytes (MFCE) based on monofluorinated fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and difluorinated bis(2-fluoroethyl) carbonate (BFC) as the main solvents, aiming to balance electrochemical performance, cost, and sustainability. We demonstrate that moderate fluorination weakens the ion–dipole interactions between Li+ and carbonate solvent molecules while maintaining sufficient salt-dissolution capability. This weak-solvation characteristic promotes anion participation in the primary solvation sheath and facilitates the formation of inorganic-rich interfacial protective layers on both electrodes. As a result, Li–Li symmetric cells using MFCE exhibit stable cycling for over 1200 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, while Li–Cu half cells maintain reversible Li plating/stripping for nearly 480 cycles under the same conditions. Moreover, Li–LiCoO2 (LCO) full cells (14 mg cm−2, 50 µm Li, N/P ratio of 3.7) using MFCE can stably cycle for over 150 cycles at a cutoff voltage of 4.6 V, significantly outperforming those using conventional carbonate electrolytes. These results demonstrate that electrolyte performance is governed by the rational regulation of fluorination degree rather than simply increasing fluorine content, providing a practical design principle for advanced carbonate electrolytes in high-voltage LMBs.

Introduction

Achieving rechargeable lithium batteries with high energy density, long cycle life, and improved sustainability has become a central objective in advanced energy-storage research.1–5 Compared with conventional lithium-ion batteries (LIBs), lithium metal batteries (LMBs), which employ metallic lithium as the anode, offer a promising route to overcoming the energy-density limitations of current battery technologies owing to the ultrahigh theoretical specific capacity of lithium metal (3860 mAh g−1) and its extremely low electrochemical potential (−3.04 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode).6,7 Meanwhile, increasing the operating voltage of layered oxide cathodes, such as lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO2, LCO), can further unlock their reversible capacity and substantially enhance the overall energy output of cells.8–11 However, integrating high-voltage LCO cathodes with lithium metal anodes (LMAs) imposes stringent requirements on the stability of both electrode/electrolyte interfaces.12 On the cathode side, lattice oxygen activation, electrolyte oxidation, and structural phase transitions during high-voltage operation continuously deteriorate the cathode-electrolyte interphase (CEI), resulting in accelerated interfacial degradation and capacity decay.13–16 On the anode side, parasitic reactions between conventional carbonate electrolytes and metallic Li lead to the formation of an unstable solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), which promotes dendritic Li growth and reduces the reversibility of Li plating/stripping.17,18 Therefore, the concurrent instability of cathode and anode interfaces remains a major obstacle to the practical development of high-voltage LMBs.19–21 As the medium in direct contact with both electrodes, the electrolyte plays a decisive role in regulating interfacial chemistry and electrochemical stability, making rational electrolyte design essential for achieving durable high-voltage LMBs.22–25

Conventional carbonate electrolytes based on fluorine-free solvents, such as ethylene carbonate (EC) and diethyl carbonate (DEC), suffer from intrinsic limitations when applied to high-voltage LMBs.26,27 Their poor reductive stability induces continuous side reactions on LMAs, while their insufficient oxidative stability accelerates electrolyte decomposition at high-voltage cathodes.28,29 To address these challenges, fluorinated electrolyte design has attracted increasing attention as an effective strategy for improving interfacial stability and extending the electrochemical stability window.30–33 Fluorinated solvents have been reported to regulate Li+ solvation structures, enhance anion participation in interphase formation, and promote the formation of mechanically and chemically robust SEI and CEI layers.34–36 For example, Xiao et al. reported a nonflammable high-voltage electrolyte composed of 1 M lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) in fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC)/bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) carbonate (BTC), in which the fluorinated carbonate solvents weakened Li+–solvent coordination, facilitated Li+ desolvation, and induced the formation of LiF-rich solid electrolyte interphase/cathode electrolyte interphase (SEI/CEI) layers, thereby enabling stable 4.7–4.8 V Li–LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) cells over a wide temperature range from −30 to 70 °C.37 Ni et al. proposed an intrinsically nonflammable fluorine-rich high-entropy electrolyte composed of 1 M LiPF6 in FEC/2,2-difluoroethyl acetate (DFEA)/methyl 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl carbonate (FEMC)/methyl trifluoroacetate (MTFA)/ethoxy(pentafluoro)cyclotriphosphazene (PFPN), in which the synergistic effect of fluorination and high entropy minimized Li+–solvent interactions, accelerated Li+ desolvation, and promoted the formation of inorganic-rich interphases, thereby enabling intrinsically safe and stable 4.8 V Li-NCM811 cells under both room-temperature and low-temperature conditions.38 Qiu et al. developed a highly depolarized solvent-based electrolyte composed of lithium bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide (LiFSI) in difluoroethylene carbonate (DFEC)/bis(2,2,2-trifluoroethyl) carbonate (TFEC), which effectively suppressed solvent concentration polarization at the electric double layer and converted the interfacial Li+ solvation structure from solvent-dominated to anion-enriched, thereby accelerating Li+ desolvation, suppressing the dissolution of disodium rhodizonate (DSR) organic electrodes, and enabling ultrafast and long-life Li-DSR batteries even at −60 °C.39 Nevertheless, excessive fluorination is not always beneficial. Due to the strong electron-withdrawing effect of fluorine atoms, highly fluorinated carbonate solvents generally exhibit reduced donor ability and lower dielectric constants, which weaken Li+ solvation and may lead to limited lithium-salt solubility, reduced salt dissociation, and sluggish ionic transport.40–45 In addition, the high cost and potential environmental impact of highly fluorinated electrolyte systems remain important concerns.46 Therefore, rationally designing moderately fluorinated electrolytes that balance solvation capability, interfacial stability, cost, and sustainability is essential for practical high-voltage LMBs.

Herein, we developed medium-fluorination carbonate electrolytes (MFCE) based on a mixed-solvent system composed of FEC and bis(2-fluoroethyl) carbonate (BFC). FEC and BFC are derived from EC and DEC through monofluoride and difluoride substitution, respectively (Fig. 1a). Compared with fluorine-free EC and DEC, the appropriate incorporation of fluorine can effectively improve the compatibility of the electrolyte with LMAs while enhancing its high-voltage stability. Moreover, because FEC and BFC contain only a limited number of F atoms, MFCE offers advantages in cost and sustainability over highly fluorinated carbonate electrolytes. We found that the medium-fluorination strategy can weaken the ion–dipole interactions between Li+ and solvent molecules while maintaining sufficient lithium-salt dissolution capability, thereby endowing FEC and BFC with weak-solvation characteristics. This solvation structure promotes anion entry into the inner solvation sheath and forms inorganic-rich interphases. Consequently, compared with conventional carbonate electrolytes (CCE), MFCE effectively suppresses Li dendrite growth and improves the high-voltage cycling stability of LCO cathodes (Fig. 1b and c). Electrochemical measurements show that Li–Li symmetric cells using MFCE cycle stably for over 1200 h at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, while Li–Cu half cells maintain stable cycling for nearly 480 cycles under the same conditions. Li-LCO cells using MFCE operated at 4.6 V retain 75.8% of their initial capacity after 400 cycles at 0.5C. Even under practical conditions, including 50 µm Li foil, high-loading LCO cathodes of 14 mg cm−2, and an N/P ratio of 3.7, Li-LCO full cells using MFCE can deliver 70.7% capacity retention after 150 cycles. This work demonstrates that electrolyte performance does not simply increase with the degree of fluorination. Rather, an appropriate fluorination degree that balances solvation capability, interfacial compatibility, cost, and sustainability is critical for developing advanced electrolytes for practical high-voltage LMBs.

Results and discussion

CCE was prepared using 1 M LiPF6 in EC/DEC (3/7, v/v), whereas MFCE was formulated using 1 M LiFSI in FEC/BFC (3/7, v/v). LiFSI was selected for MFCE because of its good compatibility with the fluorinated carbonate solvent system, which enables the formation of a homogeneous electrolyte. In addition, FSI− contains fewer F atoms than PF6−, thereby avoiding an excessive increase in the overall fluorine content of MFCE and improving the cost and sustainability profile of the electrolyte. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were conducted to clarify the effect of solvent fluorination on the Li+ coordination environment (Fig. 2a). The binding energies of Li+ with the non-fluorinated carbonate solvents EC and DEC are −2.21 and −2.08 eV, respectively, indicating their comparable Li+ coordination abilities. In comparison, the fluorinated solvents exhibit reduced Li+ affinity, with binding energies of −1.93 eV for Li+-FEC and −1.42 eV for Li+-BFC. The progressively less negative binding energies demonstrate that fluorination weakens the interaction between Li+ and carbonate solvent molecules, with the more highly fluorinated BFC displaying the weakest coordination capability. Notably, both FSI− and PF6− exhibit much stronger Li+ binding than the solvent molecules, with binding energies of −5.30 and −6.00 eV, respectively. Therefore, the weakened Li+–solvent interaction induced by fluorination may facilitate anion involvement in the primary Li+ solvation structure.47 The orbital energy results further reveal the interfacial reactivity of different Li+-coordinated species (Fig. 2b). Compared with Li+-EC and Li+-DEC, Li+-FEC and Li+-BFC possess lower highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) levels, indicating the improved oxidative stability of fluorinated solvent complexes under high-voltage conditions. Meanwhile, their lower lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels suggest that fluorinated solvents are more susceptible to reductive decomposition on the lithium metal surface, which can contribute to the formation of F-containing SEI components. These results indicate that fluorination simultaneously enhances the high-voltage stability of solvent molecules and promotes favorable interfacial reactions at the LMA. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were further conducted to compare the Li+ solvation structures in CCE and MFCE. In CCE, the radial distribution functions (RDFs) of Li+-EC(O) and Li+-DEC(O) show distinct first-shell peaks near 2 Å, and the representative Li+-DEC coordination distance is 1.976 Å (Fig. 2c and S1). These results confirm that Li+ is closely coordinated with the carbonyl oxygen atoms of EC and DEC, forming a compact, solvent-dominated solvation structure. In MFCE, however, the Li+-FSI−(O) RDF exhibits the strongest first-shell peak at approximately 2 Å, indicating direct coordination between Li+ and FSI− (Fig. 2d and S2). Although FEC still participates in Li+ coordination, the Li+-BFC(O) RDF is weak in the first coordination region, and the representative Li+⋯BFC distance reaches 4.266 Å. This result suggests that BFC is largely excluded from the primary Li+ solvation sheath and mainly acts as a weakly coordinating solvent. As a result, MFCE forms a solvation structure mainly composed of FEC and FSI−, with enhanced anion participation compared with CCE. The Raman results further support this conclusion (Fig. 2e and S3–S5). The deconvoluted peaks assigned to solvent-separated ion pair (SSIP), contact ion pair (CIP), and aggregate (AGG) show that SSIP dominates in CCE, indicating that Li+ and PF6− are mostly separated by solvent molecules. In contrast, MFCE exhibits a decreased SSIP fraction and increased CIP and AGG contributions, demonstrating stronger Li+-anion association and the formation of higher-order ion aggregates. Therefore, these results confirm that MFCE effectively weakens Li+–solvent coordination and promotes FSI− entry into. the primary solvation sheath. This anion-associated solvation structure is expected to facilitate anion-derived interfacial reactions.48

Tafel plots were collected to evaluate the interfacial charge-transfer kinetics of Li anodes in different electrolytes. The exchange current density increases from 0.035 mA cm−2 in CCE to 0.096 mA cm−2 in MFCE, indicating accelerated Li+ charge transfer across the MFCE-derived interface (Fig. 3a).49 Li–Li symmetric-cell measurements further confirm the improved interfacial stability enabled by MFCE. At 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, the CCE-based cell exhibits unstable voltage evolution and fails after a rapid increase in polarization after only 250 h, whereas the MFCE-based cell maintains stable Li plating/stripping for over 1200 h with a low overpotential (Fig. 3b). Even under a higher current density and areal capacity of 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2, the CCE-based cell undergoes severe polarization growth and short-circuit failure within approximately 150 h. In contrast, the MFCE-based cell maintains a relatively low overpotential during most of the 500 h test, demonstrating the superior capability of the MFCE-derived interface to sustain repeated Li deposition/dissolution (Fig. 3c). To further assess the interfacial stability after repeated Li plating/stripping, EIS analysis was carried out on Li–Li symmetric cells after 50 cycles. The MFCE-based cell delivered a markedly lower interfacial impedance (RSEI) than the CCE-based counterpart, demonstrating that MFCE promotes the formation of a more ionically conductive and stable interphase for Li+ migration (Fig. S6).50 Li–Cu half cell measurements were further performed to assess the reversibility of Li cycling. The coulombic efficiency (CE) of the CCE-based cell decreases sharply during the initial cycles, reflecting rapid active Li loss and continuous electrolyte decomposition (Fig. 3d). By comparison, the MFCE-based cell delivers a stable CE above 95% for over 480 cycles at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2, confirming markedly improved Li deposition/dissolution reversibility. Additional optimization tests at 1 mA cm−2 and 1 mAh cm−2 reveal that the FEC/BFC electrolyte with a volume ratio of 3 : 7 exhibits the best cycling stability, maintaining stable operation for over 190 cycles (Fig. S7). This optimized solvent ratio likely provides a suitable balance between the solvating ability of FEC and the weak-solvation character of BFC, thereby promoting anion participation in the Li+ solvation sheath while maintaining sufficient salt dissociation. The chemical composition of the SEI was investigated by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). The C 1s spectra reveal pronounced differences in the SEI composition formed in CCE and MFCE (Fig. 3e and S8). In contrast, these carbon-containing species are markedly suppressed in the MFCE-derived SEI, suggesting a reduced contribution of solvent-derived organic components. Together with the enhanced LiF-related signals observed in the F 1s spectra, these results demonstrate that the fluorinated carbonate components effectively modulate interfacial chemistry. Owing to their altered electronic structures and lower LUMO energies, FEC and BFC preferentially participate in reductive decomposition pathways that promote the formation of fluorine-rich inorganic species, thereby contributing to a more inorganic-rich and mechanically robust SEI.51,52 The F 1s spectra show that the CCE-derived SEI contains both P–F and LiF species, whereas the MFCE-derived SEI is dominated by a strong LiF signal (Fig. 3f). Such a LiF-enriched SEI can enhance interfacial passivation and facilitate uniform Li deposition. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and atomic force microscopy (AFM) analyses further reveal the beneficial effect of MFCE on Li deposition morphology. Cross-sectional SEM images show that Li deposited in CCE exhibits a thick and porous morphology with a thickness of approximately 37.1 µm (Fig. 3g and S9), whereas Li deposited in MFCE forms a thinner and denser morphology with a thickness of approximately 19.3 µm (Fig. 3j and S9). AFM-derived Young's modulus maps indicate that the CCE-derived SEI is mechanically weaker and organic-rich, whereas the MFCE-derived SEI is mechanically more robust and inorganic-rich. Consistently (Fig. 3h and k), AFM topography shows that the CCE-derived Li surface is rough, with a high Rq value of 74.4 nm (Fig. 3i), whereas the MFCE-derived Li surface is much smoother, with a reduced Rq value of 16.6 nm (Fig. 3l). These findings indicate that MFCE simultaneously optimizes the composition and mechanical properties of the SEI, thereby enabling compact Li deposition and highly reversible Li plating/stripping.53,54

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements reveal that MFCE exhibits lower oxidation currents than CCE at various potentials, indicating its superior oxidative stability under high-voltage conditions (Fig. S10). Accordingly, the rate capability of Li-LCO cells using CCE and MFCE was evaluated within a voltage window of 3.0–4.6 V (Fig. 4a). The CCE-based cell delivered discharge capacities of approximately 190.0, 174.2, 155.7, and 112.7 mAh g−1 at 0.5, 1, 2, and 5C, respectively. In comparison, the MFCE-based cell achieved higher discharge capacities of approximately 195.1, 182.5, 163.1, and 128.4 mAh g−1 at the corresponding rates. The corresponding voltage–capacity profiles further confirm that the MFCE-based cell delivers larger discharge capacities with reduced voltage polarization at different rates (Fig. 4b and c), suggesting more favorable reaction kinetics during high-voltage Li-LCO operation. Although MFCE shows lower bulk ionic conductivity than CCE, it achieves superior rate capability (Fig. S11). This behavior suggests that the improved cell kinetics are primarily governed by interfacial transport rather than bulk ion conduction alone. The inorganic-rich SEI formed in MFCE facilitates Li+ transport across the interface, thereby compensating for the reduced bulk ionic conductivity and enabling improved rate performance. Long-term cycling tests were then conducted at 0.5C within 3.0–4.6 V (Fig. 4d and S12, S13). The CCE-based cell underwent gradual capacity decay and retained only 73.0% of its initial capacity after 250 cycles. In contrast, the MFCE-based cell maintained a capacity retention of 75.8% after 400 cycles, with the coulombic efficiency remaining close to 100% throughout cycling. These results demonstrate that MFCE can more effectively sustain repeated charge–discharge processes under high-voltage conditions. To distinguish the contribution of the lithium salt from that of the solvent system, a LiFSI in EC/DEC electrolyte was introduced as an additional control. Relative to the conventional CCE, the LiFSI in EC/DEC electrolyte showed substantially poorer cycling stability, suggesting that the enhanced high-voltage performance of MFCE is mainly governed by the fluorinated solvent system rather than by the lithium salt alone (Fig. S16). Furthermore, to verify the broader applicability of the MFCE design, Li-NCM811 full cells were assembled and evaluated under a high cutoff voltage of 4.6 V. The MFCE-based Li-NCM811 cell delivered stable cycling over 100 cycles, confirming the favorable compatibility of MFCE with NCM811 cathodes (Fig. S17). To further assess its applicability under practical cell configurations, Li-LCO cells were assembled with a high LCO loading of 14 mg cm−2, a thin Li foil of 50 µm, an N/P ratio of 3.7, and a lean electrolyte amount corresponding to an E/C ratio of 2.84 µL mg−1 (Fig. 4e and S14, S15). Under these conditions, the MFCE-based cell retained 70.7% of its initial capacity after 150 cycles at 0.2C/0.5C. By contrast, the CCE-based cell suffered rapid failure after the initial cycles, with its areal capacity sharply decreasing to a low level within several tens of cycles. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of MFCE in enabling stable cycling of high-voltage LMBs under practical conditions. To evaluate the overall impact of fluorination degree on electrolyte cost, sustainability, and electrochemical performance, we compared MFCE with several representative highly fluorinated electrolyte systems recently reported in the literature, focusing on fluorine content and full cell performance (Table S1). Highly fluorinated electrolytes are generally effective in enhancing cycling stability by promoting the formation of LiF-rich interphases; however, excessive fluorination may inevitably increase material cost and reduce the sustainability of electrolyte formulations. By contrast, MFCE achieves favorable full cell performance using moderately fluorinated components while substantially reducing the overall fluorine content. This comparison highlights the advantage of moderate fluorination as a balanced electrolyte design strategy that preserves the beneficial interfacial chemistry of fluorinated electrolytes while mitigating cost, resource, and environmental concerns.

Focused ion beam-scanning electron microscopy (FIB-SEM) characterization was performed to investigate the morphological evolution of LCO cathodes after high-voltage cycling. As shown in Fig. 5a and S18, the LCO electrode cycled in CCE exhibits pronounced surface cracks on the secondary particles, indicating severe mechanical and interfacial degradation during repeated charge/discharge processes. In contrast, the LCO electrode cycled in MFCE retains a more intact particle morphology, with no obvious cracks observed in the selected region (Fig. 5b and S18). These results suggest that MFCE effectively mitigates cathode degradation and helps preserve the morphological integrity of LCO under high-voltage operation.55 The structural stability of LCO was further evaluated by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The pristine LCO electrode displays a characteristic (003) reflection at 18.66° (Fig. 5c and S19). After cycling in CCE, this peak shifts to a lower angle of 18.49°, whereas the MFCE-cycled electrode shows only a slight shift to 18.63°. The lower-angle shift of the (003) reflection indicates enlarged interlayer spacing in the layered LCO structure. Therefore, the more pronounced peak shift observed for the CCE-cycled electrode reflects more severe lattice perturbation, which is consistent with the surface cracking observed by FIB-SEM. In contrast, the nearly retained (003) peak position of the MFCE-cycled electrode demonstrates that MFCE more effectively preserves the layered structure of LCO during high-voltage cycling.56 Depth-profiling XPS was employed to investigate the depth-dependent elemental distribution and chemical composition of the CEI. The CCE-derived interphase exhibited a consistently high C/F ratio throughout Ar+ sputtering, indicating the predominance of carbonaceous species within the CEI (Fig. S20). In contrast, the MFCE-derived CEI showed a pronounced fluorine-rich composition across the sputtering depth range, suggesting the formation of an inorganic fluoride-enriched interphase (Fig. S21). F 1s XPS depth profiling further confirmed this difference. The CCE-cycled LCO electrode was mainly characterized by P–F species and only a weak LiF signal (Fig. 5d), whereas the MFCE-cycled electrode exhibited a much stronger and more persistent LiF signal, together with a detectable S–F contribution (Fig. 5e). These results indicate that MFCE promotes the construction of a LiF-rich CEI, likely arising from the decomposition of FSI− and fluorinated carbonate solvents. Such a robust inorganic-rich interphase effectively stabilizes the high-voltage LCO surface, suppressing surface degradation and mitigating structural distortion.57Fig. 5f and g schematically illustrate the distinct interfacial evolution pathways of LCO cathodes in CCE and MFCE. In CCE, continuous decomposition of EC/DEC solvents and LiPF6 leads to the formation of an unstable CEI dominated by organic species and P–F-containing decomposition products (Fig. 5f). In contrast, in MFCE, the coordinated participation of FEC/BFC solvents and FSI− anions promotes the construction of a LiF-rich CEI (Fig. 5g), which more effectively stabilizes the LCO/electrolyte interface and contributes to the improved structural stability of LCO during cycling.

Conclusions

In summary, we developed medium-fluorination carbonate electrolytes (MFCE) based on monofluorinated FEC and difluorinated BFC as the main solvents for high-voltage LMBs. Unlike conventional fluorine-free carbonate electrolytes, which suffer from insufficient interfacial stability, and highly fluorinated electrolytes, which rely on excessive fluorine incorporation, MFCE employs a moderate fluorination strategy to achieve a balanced combination of electrochemical performance, cost, and sustainability. We reveal that moderate fluorination weakens the ion–dipole interactions between Li+ and carbonate solvent molecules while maintaining sufficient lithium-salt dissolution capability. As a result, FEC and BFC exhibit weak-solvation characteristics, which promote anion entry into the first solvation sheath and promote the formation of anion-derived interfaces on both electrodes. Consequently, Li–Cu cells using MFCE deliver stable cycling for over 480 cycles at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2. Moreover, Li-LCO full cells under practical conditions, including a high LCO loading of 14 mg cm−2, 50 µm Li foil, and an N/P ratio of 3.7, maintain 70.7% capacity retention after 150 cycles at a cutoff voltage of 4.6 V. This work demonstrates that increasing the degree of fluorination is not the only route to high-performance electrolytes. Instead, a balanced electrolyte design that simultaneously considers fluorine content, cost, and sustainability provides a promising direction for the development of advanced electrolytes.
