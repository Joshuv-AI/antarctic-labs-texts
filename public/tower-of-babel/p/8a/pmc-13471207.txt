A proactive large platelet–driven screening strategy for the early recognition of MYH9-related disease: a Chinese prospective cohort study
Authors: Wenlan Chen, Abdul Rehman Arif, Yajie Ding, Limin Wang, Li Cai, Muneeba Arif, Mei Xue, Heng Mei, Yadan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate diagnosis of MYH9-related disease (MYH9-RD) is frequently impeded by a reactive process. Clinical suspicion typically arises only following immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) treatment failure, family history identification, or emergence of extra-hematological manifestations. However, de novo variants or delayed syndromic onset may obscure these indicators, leading to misdiagnosis as ITP and unnecessary immunosuppression.ObjectivesThis study prospectively evaluated a multiparameter platelet size trigger as an accessible screening entry point for early MYH9-RD recognition, independent of a family history, treatment refractoriness, or syndromic features.MethodsConsecutive patients with persistent, unexplained thrombocytopenia were screened using predefined thresholds for mean platelet volume (≥ 14.55 fL), platelet-large cell ratio (≥ 58%), or manual identification of giant platelets when automated values were unobtainable. Individuals meeting any criterion underwent targeted high-throughput sequencing for inherited thrombocytopenia genes (including MYH9), with subsequent confirmation by immunofluorescence.ResultsAmong 42 screened patients, 7 (16.7%) were diagnosed with MYH9-RD, including 2 with novel MYH9 variants in the coiled-coil domain, supported by blood smear findings (including giant platelets and neutrophil inclusions), bioinformatics assessment, and structural-modeling data. All 7 patients were diagnosed within 1 month following the screening, avoiding unnecessary ITP-directed therapy. The composite trigger (mean platelet volume, platelet-large cell ratio, and smear-based morphology) provided higher diagnostic yield than any single parameter alone.ConclusionThis streamlined 3-step strategy provides a practical, potentially resource-conscious pathway for early MYH9-RD diagnosis, 1 better aligned with current clinical practice in China than the existing diagnostic paradigms.

Introduction

MYH9-related disease (MYH9-RD) represents a group of autosomal-dominant syndromic macrothrombocytopenias caused by pathogenic variants in the MYH9 gene, which encodes nonmuscle myosin heavy chain (NMMHC)-IIA [1,2]. The hallmark of MYH9-RD is congenital macrothrombocytopenia, which can be accompanied by extra-hematological manifestations, including sensorineural hearing loss (SNHL), nephropathy, and presenile cataracts (for which the risk is influenced by genotype) [2,3]. Despite being 1 of the most common forms of inherited thrombocytopenia, the estimated incidence of MYH9-RD is ∼3.75 per million; accordingly, clinician awareness is often limited, and the disorder is significantly underrecognized in clinical practice [4]. MYH9-RD is frequently misdiagnosed as immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), resulting in inappropriate and potentially harmful treatment with corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, or even splenectomy [5,6]. Compounding this diagnostic challenge is the high rate of de novo mutations, reported in 20% to 30% of cases in European cohorts and > 50% of cases in Chinese cohorts [7,8]. This high frequency of spontaneous mutations obscures the familial inheritance pattern, substantially increasing the likelihood of misdiagnosis.

For > 2 decades, the diagnosis of MYH9-RD has largely followed a reactive pathway, in which clinical suspicion typically arises after failure of ITP therapy, identification of a relevant family history, or emergence of extra-hematological manifestations. However, these indicators may be absent in cases of de novo variants or may appear later in the disease course, leading to diagnostic delays, misclassification as ITP, and unnecessary exposure to immunosuppressive therapy [5,6,[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. A more effective diagnostic strategy should therefore shift from late clinical recognition to proactive screening, using a simple, inexpensive, and widely available trigger applicable to patients with unexplained thrombocytopenia regardless of a family history, treatment response, or syndromic features. Conceptually, this approach parallels colorectal cancer screening, in which a positive fecal occult blood test functions as an initial trigger for definitive colonoscopic evaluation [14].

Although neutrophil NMMHC-IIA inclusions represent a highly specific and pathognomonic hallmark of MYH9-RD, their detection most often requires immunofluorescence (IF) and is therefore better suited for diagnostic confirmation than for first-line screening in routine hematology practice [12,15,16]. Macrothrombocytopenia is an inherent characteristic of MYH9-RD. Several studies have confirmed that platelet size parameters, such as the mean platelet volume (MPV), platelet-large cell ratio (P-LCR), and directly measured mean platelet diameter (MPD), can effectively differentiate MYH9-RD from ITP [5,17,18]. Although MPV and P-LCR may be underestimated when using automated hematology analyzers in the presence of giant platelets, they are inexpensive and readily accessible indicators, making them ideal candidates for first-line screening triggers [19]. A Japanese study using an MPV cutoff of >14.4 fL identified previously undiagnosed MYH9-RD and Bernard–Soulier syndrome cases among patients with an initial ITP diagnosis [20]. Consistent with these findings, our prior retrospective analysis has demonstrated that MPV and P-LCR possess strong discriminatory power for differentiating inherited macrothrombocytopenias (IMTs; including MYH9-RD) from ITP and from other acquired thrombocytopenias (ATs) with large platelets, such as myelodysplastic syndrome [21].

Building on this evidence and our prior retrospective findings, this prospective study evaluates a proactive, large platelet–driven screening strategy for the early recognition of MYH9-RD. This strategy uses a composite trigger of routinely available platelet size parameters (MPV, P-LCR, and smear-based identification of giant platelets) to prioritize targeted sequencing in patients with persistent, unexplained thrombocytopenia. Subsequent confirmation by the NMMHC-IIA IF assay provides definitive phenotypic validation of MYH9-RD. By implementing and validating this streamlined, potentially resource-conscious approach, this study aims to provide a practical diagnostic framework tailored to clinical practice in China, thereby reducing misdiagnosis and treatment delays.

Methods

Study design and patient enrolment

This single-center, prospective study was conducted at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College of Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). Between January 2024 and January 2025, consecutive inpatients and outpatients with unexplained thrombocytopenia were enrolled. Unexplained thrombocytopenia was operationally defined as a platelet count < 150 × 109/L persisting without an identifiable cause after a standardized pre-enrolment workup. This workup comprised a complete blood count with differential, peripheral blood smear, liver and renal function tests, hepatobiliary and splenic ultrasonography, an autoimmune panel (antinuclear antibody, anti–double-stranded DNA, and antiphospholipid antibodies), bone marrow examination, viral serology (hepatitis B and C viruses, HIV, Epstein–Barr virus, and cytomegalovirus), and a comprehensive medication review. On this basis, secondary causes of thrombocytopenia including chronic liver disease–related thrombocytopenia, connective tissue disease–related ITP, drug-induced thrombocytopenia, active infection, and hematological malignancy were systematically excluded prior to enrolment.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) persistent thrombocytopenia (platelet count < 150 × 109/L) for >3 months; (2) an unclear diagnosis or inadequate response to prior ITP-immunosuppressive therapy; and (3) fulfillment of at least 1 platelet size criterion, MPV of ≥ 14.55 fL, P-LCR of ≥ 58%, or manual identification of giant platelets on peripheral blood smear when automated parameters were unavailable. The exclusion criterion was thrombocytopenia attributable to identifiable secondary causes as listed earlier. During the same period, age- and sex-matched patients with confirmed ITP and healthy controls were enrolled at a 1:1 ratio for comparative analyses.

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions, as well as applicable national guidelines for epidemiological research. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approval No: 2024-0649). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to enrolment.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data, including demographic information, initial clinical manifestations, timing of symptom onset, platelet counts, and a detailed bleeding history (encompassing menorrhagia, epistaxis, and visceral hemorrhage), were systematically collected from medical records. The severity of bleeding episodes was graded according to the World Health Organization bleeding assessment criteria [22,23].

Therapeutic interventions, including the specific type and duration of all administered treatments, such as glucocorticoids, intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG), thrombopoietin receptor agonists, and traditional Chinese medicine, were systematically documented. Renal involvement was assessed and categorized into isolated proteinuria, chronic kidney disease, end-stage renal failure (ESRF), and a history of renal transplantation. Proteinuria was defined as urinary protein excretion of > 0.5 g/24 h. Chronic kidney disease was diagnosed based on a glomerular filtration rate (GFR) of < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 persisting for over 3 months. ESRF was defined as a GFR f < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2 or the initiation of renal replacement therapy. SNHL was confirmed by pure-tone audiometry, with a diagnosis established when hearing thresholds exceeded 20 dB at ≥3 consecutive frequencies. Cataracts were identified through a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination documenting lens opacities.

Next-generation sequencing and variant analysis

Peripheral blood samples were collected, and targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) was conducted at Wuhan Union Hospital using a custom panel covering coding exons and ±10-bp flanking introns of 308 genes involved in bleeding, thrombosis, fibrinolysis, and platelet biology (including 74 platelet-associated genes). Genomic DNA (200 ng/sample) was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), and PE150 paired-end libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NextSeq 550AR platform (Illumina). In-house quality control tools were used for preprocessing, followed by alignment to the GRCh37 human reference genome using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner software package. Variants (single-nucleotide variants and insertions/deletions) were called using MuTect2 and annotated using ANNOtate VARiation. Quality thresholds included a minimum average on-target depth of ≥ 500× and read/base quality scores of > 30. High-throughput sequencing was performed on the Illumina NextSeq 550 platform. Bioinformatics analyses of sequencing data were performed to identify potential pathogenic variants in thrombocytopenia-associated genes. Identified variants were validated using Sanger sequencing and subjected to a segregation analysis using data for available family members. All sequence variants were interpreted and classified according to the standards and guidelines established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) [24]. The full gene panel is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Bioinformatic predictions and structural modeling

The pathogenic potential of novel variants was evaluated using an integrated computational workflow. This approach combined multiple in silico prediction tools with advanced structural modeling to assess the functional and conformational impact of the identified mutations. Detailed methodology is provided in the Supplementary Data.

Platelet analyses

All enrolled patients underwent the following platelet analyses.

Manual platelet counting

Whole-blood platelet counts were performed on EDTA-anticoagulated venous blood samples. Following dilution in 1% ammonium oxalate solution, platelets were manually counted through optical microscopy using an improved Neubauer hemacytometer, in accordance with the standardized method recommended by the International Council for Standardization in Haematology [25].

Automated platelet parameters

Platelet count, MPV, and P-LCR were determined within 2 hours of sample collection using an automated hematology analyzer (BC-6800; Mindray Bio-Medical Electronics). The system uses 2-dimensional laser light scatter technology for platelet identification and analysis. All measurements were performed using the manufacturer’s standard settings for a complete blood count analysis. When MPV and P-LCR values were unavailable owing to the presence of giant platelets, manual microscopic examination of blood smears was performed to assess platelet morphology. Giant platelets were graded according to the International Council for Standardization in Haematology criteria for platelet morphology [26]. Patients were considered trigger-positive when giant platelets were graded as 2+ or 3+, defined as ≥ 10% of platelets exceeding the diameter of a normal erythrocyte (∼7 μm) on a well-spread Wright–Giemsa-stained field, and these patients subsequently proceeded to NGS testing.

The screening thresholds used in this study (MPV ≥ 14.55 fL; P-LCR ≥ 58%) were derived from our prior retrospective receiver-operating characteristic analysis on the same Mindray BC-6800 platform, where they optimally discriminated IMTs from ATs and healthy controls [21]. As these parameters are analyzer-dependent, the cutoffs are not directly transferable across instruments; adopting centers should establish platform-specific thresholds by local receiver-operating characteristic analysis of IMTs against ATs and healthy controls.

Platelet diameter measurement

Peripheral blood smears were prepared from capillary blood without an anticoagulant. Following Wright–Giemsa staining, platelet diameters were measured using software-assisted image analysis (DI-60; Sysmex Corporation). For each platelet, the maximum diameter was recorded. The MPD was calculated from measurements of 200 individual platelets per sample.

IF detection of abnormal NMMHC-IIA localization

IF staining for NMMHC-IIA was performed as a confirmatory test on peripheral blood smears from all patients in whom NGS had identified a MYH9 variant, together with representative healthy controls. The smears were air-dried and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes at room temperature (∼20-25 °C), followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min under the same conditions. Nonspecific binding sites were blocked by incubation with 5% normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature. The samples were then incubated with a primary antibody against NMMHC-IIA (rabbit recombinant monoclonal antibody, ab138498; Abcam) at a dilution of 1:500 for 12 hours at 4 °C. After washing, the slides were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated goat antirabbit immunoglobulin G secondary antibody (ab150077; Abcam) at a dilution of 1:1000 for 12 hours at 4 °C. Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole for 40 minutes at room temperature. Finally, the slides were mounted with antifade mounting medium and visualized using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse E800).

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as means ± SD, whereas nonnormally distributed variables are shown as median values with corresponding interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-squared test or, when expected cell counts were <5, Fisher exact test. Between-group comparisons of nonnormally distributed continuous data were performed using the Mann–Whitney U-test. All tests were 2-tailed, and statistical significance was set at P < .05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM), and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software).

Clinical characteristics of patients with MYH9 variants

Clinical characteristics of the 7 patients (5 males and 2 females), including 1 child and 6 adults from 7 provinces and ranging in age from 3 to 35 years, are summarized in Table 1. The median interval between detection of thrombocytopenia and genetic diagnosis was 7 years (range, 0.3-23 years). Six patients were diagnosed initially with ITP, and 5 received ITP-directed treatment with glucocorticoids, IVIG, recombinant human thrombopoietin, thrombopoietin receptor agonists, or rituximab. Five patients reported bleeding symptoms, including petechiae, ecchymosis, epistaxis, gingival bleeding, or gastrointestinal bleeding, with World Health Organization bleeding scores of 0 to 2.

Three adult patients had extra-hematological findings. At 26 years of age, patient 7 had isolated proteinuria with normal serum creatinine. Patient 13 developed proteinuria at 16 years of age, showed increased creatinine by 20 years of age, and progressed to ESRF within 1 year. In patient 24, bilateral high-frequency SNHL was detected after genetic diagnosis.

Patient 24

A 19-year-old man had thrombocytopenia with platelet counts of 10 to 30 × 109/L and intermittent purpura since 12 years of age. He was initially diagnosed with ITP and treated with glucocorticoids, IVIG, and traditional Chinese medicine, with poor responses. In November 2024, he presented with epistaxis and ecchymosis during an upper respiratory tract infection. He was enrolled after laboratory testing showing a platelet count of 10 × 109/L, MPV of 14.7 fL, and P-LCR of 58%; following the administration of eltrombopag (50 mg/d) for 3 weeks, his platelet count rose to 30 × 109/L. NGS identified a heterozygous MYH9 c.G4550T (p.G1517V) variant. A peripheral-smear analysis showed giant platelets, and IF confirmed neutrophil inclusions (Figure 2C). Pure-tone audiometry demonstrated bilateral high-frequency SNHL. Renal function and ophthalmologic evaluation results were normal. Pedigree analysis showed normal platelet counts and no extra-hematological findings in his parents and brother. The variant was absent in all family members, consistent with a de novo mutation (Figure 3A).

All patients met at least 1 predefined criterion for macrothrombocytopenia. Four patients (patients 4, 6, 24, and 35) had MPV values exceeding the 14.55-fL threshold. Three patients (patients 6, 7, and 24) had P-LCR values of ≥ 58%. Patients 6 and 24 fulfilled both MPV and P-LCR criteria. For patients 1 and 13, MPV and P-LCR data were lacking; however, a peripheral-smear analysis confirmed numerous giant platelets.

MPD values across all 7 patients ranged from 2.84 to 4.08 μm (Tables 2 and 3), exceeding the healthy control value of 2.6 μm (P <.05). Platelet diameter distribution analyses revealed a significantly elevated proportion of large and giant platelets (diameter ≥ 4 μm), ranging from 14% to 55%, among patients. This proportion was higher than that for the healthy control group (4.6%) and a reference ITP cohort (8.5%; P < .05). Discussion

In this prospective cohort of 42 patients with persistent unexplained thrombocytopenia, a composite screening strategy based on multiple routinely available platelet size parameters achieved a diagnostic yield of 16.7% (7/42) for MYH9-RD, including 2 with novel coiled-coil domain variants. Although platelet size parameters, peripheral smear review, NMMHC-IIA IF, and molecular testing have each been employed in the diagnosis of MYH9-RD and inherited macrothrombocytopenias [5,6,9,12,[15], [16], [17], [18]]. To our knowledge, this study is the first to prospectively consolidate multiple platelet size parameters, smear morphology, NGS, and NMMHC-IIA IF into a single platelet size-triggered workflow in a Chinese cohort. This approach enabled earlier recognition based on routine platelet size abnormalities, rather than reactive clinical indicators such as family history, ITP refractoriness, or syndromic features. Patient 4, who had no relevant family history, was diagnosed within just 4 months of the initial clinical detection of thrombocytopenia based on the presence of elevated MPV levels (15.2 fL), thereby avoiding unnecessary and potentially harmful treatments. The other 6 patients experienced a relatively long period of misdiagnosis (2-23 years) but were diagnosed with MYH9-RD within 1 month after referral to our institution.

Macrothrombocytopenia is a congenital hallmark of MYH9-RD and provides a rational basis for a large platelet–driven screening strategy [5,17]. Platelet size parameters differ in their analytical advantages and limitations. MPV and P-LCR are readily available markers; however, their reliability is limited when giant platelets cause underestimation or prevent automated reporting [19]. MPD offers greater accuracy but requires time-consuming manual image-based assessment and is not routinely performed [18]. To address these limitations and build on our previous research [21], we applied a composite trigger—MPV of ≥ 14.55 fL, P-LCR ≥ 58%, or manual identification of giant platelets on smear—to identify patients with any size parameter-based abnormality. This multiparameter approach yielded a diagnostic positivity rate of 16.7% (7/42) in our cohort, which is higher than the estimate of 9.1% (1/11) reported in a Japanese study relying solely on MPV [20]. Among the 7 identified cases, when applied individually, MPV, P-LCR, and smear review detected only 9.5%, 7.1%, and 4.8% of the cohort as positive for MYH9-RD, respectively. In contrast, their combined application markedly raised the positivity rate to 16.7%, substantially exceeding that achieved by any single parameter.

Extra-hematological manifestations may provide diagnostic clues but are associated with more common system-specific disorders and tend to appear later in the disease course [3]. In our cohort, proteinuria in patients 7 and 13 was not attributed to MYH9-RD at the time of symptom onset, and 1 patient (patient 7) subsequently progressed to ESRF. Patient 24 was similarly unaware of high-frequency SNHL until testing was pursued following genetic detection. These findings underscore the value of a multiparameter approach, as platelet size parameter abnormalities offer a more consistent and earlier indicator of MYH9-RD than delayed-onset syndromic features.

Large-platelet parameters are not specific to MYH9-RD and can be seen in other IMTs, including Bernard–Soulier syndrome. Therefore, they trigger targeted screening, rather than replace a definitive diagnostic test. High-throughput sequencing techniques using targeted gene panels are increasingly being applied for screening various IMTs in patients with suspected inherited platelet disorders (IPDs) [6,9]. This molecular testing approach delivers valuable genotypic insights and facilitates genotype-guided risk assessment [3]. Our 16.7% diagnostic yield based on NGS for MYH9-RD is similar to, although slightly higher than the 12.4% (15/121) reported by Rabbolini et al. [6] in a selected Australasian cohort and the 8.5% (7/82) reported by Bastida et al. [9] in suspected IPDs. Meanwhile, NMMHC-IIA IF on blood smears has been reported to achieve near-100% diagnostic sensitivity and positive predictive value for MYH9-RD in patients with suspected IMTs [12,15,16]. Moreover, 3 prospective multicenter studies have shown that a 13-antibody IF panel accurately predicted the diagnosis of the most frequent IPDs, including MYH9-RD, BSS, and other IMTs [12,15,27]. Consequently, integrating NGS-based genotyping and IF-based phenotypic confirmation furnished robust diagnostic support [11]. As in our study, IF was applied to all 7 MYH9-positive patients and served as a strong diagnostic marker for the 2 novel variants. Our workflow used NGS prior to IF, as targeted NGS is more widely accessible in Chinese clinical practice through centralized sequencing platforms and mailed-sample systems, while standardized NMMHC-IIA IF diagnostics remain unavailable in most specialized hematology laboratories.

MYH9 variants encompass a wide mutational spectrum, and the variants identified in our cohort align with this diversity. Although all 7 patients were heterozygous and most fulfilled ACMG pathogenicity criteria, the 2 novel variants, p.K1674Q and p.G1517V, were identified as likely pathogenic through the convergence of clinical features, bioinformatic predictions, and structural modeling, with neutrophil inclusions providing additional corroboration. Both variants localize to the coiled-coil domain of nonmuscle myosin IIA, a region essential for filament assembly and cytoskeletal organization [2]. Coiled-coil domain variants are generally associated with milder thrombocytopenia and fewer syndromic manifestations than motor-domain variants [3]. However, significant phenotypic heterogeneity exists even within this domain, complicating genotype–phenotype correlations [3,28]. This phenotypic variability was evident in our cohort. The de novo p.G1517V variant was associated with severe thrombocytopenia, requiring thrombopoietin receptor agonist support, and SNHL, consistent with a previous case [29]. In contrast, p.K1674Q was linked to mild thrombocytopenia without extra-hematological features in both the proband and his father. Structural modeling suggested that p.G1517V, located on the protein surface, exerted greater effects on local hydrophobicity and coiled-coil stability than p.K1674Q (Supplementary Data), consistent with its more pronounced clinical expression.

Our study had some limitations. First, its single-center design may introduce referral bias, and the relatively small sample size warrants validation in larger, multicenter cohorts. Second, owing to the absence of a formal control or comparison arm, our conclusions regarding the benefits of early diagnosis were based on comparisons with the results of historical or published cohorts. Third, the MPV and P-LCR cutoffs we applied are instrument specific and cannot be generalized. Future efforts could focus on leveraging artificial intelligence to simplify and standardize MPD measurement, enabling its use as a universal trigger for multicenter studies. Furthermore, the relatively high positivity rate reflects our enriched, screen-positive cohort and does not represent population-level performance; consequently, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value cannot be estimated from the present design. Validation of the operating characteristics of this approach will require future cohorts in which all patients with unexplained thrombocytopenia undergo NGS irrespective of size-trigger status. Finally, longitudinal follow-up is essential to delineate the long-term risks of renal and auditory complications in patients with these novel variants, given the progressive nature of MYH9-RD.

Conclusion

In summary, this prospective study delineates a large platelet–driven, 3e-step diagnostic strategy for the early recognition of MYH9-RD (Figure 4), conceptually analogous to the international standard for colorectal cancer screening. The pathway begins with multiple routine platelet size abnormalities (elevated MPV or P-LCR or giant platelets on manual smear review) in patients with unexplained thrombocytopenia, independent of a family history, refractoriness to ITP-immunosuppressive therapy, or syndromic manifestations; trigger-positive patients then proceed to targeted NGS for identification of pathogenic MYH9 variants; and the diagnosis is finally confirmed through the integration of clinical information, bioinformatic and structural-modeling analyses, peripheral-smear morphology, and NMMHC-IIA IF.
