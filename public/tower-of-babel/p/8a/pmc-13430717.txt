Nano-armed bcg delivers Cu-Fe MOF to trigger dual cell death in bladder cancer
Authors: Yang Li, Fangdie Ye, Gang Liu, Lili Cheng, Yang Song, Shijie Zhang, Bo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntravesical Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) perfusion is widely regarded as a standard clinical intervention for non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer. Despite its established efficacy, this treatment is associated with a high tumor recurrence rate of approximately 50%, highlighting a significant clinical challenge. To address this limitation, combination strategies integrating BCG with other antitumor modalities have emerged as a promising direction for enhancing therapeutic outcomes. In this study, we engineered a novel “super-BCG” formulation by functionalizing BCG with a copper–iron metal–organic framework (CuFe MOF). This composite system leverages the natural tropism of BCG toward bladder tumor sites to achieve targeted delivery of the MOF directly into the tumor microenvironment. Once localized, the CuFe MOF induces synergistic cuproptosis and ferroptosis, two distinct forms of regulated cell death, thereby overcoming the constraints of monotherapeutic approaches that rely on a single death mechanism. This dual induction not only promotes robust tumor cell death but also potentiates antitumor immune activation by stimulating immunogenic cell death. Furthermore, the MOF works in concert with BCG’s inherent immunoadjuvant properties, resulting in a comprehensive enhancement of antitumor immunity and significantly improved immunotherapeutic efficacy. In summary, we have developed a nano-armed BCG-based therapeutic platform that integrates targeted drug delivery with dual-mode cell death induction and immune activation, offering a potent and versatile strategy for the treatment of bladder cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04590-0.Keywords: Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG), Bladder Cancer Immunotherapy, Metal–Organic Framework (CuFe MOF), Cuproptosis and Ferroptosis, Targeted Drug Delivery

Introduction

Bladder cancer ranks among the most common malignancies of the urinary system, with non-muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) representing a substantial proportion of newly diagnosed cases [1]. The mainstay of treatment for high-risk NMIBC involves transurethral resection followed by intravesical immunotherapy with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) [2]. BCG functions as an immunomodulator, activating both innate and adaptive immune responses within the bladder mucosa, thereby reducing tumor recurrence and progression [3]. For decades, this regimen has been the gold standard, demonstrating significant clinical benefit [4]. However, despite its widespread use, recurrence rates remain unacceptably high, approaching 50% within five years [5]. Moreover, a subset of patients either fails to respond initially or develops tolerance over time, leading to disease persistence and risk of progression to muscle-invasive stages [6]. These limitations underscore the urgent need for more effective and durable therapeutic strategies.

The high recurrence rate associated with BCG monotherapy highlights several clinical and mechanistic challenges [7]. BCG’s efficacy is largely dependent on the host’s immune status and the tumor microenvironment (TME), which often becomes immunosuppressive and resistant over time [8]. Additionally, BCG primarily induces apoptosis and immune activation through a single pathway, which may be insufficient for complete tumor eradication [9]. Consequently, combination strategies that enhance BCG’s antitumor immunity while introducing complementary cytotoxic mechanisms have gained significant interest [10]. Recent advances in nanomedicine and immunotherapy have enabled the development of hybrid systems designed to co-deliver immune potentiators and cytotoxic agents directly to tumor sites [11–15]. Yet, achieving efficient targeted delivery while stimulating sustained immunogenic cell death remains a major hurdle in improving bladder cancer therapy.

Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) are porous hybrid nanomaterials composed of metal cluster nodes and organic linkers, offering high surface area, tunable porosity, excellent drug-loading capacity, and stimuli-responsive release [16, 17]. MOFs enable synergistic combinations of immunotherapy with photodynamic therapy (PDT), photothermal therapy (PTT), radiotherapy, chemodynamic therapy (CDT), and sonodynamic therapy. Dual-MOF nanoplatforms (e.g., ZIF-8-based systems) have been designed for simultaneous tumor-targeted treatment and dendritic cell-targeted immunomodulation [18, 19]. MOFs functionalized with anti-CD47 antibodies and loaded with STING agonists block the “don’t eat me” signal while activating innate immunity [20], achieving significant tumor growth inhibition in melanoma models [21, 22]. Despite the significant progress of existing MOF platforms in combination therapy and immunomodulation [23], most of them lack a live carrier such as BCG that possesses both natural tumor tropism and potent immunoadjuvant activity [24], rendering it difficult to achieve precise tumor-targeted delivery, synergistic induction of multiple cell death pathways (e.g., cuproptosis and ferroptosis), and comprehensive remodeling of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [25].

In this study, we designed and synthesized a novel “super-BCG” formulation by functionalizing BCG with a bimetallic copper-iron metal–organic framework (CuFe-MOF). This composite system leverages BCG’s natural affinity for tumor cells to achieve precise delivery of CuFe-MOF nanoparticles into the tumor microenvironment. Once internalized, the MOF framework disassembles in the acidic TME, releasing copper and iron ions that synergistically induce cuproptosis and ferroptosis—two novel forms of regulated cell death that disrupt redox homeostasis and amplify lipid peroxidation. This dual induction not only bypasses the apoptotic resistance commonly seen in bladder cancer but also promotes immunogenic cell death (ICD), releasing tumor-associated antigens and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that enhance dendritic cell maturation and T-cell infiltration. Furthermore, the presence of BCG acts as a potent immune adjuvant, augmenting the activation of local and systemic antitumor immunity. Together, this nano-armed BCG platform integrates multidimensional therapeutic mechanisms, targeted delivery, dual cell death induction, and immune activation, offering a robust and versatile strategy for overcoming BCG resistance and improving long-term outcomes in bladder cancer treatment.

Results

Preparation and characterization of BCG-CuFe MOF

Simultaneous induction of ferroptosis and cuproptosis in tumor cells may overcome the limitations associated with single-mode cell death, thereby enhancing antitumor efficacy. In this study, we constructed a Cu–Fe metal–organic framework (MOF) capable of triggering both ferroptosis and cuproptosis. BCG, a clinically approved agent for bladder cancer therapy, is a live attenuated bacterium with inherent tumor-targeting capability. Here, BCG was exploited not only as a natural immunoadjuvant but also as a micron-scale carrier for Cu–Fe MOF functionalization, enabling efficient tumor-targeted delivery of the composite system.

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) results indicated that the particle size of the Cu–Fe MOF was approximately 185 nm (Fig. 1A), which was further confirmed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (Fig. 1B). After storing the Cu–Fe MOF for one week, measurements of its particle size, zeta potential, and polydispersity index (PDI) showed only minor fluctuations in these parameters, demonstrating its high stability and suggesting good potential for biomedical applications (Fig. 1C). Additionally, TEM images of the stored Cu–Fe MOF revealed no structural degradation (Fig. 1D). To evaluate the reactive oxygen species (ROS)-generating capacity of the Cu–Fe MOF, samples at different concentrations were tested. The results showed significant DPPH. and ABTS⁺ radical generation (Fig. 1E and S1), indicating that the Cu–Fe MOF possesses excellent ROS-producing ability.

Subsequently, to construct BCG-CuFeMOF, we mixed a specific ratio of Cu–Fe MOF with BCG bacteria. Due to electrostatic interactions, the Cu–Fe MOF self-assembled onto the surface of the BCG bacteria. DLS results showed a slight increase in the particle size of BCG-CuFeMOF compared to bare BCG, confirming the successful self-assembly of the nanoparticles (Fig. 1F). Furthermore, the zeta potential of BCG-CuFeMOF shifted from approximately − 42 mV to about − 24 mV, which can be attributed to the deposition of the positively charged Cu–Fe MOF (Fig. 1G). TEM imaging further demonstrated the presence of numerous nanoparticles on the bacterial surface in BCG-CuFeMOF, in contrast to bare BCG (Fig. 1H, I and S2, S3). After one-week storage in PBS, BCG-CuFeMOF showed no significant change confirmed by TEM, indicating its stability (Figure S4). UV-Vis spectroscopy revealed distinct spectral profiles among Cu–Fe MOF, BCG, and BCG-CuFeMOF (Figure S5). Finally, we investigated whether the modification with Cu–Fe MOF would cause toxicity to BCG. The results indicated that both the modified BCG-CuFeMOF and bare BCG yielded comparable numbers of bacterial colonies after plate coating, suggesting minimal adverse effects on bacterial viability (Fig. 1J and S6).

Synergistic induction of ferroptosis and cuproptosis by BCG@MOF composite for potent bladder cancer cell killing

Initial in vitro assessments on bladder cancer cells demonstrated the superior efficacy of the BCG@MOF composite and higher cellular uptake of Cy5-targeted nano-particle (Figure S7A). CCK-8 assays revealed that treatment with BCG@MOF resulted in significantly lower cell viability compared to control, MOF-only, or BCG-only treatments (Fig. 2A). Treatment with BCG@MOF led to a substantial increase in intracellular ROS, as shown by DCFH-DA staining (Fig. 2B), and a significant decrease of stemness markers’ expression, including OCT4 and NANOG (Fig. 2C), indicating disrupted redox homeostasis and lowered cancer cell stemness. To determine the BCG@MOF-induced cell death, we assessed the MDA accumulation levels. Consistently, malondialdehyde (MDA) analysis confirmed a marked increase in the BCG@MOF group (Fig. 2D). Mechanistic investigations elucidated that the combinatorial effect stems from the induction of both ferroptosis and cuproptosis, overcoming the limitations of a single cell death pathway. Western blot analysis confirmed the activation of distinct death pathways. Key regulators of ferroptosis were altered upon BCG@MOF treatment, characterized by a decrease in the protein levels of solute carrier family 7 member 11 (xCT) and increase in acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) levels (Fig. 2E&F). Concurrently, hallmarks of cuproptosis were observed, evidenced by the downregulation of dihydrolipoamide S-acetyltransferase (DLAT) and LIAS, alongside the upregulation of ferredoxin 1 (FDX1). Resorting to probe analysis, we observed a significant increase in mito-SOX and C11-BODIPY-OX intensity (Fig. 2G), triggered by BCG@MOF treatment. Moreover, a mitochondrial membrane and cell membrane rupture was observed in the BCG@MOF treatment group, indirectly sustaining the intra-cellular lipid peroxide accumulation (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, we conducted cellular experiments to determine whether the nano-platform exerts its primary cytotoxic effects through ferroptosis and cuproptosis. Using inhibitors of ferroptosis and cuproptosis, we designed two combinations: DFM + TET and FER + TET. Both inhibitor combinations effectively suppressed the cell damage induced by the drug platform, as evidenced by lower OD450 value (Figure S7B), but failed to achieve complete inhibition. This was further corroborated by western blot analysis of a DNA damage marker (Figure S7C) and higher IF staining intensity of γH2AX (Figure S7D&E). These findings collectively confirm that the BCG@MOF composite simultaneously triggers ferroptosis and cuproptosis, leading to potent and synergistic tumor cell killing.

BCG@MOF reprograms macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory phenotype in vitro

We further investigated the immunomodulatory effects of BCG@MOF, focusing on its impact on cancer cell stemness and macrophage polarization. To evaluate the subsequent immune activation, we cultured macrophages with conditioned media from the treated bladder cancer cells. Cytokine profiling of the culture supernatants showed that the BCG@MOF group induced the highest secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, protein analysis confirmed the upregulation of classic M1 markers, iNOS and CD86, in macrophages exposed to BCG@MOF-conditioned media (Fig. 3B). BCG@MOF-conditioned media also triggered an up-regulation in the ERK1/2 signaling pathway (Fig. 3C), which prompted the acceleration of glycolysis (Fig. 3D). Flow cytometry analysis for macrophage surface markers demonstrated that the BCG@MOF group markedly increased the proportion of M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophages (F4/80 + CD86+), while the BCG-only group showed a more moderate effect (Fig. 3E). Consistently, an increase of Ki67 and iNOS signal was found in the BCG@MOF group (Fig. 3F). In summary, these findings demonstrate BCG@MOF stimulates a potent anti-tumor immune response by skewing macrophage polarization towards the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype.

BCG@MOF elicits potent anti-tumor efficacy and reprograms the immune microenvironment in an orthotopic bladder cancer model

To further elucidate the antitumor activity of the BCG@MOF nanovaccine in vivo, mice were administered CY5-labeled MOF or BCG@MOF nanoparticles to study the tumor accumulation (Figure S7F). The BCG@MOF formulation showed clear colocalization of the CY5 signal with the DAPI-stained nucleus, demonstrating that the hybrid nanovaccine can successfully translocate into the cancer cells in vivo. The superior therapeutic performance of BCG@MOF was conclusively demonstrated in a mouse model of orthotopic bladder cancer. Quantitative analysis of tumor growth revealed that the BCG@MOF treatment group exhibited the most significant suppression of tumor progression compared to the control, MOF-only, and BCG-only groups (Fig. 4A). This finding was corroborated by direct measurements of excised tumor volume and weight (Fig. 4B), which were the lowest in the BCG@MOF group. Critically, the treatment with BCG@MOF proved to reduce the cancer cell stemness marker expression levels (Fig. 4C). The downregulation of DLAT and LIAS, alongside the upregulation of FDX1 were observed in the BCG@MOF treatment group, suggesting higher levels of in vivo cuproptosis process (Fig. 4D). Consistently, the highest level of ferroptosis was observed in the BCG@MOF group. The quantification of MDA and GSH suggested lowest reservation of GSH levels and highest level of MDA accumulation in BCG@MOF group (Fig. 4E&F). Histological examination of tumor tissues provided further mechanistic insights. H&E staining, along with a significant decrease in Ki67, confirmed that BCG@MOF potently inhibited cancer cell proliferation in vivo (Fig. 4G). Intriguingly, the BCG@MOF treatment significantly lowered the progression of cancer cells, which revealed a close-to-normal bladder tissue.

Analysis of the tumor immune microenvironment revealed the immunomodulatory mechanism underlying the therapeutic efficacy. Cytokine analysis (Fig. 5A) showed that BCG@MOF treatment significantly upregulated the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the peripheral blood. This indicates a robust, systemic activation of anti-tumor immunity. Furthermore, to directly visualize and quantify changes in key immune cell populations within the tumor microenvironment, we performed immunofluorescence staining for T lymphocytes (CD4 and CD8) and macrophages (CD86 and CD206). The results demonstrated that BCG and/or MOF treatment profoundly reprograms the local immune landscape. We observed a significant increase in the infiltration of both CD4 + and CD8 + T cells into tumor tissues treated with BCG and MOF, indicating a robust adaptive anti-tumor immune response. Concurrently, analysis of macrophage polarization revealed that BCG and/or MOF potently skewed macrophages towards a pro-inflammatory, anti-tumor phenotype. In the BCG@MOF group, we observed the BCG@MOF-treated TME displayed a close resemblance to normal bladder tissue (Fig. 5B-E).

BCG@MOF synergizes with anti-PDL1 therapy to achieve superior anti-tumor efficacy

The combination of BCG@MOF with immune checkpoint blockade was evaluated to assess potential synergistic effects. Mice treated with the combination of BCG@MOF and anti-PDL1 exhibited profound suppression of tumor growth, with final tumor volumes and weights significantly lower than all other groups, including BCG@MOF or anti-PDL1 monotherapies (Fig. 6A&B). Western blot analysis confirmed that the combination therapy eliminated the expression of cell stemness marker protein expression (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, the combination treatment robustly activated both ferroptosis and cuproptosis pathways in vivo, as demonstrated by the marked upregulation of ACSL4 and FDX1, alongside downregulation of xCT, DLAT, and LIAS (Fig. 6D). The combination treatment further enhanced MDA accumulation and reduced GSH reservation (Fig. 6E&F). Histological analysis of tumor tissues provided deeper mechanistic insight. H&E staining and a marked reduction in Ki67 demonstrated that BCG@MOF potently suppressed cancer cell proliferation in vivo (Fig. 6G). Notably, both BCG@MOF and BCG@MOF and anti-PDL1 combination therapies treatment markedly attenuated tumor progression, restoring bladder tissue to a near-normal architecture.

Discussion

This study successfully engineered a BCG@MOF composite using the clinical strain BCG as a natural carrier, which enables efficient tumor targeting and the synchronous induction of ferroptosis and cuproptosis for synergistic bladder cancer cell killing. BCG@MOF demonstrated potent anti-tumor efficacy both in vitro and in vivo by directly eliminating cancer cells, reducing their stemness, and reprogramming the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment towards a pro-inflammatory state through M1 macrophage polarization and enhanced T cell infiltration. Furthermore, BCG@MOF exhibited a marked synergistic effect with anti-PD-L1 checkpoint blockade therapy, achieving superior tumor eradication and immune activation, thereby presenting a promising combinatorial strategy for bladder cancer treatment. In contrast to conventional BCG or single-metal MOF systems that rely on a single cell death pathway or limited catalytic activity, our BCG@MOF composite offers a distinct mechanistic advantage by simultaneously engaging cuproptosis and ferroptosis—two regulated cell death modalities that are not effectively triggered by standard therapies. This dual attack is enabled by the bimetallic CuFe-MOF, which not only provides synergistic chemodynamic therapy with enhanced ROS-generating capacity but also preserves BCG’s intrinsic immunostimulatory function while adding direct tumoricidal effects. Consequently, the system overcomes the apoptotic resistance commonly encountered in bladder cancer and establishes a multifaceted antitumor strategy that combines targeted cytotoxicity with sustained immune activation.

Ferroptosis represents a critical therapeutic target in bladder cancer, however, tumor cells deploy a diverse array of molecular defenses to evade this form of cell death. Targeting this complex vulnerability network holds significant promise for overcoming treatment resistance in advanced disease. PhosphoGlycerol Dehydrogenase (PHGDH), an important molecule of serine metabolism, inhibits ferroptosis in bladder cancer by binding to and stabilizing the RNA-binding protein PCBP2, which in turn upregulates expression of the ferroptosis guard protein SLC7A11. This PHGDH-mediated ferroptosis axis promotes suppresses bladder cancer growth, which could be therapeutically targeted by PHGDH inhibitor [26]. Baicalin induces ferroptosis in bladder cancer cells by causing iron accumulation and ROS-driven lipid peroxidation. Its mechanism crucially involves suppressing ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1), as overexpressing FTH1 blocks this ferroptotic cell death [27]. The deubiquitinase USP52 promotes ferroptosis resistance in bladder cancer by stabilizing the xCT protein, a key component of the cystine/glutamate antiporter system. Mechanistically, USP52 directly binds to xCT and cleaves its K48-linked ubiquitin chains, thereby inhibiting its degradation and maintaining glutathione synthesis to suppress lethal lipid peroxidation [28]. However, bladder cancer employs diverse strategies to evade ferroptosis and underlines the complexity of targeting this cell death pathway for therapy. Bladder cancer elevates HSPE1 expression, which inhibits ferroptosis in bladder cancer by upregulating the key ferroptosis regulator GPX4. This mechanism is dependent on GSH, as HSPE1 promotes GSH accumulation to reduce lethal lipid peroxidation and protect cancer cells [29]. Advanced bladder cancer evades ferroptosis through a deficiency in the lipid peroxidation enzyme ALOX5, which is transcriptionally downregulated by EGR1. This loss of ALOX5 allows tumor cells to resist the iron-dependent cell death mechanism, contributing to a worse patient prognosis [30]. Ferroptosis resistance emerges as a critical hallmark of bladder cancer progression, driven by a diverse armory of molecular strategies that cancer cells employ to suppress this iron-dependent cell death pathway. In our study, while existing literature extensively documents the diverse molecular strategies bladder cancer employs to evade single-pathway ferroptosis, we demonstrate a breakthrough by simultaneously inducing both ferroptosis and cuproptosis via the BCG@MOF composite. This dual-pathway attack effectively overcomes the evasion mechanisms, while uniquely reprogramming the tumor microenvironment into a pro-inflammatory state. Our approach not only directly addresses the therapeutic resistance highlighted in prior research but also provides a synergistic platform for combination with existing immunotherapies like anti-PD-L1.

Fe‑MOFs and Cu‑MOFs serve not only as nanocarriers for drugs or photosensitizers but also as donors of Fe/Cu ions and direct ferroptosis inducers, enabling combination of ferroptosis with chemotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and immunotherapy [31]. He et al. synthesized a PFP@Fe/Cu‑SS MOF that generated ·OH via the Fenton reaction to increase intratumoral LPO, while also depleting GSH via disulfide‑thiol exchange to inactivate GPX4, further enhancing ferroptosis in a Huh‑7 liver cancer model [32]. Rao et al. designed an iron‑based MOF co‑loading BSO (buthionine sulfoximine) and oxaliplatin; by blocking de novo GSH synthesis, it achieved sustained GSH depletion and amplified ferroptosis, significantly inhibiting tumor growth and improving survival in a 4T1 tumor model [33]. Zhang et al. reported an iron‑based MOF nanoreactor that disrupted both redox and iron metabolic homeostasis to induce ferroptosis; the released tumor antigens, combined with a PD‑L1 aptamer, further enhanced immunotherapy. A cancer‑cell‑membrane‑coated MOF (mFe(SS)/DG) was constructed that integrated ROS‑ferroptosis‑glycolysis regulation, induced ICD, and remodeled the immunosuppressive microenvironment [34, 35]. Unlike conventional MOF-based platforms that rely on single-pathway ferroptosis induction and lack intrinsic immune activity, our BCG@MOF composite uniquely combines live BCG-mediated tumor targeting and immunoadjuvant function with simultaneous induction of ferroptosis and cuproptosis, thereby overcoming resistance mechanisms and activating robust antitumor immunity. This dual-death, dual-function platform also synergizes effectively with anti-PD-L1 therapy, addressing key limitations of both BCG monotherapy and existing MOF nanocarriers.

Cuproptosis has emerged as a critical biological process involved in the cancer progression. A prognostic signature based on 17 cuproptosis-related long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) was developed that independently predicts survival outcomes in bladder cancer. This model stratifies patients into risk groups and is mechanistically supported by the upregulation of these lncRNAs in bladder cancer cells following copper-induced cuproptosis [36]. In addition, a prognostic signature based on eight cuproptosis-related miRNAs was also shown to independently predict outcomes in bladder cancer by reflecting the underlying tumor immune microenvironment [37]. An eight-gene prognostic signature (including PDGFRB and COMP) was identified that stratifies bladder cancer patients into high- and low-risk subgroups based on cuproptosis-related gene expression patterns. The resulting risk score correlates with distinct tumor microenvironment features, immune checkpoint activity, and chemotherapy sensitivity, effectively predicting patient outcomes [38]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that molecular signatures centered on cuproptosis—whether derived from lncRNAs, miRNAs, or protein-coding genes—serve as robust and independent prognostic tools in bladder cancer. This consistent evidence underscores the central role of cuproptosis in disease progression and highlights its potential as a framework for guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. Regarding the mechanism study, the cuproptosis-related gene LIPT1 is downregulated in bladder cancer through m6A-mediated mRNA degradation by YTHDF2, contributing to poor prognosis. Restoring LIPT1 expression inhibits tumor progression by promoting cuproptosis and disrupting endoplasmic reticulum homeostasis via suppression of lipid droplet accumulation [39]. Therapeutically, a ROS-sensitive nanoparticle (NP@ESCu) was designed to co-deliver copper and the ionophore elesclomol into bladder cancer cells. Upon encountering high intracellular ROS levels, the nanoparticles release their cargo, enabling the efficient accumulation of copper to induce cuproptosis [40]. Another study develops a multifunctional nanotherapy (CuOTeDsP) that co-delivers terbinafine and disulfiram to simultaneously inhibit the cholesterol synthesis enzyme SQLE and generate cytotoxic copper complexes within bladder cancer cells. The nanoparticle’s enzymatic activities raise oxidative stress and alleviate hypoxia, while the drug combination disrupts cholesterol metabolism, downregulates PD-L1, and induces cuproptosis alongside apoptosis [41]. These studies collectively establish that molecular signatures based on cuproptosis-related genes provide powerful tools for prognostic stratification in bladder cancer. Furthermore, therapeutic strategies utilizing nanoparticle delivery systems to induce cuproptosis show promising efficacy in reshaping the tumor microenvironment and inhibiting tumor progression. In our study, while prior research has established the prognostic value of cuproptosis-related signatures and pioneered nanoparticle strategies for inducing this pathway as monotherapy, our work presents a distinct breakthrough. We designed a BCG-based bio-hybrid platform that simultaneously triggers both cuproptosis and ferroptosis, creating an insurmountable dual-death mechanism that overcomes the limitations of single-pathway targeting.

Cuproptosis-inducing nanomaterials. Xu et al. developed a glucose oxidase‑engineered copper(I) coordination polymer (GOx@[Cu(tz)]) that achieved 92.4% tumor suppression in an orthotopic bladder cancer (5637) model, representing the first report of cuproptosis induction by a copper‑based nanomaterial in bladder cancer [3]. Zhou et al. designed a copper‑coordinated polymer delivering lactate oxidase; by converting lactate to pyruvate, it reprogrammed energy metabolism while simultaneously promoting cuproptosis (DLAT oligomerization) and ferroptosis (iron‑independent Fenton reaction), showing potent in vivo antitumor activity [4]. Importantly, cuproptosis can induce immunogenic cell death (ICD), characterized by calreticulin exposure and HMGB1 release, thereby activating antitumor immunity. Li et al. used CRISPR/Cas9 to knock out ATG5 to block autophagy; combined with copper overload, this enhanced both cuproptosis and cytotoxic T‑lymphocyte infiltration [5]. Several reviews have systematically summarized the molecular mechanisms of cuproptosis, copper homeostasis regulation, the prognostic value of cuproptosis‑related genes (CRGs), and the translational potential of copper ionophores and nano‑delivery platforms [6, 7, 9–11].

We acknowledge that our experimental design using conditioned media from BCG@MOF-treated cancer cells does not fully exclude the possibility that residual BCG@MOF directly activates macrophages, given that BCG itself is a potent macrophage stimulator. Therefore, the relative contributions of DAMP-mediated indirect activation versus direct BCG@MOF–macrophage interaction remain to be dissected in future studies. Nevertheless, regardless of the primary mechanism, the net outcome—robust M1 polarization and enhanced antitumor immunity—is consistently observed both in vitro and in vivo, supporting the therapeutic potential of the BCG@MOF platform.

Conclusion

A BCG@MOF composite was engineered that leverages BCG as a natural carrier for tumor-targeted delivery, simultaneously inducing ferroptosis and cuproptosis while reprogramming the tumor immune microenvironment. This agent demonstrated profound anti-tumor efficacy and synergized with anti-PD-L1 therapy to achieve superior tumor control, offering a potent combinatorial strategy for bladder cancer.

Methods

Characterization

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging was conducted using a Zeiss SIGMA microscope (Carl Zeiss AG) operating at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. Samples were prepared by depositing a dilute suspension of the material onto a silicon wafer followed by gold sputtering to enhance conductivity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed on a JEM-2100 instrument (JEOL Ltd.) at an acceleration voltage of 200 kV. Samples for TEM were prepared by drop-casting smaple dispersions onto carbon-coated copper grids and allowing them to dry under ambient conditions. Particle size distribution and zeta potential measurements were carried out using a Nano-ZS ZEN3600 particle size analyzer (Malvern Instruments Ltd.). Each measurement was performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. UV-Vis absorption spectra were acquired on a Lambda Bio40 spectrophotometer over a wavelength range of 200–700 nm.

Bacterial culture

The Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) Pasteur strain (ATCC 35734) was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Bacteria were initially revived and cultivated under strict sterile conditions. For solid culture, the bacteria were streaked onto Middlebrook 7H10 agar plates supplemented with OADC enrichment (BD, 262710) and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified environment. After 6–8 weeks, distinct colonies were visible. For liquid culture, several well-isolated colonies were transferred into Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with ADC enrichment (BD, 271310) and 0.05% Tween 80 to prevent clumping. The culture was incubated in a shaking incubator at 37 °C and 150 rpm to promote aerobic growth. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring optical density at 600 nm (OD600) and through regular microscopic examination. Only cultures in the mid-log phase and showing no signs of contamination were used for further experiments.

Synthesis and characterization of CuFeMOF

The iron–copper metal–organic framework (CuFeMOF) was synthesized via a modified solvothermal approach. In a typical procedure, 1.62 g of FeCl₃·6 H₂O, 0.68 g of CuCl₂·2 H₂O, and 1.50 g of terephthalic acid (BDC-NH2, as the organic linker) were completely dissolved in 70 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under vigorous stirring. The homogeneous mixture was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave and heated at 100 °C for 12 h. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and washed several times with deionized water and ethanol to remove unreacted precursors and solvent residues. The product was then freeze-dried for 24 h to obtain a crystalline, porous CuFeMOF powder.

ABTS+ and DPPH Radical Generation Assays

The radical producing activity of CuFeMOF was evaluated using ABTS⁺and DPPH assays. For the ABTS⁺assay, an ABTS stock solution (7.4 mM) was prepared by dissolving 3 mg of ABTS diammonium salt in 0.735 mL of deionized water. A separate oxidant solution was prepared by dissolving 1 mg of potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) in 1.43 mL of deionized water to obtain a 2.6 mM solution. Then, 0.2 mL of the ABTS solution was mixed with 0.2 mL of the K2S2O8 solution and allowed to react in the dark at room temperature for 12 h to generate the ABTS+ radical cation. This mixture was diluted with deionized water until the absorbance at 734 nm reached 0.70 ± 0.02. Various concentrations of CuFeMOF dispersions were incubated with 1 mL of the ABTS+ working solution for 15 min in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm, and the radical producing activity was calculated relative to a blank control.

For the DPPH assay, 1 mg of DPPH (1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) was dissolved in 24 mL of absolute ethanol and sonicated for 5 min to facilitate complete dissolution. From this stock, 1 mL was further diluted with ethanol until the absorbance at 519 nm reached between 0.6 and 1.0. Different concentrations of CuFeMOF were mixed with 1 mL of the DPPH working solution and incubated for 30 min in the dark. The absorbance at 519 nm was measured.

Construction of BCG-CuFeMOF

The BCG-CuFeMOF complex was constructed through electrostatic self-assembly. Briefly, 1 mL of BCG suspension (10⁷ CFU/mL) in PBS (pH 7.4) was combined with 200 µL of CuFeMOF dispersion (1 mg/mL) and stirred gently at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then subjected to centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min to remove unbound nanoparticles. The pellet was washed twice with PBS to eliminate loosely adhered material and finally resuspended in 1 mL of PBS. The resulting BCG-CuFeMOF conjugate was stored at 4 °C and used within 24 h to ensure biohybrid bacterial viability. The successful conjugation was confirmed through TEM imaging and zeta potential measurements.

Viability Assessment of BCG-CuFeMOF

The viability of BCG after conjugation with CuFeMOF was assessed using the standard plate counting method. BCG and BCG-CuFeMOF complexes were adjusted to an optical density of 1.5 at 600 nm. Then, 10 µL of each suspension was inoculated into 10 mL of fresh Middlebrook 7H9 broth supplemented with ADC and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C with shaking at 150 rpm. After incubation, 100 µL of serial dilutions of each culture were spread onto Middlebrook 7H10 agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 3–4 weeks. The number of colony-forming units (CFU) was counted, and viability was expressed as CFU/mL. The experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure statistical reliability.

Cell and murine orthotopic mice model

The MB49 murine bladder cancer cell lines (ATCC) were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, as previously described [42, 43]. Matrigel (Corning, #354230) were obtained from Corning. For in vivo tumor analysis, an orthotopic MB49 model was established. Briefly, 1 × 10⁶ MB49 cells were instilled intravesically into mouse bladders (n = 3 per group). On day 14, mice were sacrificed for tumor weighing; survival was monitored daily. BCG@MOF concentrations used in vitro: 10–200 µg/mL equivalent MOF content; in vivo: 1 × 10^7 CFU BCG equivalent per mouse.

WB analysis

MB49 cells were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore), and probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies from anti-body against DLAT (13426-1-AP), LIAS (11577-1-AP), FDX (12592-1-AP), ACSL4 (22401-1-AP), xCT (26864-1-AP), NANOG (14295-1-AP), OCT4 (11263-1-AP), Lamin B1(12987-1-AP) and ACTIN (all 1:1500, Proteintech). Membranes were then incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody.

IF analysis

For immunofluorescent staining, tumor tissues and cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. This was followed by a 2-hour incubation at 25 °C with secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 1:1000). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Solarbio, 10 mmol). Images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus) and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ.

Flow cytometry analysis

Briefly, cells were resuspended in staining buffer and counted (Echo Rebel microscope). For staining, 2 × 10⁶ cells per 100 µl were blocked with TruStain FcX (Biolegend, 1:500) for 30 min at 4 °C. Cells were then incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in staining buffer containing the following antibodies: Live/Dead Aqua (Invitrogen, 1:250), CD86 (BD Biosciences, 1:1000), CD45 (BD Biosciences, 1:1000), F4/80 (Biolegend, 1:200). After staining, cells were fixed in BD Cytofix buffer (20 min, 4 °C), washed, and resuspended in FACS buffer (PBS with 1.5% BSA) until analysis.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (Yeasen). Cells (1 × 10⁴ per well) were seeded in 96-well plates, treated as indicated, and then incubated for 1 h with CCK-8 reagent. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific Varioskan Flash).

Lipid peroxidation assay

Lipid peroxidation was measured using BODIPY 581/591 C11 staining (Dojindo). Cells (1 × 10⁵ per well) were seeded in 12-well plates and treated as indicated. During the final 30 min of incubation, 1.5 µM BODIPY dye was added. Cells were then washed with HBSS and analyzed by flow cytometry.

MDA assay

To assess ferroptosis levels across different groups, we measured MDA in cell lysates using commercial kits. Specifically, the Lipid Peroxidation (MDA) Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat #: MAK085) were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging of cells

Cells were seeded in 10 cm diameter dishes. After 24 h, the cells were collected using a cell scraper, washed with PBS, and pelleted by centrifugation. The cell pellets were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M Sørensen’s phosphate buffer overnight, followed by post-fixation in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (50%–100%) and 100% acetone, and incubated at 60 °C for 24 h. Ultrathin sections were cut using a Leica UC7 ultramicrotome, stained with 2% uranyl acetate and 0.4% lead citrate, and examined under a Hitachi-7700 transmission electron microscope for image acquisition.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed by Student’s *t*-test, and comparisons among multiple groups by one-way ANOVA.
