Analysis of ovarian cancer burden among reproductive-aged women and its association with the socio-demographic index from 1990 to 2021
Authors: Wangyan Zhong, Yibing Xu, Hang Yuan, Tingting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study analyzed the global burden of ovarian cancer in reproductive-aged women (1990–2021) to identify trends, drivers, and project future trends for guiding prevention strategies.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease 2021 data, we analyzed ovarian cancer burden and its association with the Socio-demographic Index (SDI). Trends were evaluated using average annual percentage change and Age-Period-Cohort modeling, with projections to 2036 via ARIMA models.ResultsIn 2021, there were 85,749 new cases and 25,258 deaths globally among reproductive-aged women. The age-standardized rates declined from 1990 to 2021. Projections indicate stable age-standardized incidence and prevalence rates through 2036, but further declines in mortality and DALY rates. Significant regional disparities persist, with the burden decreasing in high-SDI regions but increasing in low- and middle-SDI regions. Disease risk increased after age 35. Successive birth cohorts from the 1990s onward showed a higher baseline risk. Population growth was the primary driver of increasing case numbers. High body mass index (BMI) was the leading risk factor.ConclusionsWhile the global burden of ovarian cancer is declining, substantial regional heterogeneity exists. Tailored strategies focusing on high-risk populations and modifiable factors like high BMI are crucial to mitigate the disease's impact and reduce disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04292-9.Keywords: Ovarian cancer, Women of reproductive age, Epidemiology, Incidence, Prevalence, Mortality, DALYs, Global burden

Introduction

Ovarian cancer is a major global health issue, and one of the deadliest form of gynecological cancer, with a profound impact on women’s quality of life [1]. The high mortality rate associated with this disease creates a significant burden not only for the patients themselves but also for their families and healthcare systems, especially when the patients are of reproductive age (defined here as 15–49 years in accordance with World Health Organization criteria) and play crucial roles within their families. Unlike older populations, women in this demographic face distinct clinical challenges, particularly regarding fertility preservation and hormonal regulation. Furthermore, early symptoms in reproductive-aged women are often non-specific and easily misattributed to benign gynecological conditions or pregnancy-related changes, frequently leading to diagnostic delays compared to older age groups [2]. The treatment of ovarian cancer mainly involves surgical procedures, chemotherapy, and molecular targeted therapy [3, 4]. However, the subtlety of its early symptoms often results in diagnoses occurring at advanced stages, which greatly reduces the effectiveness of existing treatment options. Additionally, significant disparities exist in access to specialized gynecologic oncology care across different countries and regions. In low-resource settings, the lack of integration between reproductive health services and oncology further exacerbates these disparities, limiting options for fertility-sparing treatments [5]. These differences are often influenced by factors such as socioeconomic development and lifestyle changes. Consequently, conducting a comparative analysis of the disease burden in various countries and regions is essential for optimizing healthcare resource allocation and guiding public health planning effectively.

Recent literature has underscored the significant impact of socioeconomic factors on the burden of various diseases, including ovarian cancer [6]. Specifically for women of reproductive age, the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) offers crucial insights, as regional economic development strongly influences not only survival rates but also the availability of fertility-preserving interventions and early screening programs tailored to this demographic [7].The SDI is a useful measure that reflects the levels of socioeconomic development across different regions [8]. Prior studies have shown a clear correlation between SDI and the burden of several diseases, indicating that variations in health outcomes may be shaped by underlying social determinants [9]. Numerous studies have established obesity as a significant risk factor for ovarian cancer [10, 11], whereas protective factors include pregnancy, breastfeeding, and oral contraceptive use [12]. However, the protective effects of pregnancy and breastfeeding may be reduced compared to the past due to the global decline in fertility rates [13]. Although previous Global Burden of Disease (GBD) studies have reported on ovarian cancer trends, they have primarily focused on the general population or postmenopausal women, leaving a gap in understanding the specific burden among women of reproductive age. Moreover, existing analyses have rarely integrated a comprehensive set of analytical methods—specifically SDI analysis, age-period-cohort (APC) analysis, decomposition analysis, and ARIMA forecasting—to dissect the drivers of burden changes within this unique subgroup. To address this gap, this study systematically examines the burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age, with particular emphasis on its temporal trends, risk determinants, and socioeconomic dimensions. By integrating decomposition analysis and APC models, we provide updated epidemiological evidence to support the design of targeted prevention strategies. These findings hold significant public health relevance for mitigating ovarian cancer incidence and mortality, especially among high-risk subgroups and in regions undergoing rapid sociodemographic transition.

Methods

Data source

Data on the global and country-specific burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age (15–49 years) were obtained from the GBD study, which encompasses information from 204 countries and territories [8]. This extensive dataset is compiled from a variety of sources, including census data, household surveys, civil registration, vital statistics, disease registries, health service utilization records, air pollution monitoring, satellite imagery, and disease notifications. In the GBD 2021 report, ovarian cancer is classified according to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10), specifically under code C56. The data have been standardized by sex, age, and year, allowing for a clearer understanding of prevalence, incidence, mortality, years of life lost (YLLs), years lived with disability (YLDs), and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). This standardization is crucial as it facilitates effective comparisons of the disease burden across different populations and over time, eliminating potential confounding factors related to variations in age distribution. The methodology employed by the GBD has been extensively documented in the existing literature [14–16].

Sociodemographic index

The SDI is a comprehensive metric designed to assess the socioeconomic development level of a country or region. It encompasses various dimensions such as economic structure and scale, educational attainment, living standards, and the availability of social welfare and security. The SDI is measured on a scale from 0 to 1, where higher values signify greater socioeconomic development. Regions are categorized into five classifications based on their SDI: low (0 to 0.46), low-middle (0.46 to 0.60), middle (0.61 to 0.69), high-middle (0.70 to 0.81), and high (0.81 to 1) [15]. A higher SDI indicates a more advanced level of development, as highlighted in earlierGBD studies. This classification system allows for a detailed analysis of how socioeconomic factors and geographical differences impact disease burden [17, 18].

Statistical analysis

In this study, we used age-standardized rates (ASRs) to illustrate the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs associated with ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age. The ASRs were calculated using the GBD world standard population.The detailed formula is provided in the Supplemantary Method 1.

The trends in the global burden of ovarian cancer from 1990 to 2021 were assessed using Joinpoint Regression Analysis (Version 5.4.0.0, National Cancer Institute, USA). This method identifies significant points (joinpoints) where the trend changes, fitting a series of linear segments to the data [19]. The Annual Percent Change (APC) for each segment and the Average Annual Percent Change (AAPC) for the entire period, along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs), were calculated [20]. An increasing trend was defined when both the APC/AAPC and the lower limit of its 95% CI were > 0; a decreasing trend was defined when both the value and the upper limit of its 95% CI were < 0. The mathematical details of APC/AAPC calculation are described in Supplementary Method 2.An age-period-cohort analysis framework was employed to explore the potential trends in the disease burden associated with ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age, categorized by age, time period, and birth cohort. Specifically, the data were stratified into consecutive 5-year age groups (e.g., 15–19, 20–24, …, 45–49 years) and consecutive 1-year intervals for the time period (from 1990 to 2021). Birth cohorts were derived by subtracting the midpoint of the age group from the midpoint of the corresponding period. The analysis was conducted utilizing R software, which facilitated the orthogonal decomposition of age, period, and cohort effects to derive both linear and nonlinear components. The parameters for linear regression were ascertained through weighted least squares, while the fit of the model was assessed using the Wald χ2 test [21].

Decomposition methods were utilized to examine the roles of aging, population expansion, and epidemiological shifts in contributing to the disease burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age. Comprehensive details regarding this model, including specific equations, can be found in previously published research [22].

We used the Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model to forecast the global burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age from 2022 to 2036, based on time-series data from 1990 to 2021. The ARIMA model captures temporal dependencies within the data for prediction [23]. The time series was first tested for stationarity using the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test. The optimal model parameters (p, d, q) were selected based on standard model diagnostics and information criteria. The adequacy of the final model was confirmed by ensuring the residuals resembled white noise. A comprehensive description of the model formulation, selection process, and validation is available in Supplementary Method 3. All data processing, analysis, and visualization were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1), and a p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Global burden of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age

Between 1990 and 2021, the global incidence rose from 47,983 new cases (95% CI: 40,523.3–55,869.6) to 85,749 (95% CI: 75,168.9–95,089.8), a 44% increase (Table 1). Similarly, the number of prevalent cases grew by 46.1%, from 281,267 (95% CI: 238,552.4–325,465.4) to 522,319 (95% CI: 458,299.9–579,937.1) (Table 2). Mortality figures also increased, with deaths rising by 38.7% from 15,490 (95% CI: 13,203.6–17,928.4) to 25,258 (95% CI: 22,277.5–27,861.2) (Table 3). The disease burden measured in DALYs saw a 38.3% increase, from 799,648 (95% CI: 674,906.2–931,352.3) to 1,294,996 (95% CI: 1,139,826.5–1,431,298.3) (Table 4). From 1990 to 2021, the AAPC for age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) all showed negative values, indicating a significant decrease in the disease burden (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Regional burden of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age

In 2021, Central Europe recorded the highest ASIR at 10.8 per 100,000 (95% CI: 9.9–11.7). In contrast, Western Sub-Saharan Africa reported the lowest incidence, at 3.6 per 100,000 (95% CI: 2.4–4.6) (Table 1). Southeast Asia and Central Europe shared the highest ASPR, each reaching 43.5 per 100,000 (95% CI: 32.1–56.6; 40–47.1), whereas Western Sub-Saharan Africa again had the lowest ASPR, at 11.8 per 100,000 (95% CI: 7.9–15.2) (Table 2). Eastern Europe reported the highest ASMR in this population, at 6 per 100,000 (95% CI: 5.4–6.7). Oceania had the lowest ASMR, recorded at 2 per 100,000 (95% CI: 1.2–2.6) (Table 3). Regarding DALYs, Central Europe also showed the highest ASDR worldwide, at 205.2 per 100,000 (95% CI: 189.4–221.3). Oceania reported the lowest ASDR, at 59 per 100,000 (95% CI: 35.1–80.9) (Table 4). Analysis of AAPC across these indicators revealed that Australasia experienced the most rapid declines in ovarian cancer burden. On the other hand, the greatest increases were observed in Andean Latin America (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4).

Between 1990 and 2021, the rates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs for ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age showed diverse trends across various regions categorized by the SDI. Notably, regions with high and middle-high SDI, despite reporting higher rates across all metrics, demonstrated a downward trend, with high SDI regions experiencing a significantly sharper decline compared to their middle-high SDI counterparts. In contrast, regions classified as middle SDI, middle-low SDI, and low SDI displayed increasing trends in these rates (Tables 1, 2, 3 and 4 and Fig. 1). Figure 2 presents the long-term trends from 1990 to 2021 in the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age, in relation to the SDI. Specifically, the disease burden increased with SDI and peaked around an SDI of 0.75, after which the burden gradually declined. Among the 21 GBD regions, Australasia, High-income North America, and Western Europe demonstrated the most significant alleviations in disease burden.

In 2021, the United Arab Emirates reported the highest ASIR of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age globally, at 25.4 per 100,000 (95% CI: 20.0–32.4). In contrast, Palau had the lowest ASIR, at 1.5 per 100,000 (95% CI: 1.1–1.9) (Fig. 3A; Supplementary Table 1.1, Fig. 1A). Regarding ASPR, Seychelles recorded the highest value worldwide in 2021, at 86.7 per 100,000 (95% CI: 63.6–105.9), while Mali exhibited the lowest ASPR, at 5.51 per 100,000 (95% CI: 3.3–7.5) (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table 1.2, Fig. 1B). Similarly, the ASDR was highest in the United Arab Emirates, at 438.1 per 100,000 (95% CI: 342.4–561.9), and lowest in Palau, at 19.8 per 100,000 (95% CI: 14.3–25.6) (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Table 1.4, Fig. 1D).

Trends based on joinpoint regression analysis

We conducted a Joinpoint regression analysis to examine the global ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR from 1990 to 2021.

Our findings revealed that the incidence rate experienced an increase from 1990 to 1995, with an APC of 0.552 (95% CI: 0.356–0.748). However, this trend reversed between 1995 and 2002, as the incidence rate declined with an APC of −0.189 (95% CI: −0.322- −0.055). A more pronounced decrease was noted from 2002 to 2015, where the APC reached −0.669 (95% CI: −0.712- −0.626). From 2015 to 2021, the trend was not statistically significant (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Table 2.1). Overall, when considering the entire period, the incidence rate demonstrated a steady annual decrease, with an AAPC of −0.229 (95% CI: −0.28- −0.177), as detailed in Supplementary Table 3.1. The trends in ASMR and ASDR largely mirrored the pattern observed for incidence (ASIR) over the historical period. However, a divergent trend emerged from 2015 to 2021. In contrast to the ASIR, which plateaued during this interval, both ASMR and ASDR exhibited a modest yet consistent decline (Figs. 4C, D and Supplementary Tables 2.3, 2.4). Notably, the High SDI region experienced a decline in ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, while the Low SDI region witnessed notable increases across these metrics (Supplementary Tables 2.1, 2.3, 2.4).​

From 1990 to 1995, there was a rapid increase in prevalence, with an APC of 0.896(95%CI: 0.693–1.099). This was followed by a slight decline between 1995 and 1998, and then a notable increase from 1998 to 2002, where the APC was 0.518(95% CI: 0.108–0.931). However, from 2002 to 2015, there was a significant decline in prevalence, reflected by an APC of −0.444(95% CI: −0.485- −0.403). In contrast, the High SDI region exhibited only one joinpoint, showing an initial increase followed by a decrease, while the Low SDI region demonstrated three joinpoints, all indicating a persistent upward trend throughout the period (Supplementary Table 2.2).​

Age-period-cohort analysis of the burden of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age

The age effect analysis revealed a strong, non-linear positive correlation with disease risk. Specifically, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR all showed a consistent increase as individuals aged, with a particularly sharp rise noted after the age of 35 (Figs. 5A-D). Furthermore, the period analysis emerged as a significant global factor influencing disease risk. After adjusting for age and cohort effects, the data indicated a stable and continuous upward trend in disease risk starting from 1990, which persisted over the years of observation (Figs. 5E-H). The most striking finding was related to cohort effects, with individuals born from the 1990 s onward exhibiting two key features compared to earlier (1940s-1970s) cohorts: a significantly elevated baseline risk and a persistently higher risk trajectory throughout their lives. Decomposition of the burden of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age

The impact of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes on the burden of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age shows considerable variation across different regions. Generally, population growth emerged as the main factor contributing to the rising burden of this disease, followed by epidemiological changes​​, while the influence of an aging in changing was found to be relatively limited. More specifically, population growth contributed to a rise in the burden of ovarian cancer across all regions. Changes in epidemiological risk factors remained a positive driver of disease burden in most parts of the world. This was particularly pronounced in ​​South Asia​​, where the contribution of epidemiological changes was nearly equivalent to that of population growth. Notably, in ​​high-SDI regions​​, epidemiological changes significantly reduced the burden of ovarian cancer, a trend notably divergent from that observed in other regions (Fig. 6).

Prediction of ovarian cancer burden among women of reproductive age for the next 15 years

Using the ARIMA model, we forecasted the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR rates for ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age globally from 2022 to 2036. Our predictions indicate that the global ASIR and ASPR are expected to stabilize, with the ASIR projected at 6.7 and the ASPR at 28.4 by 2036 (Figs. 8A and B). In contrast, both the ASMR and ASDR are anticipated to decline, with the ASMR estimated at 3.77 and the ASDR at 107.96 (Figs. 8C and D). This overall trend suggests that efforts in secondary prevention, which focuses on early detection and treatment, as well as tertiary prevention aimed at reducing complications and enhancing quality of life, have been effective. However, it is important to note that primary prevention, which seeks to fundamentally prevent the occurrence of the disease, continues to pose a significant challenge.

Discussion

This study analyzed the GBD data to systematically examine the incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age, while also assessing the relationships between these metrics and the SDI along with relevant risk factors. Our findings reveal that although the overall burden of ovarian cancer has increased globally, ASRs have actually declined. This trend reflects the complex interaction between population growth and the uneven distribution of benefits from advancements in medical technology [24]. The reduction in ASRs, which indicates a lower risk, can be largely attributed to enhancements in diagnostic and therapeutic techniques, highlighting the importance of early detection and treatment [25, 26]. This trend is particularly meaningful for reproductive-aged women, where early detection not only improves survival rates but also preserves future fertility potential, a critical outcome distinct from the geriatric population [26]. Additionally, significant disparities were noted across different regions and levels of socioeconomic development; while some areas experienced substantial improvements that helped lower the global ASR, many other regions saw an increase in absolute case numbers due to population growth and limited healthcare resources, which obscured the overall positive trend of decreasing age-specific risk [27]. Notably, our findings indicate that among women of reproductive age, particularly those nearing the end of their reproductive years (ages 45 to 49), the health risks linked to obesity and related metabolic issues are becoming increasingly significant. Obesity is a recognized risk factor for various cancers [28], and its rising prevalence could greatly impact the future burden of ovarian cancer, presenting a dual challenge by increasing cancer risk while simultaneously compromising fertility. This change underscores the urgent need for current public health strategies to focus more on interventions aimed at weight management and obesity prevention to tackle this shifting risk landscape. This study offers essential evidence that can help shape more targeted public health policies moving forward.

The innovation of this study lies in its systematic exploration of the relationship between the SDI and the global burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age, addressing a significant gap in the existing literature. Unlike previous all-age analyses [29], our focus on the 15–49 age bracket reveals distinct epidemiologic patterns that are obscured in aggregated data. Specifically, we highlight how the drivers of burden—such as obesity—differ in magnitude for this subgroup compared to post-menopausal women. Previous studies have primarily been confined to data from individual countries or have predominantly focused on elderly ovarian cancer patients [30, 31]. There remains a lack of in-depth and systematic investigation into the disease burden (such as incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs) among reproductive-aged women, as well as the association between this burden and the SDI. Our study reveals a divergent global burden of ovarian cancer, characterized by a declining trend in high-SDI regions (e.g., Australasia, High-income North America, and Western Europe) but an increasing burden in middle, middle-low, and low SDI regions. This disparity underscores the critical role of socioeconomic development and healthcare resources in disease management, as economic advancement facilitates earlier screening and improves survival rates [31]. Conversely, in low-SDI regions, the rising burden signals an urgent need for integrated healthcare services that address both oncologic and reproductive needs, as access to specialized gynecologic oncology care remains a critical barrier [32, 33]. Paradoxically, despite favorable trends, the ASIR, ASPR, ASMR, and ASDR of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age in high-SDI regions is significantly greater than in other regions. This can be attributed to two key factors: the growing challenge of modifiable risk factors like obesity [34], which underscores an urgent need for policies focused on preventing and managing obesity in this population, and more intensive case detection and registration practices [35]. However, heightened detection brings diagnostic challenges, as clinical manifestations of malignancy can be mimicked by benign conditions. Clinical evidence indicates that elevated tumor markers (such as CA-125 or CA-19–9) and the presence of a massive pelvic mass are not always synonymous with ovarian cancer; similar features can be found in benign entities like uterine leiomyomas or giant serous cystadenomas, as well as in borderline tumors [36–38]. This highlights the necessity for precise diagnostic criteria to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions amidst increased surveillance. These findings highlight the imperative for public health policies to adopt differentiated strategies tailored to specific socioeconomic contexts. In high-SDI regions, interventions should prioritize primary prevention targeting obesity and precise diagnostic criteria to address modifiable risks and diagnostic challenges. In contrast, for low- and middle-SDI regions, efforts must focus on strengthening early diagnosis and improving access to specialized gynecologic care. By aligning these targeted approaches with regional needs, we provide a scientific foundation for more effective interventions.

The age-period-cohort analysis shows that the risk of ovarian cancer in women of reproductive age is not only a function of advancing age, but is also profoundly shaped by generational shifts and period-specific factors influencing the incidence of the disease. The observed non-linear increase in disease risk starting at age 35 is particularly significant for women of reproductive age. This age marks a time when many women in contemporary societies are postponing childbirth. Since being childless or having fewer children are recognized risk factors for ovarian cancer, the trend of delaying the first birth could contribute to the rising risk seen in this age group [39]. The significant upward period effect since 1990 likely reflects changes that have particularly affected women of reproductive age. One major factor is the significant alteration in reproductive behaviors over the past few decades, characterized by declining fertility rates and a rise in the use of assisted reproductive technologies (ART). The long-term relationship between ART and ovarian cancer risk is still under active investigation [40]. On the other hand, there is a period effect that may offer some protection: the widespread use of oral contraceptive pills. However, the adoption of these pills has varied across different generations and regions, which could influence the overall trend observed during this period [41]. While our dataset did not allow us to quantify the specific effects of other protective measures such as progesterone-secreting IUDs or salpingectomy, the widespread adoption of OCPs likely serves as a major proxy for this protective period effect [41, 42]. Additionally, advancements in imaging techniques have led to a higher detection rate of incidental adnexal masses in younger women who are being examined for unrelated health issues, which might further contribute to the increasing incidence rates [43]. The most concerning finding is the significantly increased risk trajectory for birth cohorts from the 1990 s onward. This suggests that successive generations of women might be entering their reproductive years with a higher inherent risk profile. This observed pattern could be partly explained by a combination of several modern exposures that begin early in life. One potential factor driving this trend is the obesity epidemic, as younger generations are experiencing obesity and related metabolic issues for longer periods during their reproductive years, potentially creating an environment that promotes cancer development [34]. Furthermore, changes in dietary habits, such as a higher consumption of processed foods, along with exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals, may be contributing to an increased lifetime risk [44]. Additionally, a decrease in protective factors, such as the use of oral contraceptives among more recent cohorts, may also play a role in this trend [41].

Our decomposition analysis indicates that population growth is the main factor contributing to the rising burden of ovarian cancer among women of reproductive age worldwide. However, the impact of epidemiological changes varies significantly by region: in high-SDI region, the burden of ovarian cancer is notably reduced, likely due to effective preventive measures such as the use of oral contraceptives. In contrast, many low- and middle-SDI regions, especially in South Asia, are experiencing an increase in ovarian cancer burden. This disparity highlights the influence of changing risk factors, including obesity and evolving reproductive patterns, in these regions [39]. It's important to note that the effect of population aging was minimal in this analysis, as it focused solely on women within the reproductive age range. These findings emphasize the need for tailored public health strategies that prioritize primary prevention in regions undergoing transitions while maintaining and enhancing successful interventions in high-SDI areas.

Our ARIMA model projections present a nuanced outlook on the future burden of ovarian cancer in reproductive-aged women. The anticipated stabilization of ASIR and ASPR, coupled with a decline in ASMR and ASDR, signals a critical public health achievement [45]. The ongoing stabilization of incidence and prevalence rates highlights a critical issue: there have been no significant advancements in primary prevention. This stagnation suggests that modifiable risk factors, including obesity and changing reproductive patterns, continue to be widespread, and the deployment of comprehensive and effective primary prevention strategies is still lacking. Consequently, future endeavors should not only maintain the advancements made in treatment and early diagnosis but also enhance research and public health initiatives focused on primary prevention, with the ultimate goal of reducing the incidence of ovarian cancer.

Moreover, the limitations of this study are evident in several areas. First, regarding data timeliness, one limitation is the use of GBD 2021 data. While the GBD 2023 update has been released, it is currently accessible only to authorized collaborators and is not yet available for public download. Therefore, our analysis is based on the most recent publicly accessible data (GBD 2021). Second, while the GBD provides unparalleled comparability across time and geography, it relies on modeled estimates rather than direct measurement for many locations. Although the models incorporate extensive data and account for uncertainty, the absence of laboratory-confirmed or registry-based data in some regions, particularly those with limited health infrastructure or ongoing conflict, may affect the precision of local burden estimates for conditions like ovarian cancer [46]. Third, the practical interpretation of our findings is inherently constrained by the heterogeneity and varying quality of the original data sources synthesized within the GBD framework. Variations in national reporting standards, diagnostic capabilities, and cause-of-death coding practices across countries and over time can introduce bias that may not be fully resolved by statistical adjustment [47, 48]. Fourth, and specific to our research question, the GBD’s population-level estimates may not fully capture the burden distribution within key subpopulations. For example, factors such as migration, changing fertility patterns, and differential access to healthcare among women of reproductive age could influence ovarian cancer risk and detection in ways that are challenging to disentangle in ecological analyses [49].​ Finally, even with statistical correction, residual under-ascertainment of ovarian cancer cases in regions with limited diagnostic pathology services, may lead to underestimation of true incidence, and deaths prior to diagnosis may affect mortality accuracy. This suggests that the reported disparities in burden between high and low-resource settings should be considered minimum estimates. Therefore, these potential limitations should be carefully considered when interpreting the results, as they provide a more robust foundation for future research.

Conclusion

Overall, the global burden of ovarian cancer has demonstrated a noticeable decline overall, yet significant disparities remain between different countries and regions. High-SDI regions have seen marked reductions in the burden of this disease, while low-and middle-SDI regions are experiencing an increasing burden. The epidemiology of ovarian cancer is influenced by intricate patterns related to age, birth cohort, and period effects. These observations highlight the urgent need for customized prevention and control strategies that focus on high-risk populations and tackle modifiable risk factors, such as elevated body mass index, to lessen the impact of the disease and address the existing inequalities.
