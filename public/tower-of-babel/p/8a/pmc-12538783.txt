Spatiotemporal transcriptomic characteristics of immune and metabolic dysregulation during mouse brain aging
Authors: Minghao Qin, Xuanwen Jin, Yuheng Yang, Fengcongzhe Gong, Jin Wan, Jiawen Wei, Baofa Sun, Xueying Li, Yajuan Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAging and its associated neurodegenerative diseases were increasingly recognized as one of the greatest global health threats. Brain aging involved a multitude of pathological changes, including immune inflammation and metabolic dysregulation.MethodsWe systematically characterized spatiotemporal heterogeneity and dynamic gene expression profiles related to immune and metabolic dysfunction during mouse brain aging. By analyzing differentially expressed genes and conducting functional enrichment and gene set enrichment analysis, we constructed a spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of aging‐associated metabolic and immune features. We further performed transcription factor enrichment analysis, regulatory network construction, weighted gene co-expression network analysis, and single-cell deconvolution analysis to further reveal underlying mechanisms.ResultsOur results showed that, throughout aging, most brain regions underwent widespread immune-inflammatory activation with distinct timing and magnitude: svz exhibited the strongest activation at 26 months, th peaks at 21 months, while olf maintained low immune activation. In terms of metabolic function, different regions responded oppositely to oxidative stress and compensatory mechanisms: mPTP pathway’s genes were significantly upregulated in th but downregulated in cor. Moreover, each brain region possessed its own unique regulatory network, and the H3K27-methyltransferase activity of the PRC2 complex displayed region-dependent age dynamics, suggesting these epigenetic differences (especially ent and olf) might serve as novel targets for anti-aging interventions.ConclusionsThis work presented a high-resolution spatiotemporal transcriptomic atlas of mouse brain aging, revealed regionally staggered immune activation, contrasted metabolic adaptations, and illustrated PRC2-mediated epigenetic remodeling during brain aging. These insights advanced our understanding of brain aging mechanisms and identified region-specific therapeutic targets for precision interventions in neurodegenerative diseases.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07171-1.

Introduction

Brain aging referred to the progressive structural and functional changes that occurred in the brain with advancing age, often manifesting as cognitive decline, emotional instability, and behavioral changes [1]. As aging progresses, multiple domains of cognitive function tended to deteriorate. In recent years, with the intensification of global aging, brain aging and the neurodegenerative diseases it triggered had gradually became a focal point of neuroscience research [2, 3]. A substantial body of research indicated that brain aging was a multidimensional biological process involving several pathological changes, such as immune-inflammatory activation, metabolic dysregulation, neuronal functional decline, and aberrant epigenetic regulation [4–6].

Abnormalities in the immune system might represent one of the core driving factors of brain aging. Studies had found that excessive activation of microglia in the aging brain led to a chronic low-grade inflammatory state, a phenomenon termed “inflammaging” [7–9]. Further research had indicated that inflammaging was not only closely linked to neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD) but might also influence other types of neural injury and cognitive decline [10–13].

Metabolic dysfunction was another significant characteristic of brain aging. Mitochondrial dysfunction, abnormal energy metabolism, and oxidative stress had been identified as key pathological mechanisms underlying neuronal degeneration [14, 15]. Research had shown that during aging, the energy metabolic efficiency of brain tissue gradually declined, with restricted neuronal uptake and utilization of glucose, ultimately impairing neural function [16, 17]. Moreover, the reduced capacity for mitophagy associated with aging led to the accumulation of mitochondrial damage, further exacerbating oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, and accelerating neurodegenerative changes [18–20].

The application of spatiotemporal transcriptomics provided a powerful tool for elucidating the dynamic gene expression profiles and temporal variations across different brain regions during the aging process [21, 22]. By systematically analyzing the transcriptional characteristics of specific brain regions at various age stages, researchers could gain in-depth insights into the spatial distribution of aging-related signaling pathways and the local cellular interactions [23–25]. Investigating the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of brain aging was expected to advance the discovery of precise aging biomarkers and offer new theoretical foundations and potential targets for personalized interventions and precision medicine in neurodegenerative diseases [26–29].

While previous studies had made significant strides in elucidating the roles of immune inflammation and metabolic dysfunction in brain aging, they had predominantly focused on individual brain regions or isolated time points [30–32], limiting our understanding of the broader spatiotemporal landscape of aging-related molecular changes across the brain. To address this gap, we utilized and performed in-depth analyses of transcriptomic data from multiple brain regions and time points to uncover dynamic, region-specific transcriptional patterns associated with immune and metabolic alterations during aging. Through this work, we provided novel insights into the complex regulation of brain aging and laid the groundwork for identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Building upon the work of Oliver Hahn et al. [33], this study systematically analyzed the molecular mechanisms underlying immune and metabolic dysregulation during mouse brain aging and identified potential intervention targets. Most analyses were conducted in the R environment (Version 4.3.1). The main experimental and data analysis procedures were as follows.

Data collection and preprocessing

RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data from aging mouse brains were obtained from Oliver Hahn et al. [33], specifically from the corresponding dataset (GEO: GSE212336, accessed on 30 March 2024). Metadata were filtered to include samples from 15 distinct brain regions: motor cortex (cor), visual cortex (vis), entorhinal cortex (ent), dorsal hippocampus (hi), ventral hippocampus (hi2), hypothalamus (hy), thalamus (th), caudate-putamen (cp), pons (pon), medulla (med), cerebellum (cer), olfactory bulb (olf), corpus callosum (cc), choroid plexus (plx), and the neurogenic subventricular zone (svz). Samples were collected at seven time points: 3, 12, 15, 18, 21, 26, and 28 months of age.

Expression profiles for 847 samples across 20,222 genes were obtained. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the FactoMineR package (Version 2.8) [34] to identify and exclude outlier samples based on clustering patterns. A final dataset of 840 samples was retained for downstream analyses.

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analysis

Differential gene expression between each time point and the 3-month baseline was assessed for each brain region with the DESeq function in DESeq2 (Version 1.40.2) [35]. Genes with |log₂ fold change|≥ 0.58 and p < 0.05 were considered differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Results were visualized as heatmaps and line plots using ggplot2 (Version 3.4.4).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment for Biological Process (BP) terms was conducted via compareCluster in clusterProfiler (Version 4.8.3) [36]. DEGs (|log₂ fold change|≥ 0.58, p < 0.05) were mapped to ENTREZ IDs with bitr function. GO enrichment was performed separately for each region (p < 0.05, q < 1), and pairwise term similarity was computed with pairwise_termsim function to optimize layout. Results were displayed as emap plots (emapplot in enrichplot, Version 1.20.3) [37], showing the top three GO terms per region with customized node sizes, edge thicknesses, label sizes, and color schemes.

Region‑specific DEGs overlaps

UpSet plots (UpSetR Version 1.4.0) [38] illustrated overlaps of up- and down- regulated DEGs related to immune and metabolic functions across brain regions. For each region, GO enrichment was repeated by regulation trend, and the top two significantly enriched pathways per region were selected for presentation.

Immune-related gene expression and functional enrichment

Using a predefined immune gene set (1,447 genes (Supplementary Table 1)), PCA and UpSet plots were generated using the same method as above. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) compared 26- versus 3- month samples per region, ranking genes by log₂ fold change. GSEA was performed with clusterProfiler; pathways with p < 0.05 were deemed significant. A bubble plot (ggplot2) highlighted pathways enriched in ten or more regions, and a lollipop plot (ggplot2) illustrated regional, time-specific contrasts. GSEA results were combined with gene annotations into a gene-pathway Normalized Enrichment Score (NES) matrix. Gene-specific heatmaps were drawn with pheatmap (Version 1.0.12) and ComplexHeatmap (Version 2.16.0), ordering columns by brain region and age; custom palettes (via colorRampPalette and colorRamp2) spanned –0.5 to 0.5. A chord diagram (circlize, Version 0.4.16) visualized connections between enriched pathways and genes in the cp.

Transcription factor-target gene regulatory network analysis

Transcription factor enrichment was conducted with the ChEA2022 library in EnrichR (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) [39], using upregulated DEGs from early-mid (12, 15, and 18 months) and mid-late (21, 26, and 28 months) responses versus 3 months (p < 0.01, FDR-corrected). Subsequently, a transcription factor-target gene networks were visualized in Cytoscape (Version 3.9.1) [40] to delineate the dynamic regulatory roles in ent, olf and svz.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA was applied to all 15 brain regions across time points, using the 10,000 most variable genes (by MAD). Outlier samples were checked via hierarchical clustering and PCA. An unsigned network was built with blockwiseModules (WGCNA Version 1.72‑1) [41]; soft‑threshold power was chosen by scale-free topology (R2 > 0.8). Modules (min size = 50, merge height = 0.5) were correlated with region traits (Pearson). Module functions were annotated via GO using clusterProfiler and org.Mm.eg.db, and networks were exported to Cytoscape.

Mitochondrial DEGs gene analyses

Mitochondrial DEGs were identified by intersecting overall DEGs with MitoCarta3.0 genes (1,013 genes; accessed 30 October 2024) [42]. PCA, heatmaps, and line plots followed previous settings. GSEA compared 26- versus 3- month DEGs across regions; results were shown as bubble plots and lollipop plots. A ggplot2 heatmap displayed mPTP-associated gene expression across regions.

Cell type deconvolution

For cp region, single-cell RNA-seq data (GEO: GSE212576, accessed 30 October 2024) were deconvoluted with CIBERSORTx (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/) [43]. Cell-type proportions were predicted online and plotted to show dynamic changes.

Results

Gene expression changes exhibited marked regional specificity and were highly associated with aging, immune responses, metabolic processes, and neural functions

Transcriptomic analyses revealed region- and time- specific gene expression changes across 15 brain regions over the aging process in mice. The results indicated that alterations in gene expression display significant regional specificity and were primarily enriched in pathways related to immune function, metabolism, and neural activity. Initially, PCA revealed that the overall transcriptional profiles varied across brain regions; some regions clustered together—suggesting shared functional or developmental backgrounds—while others (e.g., cer, olf, med, and ent) appeared more distinct, reflecting pronounced regional specificity (Supplementary Fig. 1A).

Using 3-month-old mice as the baseline, DEGs were calculated for each brain region at various time points relative to the baseline (p < 0.05, log2FC > 0.58) and these results were aggregated to obtain the DEGs for each region. Visualization of these DEGs demonstrated that significant changes in gene expression occurred in all brain regions during aging, although the patterns of upregulation and downregulation differed markedly among them (Fig. 1A). Further time-series analysis showed that the trajectories of DEG accumulation varied: in some regions, the number of DEGs increased rapidly at early stages and remained high, whereas in others the changes were more gradual, with significant accumulation occurring only at late stages (Fig. 1B). To further clarify the biological significance of these DEGs, GO enrichment analysis was conducted separately for the upregulated and downregulated genes (Fig. 1C, D). In the upregulated gene set, there was significant enrichment in immune-related functions (as indicated by the blue and green clusters), encompassing both innate and adaptive immune processes (e.g., antigen processing and presentation, regulation of the innate immune response, response to virus, and adaptive immune response) (Fig. 1C). Additionally, metabolic pathways (red cluster), such as the uronic acid metabolic process, glucuronate metabolic process, and cellular glucuronidation, were also enriched (Fig. 1C).

In contrast, the downregulated genes were primarily enriched in pathways associated with neural function and development, including learning or memory, cognition, axonogenesis, and regulation of neurogenesis (Fig. 1D). Moreover, they involved in the processes related to ion transport and membrane potential regulation (e.g., calcium ion transport and regulation of membrane potential) as well as cytoskeletal dynamics (e.g., microtubule bundle formation, cilium movement, and chromosome segregation) (Fig. 1D). The downregulation trend of these pathways implied that aging might lead to diminished neural plasticity and synaptic regulation.

To clarify whether the DEGs at each age point exhibited similar enrichment patterns compared to the 3-month baseline, we further performed GO enrichment analyses for each age group (Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3). The results showed that DEGs at different aging stages were generally enriched in pathways related to immune, metabolic, and neural functions. Although the specific pathways enriched at each time point might differ, they collectively displayed a relatively consistent trend. In addition, we selected the top 1% most significantly enriched pathways from all DEG-enriched pathways and conducted comparative analyses across different time points (Supplementary Figs. 4 and 5) to evaluate the consistency and differences in enrichment patterns. The results indicated that while most pathways showed similar trends across time points, clear differences in activation timing and enrichment magnitude were observed.

Differential gene expression exhibited a high degree of independence across brain regions during aging

To further elucidate the region-specific gene expression patterns during brain aging, UpSet analyses were performed on both upregulated and downregulated DEGs (Supplementary Fig. 1D and E). The results indicated that most DEGs exhibited significant changes in three or fewer brain regions, supporting the conclusion of regional specificity. Notably, med showed the highest number of upregulated DEGs, and th also displayed a pronounced upregulation; the brain regions with specific gene expression patterns differed between upregulated and downregulated genes. Only a few genes changed consistently in ten or more brain regions, and these did not include highly specific regions such as olf and cer. Volcano plots were also generated comparing differential gene expression between 26-month-old and 3-month-old mice for various brain regions to further illustrate the regional differences (Supplementary Fig. 6). These findings demonstrated a high degree of independence or variability in gene expression among different brain regions during aging.

To further clarify the functional pathways associated with these region-specific DEGs, GO enrichment analyses were conducted for each brain region, with the enrichment results for upregulated and downregulated genes presented in Fig. 2A and B, respectively. The upregulated pathways exhibited marked specificity; several brain regions showed enrichment in processes such as microtubule cytoskeleton organization, synaptic vesicle cycle, positive regulation of dendrite extension, cell junction organization, endocytosis, and histone deacetylation. These findings suggested that in certain aging brain regions, microtubule dynamics, synaptic transmission, and gene expression regulation were activated or compensatorily enhanced. Additionally, some regions displayed upregulation of immune-related pathways, indicating the activation of neuroimmune interactions or inflammatory responses during aging [44, 45].

In contrast, the downregulated pathways varied across regions, indicating that different brain regions may exhibited distinct regulatory trends within the same functional modules during aging. The downregulated pathways involved in the processes related to cell cycle control and chromosome segregation (e.g., cell cycle checkpoint and G2/M transition of the mitotic cell cycle), mitochondrial function and oxidative phosphorylation (e.g., oxidative phosphorylation and the respiratory electron transport chain), as well as the processes closely associated with neural function (e.g., neuron development, axon extension, and long-term synaptic potentiation). This encompassed not only the downregulation of energy metabolism and mitochondrial function but also the attenuation of neuronal plasticity and synaptic homeostasis.

In summary, the combination of UpSet plots and functional enrichment analyses revealed significant region-specific gene expression patterns in the aging mouse brain. The DEGs across different brain regions were closely associated with distinct neural or metabolic functions, and there was pronounced spatial heterogeneity in the underlying biological processes and pathways. These findings not only deepened our understanding of region-specific aging mechanisms but also provided important clues for targeted investigations into the dysregulation of immune, metabolic, and neural functions across brain regions. Building on these conclusions, subsequent analysis focused on the transcriptional characteristics of each brain region and their dynamic changes during aging.

Immune response exhibited an overall upregulation trend during aging, as reflected by the expression of multiple cytokines, with distinct patterns observed across different brain regions

To systematically characterize the immune profile during brain aging, immune-related genes were first extracted from the complete transcriptomic dataset and PCA was performed across all time points (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The analysis revealed that most brain regions clustered relatively tightly in PCA space, while pon and med were notably distinct from the others, suggesting unique immune gene expression patterns in these regions during aging.

Next, all immune-related DEGs were matched with previously defined functional pathways and 1,447 DEGs closely associated with immune processes were identified. The UpSet plot for immune-related upregulated DEGs (infl-DEGs) showed that most of these genes were expressed in only a few brain regions, with cc and cer exhibiting a particularly high number of uniquely upregulated genes (Fig. 3A). To further elucidate the functional roles of these immune DEGs, GSEA was performed comparing 26-month-old to 3-month-old mice and the results were presented in a bubble plot for immune pathways significantly upregulated in at least ten brain regions (Fig. 3B). In this plot, bubble size represented the number of genes within each pathway, and color indicated the significance of the enrichment. The results demonstrated that the inflammatory response was significantly upregulated in fourteen brain regions, confirming the pervasive chronic inflammation associated with aging. Moreover, pathways related to the production or response to various cytokines (e.g., IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, IL-12, IFN-γ, and IFN-β) were broadly activated, reflecting a complex, multi-tiered immune regulatory network within the aging brain. Notably, although some pathways (such as T cell activation and chemokine signaling) were upregulated in multiple regions, the number of genes and the level of significance varied among brain regions, indicating that the regulatory intensity of the same immune pathway was not uniform. Interestingly, th did not exhibit significant upregulation across most immune pathways.

To further compare immune activation patterns over time across brain regions, three representative regions were selected—svz, th, and olf—and lollipop plots were constructed (Fig. 3C). The results indicated that svz exhibited the most pronounced inflammatory upregulation at 26 months, possibly due to increased damage or stimulation of its active neural stem cell (NSC) population in late aging. In contrast, th showed marked immune activation around 21 months, though to a lesser extent than svz, while olf demonstrated a weaker or even opposite trend, underscoring its unique immune regulatory pattern. Immune activation patterns were also compared over time across other brain regions (Supplementary Fig. 7). These findings highlighted significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity in inflammatory processes during brain aging, closely tied to the anatomical and functional diversity of brain regions.

At the gene level, forty key immune genes were identified as consistently upregulated and strongly associated with brain aging (Fig. 3D). The heatmap clearly illustrated the expression trends of these genes across different brain regions and time points, emphasizing both the general upregulation of inflammatory responses during aging and the notable spatiotemporal differences. For example, chemokine family members such as CCL5 and CXCL10 were significantly upregulated across multiple regions, indicating their crucial roles in mediating inflammatory responses and possibly promoting immune cell recruitment and activation during aging [46]. Additionally, TREM2—a receptor involved in microglial activation and the clearance of pathological aggregates—was also upregulated in most regions, further supporting the activation of the immune regulatory network in the aging mouse brain [47].

Finally, the chord diagram visualized the relationships between three moderately upregulated immune pathways and 25 key genes in cp (Fig. 3E). The diagram revealed that some genes participated in multiple pathways, suggesting that individual genes might exert multifaceted regulatory effects within the aging-associated inflammatory network. DEGs were also detected to be enriched in the inflammatory response pathways across other brain regions (Supplementary Fig. 8). Collectively, these results provided important insights into the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune responses during brain aging and highlighted potential intervention targets.

Transcription factor regulatory networks revealed the Polycomb Repressive Complex 2 (PRC2) as a potential epigenetic target for anti-aging interventions

In order to investigate transcription factor regulatory patterns across different brain regions during aging, three regions that displayed prominent biphasic temporal changes in both immune response and neural function: ent, olf, and svz were selected for further analysis. Two distinct sets of DEGs were identified based on temporal comparisons and grouped as early-to-middle and middle-to-late aging response clusters. Transcription factor enrichment analyses revealed distinct regulators enriched in each group, and their respective transcriptional regulatory networks showed temporal shifts in regulatory control during aging (Fig. 4A–C). A significant increase in the number of target genes regulated by PRC2 core components (MTF2, SUZ12, EZH2, JARID2) among the top upregulated genes was observed in svz and ent regions from early-middle to middle-late aging stages, which might reflect a compensatory response to epigenetic dysregulation. Conversely, in olf, the number of those target genes exhibited a downward trend, suggesting region-specific differences in PRC2 regulation during aging. Consistent with this, our analysis revealed a region-specific increase in upregulated PRC2 target genes during aging. In ent, this trend indicated a dysregulation in epigenetic repression. Key developmental regulators such as Eomes, Barhl1, and Irx2 became aberrantly re-expressed, disrupting neuronal identity and network function [49]. The reactivation of Hox genes like Hoxb5 further signaled transcriptional collapse, resembling patterns seen in PRC2-deficient neurodegeneration. In svz, a marked increase in up-regulated PRC2 targets were observed, especially at late stages. Derepressed genes such as Shh, Wnt9b, and Sox11 disrupted NSC homeostasis, forcing premature or inappropriate differentiation and depleting the stem cell reservoir [50]. Unlike other brain regions, the core components of PRC2 in olf exhibited a “relative decline trend” during middle to late aging stages, with the expression of its target genes showing a biphasic pattern of dysfunction. In the early stage, developmental genes such as Msx2 and Twist1, along with pro-apoptotic and pro-inflammatory genes including Cxcl5, Icam1, and Tnfrsf9, were significantly upregulated, leading to widespread neuronal loss. In late aging, the expression of these dysregulated genes decreased, suggesting a transcriptomic remodeling driven by selective neuronal survival and compensatory mechanisms.

WGCNA-based analysis of brain region-specific modules and construction of gene interaction networks

To comprehensively validate previous inferences regarding brain region-specific differences and functional specificity, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) was performed on the transcriptomic data from all 840 mouse samples. By selecting the top 10,000 most highly expressed genes and constructing a weighted co-expression network based on their expression dynamics from 3 to 28 months, genes were grouped with similar expression trends into distinct modules, ultimately identifying fifteen gene co-expression modules (with some genes remaining unassigned). The module eigengene expression was then correlated with the fifteen brain regions and the module-brain region correlation heatmap was generated (Fig. 5A). Particular focus was placed on the four modules with the highest correlations (e.g., pink, black, greenyellow, and brown), which were closely associated with olf, hy, th, and cer, respectively—findings that corroborated our earlier analyses. Moreover, some modules did not display high specificity to a single brain region, but rather reflected a common expression trend across multiple regions, suggesting that certain functional pathways appeared to be universally activated or suppressed during aging (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. 9).

In the functional enrichment analysis, the pink module was significantly enriched in processes such as the positive regulation of cell projection organization, modulation of the Wnt signaling pathway, postsynaptic structure, actin bundle organization, and forebrain development, which was consistent with olf’s roles in neural development and synaptic remodeling. The greenyellow module was enriched in pathways including proteoglycan metabolism, monoamine transport, neuronal apoptosis, hindbrain development, intracellular calcium homeostasis, pain perception, synaptic organization, and the regulation of adenylate cyclase-coupled G protein-coupled receptor signaling, consistent with th’s functions in sensory transmission and higher-order central regulation (Supplementary Fig. 10). Further clustering analysis of inter-module correlations revealed that some modules exhibited high similarity in their expression patterns and clustered together in the dendrogram, whereas modules with marked differences across brain regions tended to show lower aggregation (Fig. 5C).

To further elucidate the regulatory relationships within key modules, gene interaction networks were constructed using Cytoscape (Fig. 5D). In olf–related pink module, core genes such as Pbx1, Gng4, Kank3, and Rasa2 played critical roles in neural development, synaptic plasticity, and neuronal projection, which was highly consistent with olf’s functions in odor recognition and neural plasticity. In th-associated greenyellow module, genes including Lef1, Vav3, Ramp3, and Prkcd occupied central positions in the network, which were mainly involved in the Wnt signaling pathway, regulation of neuronal survival, and modulation of neural transmission, thus aligning with th’s role in sensory information relay and higher-order central regulation [51]. This analysis further revealed the core genes and their interactions within brain region-specific modules, providing potential functional targets for future experimental studies.

Brain aging was directly associated with region-specific metabolic and neural functions

Building on previous immune findings, further analyses were conducted on the impact of mitochondrial function on brain aging. Using the MitoCarta3.0 database, 1,013 mitochondrial-related genes were filtered from the overall set of DEGs and analyzed their expression changes across different brain regions and time points. The PCA revealed significant expression differences of mitochondrial genes among various brain regions; notably, certain regions (e.g., cer and pon) displayed a relatively dispersed distribution in PCA space, suggesting unique regulation of mitochondrial function (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

The cumulative trends in the number of mitochondrial DEGs across brain regions were subsequently quantified over time (Fig. 6B). In some regions, such as svz, the number of differentially expressed mitochondrial genes increased steadily with age, whereas in others the changes were relatively modest. In contrast to the immune analysis results—where chronic inflammation rose ubiquitously—the overall number of mitochondrial-related gene changes was comparatively lower (Fig. 6A, B). GSEA revealed brain-region-specific enrichment of mitochondrial pathways in 26-month-old mice compared to 3-month-old controls, as shown in Fig. 6C and D. Unexpectedly, some metabolic pathways that were generally presumed to be widely downregulated during aging did not show consistent decline across most brain regions; instead, most pathways were significantly downregulated in only a few regions. Moreover, pronounced upregulation trends were observed in specific metabolic pathways in several brain regions. For example, svz exhibited significant upregulation of the fatty acid oxidation pathway, olf was enriched for choline and betaine metabolism as well as glycine metabolism, and th demonstrated upregulation of pathways related to calcium homeostasis and Complex V (ATP synthase) function. We interpreted these findings as potential “metabolic compensation” or “adaptive responses” aimed at counteracting the energy crisis and inflammatory damage associated with aging.

Concurrently, dysregulation of calcium homeostasis in th was recognized as a key feature of brain aging. Upregulation of calcium-related pathways and Complex V function was observed in th, which might have represented a compensatory response to calcium imbalance that helped preserve basic sensory relay and neural regulatory capabilities during aging [52]. Moreover, the activation of the fatty acid oxidation pathway in svz was likely linked to the maintenance of the NSC pool and its regenerative potential, thereby assisting in countering oxidative stress and inflammatory damage induced by aging.

Of particular note, we focused on three pathways—namely, the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), Complex III subunits, and Complex IV subunits—which exhibited different trends across brain regions. For instance, the mPTP pathway was upregulated in th [53], whereas it was downregulated in cor, potentially reflecting differences in gene regulation or the influence of the local microenvironment. To explore the dynamic changes of these pathways over time, we constructed lollipop plots for svz, olf, and th (Fig. 6E). The results indicated that the same metabolic pathway could display markedly different expression patterns across brain regions and at various time points, suggesting that different regions might employ distinct metabolic compensation or adaptive strategies to cope with the energy demands and oxidative stress associated with aging. Furthermore, a comprehensive heatmap for the mPTP pathway confirmed that the expression patterns of these genes varied significantly among brain regions (Fig. 6F). We also noted that genes belonging to the SLC25 family—which encoded mitochondrial inner membrane transporters responsible for transmembrane transport of various metabolites, including fatty acids, phosphate, and calcium [54]—exhibited significant expression differences across brain regions. These variations might reveal that the brain adopted diverse strategies to maintain metabolic homeostasis in response to energy crises and oxidative stress during aging.

To further elucidate changes in cellular composition within cp region during aging, deconvolution analysis was performed using a single-cell reference dataset to predict the relative proportions of various cell types. The results indicated that pericytes consistently exhibited the highest proportion across all time points, showing either an initial increase followed by a decrease or a continuous rise over time. Astrocytes and microglia were present at all time points, but in lower relative proportions, with astrocytes gradually declining as aging progressed. Mature oligodendrocytes reached their highest proportion at 28 months, suggesting an increased demand for myelin repair or the maintenance of neural conduction. Discussion

This study, based on a large-scale, multi-region, multi-time-point transcriptomic dataset, systematically elucidated the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune and metabolic dysfunction during brain aging in mice, revealing significant heterogeneity in gene expression, functional pathways, transcriptional regulation, and cellular composition across different brain regions. As aging progresses, the brain might face increasing challenges or imbalances in immune regulation and energy metabolism, which in turn could adversely affect the structural homeostasis and physiological functions of the brain [56, 57]. Our results not only validated the central roles of “inflammaging” and mitochondrial dysfunction in brain aging but also uncovered distinct regional regulatory patterns, providing novel molecular insights into neurodegenerative diseases and their impact on cognitive function. Immune-related genes were generally upregulated in most brain regions; but the degree of activation and the underlying pathways exhibited marked regional differences. UpSet and GSEA analyses indicated that key pro-inflammatory pathways—such as the inflammatory response, T cell activation, and chemokine signaling—were significantly activated in the majority of regions, whereas th showed little change. These observations aligned with reports of hip inflammation accompanying spatial memory deficits in Alzheimer’s models [22], and also resonated with studies of endothelial inflammation in cardiovascular disease [58].

In contrast, olf maintained a relatively low pro-inflammatory state—likely via specialized, neurogenic-associated immunoprotective mechanisms—potentially mitigating early motor and cognitive decline in Parkinson’s disease. This might be related to its deep anatomical location or unique gene expression profile. Previous studies had suggested that higher levels of anti-inflammatory factors or specific signaling mechanisms in the thalamus could mitigate excessive inflammation during aging [59, 60]. Similarly, olf showed little to no upregulation in inflammatory pathways, which might have indicated an inherent immune protective mechanism. Olf maintained ongoing neurogenesis [61] and, due to its direct exposure to external pathogens, it likely required a robust first-line defense, thereby reducing the typical inflammatory responses observed in deeper brain regions [62].

Although aging was generally accompanied by reduced energy metabolism and increased oxidative stress, the specific patterns varied across brain regions. For example, svz exhibited upregulation of fatty acid oxidation pathways at late stages, suggesting a local metabolic compensation mechanism to sustain NSC regeneration—an effect reminiscent of lipid-metabolism interventions in cardiovascular aging [63]. By contrast, olf bulb showed enhanced choline and glycine metabolism, which may both alleviate oxidative damage and, through central-peripheral signaling, influence systemic blood pressure and cardiac function—thereby supporting cognitive health at the organismal level [64]. Furthermore, certain pathways (e.g., the mitochondrial permeability transition pore) displayed opposite expression trends across different brain regions, underscoring the importance of region-specific gene regulatory networks in coping with energy deficits and oxidative stress.

Notably, olf showed significant upregulation of the glycine metabolism pathway. Previous studies had indicated that supplementation with glycine and N-acetylcysteine (GlyNAC) could ameliorate oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction in elderly individuals, thereby reducing inflammation and aging markers [65]. Thus, olf might reinforce glycine metabolism to sustain its sensory and immune balance. Additionally, the cholinergic innervation of olf underwent marked changes during aging, suggesting a unique metabolic regulatory mechanism in the maintenance of olf function and neural plasticity [66, 67].

Our WGCNA co-expression and transcription factor regulatory network analyses further corroborated these findings. Distinct gene modules in each brain region showed functional enrichment in immunity, metabolism, neural development, and synaptic transmission with high regional specificity. Previous studies had shown that PRC2-mediated DNA methylation could serve as a biomarker of aging across mammalian tissues [68, 69]. In addition, other studies had demonstrated the important roles of PRC2 in neuronal protection [70], regulation of innate immunity [71], and promotion of healthy brain aging [72]. Our study further confirmed that the differential regulation of PRC2 complex activity in ent, olf, and svz reflected region-specific epigenetic adaptations to aging.

Specifically, in ent, a critical hub for memory and navigation, PRC2 maintained neuronal identity by repressing developmental genes whose reactivation destabilized mature circuits—potentially contributing to early Alzheimer’s pathology [73–75]. In svz, the brain’s largest neurogenic niche, PRC2 preserved stem cell quiescence and lineage fidelity by restraining pro-differentiation genes, thus supporting adult neurogenesis and cognitive repair [76]. In contrast, olf—highly reliant on neuroplasticity—was particularly vulnerable to epigenetic instability. Here, PRC2 modulated inflammatory and apoptotic pathways, and its early dysfunction might underlie progressive olf decline with aging [61]. Together, these findings highlighted PRC2 as a key epigenetic regulator of region-specific vulnerability in brain aging, with therapeutic potential for targeting memory and sensory deficits [77].

Finally, our single-cell deconvolution analysis revealed dynamic remodeling of cellular composition in the cp region—a part of the basal ganglia—during aging. Changes in the proportions and interactions among mature oligodendrocytes, dopaminergic neurons, and glial cells provided direct evidence of coordinated or antagonistic relationships within brain regions. Such cellular shifts correlated strongly with spatial learning and memory deficits and might also impinge upon myelin repair [78], and blood–brain barrier integrity [79], thereby influencing both cognitive function and cardiovascular health on a global scale.

Conclusions

Overall, our study elucidated the complex spatiotemporal characteristics of immune and metabolic dysfunction in the aging mouse brain. The regional differences observed in gene expression, pathway activation, and transcriptional regulation underscored that aging was not a uniform biological process but rather the result of finely tuned regulatory mechanisms. These findings provided a systematic perspective on the molecular underpinnings of brain aging and neurodegenerative diseases, while also identifying potential targets for the development of tailored intervention strategies—such as key pro-inflammatory mediators, region-specific metabolic regulators, and epigenetic modifiers.

Nevertheless, this study had certain limitations. First, transcriptomic data did not directly reflect protein levels and functional states; therefore, future research should incorporate proteomics and metabolomics to validate the roles of key regulatory factors. Second, although our analysis spanned multiple brain regions and time points, the intricate in vivo intercellular interactions and the influence of local microenvironments warranted further investigation using higher-resolution single-cell and spatial omics techniques.

Future work would focus on integrating multi-omics data and functionally validating the mechanisms of key regulatory factors to further elucidate how different brain regions coordinated immune, metabolic, and neural functions during aging. We believed that these efforts would provide a robust theoretical foundation for the design of precise anti-aging and neuroprotective strategies and would open new avenues for early intervention in neurodegenerative diseases.
