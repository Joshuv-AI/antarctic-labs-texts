Understanding pneumococcal vaccine perceptions in seven countries: Findings from a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Donovan B. Smith, Chelsea D’Silva, Scott Ratzan, Kenneth Rabin, Madison Fullerton, Jia Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPneumococcal disease remains a major global cause of morbidity and mortality, yet vaccine uptake varies. Understanding how demographic, socioeconomic, and regional factors shape pneumococcal vaccine perceptions is essential for developing effective public health strategies. We conducted a stratified, cross-sectional survey of 7,000 adults across seven countries (Canada, Brazil, France, India, Nigeria, the United Kingdom, and the United States), with sampling structured by age, sex, income, and education. Perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety, efficacy, importance, and disease concern were assessed using Likert scales. χ2 tests, Kruskal–Wallis tests, and ordinal regression models examined demographic and country-level differences, with trust in information sources included as a predictor. Confidence in pneumococcal vaccine safety and efficacy was highest among adults aged 35–44 and 65+, whereas younger adults expressed the greatest concern about pneumococcal disease (p < .001). Women showed the strongest support across all measures, including awareness and importance of vaccination at different life stages (p < .001). Higher income and education were associated with greater confidence and awareness (p < .001). Country-level variation was substantial: Brazil and India reported the highest agreement with pneumococcal vaccine safety and efficacy, while France and the UK reported the lowest, despite established health systems. Trust in healthcare providers and public health institutions was the strongest predictor of positive perceptions, while informal sources had little influence. Demographic, socioeconomic, and country-level differences significantly shape pneumococcal vaccine perceptions. Efforts to strengthen trust in healthcare providers, address misinformation among younger adults, and tailor outreach to local contexts are needed to improve uptake globally.

Introduction

Vaccines are a cornerstone of modern medicine, essential to preventing severe illness and reducing overall mortality rates from infectious diseases globally.1 The impact of widespread vaccination on improved public health outcomes cannot be understated; previous global estimates have highlighted that vaccines save between 2–3 million lives per year from mortality due to infectious diseases.2 Among these, pneumococcal vaccines play a critical role in protecting vulnerable populations, including children, seniors, and individuals living with chronic health conditions, from respiratory illnesses.3 These populations are considered vulnerable due to the state of their immune systems. In young children, immune function is still developing, while in older adults and individuals with chronic illnesses, immune function is often weakened by age or disease progression.4 Pneumococcal disease, caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, is a leading cause of pneumonia, meningitis, and upper respiratory tract infection, all of which have been linked with significant morbidity and mortality in these vulnerable populations.5

Despite widespread availability, vaccine hesitancy, specifically toward pneumococcal disease, remains high in at-risk populations around the globe.6–8 Historically, several factors have influenced vaccine perceptions and uptake, including trust in public healthcare systems, confidence in the safety and efficacy of vaccines, socioeconomic status, and level of education.9 In recent years, the COVID-19 pandemic has further changed the dynamics surrounding widespread vaccination efforts, bringing discussions surrounding infectious disease prevention into the public discourse. Specific to pneumococcal disease, a recent multi-timepoint survey found that post-COVID-19, individuals perceived greater benefits of the pneumococcal vaccine for both personal and public safety when compared to pre-pandemic views.10 While shining the spotlight on infectious diseases have helped to reinforce the importance of immunization for many individuals, it has also fueled misinformation and skepticism in some demographics.11 These shifting attitudes highlight the need for an updated understanding of vaccine perceptions to help refine public health initiatives aimed at preventing infectious diseases, specifically, pneumococcal disease.

While prior studies have examined pneumococcal vaccine hesitancy within individual countries or specific populations,7,12,13 less is known about how perceptions of this vaccine compare across diverse healthcare systems and socioeconomic conditions using a standardized framework. Multinational comparisons are essential for uncovering context-specific barriers to vaccine uptake while also revealing shared determinants of hesitancy across geographically distinct settings. Without such comparative data, it remains difficult to determine whether observed patterns reflect local structural factors or broader shifts in public trust in vaccination.

To address this gap, our study compares pneumococcal vaccine perceptions across seven countries and examine how these perceptions vary by age, sex, income, and education level, all within the lens of the post-pandemic world. The countries surveyed – Canada, Brazil, France, India, the United States, the United Kingdom, and Nigeria – were selected to reflect diverse socioeconomic and healthcare settings. A recent global review highlighted the strong link between socioeconomic status and vaccine uptake,14 underscoring the importance of capturing these differences within a standardized multinational framework. By integrating responses from these diverse countries and contexts, our analysis aims to provide actionable insights into the structural and demographic factors shaping global pneumococcal vaccine perceptions, thereby informing more targeted public health campaigns and policies to improve uptake.

Materials and methods

We conducted an observational cross-sectional survey to examine perceptions of the pneumococcal vaccine, and how these perceptions differed both with multiple demographic markers, and across the seven countries polled.

Survey instruments

The survey contained three parts: (1) demographic questions, (2) the Vaccine Trust Gauge,15 and (3) pneumococcal vaccine-related questions. The Vaccine Trust Gauge is a previously validated instrument designed to assess vaccine trust, including perceptions of vaccine safety, efficacy, and importance, as well as confidence in health authorities and information sources. The Vaccine Trust Gauge responses were measured on a Likert-type scale and aggregated into trust scores (e.g., low, medium, high) fur use in analysis; the tool has demonstrated high internal reliability in prior work.15 Demographic questions included, but were not limited to, sex, age, education, and average yearly income. Questions related to pneumococcal vaccine perceptions were included in the survey based on the recommendations of public health and infectious disease physicians. These questions were adapted from previous studies and focused on the knowledge, perceived safety and efficacy, and intention to receive the pneumococcal vaccine. The full survey can be found in the supplementary methods.

Recruitment and data collection

We collected survey data between August 7th – August 16th, 2023, from n = 7,000 respondents aged 18 y and older from Canada (n = 1,000); Brazil (n = 1,000); France (n = 1,000); India (n = 1,000); the United States (n = 1,000); the United Kingdom (n = 1,000); and Nigeria (n = 1,000). Consensus Strategies recruited participants through telephone contact, social media outreach, and direct e-mail solicitation, using an online opt-in panel. For social media outreach, they posted recruitment materials on platforms such as X and Instagram, directing participants to the online survey. The online survey was available in English, French, Portuguese, and Hindi based on the predominant languages spoken in each country.

We implemented a stratum-based sample design that focused on age, gender, statistical regions, median income, and levels of education within each country. Income was self-reported and determined as above or below average based on national median income levels for each respondent’s region. We set a minimum of n = 50 participants for each stratum and calculated target enrollment to reflect the population distribution of each subgroup. Emerson College Polling in Boston, Massachusetts, USA, administered the survey on our behalf. We did not collect or store any personally identifiable information, nor did we have direct communication with any participants; as such, Emerson College’s Institutional Review Board reviewed this project and deemed it exempt from research ethics oversight (protocol number 22-019-F-X). Researchers have previously used this survey method to gather data from a random population sample.11

Analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for all variables. We categorized responses for pneumococcal vaccine perspectives as strongly agree, somewhat agree, neutral, somewhat disagree, and strongly disagree. We performed Chi-Square tests to assess the association between vaccine beliefs and categorical variables, such as age, socioeconomic status, and country of residence. We used Pearson’s R and Spearman’s correlation coefficients to evaluate the strength and direction of the associations between variables. We conducted Kruskal-Wallis tests to evaluate differences in vaccine perceptions across countries and demographic groups. We selected the Kruskal-Wallis test rather than ANOVA because perception outcomes were measured on ordinal Likert scales and did not meet assumptions of normality. We applied Bonferroni corrections to adjust for multiple comparisons with Kruskal-Wallis tests. We used crosstabulations to explore the distribution of vaccine beliefs across various subgroups. We measured trust in health recommendation sources (healthcare providers, local health departments, national public health agencies, employers, and friends/family) using 4-point Likert scales ranging from “no trust at all” to “a great deal of trust.” We entered these variables into ordinal logistic regression models as categorical predictors using dummy coding, with “no trust” serving as the reference category. We included all trust variables simultaneously in the models. We weighted our analyses by frequency data to account for survey sampling design and population representation. We conducted all analyses using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Influence of participant demographics on pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

We implemented crosstabulations to examine how age, sex, income, and education influenced perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety and efficacy. We also examined how these factors impacted awareness of the vaccine, concerns about pneumococcal disease, and beliefs regarding the importance of vaccination for both adults and children.

Age

For perceptions on pneumococcal vaccine effectiveness, the highest overall agreement (percentage of respondents within each age group that either strongly or somewhat agreed) was among the 35–44-y-old group (31.6% strongly agree (SA), 28.4% somewhat agree (SWA)), closely followed by those 65 and older (35.1% SA, 23.2% SWA) (χ2(20) = 172.495, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.078) (Figure 1). We observed a similar trend for perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety, with respondents aged 65+ overall agreeing slightly more than those aged 35–44 (36.7% SA and 24.4% SWA in 65+ versus 38.1% SA and 22.6% SWA in 35–44) (χ2 (20) = 110.411, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.063). Notably, the 18–24-y-old group had the most overall disagreement (percent of respondents that either strongly disagreed (SD) or somewhat disagreed (SWD)) with perceptions of both pneumococcal vaccine safety (5.5% SWD, 2.0% SD) and efficacy (9.3% SWD, 2.1% SD) (Figure 1). When evaluating awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine, the 65+ group had the highest recognition of the vaccine (59.8%), while the 18–24-y-old group had the lowest (39.5%) (Figure 1). This association was statistically significant (χ2 (10) = 118.416, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.092). Age was also significantly associated with concern about pneumococcal disease, with younger respondents expressing the highest levels of SA responses (42.0% of 18–24-y-olds, 41.6% of 25–34-y-olds, and 41.6% of 35–44-y-olds) followed by older respondents (34.8% of those 45–54, 29.9% of those 45–54, and 33.4% of those 65+; χ2 (20) = 130.309, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.068) (Figure 1).

Regarding vaccine importance, the 35–44-y-old group had the highest overall agreement for both childhood vaccination (38.8% SA, 25.2% SWA) and vaccination for older/at-risk adults (45% SA, 22.6% SWA) (Figure 1). These associations were statistically significant (children: χ2 (20) = 152.411, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.074; at-risk/older adults: χ2 (20) = 106.362, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.062).

Sex

For perceptions related to the effectiveness of the pneumococcal vaccine, women had the highest overall agreement, with 30.8% responding SA and 25.6% responding SWA. This was followed by men (29.3% SA, 24.6% SWA), and non-binary individuals (19.4% SA, 22.6% SWA) (Figure 2). The association between sex and belief in pneumococcal vaccine effectiveness was statistically significant (χ2 (8) = 35.286, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.050). We saw similar results with perceptions related the safety of the pneumococcal vaccine; women had the greatest overall agreement (33.4% SA, 23.9% SWA), followed by men (32.6% SA, 24.5% SWA) and non-binary individuals (22.6% SA, 9.7% SWA) (Figure 2). However, the association between sex and belief in pneumococcal vaccine safety was not statistically significant (χ2 (8) = 11.815, p = .16, Cramer’s V = 0.029). Regarding awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine, women were the most likely to have heard of the vaccine prior to being surveyed (56.4%), while non-binary individuals were the least likely to have heard of the vaccine (34.4%) (Figure 2). Pneumococcal vaccine awareness was significantly associated with sex (χ2 (8) = 147.745, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.103). Sex was also significantly associated with concern about illness caused by pneumococcal disease, with women expressing the highest levels of concern (39.5% SA, 23.9% SWA), followed by non-binary individuals (29.0% SA, 32.3% SWA), and men (35.6% SA, 23.6% SWA) (χ2 (8) = 20.445, p = .0090, Cramer’s V = 0.038) (Figure 2).

Analyzing vaccine importance, women had the highest overall agreement for both childhood vaccination (34.0% SA, 21.4% SWA) and vaccination for older/at-risk adults (42.3% SA, 24.9% SWA) (Figure 2). Sex was significantly associated with perceptions of vaccination importance (children: χ2 (8) = 18.727, p = .016, Cramer’s V = 0.037; at-risk/older adults: χ2 (8) = 31.158, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.047).

Income status

When assessing income status on perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine effectiveness, individuals with above-average income had the greatest overall agreement (33.3% SA, 26.6&% SWA, respectively) when compared to individuals with below-average income (26.8% SA, 23.6% SWA). Similar trends were found when assessing how income status shaped perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety (above average: 37.2% SA, 24.8% SWA; below average: 28.7% SA, 23.5% SWA) (Figure 3). These associations were both statistically significant (effectiveness: χ2 (4) = 70.548, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.100; safety: χ2 (4) = 96.333, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.117). Awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine was significantly higher in above-average income individuals (53.3% versus 46.4%, χ2 (2) = 45.878, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.081), and concern over illness caused by pneumococcal disease was also significantly greater in above-average income individuals (39.1% SA and 24.5% SWA versus 36.0% SA and 23.1% SWA, χ2 (4) = 26.112, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.061) (Figure 3). Perceptions about vaccine importance followed the same trend, with above average respondents reporting stronger overall agreement on the importance of vaccination for older/at-risk adults (44.3% SA, 23.7% SWA) and for children (34.9% SA, 21.9% SWA) than their below-average counterparts (35.8% SA and 24.8% SWA for at risk/older adults; 29.3% SA and 23.3% SWA for children) (at-risk/older adults χ2 (4) = 32.099, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.068; children: χ2 (4) = 61.993, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.094) (Figure 3). Education

Individuals who received a college degree or higher reported significantly greater agreement on the efficacy of the pneumococcal vaccine when compared to those without a college degree (33.7 SA and 28.1% SWA versus 29.0% SA and 24.3% SWA, respectively). Similar trends were observed for perceptions related to pneumococcal vaccine safety when comparing those with and without a college degree (36.8% SA and 27.2% SWA versus 31.9% SA and 23.3% SWA, respectively) (effectiveness: χ2 (4) = 34.686, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.070; safety: χ2 (4) = 38.780, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.074). Awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine was also higher in individuals with a college degree (57.3% versus 47.9% without, χ2 (2) = 44.425, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.080), as was concern about illness caused by pneumococcal disease (37.7% SA and 25.8% SWA with a college degree versus 37.5% SA and 23.2% SWA without, χ2 (4) = 20.791, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.055). Perceptions on vaccine importance followed a similar trend, as college-educated respondents showed greater agreement that pneumococcal vaccines are important for both older/at-risk adults (43.3% SA and 26.5% SWA) and children (35.6% SA and 24.1% SWA) compared to those without a college degree (older/at-risk adults: 39.2% SA and 23.7% SWA; children: 31.2% SA and 22.2% SWA) (at-risk/older adults: χ2 (4) = 31.523, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.067; children: χ2 (4) = 27.313, p < .001, Cramer’s V = 0.062).

Differing pneumococcal vaccine perceptions between surveyed countries

We used Kruskal-Wallis tests to identify significant differences in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions across surveyed countries. Perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety varied significantly by country (χ2 (6) = 426.465, p < .001, η2 = 0.060). By also applying crosstabulations to this data, we found that Brazil had the highest overall agreement regarding vaccine efficacy (52.8% SA, 20.9% SWA), followed by India (35.3% SA, 27.7% SWA), the United States (31.4% SA, 25.8% SWA), Canada (23.7% SA, 27.7% SWA), Nigeria (30.5% SA, 23.4% SWA), the United Kingdom (22.0% SA, 22.5% SWA), and France (14.6% SA, 27.5% SWA) (Figure 4). We observed a similar trend for perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety, which also differed significantly between countries (χ2 (6) = 447.153, p < .001, η2 = 0.063). Brazil again had the highest overall agreement (54.3% SA, 20.7% SWA), followed by India (41.1% SA, 27.7% SWA), the United States (35.0% SA, 23.0% SWA), Nigeria (31.9% SA, 20.4% SWA), Canada (25.7% SA, 28.4% SWA), the United Kingdom (24.7% SA, 23.1% SWA), and France (17.9% SA, 25.9% SWA) (Figure 4). Additionally, our crosstabulations found that Brazil had the highest proportion of respondents expressing concern about pneumococcal disease (66.1% SA, 17.3% SWA), whereas the United States had the highest proportion of respondents who disagreed with being concerned about pneumococcal disease (9.0% SD, 6.3% SWD) (Figure 4). Role of health recommendation sources in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

We built ordinal regression models to assess how trust in health recommendations from various sources influenced perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine safety and efficacy. These models were statistically significant, indicating that trust in different health authorities significantly impacted perceptions of the vaccine (safety: χ2 (15) = 2817.921, p < .001; efficacy: χ2 (15) = 2572.547, p < .001). Both models accounted for a considerable proportion of the observed variance in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions (safety: Nagelkerke R2 = 0.36; efficacy: Nagelkerke R2 = 0.33). We used likelihood ratio tests to confirm that including trust in health recommendations significantly improved the fit of both models compared to baseline models.

Healthcare providers had the strongest influence on vaccine perceptions. Individuals with a great deal of trust in healthcare providers were significantly more likely to perceive the pneumococcal vaccine as safe (β = −2.139, p < .001, OR = 0.118, 95% CI [0.085, 0.163]) and effective (β = −2.234, p < .001, OR = 0.107, 95% CI [0.078, 0.147]), compared to those with no trust in healthcare providers.

Trust in local health departments and national public health agencies was also strongly associated with positive vaccine perceptions. Respondents with high trust in local health departments were more likely to perceive the pneumococcal vaccine as safe (β = −1.565, p < .001, OR = 0.209, 95% CI [0.149, 0.293]) and effective (β = −1.662, p < .001, OR = 0.190, 95% CI [0.137, 0.264]), while strong trust in national public health agencies was similarly associated with greater confidence in vaccine safety (β = −1.296, p < .001, OR = 0.274, 95% CI [0.190, 0.395]) and efficacy (β = −1.058, p < .001, OR = 0.347, 95% CI [0.244, 0.495]). In contrast, strong trust in health recommendations from employers, friends, and family were weaker predictors of individual pneumococcal vaccine perceptions. High trust in employer recommendations was associated with higher perceived vaccine safety (β = −0.493, p < .001, OR = 0.611, 95% CI [0.493, 0.758]); however, this association was not statistically significant for vaccine efficacy (β = −0.195, p = .071, OR = 0.823, 95% CI [0.666, 1.02]). Similarly, high trust in recommendations from close friends and family was associated with greater confidence in both vaccine safety (β = −0.562, p < .001, OR = 0.570, 95% CI [0.453, 0.717]) and efficacy (β = −0.635, p < .001, OR = 0.530, 95% CI [0.423, 0.664]); however, the magnitude of these associations remained notably smaller than those observed for recommendations from healthcare providers and public health authorities. Discussion

Our study provides a multinational examination of how participant demographics, geography, and trust in public healthcare systems shape perceptions of the pneumococcal vaccine across seven different countries. Although prior work has explored vaccine perceptions broadly and examined pneumococcal vaccination within individual countries or specific subpopulations,7,12,13 to our knowledge, limited research has applied a standardized survey framework across multiple countries to determine adult pneumococcal vaccine perceptions in parallel. Further, much of the recent vaccine confidence literature has centered on COVID-19 or influenza vaccination8,11,16; by focusing specifically on pneumococcal vaccination in the post-pandemic context, our findings offer insight into whether broader shifts in public vaccine trust extend beyond immunization efforts related to the pandemic. Integrating demographic stratification with cross-country comparisons and original regression modeling of trust in information sources allowed us to quantify the significant disparities in pneumococcal vaccine awareness, perceived efficacy and safety, and overall concern about pneumococcal disease. Together, our results highlight the need for tailored public heath strategies that address context-specific barriers to pneumococcal vaccine uptake.

Age-related differences in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

We found multiple significant associations between respondent age and perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine efficacy and safety. Middle-aged and older adults reported the highest levels of agreement that the vaccine is both effective and safe. In contrast, younger adults expressed the lowest overall agreement with vaccine efficacy and safety, despite simultaneously reporting the highest concern about illness caused by pneumococcal disease. This discrepancy suggests that younger individuals may harbor greater skepticism toward the pneumococcal vaccine, even though they recognize the risks associated with the disease itself. Previous work supports this interpretation; younger populations often perceive themselves to be in better overall health and less vulnerable to serious illness, which can contribute to greater vaccine hesitancy when compared to adults aged 50+ .17 Further, younger adults may be disproportionately exposed to vaccine misinformation through social media and online news outlets, further fueling uncertainty and distrust toward getting vaccinated.18,19 Finally, because younger adults are generally healthier and have fewer routine healthcare encounters, they may be less likely to receive direct clinician recommendations for pneumococcal vaccination, which could also reduce perceived safety, efficacy, and necessity of the vaccine.20

Our analysis also revealed that adults aged 65 and older reported the highest levels of awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine. This heightened awareness likely reflects targeted public health campaigns aimed at increasing vaccine uptake in older populations, who are at a higher risk of hospitalization and death from pneumococcal infections.21 Interestingly, our results indicated that of the age groups surveyed, adults aged 35–44 were the most likely to support vaccination for both children and older/at-risk adults. This trend may be explained by their unique caregiving position as members of the “sandwich generation” – those who care for both aging parents and young children at the same time.22 Taken together, our findings emphasize the importance of tailored public health communication strategies that will both increase the awareness and address the unique concerns each age group has regarding vaccination against pneumococcal disease.

Sex and pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

Sex was significantly associated with most pneumococcal vaccine perceptions. Women reported the highest agreement with vaccine efficacy, followed by men, while non-binary respondents showed the lowest agreement. Perceptions of vaccine safety followed a similar pattern, though differences between groups were not statistically significant. Research specifically examining sex-based perceptions of the pneumococcal vaccine is currently limited; however, studies on the COVID-19 vaccine have found that men are generally more likely than women to view vaccines as effective and safe.16,23 This could, in part be explained by research showing that pregnant women often perceive higher vaccine-related risks compared to non-pregnant women and men.24,25 As our study did not collect information on pregnancy status, we are unable to determine whether this factor contributed to the divergence between our findings and existing literature on vaccine efficacy and safety perceptions.

Women were the most likely to report prior awareness of the pneumococcal vaccine, and they also expressed the highest levels of concern about illness caused by pneumococcal disease. Further, women reported the strongest agreement relating to the importance of vaccinating both children and older/at-risk adults. Taken together, these findings may be explained by the well-documented caregiving roles that women are more likely to take on within families. Previous work has shown that women are more likely to manage health decisions for both their kids and aging relatives; this includes scheduling vaccinations, tracking health records, and attending medical appointments.26,27 As such, women could be exposed to more vaccine-related information, making them more attuned to the risks associated with pneumococcal disease. This could explain both their higher awareness and the greater importance they place on vaccination across the lifespan.

Together, these findings reveal the importance of considering gendered caregiving roles and their implications on health decision-making in vaccine research. Future work looking at pneumococcal vaccine perceptions should more explicitly examine how these social roles and life-stages, such as pregnancy, shape attitudes and opinions toward vaccines.

Impact of income and education level on pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

Pneumococcal vaccine perceptions differed significantly when stratified by both income status and level of educational attainment. Respondents with above-average income were significantly more likely to view the pneumococcal vaccine as effective and safe, and to recognize its importance for children and older/at-risk adults. Similarly, individuals with a college degree or higher demonstrated stronger agreement with pneumococcal vaccine efficacy and safety and were more aware of the vaccine than those without a degree. Taken together, these findings agree with previously-established notions that that income status and education play a large role in shaping vaccine beliefs.9 Individuals with higher income or education often have greater access to healthcare services, more reliable sources of medical information, higher health literacy, and better overall experiences with healthcare professionals – all factors which have been shown to increase positive perceptions toward vaccines.28,29 In contrast, individuals with lower income or education report barriers such as limited access to care, biased interactions with physicians, and a lack of trust in the healthcare system,30 all of which may reduce vaccine uptake and increase hesitancy.

These findings highlight the need for targeted public health messaging that addresses the access barriers faced by lower-income and less-educated individuals. Community-based outreach along with simplified messaging may help reduce these disparities and improve pneumococcal vaccine uptake among these groups.

Country-level differences in vaccine perceptions

We observed significant variation in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions across surveyed countries, suggesting that socioeconomic status, healthcare infrastructure, national immunization programs, and exposure to misinformation may influence public attitudes toward these vaccines.

Brazil and India reported the highest agreement with pneumococcal vaccine safety and efficacy, while France and the United Kingdom showed the lowest. In Brazil and India, strong public health initiatives and large-scale national immunization programs have improved vaccine awareness and acceptance by providing free vaccines to millions, including the pneumococcal vaccine.31–33 Although France and the UK also maintain national vaccination programs which heavily promote pneumococcal vaccine uptake,34,35 both countries have seen increasing vaccine hesitancy driven by political polarization and misinformation spread through digital media.36,37 In France, healthcare professionals report that pneumococcal vaccination recommendations are poorly understood by the public and identify a lack of consent among adults as key barriers to improving uptake38; the UK faces similar challenges, reporting inadequate patient knowledge about the pneumococcal vaccine, and negative public opinions surrounding vaccination.34 Collectively, these reasons may explain why respondents in France and the UK reported lower agreement levels in our data.

Healthcare infrastructure also plays a critical role in shaping vaccine perceptions. Countries with well-established provincial or national healthcare systems, such as Canada, the United Kingdom, and France, tend to deliver vaccines more equitably and efficiently, reaching underserved populations more readily than lower-income countries.39 Yet, despite having robust infrastructure, these countries still showed lower vaccine agreement than Brazil, India, and Nigeria – countries with less comprehensive healthcare systems. This disparity may again point to the role of widespread vaccine misinformation and eroding trust in public health messaging in higher-income countries.36,37 The public health consequences of these trends are concerning. Recent years have seen a global resurgence of vaccine-preventable diseases like measles, with misinformation acting as a central driver of declining vaccination rates, leading to preventable morbidity and mortality.40–42

Overall, our results highlight the necessity for tailored public health strategies that consider unique socioeconomic statuses, healthcare infrastructures, and informational environments surrounding vaccines in each country to effectively address pneumococcal vaccine hesitancy and improve immunization rates.

Influence of health recommendation sources on pneumococcal vaccine perceptions

Trust in the source of health information significantly influenced perceptions of pneumococcal vaccine efficacy and safety. Our findings revealed that individuals who trusted healthcare providers, national public health agencies, and local health departments were significantly more likely to view the vaccine as both effective and safe. These results are consistent with previous studies examining this same relationship; confidence in healthcare provider recommendations and government health institutions has been strongly linked to greater vaccine uptake and compliance with public vaccine initiatives.43,44

In contrast, strong trust in health recommendations from employers, friends, and family members had minimal influence on vaccine beliefs in our sample. Prior large-scale survey data indicate that trust in health recommendations from informal sources is far less predictive of vaccine confidence than trust in clinicians and public health institutions, and lacks the authority to influence vaccine safety and efficacy perceptions.8,45 While friends and family can shape the perceived norms surrounding vaccination, they are less likely to serve as primary sources of mechanistic beliefs about vaccine safety, effectiveness, or scientific evidence.46 This pattern is not unique to pneumococcal vaccination and has been observed in studies examining perceptions of human papillomavirus, influenza, and COVID-19 vaccines.15,47 Similar dynamics are evident in other health behaviors, where individuals routinely defer to healthcare professionals regarding exercise, diet, smoking cessation, and cancer screening decisions.48,49 Taken together, our results emphasize the central role of both public health institutions and healthcare providers in shaping vaccine confidence and perspectives for the public. Strengthening trust in these sources remains a key strategy for addressing vaccine hesitancy and improving uptake.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design used prevents causal inference regarding the relationships observed between demographic characteristics, trust in information sources, and pneumococcal vaccine perceptions. Second, all data were self-reported and may be subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. Third, participants were recruited through an online opt-in panel, which may introduce selection bias and limit representation of individuals without reliable internet access. Fourth, although sampling was stratified to reflect diverse demographic distributions, the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations outside of the seven surveyed countries. Finally, some subgroups, including non-binary respondents and healthcare workers, comprised a small proportion of the sample, and results related to these groups should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Our study findings highlight notable differences in pneumococcal vaccine perceptions across multiple demographic groups and countries. Trust in healthcare providers and public health institutions was a central factor shaping confidence in pneumococcal vaccine efficacy and safety for respondents, while socioeconomic and demographic factors influenced awareness and perceived importance of the pneumococcal vaccine. Country-specific variations in perspectives suggest that public health strategies should be adapted to reflect local healthcare infrastructure, cultural norms, and exposure to vaccine misinformation. In practice, this may include strengthening clinician-led vaccine counseling during routine healthcare visits, developing digital outreach strategies aimed at younger adults who demonstrate lower vaccine confidence, and implementing culturally tailored messaging initiatives in lower-income or underserved settings. By aligning communication and engagement strategies with the demographic and contextual drivers identified in our study, public health agencies can more effectively address pneumococcal vaccine hesitancy and improve uptake. These insights collectively underscore the value of integrating tailored communication and outreach strategies into global immunization efforts to build trust, decrease perception gaps, and promote vaccine confidence worldwide.

Finally, it should be noted that this work was conducted well before the recent escalation of challenges to vaccination that have appeared in some countries around the globe. Given the shifting landscape surrounding vaccination as a public health strategy, we feel that follow-up research is needed to test the durability of the support levels shown in this work, and to identify if key demographics that support vaccination have since altered their perceptions.
