Integrated HS-SPME-GC/MS and RNA sequencing analysis reveals metabolic differences of flavor compounds of muscle tissues with different intramuscular fat contents from Tibetan sheep
Authors: Xiayang Jin, Wu Sun, Yuhong Ma, Yan Zhang, Shike Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA key factor used to evaluate the quality of meat is intramuscular fat (IMF) content, which has a strong association with the flavor of meat. To date, however, the influence of IMF content on the volatile profiles of ovine meat has not been clarified.ResultsWe used headspace solid-phase microextraction gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC/MS) to study the aroma characteristics of low-IMF (L-IMF) and high-IMF (H-IMF) content in muscle tissue and to identify volatile compounds in L-IMF and H-IMF groups. Thirteen of 557 volatile compounds identified were significantly different between the groups (Odor Activity Values > 1, VIP values > 1), of which aldehydes, ketones, and esters were the predominant volatile compounds. We conducted RNA-seq analysis and found 985 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the L-IMF and H-IMF groups. The weighted gene coexpression network analysis (WGCNA) showed that DEGs associated with muscle aroma characteristics were enriched in lipolysis regulation in adipocytes, PPAR signaling, AMPK signaling, arachidonic acid metabolism, and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs). These pathways played a role in regulating energy metabolism and muscle lipid metabolism functions in Tibetan sheep. The combined results of DEGs, WGCNA, and gene-aroma compound interactions revealed ADIPOQ, FABP4, FADS2, GPD2, HSL, LEP, PEPCK and PLIN4 to be potential candidate genes affecting IMF and aroma compounds content.ConclusionIn this study, we established profiles of the transcriptome and volatile compounds of L-IMF and H-IMF ovine muscle tissues. The findings suggested that HSL, FABP4 and PLIN4, along with the lipolysis regulation in adipocytes and PPAR signaling pathways, contributed to the difference in lipid metabolism within muscle tissues, and biosynthesis of the UFA pathway may influence the production of volatile compound precursors in muscles. These results offer important information about regulating genes that pertain to ovine meat flavor compounds.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11997-w.Keywords: Tibetan sheep, Intramuscular fat, Flavor compound, Differentially expressed genes, Lipid deposition

Introduction

Tibetan sheep live about 3,000 m in the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau. These sheep account for China’s largest population of primitive sheep. Tibetan sheep mutton is known for its good tenderness and rich aroma, containing high-quality proteins, amino acids, and polyunsaturated fatty acids (FAs) [1]. Volatile compounds, as crucial attributes of the sensory quality of mutton, are increasingly emphasized by breeding, processing industries, and consumers as key indicators of the commercial value of mutton [2]. These volatile compounds are derived from the Maillard reaction, lipid-Maillard interaction, and lipid oxidation degradation. They include acids, alcohols, aldehydes, furans, hydrocarbons, ketones, nitrogen- and sulfur-containing compounds, and pyrazines [3–5].

Intramuscular fat (IMF) is strongly associated with meat quality attributes and flavor formation [2, 6]. Liu et al. [7] identified 61 different lipid subclasses that may produce volatile aroma compounds. IMF is rich in lipids such as triglycerides, glycerophospholipids, and fatty acyls, playing primarily two roles in flavor formation: acting as a solvent and precursor to volatile compounds [8]. Volatile compounds in meat products tend to dissolve preferentially in the lipid fraction, thereby influencing their release and perception [9]. The hydrolysis of triglycerides and glycerophospholipids is responsible for the production of FAs. After undergoing nonenzymatic oxidation, these FAs produce volatile compounds, including ketones, alcohols, aldehydes, carbonyl compounds, hydroxy epoxides, and ketone acid epoxides [7, 10]. The thermal oxidation and decomposition of ω−3 and ω−6 polyunsaturated FAs generates the volatile aroma compounds that are characteristic of mutton [11, 12]. Oleic acid primarily generates aldehydes with fruity, sweet, and fatty aromas, such as octanal, nonanal, heptanal, and (E,E)−2,4-decadienal [13]. After linoleic acid completes thermal oxidation, it degrades into 1-octen-3-ol (mushroom), hexanal (green and grassy), 2-pentylfuran (vegetable and earthy), (E)−2-octenal (grilled meat and peanut cake), (E)−2-nonenal (cucumber and fatty), and pentanal (spicy odor) as well as other volatile compounds [14, 15]. The production of benzaldehyde (bitter almond aroma), (E,Z)−2,4-heptadienal (cucumber), and (E,E)−2,4-heptadienal (grilled meat) is associated closely with alpha-linolenic acid oxidation [10, 13, 15]. Therefore, an appropriate deposition of IMF can ensure that mutton products possess good quality.

Some studies to date have examined the factors that form and influence the flavor quality of mutton. Most of this research, however, has identified volatile compounds [3] and focused on the impact of factors such as breed [4], processing conditions [16], diet [15, 17], and gender [18]. Studies on the mechanisms by which lipids affect meat flavor quality are mainly concentrated on in vitro simulation verification and the impact of adding single or multiple lipids on volatile flavor compounds [11, 19, 20]. An understanding of the relationship between volatile compounds and IMF is lacking significant research to examine mutton flavor quality and its regulatory mechanisms. This study utilized HS-SPME-GC/MS to quantitatively characterize the volatile characteristics and chemical components sampled with high and low IMF levels muscle tissues in Tibetan sheep. Using RNA-seq technology, an expression profiles analysis was conducted on the two groups of muscle tissues, and key candidate genes regulating the metabolism of volatile compounds were identified. The results of our study may reveal the relationship between volatile compounds and DEGs and clarify the impact of IMF on the flavor quality of mutton.

Materials and methods

Animals and muscle sampling

The Animal Ethics Committee of Qinghai University (Ethics approval file No. QUA-2020-0709) reviewed and approved this animal study. We randomly selected six Tibetan ewes that were two years old and that had normal development and growth. The ewes belonged to the same natural grazing flock, which was located at an altitude of 3,000 m above sea in Qilan County, Qinghai Province, China. The ewes were raised under the same environmental conditions, with natural light, and were provided the same nutrition, with free access to food and water. According to Islamic practice, the sheep were exsanguinated humanely. We immediately collected samples of muscle tissue from 10 portions of the left half of the carcass. The sampling parts included the triceps brachii muscle (m.TB), supraspinatus muscle (m.S), deltoid muscle (m.D), Longissimus thoracis et lumborum muscle (m. LTL), latissimus dorsi muscle (m.LD), psoas major muscle (m.PM), trapezius thoracalis muscle (m.TT), biceps femoris muscle (m.BF), quadriceps femoris muscle (m.QF), and gastrocnemius muscle (m.G), Figure S1 details the anatomical locations of the sampled muscle tissues. We used physiological saline and RNase-free water to rinse the samples. We collected samples for RNA-seq, reverse transcription-quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), and volatile compounds detection in cryotubes, which were immediately stored in liquid nitrogen. At the same time, we cooled the other sample for 24 h at 4 °C, which were saved for additional IMF content analysis.

IMF measurement and HS-SPME-GC/MS analysis

Following the Chinese “National Food Safety Standard—Determination of Fat in Foods” (GB 5009.6–2016), we used the Soxhlet extraction method to determine the IMF content of the muscle samples. The IMF was expressed as the percent of the meat’s weight when wet.

To stain neutral lipids, we used BODIPY 493/503 and followed the method reported by Li et al. [21]. Specifically, the frozen tissue sections were thawed at room temperature, followed by incubation with diluted Bodipy (1:200 dilution in PBS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, D3922) for 20 min at room temperature to label neutral lipids. Subsequently, slides were washed three times in PBS (pH 7.4) for 5 min each on an orbital shaker to remove unbound dye. Nuclei were counterstained by incubating with DAPI (1 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, D9542) for 10 min in the dark, followed by three additional PBS washes (5 min each). To preserve fluorescence, the sections were mounted using anti-fade mounting medium and coverslipped. Imaging was performed using the IX53 inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) with the following settings: DAPI (excitation: 330–380 nm, emission: 420 nm), Bodipy (excitation: 465–495 nm, emission: 515–555 nm), and CY3 (excitation: 510–560 nm, emission: 590 nm). To calculate the area of BODIPY staining, we randomly collected four images for each field of observation. Furthermore‌, we performed H&E staining on the aforementioned frozen sections to confirm the location of lipid droplets within the muscle fibers, following the experimental procedures described by Zhang et al.[22].

According to the research by Yang et al. [23], the volatile compounds in muscle samples were pre-treated and extracted via a headspace solid-phase micro extraction system. The volatile compounds were separated and analyzed according to the method described by Yang et al. [24], with the GC/MS conditions detailed in Table S1. We used the NIST v2.3 databases, as reported [13, 25, 26], to conduct mass spectra matching and identify the volatile compounds. We used an in-house developed library based on commercial standards to compare retention times (R.T.) and we matched the retention indices (R.I.) to the authentic compounds. We completed quantification according to the internal standard method, as reported in Oin et al. [27]. We normalized each volatile compound’s concentration to align with 3-Hexanone-2,2,4,4-d4. We used the Odor Activity Value (OAV) to identify the volatile compounds that contributed the most to the flavor of ovine meat. We set the threshold to OAV great than 1.

RNA extraction and RNA-Seq analysis

RNA extraction, transcriptome sequencing, and data analysis were performed according to the approach described by Wang et al. [28]. RNA quality was assessed using 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA), only high-quality RNA samples (RIN ≥ 6.0) was used to construct sequencing library. We generated individual complementary DNA (cDNA) libraries for five low-IMF (L-IMF) groups and five high-IMF (H-IMF) groups. We used an Illumina Novaseq6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, United States) for sequencing according to Guangzhou Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). We filtered the raw reads stored in the FASTQ file format [28] to obtain high-quality clean reads for annotation. We mapped the results to the reference genome NCBI_GCF_016772045.2 (https://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genomes/all/GCF/016/772/045/GCF_016772045.2_ARS-UI_Ramb_v3.0/) using HISAT2 (v2.2.4). We calculated fragment per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) to normalize each annotated gene’s expression abundance and variations using StringTie v1.3.1. To compare the expression levels between L-IMF and H-IMF groups, we used DESeq v2.0 [29]. We defined differentially expressed genes (DEGs) as those with a p-value < 0.01 and |Log2 (fold change)| ≥0.5. In this study, we performed KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of the DEGs using DAVID (http://david.abcc.ncifcrf.gov/) with all KEGG-annotated gene from the reference genome (Ovis aries, NCBI_GCF_016772045.2) as background. To determine the relationship between the gene expression and different volatile compounds [30], we performed WGCNA analysis using the R package ‘WGCNA’ [31].

Validation of DEGs by reverse transcription-quantitative PCR

The RT-qPCR was utilized for verifying RNA-Seq findings following the protocol outlined by Hao et al. [32]. We selected β-tubulin and β-actin as the internal references to normalize the DEG expression levels [33]. Table S2 provides the primer information for the relevant genes.

Volatile compounds in L-IMF and H-IMF

We observed an obvious separation between the L-IMF group and the H-IMF group using the OPLS-DA model, which revealed different volatile compounds in these two groups (Fig. 2A). The model had an interpretability parameter of R2Y = 0.998, a predictive ability parameter of Q2 = 0.726, and a cumulative statistic R2X = 0.801. All results exceeded 0.5. Therefore, we verified that the OPLS-DA model exhibited excellent ability to predict volatile substances in ovine meat.

Overview of volatile compounds between the L-IMF and H-IMF groups. A Results for volatile compounds from multivariate statistical analysis. B Results for the L-IMF and H-IMF groups showing the accumulation pattern of volatile compounds.

The L-IMF group and the H-IMF group collectively had a total of 557 volatile compounds, including 1 ether (0.18%); 7 nitrogen compounds (1.26%) and 7 halogenated hydrocarbons (1.26%); 16 organic acids and derivatives (2.87%); 40 terpenoids (7.18%); 56 benzene and substituted derivatives (10.05%); 65 alcohols and amides (11.67%); 73 heterocyclic compounds (13.11%); 126 hydrocarbons (22.62%); and 159 aldehyde, ketones, and esters (28.37%) as well as 8 others (1.44%) (Fig. 2B). In addition, the average volatile compounds content of the muscle in the L-IMF group was 15.21% lower than that of the muscle in the H-IMF group.

Overview of transcriptomic data and differential expression gene (DEGs) analysis

We used the muscle tissue from the L-IMF and H-IMF groups to construct 10 cDNA libraries. We assessed the results according to the RNA-seq method. Table S3 presents the summary statistics for the RNA-seq data. We produced an average of 43,497,465 raw reads from the five groups of H-IMF muscle samples and an average of 41,665,450 raw reads from the five groups of L-IMF muscle samples using the cDNA libraries constructed. We removed adaptors and low-quality reads (quality scores < Q20) to filter the results and obtained an average of 41,469,948 high-quality clean reads from the L-IMF muscle samples and 43,284,044 high-quality clean reads from the H-IMF muscle samples. We obtained a unique match ratio of 93.28% and 93.06%, respectively, of these cleaned reads, with 39,448,914 (97.75%) and 41,898,181 (97.66%) mapping to the ovine genome assembly well.

We conducted principal component analysis (PCA) analysis. As shown in Fig. 3A, the 10 grouped samples formed two distinct groups, aligned with the L-IMF and H-IMF muscle samples. The first principal components accounted for 45.6% of the variation and the second principal components accounted for 29.5% of the variation. Venn diagram analysis (Figure S2) revealed a total of 985 DEGs when comparing the H-IMF vs. L-IMF groups. Of these, 960 DEGs were common to both groups, while 15 DEGs were uniquely expressed in the H_IMF group, and 10 DEGs were uniquely expressed in the L_IMF group. As shown in Figs. 4B and 439 DEGs (44.57%) had a higher expression in the L-IMF group and 546 DEGs (55.43%) had a higher expression in the H-IMF group.

The DEGs coexpression network constructed and correlation analysis with differential volatile compounds

We used WGCNA to identify hub genes, it clusters gene sets with similar expression patterns into modules. The fitting degree of the scale-free topological model was 0.80 and the soft threshold for network construction was selected as 16 for both genotypes. Based on the FPKM expression matrix and the content of key aroma compounds, a total of 985 DEGs in 10 samples were clustered and divided into 12 modules, which we decorated with diacritical colors (Fig. 4A). Through the correlation analysis with the absolute value of cor > 0.5 and p < 0.01 as the criterion, blue module (MMblue) and black module (MMblack) were found to have significant correlation with key volatile compounds (Fig. 4B). We observed that aldehyde, ketones, and esters had significant positive correlation with the MMblack module (96 DEGs, r = 0.78, p = 0.003) and the MMblue module (161 DEGs, r = 0.54, p = 0.004). Terpenoids also showed a significant positive correlation with the MMblack module (r = 0.70, p = 0.004).

WGCNA of the DEGs. A DEGs and module division results from WGCNA shown in a clustering dendrogram. B Results of the module and differential volatile compounds indicated correlation significance. C Diagram of KEGG enrichment for MMblue. D Diagram of KEGG enrichment for MMblack.

The genes in the MMblue module were enriched primarily in organismal systems. According to the KEGG functional enrichment analysis, these DGEs were also enriched by human diseases and metabolic processes. Within organismal systems, DEGs were mainly enriched in the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes (ko04923, enrichment ratio = 0.07, p-value < 0.001). The AMPK signaling pathway (ko04152, enrichment ratio = 0.03, p-value = 0.008) and PPAR signaling pathway (ko03320, enrichment ratio = 0.05, p-value = 0.001) showed that genes such as ADIPOQ, HSL, FABP4, and PLIN4 were involved in the lipid metabolism process and significantly enriched (Fig. 4C). The main annotations in the MMblack module included metabolism, human diseases and organismal systems. We conducted additional enrichment analysis and found that most of the DEGs within the metabolism category were involved in arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism (ko00590, enrichment ratio = 0.02, p-value = 0.03) and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (UFAs) (ko01040, enrichment ratio = 0.03, p-value = 0.10) (Fig. 4D). Among them, genes such as FADS2, GPD2, CYP2J and PGFS were involved in the regulation.

As the MMblue and MMblack resulting from the WGCNA showed a strong association with the content of aroma compounds, we investigated the candidate DEGs that affected aroma compounds content in these modules. First, we selected the top 100 DEGs with the highest intramodular connectivity (K.within) as hub genes for each modules. Additionally, these genes were required to exhibit moderate-to-strong correlations with aroma compound content (|r| >0.6). Under these criteria, 53 hub genes were identified in the MMblue (Fig. 5A) and 50 in the MMblack (Fig. 5B). Comparing these genes with the key genes enriched in the regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, AMPK signaling, PPAR signaling, arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism, and biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids pathway, we found an overlap of 8 genes: ADIPOQ, FABP4, FADS2, GPD2, HSL, LEP, PEPCK and PLIN4. Correlation analysis demonstrated that (E,E)−2,4-decadienal content positively correlated with expression of LEP, FABP4, HSL, PLIN4, PEPCK, and FADS2, 2-undecenal content positively correlated with PEPCK, FADS2, and GPD2, ethyl heptanoic content positively correlated with ADIPOQ and FABP4; (E)−2-decenal, γ-octalactone, and δ-dodecalactone showed positive correlations with PEPCK, GPD2, and FADS2, respectively, while γ-decalactone exhibited a negative correlation with GPD2.

Discussion

Tibetan sheep are famous in China for their distinctive flavor, nutritional value, and palatability. One of the most important factors that has an impact on the flavor of meat is IMF content [34, 35]. According to prior research, location, genetics, diet, slaughter age, and anatomical location of the carcass all can affect IMF [36–38]. In this study, we found that the content of IMF in cuts other than the m. LTL was significantly lower. This finding agreed with other research on sheep and donkey foal muscle [39–42]. Therefore, the muscle tissues from different parts with the same genetic background may have varying IMF contents, resulting in differences in meat quality, especially lamb flavor.

Volatile compounds of two groups muscle, characterized by the lowest and highest IMF contents, were determined using HS-SPME-GC/MS. According to our results, the largest proportion of volatile compounds in muscle was found in aldehyde, esters, and ketones. These compounds were responsible for the sweet, fruity, and fatty aromas of mutton and were the largest group, which was in keeping with other research [43, 44]. The average content for volatile compounds in L-IMF was significantly lower than that in H-IMF. Accordingly, it was not the type of volatile compounds but the content of volatile compounds that appeared to be responsible for forming the aroma of mutton.

Based on the concentrations and OAVs, 13 volatile compounds exceeded their thresholds and displayed significant concentration differences between L-IMF and H-IMF groups. More than half of the volatile compounds in cooked meat were generated by lipid oxidation and degradation, which were responsible for aroma that was specific to species [45]. Of these 13 differential volatile compounds, 11 compounds, including ethyl heptanoic, (E)−2-decenal, 2-pentylfuran, 2-undecenal, butyl butanoic, γ-octalactone, (Z)-dihydro-5-(2-octenyl)furan-2(3 H)-one, δ-dodecalactone, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal, γ-decalactone, and 1-octanol were produced by degradation and lipid oxidation and degradation. Of these compounds, (E)−2-decenal, 2-pentylfuran, 2-undecenal, γ-octalactone, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal, γ-decalactone, and 1-octanol were the most important compounds in defining the flavor of lamb [7, 10, 42, 43]. Thus, it appeared that the lipid-derived components potentially contribute to the aroma characteristics of two groups, underscoring the critical role of IMF in shaping the flavor of lamb, which aligned with other studies on IMF’s impact on aroma compounds in swine [46]. The L-IMF group had lower levels of 2-undecenal, 2-pentyl-furan, (E)−2-decenal, and (E,E)−2,4-decadienal than the H-IMF group. This result also aligned with research by Ma et al. [47].

Because aldehydes have a low odor threshold, they are the chief volatile compounds responsible for lipid oxidation in a variety of meats [48]. The oxidation of UFAs (e.g., C18:1 ω9, C18:2 ω6, and C18:3 ω3) produces alkanals and alkenals [49]. Notably, 2-undecenal and (E)−2-decenal are generated by the oxidation of oleic acid; 2-pentylfuran, 2-nonenal, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal, and hexanal are generated by linoleic acid [50]. For example, in stewed mutton, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal and 2-undecenal create toasted, fatty, scallion, and biscuit odors; 2-pentylfuran creates earthy and vegetable odors; and (E)−2-decenal create a tallowy and fatty odor [10, 51]. It also has been observed that δ-dodecalactone, γ-decalactone, and γ-octalactone create flowery and sweet aromas in beef [52], which are generated by the lactonization of hydroxy FAs [53]. Linoleic acid can produce 1-octanol, which has fruity and fatty aromas [54]. On the basis of the lipid-derived compound concentrations, we observed differences in the aroma of the lamb according to various content of IMF. Among the notable compounds leading to these differences were (E)−2-decenal, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal, 2-pentylfuran, and 2-undecenal.

To investigate the potential key genes and chief metabolic pathways responsible for the differences in aroma, we used RNA-seq in this study. We identified 985 DEGs in the H-IMF group and the L-IMF group. According to data on volatile compounds content and gene expression, we categorized DEGs into 12 modules of coexpression. We used WGCNA for this task because this method is comprehensive when determining functional modules according to functional enrichment, gene dimension reduction, biological significance, and phenotype-transcriptome joint analysis [55]. We observed that the content of key volatile compounds had the highest correlation with the MMblue module and the MMblack module. The MMblue module genes were enriched significantly in lipolysis regulation in adipocytes (ko04923). We also observed significant enrichment in the AMPK signaling pathway (ko04152) and the PPAR signaling pathway (ko03320). These pathways are typical genes related to lipid metabolism.

Triglyceride metabolism is affected by lipolysis regulation in the adipocyte pathway. The associated genes, including FABP4 and HSL, play an essential role in this critical process, which is controlled by hydrolyzing triacylglycerols into monoglyceride and diacylglycerol. Ultimately, this process creates palmitic and stearic acids as well as other free saturated FAs [56]. Subsequently, monounsaturated and polyunsaturated FAs (e.g., linoleic and oleic acids) are created by these saturated FAs through FA desaturases. The multifunctional enzyme HSL regulates the balance between lipolysis and lipid storage, and is significantly involved in the dynamic control of IMF. In both intermuscular fat and IMF tissue, HSL has demonstrated expression levels that are high [57]. The activity of HSL is regulated by lipid droplet-associated proteins, such as Perilipin 1 (PLIN1). The phosphorylation of PLIN1 releases HSL, allowing it to approach the substrate on the surface of the lipid droplet, while the absence of PLIN1 significantly inhibits HSL-mediated lipolysis [58, 59]. Additionally, HSL catalyzes the hydrolysis of stored triglycerides into free fatty acids (FFAs), providing substrates for lipid oxidation and subsequent volatile compound formation [60]. In this study, the upregulation of HSL in the H-IMF group (Log2 FC = 0.61, p-value = 0.01) may drive the release of FFAs such as linoleic and α-linolenic acids—key precursors for aldehydes and ketones like (E,E)−2,4-decadienal and 2-pentylfuran. FABP4 is involved in intracellular free FA metabolism and transport. This member of the cytoplasmic protein family has high affinity with binding long-chain FAs. Through directly interacting with the N-terminal of HSL, FABP4 can enhance the enzyme’s activity [61]. The research from Zhao indicates that high-IMF breeds show elevated FABP4 expression in muscles than in lean-type pigs, and SNPs in FABP4 (e.g., c.280G > A) are associated with IMF variation, serving as genetic markers for selective breeding [62]. Similar conclusions were also observed in studies on Japanese Black cattle and Spanish commercial beef, where exhibited higher FABP4 expression in marbled muscle, and its gene polymorphisms were used to mark individuals with high IMF [63, 64]. In ruminants, FABP4 affects the transport efficiency of linoleic acid (CLA), which in turn influences the CLA content in muscles, thereby improving tenderness and flavor [59]. Lipid metabolism, flavor differences, and meat quality in mammal muscle is regulated by the PPAR signaling and genes such as ADIPOQ, FABP4, PLIN4 and PEPCK [65]. In this study, we found that ADIPOQ (Log2 FC = 1.20, p-value = 0.006) and PLIN4 (Perilipin 4) (Log2 FC = 0.84, p-value < 0.001) were upregulated in the H-IMF group. Adiponectin, encoded by the ADIPOQ genes, is a protein hormone secreted by adipocytes involved in the regulation and inhibition of lipogenesis and the stimulation of fatty acid oxidation [66]. Expression of ADIPOQ was higher in Lantang, a high-IMF pig breed, compared to that in Landrace, a low-IMF pig breed [67]. PLIN4, a coating protein, regulates lipid droplets and encourages lipid deposition in adipocytes. Because it increases the lipid droplet size, it blocks triglyceride metabolism and slows the mitochondrial respiratory chain. The research from Liu et al. [68] indicates that the expression level of PLIN4 exhibited an upregulated trend only in the middle growth stage (day 60) of Ningxiang pigs for a large amount of IMF deposition, but as IMF deposition increases, PLIN4 expression needs to be reduced later to mitigate mitochondrial damage and ensure cell survival in the finishing stage. Other research, however, has reported that reduced expression of PLIN4 is necessary because increased IMF deposition ensures adipocytes and limits mitochondrial damage [69]. Because AMPK signaling modulates energy metabolism, it also can increase intracellular ATP reserves by limiting the anabolism that is part of FA and cholesterol synthesis ribosomal proteins translation, and catabolism enhancement (e.g., glucose and fat transport, FA oxidation, and oxidative metabolism) [70]. When AMPK is activated, it encourages lipids to break down and causes glycogen and lipid synthesis inhibition [71]. In this study, LEP was significantly enriched in AMPK signaling pathways and downregulated in the H-IMF group (Log2 FC = −2.62, p-value < 0.001). LEP, an adipocyte-derived hormone, regulates energy homeostasis, metabolism, and neuroendocrine responses to nutritional alterations. It activates AMPK signaling by promoting the protein expression of CAMKK2, subsequently downregulating the expression levels of SREBF1 and FASN, and inhibiting intramuscular adipogenesis [72]. Research by Kong et al. revealed that three SNPs detected in exons 2 and 3 of the LEP gene influenced the desaturation of FA into MUFA in the LT muscle of Simmental bulls [73]. Our results showed that the DEGs associated with synthesis of FA and energy metabolism could be responsible for the different flavor compound content in the H-IMF group and L-IMF group.

According to the results of WGCNA, the MMblack genes related to the content of aldehyde, esters, and ketones were enriched primarily through in AA metabolism (ko00590), biosynthesis of UFAs (ko01040), glycerophospholipid metabolism (K00111), and steroid hormone biosynthesis (ko00140). Research by Li et al. [74] identified the AA metabolism pathway as essential in IMF deposition in the longissimus dorsi tissue of donkeys. AA, which is a polyunsaturated FA, is a precursor to bioactive mediator synthesis and is critical in lipid metabolism [75]. The biosynthesis of UFAs pathway and genes such as ELOVL6, FADS2 (a rate-limiting enzyme), and SCD corresponds to the biosynthesis of linoleic and oleic acids. UFAs are precursors to flavor compounds including 2-pentylfuran, 2-undecenal, and (E,E)−2,4-decadienal. In this study, compared with the H-IMF group, the FA desaturase 2 gene (FADS2) was downregulated in the L-IMF group. Δ−6-desaturase, which is the enzyme encoded by FADS2 gene, is a membrane-bound enzyme. It is responsible for the catalysis of γ-linolenic acid (18:3n-6) from linoleic acid (C18:2n-6) and α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3). After oxidation, linolenic acid produces aroma compounds, including benzaldehyde, (E,E)−2,4-heptadienal, and (E,Z)−2,4-heptadienal [76]. In addition, FADS2-derived UFAs are critical components of phospholipids in cell membranes. High UFAs levels activate PPARγ promoting adipocyte differentiation and lipid droplet formation, which affects the lipolysis-synthesis balance in IMF [57]. In this study, PEPCK and GDP2 were downregulation in the H-IMF group, which are collectively involved in the PPAR signaling and glycerophospholipid metabolism pathway, respectively. Whether these DEGs regulated IMF and aroma compounds content also requires further function verification at the cellular level.

DEGs were the most significant candidate genes affecting energy and lipid metabolism in muscles, including ADIPOQ, FABP4, FADS2, GPD2, HSL, LEP, PEPCK and PLIN4. Additional research should examine the underlying mechanisms as they related to aroma characteristics and IMF content in sheep.

Conclusions

In this study, we recognized the key genes and important volatile compounds that were responsible for the formation of aroma in L-IMF and H-IMF muscles. We used both HS-SPME-GC/MS and RNA-seq to identify 13 lipid-derived compounds, including (E)−2-decenal, (E,E)−2,4-decadienal, 2-undecenal, 2-pentylfuran, γ-octalactone, δ-dodecalactone, and γ-decalactone. These compounds were the potential contributors to the variability in aroma between the L-IMF and H-IMF groups. According to this comprehensive analysis, genes related to aroma characteristics and IMF content were enriched by regulating AMPK signaling, AA metabolism, biosynthesis of the UFA pathway, lipolysis in adipocytes, and PPAR signaling. This regulation was responsible for energy and muscle lipid metabolism in Tibetan sheep. The results of this study explain why IMF content is different in lambs and how this affects both characteristics of aroma.
