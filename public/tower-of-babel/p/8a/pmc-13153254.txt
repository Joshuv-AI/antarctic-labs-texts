Sex differences in the severity of non-motor symptoms in Parkinson’s disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Calogero Edoardo Cicero, Claudio Terravecchia, Lavinia Lucia Pettinato, Silvia Tabbì, Giulia Donzuso, Donatella Contrafatto, Giovanni Mostile, Mario Zappia, Alessandra Nicoletti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-motor symptoms (NMS) in Parkinson’s Disease (PD) encompass a variety of symptoms which may differ in terms of presentation and severity according to gender. However, literature evidences are often conflicting due to the use of different instruments. The Non-Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) is a validated tool widely used to holistically assess NMSs in PD. We aimed to summarize available data on gender differences in NMS severity in PD using the NMSS. A literature review has been performed in the PubMed and Scopus databases up to 1st February 2025. A random effect model was used to report pooled effects. Nine full text articles have been included in the analysis, pooling the data of 4352 PD patients [N = 2453 (58%) men]. Women showed higher symptoms severity in mood/cognition whereas higher severity in the sexual domain was found in men. These findings highlight the need for a specific gender-related approach in PD.Subject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative disease with a prevalence of more than 6 million individuals worldwide1. The clinical hallmark of PD is a motor syndrome encompassing bradykinesia, rest tremor and rigidity resulting from nigral degeneration and striatal dopaminergic depletion1. Beside motor features, a variety of non-motor symptoms (NMS) such as hyposmia, autonomic dysfunction, sleep disorders, cognitive and neuropsychiatric symptoms also characterize PD, affecting virtually all PD patients2 as a consequence of neurodegeneration also involving extranigral non-dopaminergic pathways1. Notably, some NMS such as hyposmia, constipation or REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) may precede even by decades the onset of motor symptoms3, while others such as pain, urinary dysfunction or mood disorders may constitute the presenting complain in up to 21% of PD patients4. Moreover, with the progression of the disease, NMS such as cognitive impairment and sleep disorders come to dominate the clinical picture5, representing major determinants of institutionalization and quality of life of PD patients and caregivers6,7.

In the era of precision medicine, due to a developing knowledge of sex-related differences on brain function and morphology, a growing attention has been addressed to sex effects on clinical features of neurodegenerative disorders8.

In particular, the most commonly reported sex difference in PD is its higher prevalence and incidence in men than women9. Moreover, a later motor onset, more frequent tremor-dominant phenotype, lower functional striatal dopaminergic impairment, slower disease progression as well as higher risk of motor complications such as levodopa-induced dyskinesia have also been reported quite consistently in women compared to men with PD as the possible result of a protective role of estrogens and a greater levodopa bioavailability in women8.

Concerning NMS, there are consistent evidences that report a higher prevalence of depression and anxiety in women than men with PD8,10–13, whereas men experience higher levels of sexual and urinary dysfunction10,14–16. On the other hand, less consistent data on sex differences in cardiovascular, genitourinary and gastrointestinal NMS are currently available. Thus, based on these controversial data, a knowledge gap still involves the role of sex on NMS in PD, which may be related to several factors such as the large heterogeneity on clinical and demographic characteristics of studied populations as well as on instruments used to assess the presence and severity of NMS. Indeed, the majority of published studies used specific instruments or scales to study single NMSs in PD such as anxiety, depression, or cognitive impairment. However, the use of specific scales might not accurately represent the real world impact of sex differences in NMS in PD, since their use in clinical practice is usually limited. The Non Motor Symptoms Scale (NMSS) is a validated global scale for the assessment of NMS in PD, frequently used due to the extensive validation in several languages and the holistic approach to the assessment of NMS17

The aim of the present study was to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis of the literature in order to provide a better comprehension of possible sex differences on PD-related NMS using the NMSS.

Results

The search produced a total of N = 1285 articles after removing duplicates. Of them, a total of N = 103 articles were selected after titles and abstracts screening for eligibility. After full text assessment, N = 94 studies were excluded since they did not meet all the established inclusion criteria. Among these articles, one18 was excluded because data concerning NMSS were reported as medians rather than means. Thus, a total of N = 9 studies were selected to be analyzed (Fig. 1, Table 1).

Characteristics of the included studies

To diagnose PD, seven studies used the UK Parkinson’s Disease Society Brain Bank criteria10,19–24, one study used Gelb criteria25, and another study used the MDS criteria26. All of the selected studies except two22,23 showed a NOS score ≥7. In terms of geographical location, three studies were conducted in Asia10,21,22, five in Europe20,23–26, and one was multicentric19, including North America, South America, Asia and Europe. Meta-analysis

The selected studies included N = 4352 individuals with PD, encompassing N = 2453 (58%) men and N = 1809 (42%) women. The overall NMSS score was higher for women, although borderline significant (mean difference 4.41; range -0.02-8.85; p = 0.05), and with a high heterogeneity (I2 = 69.7%) (Fig. 2A). Female sex was associated with more severe mood/cognition symptoms with an overall mean difference of 2.74 (range: 1.28-4.20; p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B) and sleep/fatigue symptoms with an overall mean difference of 1.16 (range: 0.46-1.85; p = 0.001) (Fig. 3A). Concerning the miscellaneous domain, the higher severity in women was borderline significant with a mean raw difference of 1.02 (range -1.03-2.06; p = 0.06), however for this item, data was available only for eight out of nine studies, since Bovenzi et al reported the miscellaneous items as separated26. Sexual function symptoms were more severe in men compared to women with an overall mean difference of -0.74 (range: -1.34- -0.14; p = 0.02) (Fig. 3B). We found substantial heterogeneity across included studies on mood/cognition symptoms (I2 = 73.4%), and sexual function symptoms (I2 = 89.9%) while heterogeneity was moderate for sleep/fatigue symptoms (I2 = 29.9%). No significant sex differences were found in the severity of attention/memory symptoms 0.04 (range: -0.33-0.41; p = 0.8), cardiovascular symptoms 0.33 (range: -0.26-0.92; p = 0.3), gastrointestinal symptoms -0.32 (range: -1.86-0.22; p = 0.2), perceptual problems/hallucinations 0.04 (range: -0.12- 0.20; p = 0.62), urinary symptoms -0.11 (range: -1.09-0.87; p = 0.82).

We have conducted a subgroup analysis classifying the studies according to the geographic areas in either Europe (n = 4) or Asia (n = 3). We could not include the study of Martinez-Martin19, since due to the multicentric nature, the data was not available by country. The analysis showed that the total NMSS score (Supplementary Fig. 1), attention/memory (Supplementary Fig. 2), and miscellaneous domains (Supplementary Fig. 3) were more severely impaired in European women and not in the Asian population. Whereas urinary symptoms were more severe in European women and Asian men (Supplementary Fig. 4).

A sensitivity analysis by excluding two studies with NOS score ≤610,23 was also performed, providing overlapping results with the main analysis, except for the difference in total score that was no more significant. Considering the NMSS total score, Egger test was significant (p = 0.01), indicating the presence of publication bias; a funnel plot is presented in Supplementary Fig. 5.

Meta-regression

A meta-regression to investigate the possible influence of age and disease duration on our outcomes has been performed. A significant positive correlation between urinary function domain score and age was found (coeff 0.32; 95%CI 0.01-0.62; p = 0.04), indicating a higher severity of urinary disturbances in women with increasing age (Supplementary Fig. 6). No other significant correlations between NMSS domains scores and the other variables were found.

Discussion

We have summarized all the available evidences concerning sex differences in non-motor symptoms in PD using a standardized scale, finding that women tend to display a higher severity of mood and sleep-related symptoms compared to men with PD, while more severe sexual function impairment was demonstrated in men.

Our results confirms previous evidences quite consistently reporting a higher prevalence and severity of mood disorders in women than men with PD12,13,27. These sex-related differences are also in line with evidences from the general population, in which women have a higher risk of developing depression over their lifetime28. The higher severity of mood problems in women with PD may be the result of a complex interaction of several factors, such as biological factors (hormonal influences and genetic factors), psychological factors and social factors28. In particular, the decline in estrogen can contribute to the higher frequency of depression and anxiety29 and, considering that the onset of PD in women is usually observed at a later age, with some studies even suggesting that women have a later onset of the disease than men8, it is possible to consider that menopause, and subsequently a decline in estrogen, could contribute to the increased severity of mood symptoms29.

Another finding emerging from the current meta-analysis is the higher severity of sleep/fatigue domain symptoms in women as compared to men with PD. Currently, the majority of published studies have focused on specific sleep symptoms such as RBD, finding higher prevalence and severity in men13,30 or no differences15,16,31, as well as excessive daytime sleepiness, with few studies reporting a higher men prevalence19,30 or no differences16,31. Our evidence of a more severe burden of sleep problems in PD women might depend on several factors. In particular, a bidirectional relation between sleep disorders and depression, which is higher in PD women, has been widely demonstrated32. Notably, a higher prevalence and severity of sleep disturbances in women has also been found in general population as a possible result of sex hormones’ fluctuations, especially in the perimenopausal period33. Nevertheless, other factors with possible implications on sleep/fatigue domain in PD should be considered. NMSS specifically assess the presence and severity of restless leg syndrome (RLS) which is more prevalent in women34 while it may underestimate the effects of REM sleep behavior disorder, whose prevalence and severity are higher in male PD population13.

Finally, also a higher prevalence of fatigue in PD women might contribute to the higher severity of this domain, however few studies, except those included in this meta-analysis, have assessed gender differences concerning fatigue reporting either no difference35 or a higher severity in women11.

Our results also confirmed a greater severity of sexual domain dysfunction in men as compared to women with PD, as previously reported27,36. Normal aging is related to a decline in sexual function as well as to concomitant urological and systemic conditions which may negatively affect this domain. Thus, also in this case a possible “background effect” should be taken into account. However, a meta-analysis conducted in 2019 on sexual dysfunction in PD has demonstrated a higher risk especially in men with PD compared the general population36. Biological mechanisms such as the reduced dopaminergic activity in the central nervous system are known to impair sexual function in men, since the central dopaminergic activity has a physiological central role in sexual function, mediated by several neural systems through D2 and D4 receptors37. Nevertheless, the higher severity of sexual dysfunction in men might be the result of the underreporting of sexual dysfunction by women due to social and cultural reasons38.

We found no differences in attention/memory domain, although several evidences have demonstrated that men with PD are at higher risk of developing MCI or dementia39–41, the discrepancy might be related to the extreme heterogeneity of cognitive impairment in PD that might not be adequately investigated with the NMSS or, methodologically, by the inclusion criteria of the selected studies that explicitly excluded patients with cognitive impairment19,21,23,24,26.

We also found no gender differences in cardiovascular symptoms severity, probably due to the high heterogeneity across the studies. Interestingly, few studies have assessed gender differences in cardiovascular autonomic function using an instrumental evaluation, finding no differences in de novo PD patients42 or higher prevalence of orthostatic hypotension in men30.

The lack of gender differences in hallucinations and gastrointestinal symptoms is in accordance with previous literature15,16,27. Concerning the urology domain, even if we did not find a significant difference in severity, the high heterogeneity could be explained by the effect of age that was associated with increased severity in women, a correlation well described in the general population, where urinary dysfunction prevalence has been shown to increase with age, especially in women43.

In our sensitivity analysis conducted classifying the studies according to continents, we have found that total NMSS score, attention/memory and miscellaneous domains were more severely impaired in European women only, whereas urologic problems were more severe in European women and Asian men. This analysis has to be interpreted with caution due to the lack of a significant number of participants of the multicentric study conducted by Martinez-Martin et al19, thus reducing the sample size. Also, since ethnicity was not reported in the selected studies we cannot rule out that in the PD patients’ samples there were patients with Asian ancestry enrolled in European studies and vice-versa. Considering these limitations, the higher burden of urinary symptoms in European women might depend on a different pelvic floor structure compared to Asian women, thus explaining a possible higher frequency of complains in European women44.

The main limit of the study is related to the reporting of NMSS results as sub-domains, rather than single symptoms, impacting the interpretation of the overall results, since only few studies reported the sub-items separately. Some other aspects should be further taken into account as possible limitations of the present study. Indeed, the number of included investigations was relatively small and the majority of them has been conducted in Europe and Asia, which could impact the generalizability of our findings to other geographic regions. Moreover, all the selected studies had a hospital-based design with a possible bias towards more severe cases. Finally, we could not evaluate the impact of drugs on NMSS scores, since all the included studies reported pooled results of patients both on therapy and drug naïve.

On the other hand, the inclusion of investigations assessing NMS only through a standardized tool as well as the use of a meta-regression to evaluate the role of possible clinical and demographic confounders could be considered as the main strengths of the study. Moreover, we acknowledge that the use of a specific instrument might limit the generalizability of our results, nevertheless when compared with studies that employed different scales, the results were largely overlapping15,27,30,45,46.

In conclusion, the present investigation shed light on sex differences in NMS in PD through a systematic approach, showing a higher burden of mood and sleep-related symptoms in women and a more severe sexual dysfunction in men with PD. Further studies are needed in order to unravel mechanisms underlying these differences as well as possible tailored clinical strategies.

Methods

Search strategy

According to PRISMA guidelines47, a systematic literature search was conducted up to 1st February 2025 using PubMed and Scopus with the following search terms: “(sex OR gender OR “sex factors”[MeSH]) AND (“Parkinson’s disease” OR “Parkinson”) AND (“non motor symptoms” OR “non motor” OR “nonmotor” OR “non-motor”)” and “TITLE-ABS-KEY((sex OR gender) AND (“Parkinson’s disease” OR “Parkinson”) AND (“non motor symptoms” OR “non motor” OR “nonmotor” OR “non-motor”))”, respectively. The research has been performed without language or date restrictions. A manual review of references of pertinent primary articles was also carried out to identify any potentially additional eligible studies that might have been overlooked by the initial search strategy. Study selection and quality assessment were conducted in duplicate.

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Papers were screened and selected using the following criteria: (A) studies including patients with a diagnosis of PD. Paper assessing specific PD subpopulations (i.e. specific genetic subgroups, studies on patients undergoing advanced therapies) were excluded; (B) NMS were reported separately for women and men, tabulated by sex, or in some other form to make the outcomes traceable; (C) cross-sectional observational design. In case of longitudinal studies, only baseline data were included. (D) the NMS were assessed with the NMSS. The NMSS assesses both the severity and frequency of 9 NMS domains (cardiovascular, sleep/fatigue, mood/cognition, perceptual/hallucinations, attention/memory, gastrointestinal, urinary, sexual and miscellaneous) giving both domain specific subscores and a total score, with higher scores indicating the presence of more severe symptoms17.

Conference proceedings, letters to the editor, commentaries, dissertations and animal studies were excluded. Two independent reviewers (LP, ST) screened the titles and abstracts and then the full texts for eligibility. Disagreements were discussed with a senior researcher (CEC) and consensus was reached.

Quality assessment

Two independent examiners (CEC, LP) evaluated the quality of each study using a modified version of a quality assessment tool, the Newcastle Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS) adapted for cross-sectional studies. Based on this scale, a quality score ranging from 0 to 10 was assigned to each selected study48.

Data extraction

From each selected study, information of interest was extracted and included in a specific database: main author, year of publication, country, total number of patients, total number of male patients, total number of female patients, mean scores for each NMS domain separately reported for men and women.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

To assess the differences in the severity of NMS between men and women, a meta-analysis of raw mean differences using a random effects model was performed. Statistical heterogeneity has been evaluated using the Cochran Q test and I2. The analysis was conducted separately for each of the domains of the NMSS, namely: cardiovascular including falls, sleep/fatigue, mood/cognition, perceptual problems/hallucinations, attention/memory, gastrointestinal tract, urinary, sexual function, miscellaneous. Positive values indicate a higher severity in women, whereas negative values a higher severity in men. A Meta-regression analysis was then performed to examine the possible effects of age and disease duration on the outcomes. A sensitivity analysis classifying the studies according to continent has been conducted. The asymmetry of the funnel plots and Egger’s test were computed to evaluate the presence of publication bias.
