LYCOPENE β-CYCLASE overexpression improves growth, modulates hormone content, and affects rhizospheric interactions in tobacco and tomato roots
Authors: Alice Stra, Juan C. Moreno, Jian You Wang, Ivan Petrik, Cristina Votta, Muhammad Jamil, Kit Xi Liew, Guan-Ting Erica Chen, Yagiz Alagoz, Claudia Stange, Valentina Fiorilli, Ondrej Novak, Salim Al-Babili
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Key messageExpression of plant LYCOPENE β-CYCLASEs modulates abscisic acid and strigolactone contents resulting in enhanced root length, impaired mycorrhiza colonization frequency, and reduced Striga-seed germination in tobacco and tomato.AbstractGenetic engineering of the carotenoid biosynthesis made possible the biofortification of many crops by enhancing the provitamin A content in their edible parts. Recent studies showed that overexpression of a single carotenogenic gene, the LYCOPENE β-CYCLASE (LCYB), impacted plant architecture, and improved photosynthesis efficiency and stress tolerance in tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum cv. Xanthi) and tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). Here, we show that LCYB overexpression also enhanced root growth and biomass, and affected rhizospheric interactions, causing a reduction in mycorrhization and decreased capability to induce seed germination in root parasitic plants. These below-ground effects in tobacco and tomato are associated with changes in the levels of carotenoids, apocarotenoids, and phytohormones. Our findings highlight LCYB as a key regulatory and metabolic hotspot in the carotenoid pathway. Its overexpression induces profound changes in root architecture and below-ground interactions. These results lay the foundation for a new generation of crops that can better face the future environmental stress caused by global warming and show increased resistance to root parasitic plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00299-025-03623-6.

Introduction

Carotenoids are tetraterpene pigments widely distributed in nature. They are synthetized by all photosynthetic organisms, including plants, algae, and cyanobacteria, and by many heterotrophic microorganisms (Moise et al. 2014; Sun et al. 2022). In plants, they are formed in the plastids where they play an essential role in the photosynthesis as accessory pigments contributing to light-harvesting and protecting the photosynthetic apparatus from photooxidation (Crupi et al. 2023; Stra et al. 2022). Furthermore, the accumulation of carotenoids in other plastid types, such as chromoplasts, confers yellow, orange, and red colors to fruits, flowers, and roots of different plant species, contributing to pollination and seed dispersal (Crupi et al. 2023; Tanaka et al. 2008). Plant carotenoids consist of eight isoprene units, corresponding to a 40-carbon backbone with conjugated double bond system (Maoka 2020). The unsaturated structure of carotenoids makes them prone to oxidation, leading to a variety of oxidized products called apocarotenoids (Ahrazem et al. 2016; Beltran and Stange 2016; Li et al. 2025; Moreno et al. 2021a, b), which arise either through non-specific attack of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or the action of enzymes from the carotenoid cleavage dioxygenase (CCD) family (Al-Babili and Bouwmeester 2015; Auldridge et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2021). Several apocarotenoids play key roles in different processes, including the response to biotic and abiotic stresses and growth and development (Moreno et al.2025; (D'Alessandro et al. 2018, 2019; Dickinson et al. 2019; Felemban et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2019; Votta et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2019). Additionally, apocarotenoids are also precursors of the phytohormones abscisic acid (ABA), a main regulator of adaptive stress responses and plant development, and strigolactones (SLs) that determines plant architecture and act as rhizospheric signal mediating the establishment of arbuscular-mycorrhizal symbiosis and the communication with root parasitic plants (Fiorilli et al. 2019; Jia et al. 2022; Moreno et al. 2021a, b). Up to date, approximately 35 SLs have been identified in root exudates of different plant species, and they can be divided into two major subfamilies, canonical and non-canonical, based on their chemical structure. Canonical SLs, possess a conserved tricyclic lactone moiety (ABC-ring) while non-canonical SLs, in which the core structure is substituted by alternative molecular configurations (Chen et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2024). Recent studies showed that tobacco root exudates contained around 11 SLs, including solanacol, solanacyl acetate, and orobanchol (Li et al. 2020; Wang and Bouwmeester 2018; Xie et al. 2013). Tomato plants, which are also members of the Solanaceae family, produce not only orobanchol but also several canonical SLs that are structurally related to orobanchol, such as orobanchyl acetate, 7-hydroxyorobanchol isomers, 7-oxoorobanchol, and solanacol (Wakabayashi et al. 2022). Moreover, SLs released by plant roots induce seed germination in root parasitic plants, such as different Orobanche and Phelipanche species, which infest many crops, causing severe yield losses in warm and temperate zones (Jamil et al. 2024; Parker 2012). The best-known carotenoid, β-carotene, and other carotenoids equipped with an unmodified β-ionone ring are important human nutrients, as they are the precursor of vitamin A (retinol), an alcohol derived from the carotenoid cleavage product retinal that is formed by β-CAROTENE CLEAVAGE OXYGENASE 1 (BCO1) (Grune et al. 2010; von Lintig 2012). Vitamin A deficiency (VAD), a major global health problem, leads to blindness and deficient immunity, especially in children, and is still the major reason for childhood mortality (Giuliano et al. 2003; Xu et al. 2021; Zheng et al. 2020). To combat VAD, genetic engineering of carotenoid biosynthesis in crops has focused on the biofortification of grains (Paine et al. 2005; Ye et al. 2000), tubers (Diretto et al. 2007; Li et al. 2012), and other edible plant organs (Welsch et al. 2010) with provitamin A carotenoids.

The biosynthesis of β-carotene is mediated by the enzyme LYCOPENE β-CYCLASE (LCYB), which catalyzes the cyclization of lycopene by introducing two β-ionone rings. Due to its role in β-carotene formation, the LCYB gene has been one of the preferred targets of genetic engineering to obtain crops biofortified with provitamin A (Zheng et al. 2020, 2021). Indeed, several studies showed that the transformation with LCYB from plants or bacteria leads to an accumulation of β-carotene and oxygen-containing downstream products, i.e., β-xanthophylls, making it a useful tool for provitamin A biofortification (Apel and Bock 2009; D'Ambrosio et al. 2004; Rosati et al. 2000; Wurbs et al. 2007), while possible effects of LCYB overexpression on plant growth and development were less investigated. However, recent studies show how the engineering of LCYB gene has emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing crop traits beyond biofortification (Moreno and Al-Babili 2023). For instance, the constitutive overexpression of the carrot LCYB1 gene in tobacco resulted in higher β-carotene, xanthophylls, chlorophylls, and ABA and GA contents leading to improved shoot growth and yield, enhanced photosynthesis, enhanced abiotic stress tolerance, and modulated shoot architecture (Moreno et al. 2016a, b, 2021a; Moreno et al. 2020). Recently, overexpression of plant and bacterial LCYB in tomato resulted in biomass partitioning and altered plant growth as well as enhanced abiotic stress tolerance and improved fruit yield and shelf life due to modulation of carotenoids, apocarotenoids, and phytohormones (Mi et al. 2022). Similarly, tomato fruits overexpressing the Arabidopsis AtLCYB gene under the control of the phytoene desaturase (PDS) promoter, which shows increased activity during fruit ripening (Rosati et al. 2000), produced higher ABA content and have longer shelf life (Diretto et al. 2020). However, these studies mainly focused on changes in shoots, leaves, and fruits and left below-ground tissues remained unexplored.

Here, we investigated how the overexpression of LCYB genes affects tobacco and tomato roots at physiological and metabolic level and how these changes shape rhizospheric interactions. For this purpose, we characterized the root tissues of previously generated LCYB-overexpressing tobacco and tomato lines. We found that LCYB overexpression increased root growth and biomass, and modulated the content of various metabolites, including β-carotene and the phytohormones ABA and SL. These molecular changes, particularly, the modulation in SL and ABA contents, impacted the mycorrhiza symbiosis and the capability of root exudates to germinate seeds of root parasitic plants, such as Striga, which rely on host-released SLs as germination signal. These findings highlight the promising role of LCYB in the development of crops capable of better adaptation to future environmental changes and in controlling root parasitic plants.

Materials and methods

Plant material and growth conditions

In this work, we used previously published DcLCYB1-expressing tobacco lines (Moreno et al. 2016a, b) and transplastomic NpLCYB-expressing tomato lines (Apel and Bock 2009). Phenotypic characterization was carried out with plants grown in hydroponic media or in soil. For the phenotypic characterization of tobacco roots in hydroponic system, tobacco seeds were first grown on soil under greenhouse conditions (16 h/8 h day/night regime, 450–800 μmol photons m−2s−1 combination of artificial light and sunlight, 30 °C, and 65% relative humidity; 15 cm pots) for 2 weeks. Then, they were transferred to hydroponic system consisting of 50 mL black tubes containing modified Hoagland solution [5.6mM NH4NO3, 0.8mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.8mM K2SO4, 0.18mM FeSO4·7H2O, 0.18mM Na2EDTA·2H2O, 1.6mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.8mM KNO3, 0.023mM H3BO3, 0.0045mM MnCl2·4H2O, 0.0003mM CuSO4·5H2O, 0.0015mM ZnCl2, 0.0001mM Na2MoO4·2H2O (-Pi conditions)] for 1 week. For the hydroponic characterization of tomato roots, tomato seeds were surface-sterilized in 15% sodium hypochlorite (v/v) (5.25% of NaClO stock solution) under gentle agitation and rinsed with sterile water at least five times. Then, seeds were placed in Magenta boxes containing synthetic medium (half-strength Murashige and Skoog medium with 0.4% agarose and 3% sucrose). Seeds were directly transferred to a growth chamber under controlled conditions (day/night temperature of 28/22 °C and a 12h photoperiod, 200 μmol photons/m−2s−1). The 2-week-old seedlings were then transferred to a hydroponic system consisting of 50 mL black tubes containing modified Hoagland solution [5.6mM NH4NO3, 0.8mM MgSO4·7H2O, 0.8mM K2SO4, 0.18mM FeSO4·7H2O, 0.18mM Na2EDTA·2H2O, 1.6mM CaCl2·2H2O, 0.8mM KNO3, 0.023mM H3BO3, 0.0045mM MnCl2·4H2O, 0.0003mM CuSO4·5H2O, 0.0015mM ZnCl2, 0.0001mM Na2MoO4·2H2O with 0.4mM K2HPO4·2H2O (+ Pi conditions)] for 18 days.

For the phenotypic characterization in soil system, and tissue collection for carotenoid and apocarotenoid quantification, tomato and tobacco plants were grown in soil under the greenhouse conditions mentioned above. For SL analysis in root exudates from hydroponic system, both tomato and tobacco seedlings were transferred from magenta boxes to 50 mL tubes with Hoagland solution containing + Pi for 1 week and then transferred to tubes with Hoagland solution containing 0.04 mM K2HPO4·2H2O (low Pi) for one more week. The solution was changed every other day and 6 h before the exudate collection for LC–MS/MS analysis and Striga bioassay. For SL analysis in root exudates from sand system, tobacco and tomato were grown on soil for 3 weeks and then transferred to sand for 1 week. On sand, plants were fed with 500 mL of solution without phosphate every day and 24 h before exudate collection for LC–MS/MS analysis and Striga bioassay.

Plant productivity analysis

For the biomass experiments in hydroponic media, the roots of 35-day-old plants were harvested and dried (n = 6). Then, root length and fresh weight were recorded. Afterward, roots were dried to constant weight at 70 °C. For the biomass experiments in the greenhouse under semi-controlled conditions (16 h/8 h day/night regime, 450–800 μmol photons m−2s−1 combination of artificial light and sunlight, 30 °C, and 65% relative humidity), plants were grown in 15 cm-diameter pots, randomized, and spaced 30 cm (n = 8). Roots were harvested, carefully washed (to remove any trace of soil) and then dried to constant weight at 70 °C. In all growth phenotyping experiments, root length and dry weight were recorded.

Carotenoid extraction and HPLC analysis

Carotenoid extraction was performed according to Alagoz et al. (2020). Briefly, 300 mg FW of tobacco and tomato root tissue was extracted with 500 µL of extraction buffer (acetone-ethyl acetate/EtOAc (6:4, v/v) containing 1 g butylated hydroxytoluene/BHT). Then, 500 µL of water was added and the tubes were inverted two-to-three times. Samples were centrifuged for 5 min at 15,000 RPM at 4 °C. The upper ethyl acetate phase (∼50 µL) was collected and transferred to a 2 mL amber colored glass vial for the carotenoid quantification. The absolute quantification of pigments was performed using a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Agilent 1260 Infinity) system supplemented with a YMC-C30 (250 × 4.6 mm, S-5µm) column as previously described (Alagoz et al. 2020). Pigments were identified based on their peak distribution referring to their retention time compared to known standards and their maximum UV absorbance spectra detected at 438 nm (violaxanthin), 436 nm (neoxanthin), 446 nm (lutein), and 452 nm (β-carotene) in tobacco and tomato roots.

Apocarotenoid quantification

For apocarotenoid extraction, 300 mg FW of tobacco and tomato root tissues was grinded and spiked with 2 ng of D3-Apo7 (β-cyclocitral), D3-Apo8 (all-trans-β-8’-carotenal), D3-Apo9 (β-ionone), D3-Apo10 (all-trans-β-10’-carotenal), D3-Apo11 (all-trans-β-11-carotenal), D3-Apo12 (all-trans-β-12’-carotenal), D3-OH-Apo13 (zaxinone), D3-Apo14 (all-trans-β-14’-carotenal) and D3-Apo15 (retinal). Samples were then extracted with 1.8 mL methanol containing 0.1% formic acid by sonication for 15 min in an ultrasonic bath (Branson 3510 ultrasonic bath), followed by centrifugation for 8 min at 15,000 rpm and 4 °C. The supernatants were dried under the vacuum. Dried extracts were dissolved in 120 μL of acetonitrile:water (25:75, v:v) and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter for LC–MS/MS analysis. The quantification of apocarotenoids was performed using a Vanquish Duo UHPLC Systems coupled with a TSQ Altis triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) with a heated-electrospray ionization source. The chromatographic separation was carried out on an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (100 × 2.1 mm; 1.7 μm; Waters) with an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 guard column (5 × 2.1 mm; 1.7 μm; Waters) maintained at 35 °C. Mobile phases consist of 20% aqueous acetonitrile (A) and 60:40 acetonitrile:isopropanol (B), both containing 0.01% formic acid. Both were employed for eluting APOs with the gradient program: 0–1 min, 0% B; 1–3 min, 0% B to 40% B; 3–8 min, 40% B to 80% B; 8–14 min, 80% B to 90% B; 14–15 min, 90% B to 100% B; followed by washing with 100% B and equilibration with 100% A, at 0.2 mL/min of flow rate. The MS parameters were as follows: positive ion, 4000 V; sheath gas, 40 Arb; aux gas, 20 Arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 325 °C; vaporizer temperature, 300 °C; cycle time, 1 s; Q1 resolution (FWHM), 0.7; Q3 resolution (FWHM), 0.7; CID gas (mTorr), 1.5; and chromatographic peak width (sec), 6. To see the nomenclature of the apocarotenoids quantified in this work, please see Table S1.

Hormonomics in tobacco and tomato roots

The levels of endogenous phytohormones, i.e., cytokinins, auxins, jasmonates, abscisates, and gibberellins, were determined in 1 mg DW (n = 5) of freeze-dried tobacco and tomato roots, according to a modified method described previously (Šimura et al. 2018). Hormone quantification was performed as previously described in Mi et al. (2022). To see the nomenclature of the phytohormones measured through Hormonomics, see Table S2. Briefly, the homogenized plant material was extracted in 1 mL of aqueous 60% (v/v) acetonitrile with the mixture of stable isotopic labeled internal standards (Table S3) and four zirconium oxide beads. The sample was shaken in the oscillation mill (Retsch MM400, Retsch, Germany) for 5 min at 27 Hz, sonicated for 3 min and incubated in refrigerator for 30 min. Then, the sample was centrifuged at 20,000 rpm for 10 min in benchtop centrifuge (Alegra 64, Beckman Coulter, USA). The full supernatant was purified by Oasis® HLB extraction cartridge 30 mg/1 mL (Waters, Milford, CT USA) previously conditioned with 1 mL of methanol and 1 mL of aqueous 60% (v/v) acetonitrile. The cartridge was washed with 1 mL of aqueous 60% (v/v) acetonitrile after the loading of the sample. The flow through and wash fraction was collected into the glass tube (total volume 2 mL) and evaporated to dryness in the SpeedVac concentrator (RC1010 Centrivap Jouan, ThermoFisher, USA). The sample was reconstructed in 40 µl of aqueous 25% (v/v) acetonitrile and transferred into the glass insert enclosed in the liquid chromatography vial. Then, 5 µl of the sample were injected into the Acquity UPLC® CSH™ C18 1.7 µm 2.1 × 150 mm chromatographic column (Waters, Milford, CT USA) and analyzed by Acquity UPLC® I-Class system (Waters, Milford, CT, USA) coupled with Xevo TQ-XS triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Waters, Manchester, UK) equipped with electrospray ionization. Data were acquired in multiple reaction monitoring mode. Phytohormones were quantified using external calibration and isotope dilution method. MRM transitions of targeted compounds and corresponding internal standards are specified in Table S3.

Strigolactones extraction and quantification in root exudates

For the exudate collection from hydroponic system, 50 mL of exudates was collected for each sample, kept on ice, and spiked with 2 ng of rac-GR24. Collection, extraction, and analysis of SLs from root exudates were performed by following a published protocol (Wang et al. 2022a, b). In sand system, 1 L of root exudates was collected from the bottom of the pot and spiked with 2 ng of rac-GR24. The samples, from hydroponic and sand systems, were brought on a C18-Fast Reversed-SPE column (500 mg/3 mL) and pre-conditioned with 3 mL of methanol. After washing with 3 mL of water, SLs were eluted with 5 mL of acetone. Thereafter, SLs-containing fraction was concentrated to SL aqueous solution (∼500 μL), followed by 1 mL of ethyl acetate extraction. Then, 750 μL of the SL-enriched fraction was dried under vacuum. The final extract was re-dissolved in 100 μL of acetonitrile:water (25:75, v:v) and filtered through a 0.22 μm filter for LC–MS/MS analysis. Quantification of SLs was performed using UHPLC-Triple-Stage Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific Altis). Chromatographic separation was achieved on the Hypersil GOLD C18 Selectivity HPLC Columns (150 × 4.6 mm; 3 μm; Thermo Scientific) with mobile phases consisting of water (A) and acetonitrile (B), both containing 0.1% formic acid, and the following linear gradient (flow rate, 0.5 mL/min): 0–15 min, 25–100% B, followed by washing with 100% B and equilibration with 25 B for 3 min. The injection volume was 10 μL, and the column temperature was maintained at 35 °C for each run. The MS parameters of Thermo Scientific Altis were as follows: positive ion mode, ion source of H-ESI, ion spray voltage of 5000 V, sheath gas of 40 arbitrary units, aux gas of 15 arbitrary units, sweep gas of 20 arbitrary units, ion transfer tube gas temperature of 350 °C, vaporizer temperature of 350 °C, collision energy of 17 eV, CID gas of 2 mTorr, and full width at half maximum (FWHM) 0.2 Da of Q1/Q3 mass. The characteristic Multiple Reaction Monitoring (MRM) transitions (precursor ion → product ion) were 347.14 → 329.14, 347.14 → 233.12, 347.14 → 205.12, 347.14 → 97.028 for orobanchol; 317.17 → 164.08, 317.17 → 97.028 for putative Oxo-CL (CL + 14); 345.2 → 97.028, 345.2 → 231.0; 345.2 → 327.0 for didehydroorobanchols; 365.0 → 268.0; 365.0 → 97.028 for solanacol; 299.09 → 185.06, 299.09 → 157.06, 299.09 → 97.028 for GR24.

Striga germination bioassays

Root exudates of tobacco and tomato wt (Xanthi and IPA6 +) and transgenic plants (DcLCYB1 lines 14 and 15 and pNLyc#2 and #22) were assessed with respect to their capability to induce seed germination in Striga, which reflects their SL content, by adopting a previously described protocol (Jamil et al. 2022). For this purpose, the tomato plants were grown hydroponically in 50 ml tubes for 3 weeks under low phosphorus conditions. SLs were collected from the root exudates of each treated plant (wt v/s L14 and L15; wt IPA6 v/s pNLyc#2 and #22) through C18 column and were applied on pre-conditioned Striga hermonthica seeds. For pre-conditioning, the Striga seeds were surface sterilized with 50% commercial bleach for 5 min and washed with sterilized milliQ water for six times. The seeds were dried in a laminar flow cabinet and spread uniformly on 9 mm glass fiber filter paper disks (~ 50–100 seeds per disk). Then, 12 disks with Striga seeds were transferred into a Petri plate, containing a Whatman filter paper, moistened with 3 ml sterilized water. The Petri plates were sealed and wrapped in aluminum foil and incubated at 30 °C for 10 days. The pre-conditioned seeds were treated with each sample (at 55 µL per disk) and incubated again at 30 °C for 24 h. The seeds were scanned by a microscope, and the germination ration was determined by counting germinated and total Striga seeds using the SeedQuant software (Braguy et al. 2021).

Mycorrhization analysis

Tomato seeds (wild-type and pNLyc#2) were surface-sterilized with 70% ethanol and 0.01% Tween-20 for 3 min and then in a 5% commercial hypochlorite solution for 13 min. Seeds were then transferred to Petri dishes containing 0.6% plant agar medium (Duchefa, Haarlem, The Netherlands), and germinated in the dark at 23 °C for 5 days. Seedlings were then moved to day/night conditions for another 4 days (16 h/8 h day/night regime, 23 °C in the light and 21 °C in the dark) and then transferred to pots. Seeds of wild-type and LCYB-overexpressing tobacco lines (L14 and L15) were surface-sterilized in 15% commercial hypochlorite solution for 10 min. After a series of rinses in water, seeds were transferred to Petri dishes containing MS media + 3% of sucrose and set to vernalize in the dark at 4 °C. Then, seedlings were moved to day/night conditions for another 4 days (16 h/8 h day/night regime, 23 °C in the light and 21 °C in the dark) and transferred to pots. Both tobacco and tomato plants were inoculated with a commercial inoculum of Funneliformis mosseae (BEG12, MycAgroLAb, France) mixed (15%) with sterile quartz sand. Plants were watered with a modified Hoagland nutrient solution containing low Pi concentration (50 μM Pi). All plants were sampled at 10 and 35 days post-inoculation (DPI), corresponding to early and late stages of mycorrhization. For molecular analyses, roots were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Some roots were dedicated to morphometric evaluation according to Trouvelot et al. (1986). Samples were stained with 0.1% cotton blue in lactic acid and the estimation of mycorrhizal parameters was performed with MycoCalc software (www2dijon.inra.fr/mychintec/Mycocalc-prg/download.html).

Gene expression analysis of mycorrhizal tomato and tobacco roots

Total RNA from tomato and tobacco roots was extracted from rice roots using the Qiagen Plant RNeasy Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Hilden; Germany). Following the producer's instructions, samples were treated with TURBO™ DNase (ThermoFischer). The RNA samples were routinely checked for DNA contamination through PCR analysis. Single-strand cDNA was synthesized from 1 μg of total RNA using Super-Script II (Invitrogen), according to the instructions in the user manual. Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using a Rotor-Gene Q 5plex HRM Platform (Qiagen). Each reaction was carried out in a total volume of 15 μl containing 2 μl of diluted cDNA (about 10 ng), 7.5 μl of 2 × QuantiNova SYBR Green RT-PCR Master Mix, and 2.75 μl of each primer (3 μM). The following PCR program was used: 95 °C for 120 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, and 60 °C for 10 s. A melting curve (80 steps with a heating rate of 0.5 °C per 10 s and a continuous fluorescence measurement) was recorded at the end of each run to exclude the generation of non-specific PCR products. All reactions were performed on at least three biological and two technical replicates. Baseline range and take-off values were automatically calculated using Rotor-Gene Q 5plex software. For tomato, the gene expression level of SlPt4 (phosphate transporter—AM marker gene) was calculated by normalization of a ubiquitin (SlUbq) housekeeping gene. For tobacco roots, the transcript levels of NtPt4 (phosphate transporter—AM induced gene) (Cosme et al. 2016) and Fm18S (F. mosseae housekeeping gene) were normalized using the NtACT housekeeping gene (Moreno et al. 2020).

Only take-off values leading to a mean with a standard deviation below 0.5 were considered. Primers use for this experiment can be found in Table S4.

Statistical analyses

Ordinary unpaired two-tailed t test and multiple comparisons for statistical significance were performed with GraphPad Prism 9 for Mac OS X, using a probability level of P < 0.05. For the gene expression and mycorrhization analysis, statistical elaborations were performed using PAST statistical software (version 4).

Results

LCYB overexpression promotes root length and biomass in tobacco and tomato plants

To determine the impact of LCYB overexpression on the root systems, we used the previously investigated tobacco (cv. Xanthi) lines, L14 and L15, overexpressing the LCYB1 gene from carrot in the nucleus (Moreno et al. 2016a, b; Moreno et al. 2020) and two transplastomic tomato (cv. IPA6 +) lines, pNLyc#2 and #22, overexpressing the daffodil LCYB gene in plastids (Apel and Bock 2009). Both LCYB genes were expressed under the control of the constitutive CaMV35S promoter (Apel and Bock 2009). We characterized the root growth of 35-day-old tobacco (Fig. 1A, Fig. S1; Table S5) and tomato (Fig. 1B, Fig. S2; Table S5) transgenic lines grown in hydroponic and observed an increase in root length in all four transgenic lines, compared to the corresponding controls. The increased length was accompanied by an increase in root biomass determined in fresh and dry tissues (Fig. 1A, B). Measurement of root length of plants grown in soil revealed similar results, suggesting the positive effect of LCYB overexpression on root growth in all transgenic tobacco and tomato lines (Fig. 1C, D; Table S6). Interestingly, this phenotype was maintained through different tobacco developmental stages (Fig. S3). Confirming these results, we investigated two tobacco LCYB RNAi lines 2.2 and 2.7 (Kossler et al. 2021) and observed reduced root length (Fig. S4). LCYB overexpression modulates root carotenoid and apocarotenoid contents in tobacco and tomato

Carotenoids are precursors of regulatory apocarotenoids, such as β-cyclocitral and zaxinone, which have signaling and growth-promoting properties (Dickinson et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019). To investigate if the observed growth phenotype is related to changes in carotenoid and apocarotenoid content, we determined the composition of these metabolites in the transgenic tobacco and tomato roots (Fig. 2). We found an increase in total carotenoids in L14 and L15 transgenic tobacco roots overexpressing the carrot LCYB gene (Fig. 2A; Table S7). This increase affected mainly β-carotene and violaxanthin levels, both of which are from the β-branch of the pathway, while lutein (α-branch) showed no difference when compared to the wild-type roots. However, this increase did not lead to a general increase in the levels of all β-branch apocarotenoids. Indeed, we observed a decrease in Apo-9 (β-ionone) and Apo-10’ (all-trans-β-apo-10’-carotenal), whereas the level of Apo-13 (D'Orenone) and Apo-14’ (all-trans-β-apo-14’-carotenal) increased in tobacco transgenic roots (Fig. 2B; Table S8). In tomato roots, transgenic lines exhibited a slight reduction in the level of violaxanthin and lutein, but an up to ~ 1.8-fold increase in β-carotene content (Fig. 2C; Table S9). In addition, we observed an increase in Apo-11 (all-trans-β-apo-11-carotenal), D'Orenone (all-trans-β-apo-13-carotenone), and OH-Apo-13 isomer (zaxinone isomer) (Fig. 2D; Table S10). Constitutive LCYB overexpression influences hormone pattern in tobacco and tomato roots

Carotenoids and apocarotenoids are precursors of two major phytohormones, ABA and SLs. Based on our results showing alterations in carotenoid and apocarotenoid profiles in tobacco and tomato roots, we wondered if ABA and other hormones might be also altered in these tissues. The hormones and intermediaries that were analyzed are listed in Table S2. We found that roots of LCYB-expressing transgenic lines have a higher ABA content, i.e., up to 1.8-fold (in L15) and 4.8-fold (in pNLyc#2) for tobacco and tomato, respectively (Fig. 3A, B; Tables S11–12). In contrast, jasmonic acid (JA) content remained unchanged (Table S11). The auxin indole acetic acid (IAA) content was increased in tomato (Fig. 3B; Table S12) transgenic roots while no significant changes were observed in tobacco roots (Fig. 3A; Table S11). Moreover, the amount of other auxin derivatives, such as the oxidized form of IAA (oxi-IAA), indole-3-acetic conjugated to aspartate (IAA-asp), indole-3-acetamide (IAM) and their precursor tryptophan (TRP), were reduced in tobacco roots (Fig. S5). In addition, we observed higher accumulation of the cytokinins tZR-O-glucoside (tZROG) and the total content of cytokinin O-glucosides in the tobacco transgenic lines L14 and L15 (Fig. 3A; Fig. S5). We observed a different cytokinin pattern in tomato transgenic roots that showed a reduction in the contents of the intermediates cZR-O-glucoside (cZROG), cis-zeatin-7-glucoside (cZ7G), dihydrozeatin 7-glucoside (DHZ7G) and dihydrozeatin 9-glucoside (DHZ9G; Fig. S6). Roots of LCYB-overexpressing plants release less SLs, thus reducing Striga germination

As we observed changes in carotenoids, apocarotenoids, and ABA profiles, we hypothesized that also the amount of SL released by the transgenic roots could be influenced by the overexpression of LCYB. To test this hypothesis, we collected root exudates from tobacco and tomato roots, and their SL content was quantified using UHPLC–MS/MS analysis. A first analysis was conducted with exudates of tobacco seedlings grown on hydroponic phosphate-deprived conditions. As shown in Fig. 4A (Table S13), didehydro-orobanchol was completely absent in both transgenic lines, whereas the wild-type roots released up to 3.8 pg of this SL per replicate. As it was not possible to determine SL content in tomato seedlings grown in hydroponic, we decided to analyze the root exudates collected from plants grown on sand supplemented with low-phosphate solution. Consistent with the results obtained for tobacco, tomato root exudates showed a reduced content of didehydro-orobanchol and solanacol (Fig. 4A; Table S13), while no changes were observed for orobanchol and the putative oxo-carlactone (Fig. S7). To confirm the tobacco results obtained under hydroponic conditions, we analyzed exudates collected from tobacco plants grown in sand as we did for tomato. As expected, tobacco transgenic lines showed a decreased content of orobanchol compared to the wild type (Fig. 4A; Table S13), while the content of oxo-carlactone did not change (Fig. S7). Next, we used Striga bioassay to confirm the LC–MS quantification and determine the total content of SLs released by the transgenic roots. As shown in Fig. 4B, we detected a significant reduction of Striga-seed germinating activity in LCYB-overexpressing lines, up to a 9.7- and 9.8-fold in tobacco and tomato plants, respectively (Fig. 4B; Table S14). Next, we measured the level of AM symbiosis in the LCYB-overexpressing lines and in wild-type roots, taking into consideration the promoting role of SLs in establishing this symbiosis. We assessed the level of tobacco root mycorrhization in both wild-type and OE lines evaluating the transcript levels of NtPt4 (Cosme et al. 2016), a specific AM-inducible Pi-transporter gene and the fungal housekeeping Fm18S (Votta et al. 2022) at 10 and 35 DPI corresponding to early and late stages of mycorrhization. No differences in the expression level of both genes were detected among genotypes in the two time points (Fig. S8A). However, the morphological analysis revealed a decreased trend in the frequency of colonization (F%), mycorrhizal intensity (M%), and arbuscule abundance (A%) (Fig. 4C; Table S15) in OE lines compared to the wild type in the early time-point (10 DPI), indicating a reduced level of mycorrhization in the first stage of the colonization. The mycorrhization level was also evaluated in tomato roots by measuring the transcript level of SlPt4 tomato marker gene, since it is known to be induced by mycorrhization (Balestrini et al. 2007). Transcript level of SlPt4 was analyzed in the wild type and in one transgenic line (pNLyc #22) at two different time points, 10 and 35 DPI, (Fig. S8B). As expected, SlPt4 expression was significantly reduced at both 10 DPI and 35 DPI, with a reduction of around 40-fold and 2-fold, respectively (Fig. S8B). To confirm the molecular results, a morphological analysis was performed by evaluating the F%, M%, and A% parameters at 35 DPI. Discussion

Carotenoids are isoprenoid compounds with well-known functions in photosynthesis, pollination, ROS scavenging, and as precursors of vitamin A in mammals (Moreno et al. 2021a, b). β-carotene, a major plant carotenoid synthesized by the enzyme LCYB, is the precursor for apocarotenoids and apocarotenoid-derived hormones that play key roles in abiotic and biotic stress tolerances, development, biotic interactions, and growth regulation (Moreno et al. 2025; Beltran and Stange 2016; D'Alessandro et al. 2019; Dickinson et al. 2019; Felemban et al. 2024; Jia et al. 2019; Jia et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022; Mitra et al. 2021; Moreno et al. 2021a, b; Murata et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2019). Recently, tobacco plants overexpressing the carrot LCYB1 gene were reported to have increased carotenoid, and ABA and GA contents in their leaves, resulting in enhanced photosynthetic efficiency, plant growth and yield, and stress tolerance (Moreno et al. 2021a, b; Moreno et al. 2020). Similarly, overexpression of plant and bacterial LCYB genes in different tomato cultivars modulated carotenoid, apocarotenoid, and hormone contents in leaf and fruits resulting in biomass partitioning, and enhanced stress tolerance, fruit size, and shelf life (Mi et al. 2022). Based on this evidence, LCYB was proposed as a promising target not only for vitamin A biofortification, but also for improving crop productivity and resilience (Moreno and Al-Babili 2023). However, the effect of this strategy on the growth and metabolic status of the below-ground tissues of these plants remained unexplored. This study aimed at filling this gap.

In tobacco and tomato roots, the higher accumulation of β-carotene (Fig. 2) indicates that the constitutive expression of the LCYB gene also increased the activity of the corresponding enzyme in these tissues. Total carotenoid accumulation pattern in tobacco transgenic roots was higher than in the wild type (Fig. 2) as previously reported for the leaf (Moreno et al. 2020). In contrast, total carotenoid content in tomato transgenic roots was lower than in the wild type roots (Fig. 2), similarly to the reduction in total carotenoids reported for tomato leaf (Mi et al. 2022), but with an increase in β-carotene in both plant tissues. Thus, suggesting similar changes might be triggered at apocarotenoid and hormone contents in different tissues. On the one hand, both LCYB-expressing tobacco and tomato roots have more ABA than the wild type, which is in line with higher β-carotene and violaxanthin in tobacco, and β-carotene content for tomato (Fig. 2). These results are also in line with the higher ABA content showed in tobacco and tomato leaves (Mi et al. 2022; Moreno et al. 2020). On the other hand, tobacco and tomato roots showed reduced SL accumulation in root exudates (Fig. 4A), and most likely as well in root tissues, since SL content in roots and exudates are expected to be directly correlated (Ablazov et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2019, 2020). Taken together, this evidence suggests that more β-carotene is used for the biosynthesis of ABA, and less β-carotene is available for the biosynthesis of SL. The modulation of these plant hormones affected important biotic interactions and most likely root growth in these plants. Recently, ABA was reported to inhibit Striga-seed germination and act as a rhizospheric signal (Jamil et al. 2024). By contrast, canonical forms of SLs induce Striga-seed germination (Ito et al. 2022; Li et al. 2024). The decrease in SL contents in LCYB-expressing tobacco and tomato roots directly correlated with the observed reduction in Striga-seed germination treated with the root exudates produced from these plants (Fig. 4A, B). Even though reduced SL content is an advantage with respect to the susceptibility for root parasitic weeds, such as Striga, SL positively regulates AM symbiosis (Belmondo et al. 2017; Kodama et al. 2022). It was shown that petunia, pea, rice, and tomato mutants impaired in SL biosynthesis or release showed reduced AM fungi colonization (Breuillin et al. 2010; Gomez-Roldan et al. 2008; Gutjahr et al. 2012; Kohlen et al. 2012; Kretzschmar et al. 2012; Vogel et al. 2010; Yoshida et al. 2012). In line with that body of evidence, the reduction in SL content in root exudates most likely triggered the reduction in colonization frequency, mycorrhizal intensity, and arbuscule abundance in the LCYB-overexpressing tobacco and tomato plants (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, ABA positively regulates mycorrhization in tomato. In fact, mycorrhization experiments in tomato ABA-deficient mutant sitiens (8% ABA content compared to wild type) showed that ABA is needed for complete and fully functional arbuscule development and for sustained colonization of the plant root (Herrera-Medina et al. 2007). Moreover, exogenous ABA application increases mycorrhization in wild-type tomato roots (Herrera-Medina et al. 2007). In addition, in Medicago truncatula, ABA treatment promotes AM colonization but only at low concentrations (Charpentier et al. 2014). Despite the increased ABA content measured in roots of the LCYB-overexpressing lines, we did not observe enhanced mycorrhization in these plants, most likely due to a more important role for SLs (strongly reduced) in this process. Nevertheless, field experiments should be performed to determine if this reduced AM colonization would have a penalty on the observed plant yield.

Root growth and architecture are determined by several factors, including plant hormones and some apocarotenoids. Interestingly, plants under nutrient deficiencies, particularly phosphate deficiency, tend to promote the production and exudation of SLs (Andreo-Jimenez et al. 2015; Marzec et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2022a; Yoneyama and Brewer 2021). The reduced SL content released by the LCYB-overexpressing roots might be attributed to a possible enhancement in nutrient uptake, which is reflected in improved root growth and biomass. This improvement in nutrient uptake could reduce the need for the plant to release SLs as a compensatory mechanism.

Another possible explanation for the enhanced root growth might be the accumulation of some β-carotene-derived molecules, e.g., β-cyclocitral and zaxinone, that have been reported as root growth regulators (Dickinson, et al. 2019; Wang, et al. 2019). Unfortunately, we could not identify β-cyclocitral in our root samples. Previous research showed that zaxinone application to rice plants caused a reduction in CCD7 and CCD8 expression resulting in reduced SL content and leading to enhanced root length and reduction in Striga emergence (Wang, et al. 2019). We also observed a reduction in SL content and Striga emergence in our LCYB-overexpressing tobacco and tomato roots suggesting the possibility of a similar mechanism responsible for the increased root length and biomass. However, in our case, the reduction in SL is not caused by increments in zaxinone content. Interestingly, structurally related apocarotenoids, such as D'Orenone and α-zaxinone, also triggered a reduction in SLs in roots and root exudates of rice plants treated with these apocarotenoids (Wang, et al. 2020) suggesting a similar function in regulating rice root growth. Based on this, we speculate that the increase in D'Orenone content in tobacco roots (Fig. 2B, D) might have contributed to the enhanced root growth (Fig. 1A). Additionally, we also observed increased content of D'Orenone and zaxinone isomer in tomato roots (Fig. 2D). Recent studies with anchorene (Jia et al. 2019) and its isomer iso-anchorene (Jia et al. 2021) showed the inhibition of primary root growth in Arabidopsis by exogenous application of these molecules, concluding that structurally related apocarotenoids might exert the same function. In line with this evidence, it would be possible that the increment in D'Orenone and/or zaxinone isomer might be contributing to the enhanced root length observed in tomato roots. The molecular and physiological characterization of the below-ground tissue provide another layer of understanding of the transgenic lines expressing the LCYB gene in a constitutive manner. For instance, the longer roots in these transgenic plants might be a consequence of the higher photosynthetic and growth rates previously observed in the shoot (Mi et al. 2022; Moreno et al. 2020). In addition, longer roots represent an advantage in terms of water and nutrient uptake, which are essential for plant growth. Moreover, the higher ABA content measured in the transgenic roots could explain the enhanced tolerance to salinity and drought previously observed in these plants (Mi et al. 2022; Moreno et al. 2021a, b). Thus, exposing the many fold advantages of the constitutive LCYB expression in different plant tissues.

Apocarotenoids are emerging as bioactive molecules associated with multiple functions in growth, development, and abiotic/biotic interactions (Li et al. 2025). Due to this, some apocarotenoids have been proposed as hormone candidates (Wang et al. 2021). Up to date, genes involved in apocarotenoid biosynthesis are scarce with some few exceptions, such as ZAXINONE SYNTHASE in rice (Wang et al. 2019), the CCD4b/CCD4c genes from citrus and saffron required for the production of β-cyclocitral (Havaux 2020; Rodrigo et al. 2013), and the CCD1 and CCD4 genes required for the production of β-ionone (Paparella et al. 2021). However, the CCD1 and/or CCD4 activities are not highly specific for β-cyclocitral and β-ionone biosynthesis. Thus, metabolic engineering of these compounds remains a great challenge. Here, we demonstrate that untargeted modulation of apocarotenoid and apocarotenoid-derived hormones can bring multiple advantages for the plant at once. Nevertheless, it is important to analyze the outcome of the genetic manipulation in each plant species and tissue.

Taken together, we demonstrated that LCYB-overexpressing tobacco and tomato plants accumulated more β-carotene in their roots and showed an alteration in the metabolic flux at the branching point of the carotenoid pathway, where β-carotene is produced, favoring the production of ABA (increased) over SL (reduced). Hormonal changes, and possibly also changes in the root apocarotenoid profile, influenced root growth and biotic interactions in the roots of these plants. These results together with our previous findings in aboveground tissues reinforced the biotechnological potential of the LCYB gene not only for biofortification, yield, and abiotic stress tolerance but also for biotic interactions in below-ground tissues, e.g., Striga biocontrol.
