Evaluation of gastroenterology training curriculum in Greece
Authors: Alexandros Ioannou, Konstantina Dimopoulou, Evripidis Rizos, Eleni Manthopoulou, Orestis Sidiropoulos, Evgenia Papathanasiou, Georgios Tziatzios, Konstantina Paraskeva, Ioannis S Papanikolaou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractResident Survey of Gastroenterology Training in Greece.Keywords: education, European GI education programs, residents, UEG, young GI

During the last decades, gastroenterology (GI) has seen some of the greatest evolutions as a specialty with rapid development in diagnostic and therapeutic procedures. In this fast‐moving field, it remains a key challenge to provide a high‐quality training program in order to enable GI trainees to acquire the knowledge base, procedural skills, clinical experience and become competent in all aspects of GI and hepatology by the completion of their fellowship. 1 , 2 Besides, the quality and safety of care delivered to patients with GI and liver disorders are directly linked to the quality of training provided to medical professionals. 3

In Greece, the GI training program includes 2 years of internal medicine and 4 years of fulltime GI and hepatology training. In 2019, the Greek Ministry of Health developed a new national competency‐based curriculum structured in concordance with the European recommendations released by the European Section and Board of Gastroenterology and Hepatology (ESBGH). 4 , 5 Thus, the national curriculum reports that during their training program, GI trainees are expected to develop competence in ward and outpatient clinical activities, providing clinical care in patients with gastrointestinal pathologies including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and hepatobiliary disorders, performing diagnostic and therapeutic endoscopic procedures, advanced endoscopy, and being able to tackle advanced hepatology, digestive oncology, nutrition, and GI motility disorders. Additionally, GI trainees should attend structured teaching sessions and conferences and develop research skills, as it is invaluable to achieve a balance between clinical activities and scientific productivity and realize the value of continuing education. 4

Although the European and national curriculum defines the exact criteria and expected competences for GI trainees, it is unclear whether these are applied in national specialty educational programs.

For that reason, we contacted the Greek GI fellows via email using the electronic records of the Hellenic society of gastroenterology (HSG) and invited them to participate in a survey by completing a structured questionnaire. Our questionnaire comprised of 36 questions in four separate sections, evaluating fellows' demographics, education level and satisfaction regarding every part of the Greek competency‐based curriculum on GI training and proposals of initiatives to improve the education level of existing training programs. The protocol study was proposed by the HSG Trainees Section and approved by the Governing Board of the HSG in February 2023. Confidentiality was ensured for all participants and their educational centers by not requiring specific information about training programs and specific local demographic characteristics.

The questionnaire was sent via email to all 122 GI fellows in Greece (due to population distribution, 71% of them were located in Athens and 29% in the rest of the country). A total of 71 completed the survey, giving a response rate of 58.2%. Over half of the participants (57.7%) were male. As shown in Table 1, the majority of respondents (70.4%) were between 30 and 35 years old and more than half of them (52.1%) were in their final year of fellowship. Most of the fellows were trained in GI units in Tertiary (71.8%) or University (23.9%) hospitals. Fifty‐five (77.5%) were GI fellows in hospitals located in Athens.

A significant percentage of fellows throughout Greece report that their departments have an inadequate number of gastroenterologists, both trainees (46.5%) and specialists (38%), to operate properly. Moreover, 38% of participants believe that the equipment in their endoscopy units is insufficient for them to fulfill their training purposes.

Regarding endoscopic procedures, all training centers in Greece have the necessary facilities to perform esophagogastroduodenoscopies (EGDs) and colonoscopies, both diagnostic and interventional. More than half of the participants performed an adequate amount of EGDs and colonoscopies (200 or more) and more than 10 interventional endoscopic procedures (polypectomies, hemostatic procedures, balloon dilation etc.) during the running year. On the other hand, 14.1% of the participants performed less than 100 diagnostic and 9.9% less than 5 interventional endoscopic procedures. Concerning advanced endoscopic procedures, ERCP is performed in 71.8% of the participants' training centers and EUS in less than half (49.3%) of the participants' training centers, resulting in an equivalent percentage of fellows that perform these procedures, as 50.7% and 70.4% of the participants have not performed any ECRP or EUS respectively during their latest year of fellowship. Less than half of the fellows (42.3%) are exposed to advanced techniques in hepatology, such as elastography and liver biopsy, while nearly half of them (50.7%) are trained in gastrointestinal motility procedures such as esophageal and anorectal manometry and 24h‐pH‐impedance.

All training centers throughout Greece provide outpatient clinics for GI. However, 15.5% and 9.9% of the participants do not have outpatient clinics for hepatology and IBD patients, respectively. In addition, 5.6% of the participants have not been officially trained in wards as their departments do not have beds for GI inpatients and 21.1% of them have been trained in hospitals that do not have structured emergency departments.

Participants were asked to answer 10 questions that showed their satisfaction level in the various fields of their training, as shown in Table 2. The most favorable answers regarding training were in the fields of endoscopy (69%), inpatient wards (57.8%), emergency cases (50.7%) and educational programs (47.3%), where fellows' expectations were met. On the other hand, in the fields of Neurogastroenterology (8.7%), GI oncology (26.8%), procedures in advanced Hepatology (9.8%) and nutrition (5%), only a small proportion of the participants were satisfied with the training obtained in their GI centers. Finally, most of the participants (76.1%) agreed that a short‐term transfer to another department would contribute to a more comprehensive fellowship training.

The first assessment of GI training in Europe was conducted in 2002 by Bisschops et al. revealing major disparities between 10 different European countries. 6 One third of final‐year trainees reported that they felt unconfident in some endoscopic procedures, while training in abdominal ultrasound, advanced endoscopy, radiology, and motility examinations was reported to be inadequate in most countries including Greece. 6 These data remain in line with our study results, where 69% of the GI fellows are satisfied with their endoscopy training but only 8.7% with the motility examination training.

A more recent study, evaluating training in endoscopy, revealed that in most countries the average number of endoscopic diagnostic procedures surpassed the minimum thresholds recommended by the ESBGH. Nevertheless, 50% of participants performed lower numbers of endoscopic interventional procedures than those proposed by the ESBGH. 7 Furthermore, studies assessing the quality of GI training curricula in the UK demonstrated that although the majority of trainees were satisfied with the overall training, only half of them achieved full colonoscopy certification by the completion of their specialty. 8 , 9 In our study, more than half of the GI fellows performed an acceptable number of basic endoscopic procedures and only 14.1% of them performed less than 100 diagnostic and 9.9% less than 5 interventional endoscopic procedures.

Overall, most of the European studies reported that it is essential to improve training in interventional endoscopy, especially EUS, ERCP and abdominal ultrasound. 7 Our data confirm these suggestions, revealing that 50.7% and 70.4% of GI fellows in Greece have not performed ECRP or EUS during their final year of fellowship.

A recent evaluation of GI training in Europe reported that 66% of trainees across Europe performed none or <10 esophageal manometries, while the time spent in procedures such as manometry, pH‐impedance and breath tests is minimum. 7 , 10 In our study, half of the GI fellows (50.7%) received training in gastrointestinal motility procedures, but only 8.7% of them retain this training satisfactory. The elevated number of GI fellows receiving gastrointestinal motility training may be explained by the increased number of participants from tertiary and university centers in our study.

Regarding ESBGH and national recommendations, nutrition knowledge is incorporated in the GI training as it is considered a cornerstone in patient care. 5 Nevertheless, structured training in this domain is rarely offered to GI trainees leading to educational gaps. 11 , 12 , 13 This is confirmed from our data regarding nutritional education in Greek GI fellows as well; only 5% of our respondents report that they are satisfied with their nutritional education during their fellowship.

Because of the growing complexity of GI, some training centers may lack the full capacity of training facilities and opportunities in all aspects of GI. Consequently, they cannot offer sufficient training in the complete spectrum of GI and trainees should fulfill the education program by rotating between training centers. 5 Furthermore, 86.2% of the participants in a European resident survey agreed that a universal European GI training program is necessary for achieving this goal. 7 Our study reveals that 76.1% of the Greek GI fellows agreed that a short‐term rotation to another department would contribute to a more successful fellowship training.

Despite the high clinical burden, involvement in research is considered a fundamental component of GI training. However, it has been demonstrated that European trainees devote only a few hours to theoretical lessons and scientific research while 80% of them carry out research activities outside working hours. 6 , 7 , 14 In our survey, 50.7% of the participants are not content with their participation in educational meetings and congresses and 71.8% declared themselves unsatisfied by their research activities.

Our study data confirm the existing literature evidence on heterogeneity and diversity in GI specialty training in Europe that leads to a different level of training provided to European GI residents. In Greece, the major barriers for national implementation of the European educational framework include the variability and subspecialization of training centers, absence of a legislated unified educational program and inadequate evaluation of trainers and training departments. Adoption by the Health Ministry of Greece, in collaboration with the HSG, of a unified GI educational program, structured on thematic semesters, which could also introduce an obligatory short‐term rotation of GI fellows based on capacities of training centers and their expertise, could significantly improve the apparent shortcomings in training quality.

In conclusion, the results of our study highlight the need for initiatives to be undertaken for more systematic research on education quality at a European level. The establishment of a training model that will homogenize the educational framework may reduce disparities in the specialty of GI in Europe. Therefore, national authorities should work in the direction of a greater standardization of educational programs and rigorous application of ESBGH standards. Moreover, by addressing educational issues that concern GI fellows over Europe, the latter can receive consistent and comprehensive training, which will ultimately help improve patient's care.
