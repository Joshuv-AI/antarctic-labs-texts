Lines of dehiscence as a biomechanical strategy for controlling damage during blade development in the bull kelp Nereocystis luetkeana
Authors: Alana Breitkreutz, Patrick T Martone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough tearing is most often considered mechanical damage, it plays a central role in blade development in the bull kelp Nereocystis luetkeana. Although developmental tearing has been described in other macrophytes, the presence of a line of dehiscence that directs fracture apart from reproduction or abscission remains rare and poorly understood. This study shows that lines of dehiscence in Nereocystis promote clean, longitudinal blade splits by reducing tearing strength and facilitating crack propagation in flow. These effects are especially pronounced in juvenile blades, in which anisotropic tearing properties have not yet fully developed. By combining structural weakening with flow‐driven crack extension, Nereocystis generates new blades through a mixed strategy of physiological patterning and passive fracture, revealing a previously underrecognized biomechanical strategy for controlling damage and adjusting morphology.Keywords: blade, blade splitting, damage, kelp, lamina, morphology, Phaeophyceae

INTRODUCTION

Kelp (Phaeophyceae, Laminariales) are conspicuously large, structurally complex macroalgae (Charrier et al., 2012; Fritsch, 1945) that often thrive along wave‐swept shores where drag and wave‐impingement limit organismal size (Blanchette, 1997; Denny et al., 1989, 1998, 2011; Denny & Gaylord, 2002; Koehl, 2022; Martone et al., 2012; Martone & Denny, 2008; Wolcott, 2007). A giant even among kelp, Nereocystis luetkeana occupies some of the most hydrodynamically stressful subtidal environments (Duggins et al., 2001; Fritsch, 1945; Johnson & Koehl, 1994; Koehl & Alberte, 1988). Its mature morphology includes multiple ribbon‐like blades that originate not from budding but through successive longitudinal subdivisions of an initial juvenile blade (Fritsch, 1945; MacMillan, 1899; Nicholson, 1970; Wells, 1910).

Many kelp sporophytes begin life as a single blade that divides during normal development (Fritsch, 1945; Setchell, 1908; Wells, 1910). The pattern of subdivision in this initial blade plays an important role in defining the eventual mature morphology and may represent an ancient, conserved mechanism for shaping thalli morphology. In some species, such as Postelsia and Lessoniopsis, blade division is restricted to early development (Fritsch, 1945; Wells, 1910). In others—including canopy kelps Macrocystis and Nereocystis—division continues after the thallus has obtained its characteristic adult form (Fritsch, 1945; Frye, 1930; MacMillan, 1899; Wells, 1910). Cracks typically appear proximally and propagate toward the blade apex, dividing the tissue longitudinally, long interpreted as evidence of a developmental program (Fritsch, 1945; MacMillan, 1899; Wells, 1910). In contrast, many other kelps tear backward from the blade tip in areas of high turbulence or wave‐driven movements (Armstrong, 1988; Fritsch, 1945), suggesting a passive response to mechanical stress akin to the tattering of flags in the wind (Jack & Savill, 1973; Quine & White, 1994).

Nereocystis presents an intermediate and particularly intriguing case. Blade subdivision occurs throughout development, creating multiple blades or blade clusters with separate petioles (Fritsch, 1945; Nicholson, 1970; Wells, 1910). However, the extent of blade subdivision varies with wave exposure: Thalli from wave‐protected sites have fewer blades than those from wave‐exposed sites, reflecting a plastic response to environmental flow conditions and, perhaps, tearing dynamics (Breitkreutz, 2026).

Blade divisions in Nereocystis often occur along visible lines of dehiscence (LODs; Figure 1), which run longitudinally down each blade and have not been reported in other kelps (Fritsch, 1945; Nicholson, 1970; Wells, 1910). These LODs are presumed to cause or facilitate blade splitting (Fritsch, 1945; Wells, 1910), although their roles in active versus passive blade splitting remain poorly characterized. In land plants, LODs define areas of weakness or mechanical stress concentration that allow for precise fracture (Dinneny & Yanofsky, 2005; Maier, 1973; Subramanyam & Raju, 1953; Wilson et al., 2014), primarily to aid in reproduction and dispersal. Lines of dehiscence are usually associated with the final disassembly of a structure, not morphogenesis (Dinneny & Yanofsky, 2005; Maier, 1973; Subramanyam & Raju, 1953; Wilson et al., 2014). Such weakening may arise from desiccation, cell death, or differential growth (Cremer, 1965; Duckett et al., 2009; Maier, 1973; Subramanyam & Raju, 1953), and fracture is typically driven by structural or hydrostatic stresses (Maier, 1973, 1974; Subramanyam & Raju, 1953; Sundberg, 2010).

(a) Juvenile Nereocystis luetkeana thallus with lines of dehiscence (LODs) indicated by arrows; p = pneumatocyst (diameter = 24 mm). (b) Mature thallus with LODs for comparison (pneumatocyst diameter = 42 mm).

Flow‐induced motion (e.g. bending, flapping, collisions, and rearrangements) can impose mechanical stresses that promote crack extension. In many angiosperms, the internal structure of the lamina influences tearing trajectories, often restricting crack propagation and increasing the energy required to extend a crack (Bidhendi et al., 2023; Choong et al., 1992; Read & Stokes, 2006; Sack & Scoffoni, 2013). For example, in banana leaves, strong transverse venation limits the path of cracks and creates a falsely pinnate appearance (Skutch, 1930; Taylor & Sexton, 2019). In Nereocystis, by contrast, tearing appears to be guided toward LODs, promoting fracture. Both Nereocystis and banana leaves direct fracture caused by hydrodynamic and aerodynamic forces, respectively. However, unlike banana leaves, Nereocystis appears to make one tearing axis easier rather than harder to propagate—a striking inversion of the usual structural strategy in macrophytes and likely related to the fact that this tearing is morphogenic, not destructive.

Blade subdivision mitigates drag, which is beneficial in high‐flow conditions, as was demonstrated in Nereocystis (Breitkreutz, 2026; Breitkreutz et al., 2022) and Hedophyllum (Armstrong, 1989; Milligan & DeWreede, 2004). Lines of dehiscence in Nereocystis, therefore, define potential sites for morphogenetic fracture: If flow forces are sufficient, then cracks along these lines can propagate and divide blades in order to reduce subsequent hydrodynamic loading on the thallus. We hypothesized that LODs in Nereocystis function similarly to those in land plants, creating an axis of weakness or stress concentration to control fracture, but differently in that crack propagation is driven by energy from environmental flow rather than internal structural or hydrostatic pressures. Because LODs tend to be especially common in the earliest blade divisions of Nereocystis and become less evident in later ones (see Figure 1; Figure S1), we also hypothesized that LODs play greater functional roles during juvenile blade proliferation.

To assess the functional role of LODs in blade division, we first quantified the effect of LODs on tearing strength. We then examined directional tearing properties in juvenile and mature blades to determine the importance of LODs in controlling the direction of fracture. Finally, we investigated the effect of LODs on the likelihood of blade splitting in a high‐speed water flume. We compared propagation of artificial cracks and natural cracks with LODs in flow to determine whether LODs concentrate flow‐induced stresses to promote crack extension. Together, these experiments help characterize LODs as a biomechanical strategy in Nereocystis for controlling damage and directing morphogenesis under hydrodynamic stress.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Effect of lines of dehiscence on tearing strength

In Summer 2024, one blade with a visible LOD, referred to as an LOD blade, was selected from each of 30 bull kelp thalli at Stanley Park, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada. Blades were collected intertidally by wading out at low tide. For each LOD blade, one or two control blades without an LOD were selected from separate thalli, matched for blade size and pneumatocyst diameter (maximum one blade per thallus; n = 58), representing blades of similar age and developmental stage at the site. Only undamaged blades with normal pigmentation and without epiphytes were collected.

For mechanical testing, 75 × 25 mm rectangular samples were excised with the long axis aligned to the blade. In LOD blades, the LOD was positioned along the center of the long axis, and a 10–15 mm starter crack was introduced along the LOD (on the proximal side of the rectangle; Figure 2a). In control blades, rectangles were positioned such that the starter crack would fall at the same distance from the pneumatocyst as in the corresponding LOD blade (Figure 2b). Blade thickness was measured at the end of the starter crack to the nearest 10 μm with a caliper.

Sample preparation for LOD presence/absence tearing trials. (a) Sample cut from a blade with an LOD (indicated by an arrow), with the starter crack aligned with the LOD. (b) Sample cut from a blade lacking an LOD, with the starter crack aligned to the midline of the blade.

Each sample was secured into an Instron material testing machine (Figure 3a), and trouser‐tear tests were conducted (Biedka et al., 1987; Denny et al., 1989). Data were analyzed in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024), and the package ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) was used for visualization.

Anisotropy of tearing properties

During the summers of 2023 and 2024, 34 mature Nereocystis thalli (pneumatocyst diameter > 50 mm) and 34 juvenile thalli (pneumatocyst diameter < 21 mm) were collected from Stanley Park, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada and Ogden Point, Victoria, British Columbia, Canada. Pneumatocyst diameter was used not to diagnose the exact developmental stage but to clearly separate juvenile (adult form not yet attained) and mature (adult form attained) categories. Thalli were collected intertidally by wading out at low tide. Only undamaged thalli with normal pigmentation and without epiphytes were collected. Within each developmental group, thalli were paired based on similar pneumatocyst diameter, and one individual from each pair was assigned a lengthwise starter crack and the other, a crosswise starter crack.

For each kelp, one intact, non‐dividing blade was removed, and the following testing procedure was used. A 75 × 25 mm rectangular sample piece was excised from the blade, approximately 30–100 mm from the pneumatocyst, using a razor. The long axis of the rectangle was aligned parallel to the blade's long axis for lengthwise samples and perpendicular to it for crosswise samples. A 10–15 mm starter crack was made parallel to the blade's long axis in lengthwise samples (Figure 4a) and perpendicular to it in crosswise samples (Figure 4b).

Starter crack orientations and angle of diversion measurement. (a) Samples with lengthwise starter cracks, oriented parallel to the blade's long axis; the arrow indicates the direction of the blade's long axis. (b) Samples with crosswise starter cracks, oriented perpendicular to the blade's long axis. (c) Measurement of the angle of diversion (labeled θ) between the starter crack and the extended tear following testing.

Each sample was secured in the Instron grips as shown in Figure 3a. Force was recorded continuously as each crack was extended at 20 mm · min−1 for 2 min. Angle of diversion was calculated by measuring the absolute value of the angle between each starter crack and the path of crack propagation as it extended (Figure 4c). A 0° angle of diversion would indicate that the crack propagated along the exact straight path of the starter crack. Force to tear (N) was calculated as the maximum force recorded during the test. Blade thickness was measured at the end of the starter crack and compared between age groups (mature vs. juvenile) and orientations (crosswise vs. lengthwise) using a two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), allowing us to assess whether differences in force to tear—particularly between orientations within each age group—were attributable to variation in blade thickness.

To visualize the results, force to tear (N) of juvenile and mature blades and angle of diversion were plotted against starter crack orientation using boxplots. Angle of diversion was qualitatively summarized due to limited within‐group variation. Force to tear data were log‐transformed to address heteroscedasticity. A two‐way ANOVA was used to examine the individual and interactive effects of orientation (crosswise vs. lengthwise) and age (mature vs. juvenile) on force to tear. Normality and homogeneity of variance in model residuals were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene's test, respectively. Post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) test to identify pairwise differences among all combinations of age and orientation. Data were visualized and analyzed in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024) with packages ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) and car (Fox & Weisberg, 2019).

Effect of lines of dehiscence on flow‐induced tearing probability

During the summers of 2020 and 2021, 55 Nereocystis blades with naturally occurring cracks with visible LODs were collected from Stanley Park, Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada, each from a different kelp thallus. One hundred fifty‐eight intact, non‐dividing blades were collected to serve as controls, each from a different kelp thallus. Blades were collected intertidally by wading out at low tide with no restriction to thallus size or developmental stage. Only undamaged blades with normal pigmentation and without epiphytes were collected. Artificial cracks of various lengths (5–225 mm) were introduced into these non‐dividing blades using a razor blade to mimic the range of crack lengths observed in blades with naturally occurring cracks used in this experiment. Artificial cracks were started 2–3 mm from the pneumatocyst to best mimic the position of naturally occurring cracks. For each blade, blade length, width, total area, and initial crack length were recorded. Crack propagation in flow was assessed by exposing each blade to one of five flow speeds (20, 35, 50, 65, and 80 cm · s−1) for 10 min in a high‐speed recirculating flume (Figure 5a). After each trial, crack extension was measured, and crack propagation was recorded as a binary variable (1 = crack propagated at least 2 mm, 0 = crack did not propagate or propagated <2 mm). A 2‐mm threshold was chosen to minimize false positives due to measurement error. Figure 5b,c shows an example blade before and after testing.

Methods for crack propagation trials in a high‐speed flume. (a) Experimental setup for flume trials used to quantify flow‐induced crack propagation. Each blade was mounted at its petiole to a sting (black arrow) connected to a force transducer in the flume lid. Flow direction was left to right (white arrow). (b) Blades with natural or artificial cracks were exposed to flow, and (c) the extent of crack propagation was recorded (dotted line). White arrow indicates the direction of crack propagation.

A generalized linear model (GLM) with binomial error distribution and a logit link was fit, to assess the probability of crack propagation as a function of crack origin (artificial or natural). The initial model included crack origin, initial crack length, blade length, blade width, blade area, the ratio of initial crack length to blade length, and flow speed as predictors. An interaction term between crack length and crack origin was included to test whether the effects of crack length differed by crack type. An interaction term between flow speed and blade length was also included to serve as a predictor proportional to Reynold's number. To reduce model complexity and avoid redundancy among correlated morphological variables, the full model was simplified by backward selection, sequentially removing non‐significant predictors (α = 0.05), and refitting the model at each step. This approach was used to identify a parsimonious model that retained only predictors with clear statistical support while minimizing overparameterization. The final model resulting from backward selection was used for all statistical inference and visualization. Predicted probabilities were visualized using logistic regression curves, plotted as a function of crack length for each crack type. Ninety‐five percent confidence intervals were calculated on the logit scale using standard errors of the fitted values, back‐transformed to the response scale, and plotted as shaded regions around model predictions. Data were analyzed in R 4.4.2 (R Core Team, 2024), and the package ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016) was used for visualization.

RESULTS

Effect of line of dehiscence on tearing strength

The presence of an LOD significantly reduced the tearing strength of blades (Student's t‐test; t(86) = 2.40, p < 0.01; Figure 6). The mean (± SE) tearing strength of blades with an LOD averaged 0.36 ± 0.06 N · mm−1, whereas that of blades lacking an LOD was 0.58 ± 0.06 N · mm−1. This difference corresponded to an approximate 37.7% reduction in tearing strength.

Tearing strength (N · mm−1) for blades with (right) and without (left) an LOD. The presence of an LOD significantly reduced tearing strength (p = 0.009). Sample sizes: Absent, n = 58; present, n = 30. Points are jittered horizontally for visualization.

Anisotropy of tearing properties

Lengthwise tears in mature blades did not divert from the trajectory of the starter crack (diversions ranged from 0° to 5°; Figure 7a). With the exception of two outliers in each group, crack propagation in juvenile blades closely followed the trajectory of the starter crack (diversions ranged from 0° to 7° for lengthwise tears and 0° to 10° for crosswise tears; Figure 7a). In contrast, crack propagation of crosswise starter cracks in mature blades diverted strongly toward the blade apex (diversions ranged from 78° to 94°; Figure 7a).

(a) Angle of diversion and (b) Force required to propagate tears initiated from lengthwise and crosswise starter cracks. Data for mature blades are shown on the left and juvenile blades on the right. Lower‐case letters indicate significant pairwise differences based on post hoc Tukey tests. Statistical analyses were performed on log‐transformed maximum load to meet model assumptions.

Mature blades were significantly thicker than juvenile blades, whereas thickness did not differ between crosswise and lengthwise samples within either group (Two‐way ANOVA, Age: F 1,64 = 263.92, p < 0.001; Orientation: F 1,64 = 0.19, p = 0.662; Age × Orientation: F 1,64 = 1.58, p = 0.214). The difference in force to tear mature and juvenile blades depended upon tearing direction (two‐way ANOVA, Orientation × Age: F 1,64 = 8.40, p < 0.01; Figure 7b). Mature blades required significantly more force to tear crosswise than lengthwise (Tukey's HSD, mean difference = 0.13 N; p < 0.001). Juvenile blades also required more force to tear crosswise than lengthwise, but this difference was smaller in magnitude (Tukey's HSD, mean difference = 0.02 N, p < 0.001; Figure 7b). Mature blades required significantly greater tearing force than juvenile blades in both orientations (crosswise: Tukey's HSD, mean difference = 0.144 N, p < 0.001; lengthwise: Tukey's HSD, mean difference = 0.040, p < 0.001; Figure 7b). Force to tear for mature blades lengthwise and juvenile blades crosswise was similar (Tukey's HSD, p = 0.060; Figure 7b).

Effect of line of dehiscence on flow‐induced tearing probability

Model simplification resulted in a final model that included only initial crack length and crack origin (see Table S1). Results were consistent with those obtained from the full model, in which only initial crack length and crack origin showed significant effects on propagation probability, whereas other predictors were not statistically significant. Blade width and flow speed showed positive but non‐significant effects on propagation probability, whereas other morphological variables (blade length, blade area, and the ratio of initial crack length to blade length) showed no significant effects and were removed during model simplification (see Table S1).

Cracks that were longer at the start of the trial were more likely to propagate in flow (binomial GLM, initial crack length: z = 5.08, p < 0.001; Figure 8). Naturally occurring cracks along an LOD propagated more readily than artificial cracks at every initial crack length (binomial GLM, natural origin: z = 2.27, p < 0.05). According to the model, artificial cracks required a longer initial length (~50.3 mm) to reach a 50% probability of propagation, whereas natural cracks along an LOD reached that 50% threshold at approximately half that length (~25.5 mm; Figure 8).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that Nereocystis thalli modify the mechanical properties of blade tissue through the formation of LODs that direct fracture along the longitudinal axis. Lines of dehiscence, which appear distal to splits during blade development, not only reduce tearing strength but also promote crack propagation in flow. By weakening tissue along specific lines, Nereocystis thalli facilitate the formation of clean, longitudinal splits rather than unpredictable fracture that could result in tissue loss.

Tearing strength values presented here were calculated by standardizing the force to tear by the overall blade thickness to generalize about the impact of LODs on blade properties. However, recent work showed that LODs are thinner at the microscopic level than surrounding tissue is (approx. 33.5% on average) and stain differently, suggesting subtle morphological and chemical modifications (Breitkreutz, 2026). That LODs are both 37.7% easier to tear and 33.5% thinner than surrounding tissue suggests differences in susceptibility to tearing may arise largely from tissue geometry rather than changes in material properties. Consistent with this interpretation, differences in chemical staining within the LODs may be associated with healing processes that follow blade tearing (Breitkreutz, 2026) rather than with tissue weakening.

Localized thinning would have two mechanical consequences. First, thinning reduces the second moment of area, which scales to the third power of blade thickness (Wainwright et al., 1976). Even a small decrease in thickness would make blades much more likely to bend and deform in flow (Biewener, 2005; Koehl et al., 2008; Niklas, 1998; Wainwright et al., 1976). Second, localized thinning introduces a geometric discontinuity that may concentrate stress, similar to a material notch or flaw, making breakage in that area more likely (Wainwright et al., 1976). Thus, thinning provides a simple mechanical explanation for both why cracks tend to follow LODs once initiated and how relatively subtle tissue modification can be sufficient to promote controlled fracture throughout development.

Successful blade proliferation in Nereocystis depends upon precisely controlled, directional tearing to generate new blades, making anisotropic biomechanical properties critical to growth and morphology. The low force required to tear and the weak tearing anisotropy in juvenile blades present a mechanical problem, as young blades are exposed to increasingly strong and variable hydrodynamic forces as they rise through the water column. Lines of dehiscence in juvenile thalli compensate for limited intrinsic tearing anisotropy by biasing crack propagation along a longitudinal axis, directing mechanical damage down the long axis of the blade. Lines of dehiscence occur most prominently near the pneumatocyst, where tissue loss would be the most consequential, and they diminish as the blade widens and structural consequences lessen toward the apex.

The degree of anisotropy in this species may relate to internal medullary anatomy, including the orientation of sieve filaments and phloem‐like trumpet hyphae (Nicholson, 1976). If medullary cell types are elongated primarily along the long axis of the blade for bulk transport of mannitol photosynthate (Nicholson, 1976; Nicholson & Briggs, 1972), tearing across the grain would require fracturing more cell walls than tearing along it would, thereby increasing crosswise tearing resistance. Because sieve elements elongate as the blade lengthens—far more than it widens—the degree of anisotropy may also depend on longitudinal growth rate. In young Nereocystis blades, the medulla is still developing, and early blade growth may be more nearly isometric, as the entire thallus increases in size, causing tearing strength to be more similar in all directions. A careful examination of medullary architecture across developmental stages combined with direct mechanical testing of blades would clarify how internal anatomy contributes to anisotropic tearing properties and how LODs superimpose an additional directional bias to guide crack propagation. Although we focused on Nereocystis, anisotropic tearing properties are likely common and ecologically important in kelp (see Figure S2 for a brief survey of anisotropic tearing properties in other kelp species).

The probability of crack propagation increases with initial crack length, indicating that once a split begins to extend, it becomes progressively more likely to continue. This situation suggests that blade division in Nereocystis operates partly as a positive feedback loop. We observed no evidence of rapid healing or morphological changes at crack apices (e.g., a ripstop mechanism—Padilla, 1993—or substantial crack‐tip rounding—Biedka et al., 1987; Denny et al., 1989; DeWreede et al., 1992), which would otherwise dissipate stress or inhibit further extension. Instead, extending cracks accumulate stress at their apices, making continued propagation increasingly probable. Although LODs bias crack propagation along the blade's long axis, they do not rigidly dictate where or how a crack will propagate. Cracks are more likely to follow the weakened line, but their propagation still varies with the application of external forces. Thus, LODs function more like the weakened crease in a folded or perforated sheet of paper, directing crack propagation without confining it. This aligns with the observed variation in crack propagation paths, which sometimes stray from the LODs.

Hydrodynamic forces play central roles in driving split propagation in Nereocystis. Although blade flapping is known to have a significant effect on drag (Koehl & Alberte, 1988), its consequences for tearing mechanics have never been investigated. The great flexibility of Nereocystis blades would allow the tissue on either side of an extending split to move independently, and we speculate that when two halves of a splitting blade become out of phase, mode‐III (tearing) tensile stresses may be imposed at the crack tip, promoting extension (Figure 9, and see Video S1). This interpretation would be consistent with the statistical results: Neither flow speed nor blade dimensions was a significant predictor of crack propagation, whereas initial crack length was strongly predictive. Thus, the potential for independent motion on the two sides of the split—rather than bulk hydrodynamic loading—likely drives propagation. Collisions with neighboring blades and rapid reorientation in turbulent flow may similarly bend blades and aid in crack propagation.

Conceptual illustration of a flapping‐related mechanism of crack propagation. During blade flapping, tissue on either side of a crack can move independently and when these motions become out of phase, mode‐III tensile stress would be applied at the crack tip and promote propagation.

Together, these results outline a biomechanical strategy for controlling damage during morphogenesis in Nereocystis. Lines of dehiscence compensate for the weakly anisotropic tearing properties of juvenile blades, ensuring that initial divisions follow a longitudinal path. As blades mature and develop, strong intrinsic anisotropy emerges, and this inherent mechanical bias increasingly governs the direction of crack propagation, making clean longitudinal divisions a reliable outcome even after LODs diminish. In a stressful hydrodynamic environment, LODs promote crack propagation and allow Nereocystis to regulate blade proliferation in flow. Lines of dehiscence likely underlie broader patterns of flow‐dependent morphological plasticity, as Nereocystis thalli in a faster flow tear more often and generate more blades than thalli in a slower flow (Breitkreutz, 2026). Thus, LODs and tearing anisotropy provide a mechanism by which local tissue modification interacts with external flow environments to shape growth, allowing Nereocystis to avoid damage and produce subtidal canopies across various flow environments.
