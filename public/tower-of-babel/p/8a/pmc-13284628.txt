Effect of Interval Between Neoadjuvant Immunochemotherapy and Radical Resection of Non‐Small Cell Lung Cancer: A Multicenter, Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Jiayi Wang, Pingping Song, Yong Ge, Haitang Yang, Shuyuan Li, Ran Ma, Tianyue Ma, Shoujie Feng, Cheng Zhang, Teng Sun, Feng Yao, Jun Yi, Hao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRadical surgery following neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT) has emerged as a critical therapeutic strategy in the standardized management of locally advanced non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Robust evidence on the optimal time interval between NICT and surgery is lacking. Patients were divided into three groups based on the interval between the completion of NICT and surgery: short interval group (SIG; < 4 weeks), intermediate interval group (IIG; 4–6 weeks), and long interval group (LIG; > 6 weeks). Inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) and weighted regression analysis were used to balance the baseline characteristics and compare the outcomes. 205 out of 335 screened patients met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled. When compared with the IIG, the SIG (OR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.06–0.38; p < 0.001) was significantly correlated with lower pCR rate and the DFS was worse in the LIG (HR, 2.35; 95% CI, 1.15–4.80; p = 0.019). Subgroup analysis revealed that, in patients with a poor tumor regression response, LIG was significantly associated with poorer DFS (HR, 5.15; 95% CI, 2.27–11.71; p < 0.001) and OS (HR, 4.52; 95% CI, 1.48–13.79; p = 0.008) when compared with the IIG. Similarly, in patients with non‐pCR, LIG was significantly associated with poorer DFS (HR, 3.25; 95% CI, 1.39–7.61; p = 0.006) and OS (HR, 2.72; 95% CI, 1.00–7.38; p = 0.049). Surgery performed within 4–6 weeks after the completion of NICT is associated with improved pCR and DFS. Emphasis should be placed on early surgical treatment after NICT, particularly in patients who have poor or no response to NICT.

Introduction

Lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer and the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1]. Non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancers [2]. Radical surgery is the primary curative treatment for resectable NSCLC [3], however, some patients miss the optimal window for surgery at the time of diagnosis, and 30% to 55% of those undergoing radical surgery still experience recurrence [4]. This recurrence is thought to be associated with “residual tumor cells” or “tumor micrometastases.” [5, 6] The clinical use of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has been a revolutionary milestone that has changed this status quo, showing promise in improving outcomes for some patients, both as monotherapy and in combination with other treatments [7, 8]. Neoadjuvant therapy offers benefits over adjuvant immunotherapy, such as tumor downstaging and the earlier targeting of micrometastatic lesions, potentially resulting in resections that were previously considered unattainable or too extensive, thereby improving surgical outcomes and prolonging postoperative survival [9, 10, 11].

Although numerous clinical studies on perioperative immunotherapy have established that neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (NICT) enhances both short‐term pathological response and long‐term survival rates in NSCLC [12], the optimal time interval between NICT and surgery to maximize patient survival benefits remains a contentious issue. A study by Liu et al., based on a mouse cancer model, demonstrated that an optimal time window (4–5 days) between the initiation of neoadjuvant immunotherapy and surgical tumor removal significantly influenced long‐term survival in mice, shortening or lengthening this interval resulting in a complete loss of long‐term survival [13]. This finding suggests that the timing of surgery can significantly impact the overall treatment outcome. Previous phase III clinical trials lacked a uniform and rigorous definition of this interval, and surgery was typically scheduled within 3–7 weeks after the completion of neoadjuvant therapy or within 20 weeks after the initiation of the first neoadjuvant therapy [14, 15, 16]. Currently, there is still a lack of high‐quality research evidence on this question [17, 18]. Gao et al. conducted a study on patients with stage IIIA‐N2 NSCLC who underwent surgery following neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy, and only broadly concluded that overall survival might be significantly reduced in patients who underwent surgery more than 6 weeks after neoadjuvant therapy [19]. Chen et al. investigated the relationship between the surgical interval after NICT and perioperative as well as pathological outcomes in NSCLC patients. They found similar surgical safety and pathological results across different interval groups; however, the study was limited by its single‐center design and lack of adjustment for confounders such as age and clinical stage [20]. Yang et al. reported in a study of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) that delayed surgery increased the risk of recurrence and metastasis in patients with locally advanced disease, confirming that prolonged intervals (> 8 weeks) between NICT and surgery were associated with poorer long‐term survival [21]. Nevertheless, because the tumor immune microenvironment and response mechanisms to NICT differ significantly between NSCLC and ESCC, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to NSCLC.

Consequently, the aim of this retrospective study was to investigate the impact of the interval on clinical outcomes in surgical patients following NICT, with the objective of determining the optimal time between the completion of NICT and surgery that maximizes patient benefit and minimizes potential risks.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Participants

We conducted a retrospective analysis of medical records from four key regional medical centers in China and selected patients with NSCLC who underwent surgery after completing NICT under the guidance of the multidisciplinary teams (MDT) at these centers from January 2020 to December 2022. Before and after neoadjuvant therapy, all patients underwent standard preoperative staging with at least one positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) examination for a systematic assessment. All patients underwent restaging evaluation after completion of NICT with either CT or PET/CT. Primary tumor resection and lymph node dissection were performed according to institutional standards. Exclusion criteria included age under 18 years, failure to complete a cycle of neoadjuvant therapy for any reason, incomplete or inaccurate information on treatment dates, history of prior malignancy, and comorbidities with other serious life‐threatening organic lesions. This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [22].

NICT And Surgical Resection

The indications for NICT and specific regimen selection were determined by the MDT at the medical center based on the clinical condition of patients. Typically, patients underwent radical surgical resection after completing 2–3 cycles of NICT. Surgical options included: (1) lobectomy; (2) sleeve lobectomy; and (3) total pneumonectomy. All patients received systematic hilar and mediastinal lymph node dissection. Conversion was defined as a thoracoscopic procedure that required conversion to open surgery.

Groups According to Timing From the End of NICT to Surgery

The time‐to‐operation variable was defined as the number of days from the last day of NICT to surgical resection. In this study, three waiting interval groups were defined based on the time‐to‐operation: short interval group (SIG, < 4 weeks), intermediate interval group (IIG, 4–6 weeks), and long interval group (LIG, > 6 weeks). To further explore the relationship between the time interval and prognosis, two pairs of subgroups were created based on pathological reaction and clinical efficacy evaluation: pCR versus non‐pCR and good clinical response versus poor clinical response.

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

The primary endpoints of the study were disease‐free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS). Secondary endpoints included the rates of pathologic complete remission (pCR), primary tumor regression, lymph node regression, and perioperative events. Evaluation of efficacy adhered to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) criteria (version 1.1) [23]. The general definitions discussed in this manuscript are as follows: DFS, time from completion of primary treatment to disease recurrence or death from any cause; OS, time from completion of primary treatment to death from any cause; pCR, 0% residual viable tumor cells in the primary and sampled lymph nodes. Data collection was conducted through a combination of chart reviews and telephone follow‐ups, with the most recent follow‐up occurring in August 2024. Collected patient data included gender, age, body mass index, neoadjuvant therapy regimen and cycle, time to completion of neoadjuvant therapy, CT evaluation results before and after neoadjuvant therapy, time to surgery, pathological results, surgical outcomes, and follow‐up findings.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical variables were presented as absolute frequencies and percentages. Inverse probability treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to allow for an unbiased comparison. Three interval groups were compared and a multinomial logistic regression model was applied to estimate the propensity score with its subsequent transformation to IPTW. This model included available demographic and clinical variables, including age, gender, body mass index, smoking status, tumor classification and clinical stage, type of immunotherapy and chemotherapy, neoadjuvant treatment cycle and adjuvant therapy. The intermediate interval was set as a reference category because it is the most commonly used in clinical practice. Prognostic estimation of response was performed by calculating the odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI for the outcome variables. The association of waiting intervals with survival outcomes was estimated by calculating the hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI using Cox proportional hazards models. Individual IPTW calculations were used as weights for all the described models. Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 3.4.3) and SPSS (version 26.0).

Results

A total of 335 patients with NSCLC were retrospectively analyzed. Of these, 205 patients (median [IQR] age, 62.0 [56.0–68.0] years; 174 males [84.9%]; 31 females [15.1%]) were included in the final analysis (Figure S1). The SIG, IIG, and LIG included 59 patients (28.8%), 108 patients (52.7%), and 38 patients (18.5%), respectively. The median (IQR) surgical intervals were as follows: 3.4 weeks (3.1–3.8 weeks), 4.7 weeks (4.4–5.3 weeks), and 6.9 weeks (6.3–7.7 weeks). The majority of patients were staged as clinical stage III before NICT (77.7% [159 of 205]), with proportions of 74.6% in the SIG, 75.9% in the IIG, and 86.8% in the LIG. All patients underwent R0 resection and adjuvant chemotherapy, but only some received adjuvant immune maintenance therapy. We used the IPTW to match for age, sex, BMI, smoking history, histologic type, clinical stage, immunotherapy type, chemotherapy type, neoadjuvant therapy cycle, and options for immune maintenance therapy. Then weighted multiple regression analysis was conducted for surgical outcomes, clinical efficacy, pathological response, and long‐term survival (Table 2 and S1). In the entire cohort, the SIG (OR, 3.89; 95% CI, 1.76–8.57; p < 0.001) and LIG (OR, 4.71; 95% CI, 1.84–11.97; p = 0.001) had similar operative times, which were significantly higher than those in the IIG. Additionally, the SIG was associated with more intraoperative bleeding (OR, 5.38; 95% CI, 2.40–12.07; p < 0.001) and longer drainage tube indwelling time (OR, 2.73; 95% CI, 1.28–5.83; p = 0.010) compared to the IIG. No significant associations were demonstrated between the time interval and pleural adhesions, thoracotomy or postoperative complications among the three groups, but patients in the LIG (OR, 13.0; 95% CI, 2.89–58.02; p < 0.001) had a higher rate of conversion to thoracotomy in comparison to the IIG.

pCR was obtained in 69 patients (33.7%) (SIG, 18.6% vs. IIG, 44.4% vs. LIG, 26.3%). There were no patients with complete response (CR) in the SIG and LIG, and no differences were observed in the proportion of patients with partial response (PR) among the three groups. However, a short interval was significantly associated with a higher SD rate (OR, 2.47; 95% CI, 1.01–6.04; p = 0.047) and a lower pCR rate (OR, 0.15; 95% CI, 0.06–0.38; p < 0.001).

The median follow‐up period was 32 months (95% CI, 30.3–33.7). During this period, 55 patients relapsed and 39 patients died. Remarkably, the LIG was associated with lower DFS (HR, 2.35; 95% CI, 1.15–4.80; p = 0.019) (Figure 1). No significant association of the interval with OS was observed (SIG: HR, 1.60; 95% CI, 0.71–3.60; p = 0.255; LIG: HR, 2.21; 95% CI, 0.93–5.25; p = 0.072). Subgroup analysis revealed that among patients with a good response to tumor regression (CR/PR), neither DFS (Figure 2) nor OS (Figure 3) was associated with the time interval. In contrast, in patients with a poor response to tumor regression (SD/PD), the LIG was associated with poorer DFS (HR, 2.94; 95% CI, 1.37–6.28; p = 0.005) and OS (HR, 3.14; 95% CI, 1.29–7.65; p = 0.012), with a median DFS of 18 months. Discussion

In the retrospective study, we utilized a multi‐institutional cohort to investigate the effect of interval between neoadjuvant therapy and radical resection of NSCLC. Our findings indicate that patients undergoing surgery either too early or too late are more likely to experience a more complex intraoperative scenario. Furthermore, undergoing surgery within 4–6 weeks is associated with a higher pCR, whereas a delay of more than 6 weeks between the completion of NICT and surgical resection has an adverse impact on DFS.

Our research reveals that the interval of surgery affects the complexity of surgery, but it does not significantly affect the feasibility and safety of surgery—consistent with the conclusions reported by Yang et al. in the field of esophageal cancer [21]. Although a longer surgical interval may permit partial resolution of tissue inflammation and edema, thereby potentially reducing bleeding risk and certain procedural difficulties. This adverse impact is likely attributable to an NICT‐induced inflammatory response that produces localized tissue edema, increased tissue brittleness, and subsequent fibrosis, ultimately resulting in a hardening of tissue [24, 25]. Additionally, regression of the lymph nodes and primary tumor, which is accompanied by dense adhesions and fibrosis at the hilar following the lymph nodes and tumor shrinkage, may further contribute to these challenges [26, 27]. Consequently, these factors elevate intraoperative complications and overall surgical complexity, significantly increasing the rate of conversion to thoracotomy and necessitating enhanced surgical expertise and prolonged operative times [19, 28].

Previous studies have demonstrated that pCR serves as an early surrogate endpoint that can be evaluated in clinical trials of neoadjuvant ICIs [14, 15, 29]. We hypothesized that delaying surgery would enhance patients' pCR, based on the fact that immunotherapy elicits more diverse and durable tumor response patterns compared to chemotherapy. However, in our cohort study, the hypothesis of an increased pCR rate with a greater than 6‐week delay in surgery was not met, in agreement with previously reported data [20, 21]. Nevertheless, a trend toward a lower probability of obtaining pCR in patients who waited less than 4 weeks should be noted. Although the precise mechanism by which time intervals affect pCR formation after NICT remains to be fully clarified, our results align with the fundamental biological principles of neoadjuvant immunotherapy. Initially, ICIs lift the inhibitory effects on the immune system, directly activating tumor‐specific cytotoxic T cells to recognize and attack tumor cells [30]. Subsequently, they promote dendritic cells to enhance tumor‐antigen presentation, which usually takes sufficient time to be generated after administration of ICIs [13, 30].

Current data on the relationship between the timing to surgery following NICT and long‐term survival outcomes in NSCLC remain limited. In our survival analysis, a waiting period of 4–6 weeks was associated with the highest rate of pCR and improved DFS. Although pCR is generally linked to a better long‐term prognosis, the observed improvements in lymph node downstaging and pCR rate were not accompanied by improved DFS and OS in the LIG compared to the SIG. This discrepancy suggests that while achieving pCR is a favorable indicator, it may not be the direct cause of improved long‐term outcomes. The well‐documented poor correlation between surrogate endpoints and survival, which reinforces our argument, remains a significant challenge in oncologic research [31]. The most significant impact of NICT is its ability to inhibit micrometastases [32], which persists over an extended period and substantially reduces the risk of disease recurrence—even beyond 5 years [33]. This indicates that the impact is an absolute reduction in metastatic disease rather than merely a delayed occurrence of metastases. Our findings further suggest that extending the surgical interval may negate the potential benefits of reducing disease recurrence and metastasis. Subgroup analysis revealed that delays in surgery did not affect DFS or OS in patients achieving CR/PR or pCR, whereas within SD/PD and non‐pCR groups, prolonged intervals were significantly associated with worse DFS and OS. These results may indicate that a favorable tumor biological profile is the most critical determinant of a good prognosis, whereas in patients with an unfavorable tumor biology, delays in surgery further worsen outcomes.

NICT represents a multistage dynamic process of “chemotherapy‐induced sensitization – immune activation–synergistic clearance.” Its core lies in the synergistic action between the “immunomodulatory effect” of chemotherapy and the “immune checkpoint blockade effect” of ICIs, which together shift tumors from an “immune‐tolerant state” to an “immune‐attack state,” ultimately achieving specific elimination of tumor cells and the maintenance of long‐term immune memory. This process can substantially improve pCR rate and long‐term prognosis [34]. In the first stage, chemotherapy primes the immune microenvironment, thereby creating the conditions for subsequent immune activation. In fact, chemotherapy exerts a dual effect on immune function: on the one hand, it can enhance subsequent antitumor immunity by modulating the tumor microenvironment (TME) and promoting antigen presentation; on the other hand, due to its nonspecific cytotoxicity, chemotherapy may also partially suppress the immune system [35]. Balancing these “suppressive” and “sensitizing” effects requires an appropriate interval. In the second stage, ICIs relieve immune suppression and activate tumor‐specific effector T cells. Dendritic cells (DCs) present tumor antigens, and activated tumor‐specific cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) proliferate extensively and migrate into the TME—a process that also requires sufficient time [36]. Although, in theory, extending the interval may allow more time for immune responses to develop, our study found that the pCR rate in the long‐interval group was not higher than that of the intermediate‐interval group, but slightly lower. This phenomenon may be related to “reactivation of tumor immune escape.” With longer waiting times, residual tumor cells may reconstruct an immunosuppressive microenvironment by downregulating tumor antigen expression or upregulating PD‐L1, thereby weakening antitumor immunity. Moreover, beyond 6 weeks after NICT, the immune response may enter an “exhaustion phase,” during which continuously stimulated CTLs become functionally impaired due to persistent antigen exposure, reducing their ability to eliminate residual tumor cells [37, 38]. Consequently, the pCR rate does not increase with extended intervals.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective nature, it is subject to the inherent risk of confounding and selection bias. Although our study utilized real‐world data, with only 11 out of those who completed neoadjuvant therapy being unable to undergo surgery, thus minimizing bias, the grouping strategy may have introduced potential selection bias and confounding variables during initial treatment assignment. Second, the generalizability of our findings to a broad spectrum of NSCLC is limited, as the majority of the patients included were diagnosed at stage III. Finally, small sample sizes and extreme values arising from subgroup analyses may compromise the precision of the post‐IPTW estimates and undermine the robustness of certain findings. Consequently, further prospective studies or larger sample studies are necessary to verify our findings.

In conclusion, the timing of surgery following NICT significantly influences patient outcomes and a surgical interval of 4–6 weeks is considered optimal for the vast majority of patients. Our results indicate that appropriately extended surgical intervals are advantageous for achieving higher pCR rates, with intervals of 4–6 weeks showing the most benefit, whereas extending the interval beyond 6 weeks diminishes this benefit and adversely affects DFS and OS. Although extremely short or long intervals may complicate the surgical procedure, they do not compromise the surgical feasibility or safety. Additionally, the effect of surgical timing on prognosis is dependent on treatment response. In patients exhibiting poor response to NICT, delayed surgical intervention is associated with worse DFS and OS. Clinicians should develop personalized treatment plans that account for the patient's physical condition, treatment response, and other relevant factors, thereby further improving prognosis.
