Craniofacial surgery for removing tumors in advanced stages that invade the anterior skull base
Authors: Víctor Correa-Correa, José Francisco Gallegos-Hernández, José Alberto Abrego-Vásquez, Alma Lilia Ortiz-Maldonado, Raquel Sánchez-Santiago, Gerardo Gabriel Minauro-Muñoz, José Héctor Reynaldo Arias-Ceballos, Eulalio Vivar-Acevedo, Eulalio Jiménez-González, Emanuel Alejandro Suárez-Ramírez, Jesús Eduardo Falcón-Molina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Craniofacial surgery provides optimal exposure of the sinonasal cavity, epidural space, and intracranial anterior fossa compartments, facilitating oncologically en bloc radical resection from an oncological standpoint. This study represents the largest series of patients undergoing craniofacial surgery in Latin America published to date. This study aims to characterize the clinical profiles of patients undergoing craniofacial surgery and to describe the technical nuances of the combined subfrontal–transbasal and midfacial degloving approach, which we consider the most appropriate surgical strategy for patients with anterior skull base neoplasms at our center.Methods: Between 2013 and 2025, 228 consecutive patients underwent craniofacial surgery at the Hospital de Especialidades del Centro Médico Nacional Siglo XXI, Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social, Mexico City, Mexico. Data on clinical presentation, tumor histology, type of approach, and surgical outcomes were retrospectively investigated in institutional databases.Results: There were 128 males (56.1%), and the mean age was 50.8 years. The most frequent symptoms were nasal obstruction (60%), epistaxis/rhinorrhea (54.3%), cephalea/facial pain (41.2%), and olfactory disturbances (27.1%). The most frequent neoplasms were epidermoid carcinoma (19.2%), esthesioneuroblastoma (13.5%), and inverted papilloma (8.3%). The most frequently performed cranial approach was the transbasal subfrontal approach (67.9%). A total of 32.4% of patients experience progression/recurrence of their disease. Infection was the most frequent complication (4.8%). Neoadjuvant therapy was administered at 12.7%. Adjuvant therapy, such as radiotherapy (RT), was administered at 36.8%, chemotherapy (CMT) at 5.2%, and RT + CMT at 24.5%. The mean follow-up time was 51.2 months. The immediate postoperative survival rate was 100%; at 6 months, it was 94.7%; at 1 year, it was 84.2%; and at 3 years, it was 51.7%. There was no perioperative mortality.Conclusion: The combination of a subfrontal-transbasal plus midfacial degloving approach is preferable for neoplasms located in the anterior skull base because they are generally very large at the time of diagnosis, mainly for the management of malignancies in advanced stages. Histology, localization, extension, relation with neurovascular structures, extra or intracranial, and extra or intradural invasion are factors to consider in the selection of the surgical approach. Multidisciplinary management is the key to getting successful results in the treatment of tumors in these areas.

INTRODUCTION

Craniofacial surgery is a combination of cranial and facial approaches, regardless of the technique or approach used, for the management of tumors involving the skull base in its extracranial or intracranial compartments, including sinonasal, orbital, or skull base benign or malignant neoplasms. Tumors located in the ventral cranial base were considered inoperable due to involvement of critical neurovascular elements. The various approaches used in anterior craniofacial surgery, as known today, are the result of contributions from different surgeons since the second half of the 20th century, with the first reports of tumor resections.[33] The first formal craniofacial approach for the management of cancer was described by Smith in 1954 to frontal sinus cancer, after by Malecki in 1959 to ethmoidal sinus cancer, and later by Ketcham in 1963, who achieved the cure of patients in a case series of sinonasal tumors and reported their 5-year survival.[18,23,36] Casson PR first described the midfacial degloving approach in 1974, which was subsequently modified by Nishikawa et al.[6,27] In 1990,Janecka and Sekhar reported extended combined transcranial and transfacial approaches for the resection of these tumors.[16] Since then, in the past 30 years, with the advent of endoscopic surgery, the works of Jho, Carrau, Prevedello, Snyderman, Kassam, De Divitiis, Cavallo, Frank, Gardner, Fernandez, and Pasquini have expanded the use of endoscopy for benign and malignant neoplasms located in the anterior cranial fossa and sinonasal tumors involving the skull base, providing ample resections with smaller approaches, good local control, and few complications.[24,37] Despite these advancements, complex lesions remain a clinical challenge due to the proximity of vital structures and the elevated morbidity associated with these surgeries. Craniofacial surgery provides optimal exposure of the sinonasal cavity, epidural space, and intracranial anterior fossa compartments, facilitating oncologically en bloc radical resection.[2] The most common and useful combination for removing large tumors in this region, particularly in advanced lesions that cannot be removed endoscopically, is the subfrontal-transbasal approach combined with a transfacial approach.[31]

In the present work, we aim to review the role of craniofacial surgery for removing tumors in advanced stages that invade the anterior skull base. To our knowledge, this series represents the largest number of consecutive cases operated on using a craniofacial approach in Latin America.[12,25,28] We will focus on describing the combined subfrontal-transbasal plus midfacial degloving approach because it provides safe and wide exposure of the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and anterior fossa. Furthermore, we propose an algorithm that considers the location, dural involvement, and type of tumor histology for the selection of the anterior cranial approach. Ideally, this combined approach should be performed by two surgical teams working simultaneously: the neurosurgeon from the anterior cranial fossa and the head and neck surgeon from the facial region.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population and clinical parameters

We performed a retrospective review of our clinical database from January 2013 until September 2025. We reviewed the clinical documentation files and neuropathological records of adult patients who underwent craniofacial surgery at the Hospital de Especialidades del Centro Médico Nacional Siglo XXI, Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social, Mexico City, Mexico. Cranial approaches were performed by the Neurosurgery Department of the Specialties Hospital, and the Head and Neck Surgery Department of the Oncology Hospital performed facial approaches. In cases involving the orbit or ears, the procedures were performed by the Ophthalmology Department of the Oncology Hospital and the Otorhinolaryngology Department of the Specialties Hospital, respectively. We analyze clinical patient files for age, gender, symptoms, histopathological entity, type of approach performed, postoperative complication rate, and neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy employed. We defined disease status as tumor progression/recurrence at any time, based on cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at follow-up. Follow-up time was recorded from the date of surgery until September 2025 or until the date of the patient’s death. The survival rate was evaluated at 6 months, 1 year, and 3 years.

Surgical technique: Combined subfrontal-transbasal plus midfacial degloving approach

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. It should be noted that tracheotomies before tumor removal are not routinely performed unless there is extensive involvement of the pharynx or palate, or if microvascular reconstruction with a free flap is planned. Constant communication between the surgeon and the anesthesiologist is crucial. Both arms were placed at the patient’s sides. Perioperative antibiotics, such as a third-generation cephalosporin, were administered 30 min before the incision. We do not routinely perform tarsorrhaphy before the procedure.

Positioning

To perform the approach, the patient is placed in the supine position with the head fixed in a neutral position using a three-pin head holder, slightly extended to allow the frontal lobe to separate from the roof of the orbits. If a standard transbasal approach (without frontoorbital osteotomy) is considered, it is sufficient to place the head on a foam donut or horseshoe and secure it with adhesive tape.

Incision and dissection of the superficial planes

The planned incision is marked, and then, 1% lidocaine (1:100,000) with epinephrine is injected. The skin is then prepared in the usual sterile fashion. A bicoronal incision is made from zygoma to zygoma, passing through the vertex of the skull [Figure 1a]. The superficial temporal arteries are preserved to maintain the irrigation of the flap. A subgaleal dissection of the skin flap is performed, extending posteriorly to the parietal bone, with dissection in the supraperiosteal plane to preserve the pericranium, which is incised at the vertex with a Bovie cautery. Subperiosteal dissection of the periosteum is continued with a wide periosteal elevator up to the level of the supraciliary arches, creating a long-vascularized graft for reconstruction of the skull base. This flap is covered with gauze soaked in bacitracin and retracted with blunt hooks [Figure 1b].

(c) Dissection of the temporal muscles to expose the bone for key burr holes (white arrows) and bifrontal craniotomy cuts (dotted black arrows). (d) Surgical view of an intradural approach without fronto-orbital osteotomy. (e and f) If an extended transbasal approach is required, fronto-orbital osteotomy is performed. An interfascial dissection is performed to preserve the frontal branch of the facial nerve. The incision begins on the superior temporal line, 2 cm from the orbital rim. Subsequently, a semicurved incision with ventral concavity is directed toward the posterior root of the zygomatic arch. The temporal muscles are dissected at their anterior end to expose the bone for key burr holes in the lateral facet of the frontal bone [Figure 1c].

If an extended or extensive trans-basal approach is considered, the supraorbital ridges are dissected in a subperiosteal dissection to free the supraorbital nerve, taking special care of the supraorbital and supratrochlear neurovascular bundles. The supraorbital nerves and vessels were separated from the foramen or notch, to be mobilized with the pericranial flap, and the lateral and medial walls of the orbits and the nasofrontal suture were then exposed.

Craniotomy, osteotomy, and drilling

When a standard subfrontal-transbasal approach is considered, a traditional bilateral frontal craniotomy is usually performed as close to the skull base as possible to reduce frontal lobe retraction. Two burr holes are placed in the MacCarty’s keyhole and one above the sagittal sinus at 6 or 8 cm from the glabella, depending on the level of the patient’s hairline or the extension of the tumor in case of a large intracranial extension. If the frontal sinus is opened, the mucosa must be removed and the wall of the sinus cranialized. We consider it not necessary to add a burr hole over the frontal sinus to facilitate the cut of the posterior wall of the sinus; in this case, the external cortical wall cutting can be connected by inclining the drill at this point. The first cut is ventral, as basal as possible, in a lateral-medial way. Next, the dorsal cut, from medial to lateral – to avoid injury to the dural sinus – is left until last, so that the frontal bone can be removed quickly in case of injury to the sinus [Figure 1c].

If an extended or extensive transbasal approach is required, a caudal extension of the traditional frontal craniotomy is added; this includes removal of the orbital roof and nasal bones before ethmoidectomy and sphenoidotomy. In comparison with the previously described approach, this one provides a wider surgical corridor in the rostral and caudal part of the exposure by the removal of the orbital roofs. The craniotomy is performed in two pieces: first by removing the described frontal bone flap, and second by removing the orbital bar. A wine-glass osteotomy is performed, first making the posterior cut from the foramen cecum laterally to the lateral cut, which is located just medial to the supraorbital foramen, to prevent injury to the supraorbital neurovascular bundle, using a reciprocating saw. Finally, the nasofrontal suture is cut toward the foramen cecum and crest of Galli with an oscillating saw. To complete the bar detachment, a chisel and hammer can be used [Figure 1d and e]. In the extensive approach, full orbital bars are removed, and we make a hole in the midline of the orbital roof at the junction of the middle and posterior third to facilitate the posterior cut from the foramen caecum laterally toward the frontozygomatic suture, passing by MacCarty’s keyhole. Finally, the nasofrontal suture is cut toward the foramen cecum and crest of Galli, in the same manner described previously. The approach is completed by cranializing the rest of the frontal sinus by resecting its posterior wall and removing the entire ethmoidal complex, creating an osteotomy in the shape of an inverted Christmas bell that exposes both papillary laminae.

In esthesioneuroblastoma or malignant tumors with intracranial invasion, we perform an intradural approach, sectioning both olfactory nerves at the junction of the olfactory bulb and the olfactory tract, or 1 cm posterior to the tumor limit, and cutting the dura mater in a “U” shape whose lateral limit of the durotomy is at the level of the frontoethmoidal suture or 1 cm lateral to the limits of the tumor. In this way, we can separate the olfactory unit invaded and the tumor before the ethmoidal osteotomy to complete an en bloc tumor resection [Figure 1f-h].

In benign tumors or those without intracranial invasion, when an open approach is considered, we perform an extradural dissection of the anterior fossa and skeletonization of the crista galli to cut one or both olfactory fibers extradurally toward the sphenoidal plane, making an extradural transbasal approach to prevent postoperative fistula and infection. It is important to perform a complete skeletonization of the frontal sinus up to its lateral limit, which sometimes literally covers the orbits, to remove all the mucosa infiltrated by the tumor. Drilling begins on the roof of the frontal sinus, 1 cm lateral to the foramen cecum, so that once the frontal sinus has been opened, using a dissector, the medial wall of the orbit is identified, so we can continue drilling until the medial orbital wall is fully exposed, which corresponds backward with the lamina papyracea which in turn is the lateral wall of the ethmoidal complex. These are drilled from front to back and from the lamina papyracea on the lateral side to the lateral wall of the olfactory sulcus medially. Now, we can dissect the olfactory nerve medially from the crista galli and the upper end of the perpendicular lamina of the ethmoid. Next, the olfactory nerve is cut. To continue the peeling, the anterior rough portion is dissected until the smooth portion of the sphenoid planum is exposed, to facilitate the closure of the dura at the time of reconstruction.

Exposure and tumor resection

When the approach is finished, it is now possible to have tumor exposure simultaneously from above, through the anterior cranial fossa, and from below, through the facial approach, usually the midfacial degloving approach (vide infra) [Figure 1i]. The neurosurgical team begins working on the tumor from its superior surface, identifying and protecting neurovascular structures from the start, while leaving the inferior portions for resection through the facial approach by the head and neck surgeons. Tumor resection must be performed while avoiding injury to the optic nerve and cavernous sinus structures, working from above, below, and inside these structures. The exposure provided by this approach allows the tumor to be removed, even en bloc. Certain anatomical details should be carefully kept in mind during tumor resection. The lamina papyracea occupies most of the lateral limit of the nasal cavity; however, it is also usually found to be displaced sideward by the tumor. Sometimes, it can be destroyed by the tumor; in this case, finding the dissection plane between the tumor and the periorbita is crucial. When the periorbita or internal muscle is infiltrated by a malignant tumor, exenteration should be indicated. It is important to keep in mind that the Onodi cell is located superior or lateral to the sphenoid sinus and can contain the optic nerve or the carotid artery. It serves as a reference for preserving the lateral wall of the optic canal. The main vascular structures of the anterior cranial fossa are the anterior and posterior ethmoidal arteries, which arise beneath the superior oblique muscle and pass through the anterior and posterior ethmoidal canals to reach the dura mater beside the cribriform plate, and run transversely from the medial wall of the orbit to the nasal fossa. To achieve early proximal control of these arteries, the anterior ethmoidal artery, it is usually identified crossing near the anterior edge of the cribriform plate, at the junction of the posterior wall of frontal sinus and anterior ethmoidal cells; posterior ethmoidal artery crosses near the posterior edge of the cribriform plate a few millimeters anterior to the orbital end of the optic canal, 10–12 mm behind to anterior ethmoidal artery. If necessary, a sphenoidectomy can be made, and the tumor can be dissected from both cavernous sinuses. The tumor can be dissected from the clivus in a caudoventral direction until the craniovertebral union, or it can be removed dorsally, on the sphenoid plane, the sellar region, and the interhemispheric fissure. The infiltrated dura must be removed and repaired. Finally, the cerebrum infiltrated by the tumor must be resected.

Midfacial degloving approach

The nasal septum, columella, dorsum, alar base, gingivobuccal sulcus, and infraorbital regions are infiltrated with a 1% lidocaine solution containing epinephrine at a 1:100,000 concentration. A sublabial incision is made with electrocautery, extending from canine to canine. Bilateral wide subperiosteal dissection is then carried out along the anterior wall of the maxilla and the piriform aperture up to the level of the infraorbital foramen. Concurrently, mucoperiosteal flaps are elevated bilaterally along the floor of the nasal cavity.

The anterior nasal spine and approximately 1 cm of the anterior maxillary crest are separated from the maxilla using a chisel. The septal cartilage and overlying mucosa are transected along the nasal septal base with heavy turbinate scissors, following the previous mucosal incisions. Depending on the extent of tumor invasion, either unilateral or bilateral anterior maxillary walls are removed.

For optimal exposure, a medial maxillectomy is performed as necessary, with preservation of the inferior orbital wall. The frontal process of the maxilla is removed, and the nasal septum is sectioned at its cartilaginous portion to achieve a complete en bloc resection of the nasal functional unit along with the tumor. Care must be taken to ensure that both maxillary antrums are completely cleared to improve oncological control and to avoid questionable false-positive images of recurrence during follow-up. In addition, this resection allows for wide exposure of the nasal cavities, paranasal sinuses, and nasopharynx. When required, the posterior wall of the maxillary antrum and the ascending process of the palatine bone are also removed to enhance visualization of the nasopharyngeal region. Depending on the surgical indication, a palatectomy, inferior maxillectomy, or total maxillectomy may be performed. After tumor resection, the anterior maxillary crest is repositioned anatomically. Once hemostasis of the nasal cavity is achieved, it is packed with Vaseline gauze for 5 days, which is then removed through one nostril before the patient’s discharge. The sublabial wound is closed with absorbable sutures, and the facial phase is considered complete.

Reconstruction and closure

Once the tumor has been removed, the next step is careful hemostasis and layered closure by the neurosurgery team. The floor of the skull base can be reconstructed with bone cement, titanium plates, or the internal cortex of the patient’s own bone, although this is not essential, and the fronto-orbital bar can be fixed with miniplates. The duraplasty can be completed using pericranium. Once the nasal cavity is packed (in case of sublabial approach), a piece of Gelfoam is used to occlude the skull base defect over the gauze. Then, the pericranial flap remaining is used to separate the cranial base from the nasal cavity (Bronson-Ray maneuver). In addition, the fibrin glue is spread to seal the cranial cavity.

Usually, the dissection is too big; therefore, placing a capillary drainage is recommended to prevent the formation of a hematoma in the surgical bed. The bony flap may also be fixed with miniplates, but wire or nonabsorbable suture may also be used. Finally, closure of the superficial planes and skin is done in the usual manner. If the reconstruction was difficult and there is doubt about hermetic closure or in patients previously radiated, it is mandatory to place a lumbar drainage to avoid cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage or infection.

RESULTS

Demographic and clinical features

A total of 228 patients who underwent craniofacial surgery were included in the study. There were 128 males (56.1%); the mean age was 50.8 years. The patients presented several symptoms. The nasal obstruction was the most frequent symptom, found in up to 60%. Many patients referred to epistaxis/rhinorrhea (54.3%), cephalea/facial pain (41.2%), and olfactory disturbances (27.1%). Other symptoms found less frequently were cognition, concentration/memory/cognition impairment (15.7%), facial deformity (10%), visual deficits (7%), motor disorder/dizziness/vertigo (4.3%), and diplopia (4.3%) [Table 1].

Tumor histology

The most frequent neoplasms were epidermoid carcinoma (19.2%), followed by esthesioneuroblastoma (13.5%), inverted papilloma (8.3%), melanoma (7.4%), sarcomas (7%), adenocarcinoma (7%), and adenoid cystic carcinoma (6.5%). Other less frequent neoplasms were schwannomas (5.2%), meningiomas (3.9%), nasoangiofibroma (3.9%), ameloblastoma (2.6%), solitary fibrous tumor (1.3%), and 13.5% fell into the other category for extremely rare neoplasms [Table 1].

Surgical approach

For the management of neoplasms involving the skull base, the most frequent approach used in our center was the cranial approach in 96.9% versus the endoscopic approach in 3%. The subfrontal transbasal approach corresponds to 67.9%, with the extradural approach being the most frequent (41.2%), followed by the intradural approach (26.7%). Other cranial approaches were less frequent: infratemporal transzygomatic (8.7%), pterional and variants (3.5%), oneand-a-half fronto-orbital (3%), posterior transpetrous (3%), lateral resection of temporal bone (2.1%), and anterior transpetrous (0.4%). Extremely rare approaches were included in the other category (7.8%) [Table 1].

Postoperative characteristics

There were some postsurgical complications. The most frequent were infection (4.8%) and cerebrospinal leak (3.9%). In the study, 3.9% of patients required some kind of reintervention. Regarding adjuvant therapy, there were different regimens: 36.8% of patients received RT alone, 24.5% received chemotherapy (CMT) plus RT, and 5.2% received CMT alone. Neoadjuvant therapy was administered in 12.7% of patients, primarily to reduce the initial tumor volume and simplify resection. A total of 33.3% of patients did not receive adjuvant therapy because there were no risk factors indicating its use [Table 2].

DISCUSSION

Tumor spectrum of the anterior skull base

There are many tumors involving the anterior fossa (extra or intracranial invasion): squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, esthesioneuroblastoma, inverted papilloma, adenoid cystic carcinoma, meningioma, nasoangiofibroma, melanoma, ameloblastoma, and solitary fibrous tumor. A smaller proportion of tumors originate within the anatomic limits of these areas, including schwannomas, neurofibromas, sarcomas, solitary fibrous tumors, giant cell tumors, chordomas, chondrosarcomas, lipomas, hemangiomas, and nasopharyngeal fibromas. Most of them are the result of a secondary invasion of neighboring areas, originating from the anterior cranial fossa, paranasal sinuses, nasal cavity, pterygopalatine fossa, nasopharynx, or the clivus.[11,12] In this series, we found epidermoid carcinoma, esthesioneuroblastoma, and inverted papilloma as the most frequent neoplasms requiring craniofacial surgery.

Diagnosis workup and histopathological evaluation

Accurate preoperative diagnosis is essential for appropriate management. In most cases, tissue biopsy is required to establish a definitive histopathological diagnosis before surgery. Biopsy techniques include fine-needle aspiration cytology, which is not frequently performed in the sinonasal region because the nasal cavity, paranasal sinuses, and nasopharynx are not a favored site for fine-needle aspiration cytology, as the lesions in this region become visible or palpable only when they are large or when they are in clinically advanced stages. In these cases, a direct percutaneous or transmucosal biopsy of visible or palpable masses, or computed tomography (CT), MRI, or ultrasound-guided biopsy of deep-seated sinonasal tract masses, can be useful for diagnosis in 87.5% of cases.[13,15] A transnasal endoscopic biopsy under general anesthesia is the most appropriate diagnostic technique for two main reasons: first, a larger tissue sample can be sent to the pathologist; second, control of bleeding allows optimal visualization of the tumor, which is essential to select areas without necrosis.[35] If juvenile nasoangiofibroma, solitary fibrous tumor, or other vascular tumors are suspected, a biopsy must be performed in the operating room. For a malignant tumor involving the cavernous sinus, the use of minimally invasive techniques with neuronavigation allows this goal to be achieved safely.[34]A complex neurosurgical approach is never justified only for diagnostic purposes. Histological evaluation by an expert pathologist is very important; often, the initial diagnosis is incomplete or erroneous, and the treatment could change radically.[34] It is recommended that the patient be presented at a multidisciplinary tumor board to facilitate multimodality management planning.[31]

Treatment strategy and role of surgery

If malignancy is suspected or diagnosed, the staging of the lesion will be mandatory to determine whether surgery is to be performed. Due to the variety of sites of origin and tumor heterogeneity, it is difficult to disaggregate the treatment and follow-up with each of the pathologies. In general, primary surgery is almost always necessary for most tumors involving the anterior skull base, including sinonasal tumors, benign or malignant.[10] For malignancies that present invasion to the carotid artery at the level of the cavernous sinus, primary surgery is controversial, in most of cases would not be indicated; in cases of cavernous sinus invasion with carotid artery sparing, if is technically possible and justified based on the risk-benefit ratio, oncological resection of skull base malignancy should be considered to target eradication of disease to increase overall and progression-free survival.[4,21,34]Neoadjuvant therapy can be started in some of these tumors to reduce their size and later evaluate the chances for their removal. Discerning preoperatively between resectable and unresectable tumors is crucial, since positive surgical margins or macroscopic residual tumor mean progression and significant complications before starting postoperative treatment. But even with a resectable tumor, the combination of CMT, with or without radiotherapy (RT), and subsequent surgery can facilitate the complete resection and conservation of the eye, if the orbit is incipiently affected and the tumor is sensitive to treatment.[14,29] We found that neoadjuvant therapy was administered in 12.7% of our patients. Regarding adjuvant therapy, there were different schemes, where only RT (36.8%) was the most frequent, followed by the combination of RT plus CMT (24.5%), followed by CMT alone at 5.2%. However, 33.3% of patients did not receive any adjuvant therapy. The pursuit of surgery is complete resection, that is, with tumor-free histopathological margins, although in low-grade malignancies, resection with limited margin and adjuvant treatment is a justifiable treatment if it produces a significant aesthetic or functional advantage. One must resist the temptation to perform incomplete resections, claiming to have adjuvant treatment; this does not correct the limitations of inadequately indicated or poorly performed surgery.[12]

Imaging studies

The imaging studies needed to treat these lesions are CT and MRI. Fine-cut CT with bone and soft tissue windows and reconstructions in different planes is the initial study. Coronal reconstructions allow the evaluation of the walls of the orbit, skull base, cavernous sinus, carotid artery, intracranial extension, and perineural dissemination in the maxillary, infraorbital, and vidian nerves. Axial images may reveal the involvement of the nasopharynx, pterygopalatine fossa, infratemporal fossa, medial wall and apex of the orbit, and sphenoid sinus. Bone destruction should be distinguished from remodeling, in which the bone maintains its continuity but bends and thins; this suggests benign or low-grade malignant lesions, such as mucocele, nasoangiofibroma, or nasal inverted papilloma. Contrast MRI gives details of the tumor regarding the surrounding intra- and extracranial soft tissue, including dural and/or cerebral parenchyma invasion. It shows indirect perineural and vascular invasion data and distinguishes between retained secretions or swollen mucosa of the tumor. It also evaluates the response to CMT-RT and facilitates the follow-up of reconstructed patients with flaps. The information provided by each of these studies is extremely valuable and, therefore, both should be considered when classifying the tumor and selecting the best surgical approach. The pattern of displacement of surrounding structures can serve as a guide to determine the origin of the tumor, although in many cases, because of the large size of the lesions, it becomes impossible to determine their origin precisely.[19] Angiography is mandatory in highly vascularized tumors, mostly when preoperative embolization is considered.[10] Positron emission tomography–CT (PET-CT) has a high false positive rate. PET-CT is ideal for identification or ruling out systemic disease, rather than localization of a primary tumor; it is especially useful for tumors that have a propensity for distant metastasis, such as adenoid cystic carcinoma, lymphoma, mucosal melanoma, and high-grade and undifferentiated carcinomas.[31] PET-CT is only indicated if its result substantially modifies the management.[20,30]

Indications and contraindications of surgery

Open craniofacial surgery (cranial and facial approaches) usually should be indicated to remove large tumors of the nasal cavity or paranasal sinuses invading the anterior skull base (constitute the most common indication), tumors invading the orbit located at lateral and above the optic nerve or when exenteration (periorbita, fat and extraorbital muscles invasion) is required, tumor infiltrating the palate, tumor infiltrating anterior frontal sinus, tumors with skin infiltration and plastic reconstruction needed or when en bloc tumor resection is required.[10] Sometimes, orbital exenteration cannot be determined preoperatively, so the patient should be informed that this decision may be made intraoperatively.[31]The transbasal subfrontal extradural approach is the most frequently indicated cranial approach for the treatment of neoplasms that affect the anterior base of the skull.

Endoscopic endonasal approach alone or combined with craniotomy, should be used to remove tumors located from the posterior frontal sinus to anterior foramen magnum in the sagittal plane and until the lamina papyracea in the coronal plane, invading or not the orbit, with internal rectus muscle and the lacrimal pathway sparing; this is, early-stage tumors (T1 and T2 stage) with little or no intracranial or lateral dural extension. To perform this procedure, experience of the surgical team and adequate equipment (a complete set of endoscopes, drills, and neuronavigation system) are required.[8,17] The experience with endoscopic techniques in some centers remains limited, and there is no evidence of their oncological superiority over conventional approaches.[26] However, endoscopic resections performed by experienced teams in selected patients do not compromise survival or the radicality required for resection and allow similar oncological outcomes, with fewer sequelae and complications and a shorter hospital stay.[5,22] Endoscopic resections should follow the oncological principles of achieving complete resection with negative margins; this factor may be the most important predictive variable of survival that surgeons can influence.[7] Patient selection is undoubtedly crucial to achieving good results.

Independent of the surgical technique adopted, for particularly vascular tumors with extracranial arterial feeders, such as those from distal branches in the internal maxillary or facial arteries, preoperative arteriography with embolization is a compelling option. However, all extracranial vessels involved with paranasal tumors can be accessed early in an operative approach, such as transantrally through the pterygomaxillary fossa to the internal maxillary artery. If embolization is selected, it should be performed at least 24 h before the planned operative intervention, to allow for clotting within the tumor, but not more than 2 weeks before the operation, to minimize collateral development.[10]

Surgery is contraindicated when we have criteria of unresectability, which are a low probability of complete resection or at the cost of prohibitive sequelae. Operatively, unresectable lesions include poorly controlled systemic diseases and distant metastasis, significant gross and deep brain and dural invasion, bilateral invasion of the optic nerve or chiasm, extensive involvement of the middle cranial fossa or involvement of a nerve other than the maxillary trigeminal branch, extension through the cavernous sinus, apex of orbit,content of both orbits or extensive and diffuse involvement of the skin, and obvious extension to the nasopharynx or clivus.[32] An exception is esthesioneuroblastoma, which, even with limited brain invasion, can prolong survival after resection. In radiosensitive malignancies (nasopharyngeal carcinoma, lymphoma, plasmacytoma, and some sarcomas), surgery is avoided; in these cases, RT and CMT are indicated; in plasmacytomas, localized with complete resection possible, surgery followed by RT is indicated.[21] Patients with unresectable epidermoid carcinoma are initially treated with a combination of CMT and RT; if the tumor becomes resectable, resection is considered.[14] Palliative surgery or decompression alone is not justified.

The contraindications for endoscopic surgery are highly vascular tumors, the need for orbital exenteration, soft tissue or skin involvement of the face or forehead, frontal sinus bone involvement, palate, maxilla or mandible involvement, significant (>2 cm) of brain invasion, lateral tumor extension with invasion of the pterygomaxillary space and/or infratemporal fossa, and extensive bilateral disease. In addition, in patients where the potential to achieve free margins is in doubt, an open or combined approach must be performed.[10]

Approach selection

There are different approaches used in craniofacial surgery to remove tumors involving the skull base. These are classified according to three groups: anterior, antero-lateral, and lateral. Any of these can be combined with a facial approach, which is performed using endoscopic or open techniques through the face: degloving, transmaxillary, transoral, transnasal, anterior transmandibular, and facial translocation.

In this series, the subfrontal-transbasal approach was the most frequently used technique for craniofacial surgery. It includes a frontal craniotomy and transbasal (transethmoidal) approach with different variants (classic or standard, extended, and extensive).[39] The subfrontaltransbasal approach is used in pathologies that affect the anterior base of the skull. For the selection of one of its variants, key criteria are considered, such as the sagittal location of the lesion, the presence of dural involvement, and the histopathological diagnosis obtained from a preoperative biopsy [Figures 2 and 3]. Based on these criteria, [Figure 4] demonstrates subfrontal-transbasal variants, and [Figure 5] illustrates representative cases managed using different surgical approaches. The standard or classic transbasal (ethmoidal osteotomy without fronto-orbital osteotomy) approach is preferred for lesions located in the frontal and ethmoidal sinuses, sparing the sphenoid sinus. An extended transbasal approach (ethmoid osteotomy with fronto-orbital osteotomy between the supraorbital foramen or notch without detachment of the medial canthal ligaments) is preferred for lesions located in the posterior midline that invade the sphenoid sinus. The extensive transbasal approach (removal of the entire fronto-orbital bar and lateral orbital wall) is used for large midline lesions with invasion of the clivus or unilateral or bilateral extension, and for large lesions with ventrolateral extension.

Complications

Complications are primarily related to the size and biological behavior of the tumor, medical comorbidity, prior radiation, and dural and brain involvement.[15] In cases of tumors confined to the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses invading the anterior fossa, the deficit of the first cranial nerve is the most common morbidity, with subsequent anosmia. If an extensive transbasal approach is used, a supraorbital nerve injury may occur, producing a frontal region anesthesia. Nerves III, IV, and VI are infrequently affected by the invasion of the cavernous sinus and its manipulation during the resection. Lower cranial nerve damage is frequently related to the manipulation of the tumor component toward the posterior fossa, mainly in chordomas, because of the manipulation of these nerves in the poststyloid parapharyngeal space. It is important to assess their involvement preoperatively as well as to perform necessary measures such as tracheostomy or gastrostomy, even before surgery. Intraoperative complications in large tumors close to the petroclival region or cavernous sinus are related to cranial nerve deficit and/or carotid rupture. In these cases, sometimes in benign lesions, it becomes preferable to leave a small piece of tumor attached to these structures to avoid severe neurological damage or even death.

The second common complication is the leak of CSF; to prevent it, it is mandatory to make sure a hermetic primary closure of dura or use a pericranium path, in addition to making a vascularized flap of pericranium reinforced with a fibrin adhesive. Surgical wound infections are the same as in any other surgery; however, attention should be paid to the factors that increase the risk of this complication, such as inadequate exclusion of the nasosinusal cavity and the presence of unnoticed dural defects. Both CSF leak and surgical wound infections were the main postoperative complications in our series. Nasolacrimal duct dysfunction is a frequent complication with subsequent epiphora. Malocclusion and chewing complications occur when the palate or parts of the palate are resected due to tumor invasion or when their removal occurs during surgery. In these cases, it is mandatory to use a palatine obturator as soon as possible, every time, mainly for eating.[9,38] We found CFS leak and infection as the two main complications in this series.

Potential alternative approaches

Most small lesions affecting both the nasal cavity and paranasal sinuses can be resected using simple approaches without the need to combine them with transcranial routes. Endoscopy has helped in accessing these areas through the face using a minimally invasive procedure. In cases of malignant tumors, oncological principles need to be followed, and removal should not be confined to the tumor itself. Wide tumor-free margins should be included in the surgical specimen, with the aim of achieving better long-term disease control. These malignancies must be removed with combined approaches.[21] The antero-lateral and lateral approaches are reserved for tumors with lateral invasion: a fronto-orbitozygomatic approach for extra- or intra-axial superior midline lesions, extending up to the level of the foramen of Monro; a pterional approach for extra- or intra-axial lateral extended lesions, including those invading the Sylvian fissure.[1,30]To make the approach wider, it is necessary to combine them, such as the one-and-a-half fronto-orbital approach.[3,39]Finally, some tumors invade the palate, necessitating a radical maxillectomy with the removal of the palate with the tumor and a subsequent use of a palatine obturator.[38]

CONCLUSION

The main indication for approaching the anterior skull base in craniofacial surgery is for giant tumor removal located along the cranial base, ventral and medial to the cranial nerves. Surgical resection can be performed with multiple techniques: endoscopic, endoscopic-assisted, and traditional open craniofacial approaches. All of them can be combined with different facial approaches. A combined subfrontal– transbasal and transfacial approach is preferable because neoplasms located in these regions are generally very large at the time of diagnosis, mainly for the management of malignancies in advanced stages. Histology, localization, extension, relation with neurovascular structures, extra or intracranial, and extra or intradural invasion are factors to consider in the selection of the surgical approach. Multidisciplinary management is the key to getting successful results in the treatment of tumors in these areas.
