Improving patient reported experience in inpatients with palliative care needs: a pre-post quality improvement study
Authors: Claudia Virdun, Lee Jones, Gursharan K. Singh, Patsy Yates, Jane L. Phillips, Alison Mudge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOptimising hospital care to align with what matters most for people living with advanced serious illness is a global priority area for improvement. Collection and feedback of patient reported experience measures (PREMs) is one potential method to inform improvements. This study used the integrated Promoting Action on Research implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework to inform the implementation of a complex intervention that included collection and feedback of PREM data and facilitation to empower ward-based quality improvements for inpatients with advanced serious illness.MethodsA single-site pre-post quality improvement study within a large Australian hospital. The intervention titled Listen, Empower and Act to improve Hospital Palliative care (‘LEAHP’ bundle) included three phases: ‘Listen’ – collecting and analysing patient (PREM) and clinician perspectives to understand current care experience and local context; ‘Empower’ – collating PREM feedback and facilitating local stakeholder engagement to identify and prioritise areas for improvement; and ‘Act’ – facilitating clinician led innovation development and implementation informed and monitored by continuing PREM collection and feedback. Intervention fidelity was summarised descriptively from field notes and meeting records. The primary effectiveness outcome was change in ‘very good’ experiences over time, reported from an eight-question validated PREM designed for inpatients with serious illness (consideRATE). Analysis used mixed binary logistic regression with time period as fixed effect and ward as random effect.ResultsThe three participating wards completed the intervention with some adaptations, and each implemented different innovations. The proportion of ‘very good’ responses showed a statistically significant increase for all consideRATE questions in intervention periods 1–4 compared to period 0 (baseline). All questions except ‘attention to feelings’ (Q2) were significantly improved by the first measurement period, and all except ‘attention to affairs’ (Q6) remained significantly higher than baseline during the final measurement period.ConclusionsImplementation of the LEAHP bundle led to improved palliative care experience within three wards in one large tertiary hospital setting. Listening to patients and empowering clinical teams to collectively reflect on data and lead change was crucial to study success and required skilled facilitation. Testing the sustainability, transferability and scalability of the intervention will be important next steps.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-025-01883-3.

Introduction

One third of hospitalised patients are living with advanced serious illness, and one fifth will die within the next 12 months [1–3]. Although people with palliative care needs and their families have outlined what is important to enable safe and high-quality care when in hospital [4–7], balancing acute and palliative care needs within this context can be challenging [8–10]. Care is not always aligned with what matters most for each patient with a poor prognosis (and their family), leading to potential non-beneficial treatments [10–12]. Although some patients with palliative care needs describe compassionate and responsive inpatient care [13, 14], others note key areas for improvement relating to feeling heard, feeling supported, feeling informed and navigating the clinical environment [13]. Ensuring care provision aligns with what matters most for this population is a global priority area for improvement [1–3, 13].

Embedding change within the hospital context is challenging given these systems are complex, adaptive and continuously responding to factors such as changing patient needs, new evidence or innovations, external pressures such as pandemics, fiscal constraints and environmental impacts, and changing regulatory conditions [15–18]. Collection and feedback of patient reported experience measures (PREMs) to clinicians is one promising method to improve hospital care and outcomes aligned with patient preferences and goals [19–22]. PREMs can be defined as: “instruments that capture a patient’s experience of receiving care, specifically the patient’s perception of what happened during their care encounter and how it happened.” (22 p.2) Widespread recognition of the potential value of PREMs informing clinical improvement has encouraged development and validation of tools for a range of clinical populations and settings with many health services adopting routine data collection as a performance measure [22–25]. However, there is still remarkably limited empiric data to support PREM use in improving care at patient, facility or system level [19]. Existing studies highlight several challenges in using data to improve care, particularly improvements which aim to change clinician behaviour. These challenges include timely, meaningful feedback to clinicians at the correct level as well as time and support for those clinicians to reflect on the data, identify areas of responsibility and implement meaningful and sustainable change accordingly [26–28]. This accords with the growing literature on supporting complex change in clinical environments, which are characterised by multiple actors from different disciplines, competing tasks and priorities, and highly complex social dynamics [17, 18]. It is likely that implementing change in such a setting will benefit from a structured approach [17, 18].

One widely used theoretical approach to inform implementation is the integrated Promoting Action on Research implementation in Health Services (i-PARIHS) framework [29]. This approach proposes that successful implementation of an innovation or improvement (in this case, providing high quality palliative care aligned with patient preferences) is contingent on effective facilitation that engages the recipients (e.g. patients, multidisciplinary clinicians and health service managers) within their local and organisational context [29]. Facilitation is both a role and a set of actions, enabling patients and those caring for them to create change using a range of implementation strategies that are tailored to the context [29, 30]. Previous studies have demonstrated that facilitation combined with collection and structured feedback of patient experience can improve processes of care and patient experience [20, 31].

This study aimed to evaluate the effect of a complex intervention that included collection and feedback of patient experience data and facilitation to empower ward-based quality improvements for inpatients with advanced serious illness.

Methods

Design

A single-site pre-post quality improvement study.

Context

This study was undertaken in a large Australian publicly funded hospital. Three wards were selected (oncology, general medicine/renal medicine and general medicine/respiratory medicine) due to their large number of patients with advanced serious illness [2]. These wards provide inpatient care to patients who present with acute illness to the emergency department or are referred from general practitioners or other hospitals in Queensland. Care is provided by medical teams consisting of consultant physicians who supervise training registrars and junior doctors; aligned multidisciplinary teams of allied health professionals (e.g. physiotherapists, social workers); and ward-based nursing teams led by a nurse unit manager on each ward. A specialist palliative care consultancy service (medicine and nursing) is available after medical specialist referral on weekdays, with after hours on-call support. One ward also has access to a collaborative renal supportive care service consisting of nephrology and specialist palliative care expertise (medicine and nursing).

Participants and partners

This quality improvement intervention engaged patients likely to be in their last 12 months of life cared for on the study wards (the inpatient population who are likely to have palliative care needs), two experienced patient representatives with a special interest in palliative care, and the multidisciplinary clinical teams (nurses, doctors and allied health professionals) on the study wards along with their team leaders (Nurse Unit Managers and Medical Directors) who are responsible for providing and prioritising care.

The intervention was led by an early career researcher with specialist palliative care expertise (CV), supported by a team of clinicians and researchers with expertise in medicine, nursing, palliative care, qualitative and quantitative research methods and implementation science. The project was a funded partnership between a state health department-based jurisdiction, a university and the large health service involved in the study. Implementation support for the novice facilitator was provided by an expert facilitator experienced in application of the i-PARIHS framework, and a research nurse was employed to collect PREMs and support context assessment.

Intervention

The intervention was an improvement bundle titled Listen, Empower and Act to improve Hospital Palliative care (‘LEAHP’ bundle) (Refer Fig. 1). ‘Listen’ included collecting PREMs from eligible patients to understand current quality of care, and conducting interviews with multidisciplinary clinicians to understand local barriers and enablers to delivering care. These findings are reported in detail elsewhere [13, 32]. In brief, across all three wards, PREM ratings were higher for attention to symptoms, feelings and what matters most than for attention to the environment, what to expect, and patients’ affairs, while free text responses highlighted the importance of feeling supported, feeling informed, feeling heard and navigating the clinical environment. Clinician interviews across all three wards described challenges with understanding what palliative care is, integrating care across disciplinary silos, developing clinical skills and confidence, prioritising palliative care amongst acute care demands, and harnessing organisational and clinical leadership. ‘Empower’ occurred through structured feedback of PREM data to a key stakeholder group (including multidisciplinary clinicians, managers, specialist palliative care representatives and consumer representatives) to reflect on current care and create a shared vision for local improvement. ‘Act’ consisted of 8 months of regular (1–2 weekly) facilitated working groups of key clinician stakeholders to develop local improvement strategies aligned with this vision, with monthly feedback of PREM data to monitor progress. LEAHP was explicitly informed by the i-PARIHS framework [29], engaging with local recipients to understand and tailor quality improvement interventions to patient needs and local context through active facilitation.

Study of the intervention

Intervention fidelity (against planned activities) and ward-level improvements were monitored through field notes and ward meeting minutes maintained prospectively by the facilitator. The primary outcome to establish intervention effectiveness was patient reported experience evaluated over time.

The primary outcome measure was an eight question validated patient reported experience measure designed for inpatients with serious illness called consideRATE (Refer Textbox 1) [34]. We have previously reviewed PREMs available for use within palliative care and mapped these tools to areas noted to be important for high quality care, from the patient and family perspective [23]. The consideRATE measure [34] was selected as it is brief, tailored to the needs of people with advanced serious illness, and offers the option for free text responses [23, 35, 36]. We previously established feasibility and acceptability of using consideRATE within our local context [35, 36].

Eligible inpatients (or their carers in proxy) were invited to complete consideRATE independently or with assistance if required due to their ill health. Adult inpatients were eligible to complete consideRATE if they were an inpatient on the participating wards for at least 24 h and had 2 or more general indicators of poor or deteriorating health and one or more clinical indicator of one or multiple life-limiting conditions [37] in accordance with the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicators Tool (SPICT™) [38]. Patients with a severe cognitive impairment, clinically unstable or terminal or unable to answer survey questions were not eligible to participate in this study, however their carers were eligible to participate in proxy. Patients and carers who could not converse in English were invited to participate with support from a translation service.

Each participating ward displayed a poster outlining study information in a prominent area for patient and family access. All adult patients who had received care for ≥ 24 h within participating wards were screened by the research nurse in discussion with the ward’s Nursing Unit Manager to establish eligibility. Potentially eligible inpatients and carers were provided with a study information sheet and the survey in paper form. Sufficient time was provided to enable the potential participant to read the information sheet and ask any questions. Survey completion constituted informed consent. There were no identifying patient or carer details recorded on the surveys. Fidelity of screening for eligibility, recruitment and data collection occurred through clear processes established a priori to guide research nurse activity. In addition, each research nurse communicated with the project lead and study facilitator each day of data collection to address questions and confirm key processes and data collection principles were maintained.

Patients rated each question as either: ‘very good, good, bad, very bad or doesn’t apply’ with the option to also provide free text feedback for each question provided. Data were collected on a paper version of consideRATE and entered into Qualtrics for secure data management. Data were collected throughout Period 0 (baseline), 1, 2, 3 and 4 (intervention) and analysed in 2-month periods. Three research nurses supported screening for eligibility and PREM completion with key processes in place to enhance consistency across collectors. These key processes included: development of a screening and data management guide; a structured screening process of ward handover sheets for all inpatients to determine who met the eligibility criteria; a scripted introduction for use when approaching potential participants; and clear data entry and data storage processes.

Analysis

To evaluate intervention fidelity, the chief investigator summarised meeting records and field notes for each ward in a table which was reviewed with the expert facilitator. Content was then mapped to the program logic (Fig. 1) and planned activities for each ward (Table 1), before improvement priorities and innovations were summarised for each ward and mapped to the patient and clinician themes captured during the “Listen” phase [13, 32].

To evaluate the effect of the LEAHP bundle intervention on patient-reported experience over time, PREM data were sectioned into 2-month periods, with Period 0 being pre-intervention control and Periods 1–4 being intervention (commencing approximately 6 months after Period 0 commenced). Data periods were 2-month periods where nurses collected data two days per week (excluding holiday periods such as Christmas and Easter). Respondent descriptive variables were summarised for each data collection period using appropriate descriptive statistics, along with the number of total responses and proportion of “does not apply” responses. consideRATE question ratings were dichotomised to understand the pattern of participants reporting ‘very good’ experiences as opposed to rating either ‘good,’ ‘bad’ or ‘very bad’. ‘Very good’ was chosen as the outcome measure due to the positive bias noted in rating quality of care using the consideRATE scale [35]. The “does not apply” category was excluded from analysis as it could not be categorised with yes or no and there was not a high enough frequency of this response to be analysed with multinomial logistic regression. Patterns of responses were explored using chi-square and t-tests. The proportion of patients reporting ‘very good’ experiences per period were plotted for each ward with Wilson’s 95% confidence intervals to account for proportions close to zero.

The proportion of ‘very good’ responses for each question were analysed using mixed binary logistic regression to account for correlations between wards. The final model included period as a fixed effect and ward as a random effect, as the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) indicated that between 1% and 7% of the variance in the outcomes was due to similarities within wards. Logistic models were also fit including an interaction between period and ward, but these are not presented due to wide confidence intervals caused by low frequencies.

Ethics

Townsville Hospital and Health Service Human Research Ethics Committee granted ethical approval in May 2022 (Reference number: HREC/2022/QTHS/84709) with the requirement to review proposed work to be progressed by each ward after co-design of improvement foci was complete. Amendment approvals to progress with each ward’s identified area of focus was provided for Ward 1 (Sept 2022), Ward 2 (Dec 2022) and Ward 3 (Feb 2023) with the same recorded reference number. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was secured by patient (or proxy) completion of the PREM (after provision of an information sheet), and voluntary engagement of clinicians within co-design and working group processes. Informed written consent was obtained for all clinician interviews (results reported elsewhere [32]).

The Standards for Quality Improvement Reporting Excellence (SQUIRE 2.0) guidelines [40] have been used to report the findings.

Results

Intervention fidelity

The three participating wards successfully completed the intervention as planned across all time periods, with some minor adaptations. Table 2 summarises intervention activities. Collection of baseline PREM data (n = 40 per ward) and multidisciplinary engagement in interviews and surveys were similar between wards. Key multidisciplinary stakeholders were identified and invited to co-design meetings for each ward, but Ward 1 was the only ward where allied health professionals attended. Numbers of attendees at co-design meetings was greater at Period 0 for ward 1 (39 attendees compared to n = 19 for ward 2 and n = 24 for ward 3). The proportion of invitees who voted to prioritise the improvement action was high on all wards (ward 1, 95%, ward 2, 81% and ward 3, 77%).

Facilitated working groups ran throughout Periods 1–4 on each ward to focus on the prioritised area for improvement in order to design and test innovations tailored to local context. As shown in Table 2, ward 1 and 3 had frequent meetings with multidisciplinary attendance, while ward 2 had fewer meetings, fewer attendees and only nursing attendance. PREM feedback occurred on all wards, but the method and extent of distribution of this feedback to clinician stakeholders varied between wards. Planning of innovations to address the agreed priority focus took different times so that each ward commenced an innovation at different time points: Ward 1 – towards the end of Period 3; Ward 2 – midway through Period 2; and Ward 3 – midway through Period 1 (Table 3).

Discussion

Summary

This study demonstrated the feasibility of implementing the LEAHP bundle across the key stages of ‘Listen, Empower and Act’. Collection of sufficient PREM data was achieved throughout the study using consideRATE to support meaningful identification of improvement areas (developing a shared vision) and to monitor the impact of proposed innovations. Our local context assessment [32] demonstrated the importance of organisational support and multidisciplinary clinical leadership for enabling improvements in integrated palliative care. With organisational support secured through a collaborative grant and senior stakeholder engagement, it was feasible for senior clinicians from each ward to meet on two occasions to review the PREM data and discuss and prioritise areas for improvement, although there was variable engagement across clinical disciplines. Likewise, the development of working groups to take the shared vision forward, design innovations to test within each local context and continue to review PREM data to inform progress was successfully achieved in all three wards. However, true multidisciplinary membership of these working groups was only achieved on Ward 1, with Ward 2 engaging only nurses and Ward 3 engaging medical and nursing staff. The value of allied health engagement to guide innovations in care delivery, is noted within a recent systematic review [43]. However meaningful collaboration may need to address key barriers to allied health participation in acute care including resourcing, and perceptions of reduced capability [44].

PREM data at baseline on all wards showed similar patterns, with highest performance on attention to symptoms and lowest performance on attention to the environment, and there was some variation between ward ratings at baseline (Supplementary Fig. 1). Each ward approached innovation in a different way (Table 3), prioritising varied improvements that reflected the range of challenges identified in our context assessment [32] including identifying palliative care needs before the terminal phase, raising the visibility of palliative care to enable prioritisation amongst competing needs, negotiating complex interdisciplinary and interspecialty governance, building staff skills and confidence and optimising the physical environment.

This study demonstrates significantly improved patient reported experience of care quality for key areas that matter for patients with palliative care needs (symptom management, psychosocial care, optimising the environment, effective communication and goal concordant care) following implementation of the LEAHP bundle. Significant improvements in the proportion of ‘very good’ responses was seen throughout the intervention period for all questions except for one question focused on how clinical teams supported patients in relation to their affairs (things like financial planning and preparing enduring documents), where statistical power was limited by a high proportion of ‘does not apply’ responses. Importantly, these improvements from baseline were observed across wards with varied performance at baseline, and occurred early across all three wards despite substantial differences in the innovations implemented and timing of intervention on each ward. Moreover, while ward-level innovations did not appear to address all the domains rated, improvement was demonstrated across all questions. Such findings suggest that the effects of the intervention likely relate to the core processes associated with the LEAHP bundle that focus on listening to patients and clinical teams, developing a shared vision for change and supporting collective action to improve the patient experience, rather than the specific innovations (such as a patient information leaflet, screening tool, or family trolley) that teams implemented. The statistically significant and clinically meaningful improvements demonstrated in this study are consistent with previous suggestions that audit and feedback must be meaningful for clinical teams and supported by facilitated reflection to inform change over time [45]. This outcome is important given the paucity of empiric evidence relating to the implementation and use of patient reported experience data within healthcare to drive clinical practice change [46, 47]. However, this study is unable to report on whether such improvements are sustained without ongoing facilitation and PREM feedback. Indeed, results seem to suggest this is unlikely to be the case as some improvements did start to decrease over time, despite remaining improved from the baseline by the end of the ‘Act’ phase. Further study is required to understand the scalability and sustainability of this intervention in different practice settings.

Interpretation

Consistent with the i-PARIHS implementation framework, skilled facilitation was essential to engage patients and clinicians in change that respected their understanding of local care and context and to keep the patient voice (represented by the PREM data) central. Engagement of different clinical disciplines varied across wards and influenced the approach to innovation with Ward 2 being focused mostly on nursing care in the terminal phase of care, while the other wards engaged more directly with the challenges of communication within complex multidisciplinary teams as they help patients navigate living with clinical uncertainty and a poor prognosis. Although allied health professionals engaged in initial interviews, their participation in ongoing working group activity was minimal. Given the important role allied health clinicians have within optimal palliative care [48] understanding the reasons for their lack of engagement is an important area for future research.

The high proportion of participants noting that the ‘attention to your affairs’ question ‘did not apply’ has not been previously reported in psychometric testing of the consideRATE tool [34, 39, 49]. The tool was developed and tested in the United States and our findings raise the possibility that the terminology of ‘attention to affairs’ is interpreted differently within the Australian context, with some free text responses suggesting that this is interpreted as a legal rather than health professional responsibility. This suggests a disconnect between legally recognised processes (eg enduring guardians and advance care directives) and clinical advance care planning which may be important to understand more deeply. Advance care planning has evolved globally for over 30 years as a potential solution to enabling goal-concordant care [50]. However, evidence that advance care planning achieves higher quality end of life care remains controversial with suggestions that the focus should instead be on ensuring a trusted surrogate decision maker and improving skills to enable effective shared decision making within the health care system [50].

The LEAHP bundle shows promise in enabling ward level improved palliative care. However, this study relied on the external resources of a collaborative research project to support skilled facilitation and PREM collection. Embedding these skills and resources within the health service would be required to ensure a sustained and scalable intervention. A recent systematic review analysing the barriers and enablers of sustainability of change within the hospital context noted enablers as: including clear accountability of roles and responsibilities; strong leadership and champions for the intervention and organisational support [17]. Thinking about how the LEAHP bundle can move from an externally administered intervention within a research context, to an organisationally supported initiative is an important consideration for transferability and scalability of this promising intervention. This would need to consider how local facilitators could be trained and integrated into existing health care roles; how PREM collection and feedback (including important qualitative comments) could be integrated into workflows; who would lead continuing multidisciplinary engagement; and how the value proposition would be framed for organisational decision makers. Detailed analysis of the resource requirements of this intervention and acceptability findings from staff interviews will be published separately to inform ongoing intervention refinement.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study lie within the robust approach founded within the iPARIHS framework, designed to implement the LEAHP bundle and evaluate its impact over time. A clear protocol and program logic was developed a priori but remained flexible for adaptations to local contexts. Importantly a strong patient voice remained central throughout this study both through the patient reported experience data and input from two consumer representatives who participated in co-design meetings to prioritise areas for improvement. We used the i-PARIHS framework to plan and guide implementation, conducting a rigorous context assessment with careful attention to readiness for change, barriers and facilitators to palliative care and establishing key relationships for study success. An expert facilitator supported experiential learning by the novice facilitator [51]. Our evaluation used dichotomised ratings (very good as opposed to all other ratings) to address the noted positive bias established in earlier feasibility testing of consideRATE [35]. This approach is consistent with previous validation that this ‘top box scoring’ is valid and optimal for detecting improvement over time [49].

We also recognise several limitations. This study was conducted at a single site limiting generalisability of outcomes and the demographics reflect a predominantly Caucasian population who may have different patient experiences than more diverse cultural groups. In addition, while psychometric validity of consideRATE has been reported in several studies from the United States [34, 39, 49] it is possible differences in interpretation across varied cultural contexts may influence responses. This cultural homogeneity may impact data ratings where cultural interpretations, experiences and understandings may differ. Concepts relating to dignity, respect and open communication may differ for cultures that may be more collectivist in approach than westernised cultures. Healthcare experiences for marginalised groups may impact trust and access and influence how patients respond to items within consideRATE. Finally, ensuring phrasing of questions enables true understanding across cultures is important to establish. Authors of consideRATE have commenced work in relation to cultural validation of their tool to better inform understanding of this in practice. Exploration of response differences across diverse cultures was not completed within this study given the small sample of responses from non-Caucasian patients limiting statistical power to draw meaningful inferences. Research nurses were only available two days per week to collect the PREM data which may have resulted in some selection bias, although this should not have varied across measurement periods. Three individual research nurses were involved across the study timeline and although consistent training and mentoring supported fidelity of PREM collection, it is possible that variation in individual approaches impacted ratings, particularly ‘does not apply’ responses. Finally, our study resourcing did not allow for repeated PREM collection after the intervention was completed. Repeated PREM measurement at 6 months post intervention would have been useful to inform an understanding of sustained improvements in patient experience.

Recommendations for future practice and research

Sites considering implementing PREMs for clinical improvement should recognise the need for clinical engagement, facilitated reflection and supported team-based action to realise improvements. This study has shown promising improvements with the LEAHP bundle at a single site over an 8-month period using a supported external facilitator. Better understanding of the skills and resources required in clinical practice to implement this bundle in different contexts and maintaining improvements over time will be essential to help define its value and support scale and spread of this promising intervention to improve inpatient palliative care. This will include more detailed understanding of consideRATE tool performance in different settings and populations.

Conclusions

Implementation of the LEAHP bundle led to improved palliative care experience within three wards in one large tertiary hospital setting. Improved patient experience occurred regardless of the innovation designed and tested at the local level and therefore seems to be related to the actions of developing a shared vision based on patient feedback and empowering collective action tailored to local context. Listening to patients and staff and empowering clinical teams to collectively reflect on patient data and lead change was crucial to study success. Further testing of the transferability, scalability, sustainment and costs of this intervention will help to inform ongoing systems improvement in hospital palliative care.
