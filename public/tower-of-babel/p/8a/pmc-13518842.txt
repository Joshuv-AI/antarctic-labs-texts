Projected mycotoxin contamination in European wheat under future climate and socioeconomic scenarios
Authors: Xinxin Wang, Clàudia Rodés-Bachs, H. J. Van der Fels-Klerx
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mycotoxin contamination in wheat is strongly influenced by weather conditions, yet how contamination may evolve under future climate and socioeconomic change remains poorly understood at the European scale. Here, we develop a hybrid modelling framework combining machine learning and crop phenology to assess scenario-dependent changes in mycotoxin contamination. The framework integrates historical monitoring data with climate projections under multiple Shared Socioeconomic Pathways. Model evaluation shows strong performance for low-contamination conditions, while the ability to distinguish higher contamination levels remains limited due to class imbalance. Results indicate an overall increase in contamination risk under climate change, particularly for deoxynivalenol (DON), with relatively higher risk in coastal and northwestern Europe. These findings highlight the role of climate-driven shifts in crop phenology and weather conditions, and provide a scenario-based framework for exploring future mycotoxin risk patterns rather than precise quantitative predictions.

Introduction

Climate change poses a threat to global food security through its direct effects on crop production and pests1–3 and its indirect effects on food safety4–7. While increasing crop production is critical to meet the world’s growing food demand8,9, ensuring food safety is equally important to achieve global food security5,10–12. The provision of safe food is crucial, as unsafe food poses health risks and cannot be consumed by humans or animals13. The world’s food system will need to double its crop production by 2050 to feed a population of nine billion8,14, while a number of studies on the impacts of climate change on crop growth and production report an overall global decline in yields, presumably due to a large extent to warmer temperatures15–17.

One of the main food safety hazards in arable crops is the presence of mycotoxins. Multiple studies have reported considerable mycotoxin contamination of food crops worldwide. For instance, in 1985, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) estimated that 25% of the global food crop was contaminated by mycotoxins, while a more recent study estimated that 60–80% of cereals and nuts was contaminated by mycotoxins in 202018. Due to the toxicity of mycotoxins and their detrimental effects on human19,20 and animal health21,22, harvested crop contaminated with mycotoxins above legal thresholds are prohibited for food or feed. Instead, they must be downgraded for alternative uses, such as biofuel production, or destroyed23. This downgrading reduces both the availability of edible food and feed and the economic value of the harvest. For example, in Europe between 2010 and 2019, approximately 5% of wheat grain (about 75 million tonnes) was downgraded due to Deoxynivalenol (DON) contamination above the legal limit for human consumption, leading to estimated economic losses of €3 billion (EURO\year 2020)24.

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is the most widespread staple food crop worldwide25,26. The European Union remains a major contributor to global wheat supply, with production in recent years among the highest worldwide and playing a significant role in sustaining global wheat inventories27. Climate change poses a serious threat to wheat cultivation, with a potential decline in global wheat yields by 1% to 10% per Degree Celsius of warming28,29. This decline could be particularly severe in Southern Europe, where wheat yields decline could exceed 49% by 205030. Recent studies have highlighted mycotoxins as an emerging food safety concern under climate change, with increasing attention to climate-driven shifts in fungal distribution, contamination frequency, and co-occurrence patterns in cereal crops7,31–33. In particular, for wheat, Deoxynivalenol (DON) contamination has been reported as an emerging issue, with the anticipation of increased probabilities of contamination in Northern Europe by 205033.

Two types of models may be considered to characterize patterns of change in future mycotoxin contamination of wheat: a) A climate change projection model, and b) A wheat mycotoxin contamination prediction model using weather data as inputs. While several studies have developed machine-learning or statistical models to relate weather and phenology to mycotoxin occurrence in cereals (34–37), and some quantitative reviews document general climate effects on mycotoxin risk7,12,32, qualitative studies have explored climate change impacts on mycotoxin occurrence in specific regional contexts, such as Iran and Africa38,39, none to date have combined Coupled Model Intercomparison Project 6 (CMIP6) climate projections with phenology-aware machine-learning modelling to produce future contamination risk maps for wheat across Europe. CMIP6 provides the latest reference climate change projections40 with a comprehensive ensemble of climate models, incorporating multiple pathways and scenarios with improved bias-adjustment and high geographical details41. CMIP6 data have been used in the latest IPCC report and contain different climate change scenarios and the Shared-Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs)40.

The aim of this study is to develop a framework for predicting mycotoxin contamination in European wheat under climate change. This framework, demonstrated using winter wheat as an example, is broadly applicable to various food safety concerns. Here, we focus on contamination by six major mycotoxins (i.e., Deoxynivalenol (DON), Zearalenone (ZEA), T-2 and HT-2 toxins (T2 + HT2), Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), Fumonisins (FBs), and Ochratoxin A (OTA)) contamination in winter wheat grown in Europe under climate change. Compared to previous studies, this study uses a machine-learning approach and focus on a large area in Europe. We use the latest generation ensemble climate change projections (CMIP6) and predict temporal- and spatial changes in mycotoxin contamination of wheat in Europe. This modelling framework is not intended to provide precise quantitative predictions of contamination levels, but rather to explore relative changes in mycotoxin contamination under different climate and socioeconomic scenarios. Through this work, we aim to provide insights that are useful in developing policies and strategies to mitigate the adverse effects of climate change on food safety.

Results

Machine learning model with validation results

This study utilised machine learning to develop scenario projection models for mycotoxin contamination in winter wheat at regional scale. The results should be interpreted as indicative of relative changes in contamination risk across scenarios, rather than precise quantitative predictions.

The scenario projection model of mycotoxin contamination developed in this study indicates strong performance for the dominant low-contamination class, while performance for medium and high contamination levels remains limited due to class imbalance. Result shows an overall accuracy of >90% based on 5 fold-cross validation and a leave-out testing accuracy of 87% for 2019 and 79% for 2020. Model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics to account for class imbalance in contamination levels. In addition to overall accuracy, we report balanced accuracy, macro-averaged F1 score, and class-specific precision and recall, which provide a more informative assessment of the model’s ability to distinguish between low, medium, and high contamination classes. Given the strong class imbalance in the training data, scenario projection model performance differs across contamination classes. Recall and F1 scores are highest for the low-contamination class, while medium and high contamination classes show reduced sensitivity and higher uncertainty. These patterns reflect the limited number of observed high-contamination events. Importantly, this reduced sensitivity for elevated contamination levels implies that projections indicating increased risk should be interpreted as signals of relative risk escalation rather than precise predictions of exceedance events. The comparatively strong performance for medium and high contamination classes observed in five-fold cross-validation likely reflects the use of shared country-level contamination labels during training and validation, which may inflate apparent scenario projection skill for less frequent classes. When evaluated against temporally independent data from 2019 and 2020, model performance for elevated contamination levels declines substantially, indicating that cross-validation results should be interpreted as an upper bound on achievable discrimination rather than as representative of out-of-sample scenario projection accuracy. Although a class-weighting approach was implemented to mitigate imbalance during model training, model performance in independent validation remains limited for the medium- and high-contamination classes. This reflects the extreme rarity of elevated contamination events in the available dataset, which constrains the model’s ability to generalize beyond the dominant low-contamination class. Under such conditions, class-balancing strategies can improve sensitivity to minority classes, but cannot fully compensate for the lack of sufficiently representative observations. Consequently, model evaluation metrics are largely driven by the low-contamination class, and the model has limited discriminatory power for identifying moderate to high contamination levels. Drivers of mycotoxin contamination in winter wheat

The impacts of phenology and climatic characteristics on mycotoxin contamination classes in winter wheat were ranked according to their importance (Fig. 4). Phenology features, such as period_length, FD_day of year (Flowering Day), HD_day of year (Harvest Day) were found to play a major role in the mycotoxin contamination classes (Fig. 4a), as wheat is susceptible to fungal infection and mycotoxin contamination during this critical development period (between FD and HD). Among these variables, the growth stage indicators, FD and HD, emerged as the most important drivers of mycotoxin contamination, suggesting that timing within the wheat life cycle has a strong impact on contamination. Climatic factors also contributed substantially, particularly precipitation during the week of flowering (week_0), which was associated with increased contamination. Additionally, reduced rainfall during the seventh week after flowering (week_7) and higher temperatures in the third- or fourth-week post-flowering (week_3 and week_4), were linked to higher contamination. Especially for the high-risk contamination class. Discussion

Our study has developed a scenario projection framework for mycotoxin contamination in European wheat under climate change. The modelling framework developed in this study is more appropriately interpreted as a tool for exploring relative contamination risk patterns under different scenarios, rather than for making precise quantitative predictions. This is primarily due to strong class imbalance in the available data and the limited ability to distinguish medium and high contamination levels.

Machine-learning models, used either alone, or in combination with mechanistic models, have produced higher accuracy than traditional empirical and mechanistic models42,43. Wang, et al. 34 developed a machine learning model for the projection of mycotoxin contamination in wheat grain using weather features, showing an accuracy of above 90%. Till today, most studies focused on a single or on a few countries36,43–46 instead of focusing on a large area, such as Europe.

DON was estimated to reach the highest contamination class in 2050 and 2070, which can be attributed to the climate conditions. These findings align with previous studies. For instance, one study reported that DON was more rapidly produced at 25 °C, but the maximum amount of DON produced in wheat grain was at 30 °C47, indicating that extremely high temperature (above 30 °C) hinder DON production. Another study showed that DON and ZEA occurred more frequently in areas with moderate to high rainfall (41–100 mm/month) in June and July, indicating that low relative humidity and rainfall also hinder DON production.

Our results show high correlation between the presence of DON and ZEA in wheat under the influence of climate change (with DON contamination classes higher than those of ZEA). This high correlation could be attributed to both mycotoxins being simultaneously produced by Fusarium graminearum, a pathogenic fungus that is known to infect wheat spikes6,48. OTA does not necessarily correlate with the presence of DON or ZEA49,50. Using the historical monitoring data, the mycotoxins T2 + HT2, AFB1, and FBs showed not to be present in wheat grain. Thus, the projections remain null, and we did not display their results. FB and AFB1 are mainly seen in maize, rather than in wheat. T2 + HT2 toxins are mostly seen in oats, rather than wheat51.

Mycotoxin contamination in wheat is not a direct consequence of climate conditions but is mediated by specific fungal pathogens whose infection dynamics, colonisation success, and toxin biosynthesis are strongly influenced by environmental conditions and crop phenology. In European wheat systems, deoxynivalenol (DON) and zearalenone (ZEA) are primarily produced by Fusarium graminearum and F. culmorum, which infect wheat spikes mainly around flowering. Successful infection and subsequent toxin accumulation depend on the synchrony between host susceptibility during flowering and favourable temperature and moisture conditions. Climate therefore influences mycotoxin risk indirectly, by modifying fungal infection pressure, sporulation, and toxin production, rather than acting as a direct driver of contamination. Warm and humid conditions during flowering and early grain filling promote Fusarium infection and DON/ZEA biosynthesis, whereas excessively high temperatures or dry conditions can suppress fungal growth and toxin production. By aligning climate variables with phenology-derived flowering and post-flowering periods, the modelling framework captures these biologically relevant climate–host–pathogen interactions in a statistical manner. In contrast, mycotoxins such as aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) and fumonisins (FBs), which are mainly produced by Aspergillus flavus and Fusarium verticillioides, respectively, are of limited epidemiological relevance in temperate European wheat systems. These fungal species are better adapted to warmer and drier environments and are more commonly associated with crops such as maize. While sporadic detections may occur, they do not represent dominant, field-driven contamination pathways in European wheat. T-2 and HT-2 toxins, associated primarily with F. langsethiae, are more frequently observed in oats than in wheat, which is consistent with the limited occurrence observed in the historical monitoring data used in this study. We emphasize that the present modelling framework does not explicitly simulate fungal population dynamics or toxin biosynthesis pathways. Instead, it infers contamination risk from observed outcomes conditioned on climate and phenology, implicitly integrating the combined effects of pathogen biology, host susceptibility, and environmental conditions. This approach enables continental-scale assessment of climate-related food safety risks while acknowledging that more detailed mechanistic crop–pathogen models would be required to resolve species-specific infection dynamics.

Our study shows phenology shifts under climate change. These are probably mainly due to alternation in metabolic activities of nearby soil temperature6,15,52,53. Despite the phenology dates shifts, the length of the period between FD and HD remained unchanged in all scenarios, in line with previous studies reporting a maximum period variation of two days under climate change6,54.

For the variables of high precipitation, low rainfall, high temperature around FD and HD, wheat development stages (FD and HD), showed the highest effect on the high mycotoxin contamination class. Our findings are in line with other studies: McDonald, et al.55 reported that the wheat development stage was critical for fungal growth and crop yield, surpassing all other crop-related factors, such as soil and wheat variety. Other studies showed that high relative humidity and rainfall around FD, during grain development, and grain ripening, greatly influenced DON accumulation in wheat, oats, and spring barley56.

In 2050, Southern Europe is expected to experience a decline of up to 49% in wheat yields, while the decrease may be lower in Northern Europe due to higher atmospheric CO2 concentrations and changing rainfall patterns30. In contrast, at the end-of-century (2069–2099), Jägermeyr, et al.57 projected global wheat productivity response as 18% higher under SSP5-8.5, and 9% higher under SSP1-2.6. The positive wheat productivity response is explained by a comparatively stronger CO2 response and the fact that global warming leads to wheat yield increases at high-latitude regions which are currently temperature-limited58,59. However, these negatively and positively projected wheat yield could be offset by mycotoxin contamination, resulting in production losses via downgrading. Therefore, food safety and crop yield need to be jointly considered in projections of food security to determine suitable growing regions for winter wheat in the future.

Moreover, climate change may be associated with sustained wheat yield losses due to pests and pathogens60–62. For example, Savary, et al.63 estimated yield losses due to 137 pathogens and pests of five major crops (including wheat) globally and in food security hotspots. Future studies could consider these factors as well to fully evaluate production loss, food safety and food security. The indicated increase in mycotoxin contamination in wheat due to changing climate conditions could lead to higher pesticide usage to control the associated fungal pathogens. An increased pesticide usage could lead to elevated levels of pesticide residues, contributing to additional challenges in food safety management. Pesticide residues already are a significant factor in the rejection of wheat grain for both livestock feed and human consumption, and this risk could be aggravated by climate-driven increases in mycotoxins64. Integrating data on pesticide use alongside mycotoxin contamination models would provide a more complete view of wheat safety under future climate scenarios. Further studies should explore sustainable pesticide use strategies, including integrated pest management, to manage both mycotoxin risks and pesticide residues, while safeguarding crop yield and quality.

In this study, Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) are implemented through their associated climate trajectories and are not used to explicitly model socio-economic processes. Nevertheless, SSP narratives provide a useful interpretative framework for contextualising projected mycotoxin risks within broader food-system futures, as climate-driven contamination interacts with agricultural management, governance capacity, and trade structures. SSPs differ in their underlying assumptions regarding socio-economic development, institutional capacity, technological progress, and inequality, which in turn influence adaptive capacity in agricultural and food safety systems. Pathways characterised by high adaptive capacity and effective governance (e.g. SSP1) are generally associated with greater potential for investment in crop management, disease control, monitoring infrastructure, and regulatory enforcement, which may mitigate some climate-driven increases in mycotoxin risk. Conversely, pathways characterised by constrained adaptive capacity and fragmented governance (e.g. SSP3) may face greater challenges in managing climate-related food safety hazards, potentially amplifying the impacts of increased pathogen pressure on wheat. SSP narratives also imply long-term changes in land use and crop geography, which may alter exposure of wheat production systems to climatic conditions conducive to mycotoxin contamination. Shifts in wheat cultivation towards higher latitudes or marginal environments, as projected under some scenarios, could modify pathogen–host–climate interactions and associated risk profiles. In addition, differences in trade integration across SSPs may influence how contamination risk is distributed through food and feed supply chains, affecting where food safety impacts are ultimately realised. While these socio-economic dimensions are not quantitatively represented in the present modelling framework, their consideration highlights that future mycotoxin risk is shaped by the interaction between climate change and food-system resilience. Integrating representations of agricultural management, trade, and regulatory capacity with climate–phenology–mycotoxin models represents an important direction for future research aimed at more comprehensive assessment of food safety under climate change.

The analysis in this article has limitations related to data used and approaches to scenario assessments. The analysis is limited to four SSP datasets. It would be useful to consider scenarios generated by different institutes, and different initial conditions, to produce a better overview and account for uncertainty in climate projections. Meanwhile, the current four scenarios cover a wide range of possible futures and therefore capture much of the variability relevant for this study. Another limitation of this study relates to the spatial resolution mismatch between historical contamination data and projected outputs. Because model training relies on country-level annual averages, fine-scale spatial heterogeneity in historical contamination cannot be directly learned, and grid-level projections necessarily reflect covariate-driven inference rather than observed sub-national variability. This introduces a risk of ecological fallacy if results are interpreted as local predictions. We therefore emphasize that the spatial maps are intended to illustrate relative regional gradients and shifts in contamination risk under climate change, not precise field-level outcomes. Improved access to harmonised sub-national monitoring data would be essential for validating and refining fine-scale spatial patterns in future work. Furthermore, this study is subject to multiple sources of uncertainty arising from climate projections, phenology modelling, and data aggregation. Climate-related uncertainty is partially constrained by the use of a single CMIP6 model and realisation, which does not capture inter-model spread. Additional uncertainty arises from phefnology model parameterisation and from the limited spatial and temporal resolution of historical contamination data. While these uncertainties limit the precision of absolute contamination estimates, the integrated framework remains valuable for assessing relative changes in risk and for exploring how climate-driven shifts in crop development may alter food safety hazards under different future scenarios. The observed contamination data are derived from monitoring program within a single national control system rather than from harmonised domestic surveillance in each European country. Country-level contamination estimates therefore reflect the contamination status of wheat entering the Dutch market from different origins, which may not fully represent domestic production conditions or surveillance practices in exporting countries. As a result, spatial projections should be interpreted as indicative of relative contamination risk associated with climatic and phenological conditions in wheat-producing regions, rather than as direct representations of national monitoring outcomes.

Future research could build upon the present framework by integrating harmonised European surveillance data, such as those compiled by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). In addition, this study did not account for climate extremes such as heavy rainfall and extreme temperature, which could cause extreme high mycotoxin occurrence and production downgrade losses7,31,53,65. Extending the modelling framework to explicitly account for climate extremes and compound events, such as heatwaves combined with drought or intense precipitation during sensitive phenological stages, would allow a more comprehensive assessment of mycotoxin risk under future climate variability. Incorporating these elements would help to better capture high-impact, low-probability contamination events and further enhance the relevance of climate-aware food safety risk assessments.

Our study has developed a scenario projection framework for exploring food safety risks under climate change. This framework is broadly applicable to various food safety concerns, and in this study we exemplify it over mycotoxin contamination in winter wheat.

To conclude, this study develops and applies a phenology-aware machine-learning framework to assess future mycotoxin contamination risk in European wheat under CMIP6 climate and socio-economic scenarios. The principal contribution of this work lies in the integration of crop developmental dynamics with spatially explicit climate projections and data-driven modelling, enabling a continent-scale, scenario-consistent assessment of climate-related food safety risk.

Application of the framework suggests that mycotoxin contamination risk in European wheat may increase under future climate conditions, with deoxynivalenol emerging as the dominant hazard across scenarios. The magnitude and timing of projected risk vary by scenario, with indicated increases occurring around mid-century under lower-forcing pathways and shifting towards the end of the century under high-emission scenarios. Spatially, the results point to a redistribution of risk across Europe, with coastal regions, the United Kingdom, and parts of northern France showing comparatively larger increases in the frequency of high-risk contamination. These patterns are consistently associated with climate-driven changes in temperature, humidity, precipitation, and crop phenology, underscoring the relevance of explicitly accounting for crop developmental timing when assessing climate impacts on food safety. A simplification of the modelling framework is the assumption of uniform wheat distribution across the study area. In reality, wheat production is spatially heterogeneous, and this assumption may influence the interpretation of the spatial patterns. As a result, the mapped outputs should be interpreted as reflecting environmental suitability for contamination rather than actual exposure risk associated with local production intensity.

Beyond the specific application to wheat and mycotoxins, the approach presented here offers a transferable methodology for climate-aware food safety assessment in systems where mechanistic understanding is incomplete and monitoring data are spatially heterogeneous. By coupling phenology-informed feature construction with machine learning and climate scenarios, this framework provides a scalable tool for exploring how climate change may reshape food safety risks across crops, regions, and hazard types. Such integrated approaches are essential for supporting forward-looking food safety governance and for embedding food safety considerations within broader assessments of agricultural adaptation and food system resilience.

Methods

Climate data

This study uses simulated future climate data and historical weather data. Simulated climate data for the years 2010 to 2100 was obtained from the CMIP6 multi-model output66, encompassing control experiment and four Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs)40 (for more details, see supplementary Table 2). Climate projections were derived from a single CMIP6 global climate model and one realisation for each scenario. The resulting projections should therefore be interpreted as scenario-conditional outcomes rather than as an assessment of the full range of climate model uncertainty.

The SSPs consist of narratives of future socio-economic development supported by quantitative data on a wide range of topics, such as population size, urbanization rates, income, energy consumption and production, agriculture and land use, emissions, and weather variables, among others. For this study, a range of feasible scenarios were considered, including: a sustainable scenario (SSP1-2.6); a fossil fuel development scenario (SSP5-8.5); a scenario with low socioeconomic challenges for mitigation and adaptation (SSP3-7.0); and a middle-of-the-road scenario (SSP2-4.5)67. Simulated daily weather data (selected climate variables, as listed below) was considered for each grid point, with a grid resolution of 25 km x 25 km over the European region. Observed daily climate data for Europe were obtained from the European Commission Joint Research Centre (JRC) for the period 2010–2020. These data covered various meteorological variables from weather stations interpolated to 25 km × 25 km grid cells. Data are available via the Agri4Cast data portal (agri4cast.jrc.ec.europa.eu/DataPortal/).

The derived daily climate variables from both data sources included: Daily Maximum Air Temperature (TMAX), Daily Minimum Air Temperature (TMIN), Daily Average Air Temperature (TAV), Daily Total Rainfall (PRT), and Daily Average Relative Humidity (RHAV). Vapour pressure (VAP) was calculated from daily mean air temperature and relative humidity using standard meteorological equations, by multiplying saturation vapour pressure by relative humidity (Allen, 1998).

For each climate variable, weekly mean values and cumulative sums were calculated for each grid cell over the period spanning wheat flowering to harvest, as defined by the phenology model (Section ‘Phenology parameters’). This aggregation was chosen to align short-term climate variability with biologically relevant crop development stages that are critical for fungal infection and mycotoxin accumulation, while reducing noise from daily fluctuations that are less informative at continental scale. Weeks falling outside this phenological window were excluded and labelled as “Out of Period,” reflecting the assumption that climate conditions outside the flowering-to-harvest interval have negligible direct influence on wheat mycotoxin contamination.

Phenology parameters

The Flowering Date (FD) and the Full Maturity Date (HD) of wheat grown in Europe were estimated at a 25 km x 25 km grid resolution. These estimates were derived using the outputs from the World Food Studies (WOFOST) model, developed as part of the EU Monitoring Agricultural ResourceS (MARS) Project68. The WOFOST model has been applied over the last 28 years as part of the Monitoring Agriculture with Remote Sensing (MARS) crop yield forecasting system and has been well validated using more than 48,000 observations (de Wit et al., 2019). WOFOST estimates FD and HD for each grid cell and crop type as the day in the year when the accumulated temperature (TSUM) since the crop emergence date reaches a certain temperature threshold. The thresholds for wheat FD and HD depend on the grid cell and crop type, and were downloaded from the JRC database for wheat grown in Europe, per grid cell. TSUM was computed annually using the daily increase in temperature sum and the base temperature. The calendar day (in a given year) when the TSUM reached the FD threshold in a particular grid cell was considered as FD, while the day when the TSUM reached the HD threshold was set as the HD. When either the HD or both FD and HD were not reached, “No Record” was recorded. Flowering week was defined as the calendar week corresponding to the simulated flowering date produced by the phenology model for each grid cell and year. Post-flowering weeks were defined as the consecutive calendar weeks following flowering up to the simulated harvest date. Climate variables were aggregated over these phenology-defined periods to ensure alignment with wheat developmental stages most relevant for fungal infection and mycotoxin accumulation.

Mycotoxin monitoring data

Historical monitoring data for the presence of mycotoxins in winter wheat cultivated in Europe from 2010 to 2020 was obtained from the Dutch official control program for animal feed. These instrumental analyses were carried out at Wageningen Food Safety Research, the Netherlands. Data include 2818 records related to analytical results for concentrations of mycotoxins in wheat samples, together with their respective annual Limits Of Quantification (LOQs). Data contain concentrations for each of six types of mycotoxins: Deoxynivalenol (DON), Zearalenone (ZEA), T-2 and HT-2 toxins (T2 + HT2), Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1), Fumonisins (FBs), and Ochratoxin A (OTA). This monitoring program performs routine chemical analyses of feed and feed ingredients placed on the Dutch market to ensure compliance with EU food and feed safety legislation. Each record corresponds to the laboratory analysis of a single sample and includes detailed metadata, including sampling date, analysis date, product name(here, grain product), hazard type (here, mycotoxins), reported country of origin of the commodity, analytical method information, limits of quantification, and the measured concentration with corresponding units. Although sampling and analysis are conducted within the Netherlands, the monitored feed ingredients are sourced internationally. Consequently, the dataset includes analytical results for wheat originating from a wide range of countries. In this study, contamination outcomes were associated with the reported country of origin of each sample and subsequently aggregated to the country–year level. No extrapolation, imputation, or spatial redistribution of contamination values across countries was performed. Differences in sampling intensity, analytical methods, and limits of quantification across time are inherent to official control data and are explicitly acknowledged as a source of uncertainty. The official control program itself is publicly documented, while access to detailed sample-level analytical data is subject to regulatory data-sharing conditions.

Mycotoxin monitoring data was available for wheat samples at country level for Belgium, Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, The Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Sweden, Turkey, Ukraine, and the United Kingdom. For each of these countries, the average annual mycotoxin concentration was used to represent the mycotoxin contamination in all grid cells of the respective country. Consequently, all observations within a given country and year share the same contamination label during model training. Sub-national variability in contamination is therefore not directly observed in the training data but is implicitly inferred through spatially explicit covariates, including climate variables and phenology-derived crop development metrics. Wheat distribution and exposure were assumed to be uniform across the study area due to the absence of spatially explicit, harmonized data at the resolution required for the modelling framework.

Data processing and linkage

Mycotoxin concentrations were processed into three different levels: low (≤ respective LOQ set to zero), medium (> LOQ and < respective threshold value), and high (≥ respective threshold value). Thresholds values were set at one tenth (0.1) of the respective maximum limit (ML) or guidance value for the corresponding mycotoxin. The EC maximum limit for aflatoxins (0.02 mg/kg for AFB1) and the guidance value for the other mycotoxins (60 mg/kg for FBs, 8 mg/kg for DON, 2 mg/kg for ZEA, 0.25 mg/kg for OTA, 0.1 mg/kg for T2 + HT2) in wheat in feed were linked to the respective mycotoxins. For further description of the dataset and data processing, please refer to Wang et al.34.

Following contamination classification, individual sample results were aggregated to the country–year level based on the reported country of origin. For each country and year, contamination classes were summarised across all available samples to derive annual country-level contamination outcomes used as the response variable in model training. Climate variables were first processed at the grid-cell level and summarised on a weekly basis within the phenology-defined window between wheat flowering and harvest. Phenological dates were derived independently for each grid cell and year and used to ensure that climate features reflected conditions relevant to fungal infection and mycotoxin accumulation. Grid-level climate and phenology features were then associated with the corresponding country–year contamination outcome, resulting in a harmonised dataset in which each observation represents a country–year combination characterised by spatially explicit climate and phenological conditions. This unified dataset formed the basis for model training, cross-validation, and future scenario projections.

Scenario projection model

The input variables for the development of the scenario projection model for mycotoxins in wheat at the European scale included: 1) Climate variables: weekly average and weekly accumulated sum of TMAX, TMIN, TAV, PRT, VAP, and RHAV in the period between wheat FD and HD per grid cell; 2) Phenology variables: wheat Flowering Date (FD), wheat Maturity Date (HD), and length of the period between flowering and full maturity (in number of days); 3) Mycotoxin type (one of the six possible mycotoxins).

The output variables included the contamination class (low, medium, and high) of mycotoxin contamination in European wheat per grid cell. We assumed all grids in Europe have the same amount of wheat yield and that all wheat grown in the European region was suitable for human consumption. The data units of the different data sources and the requirements of the models are shown in supplementary Table 1.

Model development and internal and external validation followed a similar approach, as described by Wang et al.34. In short, a machine learning module was developed to project the mycotoxin contamination class (low, medium, high) in European wheat at grid level using the above-mentioned variables. The dataset was divided into three sets for model training, internal validation, and external validation. The training dataset was randomly created containing 80% of the data between the years 2010 and 2018. The testing dataset was created with the remaining 20% and used for internal validation. Data from 2019 and 2020, which was not used in model training and internal validation, was used for external validation. The machine learning algorithm eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGB) was used to develop the scenario projection model. To address class imbalance in contamination levels, we employed the inherent class-weighting mechanism available in the XGBoost framework, whereby misclassification penalties are adjusted inversely proportional to class frequencies during model training. This weighting strategy increases the influence of medium and high contamination observations on the optimisation process without altering the underlying data distribution. The model was trained using the CMIP6 historical data from 2010–2018, with fivefold cross validation. External validation was performed using two different datasets: CMIP6 data for 2019-2020 and JRC data from 2010–2020. These datasets allowed the evaluation of the performance of the model for projecting mycotoxin contamination classes in future years (not used for model training). After the validation, the developed model was applied for estimating future mycotoxin contamination of wheat under climate projection scenarios from 2020-2100. The model development process is shown in Fig. 5. When applied to spatially gridded climate and phenology inputs, the trained model generates grid-level projections that reflect relative differences in contamination risk driven by spatial gradients in environmental and crop development conditions. These projections should not be interpreted as precise local contamination estimates but rather as model-inferred spatial patterns conditional on country-level training data. The grid resolution is used to explore potential regional shifts and emerging risk gradients under future climate scenarios, rather than to provide site-specific projection. The machine-learning classifier generates class membership probabilities, which are subsequently mapped to discrete contamination classes for interpretability and consistency with regulatory thresholds. EXplainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) was used to provide understandable and interpretable explanations for the decisions of the Artificial Intelligence (AI) models. One approach of XAI, Feature Importance Analysis, was applied using the Tree SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) algorithm69,70 to reflect the drivers of mycotoxin contamination in wheat. Spatial projection map generation

The mycotoxin contamination classes in wheat in Europe are shown as risk maps using georeferenced grid points. On the baseline map, only countries with historical mycotoxin contamination data are shown (see Section 2.1.1). However, projections of the mycotoxin contamination classes were estimated in wheat growing countries according to the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) (https://openknowledge.fao.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/dc1b16d6-44af-4d50-9544-10fcb8a3779f/content): Belgium, Czech Republic, Denmark, France, Germany, Hungary, Italy, Latvia, The Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Serbia, Sweden, Turkey, Ukraine, and The United Kingdom, Lithuania, Spain, and Bulgaria (the final three countries were countries without historical mycotoxin contamination data). The coverage of the scenario projection map of mycotoxin contamination was thus expanded in previously unassessed geographic areas. It is important to distinguish between observed contamination data and model-generated projections in this spatial analysis. Observed mycotoxin contamination data used for model training are derived exclusively from official control monitoring conducted within the Netherlands and are linked to the reported European countries of origin of sampled wheat. By contrast, contamination values presented for European countries (Lithuania, Spain, and Bulgaria) beyond the observed data coverage are model-generated projections obtained by applying the trained model to climate and phenology inputs for FAO-defined wheat-growing areas, rather than direct observations from national surveillance systems. Accordingly, the resulting maps represent relative contamination risk patterns inferred from climate–phenology relationships and should not be interpreted as observed national contamination levels.
