Oral microbiome dysbiosis mediates the link between emotional disorders and systemic inflammation in heart failure
Authors: Qingyang Wu, Ziqing Yu, Jinya Cao, Hanyu Li, Shuyang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmotional disorders (EDs) are prevalent in heart failure (HF) and predict poor outcomes, yet their interplay with oral health and systemic inflammation remains unclear. This integrated cross-sectional analysis of NHANES data and a prospective oral microbiome cohort revealed that EDs were significantly associated with poorer cardiovascular health (Life's Essential 8 score), an effect partially mediated by tooth loss and self-rated oral health. Furthermore, HF patients with EDs exhibited distinct oral dysbiosis, marked by depletion of beneficial bacteria (e.g., Cardiobacterium) and enrichment of pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g., Megasphaera), which correlated with systemic inflammation. Our findings posit that EDs may impact cardiovascular health by disrupting the oral microbiome and promoting inflammation, highlighting a potential psychoneuroimmunological pathway in HF progression.Keywords: Emotional disorders, Heart failure, Oral microbiome

Introduction

Heart failure (HF), a complex clinical syndrome, has emerged as a critical global public health issue. Its high prevalence, frequent hospital readmissions, and persistently elevated mortality rates impose a substantial burden on healthcare systems (Manzi et al., 2024; McDonagh et al., 2022; Savarese et al., 2023). Despite remarkable advances in pharmacological therapies and improved management strategies, the prognosis of HF remains poor, with a five-year survival rate still below 50% (Sayour et al., 2024). The complexity and heterogeneity of HF imply that its pathogenesis and progression cannot be fully explained by traditional risk factors such as hypertension, coronary artery disease, and diabetes. In recent years, growing attention has been directed toward the role of non-traditional risk factors, particularly psychosocial factors and oral health, in the pathophysiology of HF, offering new perspectives for its comprehensive management.

Epidemiological data indicate that emotional disorders (EDs)—primarily comprising depression and anxiety—affects over 30% of individuals with cardiovascular diseases (CVD) (Karami et al., 2023). EDs may influence the progression of HF through multiple pathways, including behavioral mechanisms and physiological mechanisms (Guo et al., 2023; Martínez et al., 2021). However, the precise and comprehensive mechanisms by which EDs affect HF remain incompletely understood. In particular, it remains unclear whether they exert indirect effects via modulation of other physiological systems, such as the oral microbiome.

Meanwhile, a bidirectional association between oral health and cardiovascular disease has garnered extensive research support (Ruff et al., 2020). As the largest mucosal surface in the human body, the homeostasis of the oral microbiome is crucial for systemic health. Under physiological conditions, a symbiotic equilibrium exists between oral microorganisms and the host. Its disruption—oral dysbiosis—can lead to local diseases like periodontitis and contribute to CVD pathogenesis via systemic inflammation, bacterial invasion, and autoimmune responses (Arifuzzaman et al., 2024; Luo et al., 2022). A large-scale cohort study in England, following 64,000 patients with periodontal disease, demonstrated an 18% increased risk of CVD (Zemedikun et al., 2021). Furthermore, specific oral microbial features are emerging as potential diagnostic biomarkers for HF, supporting a direct role for the oral microbiota in its pathophysiology.

A particularly intriguing phenomenon is the close and likely bidirectional interaction between EDs and oral health. Psychological stress and negative emotions may disrupt the stability of the oral microbiome by affecting autonomic nervous regulation, neuroendocrine-immune responses, and behavioral patterns, thereby promoting the proliferation of opportunistic pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum (Pedersen et al., 2018). On the other hand, poor oral health—manifested as halitosis, tooth loss, or other conditions—can lead to impaired masticatory function, social avoidance, and diminished self-image, which may further exacerbate psychological distress and negative emotions, potentially forming a vicious cycle (Ball and Darby, 2022). The traditional Chinese medicine theory that “the heart opens into the tongue” offers a unique perspective on this relationship. Clinical observations have revealed that patients with coronary heart disease and HF often present with characteristic tongue features such as a dark-purple tongue body with a yellow-greasy coating. Moreover, the microbial architecture of the tongue coating in these patients has been shown to differ significantly from that of healthy individuals (Zhu et al., 2025).

Although pairwise associations among EDs, HF, and oral health are established, their integration into a unified framework remains limited. It is unclear whether EDs act as a bridge linking oral health to HF progression. This study aims to investigate this interaction network through a multi-level approach. We hypothesize that EDs mediate the impact of poor oral health on HF progression, potentially by disrupting oral microbiota homeostasis and exacerbating systemic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. To test this, we analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine epidemiological associations among EDs, oral health indicators, and cardiovascular risk profiles. Concurrently, we applied high-throughput sequencing (16S rRNA and ITS) to compare the oral microbiomes of HF patients with and without EDs, identifying microbiota signatures linked to emotional states. We anticipate that this study will establish a novel bio-psycho-social intervention strategy, integrating psychological and oral health management into HF care, thereby providing new perspectives and therapeutic targets for improving patient prognosis.

Material and methods

Design of NHANES

We analyzed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey assessing the health of the noninstitutionalized U.S. population (Liu et al., 2018). It employs a complex, multistage sampling design for robustness and collects demographic, examination, laboratory, and dietary data. All data and documentation are publicly available. This study followed STROBE guidelines and utilized de-identified data, with participant confidentiality protected by anonymous sequence numbers. The NHANES protocol was approved by an ethics review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Oral health and cardiovascular health variables

Three indicators were selected to assess oral health status in this study. The first indicator was periodontitis, which was evaluated based on clinical attachment loss (AL) and probing depth (PD) (Manresa et al., 2018). Licensed dental examiners performed a full-mouth examination, measuring AL and PD at six sites per tooth in eligible participants (Page and Eke, 2007). Periodontitis was defined according to the CDC/AAP classification criteria established by Eke et al. (2012) (Eke et al., 2012)(refer to Supplementary Table 2 for detailed CDC/AAP classification). Based on this criterion, participants were categorized as having no, mild, moderate, or severe periodontitis. The second indicator was tooth loss, assessed through clinical examination by dental professionals. The third indicator was self-rated oral health, derived from the oral health questionnaire. Specifically, item OHQ845 (“Rate the health of your teeth and gums”) was used. Responses were recategorized from the original five-level scale into a binary variable: “Good” (combining “Excellent”, “Very good”, and “Good”) and “Poor” (combining “Fair” and “Poor”).

Given its established relevance to HF, this study adopted the American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8 (LE8) as the primary cardiovascular health (CVH) metric (Lloyd-Jones et al., 2022). Higher LE8 scores are strongly associated with lower HF risk (Liu et al., 2025). The LE8 score integrates eight components across health behaviors (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep) and health factors (BMI, non-HDL cholesterol, blood glucose, blood pressure). Each component is scored from 0 to 100, and the overall LE8 score is the unweighted average of these eight metrics. Participants were subsequently classified into high (80-100), moderate (50-79), or low (0-49) CVH categories based on established thresholds (Lloyd-Jones et al., 2022; Reedy et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2023).

Assessment of EDs

In this study, EDs was operationalized as the presence of anxiety and/or depressive states. Following consultation with specialists in psychosomatic medicine, anxiety state and/or depressive state were selected as the defining features of EDs, both of which can be objectively assessed using well-validated scales. The patient health questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), available in the NHANES database, was scored as follows: 0–4 (none), 5–9 (mild), 10–14 (moderate), 15–19 (moderately severe), and 20–27 (severe). A score of ≥5 was used to define the presence of at least mild depressive symptoms or a depressive state (Kroenke et al., 2001). Anxiety was evaluated using item HSQ496: “Over the last 30 days, how many days have you felt anxious?”. Responses were dichotomized such that participants reporting anxiety on 7–30 days were classified as having an anxious state, while those reporting 0–6 days were considered non-anxious (Gui et al., 2023). A threshold of ≥7 days was applied to define at least mild anxiety. Participants meeting either the depressive state criterion (PHQ-9≥5) or the anxious state criterion (≥7 anxious days) were classified as having EDs.

Covariate and mediator variable selection

This study considered the inclusion of covariates covering a broad range of sociodemographic characteristics. These included age, sex (male and female), race and ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, other races), marital status (married/cohabiting with partner, never married/widowed/divorced/separated), number of household members (>3, 1-3), annual household income (<$20,000, $20,000-$34,999, $35,000-$74,999, ≥$75,000), educational level (less than high school, high school graduate or equivalent, some college or above), and alcohol use status (yes, no).

To better elucidate the relationship between oral health and cardiovascular health, systemic inflammation was selected as a mediator for investigation. Based on established literature, the systemic immune-inflammation index (SII) was chosen as the quantitative measure. The SII is calculated as follows: (platelet count × neutrophil count)/lymphocyte count (Hu et al., 2014).

Study population and inclusion/exclusion criteria

NHANES part: After variable selection, we aimed to include participants with complete data for all variables of interest within the same NHANES cycle. Therefore, data from the 2009–2012 cycles (two consecutive cycles) were selected, as only these years contained all necessary measures for LE8 assessment, periodontal disease classification, oral health questionnaires, and anxiety-related items. The initial population comprised 20,293 participants from the 2009–2012 surveys. The exclusion criteria included missing data and pregnancy. After applying these criteria, a total of 4823 participants were included in the final analysis. A detailed flowchart of the inclusion and exclusion process is presented in Fig. 1.

Microbiome part

Participants for the oral microbiome study were recruited between December 2024 and January 2025. Suspected HF cases were initially screened based on symptoms and signs. Exclusion criteria included one or more of the following: 1) evidence of acute myocardial infarction; 2) cardiac surgery within the past 6 months; 3) systemic diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus or malignant tumors; 4) end-stage renal disease (eGFR<15 mL/min/1.73 m2 as per CKD-EPI equation); or 5) antibiotic use for more than 3 days within the past 3 months. HF patients were identified and diagnosed in accordance with the 2021 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) guidelines (McDonagh et al., 2022), based on a combination of symptoms, signs, imaging results, and biomarker assessment, and was validated by at least two experienced cardiologists. Exercise capacity was evaluated according to the New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classification. EDs were assessed using the standardized PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scales. HF patients were classified into those with EDs (HFEmotion group) if they scored≥5 on either scale, and those without EDs (HFNon group) if they scored below these thresholds. Healthy controls (HC) were recruited based on the absence of HF diagnosis and significant emotional disorder symptoms. A total of 28 participants were enrolled, forming three groups for final analysis: healthy controls (HC group, n = 10), heart failure patients without EDs (HFNon group, n = 7), and heart failure patients with EDs (HFEmotion group, n = 11). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. For HF patients, detailed demographic, clinical, laboratory, and echocardiographic data were obtained from the hospital's electronic medical record system. Oral health status (including caries, denture use, and missing teeth) for all participants was recorded via structured face-to-face interviews.

Microbiome sample processing

Supragingival plaque samples were collected from 28 subjects (18 HF patients and 10 healthy controls) using sterile swabs. To standardize the baseline microbial community, participants were instructed to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), brushing teeth, or using any oral cleansers for at least 2 h prior to sampling. Immediately before collection, all participants rinsed their mouths with 0.9% saline solution for 30 s to remove loose debris. Collected plaque samples were placed in sterile cryotubes, immediately placed on dry ice, and transferred to the laboratory. All samples were permanently frozen at −80 °C within 2 h of collection until DNA extraction. Genomic DNA was extracted via the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the ITS1–1F region of the fungal ITS gene were amplified and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a PE250 strategy. The detailed sequencing protocol can be seen in Supplementary Methods part.

For data processing, primer and barcode trimming, paired-end read merging, and quality filtering were performed using FLASH (version 1.2.11, http://ccb.jhu.edu/software/FLASH/) (Magoč and Salzberg, 2011) and fastp (version 0.23.1) (Bokulich et al., 2013). Chimeric sequences were detected and removed by aligning reads against the Silva (16S) and UNITE (ITS) databases(Silva database, https://www.arb-silva.de/for 16S/18S, Unite database, https://unite.ut.ee/for ITS) with the UCHIME algorithm (Edgar et al., 2011). High-quality reads were denoised into amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) using DADA2 in QIIME2 (version 202202). ASVs with a total count below 5 were filtered out. Taxonomic assignment was performed based on the Silva 138.1 and UNITE v9.0 databases, and phylogenetic relationships were inferred via multiple sequence alignment. Absolute ASV abundances were rarefied to the minimum sequence count per sample for subsequent bioinformatic analyses.

Statistical analysis, sequencing data analysis and visualization

NHANES part: All analyses incorporate sampling weights. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics: categorical variables are presented as numbers and weighted percentages, and continuous variables as means with their corresponding standard errors (SE). The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For normally distributed variables, group comparisons were performed using the two-sample t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, for more than two groups). Weighted logistic regression models, adjusted for sociodemographic and clinical covariates, were employed to assess associations, presenting results as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Stratified subgroup analyses were conducted to examine potential variations in the association between EDs and oral health outcomes across different population groups. Mediation analysis was performed to assess whether SII and oral health indicators mediated the relationship between EDs and cardiovascular health (Fig. 2). To examine potential nonlinear relationships, restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were integrated into the regression models, with knots determined by the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) (Johannesen et al., 2020). Sex-specific RCS analyses were also conducted.

The Mediation Relation

The analysis involved estimating three paths: (a) exposure (emotional disorders) to mediator, (b) mediator to outcome (LE8 score), and (c’) exposure to outcome (direct effect). The total effect was decomposed into direct (mediated) effects. The proportion of the total was calculated as (indirect effect/total effect) × 100.

Microbiome part: Continuous and categorical variables were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, median [IQR], or counts [%]) informed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Two-group comparisons were performed using t-tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or chi-square/Fisher's exact tests as applicable. For multiple groups, ANOVA (with post-hoc corrections) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied. To identify dominant taxa, ternary plots of top species/genera were generated (Bulgarelli et al., 2015), and differential abundance was tested (Kruskal–Wallis with Nemenyi post-hoc and Holm-Bonferroni correction). Function was predicted via Bugbase (bacteria) and FUNGuild (fungi), with pathogenic potential visualized in a clustered heatmap.

All bioinformatic visualizations were generated using R software (version 4.4.2) in the RStudio environment. A full list of databases and software tool can be seen from Supplementary Table 1.

Results

Characteristics of the NHANES study population

A total of 4823 participants were included in the NHANES analysis. Table 1 summarizes the baseline characteristics stratified by the presence or absence of EDs. Participants with EDs were significantly younger than those without (p < 0.0001) and had lower LE8 scores (p < 0.0001). The group with EDs also had a higher proportion of females (p < 0.001), a greater likelihood of living alone (p = 0.03), lower educational attainment (p < 0.0001), and lower average annual household income (p < 0.0001). Additionally, a history of alcohol use was more common in this group (p = 0.02). Regarding oral health variables, the presence of EDs was not significantly associated with the severity (p = 0.09) or categorical classification (p = 0.85) of periodontitis. However, participants with EDs reported significantly poorer self-rated oral health (p < 0.0001). They also exhibited a higher number of missing teeth (p = 0.01) and a greater proportion of tooth loss (p = 0.01).

Weighted multivariable logistic regression and stratified analysis

Weighted logistic regression models were constructed to evaluate the associations between EDs and CVH, as well as oral health indicators (Table 2). All models included the following fixed covariates: EDs, age, sex, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational level, number of household members, annual household income, alcohol use, and SII. For models with CVH (assessed by LE8 score) as the outcome, additional adjustments were made for oral health variables, including periodontitis status, self-rated oral health, and number of missing teeth. Conversely, models with oral health indicators as outcomes were further adjusted for LE8 score. The presence of EDs was significantly associated with a decrease in LE8 score (Table 2A, p < 0.0001). As more covariates were incrementally included in the model, the weighted estimates initially decreased and then increased, suggesting that fuller adjustment for confounders refined the precision of the association between EDs and LE8 score. Regarding oral health outcomes, EDs were significantly associated with a greater number of missing teeth (Table 2B, p = 0.005) and poorer self-rated oral health (Table 2B, p < 0.001). However, no statistically significant association was observed between EDs and periodontitis (Table 2B, p = 0.53).

Stratified analyses demonstrated that significant differences in the prevalence of EDs were observed across CVH categories (high, moderate, low) in nearly all subgroups (Table 3A). However, no statistically significant trend was identified in the following subgroups: age≥65 years (p-trend = 0.24), annual household income>$75,000 (p-trend = 0.22), former smokers (p-trend = 0.12), and never drinkers (p-trend = 0.26). In the subgroup analysis stratified by the presence of EDs, significantly greater tooth loss was observed among participants with EDs in the following subgroups: age <65 years (p = 0.01), female (p = 0.01), black individuals (p = 0.05),married/with partner (p = 0.02), education beyond high school (p = 0.02), family size 1-3(p = 0.02), annual household income >$75,000 (p = 0.04), never smokers (p = 0.02), current smokers (p = 0.03), current drinkers (p = 0.003), low CVH (p = 0.03), and high CVH (p = 0.001) (Table 3B). A significant association was found between EDs and periodontitis only in the “Other race” subgroup (p = 0.03) and among former smokers (p = 0.03). Interestingly, in these subgroups, the presence of EDs was associated with a lower prevalence of periodontitis (Table 3C). Self-rated oral health scores were significantly lower in most subgroups among participants with EDs. No significant differences were observed in the following subgroups: age≥65 years (p = 0.09), Black individuals (p = 0.05), Other race (p = 0.06), annual household income $20,000–$35,000 (p = 0.09), former smokers (p = 0.08), former drinkers (p = 0.20), and the high CVH group (p = 0.12) (Table 3D).

Mediation analysis

As shown in Table 4, the mediation effect of SII between EDs and CVH was not statistically significant (p = 0.16). In contrast, oral health indicators—specifically, tooth loss and self-rated oral health—significantly mediated this relationship (Table 5). Tooth loss accounted for 1.93% (p = 0.02) of the total effect of EDs on LE8 score, indicating a partial mediating role in the association between EDs and CVH. However, the direct effect of EDs remained the dominant component of the total observed effect. Self-rated oral health mediated 9.55% (p < 0.0001) of the total effect of EDs on LE8 score. The presence of EDs was associated with an average decrease of 0.5 points in the LE8 score mediated through self-rated oral health. No significant mediating effect was observed for periodontitis (p = 0.78) in the pathway from EDs to LE8 score.

RCS analysis

The association between LE8 score and EDs was first evaluated using RCS curves (Fig. 3A and B). A significant linear inverse relationship was observed in the overall population (p for overall<0.05, p for nonlinear = 0.07), indicating that the prevalence of EDs decreased progressively with increasing LE8 scores. A similar linear and inverse association was identified in females (p for overall<0.05, p for nonlinear = 0.06). In contrast, among males, the relationship between LE8 score and EDs was nonlinear (p for overall<0.05, p for nonlinear<0.05). A transition point was observed at an LE8 score of 67.5. Below this threshold, the prevalence of EDs decreased with increasing LE8 scores. Above 67.5, however, the prevalence initially increased and then began to decrease again after a second transition point around a score of 78, which remains within the moderate CVH range. It should be noted that the confidence intervals widened substantially in this region, crossing the reference line, rendering this segment of the association statistically nonsignificant.

To further identify whether EDs exert a negative influence on the oral microbiome of HF patients, we performed multi-group differential abundance analysis at the genus level (Fig. 4C–D), focusing on two abundance patterns: “HC > HFNon > HFEmotion” and “HFEmotion > HFNon > HC”. Discussion

This study is the first to integrate EDs, oral health, and the progression of HF within a unified “Brain-Oral-Heart” axis framework. It moves beyond the traditional biomedical model by expanding the perspective of HF management to include psychological and oral health dimensions. Both EDs and oral health issues are well-established risk factors for cardiovascular disease (Mommersteeg et al., 2016; Walther et al., 2022). If a potential pathway such as “EDs → oral microbiota dysbiosis → systemic inflammation/endothelial dysfunction → worsened HF” can be substantiated, it would provide a novel intervention target and a theoretical foundation for the comprehensive management of HF.

To test this hypothesis, we first utilized the NHANES database to investigate the relationships between EDs, oral health, and CVH. Our analysis revealed that EDs exerted a statistically significant impact on LE8 scores. Notably, this effect was mediated not only through objective oral health indicators (such as tooth loss) but also through the more subjective measure of self-rated oral health. This finding suggests that emotional management may ultimately influence overall CVH status by improving oral health. Furthermore, we conducted a microbiome study to directly compare the effects of EDs on the oral microbiota among HF patients. The results demonstrated that the presence of EDs was associated with an increase in potentially pathogenic bacteria and/or a decrease in beneficial bacteria within the oral microbiome.

Within the bacterial kingdom, Cardiobacterium is a genus of Gram-negative bacilli belonging to the family Enterobacteriaceae, and constitutes part of the normal microbiome of the human oral cavity and upper respiratory tract (Khaledi et al., 2022). Cardiobacterium species are generally considered opportunistic pathogens; while typically harmless and potentially even beneficial under normal conditions, they may cause infections—such as infective endocarditis—under specific circumstances (e.g., immune suppression or microbial dysbiosis), particularly certain species like C. hominis and C. valvarum (Savage et al., 1977). Our study demonstrated that the abundance of Cardiobacterium was highest in healthy controls, a finding consistent with previously reported characteristics of control groups (Liu et al., 2023). A higher abundance of Cardiobacterium in healthy individuals may indicate its role in maintaining oral microbial equilibrium and suppressing the growth of harmful bacteria, thereby reducing inflammatory responses and potentially supporting CVH. EDs may disrupt this balance via neuroendocrine pathways that impair immune function and alter microbial composition (Guo et al., 2023), ultimately leading to a reduction in Cardiobacterium abundance.

The genus Propionivibrio, belonging to the order Clostridiales within the phylum Firmicutes, comprises gram-positive, facultatively anaerobic or microaerophilic bacteria. It is primarily found in the human oral cavity and gut, and its abundance was observed to be elevated in healthy participants in our study. Propionivibrio may contribute to host health by helping to maintain microecological balance in oral and intestinal environments through the production of short-chain fatty acids or other metabolic byproducts (Yang et al., 2022). Additionally, this genus may confer protective effects on CVH by modulating the host immune system and attenuating inflammatory responses (Bartolomaeus et al., 2019).

The genus Megasphaera comprises obligately anaerobic bacteria that may play multifaceted roles in the human body. On one hand, certain species are capable of producing butyrate, a short-chain fatty acid known to contribute to intestinal health (Visuthranukul et al., 2024). On the other hand, Megasphaera has also been associated with infections and dysbiosis, such as disruptions in the cervicovaginal microbial community (Molina et al., 2024). In our results, the abundance of Megasphaera was significantly higher in HF patients with EDs, suggesting a potential role in promoting microbial dysbiosis within the oral ecosystem in this susceptible population.

Among fungi, the family Malasseziaceae was found to be particularly abundant in HF patients with EDs. Malasseziaceae, belonging to the phylum Ascomycota, has previously been reported in the oral microbiota of pregnant individuals (Gilbert Klaczko et al., 2023). However, most research has focused on its role in skin colonization, where Malassezia species can trigger epithelial responses, activate innate immunity, and stimulate adaptive immune responses involving CD4+ T cells (Ianiri et al., 2022). Malassezia has also been implicated in intestinal dysbiosis. A study by Limon et al. demonstrated an association between certain Malassezia species and Crohn's disease, and showed that these fungi can induce ulcerative colitis in mouse models (Limon et al., 2019). Together, these studies underscore the pathogenic potential of Malasseziaceae. Under conditions of immune suppression or compromised host defense mechanisms—such as in HF patients, who often exhibit reduced immunity and heightened susceptibility to infection—the persistence of Malasseziaceae may promote chronic inflammation and increase the risk of further damage to oral tissues.

Based on the findings of our study, we compared our results with previous research investigating the relationship between HF and the oral microbiome. Oral microbiota's hazard to HF was described as travel problem which meant some acid-resistant oral microbiota can travel via the saliva from the mouth to the gut and induce local dysbiosis of gut microbiota (Tonelli et al., 2023). A 13-year of longitudinal follow-up demonstrated preliminary evidence of an association between HF and Treponema denticola and Porphyromonas gingivalis (Yuzefpolskaya et al., 2023).

Our study not only confirmed the presence of several oral microbial species previously reported in HF patients, but more importantly, identified novel microbiota alterations specifically associated with EDs. Interestingly, fungal functional prediction results did not fully align with our initial hypotheses. The higher abundance of potential pathogens in the HFNon group compared to the HFEmotion group suggests that fungi may play a more substantial role in the HF disease state itself, rather than being deeply involved in the pathogenesis or progression of EDs. Among the 18 HF patients included in this subset analysis, 11 were identified with EDs, indicating a high prevalence of psychological distress in this population. This finding underscores that timely emotional management interventions may significantly benefit the overall treatment and prognosis of HF patients. These results demonstrate that EDs are not only directly associated with HF outcomes, but may also indirectly exacerbate cardiovascular damage by inducing oral microbial dysbiosis and localized-systemic inflammation. This discovery strongly supports the integration of routine emotional screening and oral health assessment into standard HF management, advancing the concept of “dual-heart treatment” from theory to clinical practice. Future development of targeted integrated psychological and oral interventions may open new multidisciplinary, multi-target therapeutic avenues for improving quality of life and long-term prognosis in these patients.

However, several limitations should be considered. First, the cross-sectional nature of the NHANES component limits our ability to infer temporal or causal relationships between EDs, oral microbiome alterations, and HF outcomes. Residual confounding may still influence results. Second, the sample size of the microbiome cohort remains modest, which may limit statistical power to detect more subtle microbial shifts or perform more complex subgroup analyses. Third, while we excluded participants reporting recent systemic antibiotic use, we lacked data on the use of topical antimicrobial agents, which could also influence the local oral microbiota. Fourth, functional predictions of microbial traits were computationally inferred rather than directly measured using metagenomic or metabolomic approaches. Additionally, although standardized instruments were used, some measures—such as self-rated oral health—are subjective, and emotional status was assessed by questionnaire rather than clinical diagnosis. The use of a single-item measure for anxiety, in contrast to a multi-item scale for depression, represents a methodological limitation. Although this approach was pragmatic given the available data, it may not fully capture the complexity of anxiety symptomatology. Finally, as the primary data are derived from a U.S. national survey and a single-center Chinese cohort, the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic and health care settings requires further validation. Despite these limitations, our study provides compelling evidence supporting the role of EDs and oral microbiota dysbiosis in HF progression. The complementary use of population-based and molecular data offers a solid foundation for future research aimed at elucidating causal pathways and developing integrated bio-psycho-social interventions for HF management.

At the inception of this study in 2024, the Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Heart Failure (2024) acknowledged the importance of psychosocial support in improving patients’ quality of life, though it was not explicitly listed as a standalone recommendation—unlike patient education and exercise rehabilitation (Chinese Society of CardiologyChinese College of Cardiovascular PhysiciansChinese Heart Failure Association of Chinese Medical Doctor Association& Editorial Board of Chinese Journal of Cardiology, 2024). However, a Clinical Consensus Statement on Mental Health and Cardiovascular Disease was released at the recent ESC Congress in 2025, which for the first time systematically established a bidirectional framework linking cardiovascular disease and mental health. This consensus advocates for a “dual-heart” treatment approach—integrating both cardiac and mental health care—and emphasizes that psychological screening should be embedded throughout the entire cardiovascular care continuum (Tonelli et al., 2023). Our findings align closely with this new consensus and underscore the potential value of emotional management in HF therapy—much like how mood regulation has already been incorporated into the systematic “ABCDE” management approach for coronary artery disease. Based on the results of this study, future research should focus on elucidating underlying mechanisms and promoting clinical translation. Longitudinal cohort studies integrated with multi-omics analyses could help clarify the dynamic causal pathway and identify key effector molecules through which EDs induce oral microbiota dysbiosis and subsequently influence HF progression. Medium-to long-term research directions include establishing animal models of emotional stress to validate specific pathways within the “brain-oral-heart” axis; developing disease-prediction models based on oral microbiota and metabolomic profiles; and ultimately designing randomized controlled trials to explore novel strategies—such as professional oral care, targeted probiotic interventions, or dietary modifications—that modulate the oral microbiome to improve HF outcomes.

The common lifestyle advice to “maintain a positive mood” and “take good care of your teeth” here transcends conventional wisdom, emerging as a clinically relevant strategy to improve HF outcomes. Like two arrows striking the same apple, psychological well-being and oral hygiene jointly target the progression of HF, reflecting a shift toward a more holistic, human-centered bio-psycho-social model of care.
