Eruptive halo nevi and vitiligo in a 40-year-old woman
Authors: Camille Hamm, Jennifer Beecker
Published: 2025

A 40-year-old woman presented to our dermatology clinic for assessment of new nevi surrounded by depigmented skin, as well as separate depigmented patches, which had appeared suddenly within 1 month of SARS-CoV-2 infection in 2021. She reported a history of multiple nevi since childhood but no history of halo nevi, vitiligo, or malignancy. She was otherwise healthy, took no regular medications, and had no relevant family history. She had recently had an ophthalmological examination that was normal. Full-body skin examination revealed 10 intradermal nevi with surrounding depigmented round patches, consistent with halo nevi (Figure 1A). On the right shoulder, left anterior hip, and forehead, she had well-defined depigmented patches, consistent with vitiligo (Figure 1B). We performed dermoscopy, and her nevi appeared benign, demonstrating a regular reticular pattern with no atypical features. As she was not bothered cosmetically by her vitiligo, we did not pursue treatment, but we arranged a 1-year follow-up appointment to monitor for cutaneous melanoma.

(A) Eruptive halo nevi on the back of a 40-year-old woman, with surrounding pre-existing intradermal nevi. (B) Well-defined depigmented plaque consistent with vitiligo, which had appeared within 1 month of SARS-CoV-2 infection.

Halo nevi are defined as the circular loss of pigment surrounding a benign acquired melanocytic nevus. They are the result of cytotoxic destruction of melanocytes, similar to the pathogenesis of vitiligo.1 Because of this link, vitiligo commonly co-occurs with halo nevi. When observed in children, halo nevi are considered a benign entity; however, when they develop in adulthood, this raises concern for potential melanoma-induced leukoderma.2 Adult-onset halo nevi are associated with a 1% risk of developing primary cutaneous melanoma 1 year after halo nevus diagnosis, similar to the risk of an individual with a history of multiple atypical nevi or a personal or family history of melanoma. 2 New nevi, or eruptive nevi, can be triggered by immunocompromised state (e.g., vaccination, treatment with immune checkpoint inhibitors or, in this patient, SARS-CoV-2 infection).3 Patients with new-onset halo nevi without a clear trigger should have a full-body skin examination; an ophthalmology examination may be considered to exclude cutaneous or ocular melanoma.1,3
