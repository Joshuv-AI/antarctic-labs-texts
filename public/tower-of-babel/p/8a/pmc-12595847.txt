The independent association of social isolation and loneliness with cognitive frailty in Chinese older adults: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lian Li, Hongying Yang, Lujie Yu, Ying Zhou, Sa Xiao, Guolin Bian, Fengsheng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive frailty (CF) is a distinct clinical phenotype characterized by the co-occurrence of physical frailty and cognitive impairment (excluding Alzheimer’s disease and other forms of dementia). Both of social isolation and loneliness are risk factors for cognitive frailty in older adults. However, limited research has explored their independent and combined associations with CF.MethodsA multi-stage cluster sampling approach was employed to recruit adults aged ≥ 65 years from communities in Ningbo. Basic demographic and health-related information was collected through face-to-face interviews using self-reported questionnaires. CF was defined as the co-occurrence of physical frailty, assessed using the FRAIL scale, and cognitive impairment, evaluated with the Brief Screening Scale for Dementia. Social isolation and loneliness were assessed using standardized questions. Logistic regression was employed to examine their associations with CF, and multiplicative and additive interactions were analyzed to assess potential interaction effects.ResultsOverall, 10,151 older adults with a mean age of 72.83 years were included in our study. The prevalence of social isolation, loneliness and CF were 32.3%, 11.8% and 7.22%. After adjusting for covariates, social isolation (odds ratio [OR] = 1.325, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.106–1.586) and loneliness (OR = 1.492, 95% CI: 1.196–1.862) were independently associated with CF. However, social isolation and loneliness showed no multiplicative or additive interaction effects on CF (Both P > 0.05).ConclusionSocial isolation and loneliness are independently associated with CF in Chinese older adults, highlighting the necessity of implementing effective and feasible interventions to concurrently address both factors in the prevention of CF among the elderly population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25178-7.Keywords: Social isolation, Loneliness, Cognitive frailty, Interactive effect

Background

The world is witnessing a significant transformation in its population structure marked by an accelerated pace of aging, which has led to a dramatic increase in the prevalence of conditions associated with advanced age [1]. China has the largest aging population in the world. The population aged 65 and above was 191 million, and accounting for nearly 14% of the total population in 2020 [2]. Physical frailty and cognitive impairment are among the most common aging symptoms in community-dwelling older adults [3–5].

Physical frailty is a biological syndrome characterized by decreased reserve and resistance to stressors resulting from cumulative declines across multiple physiological systems [6, 7]. Mild cognitive impairment is an age-related decline in cognitive function that is not severe enough to cause significant impairment of daily function [8, 9]. Numerous epidemiological studies have demonstrated a significant link between physical frailty and cognitive impairment, which have similar influencing factors and pathophysiological mechanisms [10, 11]. Consequently, in 2013, the international consensus group from the International Academy of Nutrition and Aging and the International Association of Gerontology and Geriatrics collaboratively established “cognitive frailty” (CF) as a distinct clinical phenotype marked by the co-occurrence of physical frailty and cognitive dysfunction (excluding Alzheimer’s disease and other type of dementia) [12]. Studies have reported that older adults with CF have a higher risk of adverse health outcomes such as falls, disability, dementia, and even mortality than do healthy older adults or those with physical frailty or cognitive impairment alone [13–15].

A comprehensive meta-analysis incorporating data from 31 studies in Chinese populations reported a pooled prevalence estimate of 14.3% for CF among community-dwelling older adults [16]. Notably, cognitive frailty is a precursor to the neurodegenerative processes and could be potentially reversed using targeted measures to restore physical strength and/or cognitive health [10, 17]. Therefore, the identification of modifiable risk factors for CF is of great importance, which may be promising targets of interventions for the prevention of age-related diseases [18]. Studies have identified a series of risk factors for CF, encompassing biological, psychological, and social factors such as advancing age, low education levels, depression, chronic diseases, and lack of social interactions and social support [3, 19, 20]. Interestingly, emerging research has increasingly investigated the potential links between social isolation, perceived loneliness, and the development of CF in older populations [21–23].

Social isolation and perceived loneliness are prevalent but underestimated psychosocial stressors in older adults [24]. Social isolation and loneliness were both reported having impact on the cognitive function [25, 26] and frailty [27]. Studies have revealed that social isolation [23] (odds ratio [OR] = 1.77, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.02–3.08) and loneliness [22] (OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.06–1.20) are significantly associated with CF on community-dwelling older adults. Despite these findings, studies have primarily examined these factors in isolation, leaving the differential effects of social isolation and perceived loneliness on CF poorly understood. Furthermore, social isolation primarily reflects “structural social deprivation” whereas loneliness is primarily linked to “subjective emotional distress”, it remains unclear whether the co-occurrence of social isolation and loneliness exacerbates the risk of CF.

Given this background, the aim of this cross-sectional study is to elucidate the independent and potentially synergistic effects of social isolation and loneliness on CF among older adults, thereby contributing to a deeper understanding of the psychosocial pathways underlying cognitive frailty.

Materials and methods

Study population

Data were obtained from a Chinese cross-sectional study of community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years and above, which was conducted from April to December 2024 in Ningbo, China. Participants were recruited using a multi-stage random cluster sampling method. Briefly, sampling was divided into three stages. In the first stage, half of ten districts and counties were randomly selected from Ningbo, China. In the second stage, a street or town was randomly selected from each selected district or county. In the third stage, a community or village was randomly selected from each selected street or town. The inclusion criteria were an age ≥ 65 years, having resided in the area for more than 1 year, willingness to participate in the study, and the ability to understand the questionnaire content. The exclusion criteria were severe mental illness, Alzheimer’s disease or other types of dementia, severe physical illness, or failure to sign the informed consent form.

Study data were collected using structured questionnaires as our previously study [28] completed through face-to-face, one-on-one interviews by researchers who are medical workers. Before the investigation, the evaluation scale and communication method were trained to be standardized. In the investigation, the researchers described the purpose, content, and information confidentiality of the study to the participants. Written informed consent forms were obtained from all eligible participants. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Kangning Hospital of Ningbo University (Approval No: NBKNYY-2024-LC-10).

Measurements

Dependent variable

The Chinese version of the 30-item Brief screening Scale for Dementia (BSSD) was used to evaluate cognitive function across eight cognitive domains: common sense and image understanding, short-term and instant memory, attention and calculation, location and time targeting, language and naming ability [29]. Cronbach’s alpha for the BSSD in Chinese older adults was 0.88 [29]. The total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better global cognitive function. Global cognitive function was determined according to the education level of the participants. As in a previous study, cognitive impairment was defined as having a BSSD score of ≤ 18 for participants who were illiterate, ≤ 21 for those who had completed only primary school education, and ≤ 24 for those who had completed junior high school or higher education [29].

The Chinese version of the FRAIL scale was used to assess physical frailty across five domains: fatigue, low resistance, ambulation, illnesses, and loss of weight [30]. Cronbach’s alpha for the FRAIL scale in Chinese older adults was 0.83. The total scores range from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating higher levels of physical frailty. As such, individuals with scores of 0, 1–2, and 3–5 were considered to be robust, pre-frail, and frail, respectively [30].

Cognitive frailty was defined as the presence of concurrent physical frailty (including pre-frailty and frailty) and cognitive impairment (excluding Alzheimer’s disease and other types of dementia) [10].

Independent variables

Social isolation was defined using three questions, one each regarding living alone (“Do you live with other people?”; a response of “Yes” was scored 1), social contact (“How many times a month do you visit a friend or family member?”; a response of “Visited less than once/month” was scored 1), and participation in social activities (“How many times a month do you participate in social activities”; a response of “Participated less than once per week” was scored 1) [31]. The total scores range from 0 to 3, with total scores ≥ 2 considered to indicate social isolation [31]. Loneliness was measured using a question on the frequency of feeling lonely (“How many times do you feel lonely?”), with responses of “sometimes,” “often,” and “always” considered to indicate loneliness.

Covariates

Data were obtained on the following covariates : age, sex, years of education (< 6 or ≥ 6 years), marital status (married or others), drinking (yes or no), smoking (yes or no), hearing loss (yes or no), chronic diseases (yes or no), sleep quality (good or poor), physical activity (< 3 or ≥ 3 times per week) and depressive symptoms (yes or no) [28]. Sleep quality was evaluated using the Chinese version of the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.85 and to be applicable to Chinese older adults [32]; the total scores range from 0 to 21, with scores ≥ 8 considered to indicate poor sleep quality. Having depressive symptoms was evaluated using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire, which have Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.87 and to be applicable to Chinese older adults [33]; the total scores range from 0 to 27, with scores ≥ 10 considered to indicate the presence of depressive symptoms.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and compared between groups using the t-test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and compared between groups using the χ2 test. A post-hoc subgroup analysis was performed to investigate whether the observed differences varied across subgroups defined by age and gender. Logistic regression was used to determine the relationship between social isolation, loneliness, and CF. The multiplicative interaction effects were calculated by including social isolation, loneliness, and their product in the logistic regression model. The additive interaction effects were calculated using an Excel sheet developed by Anderss and co-author [34]. Relevant indicators of the additive interaction were evaluated using the relative excess risk of interaction (RERI), attributable proportion of interaction (AP), and synergy index (S), and their 95% CIs and P values were calculated using the delta method. An RERI >0, AP >0, or S >1 indicated the presence of an additive interaction between the two variables [35]. P value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significant. All statistical analyses were were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Overall, 10,151 community-dwelling older adults (mean age: 72.83 ± 5.76 years), comprising 4,511 men (44.4%; 73.07 ± 5.87 years) and 5,640 women (55.6%; 72.63 ± 5.65 years), were included in our study. The participants’ characteristics are presented in Table 1. The prevalence of social isolation was 32.3%, while that of loneliness was 11.8%; the mean FRAIL and BSSD scores were 0.47 ± 0.81 and 26.62 ± 4.02, respectively; and the prevalence of CF was 7.22%. Compared with the participants without CF, participants with CF were more likely to be older, female, no married and to have a higher education level, hearing loss, without drinking, without smoking, chronic diseases, good sleep quality, depression, social isolation and loneliness (all P < 0.05, Table 1). A post-hoc subgroup analysis confirmed that the differences in social isolation and loneliness between the two groups remained consistent across various age and gender subgroups (STable 1, and 2).

Discussion

The results of this study identify social isolation and loneliness as significant independent risk factors for cognitive frailty (CF) after adjusting for covariates. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence regarding the psychosocial determinants of cognitive frailty [22, 23]. This “twilight zone” of cognitive–physical decline demands urgent public health attention, given China’s rapidly aging population.

In our study, the prevalence of CF was 7.22%, which is lower than the 14.3% pooled prevalence of CF among Chinese community-dwelling older adults reported in a previous systematic review and meta-analysis [16]. This inconsistency in the prevalence rates may be partly due to the lack of consensus on the operational definition and measurement scales of cognitive frailty. The observation that individuals with cognitive frailty tend to have higher education essentially reveals the powerful buffering effect of cognitive reserve [36]. Higher education allows individuals to maintain relatively normal cognitive function despite more severe neuropathological damage, thereby delaying the point at which their cognitive impairment reaches the clinical diagnostic threshold for cognitive frailty [36]. Consequently, when individuals with high reserve are ultimately diagnosed with cognitive frailty, their brains are often already in a more advanced state of pathological damage. This phenomenon in no way implies that higher education causes cognitive frailty; rather, it highlights the crucial protective role of education in shaping brain resilience and delaying the onset of clinical disability.

Our results demonstrate that social isolation was significantly associated with cognitive frailty (OR = 1.325, 95% CI: 1.106–1.586), further corroborating the findings of previous studies [17, 19, 21, 23]. A prospective cohort study demonstrated that maintaining high levels of social engagement, particularly through participation in senior activity centers and organized social group activities, significantly reduces the risk of cognitive frailty progression among cognitively normal individuals [37]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the association between social isolation and cognitive frailty in older adults. First, the cognitive reserve theory suggests that social isolation limits cognitive stimulation in older adults and impairs reserve accumulation. This deficiency heightens vulnerability to structural brain damage from aging or pathology, accelerating cognitive decline [36, 38]. Second, social isolation increases susceptibility to psychological stress, inducing hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis dysregulation and chronic hypercortisolemia. This neuroendocrine dysfunction induces neurotoxic effects, particularly on hippocampal neurons, through glucocorticoid-mediated pathways, substantially increasing the risk of cognitive impairment and neurodegeneration [39]. Third, isolated older adults exhibit high rates of physical inactivity [40] and poor nutrition [41]. These behaviors promote cardiovascular comorbidities and malnutrition, which directly and indirectly drive cognitive–physical frailty progression. Finally, social isolation is correlated with elevated interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein levels [42]. Such inflammatory markers are independently associated with cognitive frailty severity, suggesting shared pathophysiology [43, 44].

Further, our study also revealed a significant positive association between loneliness and cognitive frailty (OR = 1.492, 95% CI: 1.196–1.862). Notably, a cross-sectional study conducted among community-dwelling older adults demonstrated a similar association between loneliness and cognitive frailty [22]. However, current research investigating the direct association between loneliness and cognitive frailty remains limited. A substantial body of literature [45–47] has examined the relationships of loneliness with cognitive function and frailty separately, providing indirect evidence that may elucidate the potential connection between loneliness and cognitive frailty. At the biological level, evidence suggests that loneliness is associated with accelerated telomere shortening in leukocytes later in life [48] and with increased cortical amyloid deposition in cognitively normal older adults [49], potentially contributing to the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative disorders. From a behavioral perspective, loneliness is frequently associated with maladaptive lifestyle patterns [45], including physical inactivity [50], irregular dietary habits [51], and subsequent health complications such as sarcopenia [52] and cardiovascular dysfunction [45], all of which constitute significant risk factors for cognitive decline or frailty. Furthermore, the psychosocial consequences of loneliness, characterized by reduced social engagement, diminished cognitive reserve, and heightened vulnerability to depressive symptoms, may further exacerbate the risk of cognitive impairment through neuroendocrine and neuroinflammatory mechanisms [22, 53, 54].

In addition, no interaction effect of social isolation and loneliness on CF was observed, indicating that the simultaneous presence of both conditions did not further increase the risk of CF beyond their individual effects. Social isolation and loneliness represent distinct constructs reflecting deficits in social connection: the former denotes an objective lack of social ties, while the latter reflects a subjective negative emotional experience. Existing theories, such as Cacioppo’s evolutionary model of loneliness, propose distinct mechanistic pathways for these constructs—social isolation primarily reflects “structural social deprivation” whereas loneliness is primarily linked to “subjective emotional distress” [55]. This mechanistic divergence likely accounts for the lack of an interaction effect between social isolation and loneliness observed in this study. Although these factors did not demonstrate a synergistic effect, their independent influences highlight the critical importance of addressing them in cognitive frailty interventions. Drawing upon the National Dementia Prevention Initiative (2024–2030) [56], we propose three targeted interventions to address social isolation and loneliness in older adults, thereby mitigating the risk of cognitive frailty: (1) establish neighborhood social hubs offering structured group activities and cognitive exercises to enhance engagement; (2) develop age-friendly digital platforms with guided training to promote virtual connectivity; and (3) implement routine psychosocial risk screening in primary care, followed by tailored support programs. These evidence-based measures address the modifiable risk factors for cognitive frailty through enhanced social participation and mental stimulation, supporting national objectives for cognitive health maintenance and dementia prevention in aging populations.

Strengths and limitations

In sum, this study expanded on prior research by distinguishing the differential effects of objective social isolation and subjective loneliness—a distinction rarely addressed in the literature. Furthermore, we explored whether the co-occurrence of objective social isolation and subjective loneliness exacerbates the the association with CF. However, this investigation also has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, key variables, including loneliness and frailty, were assessed through self-reported measures, potentially introducing recall bias. Furthermore, although the use of single-item assessments to evaluate the presence of loneliness is common in epidemiological studies, this method tends to underestimate the prevalence of loneliness when compared to multi-item loneliness scales, such as the UCLA Loneliness Scale [57]. Second, there is a significant connection between chronic loneliness and cognitive decline [58]. However, our research lacks information regarding the persistence of loneliness. Third, the cross-sectional design limited causal inferences, and thus causal associations between social isolation, loneliness, and cognitive frailty cannot be assumed, necessitating future longitudinal studies to establish temporal relationships. Fourth, the single-city sampling frame limits generalizability due to differences in social culture, economy, and lifestyle; therefore, these research results may only be applicable to the local area, and their generalizability still needs to be further verified. Finally, only the variables we investigated were adjusted in this study, and there may be other variables such as socioeconomic status and social support that affect the associations.

Conclusion

This cross-sectional study highlights the independent associations between social isolation, loneliness, and cognitive frailty. Addressing these modifiable risk factors through early identification and targeted interventions is crucial to reducing cognitive decline and its associated health burdens. Future research should focus on understanding the causal inferences of and mechanisms underlying the associations between social isolation, loneliness, and cognitive frailty and developing practical strategies to enhance social engagement and emotional well-being in aging populations.
