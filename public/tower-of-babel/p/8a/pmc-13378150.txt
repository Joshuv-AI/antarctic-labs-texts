Ecological quality carbon storage coupling via an enhanced water retention integrated remote sensing index
Authors: Xiaotang Xia, Yulin Xu, Jian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study proposes an enhanced remote sensing ecological index (ERSEI) incorporating water-retention dynamics to quantify spatiotemporal variations in ecological quality and carbon storage in Yichang city (2003–2023). Utilizing principal-component analysis (PCA) and GeoDetector, we evaluated ERSEI trajectories and underlying mechanisms. Results reveal that mean ERSEI increased by approximately 12.5%, exhibiting pronounced spatial heterogeneity (higher in western mountainous zones, lower in eastern plains). Driver analysis demonstrated a temporal shift from topography- and vegetation-dominated controls to intensified socioeconomic and climatic influences (notably gross domestic product [GDP] and temperature) post-2013, characterized by nonlinear synergistic interactions. Furthermore, bivariate spatial autocorrelation confirmed a significant coupling between ERSEI and InVEST-estimated carbon storage; regions with improving ecological quality achieved a net carbon increment of approximately 2.11 t hm−2. These findings informed a three-axis zoning framework (regional, western barrier, and eastern industrial cluster), providing a transferable paradigm for restoration prioritization in hydrologically complex river basins.Keywords: Enhanced Remote Sensing Ecological Index, ERSEI, ecological quality, carbon storage, spatiotemporal dynamics, mechanistic drivers

Introduction

The ecological environment underpins human well-being and sustainable development, with river basins serving as critical socio-ecological systems. The Yangtze river, the longest river in Asia and the third longest globally (approximately 6,300 km), provides essential water resources that sustain economic growth and ecological security in China.1,2,3 However, rapid development under the Yangtze river economic belt has intensified ecological pressures.4 In response, China has enacted landmark regulations such as the Yangtze River Protection Law5,6 and the Ecological Protection Compensation Regulations,7,8 aiming to reconcile upstream-downstream conflicts, enhance ecological flow and biodiversity conservation, and promote a legal framework for basin-wide ecological governance. While ecological protection policies have advanced, challenges remain in achieving sustainable development within the Yangtze river basin (YRB). Recent basin-scale assessments based on remote sensing have shown that, although the overall ecological quality of the YRB has improved over the past two decades, pronounced spatial heterogeneity persists, with localized degradation closely associated with surface temperature, land-cover change, and topographic constraints.9 These findings highlight the need for refined, mechanism-oriented ecological evaluation frameworks tailored to large river systems.

Advances in remote sensing have enabled integrative, highly visual assessments of ecological quality across space and time.10,11 Indicator-based frameworks are widely used, yet many rely on subjective weighting schemes that may yield inconsistent results.12 To reduce arbitrariness, the remote sensing ecological index (RSEI) derives a composite metric via principal component analysis from greenness, wetness, heat, and dryness, and has been validated across diverse settings—including coasts, arid regions, and mining areas.13,14,15,16 Notably, RSEI has also been widely applied to quantify ecological disturbance and post-disturbance recovery following major natural hazards, such as the Nepal and Yushu earthquakes, highlighting its robustness in capturing spatiotemporal dynamics under strong external perturbations.17,18 Beyond hazard-driven disturbances, rapid urbanization has emerged as another dominant stressor on ecological systems; in highly urbanized areas along the lower reaches of the Yangtze river, intensifying human-land conflicts have further amplified ecological risks, underscoring the complex feedbacks between urban expansion, ecological vulnerability, and spatial clustering of environmental stress.19 Building on RSEI, tailored variants, such as T-RSEI, RSUSEI, and formulations integrating habitat quality or biological abundance (BAI), as well as the mining-oriented MSEEI, have improved contextual sensitivity.20,21,22,23,24 Nonetheless, a basin-scale, node-focused evaluation framework suitable for major river systems with large hydraulic projects remains underdeveloped. Complementarily, geographic detector approaches—including optimal-parameter implementations—have been applied to disentangle drivers of spatiotemporal heterogeneity in ecological quality.25,26,27 Evidence indicates that while remote-sensing variables (e.g., normalized difference vegetation index [NDVI], normalized difference built-up and soil index [NDBSI], and land surface temperature [LST]) often have high explanatory power, multifactor interactions—particularly those coupling socioeconomic variables (e.g., per capita gross domestic product [GDP] with sectoral or biophysical factors—tend to dominate, whereas single-factor contributions are weaker; moreover, the positive effects of climate and terrain are waning relative to intensifying urbanization pressures.28 These insights motivate index enhancement and mechanism-oriented analyses tailored to large, water-rich basins. Ecological restoration is a cornerstone of global responses to environmental degradation and climate change, with carbon storage functioning both as an outcome and an indicator of restoration success.29 Land-use transitions driven by urban expansion have been shown to substantially alter regional carbon storage, with conversions from cultivated land and forest to construction land leading to pronounced carbon losses.30 Empirical studies demonstrate that interventions such as water conservation, soil protection, and biodiversity enhancement increase carbon storage while improving environmental quality.31,32,33 Process-based and scenario models—including InVEST, PLUS, and i-Tree Eco—enable spatially explicit quantification of carbon-sequestration benefits and their co-benefits for ecosystem services.34,35,36,37,38 These tools underscore the tight coupling between ecological quality improvements and carbon outcomes, providing a tractable pathway to evaluate climate-aligned restoration. Despite progress, prior work often considers a limited set of meteorological and anthropogenic drivers and adopts a narrow socioeconomic perspective, overlooking critical basin-specific features such as water-system distribution in node cities of large river basins.39 Consequently, evaluation systems remain insufficiently comprehensive, and quantitative evidence linking ecological-quality change to tangible ecological benefits—especially carbon storage and ecosystem services—remains scarce.

Yichang is a strategic node for ecological protection in the middle reaches of the Yangtze river urban agglomeration.40 Its rich ecological assets, geographic position, and accumulated planning practice in restoration, water management, biodiversity, and sustainability make it an ideal regional test bed.41 Recent basin-wide studies have shown that eco-environmental quality in the Yangtze region exhibits marked spatial heterogeneity and increasing sensitivity to urbanization and water-system regulation, with multifactor interactions—particularly those coupling BAI, population density (PD), and impervious expansion—playing a dominant role.17,42 Modified RSEI frameworks further emphasize the necessity of incorporating pollution and urban pressures in rapidly developing agglomerations,43 while assessments in large water-diversion and hydraulic-project areas reveal that precipitation, impervious surface, and vegetation coverage critically shape local ecological trajectories.44 Yet complex terrain and rapid urban-industrial expansion heighten ecological fragility. With stricter protection upstream and industrial transfer downstream, the middle reaches—including Yichang—have become focal areas for equipment manufacturing, petrochemicals, and metallurgy, amplifying pressures on resources, energy, and emissions.6,45 Intensifying land demand further threatens ecologically sensitive areas such as reserves, ecological public-welfare forests, water-source protection zones, urban green spaces, and water bodies.46 These conditions underscore the need for comprehensive, mechanism-oriented assessments to guide restoration, water governance innovation, sustainable ecotourism, and climate adaptation.

Guided by these needs, this regional case study focuses on basin-node cities to evaluate the balance between ecological preservation and economic development. Methodologically, we address a critical flaw of the traditional RSEI in water-rich contexts: its wetness (WET) component merely reflects surface moisture and cannot distinguish between “open-water coverage (e.g., rivers, reservoirs, etc.)” and “ecosystem water retention (WR) capacity (e.g., forest water conservation, soil water-holding, etc.).” This confusion leads to distorted ecological quality assessments in basin-node cities with dense hydraulic infrastructure. To resolve this and provide a robust scientific rationale for our methodological innovation, we propose an enhanced RSEI (ERSEI), explicitly integrating the WR indicator into RSEI framework. Preserving the traditional core components (greenness, wetness, heat, and dryness) of the RSEI, this index adds WR as an independent indicator to characterize the actual hydrological regulation function of ecosystems. This integration establishes a five-dimensional assessment system that decouples “surface moisture” and “ecological water retention,” effectively capturing deep eco-hydrological processes that conventional surface indices fail to represent. A core objective of this study is to systematically demonstrate the comparative effectiveness of ERSEI against the traditional RSEI, particularly its superior ability to correct ecological misclassifications in complex urban-river watersheds. We further construct a comprehensive driver system—expanding socioeconomic variables (e.g., industrial restructuring rate) and incorporating basin-specific factors (e.g., river network density, distance to water, etc.)—and apply single-factor and interaction detection to quantitatively reveal the mechanisms through which natural, anthropogenic, and hydrological factors influence ecological quality. Finally, we couple ERSEI with the InVEST carbon storage model to quantify the tangible ecological benefits (carbon sequestration) resulting from changes in ecological quality. The study compares the spatiotemporal co-evolution characteristics of the ERSEI and carbon storage, and estimates the average carbon storage gains and losses in areas with improved or degraded ecological quality. Collectively, this study establishes a generalizable, mechanism-oriented assessment framework tailored to large river basins with extensive hydraulic projects, providing an innovative support paradigm for ecological-civilization goals and climate-aligned planning in Yichang and similar basin-node cities. Despite these methodological advances, existing RSEI applications often overlook water-retention dynamics, which constrains their applicability in large, water-rich basins and node cities. Unlike most improved RSEI models that primarily rely on indicator optimization or weighting adjustments, ERSEI explicitly incorporates water-retention capacity as a functional constraint reflecting hydrological regulation processes. The key innovations of this work are (1) methodological enhancement of RSEI by integrating water-retention capacity, (2) coupling ERSEI with carbon storage to quantify ecological-carbon co-benefits, and (3) providing a spatially explicit, basin-scale framework for ecological assessment and decision support. This approach enables a more comprehensive evaluation of ecological quality changes and their tangible benefits, offering a robust basis for evidence-driven restoration and sustainable management in complex riverine environments.

Results

Temporal analysis of ERSEI (2003–2023)

Between 2003 and 2023, PCA results indicate that the five indicators—NDVI, WET, WR, NDBSI, and LST—captured the majority of ecological variation in Yichang city. Notably, the first principal component (PC1) consistently explained a highly significant portion of the total variance (ranging from 71.49% to 74.93%), significantly surpassing conventional thresholds and confirming the exceptional robustness of the proposed ERSEI framework (Table 1). NDVI, WET, and WR loaded positively, while LST and NDBSI loaded negatively (the detailed computational formulas for these five normalized indicators are summarized in Table 2), suggesting that vegetation, moisture, and water retention enhance ecological quality, whereas heat and dryness exert adverse effects. More importantly, while the traditional WET indicator effectively captures surface moisture, WR consistently exhibited strong, independent positive loadings (ranging from 0.41 to 0.51). This statistical distinction provides a robust quantitative rationale for our methodological innovation: it demonstrates that subsurface WR acts as a fundamental, non-redundant driver of regional ecological quality that cannot be substituted or fully captured by conventional surface wetness indices alone.

Ecological environment driving factors

Based on the continuous annual ERSEI dataset from 2003 to 2023, ecological quality in Yichang city exhibits a persistent long-term increasing trend with moderate interannual variability. Mean ERSEI rises steadily from the early 2000s, with gradual improvement before 2010, followed by relatively stable growth during 2010–2015, and more pronounced enhancement after 2015. Peak values occur around 2018–2021, after which ERSEI shows a slight decline in 2022–2023.

Distributional statistics further reveal a systematic upward shift in ecological quality (Figure 3). The median ERSEI (Q2) increases from 0.594 in 2003 to above 0.70 after 2018, while the interquartile range decreases from 0.24 to approximately 0.18–0.19, indicating both overall improvement and reduced spatial heterogeneity. High maximum values (>0.95) persist throughout the study period, suggesting the long-term stability of high-quality ecological zones. These results demonstrate that the observed improvements are not driven by isolated time points but reflect sustained ecological enhancement over the past two decades.

Spatial analysis of ERSEI (2003–2023)

The spatial distribution of ERSEI in Yichang city (2003–2023; Figure 4) reveals marked regional heterogeneity. High ERSEI values were concentrated in the northern and southern mountainous areas, including Wufeng Tujia Autonomous County, Dalaoling, Heiliangshan, Huangjieya, Guanghe Mountain, and adjacent regions of Xingshan County and Shennongjia. Conversely, the eastern and northwestern plains—particularly the eastern and southeastern Dangyang—showed persistently low ERSEI due to intensive human activity, dense settlements, and agricultural use. Low-quality zones were also found along the Yangtze river, historically affected by soil erosion and sparse vegetation.

Factor detection analysis

We employed a geographical detector model to quantify the influence of driving forces on ERSEI. The selected independent variables—natural environment, water resources, and socioeconomic factors—were chosen based on their direct effects on ecological processes and human-induced landscape changes. Natural factors such as elevation (ELE) and annual temperature (AAT) influence microclimate and hydrology, while anthropogenic factors like GDP and PD reflect development pressures altering land use patterns.

As shown in Table 6, for 2003–2013, all factors were statistically significant (p < 0.05). The strongest drivers were ELE (0.747), green space area (GA) (0.671), Rate of ecological land transfer (RELT) (0.625), AAP (0.556), and distance to water (DTW) (0.515), indicating that topography, vegetation, ecological land protection, precipitation, and proximity to water exerted predominant control over ecological quality. The q values represent the proportion of spatial variance in ERSEI explained by each factor, highlighting their relative explanatory power.

Interaction detection analysis

Beyond individual effects, we examined the interactions among driving factors based on their spatialized net changes (Δ) during each period to capture the mechanisms of ecological evolution. Instead of using multi-year averages, the input variables were defined as the difference between the terminal and initial years (e.g., Value2023 – Value2013), ensuring that the analysis reflects the spatial heterogeneity of dynamic transitions rather than static states.

From 2003 to 2013, all factor pairs exhibited either non-linear enhancing or double-enhancing effects (Figure 6), rather than independent or diminishing interactions. Double-enhancing interactions indicate that the combined effect exceeds the sum of individual effects, exemplified by GA-ELE (0.898), TWR-RELT (0.672), and GDP-PD (0.844). Non-linear enhancements, representing the strongest synergies, occurred between AAT-AAP (0.713), AAT-ELE (0.820), and AGFG-GDP (0.839). Even factors with minor individual influence, such as DTR, reinforced ecological outcomes when interacting with other drivers, particularly in populated areas. Figure 6A highlights the ELE-GA interaction as the most prominent.

For 2013–2023, interaction patterns remained non-linear or double-enhancing (Figure 6B). Key examples include GA-AAT (0.909), TWR-DTW (0.684), and GDP-RND (0.860). Strong non-linear enhancements were observed for AGFG-DTR (0.479), RELT-GDP (0.894), and AGFG-PBA (0.630). ELE-GA interactions persisted as the most critical, while GDP and TWR showed increasing influence over time, reflecting the intensifying interplay between socioeconomic pressures and ecological constraints.

The rationale for this change-based approach lies in its ability to circumvent the smoothing effect of long-term averaging, thereby revealing how synergistic drivers dictate regional carbon sequestration and ecological shifts over a ten-year cycle. Overall, these interactions elucidate the principal pathways shaping ecological quality in Yichang. Elevation governs spatial constraints and vertical ecological dynamics, while green space sustains ecosystem services. Their arrangement, combined with synergistic effects of human and hydrological drivers, drives temporal and spatial variation in ecological quality.

Impact mechanisms

Precisely elucidating the mechanisms underlying ecological quality changes is essential for effective environmental management.47 Analysis of temporal and spatial patterns, along with factor and interaction detection, indicates that ecological quality variations in Yichang city are governed by the combined effects of natural conditions and human activities.

Regions with relatively high ecological quality are concentrated in the southwestern and northwestern mountainous areas, where ELE and GA play dominant roles. Elevated terrain promotes orographic precipitation, alleviating temperature constraints at high altitudes, while extensive forest cover and grassland cover directly enhance ecosystem functionality, which is captured by ERSEI. Long-term ecological restoration initiatives, including farmland-to-forest conversion and protection of natural forests, have further strengthened these benefits, contributing to high ERSEI values.48,49 In contrast, low-quality areas are primarily situated in the eastern plains, where intensive urbanization, industrial activity, and complex hydrological conditions interact to limit ecological improvement. GDP-driven land development and high PD exacerbate soil saturation and vegetation stress, while rivers in low-elevation zones amplify hydrological challenges. These factors collectively constrain ecological resilience, leading to persistently lower ERSEI values.

The regional differentiation of ecological quality thus emerges from multiscale interactions supported by quantitative evidence. Precipitation primarily enhances vegetation growth and soil moisture, thereby exerting a positive impact on ERSEI (q = 0.556 in 2003–2013). Meanwhile, precipitation and temperature exhibit a nonlinear enhancing interaction, with their interactive explanatory power q (AAP∩ AAT) satisfying the relationship of q (AAP ∩ AAT) = 0.713 > q (AAP) + q (AAT) = 0.607; this indicates that their synergistic effect exerts a stronger driving impact on the research object than the independent effect of either single factor, presenting a characteristic of non-superlinear synergistic enhancement. In contrast, hydrological conditions, represented by total water resources (TWRs) and DTW, reflect spatial constraints and exert heterogeneous impacts (DTW q = 0.515; TWR q = 0.599 in 2013–2023), particularly limiting ecological quality in low-lying plains. Based on these quantified relationships, elevated highlands benefit from a “topography-precipitation-vegetation enhancement” mechanism (Figure 7), whereas low-lying plains experience a “hydrology-development stress” effect driven by water-resource limitations and socioeconomic pressures (e.g., RELT-GDP = 0.894). This dual-core model highlights the distinctiveness of our approach, showing how ERSEI captures both natural and anthropogenic drivers and their interaction with carbon storage.

Discussion

This section focuses on evaluating the methodological advantages, robustness, and applicability of the proposed ERSEI framework, beyond the site-specific ecological outcomes. By explicitly comparing ERSEI with the conventional RSEI and examining its behavior across multiple ecological dimensions, we aim to demonstrate the added value of the methodological enhancement and its relevance for complex, water-influenced urban-river systems.

Index validity and methodological superiority

The computed ERSEI for Yichang city was juxtaposed with the conventional RSEI index, showing a strong linear relationship (Figure 8). The R2 and Pearson’s R reached 0.94 and 0.98, respectively (p < 0.01), indicating that ERSEI more precisely captures the overall ecological quality patterns in the study area. Recent studies have pointed out that the calculation process of traditional RSEI often fails to comprehensively consider water elements, limiting its ability to accurately reflect the ecological benefits of water bodies and their surrounding environments.50 However, ERSEI goes beyond merely aligning with RSEI by providing a broader and more continuous distribution across ecological levels, which allows for more detailed and finer discrimination of spatial heterogeneity. Unlike traditional models that primarily rely on surface wetness components—which can underestimate the actual water-holding capacity of varied topographies, particularly in water-rich zones with moderate surface vegetation but dense underlying hydrological networks—this enhanced resolution of ecological levels effectively corrects the misclassification of these habitats and facilitates the detection of subtle variations in environmental quality, which is especially useful in complex ecological systems like those found in the middle reaches of the Yangtze river. Moreover, the incorporation of additional indicators in ERSEI, such as water retention, provides a robust methodological rationale. As demonstrated by our principal component analysis, WR consistently exhibits a strong, independent positive loading within a highly stable framework. This confirms that subsurface hydrological capacity is a fundamental, non-redundant driver, making the ERSEI more suitable for the specific characteristics of this region, offering a more stable and comprehensive representation of the ecological dynamics at play (Figures 8A–8C).

Spatial coupling of ERSEI and carbon storage

The InVEST model reveals that carbon storage in Yichang ity from 2003 to 2023 exhibits strong spatial correspondence with ERSEI values (Figure 9). Rather than a mere statistical coincidence, this synergy is deeply rooted in local natural geography and anthropogenic interventions. Northwestern mountainous and hilly areas, including the Xing Shan-Shennongjia border region and Dalaoling National Nature Reserve, show both high ecological quality and high carbon storage, consistently performing far above the city’s average. These areas are dominated by primary and secondary forests and mixed coniferous-broadleaf forests, whose complex vertical structure ensures a stable, three-dimensional carbon pool. Mechanistically, the high greenness (NDVI) and abundant moisture (WET) components of ERSEI in these regions directly correspond to vigorous plant photosynthesis and elevated gross primary production,51,52 driving the continuous accumulation of above-ground biomass carbon. The continuous improvement in these areas can be largely attributed to stringent ecological conservation policies, such as natural forest protection initiatives, which stabilize local microclimates and regulate hydrological cycles.

Crucially, the integration of WR in our framework clarifies the subsurface carbon-preservation pathway: robust water retention capacity effectively mitigates soil erosion—a primary driver of soil organic carbon depletion in mountainous terrains—while maintaining the stable hydrological microenvironments necessary for soil microbial humification. In contrast, the eastern plains, Dangyang city, Zhijiang city, and the Xiaoting industrial belt show low ERSEI and correspondingly low carbon storage due to intensive urbanization and agricultural activity. From a biogeochemical perspective, the elevated heat (LST) and dryness (NDBSI) in these heavily urbanized zones not only suppress vegetation photosynthetic efficiency but also accelerate the heterotrophic respiration of soil organic matter,53 transforming these areas into potential carbon sources. The transformation of natural permeable surfaces into impervious urban lands directly disrupts local water retention capacities and depletes biomass carbon pools.

Coupling analysis of carbon sequestration and ERSEI

GeoDa software was applied to calculate the Moran’s I index for an in-depth exploration of the spatial correlation between ERSEI and carbon storage. Based on the results of the bivariate spatial autocorrelation distribution maps in Figure 10A1, B1 and C1, the spatial distribution patterns across the three periods exhibited significant coupling characteristics. Among them, the spatial clustering of ERSEI and carbon storage in Yichang was dominated by two types, namely “high-high” and “low-low” clustering. Meanwhile, the spatial coupling effect of ecological degradation and carbon storage loss caused by the rapid advancement of urbanization during 2003–2013 was notably enhanced. Figure 10A3, B3 and C3 indicated that ERSEI and carbon storage in Yichang displayed a significant positive correlation aggregation feature in the overall spatial distribution. These findings demonstrate that ERSEI and carbon storage in Yichang are not only highly coupled in spatial distribution, but also that this coupling relationship possesses statistical significance and robustness, which verifies the existence of a genuine and reliable spatial synergy law between the two variables.

Changes in ERSEI and carbon storage demonstrate a clear synergistic effect (Table 7). From 2003 to 2013, the overall ERSEI increased, accompanied by a corresponding average carbon storage gain. In regions with improved ERSEI, carbon storage increased significantly, whereas in regions with declining ERSEI, carbon storage decreased. From 2013 to 2023, ecological restoration further enhanced carbon storage in improving regions and mitigated losses in declining regions, reflecting shifting ecosystem vulnerability. These results underscore that ERSEI improvement has a direct, increasingly significant impact on regional carbon sequestration. Importantly, carbon storage is used here not as a variable directly affected by water retention, but as an indicator reflecting the ecological benefits brought by ERSEI improvements. However, this macroscopic reflection is underpinned by profound biogeochemical coupling mechanisms. Rather than a simple spatial coincidence, ERSEI serves as a comprehensive proxy for the eco-hydrological prerequisites (e.g., moisture stability, soil conservation, and vegetation vigor) that intrinsically dictate regional carbon sink capacities. This dynamic indicates that targeted ecological restoration can effectively reverse prior anthropogenic losses, underscoring that improving the regional water-retention-integrated ERSEI directly bolsters comprehensive carbon sequestration capabilities. Quantitatively, regions with improving ecological quality achieved a mean net carbon increment of approximately 2.11 t hm−2 across the two decades, representing the average of the two study periods (0.54 t hm−2 in 2003–2013 and 3.68 t hm−2 in 2013–2023, as shown in Table 7). Furthermore, the methodological core of this water-retention-integrated ERSEI framework possesses broader global transferability beyond Yichang city, as it relies entirely on open-access, standard data streams (Landsat, China Land Cover Dataset, and InVEST) that can be seamlessly replicated in any geographic region. This study develops and validates an ERSEI by explicitly integrating water-retention dynamics into the RSEI framework, demonstrating its methodological advantages in capturing coupled ecological quality and carbon storage dynamics, and applies it to quantify spatiotemporal variations in Yichang city (2003–2023). Using ERSEI together with PCA and Geographical Detector, we obtain three principal findings. The main findings are as follows: first, mean ERSEI increased by approximately 12.5% across 2003–2023, while degradation hotspots expanded under urbanization pressure. Second, drivers of ecological quality shifted over time from natural constraints toward coupled human-land–climate influences, with socioeconomic (GDP) and climatic (temperature) factors gaining explanatory power. Third, notably, bivariate spatial autocorrelation results show significant spatial coupling between ERSEI and carbon storage: high ERSEI zones correspond to substantial carbon gains, while declining ERSEI areas experience corresponding losses, indicating a strong association between ecological quality improvement and carbon storage enhancement. ERSEI improves explanatory performance relative to the conventional RSEI (study results, R2 ≈ 0.94; Pearson r ≈ 0.98; PCA variance explained improved by approximately 3.4% versus the baseline configuration).

Overall, the ERSEI-based framework presented here provides a methodologically robust and transferable operational paradigm, linking ecological quality assessment with carbon dynamics and spatially explicit zoning. This innovation strengthens both scientific understanding and practical decision-making for sustainable urban-river governance and ecosystem management.

Limitations of the study

Despite the novel insights provided by this study, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The research area, while providing detailed high-resolution ecological and carbon data for Yichang city, represents a relatively small spatial extent compared with larger regional studies, which may constrain generalization of the absolute values obtained. Some potentially influential drivers, such as detailed hydrological networks and large-scale water conservancy infrastructure, were not included. Future research should aim to couple ERSEI with localized hydro-dynamic models and expand the spatiotemporal scale. Nevertheless, the ERSEI framework and methodological approach remain robust, adaptable, and replicable, offering a valuable paradigm for integrating ecological quality assessment with industrial and spatial planning in other urban-river systems.

Method details

Overview of the study area

Yichang City (29°56′–31°34′N, 109°49′–112°04′E) lies in the middle–upper Yangtze River and is known as the “Gateway to the Three Gorges.” The region has a subtropical monsoon climate with four seasons, featuring mild, humid winters and hot, wet summers. Terrain is diverse (Figure 1): elevations range from −27 m to 2365 m; the Wuling and Daba Mountains dominate the west and north, while the center–east comprises alluvial plains and hills shaped by the Yangtze and its tributaries, forming a west-high/east-low gradient.54,55 Forest resources are extensive, with approximately1.14 million ha of woodland and 59.67% forest cover.

Data sources

To ensure methodological rigor and data reliability, datasets were selected based on three core principles: (1) spatial resolution unification (1 km for all raster datasets to ensure consistency in subsequent analysis, accommodating the inherently coarser native resolutions of meteorological and hydrological inputs to avoid false precision, while optimally capturing macro-level ecological gradients); (2) temporal coverage consistency (aligning with the 2003–2023 study period); (3) high quality and academic recognition (e.g., validated products with published accuracy metrics). Specifically, the Landsat series (TM/OLI) were selected as the primary remote sensing source because their multi-spectral and thermal infrared bands provide the essential components (NDVI, WET, LST, and NDBSI) required for RSEI construction. The China Land Cover Dataset (CLCD) was employed due to its long-term consistency and validated high overall accuracy (OA of 79.30% ± 1.99%), serving as a robust benchmark for land use dynamic analysis. Detailed information for all utilized datasets is provided in key resource table.

Regarding data preprocessing, all spatial datasets were first unified to the WGS_1984_Albers coordinate system to ensure spatial overlay accuracy. Landsat Level-2 products were acquired after rigorous preprocessing (radiometric calibration, atmospheric correction) by the United States Geological Survey (USGS), ensuring surface reflectance accuracy. To achieve the target 1 km resolution, we applied bilinear interpolation for continuous variables (e.g., Landsat indices, meteorological data) and nearest-neighbor resampling for categorical variables (e.g., CLCD). For statistical data lacking a fixed spatial resolution (denoted as “-” in key resource table), records were spatialized and normalized to match the 1 km raster grids.

Notably, temporal inconsistencies exist for two statistical datasets (Total Water Resources, River Network Density), which start from 2005 due to the unavailability of official statistical records before 2005. For the missing 2003–2004 data, we supplemented them using linear interpolation in Stata 17.0 based on the long-term (2005–2023) statistical trends. The regression fitting coefficient (R2) of all interpolated variables exceeds 0.85, indicating the interpolated data are consistent with actual temporal variation patterns and meet academic reliability requirements.

To systematically address data robustness and reproducibility, a comprehensive uncertainty assessment—detailing dataset-specific accuracy metrics (e.g., RMSE, coefficient of variation), extensive preprocessing workflows, and selection justifications for auxiliary environmental and carbon density data—is provided in Table S2.

Indicator computation and ERSEI construction

We selected five ecological indicators to construct the ERSEI: greenness (NDVI), wetness (WET), dryness (NDBSI), heat (LST), and WR. These indices comprehensively reflect vegetation status, hydrological conditions, land surface temperature, soil/impervious effects, and ecosystem water–energy balance. Their computational formulas are summarized in Table 2.

To validate method improvements, we compared the proposed ERSEI with conventional RSEI and several alternative index formulations; comparative metrics are reported in Supplementary S3. Complete computation formulas and variable definitions (expanded Table 2) are provided in Supplementary S4.To ensure comparability across indicators with heterogeneous dimensions, each index was normalized to the range [0,1]. The normalized values were then integrated in the order of NDVI, WET, NDBSI, LST, and WR through principal component analysis (PCA). The first principal component (ERSEI0) was extracted, followed by min–max normalization to derive the final ERSEI, which serves as a synthetic measure of ecological environmental quality.56 ERSEI key parameters, PCA loadings, and reproduction resources (parameter tables and example scripts) are listed in Supplementary S5.

Following the hierarchical research approach proposed by Xu Hanqiu,57 ERSEI values were classified into five levels of ecological environment status (Table 4), ranging from very poor (0–0.(2) to excellent (0.8–1.0). This classification enables a detailed evaluation of ecological gradients, highlights spatial disparities, and provides a practical framework for ecological monitoring and management.

Carbon storage calculation

Carbon storage was assessed for aboveground biomass, belowground biomass, and soil organic carbon, excluding underground dead organic carbon due to incomplete data. The InVEST carbon storage module was used, requiring reclassified land use/cover data.58 Carbon density for each pool was interpolated via Kriging using the China terrestrial carbon density dataset in ArcGIS 10.5, producing rasters for each land use type within the YRB.59,60 All raster inputs were standardized in projection and units before InVEST calculation. National-scale carbon density data were preferred to reduce uncertainty, with regional averages applied when necessary. Total carbon storage (Ctot) was computed as the sum of aboveground (Cabove), belowground (Cbelow), and soil carbon (Csoil), providing a spatially explicit assessment of carbon sequestration in Yichang city. Regional ERSEI–carbon coupling summaries are provided in Supplementary S6.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Spatiotemporal trend and persistence analysis

The geographical detector quantifies spatial heterogeneity and identifies the drivers of ecological patterns.61 It evaluates the explanatory power of factors and their interactions, widely applied in natural sciences.62,63 Factor detection and interaction detection were applied to ERSEI, with the factor detector q (0–(1) calculated as =1−∑h=1LNhσh2Nσ2 , where L denotes stratification, Nh and σh2 are units and variance per layer, and N and σ2 are total units and variance. Based on previous studies and established factor frameworks, the selected influencing factors in this study are classified into natural factors, water conservancy factors, and economic factors (Table 5). Natural factors include climatic conditions (average annual precipitation and temperature), topography (elevation), vegetation status (cover changes), and hydrological distribution (distance to water, river network density), which are key drivers of regional ecosystem quality.64,65 Water conservancy factors include water resource conditions (total water resources and spatial relation to the ecosystem), reflecting hydrological regulation of ecological processes. Economic factors include population density, GDP, distance to roads, proportion of built-up areas, and land management indicators (ecological land transfer rate, afforested area, and industrial restructuring rate), representing population concentration, economic activity intensity, and policy-driven land management practices.66 Therefore, these fourteen factors were selected to comprehensively analyze the driving mechanisms of ecological quality changes in Yichang city from 2003 to 2023, while ensuring comparability across different units and value ranges. Robustness checks (waterbody masking, carbon-density perturbations, PCA variants, climate adjustment) are summarized in Supplementary S7.

Moran’s index

The bivariate Moran’s index, integrating both global and local statistical perspectives, enables an accurate assessment of spatial dependence and heterogeneity between variables and effectively characterizes the spatial coupling between ERSEI and carbon storage. The analysis was conducted using GeoDa software. The global Moran’s I ranges from −1 to 1, where positive values indicate spatial clustering between the two variables, with larger values reflecting stronger positive correlations; negative values denote spatial dispersion, with smaller values indicating stronger negative correlations; and values approaching zero suggest no significant spatial association. Spatial heterogeneity between the two variables was further identified using the local Moran’s I, and the local indicators of spatial association (LISA) cluster analysis classified the spatial patterns into five categories: high–high (H–H), high–low (H–L), low–high (L–H), low–low (L–L), and non-significant. In H–H areas, both variables exhibit high values, while in L–L areas both variables show low values, and both patterns represent positive spatial correlations. In H–L areas, ERSEI shows high values whereas carbon storage is low, while in L–H areas ERSEI is low and carbon storage is high; both patterns indicate significant negative spatial associations.
