Delayed-onset cavernous sinus dural arteriovenous fistula after nasoseptal flap reconstruction: illustrative case
Authors: Toshiki Ishikura, Kentaro Horiguchi, Katsuya Hayashi, Tatsuma Matsuda, Masaaki Kubota, Yosuke Tajima, Yoshinori Higuchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDPedicled nasoseptal flap (NSF) is the gold-standard skull base reconstruction method in endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES). Although vascular complications, such as internal carotid artery injury or carotid-cavernous fistula (CCF), are well documented, dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF) originating from the vascular network of an NSF is exceedingly rare. Endoscopic endonasal surgery (EES) has become the standard approach for skull base tumors, with pedicled nasoseptal flap (NSF) serving as the gold standard for reconstruction.1–4 Although acute vascular injuries, such as internal carotid artery laceration5 or carotid-cavernous fistula (CCF),6,7 have been well documented, delayed vascular complications such as dural arteriovenous fistula (dAVF) remain rare. Although postcraniotomy dAVFs8 are known to occur during healing of the dura mater, dAVFs arising from an extracranial tissue graft such as the NSF have not been previously reported. Herein, we present a unique case in which the vascular supply of the NSF induced a shunt into the cavernous sinus (CS).

Illustrative Case

History

A 50-year-old woman presented with visual impairment due to a tuberculum sellae meningioma (Fig. 1A and B). The patient underwent gross-total resection via EES (Fig. 1C and D), followed by multilayered skull base reconstruction using a pedicled NSF (Fig. 1E–H). Surgical Procedure

An endoscopic endonasal approach was performed for tumor resection. During the initial phase, the right SPA was carefully identified using a Doppler probe and preserved to harvest a highly vascularized right pedicled NSF. Following bone removal over the sella and planum sphenoidale, intraoperative venous bleeding from the CS was encountered. This was meticulously controlled using a flowable hemostatic matrix and a collagen matrix. After achieving a Simpson grade 2 resection of the tuberculum sellae meningioma, a rigorous multilayered skull base reconstruction was performed. A fascia lata graft was placed in an inlay fashion and sutured to the superior dural margin using 6-0 Prolene. This was followed by an onlay fascia lata graft and rigid reinforcement using the bony nasal septum. Finally, the preserved right NSF was rotated to cover the defect as the outermost layer of the multilayered reconstruction, which was then reinforced with a superficial layer of collagen matrix and fibrin glue.

Postoperative Course

Twenty-one days postoperatively, the patient experienced delayed hemorrhage from the sphenoid sinus. Endoscopic inspection revealed bleeding from the granulation tissue in the deep nasal cavity, ultimately managed with bipolar coagulation and gauze packing (Fig. 2). Diagnosis

Five months later, the patient developed pulsatile tinnitus on the right side. MRI revealed abnormal early enhancement near the right CS (Fig. 3). Digital subtraction angiography confirmed a dAVF (Borden type I9), with feeders from the sphenopalatine artery (SPA), accessory meningeal artery (AMA), and artery of foramen rotundum (AFR) supplying the NSF vascular bed, with subsequent arterial inflow into the CS. Drainage was noted through the pterygoid plexus and inferior petrosal sinus (IPS) without cortical venous reflux (Fig. 4). Management

Initially, daily manual compression was recommended, but the symptoms and MRI findings did not improve. Endovascular treatment, specifically transvenous coil embolization via the IPS, was subsequently considered. However, the patient declined the intervention due to work commitments and mild symptomatology. Importantly, the dAVF was classified as Borden type I, with no cortical venous reflux and no disease progression. Therefore, we opted for close clinical and radiological observation.

Discussion

Observations

This case represents a rare instance of dAVF developing after endoscopic endonasal skull base reconstruction. The clinical course suggests a unique pathophysiological process associated with staged surgical and hemostatic interventions. The pathophysiology in this case highlights a novel mechanism of dAVF formation following skull base reconstruction.

Mechanism of Shunt Formation: Double-Hit Hypothesis

The development of dAVFs is commonly associated with local inflammation, venous hypertension, or surgical trauma.10 In the present case, we propose that a “double-hit” mechanism of external factors was crucial. The “first hit” was the primary surgical approach (EES), while the “second hit” was the hemostatic procedures—specifically bipolar coagulation and pressure packing—conducted to treat the delayed hemorrhage 21 days postoperatively. This 3-week interval coincides with the peak of the proliferative phase in wound healing, in which intense neovascularization occurs. The combination of these interventions likely promotes vessel wall fragility, venous congestion, and localized inflammation.

While pedicled NSF is superior for reconstruction due to its robust vascularity and healing potential, these physiological advantages, including the rich cytokine release and active angiogenesis, may have paradoxically facilitated the formation of a pathological shunt between the well-vascularized flap and the CS. We hypothesized that this highly angiogenic environment drives the significant upregulation of factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor, which is known to play a key role in the pathogenesis of dAVFs.11

Anatomical Considerations

Anatomical factors provide further insight into the development of this specific shunt. Under normal physiological conditions, the SPA supplies the lateral nasal wall and septum.12 However, during EES, the SPA is transposed to an ectopic location, such as the floor of the sphenoid sinus or the planum sphenoidale. This repositioning brings the highly vascularized NSF closer to the CS. Furthermore, the AMA likely plays a significant role in the formation of a dAVF. Indeed, the AMA passes through the foramen ovale,13 placing it in close physical contact with the vascular bed of the transposed NSF. Similarly, the AFR passes through the foramen rotundum, sharing this close anatomical relationship. This anatomical proximity of both arteries, combined with the abovementioned inflammatory state, likely facilitated abnormal anastomosis and subsequent arterial inflow into the CS.14

Comparison With Existing Literature

Table 1 shows the existing literature on postoperative AVFs. Doi et al. reported on a postcraniotomy dAVF.8 Such cases typically involve flow from the middle meningeal artery (MMA) into the sinus through a dural incision line. Our case is distinct from theirs in that the shunt occurred through the transposed “nasal mucosa” of the NSF rather than the native dura. Lannon et al. and Akdag et al. reported on extracranial flaps.15, 16 While AVFs have been reported following free-flap transfers in head and neck surgery, our case is a unique observation for a pedicled NSF used in skull base surgery. Clinical Significance

Overall, this case highlights that pulsatile tinnitus after EES should not be dismissed as a minor postoperative change or simple eustachian tube dysfunction. In patients who have undergone reconstruction with NSF, particularly those with a history of postoperative bleeding or local inflammation, prompt vascular evaluation via MR angiography or digital subtraction angiography is required to rule out dAVF. Early diagnosis is also essential for managing the potential risks of hemorrhage and neurological complications.

Lessons

This case shows that a pedicled NSF can serve as a vascular feeder for dAVFs after skull base reconstruction. Surgeons should be aware of this rare delayed complication, particularly in patients who present with symptoms such as pulsatile tinnitus months after the initial procedure.
