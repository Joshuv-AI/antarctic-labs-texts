Smart Nanogel Systems for Periodontitis Management: A Review of Microenvironment-Targeted Therapies
Authors: Shuqi Sun, Dongning He, Jingyi Yang, Wenjing Hou, Yang Yang, Lijun Xue, Bin Zhao, Feiyan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeriodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease driven by microbial dysbiosis and characterized by an exaggerated host immune response, leading to progressive and irreversible alveolar bone resorption. Current therapies, such as mechanical debridement and antibiotics, are empirical and fail to address the dynamic pathological microenvironment, resulting in incomplete pathogen eradication and poor tissue regeneration. Recent advances in nanotechnology have enabled the development of nanogel-based systems that intelligently adapt to key microenvironmental features, including pH fluctuations, reactive oxygen species (ROS), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and microbial biofilms, enabling on-demand drug release, synergistic antibacterial effects, and enhanced regeneration. This review aims to provide a comprehensive and critical overview of responsive nanogels for precise periodontitis treatment. Based on a systematic analysis of the literature published in recent years, the material basis and design principles of nanogels are comprehensively summarized, with emphasis on innovative strategies for periodontal microgels, and the mechanisms of regulation of infection, inflammation and tissue remodeling are discussed. Special emphasis is placed on recent advances in multifunctional nanogel systems that enable more precise and controlled therapeutic interventions. In addition, this review critically discusses the advantages and limitations of different response strategies and outlines key challenges related to biosafety, reproducibility, scalability, and clinical transition. Overall, responsive nanogels represent a promising platform for improving efficacy and specificity of periodontal treatments, although further efforts are needed to facilitate their translation into clinical applications.Keywords: nanogel, periodontitis, microenvironment, stimuli-responsive drug delivery, tissue regeneration

Introduction

Periodontitis is one of the most prevalent oral diseases, affecting millions of individuals worldwide and recognized as a leading cause of tooth loss among adults. Beyond its impact on oral health, periodontitis has been strongly associated with systemic conditions such as diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and adverse pregnancy outcomes, underscoring its substantial public health burden.1 In particular, a bidirectional relationship has been established between periodontitis and diabetes, in which chronic inflammation may exacerbate insulin resistance and hyperglycemia may further aggravate periodontal tissue. This interaction demonstrates the importance of effective periodontal treatment not only for oral health but also for overall systemic disease management.2 The disease is initiated by microbial biofilm accumulation, which triggers host immune and inflammatory responses. This host-microbe interaction leads to pH fluctuations, excessive accumulation of ROS, and elevated levels of MMPs. ROS, which are generated during inflammation and possess strong tissue-damaging capabilities, can cause oxidative injury. MMPs, which are enzymes that decompose structural proteins such as collagen, accelerate tissue degradation. Collectively, these effects contribute to immune dysregulation and progressive destruction of periodontal tissue.3 These pathological factors act synergistically, perpetuating chronic inflammation and tissue degradation.4 Conventional therapeutic approaches, including scaling and root planing, as well as systemic or topical antibiotics and antibiotic modulators, offer certain clinical benefits but are limited by challenges such as poor drug penetration into the periodontal pocket, short residence time, and potential drug interactions.5 These limitations are intrinsically linked to the dynamic, heterogeneous, and multifactorial nature of the periodontal microenvironment, which conventional static delivery systems cannot adequately address. Therefore, there is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies capable of precisely targeting and reconstructing the periodontal microenvironment.

Nanotechnology-based drug delivery systems have emerged as promising solutions to address the limitations of conventional periodontal therapies. Among these, nanogels, which are three-dimensional, cross-linked polymeric networks at the nanoscale, offer distinct advantages, including high biocompatibility, tunable size and porosity, and the capacity to encapsulate a wide range of therapeutic agents.6 Notably, nanogels can be engineered to respond to endogenous stimuli inherent to the pathological microenvironment, such as microenvironmental pH fluctuations and elevated reactive oxygen species, as well as to external triggers including temperature and light. This responsiveness enables precise spatiotemporal control over drug release and enhances adaptability within inflamed periodontal tissues.7 Crucially, the unique combination of the hydrogel’s hydrated, biocompatible nature and the nanoparticle’s high penetration efficiency makes nanogels an ideal platform for navigating the complex periodontal milieu. By leveraging these microenvironment-responsive properties, nanogel-based systems facilitate targeted and dynamic therapeutic interventions, offering new prospects for effective periodontitis management. In addition to nanogels, various nanocarrier-based systems, including liposomes, polymer micelles, and inorganic nanoparticles, have been explored for periodontal therapy.1,8 These systems also show advantages in drug encapsulation and delivery; However, they are often subject to limitations, such as insufficient local retention, limited response to dynamic periodontal microenvironment or lack of multifunctional integration. In contrast, nanogels uniquely combine the structural characteristics of hydrogels with nanosize, resulting in enhanced tissue permeability, high drug loading, and responsive behavior. In this review, we first outline the material basis and structural design principles for nanogel fabrication, then analyze in depth representative mechanisms and examples, and explore the role of these systems in regulating periodontal microstructures through antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue regeneration pathways. From a clinical point of view, the possibility of advanced nanogel systems offers new opportunities to improve local drug delivery, improve therapeutic efficacy, and systemic side effects in periodontal treatment. Despite the rapidity of nanogel-based systems, several important challenges remain inadequately addressed. Current studies generally focus on proof-of-concept designs, with limited comparative analysis of different response strategies and insufficient evaluation of their reproducibility, biosafety and clinical applicability. In addition, the dynamic changes of periodontal microstructures present a major challenge to achieve precise treatment. Therefore, a comprehensive and critical summary of current advances is needed to better understand the strengths and limitations of different nanogel systems and to identify key barriers to clinical translation. Finally, we summarize current challenges and suggest future directions for clinical translation. This review aims to systematically summarize the design strategies and functional mechanisms of responsive nanogels in periodontal therapy, while also providing an important basis for their limitations and future translational potential.

The Periodontitis Microenvironment: A Complex Pathological Milieu

Microbial Dysbiosis as the Initiating Factor of Periodontitis

Dental plaque biofilm is widely recognized as the primary etiological factor in periodontitis. Composed of bacteria and their secreted extracellular polymeric substances, it forms a complex, adherent structure on tooth surfaces, within interproximal spaces, and around restorative materials.9 This biofilm constitutes not only a highly organized microbial ecosystem but also exhibits enhanced resistance to host immune defenses and antimicrobial therapies due to its intricate three-dimensional architecture and diverse microbial composition, thereby playing a central role in the initiation and progression of periodontal disease.10 Under physiological conditions, the host maintains a dynamic equilibrium with the microorganisms colonizing the periodontal environment, preserving microbial homeostasis. However, in the pathological state of periodontitis, dysregulation of the host immune response interacts synergistically with the colonization and expansion of pathogenic bacterial communities, resulting in sustained inflammatory stimulation.11 This process leads not only to progressive degradation of both soft and hard periodontal tissues but also significantly increases therapeutic complexity, thereby imposing greater demands on the precision and efficacy of drug delivery systems. These pathological features suggest that advanced drug delivery systems that can improve biofilm-associated drug resistance and achieve precise treatment in periodontal microscopes are very important for the treatment of periodontitis.

Immune Dysregulation and Consequent Microenvironmental Alterations

As the disease progresses, pathogenic bacteria disrupt the homeostasis between the host and the microbial community, triggering an exaggerated inflammatory response that results in the destruction of alveolar bone and periodontal attachment structures.12 Concurrently, metabolic byproducts generated from tissue degradation serve as nutrient sources for pathogenic microorganisms, further aggravating microbial dysbiosis.13 Consequently, the pathological microenvironment of periodontitis arises from the complex interplay among these pathological processes, forming a dynamic cascade characterized by initiation, inflammatory amplification, tissue destruction, and the establishment of a self-perpetuating vicious cycle. Within this evolving milieu, microenvironmental pH fluctuations, ROS, MMPs, and microbial imbalance function as key interconnected factors that mutually reinforce one another (Figure 1). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the intricate characteristics of the periodontal microenvironment is essential not only for elucidating the underlying pathogenesis of periodontitis but also for informing the development of precise drug delivery systems and targeted therapeutic interventions.

The Dynamic pH Environment

Although the precise pathogenesis of periodontitis has not yet been fully elucidated, extensive research consistently indicates that genetic susceptibility and bacterial biofilm formation are central to disease initiation and progression. Among the diverse array of periodontal pathogens, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Fusobacterium nucleatum, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Prevotella intermedia, and Treponema denticola are widely recognized as key etiological agents.14 Within mature dental plaque biofilms, steep chemical gradients develop as a result of bacterial metabolic activity.9 In the deeper layers of the biofilm, concentrations of oxygen and nutrients decrease progressively, along with a decline in pH, while metabolic by-products, such as organic acids including lactic acid and butyric acid, accumulate, leading to transiently acidified microdomains within the periodontal pocket. This acidic microenvironment compromises the structural integrity of the gingival epithelial barrier, facilitates the invasion of pathogens and their virulence factors into underlying tissues, and reduces the chemical stability and bioactivity of antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents, thereby diminishing therapeutic efficacy.15 Clinically, this slight change is closely related to the depth of periodontal pocket and the poor treatment effect.

However, recent studies have provided new insights into the dynamic nature of pH in periodontal lesions. In healthy individuals, gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) typically maintains a neutral pH of approximately 7.0.16 Notably, clinical investigations have shown that the severity of periodontitis often correlates with an increase in GCF pH.17 This alkalinization is closely linked to metabolic shifts in the subgingival microbiota.18 As a result, the pH at periodontal sites exhibits significant spatiotemporal variability and heterogeneity: transient acidification may occur during acute inflammatory episodes or localized anaerobic metabolism, whereas chronic or advanced lesions frequently display a neutral to mildly alkaline environment.

These dynamic pH fluctuations provide a critical theoretical basis for the rational design of intelligent drug delivery systems. To date, the majority of pH-responsive nanogels have been engineered to respond to acidic conditions, with drug release triggered by a drop in pH, making them suitable for early-stage inflammation characterized by local acidification. However, in chronic or late-stage periodontitis, where the microenvironment tends to be neutral or slightly alkaline, such acid-responsive systems may fail to achieve adequate activation. Therefore, the development of alkaline-responsive nanogels capable of stage-specific responsiveness and precise modulation across different phases of periodontal disease represents a promising and innovative direction for future therapeutic strategies.

Excessive ROS

ROS exhibit a classic double-edged sword effect in the pathophysiology of periodontitis. At physiological levels, ROS play a crucial role in host defense mechanisms; however, when overproduced, they become a primary contributor to tissue damage. The colonization of periodontal pathogens leads to the continuous recruitment and activation of polymorphonuclear leukocytes (PMNs), which generate substantial amounts of ROS via the NADPH oxidase pathway. These cells also synergistically release defensins and antimicrobial peptides and can form neutrophil extracellular trap networks (NETs) to limit pathogen dissemination.

Nevertheless, during both the early and chronic stages of periodontitis, persistent inflammatory stimulation results in ROS production that significantly exceeds the capacity of endogenous antioxidant defenses, leading to a state of oxidative stress. Under these conditions, ROS are no longer confined to their bactericidal functions but begin to inflict damage on host tissues: they disrupt the lipid bilayer of cell membranes, initiating lipid peroxidation; induce DNA strand breaks and oxidative DNA lesions;19 promote protein oxidation and conformational alterations;20 and stimulate the release of pro-inflammatory mediators by activating key signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK, thereby amplifying tissue destruction.21 Numerous studies indicate that in this oxidative milieu, the apoptosis rate of gingival fibroblasts is elevated, impairing their proliferative and regenerative capacities. This contributes to enhanced alveolar bone resorption and progressive degradation of both soft and hard periodontal tissues. These pathological changes are closely related to clinical manifestations such as attachment loss and tooth loosening.

Thus, excessive ROS accumulation in periodontitis serves dual roles as a component of innate immunity and as a key mediator of pathological tissue breakdown. An imbalance in redox homeostasis is now recognized as one of the core mechanisms underlying disease initiation and progression. ROS levels correlate closely with clinical severity and represent promising targets for precision therapeutic interventions. In recent years, several antioxidant-based strategies have been explored in preclinical models aiming to restore redox balance, demonstrating potential in mitigating inflammation and supporting tissue regeneration. Oxidative stress thus functions not only as a consequence of chronic inflammation but also as an active driver of disease progression. It causes direct cellular injury, enhances MMP activation, and interacts synergistically with acidic microenvironmental conditions to further compromise tissue integrity. Consequently, targeting oxidative stress to reestablish redox equilibrium has emerged as a rational and promising strategy for controlling inflammation and promoting periodontal tissue repair. Importantly, elevated ROS levels in periodontitis tissues provide specific triggers for the design of ROS-responsive nanogels. This specific system can achieve precise drug release in response to oxidative stress, thereby improving therapeutic accuracy.22

Overexpression of MMPs

The primary histopathological features of periodontitis include extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells, degradation of the principal fiber bundles of the periodontal ligament, and progressive resorption of alveolar bone. This pathological process is driven by a cascade of inflammatory mediators and effector molecules, including interleukins (ILs), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), and MMPs. Among these, MMPs are regarded as central effector enzymes responsible for the destruction of periodontal tissues.23 MMPs constitute a family of zinc-dependent endopeptidases capable of degrading various components of the extracellular matrix (ECM) and basement membrane.24 Under physiological conditions, MMP activity is tightly regulated through a dynamic balance with their endogenous inhibitors, tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinases (TIMPs), ensuring controlled ECM turnover and tissue remodeling.25 However, in periodontitis, this equilibrium is disrupted: MMPs are markedly overexpressed, while TIMP-mediated inhibition is insufficient, resulting in a net shift toward excessive tissue degradation over repair. In particular, MMP-8 and MMP-9 are strongly implicated in the breakdown of periodontal supporting structures. Elevated levels of these enzymes in GCF and periodontal tissues consistently correlate with clinical disease severity, positioning them as valuable biomarkers for both diagnosis and assessment of disease activity.26

Notably, MMPs can also indirectly amplify inflammation by cleaving and activating cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors, thereby exacerbating local tissue damage.27 The sustained accumulation of inflammatory cells at periodontal lesion sites serves as a major source of aberrant MMP production.28 Concurrently, pathogens within dental plaque biofilms can stimulate host cells, including fibroblasts, macrophages, and epithelial cells, to upregulate MMP expression. Moreover, MMP expression is modulated by multiple regulatory factors, such as hormonal fluctuations, oncogenic signaling pathways, and interactions between cells and the ECM. Importantly, MMP overexpression acts as a critical downstream consequence of acidic microenvironments and ROS-induced oxidative stress, directly degrading structural ECM components and cell adhesion molecules while further propagating inflammation via cytokine processing. This creates a self-sustaining cycle of tissue destruction. Consequently, dysregulated MMP activity and the imbalance in the MMP/TIMP ratio are widely acknowledged as pivotal mechanisms in the pathogenesis and progression of periodontitis. These features provide a theoretical basis for MMPs-responsive nanogels that target drug release at active tissue sites and may inhibit ECM degradation while promoting tissue repair. These molecular alterations not only deepen our understanding of disease mechanisms but also offer promising targets for novel diagnostic tools and precision therapeutic interventions.

Microbial Dysbiosis

Microbial dysbiosis plays a central role in the progression of periodontitis. Driven by genetic susceptibility or environmental factors such as smoking and poor oral hygiene, the host immune response becomes aberrantly activated, resulting in a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation that further destabilizes the oral microbial ecosystem.29,30 Anaerobic pathogens colonize and proliferate within periodontal pockets and on exposed dentin surfaces, secreting virulence factors, including toxins and proteases, that compromise the integrity of the gingival epithelial barrier and contribute to tissue destruction.31

Notably, Porphyromonas gingivalis, recognized as a “keystone pathogen”, can restructure both the composition and functional profile of the entire subgingival microbial community. Even at low abundance, it promotes persistent inflammation by subverting host immune defenses and manipulating inflammatory signaling pathways.32 Furthermore, these pathogens do not exist in isolation but adhere to tooth surfaces as highly organized, multilayered biofilms. Such biofilms confer significant advantages: they serve as physical barriers that limit the penetration of antimicrobial agents and shield bacteria from host immune effectors, while also enabling coordinated regulation of metabolism and virulence gene expression through quorum-sensing mechanisms. These features substantially enhance bacterial resilience, promoting antimicrobial resistance and immune evasion.33 Therefore, drug delivery systems that can penetrate biofilms, regulate microbial composition, and overcome bacterial resistance characteristics are extremely necessary for the treatment of periodontitis, and these problems are expected to be effectively resolved through nanogel-based platforms.

Inflammatory byproducts and oxidative metabolites generated during tissue breakdown serve as nutrient sources that facilitate bacterial growth, whereas bacterial toxins perpetuate and amplify the inflammatory response. This establishes a self-reinforcing feedback loop between microbial dysbiosis and chronic inflammation. Collectively, these interactions underscore that the pathogenesis of periodontitis is not driven by isolated factors but emerges from a complex, interconnected network involving microbial communities, host immune dysregulation, and pathological mediators. Collectively, these interacting pathological features highlight the complexity of periodontal microdentition, which poses a major challenge to conventional treatment strategies. These features highlight the need for advanced drug delivery systems capable of responding to multiple microenvironment with precision and controllability. In this context, responsive nanogels have become particularly promising platforms due to their ability to integrate targeted delivery, responsiveness, and multifunctional therapeutic capabilities.

Current Therapeutic Approaches and Their Limitations

In the clinical management of periodontitis, treatment strategies typically involve a multimodal approach that integrates basic interventions, pharmacological support, surgical procedures, and long-term maintenance, tailored to disease severity, in order to control inflammation and restore periodontal health. The cornerstone of nonsurgical therapy remains mechanical debridement, including supragingival scaling, subgingival curettage, and root planing.34 However, the complex anatomical configuration of deep periodontal pockets and furcation areas, combined with the presence of pathogenic biofilms, limits complete microbial clearance. In addition, treatment outcomes show considerable individual variation and are significantly influenced by patient-related factors such as smoking, diabetes mellitus, oral hygiene habits and treatment adherence, all of which affect treatment efficacy and disease relapse.35

Adjunctive pharmacotherapy using locally delivered sustained-release antimicrobial agents or short-term systemic antibiotics can partially suppress periodontal pathogens. Nevertheless, bacterial biofilms exhibit high levels of drug tolerance, locally applied formulations often suffer from poor retention within the pocket, and systemic administration carries risks of microbiome disruption and organ toxicity.36 While these pharmacological strategies may provide short-term clinical benefits, they often have unachievable and predictable treatment outcomes.

Surgical approaches, including flap surgery, bone grafting, and guided tissue regeneration, aim to improve access, reduce pocket depth, and facilitate tissue regeneration. While these procedures can modify the local environment and support structural recovery, they are associated with significant invasiveness, prolonged healing periods, and outcomes that depend heavily on both patient-specific factors and operator expertise. Therefore, the efficacy of these methods remains uncertain, especially in patients with moderate to severe periodontitis.

In conclusion, conventional treatment of periodontitis has clear clinical value, but is limited by incomplete biofilm removal, insufficient drug retention, systemic side effects and high recurrence rate. These limitations are closely related to the complex periodontal microstructure. Consequently, material-based therapeutic strategies have gained increasing attention for their potential to overcome the multifaceted challenges posed by this hostile milieu. In particular, localized drug delivery systems that combine nanoparticles with hydrogels offer enhanced drug retention within periodontal pockets and improved penetration through biofilm structures, enabling synergistic antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative effects.37 As versatile and stimuli-responsive carriers, nanogels can encapsulate multiple therapeutic agents, including antibiotics, anti-inflammatory molecules, and growth factors, and facilitate precise, microenvironment-triggered release. This makes them a promising platform for intelligent, multifunctional periodontal therapy.38,39 These properties make nanogels a promising candidate for smart and versatile periodontal treatments. However, nanogel-based systems also have some important limitations. Their therapeutic performance is highly dependent on periodontal microheterogeneity, which can lead to inconsistent reactivity and unpredictable treatment outcomes.40 In addition, biosafety-related challenges, including degradation behaviour, potential accumulation and immunogenicity, remain understudied. And the large-scale manufacturing of nanogels, especially for complex multi-response systems, remains technically demanding.41 Therefore, for nanogels, equilibrium assessment of its advantages and limitations is essential. Addressing these challenges is essential to advance nanogel systems towards intra-clinical periodontal therapy.

Material Basis and Design Principles of Nanogels

Concept and Inherent Advantages

Nanogels are a class of nanoscale hydrogel particles formed through the chemical or physical cross-linking of hydrophilic polymers, combining the high biocompatibility of hydrogels with the large specific surface area characteristic of nanocarriers. Their highly hydrated, three-dimensional network structure mimics the natural tissue microenvironment, effectively protecting encapsulated drugs from degradation or inactivation, thereby enhancing drug stability. Simultaneously, nanogels improve drug permeability and local bioavailability within periodontal tissues, leading to enhanced therapeutic efficacy.42

Hydrogel-based drug delivery systems also have controllable drug release characteristics, diverse loads, and responsiveness to responses,43 but generally speaking, they are difficult to reach deep periodontal pockets and cannot well enter dense biofilms.44 Because of its nano-scale size and large surface area to volume ratio, nano-gel can better penetrate into complex periodontal microstructures and have the advantages of hydrogels. This distinction highlights the unique value of nanogels over hydrogel systems for periodontal applications.

To overcome these limitations, nanogels integrate the properties of nanoparticles and hydrogels into a “cascade delivery system”, capitalizing on the advantages of both platforms.45 On one hand, the nanoscale size and high surface-to-volume ratio of nanoparticles significantly enhance penetration into periodontal tissues and biofilm structures, while the hydrogel matrix prolongs local retention, resulting in synergistic improvements in delivery efficiency.46 On the other hand, multifunctional composite designs can combine nanocatalytic functionalities with the anti-inflammatory or osteogenic properties of hydrogels, enabling multi-targeted therapeutic strategies. Moreover, surface engineering of nanoparticles, combined with microenvironment-responsive drug release from the hydrogel component, can enhance selective targeting and killing of periodontal pathogens, offering a novel approach for modulating the periodontal microbiome with precision.

Material Types and Functional Properties

The selection of materials for nanogels directly influences their biocompatibility, drug loading capacity, and responsiveness to the pathological microenvironment. Currently, commonly used materials primarily include natural polymers, synthetic polymers, and functionalized or chemically modified components. Natural polymers, such as gelatin, hyaluronic acid (HA), and alginates, exhibit excellent biodegradability and cellular compatibility, enabling efficient encapsulation of anti-inflammatory agents and bioactive molecules through electrostatic interactions or covalent bonding. Zussman et al47 developed an injectable gelatin-based hydrogel capable of sustained in vitro release of chlorhexidine for periodontitis treatment. This system facilitates prolonged drug delivery under acidic conditions, effectively inhibiting the growth of pathogenic bacteria (Figure 2A). The therapeutic potential of hyaluronic acid in gingival and periodontal tissues is well supported by extensive clinical and preclinical evidence, demonstrating that HA-based gels can ameliorate inflammatory conditions and promote regeneration of both soft and hard periodontal tissues. Rosaming et al48 formulated in situ-forming hydrogels using methylated hyaluronic acid (MeHA) combined with short-chain HA. In both in vivo and in vitro studies, these formulations exhibited favorable gelation kinetics, mechanical strength, volumetric expansion, and controlled degradation profiles, while also supporting periodontal tissue repair (Figure 2B). Hyaluronic acid-based nanogels not only prolong retention within periodontal pockets but also actively facilitate gingival tissue regeneration.49

Synthetic polymers such as polyethylene glycol (PEG), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), and polyacrylic acid (PAA) are widely employed due to their structural tunability and consistent reproducibility across batches. By modulating molecular weight, crosslinking density, and hydrophilic–hydrophobic ratios, the drug release kinetics and mechanical properties of nanogels can be precisely tailored. For example, PEG-crosslinked nanogels loaded with anti-inflammatory drugs utilize oxidative stress-sensitive linkages that undergo cleavage in high-ROS environments, enabling site-specific drug release and minimizing systemic side effects50 (Figure 2C). PAA-based nanogels respond to the acidic microenvironment characteristic of periodontitis, facilitating rapid and pH-triggered drug release51 (Figure 2D).

The integration of functionalized moieties further enhances the stimulus-responsive capabilities of nanogels. For instance, thiol and borate groups exhibit sensitivity to elevated ROS levels, undergoing oxidation in inflammatory microenvironments where ROS are overproduced, thereby triggering controlled drug release52 (Figure 2E). Similarly, dynamic covalent structures such as Schiff bases are pH-labile and degrade under acidic conditions, enabling microenvironment-responsive payload delivery53 (Figure 2F). The incorporation of such responsive chemical motifs allows nanogels to achieve spatiotemporally precise drug release in complex disease settings like periodontitis, which are characterized by concurrent acidity and oxidative stress.

Although these materials have good application prospects, safety issues remain a major concern before clinical application. Cytotoxicity tests for synthetic polymers, degradation byproducts, and residual cross-linkers are required. In addition, due to the interaction between nanogels and the host immune system, it may cause unnecessary inflammation or immune response. Moreover, nano-gel is used to kill pathogenic bacteria, and it may also have a normal oral flora balance, which is not conducive to periodontal maintenance. Therefore, in future materials, we should not only pay attention to efficacy, but also biosafety, immune compatibility and microecological balance.

Advanced Drug Loading and Controlled Release Mechanisms

The high specific surface area and tunable three-dimensional network structure of nanogels confer distinct advantages for drug loading and controlled release. Typically, drugs can be incorporated into nanogels through three primary mechanisms: First, physical adsorption relies on electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic forces to encapsulate small-molecule antibacterial or anti-inflammatory agents; while simple and effective, this method often results in burst release. Second, chemical conjugation involves anchoring drug molecules to the polymer backbone via cleavable covalent bonds, enabling significantly enhanced control over release kinetics. Third, molecular self-assembly, particularly in peptide-based or block copolymer systems, facilitates efficient drug incorporation through reversible intermolecular interactions, emerging in recent years as a promising strategy for improving loading capacity and stability.54

Controlled drug release is a central design principle in nanogel development. By modulating crosslinking density, polymer chain flexibility, and network pore size, the diffusion rate of encapsulated therapeutics can be precisely regulated. For example, lower crosslinking density allows faster drug release, making it suitable for rapid suppression of early-stage inflammation; in contrast, highly crosslinked networks prolong drug retention, supporting sustained therapeutic action in the management of chronic periodontitis. Moreover, the hydrophilic–hydrophobic balance of the matrix directly affects the release behavior of hydrophobic anti-inflammatory agents, a critical consideration in the complex and dynamic environment of periodontal pockets. In clinical practice, effective treatment of periodontitis requires balancing multiple therapeutic objectives: immediate antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory effects must be integrated with long-term stimulation of tissue regeneration. To meet these demands, researchers are increasingly adopting multi-drug delivery systems designed for sequential release, such as an initial anti-inflammatory followed by osteogenic phase, achieved through spatial compartmentalization or hierarchical structural engineering. For instance, antibacterial agents and osteoinductive factors can be loaded into separate functional layers within the nanogel, enabling time-programmed release via differential diffusion rates that align with the temporal progression of disease pathology.

In addition, the problems that may be encountered in the clinical transformation process also need to be considered. How to accurately control the size of nano-gel, cross-linking degree and loading rate. Sterilization will lead to some physical or chemical changes in nanogel. In addition, ensuring consistency in largescale production is also difficult, especially for more complex multi-component or responsive systems. Overall, the drug loading and release mechanisms of nanogels are determined not only by the intrinsic physicochemical properties of the constituent materials but also by the need for temporal precision and spatial targeting in periodontal therapy. Future research will likely focus on integrating multiple loading strategies with stimuli-responsive and tiered release profiles to develop more intelligent, adaptive, and personalized drug delivery platforms.

Design Principles for Periodontal Applications

When designing nanogels for the treatment of periodontitis, the pathological characteristics of the lesion microenvironment must be comprehensively considered. First, acid-sensitive chemical linkages should be incorporated to respond to acidic conditions, oxidation-sensitive functional groups introduced to react to elevated ROS levels, and enzyme-labile cross-linked structures implemented to enable degradation in protease-rich environments. Second, nanogel size should be optimized to facilitate penetration into gingival tissues and deep periodontal pockets while maintaining controlled drug release kinetics. In terms of design, biocompatibility and safety are paramount; Materials must have good biodegradability, minimal cytotoxicity, low immunogenicity and minimal to normal oral microorganisms. In addition, the multi-response strategy allows precise release of drugs spatiotemporally, resulting in improved targeting accuracy and therapeutic efficacy. Importantly, factors related to manufacturability, sterilization compatibility and scalability should be considered early in the design phase to enhance the translational potential of nanogel-based systems.

Stimuli-Responsive Nanogels for Precision Targeting

The complex microenvironment of periodontitis is characterized not only by biochemical factors such as acidity, oxidative stress, and enzyme overexpression, but also by modulation through exogenous physical stimuli. Consequently, the development of smart nanogels capable of sensing and responding to these pathological cues has become a key focus in recent years for localized, precision-based treatment of periodontal diseases. These advanced materials go beyond the conventional role of “passive drug carriers”, evolving into multifunctional “therapeutic platforms” that actively detect disease sites, enable stimulus-responsive drug release, and concurrently contribute to the remodeling of the pathological microenvironment.

pH-Responsive Nanogels for Acidic Microdomains

Although the overall pH of periodontal lesions can fluctuate between mildly acidic and neutral-to-alkaline levels depending on disease stage and local microbial activity, transient acidic microdomains frequently develop within dental plaque biofilms and inflamed periodontal tissuesparticularly during the acute phase of infection. These localized acidified regions serve as effective physiological triggers for site-specific drug release. pH-responsive nanogels are typically engineered using acid-labile chemical bonds or ionizable functional groups. Upon exposure to a lower pH environment, the polymer network undergoes structural changes such as degradation or swelling, thereby facilitating controlled drug release. For instance, Ma et al53 developed a polyglycerol-based composite nanogel (NG-TCS-DFO) co-loaded with the antibacterial agent triclosan and the pro-angiogenic molecule deferoxamine. This system rapidly degrades under acidic conditions, enabling prompt drug release, and demonstrates potent inhibition of Porphyromonas gingivalis biofilms in vitro, as well as enhanced alveolar bone regeneration in animal models (Figure 3A). Similarly, the chitosan-based dual-drug nanogel developed by Aminu et al,55 encapsulating both triclosan and flurbiprofen, exhibited accelerated drug release under acidic conditions and strong mucoadhesive properties. In vivo studies showed significant reductions in gingival inflammation indices and plaque scores, highlighting the therapeutic advantages of pH-responsive combination therapy (Figure 3B). Furthermore, Zhu et al56 fabricated an acid-responsive smart hydrogel (APPG) by grafting phenylboronic acid groups onto an alginate-methacrylate (AlgMA) scaffold. This construct rapidly disassembles at pH 4.5 to release gentamicin, while concurrently exhibiting ROS scavenging activity and promoting fibroblast migration. Another study utilized a chitosan/sodium alginate crosslinked network to modulate peptide delivery through pH-dependent charge alterations, further illustrating the potential of natural polysaccharides as versatile building blocks for pH-responsive drug delivery systems.57 Collectively, pH-responsive platforms enable multifunctional therapeutic outcomes, including rapid anti-inflammatory action, sustained antibacterial effects, and stimulation of tissue repair by dynamically responding to the acidic microenvironment characteristic of periodontal pockets.

ROS-Scavenging Nanogels for Oxidative Stress Regulation

Excessive ROS are recognized as a key pathological mechanism underlying the persistent tissue destruction observed in periodontitis. ROS-responsive nanogels typically incorporate redox-sensitive components, such as borate ester bonds, thioether linkages, or nanoenzyme systems to trigger carrier degradation and site-specific drug release in high-ROS microenvironments. The CuTA nanozyme-hydrogel system developed by Xu et al58 mimics the dual enzymatic activities of endogenous superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), enabling efficient scavenging of superoxide anions (·O2−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). In preclinical animal models, this system significantly inhibited alveolar bone resorption (Figure 3C). In studies on diabetes-associated periodontitis, Conte et al59 demonstrated that RSV@CLgel effectively reduces intracellular ROS levels, restores mitochondrial function, and promotes M2 polarization of macrophages, thereby ameliorating the inflammatory microenvironment and enhancing bone regeneration (Figure 3D). Zhan et al developed a CGRP@PVA/tsPBA hydrogel capable of selectively releasing calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) under elevated ROS conditions. This platform restored vascular-osteogenic coupling in diabetic periodontitis rats, significantly promoting H-type vessel formation and new bone formation.60 Furthermore, both Zhu et al7 and Yao et al61 engineered ROS-sensitive hydrogels crosslinked via borate ester bonds and loaded with antimicrobial agents and anti-inflammatory nanoparticles, achieving multifunctional therapeutic effects, including antibacterial activity, ROS neutralization, immunomodulation, and stimulation of stem cell osteogenic differentiation (Figure 3E). Collectively, these findings indicate that ROS-responsive nanogels transcend their traditional role as passive drug delivery vehicles and function as active “microenvironment modulators”, offering distinct advantages for targeted intervention in the complex and dynamic pathology of periodontitis.

Enzyme-Responsive Nanogels for Targeted Delivery

Overexpression of proteases represents another hallmark feature of the periodontal disease microenvironment, offering a natural biochemical trigger for enzyme-responsive nanogel systems. By integrating matrix metalloproteinase sensitive peptide sequences into the polymer network, these hydrogels can undergo site-specific degradation at inflamed tissues, enabling localized drug release. For instance, Zhou et al62 developed an MMP-2-responsive hydrogel for the delivery of GSK2606414, a selective inhibitor of the unfolded protein response (UPR)/PERK signaling pathway. In the periodontal pathological environment, elevated MMP-2 activity cleaves the peptide-based crosslinkers, thereby triggering controlled drug release. This system significantly enhanced the osteogenic differentiation capacity of periodontal ligament stem cells and reduced alveolar bone resorption in animal models (Figure 4A). Such a targeted design not only improves local drug bioavailability but also minimizes off-target effects on healthy tissues, representing a promising strategy in precision therapeutics.

Externally Triggered Nanogels (Light, Magnetic, Ultrasound)

Unlike endogenous stimuli, exogenous physical signals offer enhanced controllability for modulating the local periodontal microenvironment. Light-responsive nanogels are frequently integrated with photodynamic or photothermal therapy, enabling deep-tissue antimicrobial action and microenvironment regulation through near-infrared (NIR) light irradiation. Wang et al63 developed an NIR-II-responsive hydrogel capable of delivering combined photothermal and photodynamic therapy under 808 nm laser irradiation, while simultaneously enabling sustained release of bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2) to promote bone regeneration (Figure 4B). Zhou et al64 reported a photothermal-responsive hydrogel fabricated from a PDMM@LF-HAMA composite material, which generates mild hyperthermia upon light exposure, effectively eliminating bacteria and enhancing osteogenic differentiation of stem cells (Figure 4C). Temperature-sensitive nanogel systems represent another class of exogenous stimulus-responsive materials. The OFI@tgel system developed by Conte et al65 exploits the slight temperature elevation associated with inflammatory activity in periodontal pockets to induce sol-gel phase transition, thereby achieving prolonged local retention and controlled drug release, while significantly attenuating inflammation and modulating immune responses. Furthermore, magnetically responsive and ultrasound-triggered nanogels are currently under investigation; these systems can enhance drug diffusion and enable spatially targeted release through externally applied fields or devices. Although clinical translation of exogenous stimulus-driven systems remains limited by device dependency and procedural complexity, their capacity for multimodal, on-demand intervention highlights their potential for advancing personalized and precision-based therapeutic strategies in periodontology.

Each response strategy has its own advantages, but it also faces inherent limitations. The ph response system is conceptually simple but depends on adequate acidity, which may not be present in a certain stage of lesion.66 Similarly, ros-responsive nanogels depend on local levels of oxidative stress; Insufficiency of reactive oxygen species may lead to insufficient activation, while excessive scavenging of reactive oxygen species may lead to physiological defense of the host. The enzymatic reaction system provides higher specificity but is limited by the variability of enzymatic expression, which may lead to different treatment outcomes.67 External trigger systems can be controlled accurately, but they usually require specialized equipment, limiting their clinical usefulness. While multi-responsive nanogels are increasingly being proposed to overcome these limitations, the complexity of the system may lead to controllability and reproducibility while presenting challenges for mass production. Moreover, achieving well-coordinated sequential drug release is still technically difficult, and mismatched timing may affect efficacy. In addition, most of the currently reported nanogel systems are still in vitro or small animal experiments, and there are limited clinical data to verify their safety and efficacy in human subjects.42 Taken together, these challenges highlight that the clinical performance of responsive nanogels depends not only on the complexity of their design, but also on the complex interactions between material properties and dynamic periodontal microenvironment. Therefore, future research should prioritize simplified but stable response systems while systematically evaluating safety, reproducibility, and clinical therapeutic efficacy to bridge the gap between experimental innovation and clinical application.

Multifunctional Applications of Nanogels in Periodontitis Management

Disrupting Biofilms and Eradicating Pathogens

The onset of periodontitis is initiated by microbial dysbiosis and the establishment of drug-resistant biofilms. Key pathogens such as Porphyromonas gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum can form complex, structured biofilms through quorum sensing and the production of an extracellular polysaccharide matrix, which significantly impedes the penetration of antimicrobial agents and compromises the efficacy of host immune defenses. Conventional local therapies, including systemic antibiotics and antiseptic mouthwashes, often fail to achieve therapeutic concentrations within periodontal pockets due to limited residence time and rapid clearance. This clinical challenge has prompted extensive research into novel drug delivery platforms, among which nanogels exhibit distinct advantages owing to their superior tissue adhesion, prolonged retention, and controllable release profiles.

In recent years, researchers have adopted diverse design strategies to enhance the biofilm-disrupting capabilities of nanogels. One strategy focuses on increasing local drug concentration and extending the duration of therapeutic action. The glycopeptide hydrogel developed by Zhao et al68 undergoes adaptive in situ gelation within periodontal pockets and enables early-stage burst release of minocycline. This approach effectively eradicates major periodontal pathogens such as P. gingivalis and F. nucleatum while disrupting mature biofilm architectures (Figure 5A). Javadkhani et al69 recently engineered a chitosan-based nano-catechin gel that not only demonstrates potent antibacterial activity against clinically isolated P. gingivalis strains but also achieves sustained drug release over 70 days, with no observed cytotoxicity toward dental pulp stem cells (Figure 5B and C). A second strategy leverages pathogen-responsive release mechanisms. Xu et al reported that copper tannic acid nanoplate-incorporated hydrogels undergo hydrolytic degradation in high-MMP environments, enabling targeted release of nanoenzymes. This system exhibits broad-spectrum antibacterial effects against multiple periodontal pathogens and significantly destabilizes biofilm integrity.58 Similarly, Li et al developed a disulfide-modified hyaluronic acid-based hydrogel that degrades specifically in response to metabolites produced by P. gingivalis, facilitating precise drug release and effective clearance of intracellular bacteria, a critical advancement over conventional antibiotics, which often fail to penetrate cellular barriers.70 Beyond traditional antibiotic delivery, inorganic ion-mediated systems offer innovative antimicrobial approaches. The NapKFF-nano CaF2 hydrogel developed by Zhang et al71 exerts potent inhibition against P. gingivalis and MRSA through sustained fluoride ion release, achieving over 93% antimicrobial efficacy, thereby offering a promising alternative to conventional antibiotic regimens (Figure 5D).

Beyond their role as drug carriers, the intrinsic physicochemical properties of nanogel materials themselves contribute additional antimicrobial functionalities. For example, the silver nanoparticle/chitosan composite scaffold constructed by Srinivasan et al enhances antimicrobial activity via sustained release of Ag⁺ ions and demonstrates strong bactericidal effects against Gram-negative pathogens in vitro.74 Similarly, Kumari et al utilized green-synthesized silver nanoparticles derived from plant extracts to formulate a nanogel with dual antibacterial and antioxidant properties, showing particular promise in preclinical models of diabetic periodontitis.75 These findings suggest that future antimicrobial platforms should exploit synergistic interactions between material-inherent properties and drug-loading capacity, rather than relying exclusively on pharmacological payloads.

Notably, emerging studies have focused on integrating multifunctional systems to achieve coordinated “antibacterial–anti-inflammatory–regenerative” regulation. The bilayer hydrogel membrane designed by Dos Santos et al enables sustained co-delivery of simvastatin and grape seed extract, simultaneously suppressing pathogenic bacteria and promoting osteogenesis through immunomodulation, highlighting the potential of combinatorial therapeutic strategies.76 Compared to single-target antibacterial approaches, such integrated designs more accurately reflect the multifactorial nature of the periodontitis microenvironment.

Collectively, research on nanogel-based strategies for infection control and biofilm disruption has evolved from unifunctional antimicrobial delivery toward comprehensive, multifunctional regulation. Future development will increasingly emphasize not only enhanced delivery efficiency but also holistic modulation of microbial communities, host immune responses, and local tissue homeostasis, achieved through synergistic integration of material design and therapeutic payload. This conceptual shift provides a robust framework for advancing next-generation periodontal therapeutics.

Regulating Oxidative Stress and Inflammatory Responses

In periodontitis lesions, excessive ROS production and the sustained release of inflammatory mediators engage in a self-amplifying feedback loop, mutually exacerbating tissue damage. This pathological imbalance not only accelerates the degradation of periodontal tissues but also severely compromises endogenous repair mechanisms. Consequently, achieving dual local regulation through combined anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects has become a central objective in the design of advanced nanogel systems. A key strategy to accomplish this involves integrating anti-inflammatory agents with intrinsic antioxidant functionalities. The BiCD-Ber system developed by Li et al70 demonstrates such synergy: it not only exerts direct antimicrobial activity but also inhibits gingipain enzymes secreted by P. gingivalis, thereby preserving host adhesion proteins, minimizing cellular injury, and suppressing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Figure 5E). The CuTA nanozyme reported by Xu et al further advances this approach by exhibiting dual superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like and catalase (CAT)-like enzymatic activities. When encapsulated within MMP-responsive hydrogels, it enables site-specific delivery and controlled release at inflamed sites, effectively scavenging multiple ROS species and mitigating oxidative stress.58 Similarly, Li et al’s OBGAP system utilizes pH-sensitive Schiff base linkages to enable sustained release of gallic acid, significantly attenuating both ROS generation and inflammatory responses in a diabetic periodontitis model.77 Yu et al’s72 GPM hydrogel incorporates MXene nanosheets to achieve efficient radical scavenging; while reducing intracellular ROS levels in fibroblasts, it concurrently exerts potent anti-inflammatory effects, thus creating a favorable microenvironment for tissue regeneration (Figure 5F).

An innovative direction lies in leveraging the material synthesis process itself to confer intrinsic antioxidant properties. Kumari et al employed acerola cherry extract for the green synthesis of silver (AgNPs) and copper oxide nanoparticles (CuONPs). The high vitamin C content serves not only as a natural reducing agent during nanoparticle formation but also imparts inherent antioxidant capacity to the final formulation. This “green synthesis” strategy transcends conventional drug-loading paradigms, enabling the nanogel to simultaneously combat oxidative stress and maintain robust antibacterial activity.75 Furthermore, Zheng et al73 designed a mitochondrial-targeted TPP-SAT nanogel that converts ROS into molecular oxygen via a cascade reaction mimicking SOD and CAT activities. This system not only neutralizes oxidative stress at its primary source but also alleviates local hypoxia, resulting in significant anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective outcomes (Figure 5G). Collectively, these studies illustrate a paradigm shift in nanogel functionality—from passive drug carriers to active “microenvironment homeostasis regulators”. While diverse in material composition and design, these strategies share a common goal: disrupting the pathological positive feedback loop between chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in periodontitis.

Modulate the Immune Response

The destructive process of periodontitis arises not only from direct bacterial invasion but also from dysregulation of the host immune response. Overactivated neutrophils and M1-polarized macrophages release excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines and MMPs, which collectively accelerate connective tissue degradation and alveolar bone resorption. Consequently, restoring immunological homeostasis has emerged as a pivotal objective in modern therapeutic strategies. Nanogels offer a versatile platform for targeted immune modulation. On one hand, they serve as efficient carriers for nucleic acid-based therapeutics, enabling site-specific delivery of siRNA or miRNA to silence key genes involved in inflammatory signaling pathways. For example, studies have demonstrated marked reductions in inflammatory mediator expression through suppression of NF-κB pathway components. On the other hand, nanogels can encapsulate immunomodulatory agents such as interleukin-10 (IL-10), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), or mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived exosomes to promote macrophage polarization toward the reparative M2 phenotype, thereby mitigating tissue damage and facilitating regenerative processes.78 The BiCD-Ber system reported by Li et al exemplifies this dual functionality: it not only exerts antimicrobial effects but also inhibits gingipains—key virulence factors produced by pathogenic bacteria—thus preserving host cell surface proteins and rebalancing cytokine profiles, indicating potential for active immune reprogramming.70

Research on biomimetic nanomaterials presents particularly innovative advances. The F127/nHAM system developed by Zhao et al incorporates membrane vesicles derived from myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs). By leveraging surface ectoenzymes to convert extracellular ATP into adenosine, a potent immunosuppressive molecule, it effectively dampens T-cell hyperactivation and significantly reduces levels of inflammatory cytokines.79 Yan et al engineered a biomimetic nanoparticle formulation, MZ@PNM, coated with macrophage membranes. This system mimics natural receptor-ligand interactions by binding specifically to P. gingivalis, thereby preventing bacterial interference with host immunity while restoring macrophage phagocytic and bactericidal functions.80 These approaches illustrate that nanogels transcend their traditional role as passive delivery vehicles; through biomimetic engineering, they can actively interact with or “decoy” pathogens and immune cells to achieve precise and context-responsive immunomodulation.

Notably, emerging strategies emphasize the bidirectional coupling of immune regulation and bone metabolism. In a study by Zhao et al, incorporation of the high-affinity W9 peptide to block the RANKL/RANK interaction not only suppressed osteoclastogenesis but also enhanced osteoblast differentiation, achieving coordinated regulation of skeletal homeostasis.68

Collectively, immunomodulatory nanogels are catalyzing a paradigm shift in periodontitis therapy from broad immunosuppression toward active immune reconstruction. This approach focuses on rationally reprogramming the local immune microenvironment through material-driven cues, converting destructive inflammatory responses into regenerative outcomes. Compared to conventional anti-inflammatory treatments, such strategies are better aligned with the chronic, relapsing nature of periodontitis and offer enhanced prospects for clinical translation.

Promote Periodontal Tissue Regeneration

The ultimate pathological consequence of periodontitis lies in the irreversible loss of periodontal attachment and progressive alveolar bone destruction, making regenerative therapy the definitive objective of all clinical interventions. Although conventional guided bone regeneration (GBR) and guided tissue regeneration (GTR) materials have been employed for decades, their therapeutic efficacy remains suboptimal due to uncontrolled drug release profiles and limited multifunctionality. Nanogels, with their tunable structural and functional properties, offer a promising alternative platform for advanced periodontal regeneration. A key advantage of nanogels is their capacity for sequential and temporally controlled drug release. The PDA/BBR@Gel@BMP9-PDLSC microspheres developed by Zhou et al81 co-encapsulate the antibacterial agent berberine and the osteogenic growth factor BMP9. By leveraging gelatin-based microspheres, this system achieves staged release, initially suppressing inflammation followed by stimulation of bone formation, resulting in significantly enhanced alveolar bone regeneration in chronic infection models (Figure 6A). Similarly, the TM/BHT/CuTA hydrogel developed by Xu et al exhibits not only antimicrobial and antioxidant activities but also upregulates the expression of osteogenic genes, leading to markedly reduced alveolar bone resorption in rat periodontitis models.58 Li et al’s77 OBGAP system introduces an additional innovation: the released vanadium ions (V4⁺ and V5⁺) demonstrate dual antioxidant and pro-osteogenic effects, achieving substantial bone regeneration in a diabetic periodontitis model (Figure 6B).

Moreover, composite strategies that integrate nanogels with scaffold materials show considerable translational potential. The 3D-printed composite nanogel system reported by Dos Santos et al76 significantly enhances stem cell osteogenic differentiation and promotes alveolar bone repair in preclinical animal studies (Figure 6C). The NG-TCS-DFO system developed by Ma et al53 enables sequential therapeutic intervention through time-regulated release of antimicrobial and pro-angiogenic agents, establishing a continuous cascade of antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and regenerative effects (Figure 6D). Ming et al82 engineered Se-CuSrHA@EGCG nanoparticles capable of releasing Cu2⁺ and Sr2⁺ ions, which synergistically enhance angiogenesis in human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) and osteogenic differentiation in human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs). In vivo experiments demonstrated increased bone mineral density (BMD) and bone volume fraction (BV/TV), improved collagen fiber organization, and robust neovascularization. High expression levels of CD31 and BMP-2 further confirm the “osteogenic–angiogenic coupling” effect of this nanoplatform (Figure 6E).

Collectively, the core value of multifunctional nanogels resides in their ability to integrate anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antioxidant, and regenerative functions into a single, dynamically responsive platform, enabling a coherent therapeutic sequence of “inflammation control → tissue repair → regeneration”. Current evidence indicates that these systems surpass traditional delivery carriers and are progressively evolving into active tools in periodontal tissue engineering. Future research should prioritize temporal coordination among functional modules rather than simply stacking multiple components. The next breakthrough is likely to emerge from “intelligent regenerative systems” capable of adaptive, stimulus-responsive regulation that autonomously transition from inflammatory suppression to tissue regeneration upon resolution of inflammation, thereby closely mimicking the natural wound healing cascade.

The progression from antimicrobial to immunomodulatory and regenerative functions highlights the evolution of nanogels from simple carriers to sophisticated “theranostic” platforms. The core challenge lies not in simply stacking multiple functions, but in their temporal and spatial coordination to mimic the natural healing cascade. An ideal system would sequentially prioritize anti-biofilm action, followed by inflammation resolution, and culminate in pro-regenerative signaling. Future designs must also consider potential side-effects: overzealous antimicrobial strategies might disrupt commensal flora, while potent immunomodulation could impair necessary host defenses. The integration of feedback mechanisms, where the nanogel’s action is modulated by the changing microenvironment, represents the next frontier in intelligent periodontal therapy.

Conclusion and Future Outlook

Nanogel sustained-release system has outstanding advantages in local improvement of periodontitis and systemic side effects, and is a new strategy for precise treatment of periodontitis. By targeting key pathological features of periodontal microorganisms, such as microbial biofilms, dynamic pH, production of reactive oxygen species, and increased activity of matrix metalloproteinases, nanogels achieve spatiotemporally controlled drug release, and have antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and tissue repair effects. Therefore, nano-gel can be used as an effective supplement or even an alternative for treatment, especially for patients who are difficult to cure or have recurrent attacks. Summary of Nanogel Therapeutic Strategies for the Microenvironment of Periodontitis

Despite these advantages, several key challenges must be addressed before clinical implementation. Periodontitis is different in different patients, so a universal nanogel platform may not be suitable for all patients, highlighting the need for personalized treatment strategies. Moreover, the biosafety, degradation and immune response of nano-gels need further research. However, the high-volume production and batch-to-batch consistency, costs, storage conditions and changing regulatory approaches faced in the clinical translation process remain major obstacles.

Looking ahead, future studies should aim to balance functional complexity and clinical feasibility. New strategies such as bionics methods, nanogels combined with 3D printing to repair defects with individualized needs provide promising directions.83 At the same time, the development of diagnosis and treatment nanogels capable of simultaneous diagnosis and treatment may enable real-time monitoring and precise guidance. In addition, in order to make nano-gel truly applied in clinical practice, it is necessary to make corresponding improvements in material preparation, safety and large-scale production.

In conclusion, nanogels have great promise as a next-generation periodontal treatment platform. However, its successful clinical translation depends not only on innovative design, but also on its ability to achieve safety, reliability, and utility in real-world clinics.
