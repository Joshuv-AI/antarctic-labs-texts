Incidence, aetiologies and survival outcomes of paediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest in Singapore
Authors: Dalton Shaohan Chee, Stephanie Fook-Chong, Nur Shahidah, Benjamin Sieu-Hon Leong, Michael Yih Chong Chia, Desmond Renhao Mao, Wei Ming Ng, Han Nee Gan, Nausheen Edwin Doctor, David Pflug, Marcus EH Ong, Lai Peng Tham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPaediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrests (OHCA) result in poor survival outcomes globally. This study aims to present the incidence, aetiology and survival outcomes of paediatric OHCA in Singapore.MethodsData on persons aged less than 18 years, who sustained OHCA in Singapore from 2010 to 2021, were extracted from the Pan Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study (PAROS) Registry, a prospective international database of participating sites in 11 Asia-Pacific countries. The final aetiologies were obtained from the discharge summaries in the electronic medical records, and coroner’s reports. Descriptive statistics were obtained for baseline characteristics, and survival outcomes. Multivariate analyses were performed to identify predictors of survival.ResultsA total of 414 patients were analysed. The overall incidence of paediatric OHCA in Singapore was 4.62 per 100,000 person-years, with highest in infants less than 1 year old (33.5 per 100,000 person-years). Bystander CPR rate was 64.5% for the whole cohort. The most common aetiologies were respiratory (21%), drowning (20.2%) and trauma (18.5%). Overall, 35.5% of the patients had return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), 11.1% of the patients had survival to 30 days and 8% had good neurological outcome (Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) 1 or 2). There was increase in 30-day survival for time period 2019 to 2021, compared with 2010 to 2012 (aOR 4.34; 95% CI 1.09–17.22; p = 0.037).ConclusionIncidence of paediatric OHCA in Singapore was 4.62 per 100,000 person-years. Most common aetiologies were respiratory, drowning and trauma. Introduction

Paediatric out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is uncommon, the incidence ranging from 0.5 to 37.9 per 100,000 person-years.1 Despite the rare occurrence, they continue to have devastating impacts on patients, family members and the community. Several studies have been conducted globally to study the epidemiology of paediatric OHCA.2, 3, 4

In Singapore, the most recent study to determine the epidemiology of paediatric OHCA was a collaborative study with emergency medical services involved in the Pan Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study (PAROS). The authors reported a survival outcome of 3.4% for survival to discharge from 2010 to 2012.5 Since 2012, several public health initiatives, including dispatcher assisted cardio-pulmonary resuscitation (DACPR), training of paramedics in paediatric advanced life support skills, have been implemented to improve survival outcomes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrests.6 In this study, we aimed to provide an update to the incidence, epidemiology, aetiologies and survival outcomes of paediatric OHCA in Singapore from 2010 to 2021.

Methods

Study setting and population

Singapore is a single city-state island in Southeast Asia, with a population density of 7485/km2 in 2021.7 The paediatric population (less than 18 years old) was 696,986, accounting for 17.5% of the population of Singapore residents (citizens and permanent residents) which was 3,986,842 in 2021.7

Participants and data source

We included all children less than 18 years who sustained OHCA, confirmed by the absence of pulse, unresponsiveness and apnoea, brought to the emergency departments (ED) by Emergency Medical Service (EMS) or private ambulances, with resuscitation attempted by pre-hospital providers, and patients transported by private vehicles to the ED. For patients that received bystander CPR before the arrival of EMS, we included if they were assessed to be unconscious and not breathing by the bystanders and CPR commenced for at least 1 min. We excluded adults 18 years and above, and patients whereby EMS or ED did not attempt resuscitation (e.g., rigour mortis, decapitation).

Paediatric data from Singapore between 2010 and 2021 was extracted from the Pan Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study (PAROS) database, which is a prospective international database consisting of multiple participating sites in eleven Asia-Pacific countries, including Singapore. Data from the Singapore registry was collected from dispatch audio recordings, ambulance notes, emergency department records and in-hospital notes from all the public hospitals in Singapore. Standardised case record forms were input via an electronic data capture system or via an export field entry process, which auto populated the PAROS registry. Further details on the data collection processes were published previously.8 This study was approved by the local Institutional Review Boards with waiver of informed consent.

Study variables

The patients were grouped firstly, into four age categories (less than 1 year old, 1–5 years old, 6–12 years old, 13–17 years old) in the study. Baseline characteristics of the patients, data on prehospital and ED/inpatient interventions in each age category were extracted from the registry and analysed.

Secondly, the patients were grouped into 4 temporal time periods of when they presented: 2010–2012, 2013–2015, 2016–2018, and 2019–2021. Baseline characteristics of the patients, data on prehospital and ED/inpatient interventions were extracted and analysed for each time period.

The incidence rate was calculated as the number of new OHCA patients per 100,000 of the population of Singapore residents (citizens and permanent residents) of the respective age groups per year. The population statistics was obtained from Department of Statistics Singapore (SingStat Table Builder).7 Age-stratified data in the population by single year for age is available only for Singapore residents.

The aetiologies of the cardiac arrests were extracted from the Inpatient Discharge Summaries in the electronic medical records, and Coroner’s reports for those who died.

Outcomes

The primary outcome studied was survival to 30 days, and the secondary outcomes were presence of return of spontaneous circulation, and survival with favourable neurological outcome. Neurological outcome was measured using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale, with CPC 1 and 2 indicating favourable neurological outcome.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS for Statistics version 29 was used to analyse the data. Patient characteristics, EMS factors, ED factors and patient outcomes were summarised using descriptive statistics. Continuous data were summarised as median (interquartile range) and categorical data as count (percentage) overall and by the 4 age groups. Statistical test for comparison of variables between the age groups was done using chi-square test. This comparison was repeated for time periods. The incidence of OHCA was plotted over 4 consecutive time periods using our data and population data from Department of Statistics Singapore (SingStat Table Builder).7 In addition, we calculated a 5-year moving average for incidence, and performed a Poisson regression for each age group, modelling the annual OHCA counts with the calendar year as a continuous predictor and the age-specific resident population as an offset.

Next, regression models were built for the primary and secondary outcomes. The association of factors with these outcomes was assessed using multivariate logistic regression with forward stepwise variable selection. Factors were considered for inclusion in the final model based on clinical relevance and statistical significance in univariable analyses (p < 0.10). As there was presence of statistical issue of separation due to perfect prediction of outcome by some variables in the model, Firth multivariate logistic regression was performed and results of the association of each factor with outcome were reported as odds ratio (95% confidence interval). To evaluate temporal trends in survival outcomes, time periods that the patients presented was included as a covariate in the multivariate models, with 2010–2012 as the reference group. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Similar subgroup multivariate analysis was performed for the non-trauma OHCA cohort.

Incidence

Fig. 2 shows the incidence of paediatric OHCA per 100,000 person-years, across age groups, and over the years from 2010 to 2021. Overall, the incidence of OHCA was 4.62 per 100,000 person-years. The incidence was much higher in infants (33.5 per 100,000 person-years), as compared to children aged 1–5 years (4.2 per 100,000 person-years), 6–12 years (2.6 per 100,000 person-years) and 13–17 years (3.3 per 100,000 person-years). A statistically significant increasing trend was observed only in the infant cohort (Incidence Rate Ratio, IRR 1.059; 95% CI 1.008–1.114; p = 0.026), while the older age groups (1–17 years) remained stable. Additional data on incidence rates are included in Supplementary Figs. 1–9.

An initial shockable (aOR 22.90; 95% CI 1.99–263.06; p = 0.012) or PEA/non-shockable (aOR 27.54; 95% CI 7.34–103.37; p < 0.001) rhythm, as compared to an initial asystole rhythm, was associated with higher 30-day survivals. Initial rhythm of asystole completely predicts poor neurological outcome.

There was no significant difference in survival outcomes (ROSC, 30-day, CPC 1–2) for other factors, including age, gender, location, witnessed status, bystander CPR and prehospital defibrillation for the whole cohort.

Temporal trends of survival outcomes

An increasing trend of 30-day survival was observed from 2010 to 2021 (Fig. 3). There was statistically significant increase in 30-day survival for time period 2019–2021, compared with time period 2010–2012 (15% vs 3.7%; aOR 4.34; 95% CI 1.09–17.22; p = 0.037) (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1). For outcomes of survival with CPC 1 or 2 (10% vs 1.2%; aOR 6.262; 95% CI 0.853–45.94; p = 0.071) and ROSC (41% vs 27%; aOR 2.071; 95% CI 0.99–4.332; p = 0.053), the trends did not reach statistical significance for the whole cohort. For non-trauma OHCA cohort, higher survivals were observed for time period 2019–2021, compared to 2010–2012 for ROSC, 30-day, and CPC 1 or 2. For time period 2016–2018, compared to 2010–2012, more survivals were observed at 30-days and survival with CPC 1 or 2 (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

This is the first population-based study on paediatric OHCA in Singapore from 2010 to 2021. The overall incidence is 4.62 per 100,000 person-years, with 30-day survival of 11.1%, and survival with CPC 1 or 2 of 8%.

The incidence of paediatric OHCA including trauma and non-trauma causes in Singapore (4.62 per 100,000 person-years), is comparable to other countries in the Asia-Pacific, such as Hong Kong (5.37 per 100,000 person-years), China (5.5 per 100,000 person-years), Australia (4.9 per 100,000 person-years for non-trauma cases), and globally, such as Canada and North America (4.2 per 100,000 and 8.3 per 100,000 for non-trauma paediatric OHCA, respectively).3, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13

The incidence of OHCA in infants less than 1 year old (33.5 per 100,000 person-years) is highest compared to older paediatric age categories. This pattern is consistent with studies conducted in other countries, such as one in 11 US and Canadian centres (72.71 per 100,000 person-years), and another in Korea (44.55 per 100,000 person-years).2, 3 In our study, there was an increasing trend in the incidence of OHCA in infants between 2010 and 2018, from 18 per 100,000 person-years to 42 per 100,000 person-years. We hypothesise that improved neonatal care resulting in critically ill infants or infants with severe congenital malformations surviving beyond the neonatal period might be one of the reasons. These infants may be technology-dependent (e.g., on home BiPAP ventilators) and had increased risks of cardiorespiratory arrests and may have contributed to the rise in incidence. However, this study was not designed to study the causes of rise in incidence and will need further research to get more clarity.

We observed an increasing trend of survival from 2010 to 2021, with overall 11.1% survival to 30-days, and 8% survival with good neurological status (CPC 1 or 2). Paramedic training and community programmes may have contributed to this improvement over the last 2 decades. Since 2015, EMS paramedics undergo training in Prehospital Paediatric Life Support (PPLS) Course, with regular re-certifications, resulting in higher competencies in paediatric assessments and resuscitations. The PPLS course is a pre-hospital certification course accredited by Singapore Resuscitation and First Aid Council (SRFAC) since 2016, with KK Women’s and Children’s Hospital (KKH) and Civil Defence Academy of SCDF as joint training centres.14 Dispatcher-assisted CPR (DACPR) was implemented in 2012 by SCDF, whereby rapid over-the-phone assessment by EMS dispatchers, followed by concise CPR instructions to the callers to provide bystander CPR if required.15

Various community-based programmes were implemented to improve community responses to OHCA, such as Save-a-Life (SAL) initiatives which was launched in 2015. This is a collaboration between SCDF, Ministry of Health, Singapore Heart Foundation and the People’s Association, which consists of free CPR/AED training sessions for residents coupled with AED installation at public housing blocks, which complements the DACPR programme.16, 17 MyResponder, a smartphone application created by SCDF and implemented in 2015, has capability to alert active responders who are app users to nearby OHCA cases occurring within 400 m of their location.18 The app also has an AED registry for users to locate the nearest AED. The OHCA interventional bundle (SAL initiative, DA-CPR, myResponder) has been shown to increase bystander CPR rates, AED applied by bystanders, and improve survival in adult population in Singapore.6, 19 Another community-based programme, ‘Dispatcher-Assisted first Responder (DARE)’ was launched in 2014 in Singapore, which is a 45-min video-guided along with hands-on training, teaches participants simplified compression-only CPR and the use of an AED.16

Bystander CPR rate of 35.6% for paediatric OHCA patients in Singapore, compared to 53.5% in Korea, between 2010 and 2012 was previously reported.5 Since then, there has been an increasing trend in the provision of bystander CPR over the years from 2010 to 2021, with 64.5% of patients receiving CPR from bystanders. Paediatric patients with an initial shockable rhythm experienced significantly greater odds of survival to 30-days, as compared to an initial asystole rhythm. This is a consistently reported phenomenon in other studies and might be because shockable rhythms have a higher potential for reversibility with prompt defibrillation.1, 3

Causes of paediatric OHCA in Singapore are varied; the most common being respiratory (21%), drowning (20.2%) and trauma (18.5%). In our study, the aetiologies of OHCA were derived from the patient discharge summaries or the coroner’s reports for available records, allowing higher accuracies in the data collected. A single centre study in Singapore using a similar methodology, including 85 children less than 18 years that presented to ED in cardiac arrests, published in 2005, found that the most common causes of OHCA were respiratory diseases (32.9%), cardiac disorders (22.5%) and trauma (11.8%).20 Ong M.E.H. et al (2006) using the coroners diagnoses, reported that sudden infant death syndrome and drowning were the most common cause of natural and unnatural deaths, respectively) in Canada.21 In comparison, in other studies such as in the Netherlands and Japan, cardiac cause of paediatric OHCA is presumed unless indicated otherwise, thus potentially overestimating the cardiac causes of OHCA, and underestimating other aetiologies.22, 23 This paper’s method of dual validation minimises misclassification (with respect to aetiologies), and may be useful for targeted public health interventions, resource allocation, and refining resuscitation protocols.

Drowning aetiology, in our study, showed higher survival across all survival outcomes, as compared to non-drowning aetiologies. A retrospective multicentre cohort study of paediatric OHCA from fifteen hospitals under the Paediatric Emergency Care Applied Research Network in USA, found aetiology of drowning or asphyxia to be associated with lower mortality.24 The Therapeutic Hypothermia after Paediatric Cardiac Arrest (THAPCA) Trial Investigators reported that drowning aetiology (versus all other respiratory aetiologies) was associated with better 12-month neurobehavioral outcomes. The authors postulated that other respiratory aetiologies (such as Asthma) might have significant period of hypoxia due to respiratory distress prior to OHCA and therefore may have incurred a more severe hypoxic insult.25 However, there may be other reasons for better survivals in drowning aetiology, such as presence of bystander CPR. Further multi-centre studies may be conducted to study the effect of pre-hospital interventions such as bystander CPR on survival outcomes in drowning.

In our study, there was no survival to 30-days for patients with a traumatic aetiology. Such poor prognosis is comparable to other studies – a meta-analysis of 19 cohort studies found the pooled rate of survival to 30-days to be 1.2%, for paediatric patient experiencing traumatic OHCA.26 However, in comparison to medical aetiologies, factors characterising and prognosticating traumatic aetiologies, such as the predictors of survival, remains poorly understood, as the rarity of cases limits the availability of robust data.27 As trauma is an important cause of paediatric OHCA in Singapore (18.5%), public health measures on injury prevention can be strengthened.28, 29

Limitations

The data for the study consisted of eight public hospitals in Singapore, of which seven only included patients who presented via the EMS and excluded patients who came via private transport. The number of patients that arrived at non-paediatric public hospital ED was estimated to be low, at one patient every few years per public hospital. The data from private hospitals were also not represented in this study, thereby possibly under-representing the incidence of paediatric OHCA. However, we estimated that paediatric OHCA patients presenting to private hospitals in private transports would be extremely rare events. In addition, the incidence rate in our study was calculated using the population of Singapore residents as the denominator. This may result in a slight overestimation as among the patients that sustained OHCA, a small proportion was non-residents.

We did not observe pre-hospital intervention of bystander CPR or prehospital defibrillation to be associated with higher survivals in the multivariate analyses. We hypothesise that this is primarily due to our small sample size for paediatric OHCA, which limits the statistical power to detect the independent effects of these specific interventions. To better capture the cumulative impact of the various pre-hospital initiatives implemented in Singapore over the last decade (paramedic training, dispatcher guided bystander CPR, bystander AED, EMS interventions), we incorporated 'time period' as a factor in our multivariate analysis. In epidemiological studies, time-period is frequently used to model secular trends as a surrogate for the aggregate effect of system-wide implementation programmes that are rolled out concurrently.6, 30, 31

Importantly, our multivariate analysis demonstrated a significant independent effect of this secular trend on 30-day survival (OR 4.337; 95% CI 1.092–17.224 for the 2019–2021 period compared to 2010–2012). This significant temporal improvement reflects synergistic impact of Singapore's ongoing efforts in pre-hospital care and community training. Lastly, we utilised CPC rather than PCPC to measure neurological outcome, as only CPC was core data in early years of the registry.

Conclusion

Infants had the highest incidence of paediatric OHCA in Singapore, and poorest survival outcomes compared to older children. Most common causes were respiratory diseases, drowning and trauma. We observed improving trend of survival outcomes from 2010 to 2021, with overall 11.1% survival to 30-days, and 8% survival with good neurological status (CPC 1 or 2). Further research is needed to study the direct impact of community interventions on survival outcomes of children in Singapore.
