Post-COVID syndrome in symptomatic COVID-19 patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Nhu Ngoc Nguyen, Hervé Tissot-Dupont, Philippe Brouqui, Philippe Gautret
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough post-COVID symptoms have been documented in the literature, the risk factors and time required for full recovery remain unclear. We conducted a retrospective analysis of medical records of COVID-19 patients to investigate the prevalence of symptoms after an acute episode of COVID-19 and the risk factors for persistence of symptoms.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analysis examined hospital records of post-COVID individuals with previously confirmed or probable SARS-CoV-2 infection and endurring symptom continuation for at least 3 months post-infection or presenting new symptoms persisting for at least 2 months. Follow-up was conducted during at least six months to access longer-term outcomes. The majority of patients received specialized examination and at least two medical examinations. Descriptive and logistic regression analysis was applied to determine the prevalence and risk factors for post-COVID syndrom.ResultThe mean age of the 319 patients was 47.74 ± 11.61 years, and 225 (70.1%) were female. Of the 250/319 patients for whom information on acute infection was available; fever (60.8%), smell disorder (60.8%), asthenia (60.4%) and headache (59.2%) were the most frequent symptoms. The most frequent persisting symptoms were neurological (84.6%), asthenia (80.9%) and cardiac-respiratory symptoms (67.4%). About 80–81% patients reported symptom improvement at six to twelve months of follow-up. Being male and having fever or taste disorders during the acute phase of COVID-19 were independent risk factors for the persistence of long COVID symptoms.ConclusionThese results could possibly serve to identify patients at a higher risk for the persistence of long COVID symptoms and target them for reinforced therapeutic measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11360-0.Keywords: SARS-CoV-2, COVID-19, Long COVID, Post-COVID, Recovery, Risk factors

Introduction

In late 2019, in Wuhan, China, Severe Acute Respiratory Syndrome Coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) was discovered to be the pathogen responsible for coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [1]. The clinical symptoms and consequences of acute COVID-19 have been clearly described [2]. According to the findings of one cohort study on 270 recovered COVID-19 patients in the United States, the majority of patients reported a complete resolution of their symptoms, while one-third of patients continued to report symptoms after 21 days of follow-up [3]. In another cohort study conducted in Switzerland, more than half of COVID-19 patients reported symptoms up to ten months following their discharge from hospital [4]. “Post-COVID syndrome”, also known as “long COVID”, affects people who continue to have signs and symptoms long after recovering from the infection with SARS-CoV-2 [5].

Long COVID-19 is a “post-COVID-19 condition” described by the World Health Organization (WHO) as affecting people who have had a probable or confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection, who are still symptomatic three months after the acute infection or develop new symptoms, and whose symptoms have been ongoing for at least two months and cannot be explained by another illness [6]. According to a meta-analysis of 8591 patients from 18 studies, the pooled prevalence of fatigue was estimated at 28% at one year of follow-up (95% CI: 18–39) [7]. A recent cohort study conducted on 34,802 patients in Korea reported that, at 12 months of follow-up [8], individuals with a history of COVID-19 infection had significantly higher odds of developing long-term sequelae when compared to the control group including coagulopathies (OR = 2.70, 95% CI [2.24, 3.28], p < 0.001), chronic lower respiratory disease (OR = 1.96, 95%CI [1.80, 2.14], p < 0.001), mood disorders (OR = 1.67, 95% CI [1.51, 1.86], p < 0.001) cardiac disease (OR = 1.39, 95%CI [1.21, 1.59], p < 0.001) and symptoms of cognition, perception, emotional state and behaviour (OR = 1.15 95%CI [1.04, 1.27], p = 0.005) [8].

The risk factors and time required for full recovery from post-COVID symptoms have been, however poorly studied. To fill this gap, we performed a retrospective analysis on 319 patients who were monitored for at least six months in order to describe their clinical characteristics and to identify risk factors for symptoms persistence.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this retrospective cohort study, we extracted data from the hospital information system on patients treated for post-COVID symptoms within our institute, who met the following criteria of inclusion:

Age ≥ 15 years old,

Age ≥ 15 years old,

Previous symptoms of acute infection and a confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (positive PCR, antigenic test, or serology in unvaccinated patients), or probable SARS-CoV-2 infection based on symptoms and/or exposure to a confirmed case.

Previous symptoms of acute infection and a confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection (positive PCR, antigenic test, or serology in unvaccinated patients), or probable SARS-CoV-2 infection based on symptoms and/or exposure to a confirmed case.

Post-COVID symptoms appearing three months after the onset of the symptoms of the acute phase of COVID-19 and persisting for at least two months without any other explanation [6].

Post-COVID symptoms appearing three months after the onset of the symptoms of the acute phase of COVID-19 and persisting for at least two months without any other explanation [6].

The majority of patients underwent at least two initial medical examinations, with the exception of those who had been comprehensively managed previously. During the initial consultation, medical history, symptoms and treatment at the acute phase, and persistent symptoms were documented by a qualified physician using a standardised form (Supplementary document). Patients were prescribed further investigations based on their symptoms and were re-evaluated at a second consultation. Depending on the symptoms, patients were prescribed pulmonary rehabilitation, including breathing exercises, resistance training, smell retraining, rehabilitation for cognitive impairment, pain therapy, insomnia therapy, and psychotherapy. Pacing strategy was proposed for all patients suffering from asthenia and included full-time or part-time sick leave [9].

Follow-up

The clinical and functional status of patients at, at least less than six months of follow-up was retrieved from their medical files.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 15.1 (StataCorps, College Station, TX, USA). Categorical variables were presented as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as a mean ± SD (standard deviation). We investigated the risk factors for post-COVID syndrome during the follow-up. Variables with a P-value < 0.2 in the univariate analysis were included in the multivariate analysis to determine the risk factors for symptom persistence [10]. There were no variables included in the multivariate study that had more than 5% missing data.

Results

Patient demographics and medical history

A total of 319 patients meeting the inclusion criteria consulted for post-COVID symptoms between 29 June 2020 and 4 July 2022 (Fig. 1). Their mean age was 47.74 ± 11.61 years with the majority of patients aged 15–50 years. Two hundred and twenty-five of the patients (70.5%) were female. Hypertension was the most frequent comorbidity (12.5% of all patients), followed by chronic respiratory disease (10.0%), chronic heart disease (8.5%), obesity (7.2%), and diabetes (6.6%). Thirty of 291 (10.3%) patients for whom information was available had been vaccinated against COVID-19 before contracting SARS-CoV-2 infection (Table 1).

Clinical characteristics during acute SARS-CoV-2 infection

A total of 250/319 patients provided clinical information about the acute phase of their SARS-CoV-2 infection (Table 2). The most common symptoms were fever (60.8%), smell disorder (60.8%), asthenia (60.4%), headaches (59.2%), cough (54.8%), taste disorder (54.4%), dyspnoea (47.6%), and myalgia (44.4%). Some 10.7% of patients were hospitalised and 3.5% required admission to the intensive care unit (ICU). The diagnosis was confirmed in most cases (92.8%). The most frequent treatments taken by patients during their initial SARS-Cov-2 infection were zinc (27.4%), a combination of azithromycin and hydroxychloroquine (20.5%), azithromycin alone (18.9%), and paracetamol (20.5%). In addition, 13.0% patients took steroids. Identification of SARS-CoV-2 variants was available for only 72 patients, with most cases being due to the Alpha (B.1.17), Delta (B.1.617.2), and Marseille 4 (B.1.160) variants.

Long COVID symptoms and results of supplementary investigations

The interval between the acute phase of COVID-19 and the initial consultation for post-COVID symptoms was 9.59 ± 5.58 months, with a range of 1 to 33 months. Most patients had persistence of symptoms for more than 3 months after the acute phase and 18 patients presented with new symptoms which had persisted for at least two months. Neurological symptoms (84.6%), particularly memory (70.9%) and concentration (69.3%) problems, headache (35.7%) and sleep disorder (35.4%), asthenia (80.9%) and cardiac-respiratory symptoms (67.4%), mostly dyspnoea (54.9%), chest pain (33.5%) and palpitations (26.3%), were the most frequent complaints. In addition, 48.6% of patients reported ear, nose and throat (ENT) symptoms, including mostly smell (38.9%) and taste (31.7%) disorders. Psychological problems (36.4%) and osteo-articular pain (25.4%) were also prevalent (Supplementary Table 1).

Of 329 patients, information was available about symptom persistence at six months for 227 of them. One hundred and eighty-one of them (79.7%) reported a partial improvement in their symptoms at six months, while 46 (20.3%) reported no improvement (Fig. 1). In a multivariate analysis, demographics, medical history, symptoms, and the severity and treatment of the initial acute COVID-19 episode had no effect on the persistence of long COVID symptoms at six months of follow-up, with one exception: patients with a taste disorder during the acute phase had a four-fold increased risk of reporting persistent post-COVID symptoms.

At nine months of follow-up, 207 patients had information available about symptom persistence with 166 reporting improvement (80.2%) and 41 (19.8%) no improvement. Patients who reported fever and a taste disorder during the acute phase had a three-fold increased risk of the persistence of symptoms. Male patients were twice as likely to report persistent symptoms, but the effect was not statistically significant.

At 12 months of follow-up, 162 patients had information available about symptom persistent with 132 reporting improvement (81.5%) and 30 (18.5%) no improvement. Male patients and those with a fever during the acute phase had a four-fold increased risk of symptom persistence. Patients with a taste disorder during the acute phase were three times more likely to report persistent symptoms, but the difference was not statistically significant.

Discussion

Reports from different countries showed varying prevalence of long COVID-19. A systematic review of 40 studies including a total of 10 945 patients found that 64% had at least one symptom at six months of follow-up, and this number dropped to 59% at 12 months of follow-up. Among the symptoms, fatigue and muscle weakness were the most frequent [11]. Using data from 194 studies and a total of 483 531 patients, a recent meta-analysis found that the prevalence of patients experiencing at least one symptom increased from 50.9% at 27–29 weeks to 77.6% after more than a year of follow-up [12]. Our descriptive study revealed that the majority of patients experienced neurological symptoms, including memory and concentration problems, followed by fatigue and cardiac-respiratory symptoms, mostly dyspnoea. These findings are consistent with an international study conducted by an online survey [13]. The risk factors potentially associated with the development of long COVID have been identified by different meta-analyses and included being female, the severity of the initial infection, being older, being white, and the presence of comorbidities, notably obesity and asthma [14]. To date, the only factor formally associated with a higher risk of developing long COVID is being female [15]. However, the factors associated with improvement in patients with long COVID have been rarely evaluated. One French study conducted in 231 patients with long COVID at 12 months of follow-up found that patients who had SARS-CoV-2 antibodies at least once before receiving their COVID vaccination had a higher probability of significant improvement, while patients who had agueusia at the onset of long COVID had a lower probability of significant improvement [16]. A Dutch k-means clustering analysis study conducted on 458 patients with long COVID concluded that those in the high malaise-neurocognitive-psychosocial cluster were more likely to experience symptoms for more than a year [17].

Our study had some limitations. First, this study was based on patients self-reporting. Second, we included only patients who had visited our institute, which may have introduced a selection bias. Third, potential risk factors including tobacco consumption and occupation were not investigated. Fourth, we did not investigate the possible relationship between radiological markers of neurological manifestations of long COVID [18] and recovery. Fifth, we had a significant proportion of patients who were lost to follow-up. Sixth, we did not evaluate the effect of compliance with the therapeutic measures against long COVID that the patients were prescribed. Moreover, 225 out of 319 patients were females, suggesting that the study was gender biased. It could be explained by the fact that females are often more concerned about their health than males. Lastly, patients without laboratory confirmed SARS-CoV-2 infection may have been infected with other viruses such as influenza A and influenza B which may be responsible for COVID-19 like symptoms [19]. Nevertheless, we observed that male patients may be more at risk of a slow recovery from long COVID and found that the presence of fever and taste disorder at the initial acute SARS-CoV-2 infection phase, that may suggest potential predictors of slow recovery in case of long COVID. Interestingly, a taste disorder at the onset of long COVID was also found to be associated with a lower probability of improvement in another independent study [16]. These results could serve to identify patients at a higher risk of persistence of long COVID symptoms and to target them for reinforced therapeutic measures. Further studies, including the analysis of biomarkers such as the SARS-CoV-2 spike, which has been recently found in 60% of patients with long COVID while it was not detected in COVID-19 patients who did not develop long COVID [20], are needed.
