Patients’ experiences of thirst in the perioperative period: a phenomonological study
Authors: Hatice Özsoy, Gönül Kara Söylemez
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThirst is a common symptom among surgical patients and an important symptom affecting patient comfort. Thirst in the perioperative period is often described by patients as a more uncomfortable experience than pain or hunger. This study was conducted to determine the thirst experiences of surgical patients in the perioperative period.MethodsIn this study, the phenomenological design within the qualitative research model was used. The study was conducted in the general surgery clinic of a university hospital between January 01, 2025 and April 01, 2025. The sample of the study consisted of 13 patients who underwent surgical intervention and the participants were selected by purposive sampling method. The research data were collected using the Patient Introduction Form, Data Collection Form for the Surgical Period and Semi-structured Interview Form. Qualitative data were analyzed using Colaizzi's seven-step method.ResultsAs a result of the study, five main themes and a total of 12 sub-themes were identified: “thirst feeling”, “thirst effect”, “reaction to thirst”, “thirst period” and “severity of thirst”. In the individual interviews with the patients, it was found that the patients generally expressed that they physically experienced dry mouth, lips, palate and throat, and emotionally they felt stress, anxiety and restlessness.ConclusionsThirst in the perioperative period caused patients to experience not only physiologic but also emotional discomfort and negatively affected their overall experience of the surgical process. Accordingly, it is recommended that healthcare professionals adopt an approach that is sensitive to both physiological symptoms and emotional needs.

Background

Thirst is one of the most frequently reported and most prominent subjective discomforts in perioperative fasted patients. Thirst is a common symptom among surgical patients and an important symptom affecting patient comfort [1]. Thirst in surgical patients is a multifaceted condition that involves both physiological and psychological dimensions. This condition goes beyond being a basic physiological need and can lead to psychological distress such as anxiety, discomfort, and dissatisfaction. These psychological disturbances can negatively affect the recovery process and reduce the patient's satisfaction with the surgical process [2, 3]. A study has shown that patients experience weakness and disappointment due to their inability to quench their thirst, feel a lack of control, and are forced to endure the situation. In addition, it has been reported that they experience anxiety and helplessness due to the fear of not being able to quench their thirst again [4]. In the perioperative period, the incidence of thirst in patients ranged from 43.1% to 89.6% [5, 6]. Thirst in the perioperative period is often described by patients as a more uncomfortable experience than pain or hunger [7]. Thirst is a source of discomfort in surgical patients that often takes a back seat in clinical practice [8]. During recovery from general anesthesia, as patients regain consciousness, the desire to drink water becomes a primary concern, as well as pain relief [9]. In this context, it remains one of the intervention areas that should be prioritized in care planning [8].

Thirst is perceived by brain impulses caused by increased plasma osmolality and hypovolemia [7]. Increased osmolality stimulates the release of antidiuretic hormone (ADH), while hypovolemia causes activation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system. When these physiologic mechanisms are not sufficiently effective, a feeling of thirst occurs. Dry mouth is usually the result of decreased salivation and may be related to hypovolemia. Although it has been suggested that there may be a relationship between the perception of thirst and dry mouth, this relationship has not yet been clearly established [10]. Thirst not only leads to disorders such as dehydration in post-operative patients, but also has negative effects on cognitive functions and patients'recovery [11, 12]. Therefore, it is of great importance to identify the risk factors associated with thirst in the perioperative period and to implement management strategies to support patient safety and comfort [13].

In the literature, various interventions aimed at preventing thirst have been employed, and these interventions have generally yielded positive results. These methods include ice applications [14], cold water spray [15], and menthol lozenges [16]. In addition, chewing gum, acupressure, early fluid intake, artificial saliva, and the use of a thin straw are also methods used to relieve thirst [17]. Ice application has been reported to reduce postoperative thirst severity [14], and menthol intervention has been reported to reduce thirst intensity and associated discomfort during the fasting period in surgical patients [18]. The refreshing effect of cold water spray can create psychological relief, reduce stress hormone cortisol levels, and activate the parasympathetic nervous system. These mechanisms can alleviate discomfort caused by thirst by affecting physiological parameters such as heart rate, respiration, blood pressure, metabolic rate, pain perception, and anxiety [19, 20].

Although there were some studies in the literature examining the thirst experiences of surgical patients, it is seen that most of these studies were conducted with patients followed up in the intensive care unit [2, 21]. This situation reveals a need for a more comprehensive examination of the perioperative thirst experiences of general surgery patients. Analyzing the thirst experiences of patients in the perioperative period will provide an understanding of thirst, which is a common and disturbing symptom encountered in the surgical process. This study was conducted to determine the thirst experiences of surgical patients in the perioperative period.

Methods

Study design and setting

In this study, phenomenological design, which is a qualitative research model, was used. In the phenomenological design, information about the phenomenon is obtained by utilizing the experiences of the participants [22]. Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) was used to report the research [23, 24]. The study was conducted in the general surgery clinic of a university hospital between January 01, 2025 and April 01, 2025.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of patients who underwent surgical intervention and were hospitalized in the general surgery clinic on the dates of the study. The study sample consisted of 13 patients who volunteered to participate in the study and met the inclusion criteria. The patients were determined by maximum variation sampling to include individuals with different age, gender and surgical characteristics within the scope of purposeful sampling strategy in accordance with the qualitative research approach [25]. In qualitative research, the sample size is determined by the saturation point, which is determined by continuing to collect data until the point at which concepts begin to repeat themselves [26]. Interviews were terminated when data saturation was reached.

Participants

This study included individuals who volunteered to participate in the study, were older than 18 years of age, were admitted to the general surgery clinic after surgery, underwent general anesthesia and experienced a feeling of thirst in the perioperative period. Individuals with psychiatric or neurologic disorders and individuals with hearing problems were excluded. In addition, patients who were not fully conscious prior to the interview and who had discomfort such as pain, nausea, or vomiting were not included in the study.

Data collection tools

The data of the study were collected using the Patient Introduction Form, Surgical Period Data Collection Form and Semi-structured Interview Form.

Patient introduction form

This form, which was created by the researchers by reviewing the literature [17, 27] consists of a total of four questions evaluating age, gender, chronic disease, body mass index (BMI).

Surgical period data collection form

This form, which was created by the researchers by reviewing the literature [28–30] consists of seven questions including surgical intervention and technique, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification, preoperative fasting time, duration of surgery, postoperative vomiting and postoperative fasting time.

Semi-structured interview form

The semi-structured interview form, which was developed by the researchers by reviewing the relevant literature, consists of eight open-ended questions [4, 8]. Research data collection

The researcher G.K.S. explained the purpose of the study to the patients. The participants were informed that they would be asked questions during the interview and that they could talk as much as they wanted. Verbal and written informed consent was obtained from the patients who agreed to participate in the study. The quantitative and qualitative data of the study were collected in the patient room one day after surgery. Quantitative data were collected using the “Patient Introduction Form” and the “Data Collection Form for the Surgical Period”; qualitative data were collected using the “Semi-structured Interview Form” consisting of eight questions prepared by the researchers. Before starting the interview, the data of the quantitative data collection forms were obtained from the patients and the patient file. All interviews were conducted by researcher G.K.S., who was trained and experienced in qualitative research methods. Face-to-face individual interviews were recorded on a voice recorder with the permission of the patients. Patients were numbered according to their order of participation in the study and the participant number was used instead of their name throughout the interview. Each interview lasted an average of 25–30 min. Interviews were terminated when the individual data reached data saturation. All participants in our study completed the interviews, and there were no dropouts.

Data analysis

In the analysis of the qualitative data obtained from individual interviews, the analysis method developed by Colaizzi (1978) for phenomenological studies and consisting of seven stages in total was used [31]. In the first stage, all interviews recorded with a voice recorder were transcribed and each patient was assigned codes such as P1, P2. The transcribed interview texts were read independently and repeatedly by two researchers and important points were noted. In the second stage, important statements expressing the participants'experiences were identified from each transcript. In the third stage, meanings were formulated from these important statements. In the fourth stage, two experts experienced in qualitative research were consulted to verify the formulated meanings. In the fifth stage, the formulated meanings were divided into themes and sub-themes. Participants'extensive descriptions of their experiences were integrated into themes and subthemes. In the final stage, the basic structure of the experience of thirst in surgical patients was defined [31, 32].

Validity and reliability

The semi-structured interview form was submitted to three academics specializing in the field for evaluation, and the data collection tool was finalized after necessary revisions were made in line with the experts'opinions. During the data collection process, the researcher spent sufficient time in the work environment to establish in-depth relationships with the participants and ensure that the data was collected comprehensively. Throughout the research, the researcher maintained objectivity by remaining aware of their own biases and assumptions; the researcher deliberately bracketed personal experiences with thirst and perioperative care to focus attentively on the participants’ statements.

Additionally, the researcher kept a reflective journal to reflect their own thoughts during the interviews, and the themes that emerged during the analysis process were evaluated together with the research team to prevent one-sided interpretations. These methods ensured that the findings accurately reflected the participants'experiences. Participants'statements regarding the themes generated by the analysis are presented without commentary.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Approval for this study was obtained from Burdur Mehmet Akif Ersoy University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (date/decision no: 05.11.202/GO 2024/447). Necessary official permissions were obtained from the institution where the study would be conducted. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki were adhered to throughout the research process. The purpose of the study was explained to the participants in detail and it was stated that participation was completely voluntary. Both written and verbal informed consent was obtained from each participant. During the interview process, the participants were informed in detail about the confidentiality of the data provided and it was emphasized that they could withdraw from the study at any stage if they wished. In addition, all interview records were kept anonymously; the confidentiality of identity information was meticulously protected and only the researchers had access to the data. The audio recordings obtained in the study will be stored securely for seven years. All other data, such as personal information including names, addresses, and telephone numbers, has been anonymized and deleted prior to analysis. This process has been carried out with meticulous care to protect the confidentiality of the participants.

Discussion

This study revealed how thirst, which is a common and uncomfortable condition in surgical patients, is perceived by patients in the perioperative period and what kind of feelings these experiences are expressed in physical and emotional dimensions. The findings of the study show that surgical patients experience thirst in the postoperative period not only as a physiological need but also as a multidimensional and disturbing experience.

In this study, five main themes were identified: “thirst feeling”, “thirst effect”, “reaction to thirst”, “thirst period” and “thirst severity”. In a study conducted with elderly patients undergoing gastrointestinal surgery and general anesthesia, four main themes were identified regarding postoperative thirst discomfort and related needs. These themes were classified as intense postoperative thirst, emotional experiences related to thirst, practical difficulties in quenching thirst, and patient expectations regarding nursing care [2]. Although our study results are similar to the literature, it is thought that thirst is expressed by patients not only as a physiological condition but also as a multidimensional experience shaped by psychological distress, mental preoccupation and expectations for nursing care.

Patients described the feeling of thirst as an unpleasant sensation, like being thirsty in a desert. Li et al. (2025) reported that most patients described postoperative thirst as a persistent and overwhelming discomfort, primarily manifested as dry mouth and itching in the throat, and some patients described it as desert-like dryness [2]. In another study of surgical patients, the significant physical changes experienced by patients in the perioperative period in relation to thirst were noted. Participants reported that symptoms such as dry mouth, dry and chapped lips, decreased saliva, sensation of changes in tongue texture, feeling of suffocation and weakness were directly related to thirst and that they quickly recognized these symptoms [8].

In this study, codes such as dry mouth, dry lips, cracked lips, dry palate, burning/dry throat and difficulty in swallowing were identified in the sub-theme of physical effects of thirst defined by patients. In a different study conducted with intensive care unit patients, the experience of thirst was addressed under the main code of “physical experiences” and this theme was detailed by dividing it into 18 different sub-codes. These sub-codes include dryness, difficulty speaking, weakness, head and body aches, breathing problems, stickiness, difficulty swallowing, burning and fever, cough, stomach disorders, knee fatigue, sweating, phlegm, bad breath and bitterness, blood pressure irregularities, hand and foot tremors, sleep disorders and vision problems [33]. Our study results overlap with the results obtained in the study conducted by Aslan and Özkan (2025) with intensive care unit patients. The results of both studies show that the experience of thirst is not only limited to the feeling of thirst; it is also a multidimensional phenomenon that includes various physiological disorders.

In the perioperative period, patients often perceive thirst as more uncomfortable than hunger, and in some cases thirst has been reported to be more distressing than physical discomfort [34]. Lin et al. (2022) report that unaddressed thirst can lead to negative emotional states such as anxiety, stress and irritability [2]. Within the scope of the “emotional impact” sub-theme obtained in this study, it was observed that various emotional and emotional states such as stress, anxiety, restlessness, irritability, fatigue and weakness came to the fore in the participant statements. In a qualitative study conducted by Li et al. (2025), it was reported that patients expressed various emotional reactions such as anxiety, irritability, restlessness and helplessness within the sub-dimensions of the theme of “emotional experiences of thirst-related discomfort.” In the same study, anxiety was particularly prominent among the most frequently reported emotional responses [2]. Kjeldsen et al. (2018) also reported feelings of anxiety and fear with thirst [4]. The results of the study are similar to the literature. This suggests that the experience of thirst, beyond being a biological need, also significantly affects individuals'psychological well-being.

Cold water sprays, ice cubes, ice chips and cold saline are reported to be effective in reducing thirst intensity and dry mouth [15, 20, 35]. In this study, patients were found to make various attempts to quench their thirst, such as wiping or wetting their Lips, rinsing their mouth, performing oral care, washing hands and face, and drinking 1–2 drops of water. Similarly, in the literature, in one study, it was stated that the nurse applied lip moisturizing with a cotton swab to alleviate the patient's thirst, this application provided short-term relief at the beginning, but the effect disappeared in a very short time and the feeling of discomfort reappeared [2]. Another study reported that patients developed various coping mechanisms to reduce the discomfort of thirst, such as moisturizing the oral cavity with their own saliva [8]. These findings suggest that patients have developed a variety of strategies to cope with thirst and that the impact of current interventions may be limited.

In this study, the fact that patients described the moment they accessed water with a sense of relief and revitalization on both physical and emotional levels revealed that thirst is experienced not only as a physiological need but also as a psychological need. It is noteworthy that the participants described this situation with the expression “finding life”. Similarly, Nascimento et al. (2021) emphasize that early recognition and effective management of thirst in surgical patients is one of the essential components of nursing care in terms of increasing patient comfort and reducing the level of discomfort [36]. These findings once again reveal the importance of nurses to address thirst management with a holistic approach.

In this study, patients who reported excessive thirst stated that this negatively affected their surgical experience. Similarly, in a study conducted with patients undergoing colorectal surgery, it was reported that an increase in thirst severity decreased patient comfort and quality of recovery [21]. Postoperative thirst was described as an uncomfortable and challenging experience by patients. In line with these findings, it is recommended to plan and implement effective interventions for thirst in nursing care in order to increase patient comfort and support the healing process.

Limitations of the study

The results of this study, which was conducted in a phenomenological research design, can only be generalized to the patients interviewed due to the research method. The fact that only general surgery patients were included in the study limits the generalizability of the findings to patients undergoing surgery in other surgical branches. Therefore, the results obtained should be limited to general surgery patients.

Member checking could not be performed because most participants were discharged on the second or third day after surgery. However, during the interviews, explanatory questions were asked and the statements were carefully interpreted to ensure that the themes created accurately reflected the meanings that the participants wanted to express. Although this is one of the limitations of the study, the methods used support the reliability of the study.

Conclusion

This study adopted a qualitative approach to investigate the thirst experiences of patients undergoing surgery under general anesthesia and focused on postoperative thirst discomfort. The findings revealed that the thirst experienced by patients in the perioperative period negatively affected the surgical experience by creating not only physiological but also emotional discomfort. Accordingly, it is important for healthcare providers to consider not only physiological care but also the emotional needs of patients. In patients who experience discomfort due to thirst, nurses'early assessment of thirst status and timely planning of nursing interventions appropriate to individual needs play a critical role in increasing patient comfort and supporting the healing process.

In future research, examining the experience of thirst in different surgical branches and in patient groups with different demographic characteristics may contribute to a better understanding of the multidimensional nature of the issue. Intervention studies that measure the effectiveness of strategies to cope with thirst (e.g., comparison of practices such as cold vapor, ice chips, oral care protocols) can be planned. It is recommended to conduct mixed-method studies that address both the physiological effects of thirst and its reflections in nursing care processes.
