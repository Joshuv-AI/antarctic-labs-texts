Human CSTF2 RNA Recognition Motif Domain Binds to a U-Rich RNA Sequence through a Multistep Binding Process
Authors: Elahe Masoumzadeh, Michael P. Latham
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The RNA recognition motif (RRM) is a conserved and ubiquitous RNA-binding domain that plays essential roles in mRNA splicing, polyadenylation, transport, and stability. RRM domains exhibit remarkable diversity in binding partners, interacting with various sequences of single- and double-stranded RNA, despite their small size and compact fold. During pre-mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation, the RRM domain from CSTF2 recognizes U- or G/U-rich RNA sequences downstream from the cleavage and polyadenylation site to regulate the process. Given the importance of alternative cleavage and polyadenylation in increasing the diversity of mRNAs, the exact mechanism of binding of RNA to the RRM of CSTF2 remains unclear, particularly in the absence of a structure of this RRM bound to a native RNA substrate. Here, we performed a series of NMR titration and spin relaxation experiments, which were complemented by paramagnetic relaxation enhancement measurements and rigid-body docking, to characterize the interactions of the CSTF2 RRM with a U-rich ligand. Our results reveal a multistep binding process involving differences in ps-ns time scale dynamics and potential structural changes, particularly in the C-terminalα-helix. These results provide insights into how the CSTF2 RRM domain binds to U-rich RNA ligands and offer a greater understanding for the molecular basis of the regulation of pre-mRNA cleavage and polyadenylation.

Introduction

The RNA recognition motif (RRM) is a conserved structural domain and is the most abundant RNA-binding domain in eukaryotes.1 RRM-containing proteins play essential roles in various post-transcriptional regulation processes, including mRNA maturation (i.e., splicing and polyadenylation), RNA transport, and RNA stability.2 RRM domains are generally composed of ∼60–90 amino acids that fold into a canonical βαββαβ fold where the two α-helices fold against the four-stranded antiparallel β-sheet.1,3 The base of the β-sheet, which most often forms a shallow cleft for RNA (or DNA) binding, contains two conserved ribonucleoprotein domain motifs with aromatic amino acids that interact with the nucleic acid ligand through base stacking and hydrophobic interactions.1,3 RRMs can be found in isolation or in conjunction with other RRMs or domains within a protein sequence. Despite their small size and conserved fold, RRMs can bind to a wide variety of targets-mostly single-stranded nucleic acids but also double-stranded or otherwise structured (e.g., hairpin) nucleic acids.4,5 In some cases, RRMs bind DNA or other proteins (reviewed in ref (3)). Given the complexity and plasticity of the domain, it is not surprising that the RRM ligand binding reaction can take many forms from a simple two-state model to more complicated multistep processes.6−8

The cleavage and polyadenylation (C/P) of pre-mRNAs is a critical step in the post-transcriptional maturation of mRNAs and affects mRNA stability, export, and translation efficiency. Additionally, C/P is central to increasing the diversity of mRNAs: ∼70% of pre-mRNAs have alternative C/P sites leading to different mature mRNAs and therefore different isoforms of the subsequently translated proteins.9,10 The cleavage stimulation factor (CstF) is among the protein complexes that participate in C/P through its recognition of regulatory G/U-rich RNA sequences downstream of the C/P site.11−13 CstF is a dimer of heterotrimers comprising proteins CSTF1, CSTF2, and CSTF3 (formally known as CstF-50, CstF-64, and CstF-77, respectively). The RNA binding function of CstF resides in the single RRM domain of CSTF2 (Figure 1A), although CSTF1 and CSTF3 can alter its RNA binding affinity.13−15

Despite the CSTF2 RRM domain being highly studied (e.g., refs (14–17)), the precise mechanism of RNA binding is not well understood, in part because there is no structure of this RRM domain bound to a native G/U- or U-rich RNA ligand. Because of this, it remains unclear how this RRM achieves preference for G/U- or U-rich sequences and what role observed conformational changes within the domain play in the activity of CstF. Together, evidence from an X-ray crystal structure of the yeast homologue bound to a nucleotide,18 NMR binding data,16 and sequence homology suggest that the RRM binds to its RNA ligand through three binding sites: Tyr25 and Arg85 (site I), Phe19 and Asn91 (site II), and Tyr59 and Phe61 (site III; Figure 1A). We have previously characterized the only known disease-associated mutation in the CSTF2 RRM domain (D50A), which resides in the β2-β3 loop. Males carrying the single copy of this mutation suffer from intellectual disabilities (note that CSTF2 is on the X chromosome). The D50A mutant results in faster and tighter RNA binding, reduced C/P efficiency, and a preference for processing at C/P sites closer to the transcriptional start sites of mRNAs from mice brains.17 We subsequently investigated the function of electrostatic interactions in RRM RNA binding, using a number of charge reversal mutants, and uncovered a role for RRM dynamics in the process.19 In fact, these studies suggest that increases in picosecond-to-nanosecond protein dynamics and/or conformational changes to the C-terminal α-helix (α4) of the CSTF2 RRM domain function in RNA binding by partially offsetting an overall unfavorable entropy for binding. Interestingly, we observed an inverse correlation between RNA affinity and C/P efficiency in vivo (e.g., mutants with a higher RNA affinity had a worse C/P efficiency in vivo). To further characterize the mechanism of G/U- and U-rich RNA recognition by the CSTF2 RRM domain, we describe herein a series of NMR-detected titration experiments as a function of the temperature. We also utilized NMR spectroscopy to monitor the binding of RNAs of various lengths to the RRM domain. These titration experiments are supported with 15N spin relaxation data, which report on the ps-to-ns time scale dynamics of the backbone amide. Finally, we used paramagnetic relaxation enhancements (PREs) and rigid-body docking to determine an ensemble of structures for the CSTF2 RRM bound to a 16 nucleotide native U-rich RNA ligand. The ensemble suggests a role for loop 3 in RNA binding, along with other noncanonical RNA binding residues, which we confirm via mutagenesis. Collectively, these data suggest that the CSTF2 RRM domain recognizes its biological U-rich substrate through a multistep binding reaction that involves changes in both structure and dynamics, where the initial recognition of the RNA ligand is followed by optimization of the correct binding register.

Results

Conformational Dynamics of the U-Rich RNA Bound CSTF2 RRM Domain

We have previously used NMR titration experiments to characterize the kinetics and thermodynamics of RNA binding to the RRM domain of Homo sapiens CSTF2.14,17,19 Specifically, RRM domain backbone amide resonances undergoing intermediate exchange on the chemical shift time scale were used to determine the on- and off-rates for binding to a 16 nucleotide fragment from a native single-stranded U-rich RNA substrate—the simian virus 40 late polyadenylation signal (SVL16—5′-AUUUUAUGUUUCAGGU-3′)13—to the RRM domain. To further study the RNA binding mechanism of the CSTF2 RRM, we performed NMR-detected titration experiments with SVL16 RNA at temperatures ranging from 20 to 50 °C (Figure 1B). We analyzed the temperature-dependent SVL RNA titration data to determine the changes in the amide proton chemical shift (ΔδNH) as a function of temperature (i.e., temperature coefficients – ΔδNH/ΔT) for the apo and RNA-bound states. A linear ΔδNH/ΔT is most often interpreted as a reporter of hydrogen bond formation20−22 but can also report on the general presence of structure and can be a measure of structural stability.23 In the latter framework, the average ΔδNH/ΔT values for apo and SVL-bound RRM domain (−2.631 and −3.714 ppb/K, respectively) suggest that the RNA-free RRM domain is a more stable structure (note, the more positive ΔδNH/ΔT, the more likely the amide proton is hydrogen bonded or otherwise structured). To investigate the difference further, we calculated per residue ΔΔδNH/ΔT values (ΔδNH/ΔT, bound – ΔδNH/ΔT, apo; Figure 1C). Here, negative or positive ΔΔδNH/ΔT values indicate a higher or lower propensity, respectively, to become disordered with the temperature in the SVL-bound RRM relative to the apo. As shown in Figure 1C, positive ΔΔδNH/ΔT values were obtained for much of the β-sheet, which contains several RNA binding site residues, indicating that this region has less probability of becoming disordered upon increasing temperature. On the other hand, significantly large and negative ΔΔδNH/ΔT values were calculated for the C-terminal α-helix (α4), indicating that this region of RRM has a higher propensity to become disordered at higher temperatures in the RNA-bound state. These observations are consistent with our previously reported backbone relaxation data, which showed the RRM domain becoming more rigid in the core β-sheet yet more flexible in the C-terminal α-helix upon SVL binding.19

Additionally, curve-shaped amide proton chemical shift perturbation (CSP) trajectories were observed for several residues as the temperature of the apo and RNA-bound RRM samples was increased from 20 to 50 °C (Figure S1). Nonlinear ΔδNH/ΔT curves have been interpreted as an indication of conformational heterogeneity (i.e., alternative states).24−28 Of the 102 nonproline residues in the RRM domain, 14 and 18 amide protons in the apo and SVL-bound spectra, respectively, displayed curved shaped ΔδNH/ΔT profiles (Figure 1D). Although only three residues (Glu81, Ala92, and Ser94) were common between the apo and bound conditions, similar regions of the RRM domain displayed curvature in ΔδNH/ΔT in both conditions, as shown in Figure 1D. These regions encompass the β2 and β3 strands and connecting loop 3, which contains the site of the aforementioned human disease-associated mutant of the RRM domain Asp50,17 as well as the region where α1 packs again β4 and α4.

In the 15N–1H HSQC titration spectra, curve-shaped peak movements were also observed for several RRM residues upon titration of SVL RNA (Figure 2) at every temperature. Curved shaped titration trajectories were identified by an F-test statistical analysis comparing the difference in first- and second-order polynomial fits to the 15N and 1H chemical shift data (Figure 2B). Notably, residues in each of the three conserved RNA binding sites (Arg85-site I; Phe19 and Asn91-site II; and Phe61-site III) displayed curved-shaped SVL titration data (Figure 2C). Nonlinear titration data were also observed for residues in loop 3 and the C-terminal helix α4 helix. Curve-shaped peak movement in a titration experiment can be indicative of a binding mechanism with more than one step. In a simple two-state binding reaction (i.e., RRM + RNA ⇌ RRM·RNA), the movement of resonances in intermediate and fast exchange produces a straight line between the chemical shifts of the free and bound states. On the other hand, binding reactions involving intermediate or alternative-bound states could produce curve-shaped peak movements, if these other conformations have different chemical shifts than the free and bound states.29,30 Nonlinear CSP trajectories can also be caused by an intermediate conformational change within the protein on the pathway to the bound state.31 Together, our analysis of curve-shaped amide proton temperature coefficients and SVL16 titration CSPs suggests that U-rich RNA binding to the CSTF2 RRM domain likely proceeds in a multistep manner.

NMR Reveals a Multistep Process for the CSTF2 RRM Domain Binding to U-Rich RNA

To further probe the mechanism of RNA binding to the RRM domain, we also performed NMR titration experiments using a common model G/U-rich RNA sequence, which does not contain any unfavorable A/C nucleotides: (GU)8 consists of eight repeats of GU dinucleotides that are favorable for binding (Table 1). Figure 3A compares a region from the 2D 15N, 1H correlation spectra of the RRM domain titrated with SVL16 or (GU)8 RNA. Generally, we observed that a similar set of RRM residues were involved in binding the two different RNAs. However, peaks in the SVL16 titration were largely in the fast and intermediate exchange regimes (Figure S2A), whereas the (GU)8 titration was characteristic of intermediate-to-slow exchange regimes (Figure S2B). This difference highlights the distinct binding kinetics and affinities [KDs of 1.45 vs 0.26 μM for SVL16 and (GU)8, respectively17] of the CSTF2 RRM domain for these two RNA ligands. Additionally, the magnitudes of the CSPs for some residues were not the same for the two RNA ligands; in particular, larger CSPs were observed for residues in the β2-strand (Ser44-Val48) upon the addition of (GU)8 reflecting subtle differences in binding for these two ligands (Figure 3A, right). This variation could be due to possible changes in the structure of the RRM when (GU)8-RNA binds to this region, which do not occur upon SVL16 binding. Alternatively, the (GU)8-bound RRM might populate the final fully bound conformation more.

Next, we titrated RRM with RNAs of different lengths derived from (GU)8 or SVL16 (Figure S3). In the case of the six nucleotide (GU)3 substrate, a similar 2D correlation spectrum and CSPs were observed as when the (GU)8 RNA was added (Figure S4). This result indicates that regardless of the length of this idealized substrate, only one stable RNA-bound complex is formed. Moreover, this data suggests that a six nucleotide RNA ligand is sufficient to occupy the specific and high affinity RNA binding site(s) of the CSTF2 RRM domain. In stark contrast, most residues in the titration of RRM with SVL RNAs of increasing lengths (Table 1) surprisingly showed linear perturbations, where the RNA-bound peak position moved further with increasing RNA size (Figure 3B) until the peak reached the position observed in the SVL16-bound spectrum. For amide groups with large CSPs between RNA-free and SVL16 bound (e.g., Asn91, Figure 3), the resonances underwent intermediate exchange line broadening for SVL-derived RNAs six and eight nucleotides in length. Thus, the RRM resonances appear to be in a “fast” to “intermediate” exchange regime as a function of SVL RNA length. Additionally, ten residues (Gly21, Glu26, Thr28, Arg46, Leu47, Tyr49, Asp50, Arg51, Thr53, and Ser83) displayed nonlinear (Figure 3B) perturbations as a function of SVL length. Most of these residues (7 of 10) also displayed nonlinear CSPs in the titration of SVL16 implying that the “exchange” observed here originates from the same structural and kinetic RNA binding process leading to the curve-shaped CSPs described above. Thus, the SVL RNA binding data further support a mechanism that is more complicated than a simple two-state binding equilibrium. These data suggest that the RRM and biological SVL RNA may be sampling an encounter complex and/or the RRM might be scanning for a G/U-rich sequence on the pathway to the high affinity/specificity bound form.

RRM Dynamics Are Altered upon RNA Binding

We have previously used the amplitudes of fast (pico-to-nanosecond) time scale backbone amide group motions as a reporter for the change in conformational entropy upon SVL binding.19 To further study the relationship between binding specificity and backbone flexibility, we measured the changes in fast time scale motion resulting from different RNAs binding to RRM. 15N R1, R2, and {1H}–15N heteronuclear NOE relaxation data were recorded at 14.1 T (600 MHz 1H frequency) and 27 °C (Figure S5). Motional properties, including the global tumbling time (τc), the order parameter (S2), and the time scale of internal motion (τe), were extracted from these relaxation data using the model-free approach.32,33 The RRM domain became slightly more rigid (i.e., increased S2) through much of the β-sheet and loops and much less rigid (i.e., decreased S2) in the C-terminal α4 helix upon (GU)8 RNA binding, similar to our previous studies on the SVL16-bound form (Figure S6).

To understand the effect that the binding of RNAs of different origins and lengths had on RRM dynamics, we examined the correlations between S2 of the apo and the various forms of RNA-bound RRM. For (GU)8 and SVL16 substrates, the correlation plot of S2 for the RNA-free versus RNA-bound RRMs showed root mean squared deviation (rmsd) of 0.134 and 0.107 (Pearson correlation coefficients [RP] of 0.581 and 0.603), respectively, which again highlights the change in backbone dynamics that occurs within the RRM domain upon RNA binding, particularly in the C-terminal α4 helix (Figure 4A,B). When the amide backbone dynamics for the (GU)8- and SVL16-bound forms were compared, a significant correlation between the data was observed (rmsd = 0.092 and RP = 0.802; Figure 4C). Thus, the structure, as gauged from the 15N, 1H correlation spectra, and dynamics of the (GU)8- and SVL16-bound complexes are highly similar and may reflect the fully bound state where the RRM domain has recognized the G/U- or U-rich RNA sequence in the high affinity/specificity binding site(s).

We next determined the backbone flexibilities of the (GU)3- and SVL5-bound forms of RRM. When the amplitudes of backbone amide group dynamics of (GU)3-bound RRM were compared to the dynamics determined in the presence of (GU)8, an excellent correlation (RP = 0.912) and low rmsd (0.081) were observed (Figure 4D). From the large degree of correlation between the structural (2D 15N, 1H HSQC spectra) and dynamics (S2) data, we can conclude that idealized GU substrate interacts with the RRM domain via a simple two-state binding model whereby the RNA finds the high affinity/specificity binding site(s) upon encountering the RRM domain. On the other hand, when the amide S2 values for SVL5-bound RRM were compared to the dynamics for SVL16-bound RRM, a poor correlation (RP = 0.174) and high rmsd (0.132) were observed (Figure 4E). This observation is consistent with the 2D correlation spectra presented above (Figure 3B), where the shorter nonidealized SVL5 RNA is unable to fully engage with the high affinity/specificity binding sites. We propose that the intermediate shifts in the HSQC spectra and these backbone dynamics reflect a state of the RRM domain that is searching for the “correct” RNA sequence.

Ensemble of the CSTF2 RRM Bound to a U-Rich RNA

Two X-ray crystal structures of di- or trinucleotides bound to the budding yeast CSTF2 (Rna15) RRM domain have been determined.18 Because of the small size of the resolved oligonucleotides, we cannot put the NMR data presented above into a proper structural context. To assist in RRM-SVL structure calculations, 1HN PREs were measured between a nitroxide spin label attached to a phosphorothioate modification34 at three distinct sites within the backbone of the SVL16 RNA (nucleotides 2, 9, and 13) and the amide 1HNs of RRM (Figure 5A). RRM backbone amide 1HNR2 relaxation rates were measured in the presence and absence of the nitroxide spin label, and PRE was determined by PRE = R2,spin-labeled – R2,unlabeled (Figure 5B). Because of the 1/r6 distance dependence of the PRE, each of the three spin labels would be expected to produce different PREs across the RRM within a single stable bimolecular complex, as the spin label at one nucleotide would affect different RRM residues compared with the spin label at the other nucleotides. And if the spin label is not near the RRM, no PRE would be observed. Unexpectedly, the PRE values arising from the three different spin labels, at nucleotides 2, 9, and 13 of SVL16, are nearly the same across the structure of the RRM domain (Figure 5C). There are, however, some differences in the PRE values at the N- and C-termini of the RRM arising from the spin label at the 5′ and 3′ ends (i.e., nucleotides 2 and 13) versus the middle of the RNA (i.e., nucleotide 9). The overall similarity in PREs from these three labeled sites suggests that the RRM binds to the U-rich SVL16 RNA in a variety of conformations and perhaps orientations/polarity and that these conformations must be in fast exchange on the time scale of the PRE, resulting in a populated weighted average effect. Therefore, an ensemble approach was applied to structurally characterize SVL bound to RRM.35

We began by using the HADDOCK v2.4 Web server36−38 to generate a large number of RRM-SVL16 complexes. The input RRM structures for HADDOCK consisted of five models from a CS-Rosetta calculation that utilized backbone chemical shifts and 1H–15N residual dipolar couplings measured on the SVL-bound form along with three RNA-free RRM structures from our previous study.17 The only significant difference between these eight structures was the location of the C-terminal α4 helix relative to the β-sheet (Figure S7), which, based on our NMR data, could have an altered conformation in the RNA-bound state (Figures 1 and S6). For the input RNA, a 10 ns molecular dynamics simulation was run on a model of the SVL RNA, using GROMACS 5.2.2; 50 random structures from the simulation were chosen and were inputted into HADDOCK as an “ensemble”. For each docking simulation, HADDOCK randomly chooses one of these 50 RNA structures to dock with the RRM. Each of the eight RRM structures were used in separate HADDOCK docking simulations with the “ensemble” of 50 RNA structures. This approach was used to add flexibility to the rigid body calculation of HADDOCK and to build a diverse set of starting structures from which to select the best fitting RNA-bound ensemble. For the docking, two different sets of “active” ambiguous restraints were used to drive the association of the RRM to SVL16: the RNA binding residues (Phe19, Tyr25, Tyr59, Phe61, Arg85, and Asn91) or all RRM residues that experienced CSPs greater than 0.5 times the standard deviation above the mean. In both cases, all the nucleotides of SVL16 were “active” restraints to the RRM. Ultimately, ∼500 RRM-SVL16 complex models were generated.

The HADDOCK-generated models were scored against the experimental PRE data. For each model, the back-calculated distances were determined between the approximate position of the PROXYL spin label from the ribose phosphate backbone of SVL16 and the 1HN of RRM. These distances were compared to the experimental distances calculated from the PREs by using a χ2 function. Individually, the ∼500 RRM-RNA HADDOCK models resulted in χ2 values ranging from 510.5 to 7225.8 (the best- and worst-score structures, respectively). Next, ensembles of two-to-ten structures were randomly selected from the pool of 500, and their calculated RNA-RRM distances were averaged and scored against the experimental data. This process was repeated 100,000 times for each size of ensemble. The box and whisker plot of the randomly generated ensembles shows that median χ2 improved with increasing the size of the ensemble until the median χ2 reached a plateau after ensembles of larger than eight conformers (Figure 6A). This behavior indicated that an ensemble of structures was a better representation of the RNA-bound state than any individual structure. We chose the lowest scored ensemble (χ2 = 411.0) of eight structures as the best RRM-SVL complex model (Figures 6C and S8). The plot of the averaged distance calculated from the structural ensemble against the experimental PRE distances for each labeled RNA showed a better fit (Figure 6B, right) than the best structure model in the ensemble, which had a χ2 score of 555.4 (Figure 6B, left).

The ensemble model revealed that nearly the entire sequence of SVL RNA interacts with the RRM. Thus, there is no specific SVL sequence preference for RRM to bind (or there are multiple binding positions). In half of the structures, the N-terminus was involved in binding to SVL16 and in seven out of eight structures in the ensemble, the SVL16 RNA interacted via the C-terminus. In three models, the polarity of the RNA as it binds to the RRM is switched compared to the other five models (i.e., 3′ → 5′ vs 5′ → 3′). Additionally, in one of the models, the RRM C-terminal α-helix is not associated with the core β-sheet and in three of the remaining structures, the C-terminal α-helix adopts an unfolded conformation. For all eight structures in the ensemble, various residues are involved in hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and π–cation RNA-RRM binding interactions. Specifically examining the canonical binding site residues, in 25% of the structures, Phe61 interacted with the RNA; in 62.5%, Phe19 and Tyr25 interacted; in 75%, Arg85 interacted; in 87.5%, Tyr59 interacted; and in 100% of the structures, Asn91 interacted with RNA. For example, in one structure of the ensemble, binding site I residue Arg85 formed a π–cation interaction with A6 and U7, and Tyr25 interacted with U5 through hydrogen bonding. In another member of the ensemble, binding site II residue Phe19 forms a π–edge interaction with U7, while Asn91 forms hydrogen bonds with U2 and U3. As a final example, binding site III residue Tyr59 along with site II residue Phe19 interacted with G8 with π–π interactions (Figure 7A). In addition to the canonical CSTF2 RRM RNA binding site residues, there are eight other RRM residues that interact with the RNA in the ensemble. In two structures, Arg13, Glu26, and Arg46 interacted; in 50% of structures, Asp50, Arg51, Arg88, and Asp90 interacted; in 75%, Asn22 and Lys55 interacted; and in 7 of the 8 ensemble models, Lys57 was involved in RNA binding. Five of these displayed significant CSPs upon RNA binding (Asn22, Arg46, Lys57, Arg88, and Asp90). For example, Arg88 and Asp90 form hydrogen bonds with the bases of U3, U4, and U5; Arg 13 and Arg46 also formed hydrogen bonds with U3, U4, and U5; and Arg55 and Lys57 formed hydrogen bonds with A13, C12, U10, and G8, respectively (Figure 7B). Of these noncanonical RNA binding residues, residues within loop 3 connecting β2-β3 (residues 47–60) interacted with RNA; thus, these interactions represent a new potential binding region for the CSTF2 RRM domain.

To validate the novel interactions observed within the RRM-SVL ensemble (i.e., noncanonical binding site residues), we mutated Arg46, Lys55, and Lys57 to glutamate (R46E, K55E, and K57E, Figure 7B). As described above, these residues form interactions with the SVL RNA in 25%, 75%, and 87.5% of the models within the ensemble, respectively. Moreover, Arg46 and Lys57 form ionic interactions with the α4 helix in several of the initial models used as an input for HADDOCK and would be exposed for RNA binding if/when the α4 helix undocks from the β-sheet; Lys55 is part of loop 3. The RNA binding affinity, as measured with isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC), for K55E was lower than that for wildtype (12.1 ± 2.3 μM compared to 1.45 μM for wildtype), whereas no binding to the SVL16 RNA was observed for the R46E and K57E mutations (Figure 8A). Changing the positively charged arginine or lysine to negatively charged glutamate likely leads to electrostatic repulsion between the RRM and RNA, as we have previously shown.19 Thus, we expected to observe reduced binding to the RNA for all three mutants; however, the abolished RNA binding observed for R46E and K57E was unexpected.

(B) Plot of the CSPs versus residue number comparing the differences between wild type and mutant RRM binding to SVL16 RNA. Cartoon schematic above depicts the secondary structure of the RRM. (C) Model of RNA binding and recognition by CSTF2 RRM domain, where the RRM must first search for the correct G/U- or U-rich RNA sequence prior to binding. The α4 helix is either directly involved in binding the RNA or has become unstructured and moved out of the way.

To understand the differences in binding affinities more clearly, we compared the backbone amide chemical shifts for these RRM mutants to wild type (Figure S9). The 2D 15N 1H HSQC spectrum for K55E was similar to the wild type with the majority of the perturbations occurring to the residues around the site of the mutation (Figure 8B). Therefore, the diminished level of binding for K55E probably stems from electrostatic repulsion as intended by the mutation. Likewise, the spectra for R46E and K57E mutants also showed changes in amide chemical shift local to the site of the mutation; however, there were also larger CSPs for residues in the C-terminal α4 helix (Figure 8B), which might occur if the mutants altered the conformation of the C-terminal α4 helix in some way. Thus, we hypothesize that the loss of SVL binding for the R46E and K57E mutants results from a combination of electrostatic repulsion and alteration of the conformation of the C-terminal helix. This finding further supports a role for the C-terminal helix in “luring” or “locking” the RNA into the binding pocket (Figure 8C).

Discussion

We have examined the binding properties of the CSTF2 RRM domain to various RNA ligands using solution-state NMR spectroscopy to develop a model for the recognition of U- and G/U-rich RNA elements that regulate pre-mRNA C/P. Because of the idealized sequence, the fully bound (GU)3 and (GU)8 RRM complexes produced nearly identical 1H–15N correlation spectra and ps-to-ns time scale 15N relaxation. Thus, we propose that the (GU)-bound state reports on a high affinity, fully bound conformation where the RRM does not need to discriminate between favorable (G/U nucleotides) and unfavorable (A/C nucleotides) RNA sequences. We suggest that this conformation represents the stably RNA-bound state, where the G/U sequence has been recognized. On the other hand, when binding to a nonidealized sequence (e.g., U-rich SVL RNA that contains A/C nucleotides), the RRM domain must search for the proper sequence (Figure 8C). We demonstrated here that this search likely occurs on the μs–ms time scale, as judged by the titration of different SVL lengths and the curved CSPs in the SVL16 titration, and likely employs ps-ns dynamics within the C-terminal helix of the RRM domain (i.e., “Searching/α4 “Luring”” state in Figure 8C). Previous studies have shown that the CSTF2 C-terminal helix changes conformations upon RNA binding.16,39 We have previously shown that fast time scale dynamics within this helix help to maintain the enthalpy–entropy compensation present when the RRM binds to RNA.19 From the data presented here, we hypothesize that the dynamic C-terminal helix may be crucial in either “luring” the RNA (i.e., helping to find the correct sequence) or “locking” it into the binding pocket (i.e., directly participating in the high affinity complex), where the latter would occur once the RRM has recognized the proper G/U- or U-rich RNA (Figure 8C). Seven of the eight models in the PRE-derived ensemble (Figure 6) show interactions between the C-terminal region and the RNA, which suggests a direct role for a dynamic C-terminus in binding. However, one model has its C-terminus unstructured and completely detached from the RNA, so it is also possible that the C-terminal α-helix is simply displaced from the core β-sheet and becomes unstructured as RNA binds. Moreover, our ensemble of RRM–SVL16 models suggests an RNA binding function for loop 3 (the loop between β2 and β3) that has not been proposed before for the CSTF2 RRM; again, loop 3 may also play a role in “luring” the RNA. In support of this notion, it has been proposed that loop 3 of the RRM domains from RBMY and U1A is involved in shape recognition of RNA and RNA-binding, respectively.4,40,41

Varani and co-workers have characterized millisecond time scale motions within the RNA-bound the CSTF2 RRM domain via Carr–Purcell–Meibloom–Gill relaxation dispersion experiments and spin relaxation data collected at two fields.39 Using these methods, 14 residues in the RNA-bound RRM domain displayed characteristics of conformational exchange on the millisecond time scale, with the residues largely mapping to the β2 and β3 strands next to the C-terminal α4 helix. Five of these residues are common to amide protons that had nonlinear ΔδNH/ΔT curves in this study in either apo (Leu47, Asp50, and Asp90) or RNA-bound (Thr53 and Lys57) RRM domain and are mostly found in the β2 and β3 connecting loop (Figure 1C). When compared with the relaxation dispersion data previously described by Varani and co-workers,39 10 of 14 residues that displayed millisecond time scale motions also had curved peak trajectories in the SVL titration presented here. Thus, our RNA titration data provide additional support for multiple states within the CSTF2 RRM domain that may be important for RNA binding.

Allain and co-workers have previously described a role for protein dynamics to accommodate adenylate or uridate nucleotides in the first and fourth binding pockets of the HuR RRM3 domain, which recognizes U- or A/U-rich sequences.8 They suggest that the flexibility in the first and last binding pockets allows the HuR RRM3 domain to recognize multiple A/U RNA sequences with a similar affinity. Furthermore, Šponer and co-workers have recently described a multistep binding model for the RRM3 of HuR binding to the U5 substrate derived from μs-time scale molecular dynamics simulations and optimized force-fields.6 After initial contact, the HuR RRM3 and U5 ligand populate a prebinding state, where the RNA ligand begins to interact with some of the binding pockets. The RRM3–RNA complex can then form optimal and nonoptimal native complexes, which differ by the register of the poly-U RNA within the RRM3 binding pockets. Exchange between the two native complexes is possible by first repopulating the prebinding state and then shifting the binding register the ligand. Our results for the CSTF2 RRM domain support a similar model of ligand RNA binding and recognition. Additionally, Sattler and co-workers have recently determined the crystal structure of RRM1-Zinc Finger domain from RBM5 bound to pyrimidine-rich RNA. In this structure, the RNA was sandwiched between the RRM domain and the C-terminal Zinc Finger domain, with the zinc finger domain contributing significantly to the recognition of a GG dinucleotide RNA motif found within the RNA ligand.42 Although not explicitly seen in our ensemble, it is possible that the C-terminal helix of the CSTF2 RRM domain is also “locking” down the RNA as observed for the RBM5 RRM1-zinc finger domain structure. Note, the CSTF2 C-terminal helix leads into a helical bundle domain that mediates protein–protein interactions between CSTF2 and CSTF3. Finally, Allain and co-workers solved the NMR structure of RBM39 RRM2 domain bound to a seven nucleotide U-rich ssRNA and observed interactions between the 5′-end of the RNA and the β2-β3 loop, as seen in our ensemble of SVL-bound CSTF2 RRM domain.43

Because of the <r–6> distance dependence (where < > denotes the average over the ensemble), PREs are sensitive to even small conformational changes and can therefore be noisy.44 Small conformational changes of the spin label occur due to the different rotameric states of the linker between the spin label and macromolecule;45 in our case, larger conformational changes will also occur as the intrinsically disordered SVL RNA samples a variety of states even when bound to the RRM. It is indeed likely that we have not fully sampled the conformation space of the SVL RNA. Nevertheless, an improvement in the quality of the fits is observed when an ensemble approach is used to find a number of structures in better agreement to the experimental PRE data. The resulting structural ensemble provides new details of the recognition of U-rich RNA by the CSTF2 RRM domain. Namely, new putative roles for loop 3 (connecting the β2-β3 strands) as well as Arg46, Lys55, and Lys57 have emerged, with the latter trio of positively charged residues providing further support for the important role that the C-terminal helix plays in supporting RNA binding.

Materials and Methods

Protein Expression and Purification

Escherichia coli Rosetta (DE3) pLysS Ca2+-competent cells (Novagen) were transformed with a pET22 expression vector containing the RRM domain from H. sapiens CSTF2 (CstF-64 amino acids 1–107). Transformed cells were grown in 2× M9 minimal media46 using 15NH4Cl (1 g/L) and unlabeled d-glucose (3 g/L) as the sole nitrogen and carbon sources. The protein expression was induced with 0.5 mM IPTG for 18 h at 25 °C. Cells were resuspended in Lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES, 0.3 M sodium chloride, 10 mM imidazole, and 0.05% sodium azide, pH 7) and lysed by homogenization. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 17,000g for 45 min at 4 °C and loaded onto a HisTrap Ni-affinity column (Cytiva) pre-equilibrated with Lysis Buffer. The N-terminally 6xHis-tagged RRM domain was eluted in 25 mM HEPES, 0.3 M sodium chloride, 0.3 M imidazole, and 0.05% sodium azide, pH 7. The 6x-His tag was removed with the addition of TEV protease; the 6x-His tag and 6x-His tagged TEV protease were then separated from the RRM on the HisTrap column using the buffers indicated above. The RRM was buffer exchanged into NMR Buffer [10 mM sodium phosphate, 1 mM TCEP, 0.05% w/v sodium azide, 0.1 mg/mL 4-(2-aminoethyl)benzenesulfonyl fluoride, 10% D2O, pH 6.0] and concentrated to between 0.3 and 1.5 mM for NMR spectroscopy using a 5 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal concentrator (Millipore-Sigma).

SVLx and (GU)x RNAs (Table 1) were synthesized by Millipore-Sigma and used without further purification. RNAs were dissolved in NMR Buffer for subsequent NMR experiments.

Paramagnetically labeled RNAs for PRE measurements were prepared by adding 3 mmol of a freshly prepared solution of IAM-PROXYL (Toronto Research Chemicals, Inc.) in DMF to 300 nmol of phosphorothioate SVL RNAs (modified at nucleotide 2, 9, or 13; Millipore-Sigma) and 0.3 mmol TEAA, pH 6.5, as previously described.34 The mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 24 h, and the solvent was eliminated in a speed-vac at 35 °C overnight. The modified RNAs were dissolved and dialyzed against NMR Buffer for 4 h at room temperature and then stored at −20 °C.

NMR Experiments

NMR experiments were recorded with an Agilent 600 MHz (14.1 T) DD2 NMR spectrometer equipped with a room-temperature HCN z-axis gradient probe. Data were processed with NMRPipe/NMRDraw47 and analyzed with CCPN analysis.48 RNA titration experiments were performed by adding unlabeled RNAs to the 15N-labeled protein and monitoring the change in amide chemical shifts in 2D 15N, 1H HSQC spectra until saturation was reached. Throughout the SVL16 RNA titration, 2D 15N, 1H HSQC spectra were measured at 20, 25, 27, 30, 35, 40, 45, and 50 °C; titration spectra for all other RNA substrates were recorded at 27 °C. Amide proton temperature coefficients (ΔδNH/ΔT) and the presence of curvature in ΔδNH/ΔT for the RNA-free and SVL-bound RRM domain were determined from ShiftTrack and Curvalyzer (https://github.com/meiering-lab/Shift-T).49 Amide CSPs were calculated for the RNA-free and RNA-bound states aswhere δN and δHN are the amide nitrogen and proton chemical shifts, respectively, and i and j are two different conditions.50 Curved-shaped titration trajectories were identified by fitting the δN and δHN values from each concentration of the SVL16 titration to a first- (ax + b) or second-order (ax2 + bx + c) polynomial and performing an F-test statistical analysis. Residues with a p-value < 0.1 were considered to have curved-shaped SVL RNA titration profiles.

Backbone 15N R1, R1ρ, and heteronuclear {1H}–15N NOE relaxation data51−53 for the RRM domain in the absence and presence of RNAs were also acquired at 600 MHz and 27 °C. 15N R1 and R1ρ relaxation rates were determined from six time points ranging from 10–500 ms (R1) or 2–60 ms (R1ρ). R1ρ rates were converted to R2 rates using the relation R1ρ = R1 cos2 θ + R2 sin2 θ, where θ = tan–1 (νSL/Ω) is the effective tilt angle in the rotating frame, νSL is the strength of the applied spin lock field (∼2000 Hz), and Ω is the offset of the peak from the 15N carrier position. {1H}–15N NOE data were calculated from interleaved spectra collected with 5 s relaxation delay followed by 7 s 1H saturation or with a 12 s relaxation delay. Errors in the relaxation rates were calculated from the covariance of the fits. 15N relaxation data were then used to calculate residue-specific order parameters (S2) and effective correlation times for local motion (τe) along with the global correlation time (τc) from the model-free approach32,33 using “model 2” and an axially symmetric diffusion model in modelfree v4.2 (https://comdnmr.nysbc.org/comd-nmr-dissem/comd-nmr-software/software/modelfree). The 15N chemical shift anisotropy and N–H bond lengths of −160 ppm and 1.02 Å, respectively, were used in the calculation. Errors in S2 were taken directly from modelfree.

RRM amide 1H PREs were calculated from 1HNR2 relaxation rates measured from spin-labeled (R2,para) and unmodified (R2,dia) SVL16–bound RRM complexes. 1HNR2 relaxation rates were determined from six time points ranging from 15 to 65 ms. The errors in the peak intensities were estimated from the noise of the NMR data, whereas the errors in the relaxation rates were taken from the covariance of the fits. PREs were then determined as PRE = R2,para – R2,dia, and the uncertainties in the PREs were propagated from the errors in the relaxation rates. Distances (r) between the nitroxide spin label and the amide proton were calculated according towhere μ0 is the permeability of free space, γH is the 1H gyromagnetic ration, g is the electron g value, β is the Bohr magneton, S is the electron spin quantum number (1/2), τc was assumed to be the global correlation time of the RRM–SVL complex (7.65 ns), and ωH is the 1H Larmour frequency (600 MHz) in rad s–1.54,55 Analysis of all relaxation data was performed with in-house python scripts that make use the nmrglue python library.56

Calculation of SVL–Bound RRM Structures

Molecular dynamics simulations were used to generate unstructured RNA models for input into the HADDOCK docking simulation. Idealized A-form SVL RNA, generated via 3D-Nus Web server (https://iith.ac.in/3dnus/),57 was relaxed using the AMBER-99 force field within the GROMACS v5.2.2 molecular dynamics software package. The TIP3P model was used to simulate water molecules, and Na+ ions were added to the system to neutralize the net charge on SVL RNA. A box with 2.8 × 2.8 × 4.2 nm dimensions was used to confine the RNA, ions, and solvent with the RNA in the center. Simulations were performed under isothermal–isobaric conditions (constant pressure of 1 bar and temperature of 300 K). After equilibration, a total 10 ns of molecular dynamics simulations were performed to relax the RNA structure. From this simulation, an ensemble of 50 RNA structures were chosen at random as an input for HADDOCK.

Structures of the SVL16-bound RRM domain were modeled with the HADDOCK Web server v2.4 (https://wenmr.science.uu.nl/haddock2.4/).37,38 One of the randomly chosen RNA models (set as segid B in HADDOCK) was docked onto a NMR-derived structure of the CSTF2 RRM domain (segid A; PDB ID: 6Q2I). RRM RNA binding residues (Phe19, Tyr25, Tyr59, Phe61, and Asn91) and RRM residues with SVL16 NMR CSPs greater than 0.5 times the standard deviation above the median (i.e., >0.5 × 0.108 + 0.066) were selected as “active” ambiguous restraints (i.e., amino acids 5, 11, 14, 17, 18, 20, 22, 23, 27, 28, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48, 57, 58, 59, 60, 84, 85, 88, 90, 91, 94, 95, 97, 98, 100, 101, 104, 105) and any solvent exposed neighboring RRM residues to these were automatically set as “passive” ambiguous restraints. The entire SVL16 sequence was used for “active” ambiguous restraints. Random 50% of the ambiguous restraints were excluded for each calculation. RRM residues 91–102, which include the C-terminal α-helix, and the entire SVL16 sequence were set to be fully flexible. A tight center of mass and surface contact restraints were also used. 5000/500/250 structures were calculated during the initial rigid body minimization, flexible simulated annealing, and refinement in explicit water solvent, respectively.

To calculate an ensemble of RRM–SVL16 structures, a random sampling procedure was employed where a number of structures (between two and eight) were selected from the pool of 500 HADDOCK models. For each selected structure, the distance (r) was calculated between the amide protons of RRM and the SVL phosphate atoms, where the PROXYL spin label was attached (i.e., nucleotides 2, 9, and 13). We then averaged the back-calculated distances from the models (rcalc) and compared those to the experimental distances (rexp) calculated from the PREs using a χ2 functionwhere i ranges over the total number of PRE calculated distances (243). For each ensemble size, this process was repeated 100,000 times. The set of models with the lowest overall χ2 value was chosen as the RRM–SVL RNA-bound ensemble.

Isothermal Titration Calorimetry

Wildtype and mutant RRM proteins and SVL RNA (5′-AUUUUAUGUUUCAGGU-3′; Millipore-Sigma) were dialyzed into 10 mM sodium phosphate, 1 mM TCEP, 0.05% w/v sodium azide, pH 6 buffer using a 2 kDa MWCO dialysis unit (ThermoFisher), and then degassed. Heats of binding were measured from the titration of RNA into the protein using a MicroCal iTC200 calorimeter (GE Healthcare) with a stirring rate of 1000 rpm at 27 °C. For all titrations, isotherms were corrected by subtracting the heats of RNA dilution. The concentrations of proteins and RNA were calculated by absorbance spectroscopy with extinction coefficients of ε280 = 5960 M–1 cm–1 and ε260 = 165.7 mM–1 cm–1, respectively. The RRM concentrations were ∼40 μM, and the SVL concentration was ∼600 μM. Binding isotherms were fit to the “One Set of Sites” model in Origin 7. The reported values and errors are the average and standard deviations of at least two experiments.
