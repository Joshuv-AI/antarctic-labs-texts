The involvement of multiple ABC transporters in daunorubicin efflux in Streptomyces coeruleorubidus
Authors: Jianxin Dong, Jiali Ning, Yu Tian, Han Li, Hua Chen, Wenjun Guan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract Streptomyces genus produces a large number of antibiotics, which are always synthesized by specific biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs). To resist autotoxicity, transporters encoded by genes located within BGC occasionally pump antibiotic along with transporter encoded by gene located outside BGC. Daunorubicin is an anthracycline antibiotic biosynthesized by Streptomyces species, playing a crucial role in the treatment of leukaemia. In existing studies, only one two‐component ATP‐binding cassette (ABC) transporter, encoded by drrA1‐drrB1 (abbreviated as drrAB1) and located within the daunorubicin BGC, has been proven to extrude daunorubicin. In this work, two other two‐component ABC transporters, encoded by drrAB2 and drrAB3 and located outside the cluster, were found to play the complementary role in daunorubicin efflux in S. coeruleorubidus. Disruption of three drrABs resulted in a 94% decrease in daunorubicin production. Furthermore, drrAB2 is regulated by the TetR family regulator DrrR1, responding to the intracellular accumulation of daunorubicin and suggesting its role in stress response and self‐resistance. Although the homologues of DrrAB1 are only found in three anthracycline BGCs, the homologues of DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are spread in many Streptomyces strains which do not contain any known anthracycline BGC. This indicates that DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 may recognize and extrude a broader range of substrates besides daunorubicin, thus playing a more extensive role in cellular detoxification.

INTRODUCTION

The genus Streptomyces is well‐known for its outstanding ability to produce various secondary metabolites with high pharmacological values at considerably high quantities (Watve et al., 2001). Many bioactive natural products (NPs) such as tetracycline (anti‐infection), daunorubicin (anti‐cancer), and rapamycin (immunosuppressant) were produced using Streptomyces as primary industrial producer (Bérdy, 2005). However, many of these bioactive NPs are toxic to their producing strains, which make self‐resistance an important requirement for the producers. There are several known methods for detoxification, including inactivation of the NPs, modification of the NPs' targets, and efflux of the toxic NPs (Yan et al., 2020). Among them, export of the toxic NPs is one of the most direct and efficient strategies to confer self‐resistance (Mak et al., 2014).

The anthracycline antibiotics daunorubicin (DNR) and doxorubicin (DOX) are widely used as clinical anti‐cancer agents (Aubel‐Sadron & Londos‐Gagliardi, 1984; Grein, 1987; Hutchinson, 1997). Free radical formation, inhibition of DNA replication and transcription are the main action mechanisms of DNR and DOX (Eliot et al., 1984; Zunino et al., 1972). The presence of very similar daunorubicin biosynthetic gene cluster (DNR BGC) had been reported in Streptomyces peucetius (Otten et al., 1990; Stutzman‐Engwall et al., 1992) and Streptomyces coeruleorubidus (Hutchinson, 1997; Mcguire et al., 1980). Three genes for daunorubicin resistance, drrA, drrB, and drrC, were identified from the DNR BGC (Guilfoile & Hutchinson, 1991; Lomovskaya et al., 1996). drrC encodes a type II UvrA‐like protein involved in nucleotide excision repair (Erlandson et al., 2022; Lomovskaya et al., 1996). drrA and drrB encode an ATP‐binding protein and a transmembrane protein, respectively, to constitute a two‐component ATP‐binding cassette transporter (ABC transporter) for DNR/DOX efflux (Li et al., 2014). Previous studies had shown that DrrAB played a very important role in DNR resistance (Kaur, 1997; Lomovskaya et al., 1996), the DNR production was decreased by 89% after disruption of drrAB in S. peucetius (Srinivasan et al., 2010).

To date, only drrA, drrB and drrC that located within the DNR BGC have been reported as DNR resistance genes in DNR producing strains. Hence, we are curious whether any other genes, besides drrABC, are involved in DNR resistance in S. coeruleorubidus. In this study, based on comparative transcriptomic analysis, two two‐component ABC transporter genes, drrAB2 and drrAB3, located outside the DNR BGC, were identified in S. coeruleorubidus and shown to be involved in DNR efflux along with drrAB1.

Bacterial cultivation, fermentation, and HPLC analysis

All Escherichia coli strains were grown in Lysogeny Broth (LB) broth at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm. When appropriate, apramycin, kanamycin, chloramphenicol, or nalidixic acid was added to the media at a final concentration of 50, 50, 25, or 25 μg/mL, respectively. The sporulation medium and seed medium for S. coeruleorubidus strains were described in our previous study (Dong et al., 2021), while fermentation procedure was performed using Nitrate Defined plus Yeast Extract (NDYE) broth (Malla et al., 2009). The titre of daunorubicin was determined using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) as described previously (Dong et al., 2021), the measurements were based on the entire cell culture, including both intracellular and extracellular DNR.

Construction of gene deletion mutants

All primers used for vector construction are listed in Table S1. The markerless gene deletion procedure was performed as we described previously (Dong et al., 2021). First, the upstream and downstream homologous fragments of the target gene were amplified from the genomic DNA of S. coeruleorubidus (Genebank Accession number GCA_033216215.1). Consequently, the homologous fragments were inserted into pSUC01 vector using seamless cloning method to generate pSUC01‐drrAB1, pSUC01‐drrAB2, pSUC01‐drrAB3, or pSUC01‐drrR1, which was used for deletion of drrAB1 (R5U08_RS05025, R5U08_RS05030), drrAB2 (R5U08_RS15990, R5U08_RS15995), drrAB3 (R5U08_RS20725, R5U08_RS20730), or drrR1 (R5U08_RS16000), respectively. The amino acid coordinates for the knockouts in these genes are as follows: DrrA1, 25–331; DrrB1, 1–270; DrrA2, 10–323; DrrB2, 1–250; DrrA3, 24–317; DrrB3, 1–198; and DrrR1, 39–215. After conjugation and several rounds of non‐selective growth, mutants were screened and confirmed by PCR, and the DNA sequence of PCR product was further confirmed by DNA‐sequencing. The mutant strains were named ΔdrrAB1, ΔdrrAB2, ΔdrrAB3, ΔdrrAB1&2, ΔdrrAB1&3, ΔdrrAB2&3, ΔdrrAB1&2&3, and ΔdrrR1.

Complementation of drrAB1 , drrAB2 , and drrAB3

The coding regions of drrAB1, drrAB2, and drrAB3 were amplified and cloned into the AseI site of vector pIJ8660m to generate the vector pdrrAB1, pdrrAB2, or pdrrAB3. pIJ8660m is a pIJ8660 derived vector with MCS‐EGFP replaced by MCS‐Potr*, carrying the oxytetracycline‐inducible promoter Potr* (Wang et al., 2016) and a multiple cloning site. These vectors were then transferred into the ΔdrrAB1&2&3 mutant by conjugation, respectively. The mutants were identified by apramycin overlay and confirmed by PCR, and the DNR sequence of PCR product was further confirmed by DNA‐sequencing. The mutant strains were named ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB1, ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB2, and ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB3.

RNA‐seq analysis and real‐time quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR)

For total RNA extraction, S. coeruleorubidus and its derivative mutants were grown in NDYE broth. Three replicates of mycelia were collected at different time points. These mycelia were quickly placed in liquid nitrogen and then stored at −80°C until RNA extraction. Total RNA isolation was performed as previously described (Wang et al., 2017). RT‐qPCR was performed according to manufacturer's protocol using ChamQ STBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme), and hrdB was used as reference gene for relative expression level calculation.

Samples were sent to Novogene Biotech for transcriptome sequencing. The sequencing was performed on Illumina platform (Illumina). The raw reads were filtered into clean reads using Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014), then the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome sequence using HISAT2 (Kim et al., 2015). The calculation of gene read counts were performed using StringTie (Pertea et al., 2015). The analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using DEGseq2 (Wang et al., 2010).

The following statistical thresholds were used to identify DEGs: a fold change (FC) threshold of ≥2 and an adjusted p‐value (p adj) of ≤0.01. To assess the consistency of the RNA‐seq data, we selected genes that were significantly upregulated in the wild‐type (WT) strain for heatmap visualization.

Expression and purification of DrrR1

The DNA sequence of drrR1 was cloned from the genomic DNA of S. coeruleorubidus and inserted into the BamHI/BglII sites of pET28a‐SUMO (Ma et al., 2019) to construct a recombinant vector pET28a‐SUMO‐DrrR1. Then pET28a‐SUMO‐DrrR1 was transformed into E. coli BL‐21 strain to express recombinant protein SUMO‐DrrR1 inducing with isopropyl β‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG, 400 μM) at 18 °C for 16 h (Kee et al., 2023). SUMO‐DrrR1 was purified with HisPur Ni‐NTA resin (Sunresin New Materials), before removing the 6 × His‐SUMO tag with SUMO protease 6 × His‐ULP1 (Meng et al., 2019). Finally, the target protein DrrR1 was reversely purified with HisPur Ni‐NTA resin from the mixture of 6 × His‐ULP1, 6 × His‐SUMO and DrrR1, and was analysed by SDS‐PAGE.

Electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA)

The primers of DNA probes were synthesized (Youkang Biotechnology), then double‐strand biotin‐labelled probe and unlabelled non‐radioactive probe were obtained by PCR, whereas the unlabelled mutant probe was acquired by annealing of two corresponding primers. EMSA assay was conducted using the chemiluminescent EMSA kit (Kanglang Biotechnology), and the unlabelled probe was added in 200‐fold excess (Meng et al., 2019).

Daunorubicin resistance assay

After culturing these strains in TSB medium at 30°C and 220 rpm for 36 h, the suspensions were then diluted to OD600 values of 2, 0.2, 0.02, and 0.002 per ml. Subsequently, 5 μL of bacterial suspensions of different concentrations were spotted onto R4 agar plates (Xu et al., 2019) harbouring daunorubicin (Shanghai Yuanye Bio‐Technology Co., Ltd.) and incubated at 30°C.

Determination of biomass

A volume of 1 mL of fermentation broth was collected to a pre‐weighed dry Eppendorf tube, the supernatant was removed after centrifugation at 1368 × g for 10 min, then the tube containing pellets was dried at 80°C to a constant weight, and the dry cell weight (DCW) was recorded.

Bioinformatics analysis

The gene cluster comparison analyses were conducted using Clinker (Gilchrist & Chooi, 2021). The conserved binding motif logo was created using TBtools (Chen et al., 2020).

Availability of data and materials

The sequence reads of S. coeruleorubidus WT strain and ΔDNR mutant obtained by RNA‐seq were deposited in the SRA database under accession number SRX14471256 and SRX14471257.

RESULTS

Disruption of drrAB1 located within DNR BGC in S. coeruleorubidus

To investigate the contribution of DrrAB (designated as DrrAB1 in this study) within the DNR BGC in DNR efflux in S. coeruleorubidus, the drrAB1 operon was disrupted using the pSUC01‐based gene deletion system (Dong et al., 2021), and the resulting mutant was designated as ΔdrrAB1. As shown in Figure 1, distinct from S. peucetius (Srinivasan et al., 2010), the DNR production was only decreased by 31% after drrAB1 disruption in S. coeruleorubidus. Besides, the drrAB1 mutant exhibited no growth defect compared with the S. coeruleorubidus WT strain. These results suggest that S. coeruleorubidus may harbour other transporters for DNR efflux.

The relevant strains were cultured in NDYE medium for 120 h. Significance markers indicate the comparison between WT and ΔdrrAB1, analysed using the Mann–Whitney test. *denotes p < 0.05, and ns denotes no significance.

Identification of two potential DNR efflux transporters located outside DNR BGC

To explore putative additional transporter genes involved in DNR efflux, comparative transcriptomic analysis was performed between the S. coeruleorubidus WT strain and the ΔDNR mutant, which was reported in our previous work (Dong et al., 2021). The ΔDNR mutant was constructed by deleting almost the whole sequence of DNR BGC (Figure S1A) and yields no DNR (Figure 2A).

Comparative transcriptomic analysis between the WT strain and the ΔDNR mutant of S. coeruleorubidus. (A) Colour comparison of the substrate mycelium cultured for 5 days. (B) Volcano map of differentially expressed genes. (C) The FPKM values of drrABs in the WT strain and the ΔDNR mutant. (D) RT‐qPCR data of the drrABs in the WT and the ΔDNR mutant. The relevant strains were cultured in fermentation medium for 48 h to exponential growth phase. Then the mycelia in fermentation broth were collected for RNA‐seq and RT‐qPCR. Significance markers indicate the comparison between WT and ΔDNR, analysed using the Mann–Whitney test. **denotes p < 0.01, and ns denotes no significance.

According to our RNA‐seq data, among the 8442 expressed genes (37 genes within DNR BGC were excluded) in S. coeruleorubidus, 474 or 765 genes were significantly up‐regulated or down‐regulated in the WT strain compared with the ΔDNR mutant, respectively (Figure 2B). In the most significantly up‐regulated genes, the two‐component ABC transporter‐encoding genes located outside the DNR BGC (Figure S1B), drrA2 (R5U08_RS15995) and drrB2 (R5U08_RS15990) attracted our attention. The expression levels of drrA2 and drrB2 were very low in the ΔDNR mutant, but significantly increased in the WT strain (Figure 2C). The sequence alignment showed that DrrA2 and DrrB2 have high sequence similarity with DrrA1 (identity 50%) and DrrB1 (identity 31%), respectively (Figure S2). In addition to DrrAB2, the sequence alignment identified another two‐component ABC transporter showing similarity. The genes encoding this transporter were designated as drrA3 (R5U08_RS20725) and drrB3 (R5U08_RS20730), and they are also situated outside the DNR BGC. DrrA3 and DrrB3 have 52% or 37% amino acid sequence similarity to DrrA1 and DrrB1, respectively (Figure S2). Unlike drrAB2, drrAB3 exhibited high expression level in both the WT strain and the ΔDNR mutant. As shown in Figure 2C, the FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million fragments mapped) value of drrAB3 was about 10 times higher than that of the house‐keeping gene hrdB in S. coeruleorubidus. Additionally, the RT‐qPCR results were consistent with the RNA‐seq data (Figure 2D). The sequence similarity and the transcription pattern gave reason to assume that DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are the most likely candidates participating in DNR efflux in S. coeruleorubidus.

DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 participate in DNR efflux

To investigate whether DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are involved in DNR efflux, the mutants with single, double or triple disruption of drrAB1, drrAB2, and drrAB3 (ΔdrrAB2, ΔdrrAB3, ΔdrrAB1&2, ΔdrrAB1&3, ΔdrrAB2&3, ΔdrrAB1&2&3) were constructed. The WT and mutant strains were cultured in NDYE medium for 120 h, and DNR titre was measured using HPLC. As shown in Figure 3A,B, disruption of drrAB2 decreased DNR titre by 33%, whereas disruption of drrAB3 decreased DNR titre by 79%. The titre of DNR was further decreased to less than 25% of the WT strain in all the double disruption mutants. Moreover, the DNR production was almost blocked in the ∆drrAB1&2&3 mutant, while its biomass at 120 h was slightly higher than that of the WT strain (Figure S4A).

(C) log2Foldchange of the expression levels of drrABs and dnrI of the mutants compared with that of the WT strain. (D) DNR resistance assay. The cultures were diluted to OD600 values of 2 per ml, and then 5 μL of bacterial suspensions were spotted onto plates and incubated for 5 days. Significant differences between mutant strains and the wild‐type were determined by One‐way ANOVA, where **denotes p < 0.01 and **** denotes p < 0.0001.

These results suggest that the disruption of drrAB1, drrAB2, and drrAB3 could impair the efflux of DNR, resulting in intracellular accumulation of DNR and cytotoxicity, which may induce the negative feedback regulation of the DNR BGC expression as previous report (Srinivasan et al., 2010). DnrI is a positive pathway‐specific regulator of DNR biosynthesis and controls the transcription of almost all genes in DNR BGC (Park et al., 2005). As shown in Figure 3C, the expression levels of dnrI in all mutant strains were far below that of the WT strain, suggesting that disruption of drrAB1, drrAB2, or drrAB3 indeed induced the feedback inhibition of DNR BGC expression. Moreover, the expression levels of the remaining two drrAB operons were up‐regulated in each single ∆drrAB mutant, indicating their functional complementarity in DNR efflux.

To further confirm the DNR efflux ability of DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 in S. coeruleorubidus, the DNR resistance assay was performed. As shown in Figure 3D, compared with the WT strain, the ∆drrAB1 mutant exhibited no significant change on DNR sensitivity, whereas the ∆drrAB2 and the ∆drrAB3 mutants were more sensitive to DNR. These results further indicate that DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are involved in DNR efflux.

To further verify the contribution of the three DrrABs in DNR efflux, we constructed the ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB1, ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB2, and ΔdrrAB1&2&3/pdrrAB3 strains. Considering the DNR concentration‐dependent expression of drrAB1 and drrAB2 under their native promoters, we used an oxytetracycline‐inducible promoter, Potr*, for all three drrABs in the complementation mutants.

As shown in Figure 4A, after 120 h fermentation process, 1 μM oxytetracycline induced expression of drrAB1, drrAB2, and drrAB3 increased the DNR titre by 82.3%, 99.6%, and 52.7% compared with the control group. In addition, the complementation of three DrrABs significantly enhanced the resistance of ΔdrrAB1&2&3 strain to DNR (Figure 4B). Among them, DrrAB1 and DrrAB2 exhibited the similar resistance effects, whereas DrrAB3 played a weaker role under the control of same promoter. It suggests that the efficiency of DrrAB3 to export DNR is lower than that of DrrAB1 or DrrAB2.

DrrR1 directly and negatively regulates the drrAB2 expression

As shown in Figure 3C, the expression level of drrAB2 was dramatically up‐regulated in the S. coeruleorubidus WT strain compared with that in the ∆drrAB1 or ∆drrAB3 mutant, suggesting that the expression of drrAB2 may be in response to the intracellular accumulation of DNR. In addition, we determined the expression of drrAB2 in the WT strain at different timepoints (24, 48, 72, and 96 h) and found that, the transcriptional level of drrAB2 increased markedly as fermentation progressed (Figure 5A). To investigate whether drrAB2 responds to the increase of environmental DNR, DNR with a final concentration of 50 mg/L was added to NDYE broth of the ∆DNR strain after 48 hours of fermentation. As the fermentation process continued, the transcriptional level of drrAB2 was significantly up‐regulated, while no significant change was observed in the control group without DNR addition (Figure 5B).

According to the genomic analysis of S. coeruleorubidus, a TetR family regulator (TFR) encoding gene drrR1 (R508_RS16000) located adjacent to the drrAB2 locus caught our attention. Previous studies had proved that the TFRs were involved in regulation of many multidrug exporters and response to cellular stress (Romero‐Rodríguez et al., 2015). To explore whether DrrR1 directly regulates the expression of drrAB2, a drrR1 disruption mutant ΔdrrR1 was constructed. As shown in Figure 5C, the transcriptional level of drrAB2 was significantly up‐regulated in the ∆drrR1 mutant compared with that in the WT strain.

It is well known that TFRs consist of a larger C‐terminal ligand‐binding domain and an N‐terminal DNA‐binding domain. In most cases their C‐terminal domains interact with one or more ligands, in turn altering the ability to bind DNA (Cuthbertson & Nodwell, 2013). Moreover, the N‐terminal domain is generally bound to palindromic DNA sequences in the promoter region of target genes. This binding pattern is attributable to the general propensity of TFRs to function as dimers (Yu et al., 2010). To identify the DNA binding sites of DrrR1, the secondary structure of intergenic region between drrAB2 and drrR1 (length of 110 bp) was analysed, and a 44‐bp symmetrical palindrome AACTGAGTACAGCGTACCCGATTACGCGTACGCCGTACCCAGTT was found (Figure 5D), which was designated as DrrR1‐BS (DrrR1 Binding Sties) in this study.

To investigate whether DrrR1 directly regulates drrAB1 expression through binding to DrrR1‐BS, the recombinant DrrR1 was expressed and purified (Figure S4B). EMSA was performed using the purified DrrR1 protein with two Biotin‐labelled probes containing different regions. As shown in Figure 5E, DrrR1 could directly bind to the drrAB2 promoter (Biotin‐DNA, 110 bp of the drrR1‐drrAB2 intergenic region containing DrrR1‐BS) and formed complex bands. The binding potential of DNA containing DrrR1‐BS was additionally demonstrated by competition experiments, a 200‐fold excess of DNA containing DrrR1‐BS abolished Biotin‐DNA‐protein complex formation. In contrast, a 200‐fold excess of mutant‐DNA (the drrR1‐drrAB2 intergenic region with removal of DrrR1‐BS) was unable to abolish the complex formation, indicating the binding specificity of DrrR1 to the DrrR1‐BS.

To determine whether DrrR1 could respond to DNR related metabolites and affect the drrAB2 expression, we incubated DNR or DOX with DrrR1 and biotin‐DNA containing DrrR1‐BS. We observed that both molecules could induce DrrR1 dissociation from DrrR1‐BS (Figure 5F). It showed that 20 μM DNR is effective in dissociating DrrR1 from its DNA complex in vitro, while a lower concentration of approximately 10 μM DOX is required to elicit a similar dissociation under the same experimental condition. This observation suggests a higher sensitivity of DrrR1 to DOX in comparison to DNR. In addition, ampicillin cannot release DrrR1 from the DNA complex at the same concentrations (Figure S4C). All these indicate that DrrR1 can respond to the intracellular accumulation of DNR and DOX and induce the overexpression of drrAB2, which acts as a stress responder to decrease cell damage.

Homologues of DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are widespread in Streptomyces

As described above, we proved that both DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 were involved in DNR efflux, while it is not known if they are specific for DNR related metabolites. It was reported that the drrAB operon presented within the DNR BGC in S. peucetius could export not only DNR and DOX but other substrates like Hoechst 33342 and ethidium bromide (Li et al., 2014). Alignment analysis showed that the amino acid sequence of DrrAB1 in S. coeruleorubidus was almost the same as DrrAB in S. peucetius, so this conclusion is also applicable to DrrAB1. To investigate whether DrrAB1, DrrAB2, and DrrAB3 are distributed in other organisms besides S. coeruleorubidus, we searched homologues of DrrB1, DrrB2, and DrrB3 in the NCBI's non‐redundant protein sequences (“nr”) database using blastp (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). DrrA1, DrrA2, and DrrA3 were not included because they were highly conserved ATP‐binding protein with little divergence information.

The blast results showed that there were 15, 353, and 359 organisms that contain the homologues of DrrB1, DrrB2, and DrrB3, respectively (sequence identity >60% and length coverage >80%, the unclassified and duplicate organisms were removed), indicating that DrrB2 and DrrB3 are much more widespread than DrrB1, and most of them were found in Streptomyces genus. In addition to Streptomyces genus, there are 30 other genera such as Nonomuraea, Marinitenerispora, Spirillospora, and Nocardia containing the homologues of DrrB1, DrrB2, or DrrB3 (Table S4). Subsequently, we performed Venn analysis to identify the organisms that contain the homologues of all three DrrBs, like S. coeruleorubidus does. As shown in Figure 6A, four Streptomyces species (S. coeruleorubidus, Genebank Accession number GCA_033216215.1; S. prasinopilosus, Genebank Accession number GCA_900101585.1; S. fructofermentans, Genebank Accession number GCA_014651055.1; and S. dioscori, Genebank Accession number GCF_003024195.1) were identified to contain the homologues both DrrB1, DrrB2, and DrrB3. Among them, S. coeruleorubidus and S. prasinopilosus contain a DNR BGC, whereas S. fructofermentans and S. dioscori contain a gene cluster for biosynthesizing cinerubin B, which was confirmed to be a glycosylated anthracycline (Li et al., 2015). Compared with S. coeruleorubidus, the well‐known DNR producer, S. peucetius, does not have the homologue of DrrAB3 (Table S5). In addition, although predicted to contain DNR BGC and all homologues of three DrrBs, S. prasinopilosus was never reported to produce DNR or DOX. There are 23 genes in cinerubin B BGC showing high sequence similarity with the genes in DNR BGC, including DrrAB1. In addition, among the 15 strains predicted to contain anthracycline BGC including DNR, cinerubin B, or cytorhodin BGC (Gui et al., 2019) (Figure S5), eight strains do not have the homologues of DrrAB2, whereas seven strains do not have the homologues of DrrAB3 (Table S5), indicating the way that three DrrABs work together is not fixed.

Four Streptomyces strains contain the homologues of all three DrrBs and the conserved binding motif of DrrR1 homologues. (A) Comparison of the DrrAB1 homologue containing BGCs from different organisms that contain all three DrrBs. (B) The conserved binding motif of DrrR1 homologues.

Taken together, although the homologues of DrrAB1 were just found in the anthracycline BGCs mentioned above, the homologues of DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are spread in many Streptomyces strains which do not contain the known anthracycline BGCs, indicating that DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 may have the ability to recognize and pump more substrates than anthracycline.

The drrR1‐drrAB2 stress response system spreads in many Streptomyces and Nocardia species

As described above, DrrR1 negatively regulates the expression of drrAB2 by binding to DrrR1‐BS located in the intergenic region of drrAB2 and drrR1. Given the presence of DrrAB2 homologues in various genera, would DrrR1 homologues also appear with these DrrAB2 homologues? Therefore, the amino acid sequence of DrrR1 was used for blastp to find its homologues. As listed in Table S2, 172 organisms with high‐quality genome uploaded to NCBI were identified to harbour the DrrR1 homologues (sequence identity >60% and length coverage >80%, the unclassified and duplicate organisms were removed). Venn analysis showed that there were 160 organisms containing both DrrR1 and DrrAB2 homologues. Among them, 136 belonged to genus Streptomyces, 23 belonged to genus Nocardia genus, and the left one belonged to genus Actinospica.

Next, the gene locus of each DrrR1 or DrrAB2 homologue was mapped to their genomes based on local blast. The mapping results showed that, the homologues of DrrR1 and DrrAB2 were located adjacent to each other in 146 of the 160 organisms. Then, 146 intergenic sequences of drrR1‐drrAB2 homologues were extracted, the length of which ranged from 100 to 785 bp. These sequences were mined for conserved binding motifs of DrrR1 homologues using MEME suite tool (Bailey et al., 2015). As shown in Figure 6B, a very conserved and symmetrical motif 5’‐AACTGRGTACACCGTACCCGATTRMGMGTACRMCGTACCCAGTT‐3′ was discovered in 139 out of 145 intergenic sequences (Table S3). The spread of drrR1‐drrAB2 and the conservation of symmetrical sequence for DrrR1 binding indicate that drrR1‐drrAB2 should be an important and conserved detoxification element. In fact, we also found the drrR1‐drrAB2 homologues present in the species that do not contain predicted anthracycline BGC (data not shown), suggesting that drrR1‐drrAB2 is not limited to response to anthracyclines, but also play a broader role in cellular detoxification.

DISCUSSION

In general, secondary metabolite BGCs often harbour transporter genes to pump their toxic products (Wu et al., 2000). Besides, many studies reveal that transporters encoded by genes located within BGC are occasionally pump antibiotic along with transporters encoded by genes located outside BGC (Liu et al., 2022; Shan et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2017). In this study, we discovered two two‐component ABC transporters involved in DNR efflux in S. coeruleorubidus.

As the two‐component ABC transporter genes carried by DNR BGC, drrAB1 performs the basic DNR efflux function and can be horizontally transferred to different strains together with the BGC. Upon heterologous expression of the DNR BGC in Streptomyces albus ZD11 (Wei et al., 2022), we found that the transcriptional level of drrAB1 was much lower than those of most enzyme genes in the BGC (data not shown). It suggests that the transcriptional regulation of drrAB1 is not only mediated by the pathway‐specific regulator DnrI and may be very complex. When DrrAB1 is unable to fully pump DNR out of the cell and results in intracellular accumulation, DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 will assist DrrAB1 in DNR efflux. In this assisted efflux mode, DrrAB3 maintains a largely consistent efficiency, whereas DrrAB2 works in a stress‐responsive manner based on DrrR1 regulation. Although its efflux efficiency of DNR is relatively lower (Figure 4), DrrAB3 still plays a key role in DNR efflux due to its high expression level (Figure 2C,D). Since DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 are not present in all strains having the DNR BGC, these strains may not be able to export DNR efficiently as S. coeruleorubidus does. The discovery of DrrAB2 and DrrAB3 as complementary players in DNR efflux marks a significant advancement in understanding the self‐resistance mechanisms of DNR producing strain.

The regulatory role of DrrR1 in controlling drrAB2 expression in response to intracellular DNR/DOX accumulation is particularly intriguing. It highlights an adaptive response mechanism where the bacterial strain modulates gene expression to mitigate the toxic effects of its own metabolites. Moreover, we find that the homologues of drrR1‐drrAB2 were spread in many organisms which have no DNR BGC, and the perfectly symmetrical palindrome DrrR1‐BS is also highly conserved in these organisms. This finding indicates that DrrR1 can respond to the accumulation of intracellular toxins and mediates DrrAB2 to export multiple substrates and performs detoxification.

The existence of independent ABC transporters near transcriptional regulatory genes (including TetR family genes) has been reported several times. For example, Matthias Richard et al. reported that a TetR family protein MAB_4384 negatively regulated expression of its locus adjacent transporter genes mmpS5/mmpL5 in mycobacteria (Richard et al., 2018). Yukun Lei et al. found that three TFRs (SCO1718, SCO4358, and SCO5384) repressed the expression of each adjacent ABC transporter in S. coelicolor A3(2) and had similar effects on the phenotype for both secondary metabolism and antibiotic resistance (Lei et al., 2023). Tanjun Wang et al. identified a TetR family protein Mfo1 which regulated its locus adjacent gene encoding a major facilitator superfamily transporter MFS1 in response to intracellular accumulation of natamycin and 4,5‐deepoxynatamycin in Streptomyces chattanoogensis L10 (Wang et al., 2017). In S. coeruleorubidus, we also found six groups of adjacent TetR‐ABC transporter besides DrrR1‐DrrAB2 (data not shown). Since these adjacent TetR‐ABC transporters do not belong to any predicted secondary metabolite BGC, their complex functions and relationships with cell metabolism will be well worth further study.

In conclusion, this study not only contributes to a deeper understanding of the self‐resistance mechanisms in Streptomyces but also provides a foundation for future research in antibiotic production, efflux system engineering, and stress response mechanisms in bacteria.
