α-Viniferin Induces Intrinsic Apoptosis in Triple-Negative Breast Cancer Cells and Synergizes With SB203580 Independently of p38 Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase
Authors: Do-Gyeong Kim, Gyeongmi Kim, Ji Yea Kim, Se-Kyeong Jang, Hyunggee Kim, Hyeon-Ok Jin, Min-Ki Seong, Chan Sub Park, In-Chul Park, Hyun-Ah Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is recognized as the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer. Due to the absence of established molecular targets, treatment options remain limited. In this study, we investigated the anticancer effects of α-viniferin in TNBC cells.MethodsThe impact of α-viniferin on cell viability was evaluated across several breast cancer cell lines representing different molecular subtypes, as well as in human dermal fibroblasts, using MTT assays. Apoptotic cell death, mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were assessed through flow cytometry. The involvement of signaling pathways was investigated via western blotting and small interfering RNA (siRNA)-mediated knockdown of p38α and p38β.ResultsOur findings revealed that α-viniferin induced apoptotic cell death in a time- and dose-dependent manner more effectively in TNBC cells than in other breast cancer subtypes. α-Viniferin triggered intrinsic apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells by downregulating anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, disrupting MMP, and cleaving poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase and caspase-3, which is accompanied by increased intracellular ROS levels. Notably, SB203580, a selective p38 inhibitor, synergistically enhanced α-viniferin-induced cytotoxicity. However, this synergistic effect was independent of p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling, as siRNA-mediated knockdown of p38α and p38β did not replicate the observed synergistic cell death in TNBC cells.ConclusionCollectively, these findings indicate that α-viniferin induces apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells and that SB203580 enhances this cytotoxic effect through a mechanism independent of p38 MAPK signaling, suggesting a potential combination strategy for the treatment of TNBC.Keywords: Alpha-viniferin, Apoptosis, SB 203580, Triple Negative Breast Neoplasms

INTRODUCTION

Breast cancer is among the most prevalent malignancies affecting women worldwide, with over two million new cases diagnosed annually [1]. Among the various molecular subtypes of breast cancer, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression—is recognized as the most aggressive and therapeutically refractory subtype [2,3]. TNBC is marked by significant molecular heterogeneity, rapid disease progression, and a high likelihood of early recurrence and distant metastasis [4]. Patients with advanced or metastatic TNBC often experience poor clinical outcomes, with median overall survival rarely exceeding 12–18 months, even with systemic chemotherapy [5]. Although recent studies have led to the emergence of new therapeutic agents for TNBC, the lack of well-defined molecular targets has limited their clinical efficacy, underscoring the need for novel treatment strategies.

Apoptosis, a well-characterized form of programmed cell death, is a crucial mechanism exploited by numerous anticancer agents [6]. This process can be initiated through distinct pathways, including the extrinsic (death receptor-mediated) and intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathways [6]. The extrinsic pathway is initiated by the activation of death receptors located on the cell surface, which leads to the recruitment of adaptor proteins and subsequent activation of caspases [6,7]. Conversely, the intrinsic pathway is triggered by mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and the release of cytochrome c, culminating in caspase activation, and is regulated by pro- and anti-apoptotic members of the Bcl-2 family [6,8]. In the context of TNBC, chemotherapeutic agents such as anthracyclines, taxanes, and platinum-based drugs, which promote apoptosis, are commonly used as first-line treatments [9]. Nonetheless, treatment options for TNBC remain limited, and novel apoptosis-inducing agents that can selectively target this subtype of breast cancer are still needed.

α-Viniferin, a trimeric resveratrol derivative, is a naturally occurring stilbene oligomer found in Caragana species [10]. It belongs to the stilbenoid family of phytoalexins, which are produced by plants in response to environmental stressors [11]. Studies have shown that α-viniferin exhibits antioxidant effects in vitro and suppresses inflammatory responses in lipopolysaccharide-stimulated microglial cells and macrophages [12,13]. Despite their antioxidant properties, polyphenolic compounds, including resveratrol derivatives, may exert pro-oxidant effects in cancer cells under specific conditions, resulting in apoptotic cell death [14,15,16]. Recent studies have reported that α-viniferin demonstrates anticancer properties in non-small cell lung cancer by inhibiting cancer cell migration and invasion [17]. However, the potential relevance of α-viniferin in TNBC remains largely unexplored, highlighting the need for further investigation.

In this study, we investigated whether α-viniferin induces apoptotic cell death in multiple breast cancer cell lines, particularly in TNBC cells, and aimed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, including mitochondrial dysfunction, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and the regulation of Bcl-2 family proteins. In addition, we examined the potential of a combination strategy using SB203580, a p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) inhibitor, to assess whether it enhances the effects of α-viniferin. This study provides a basis for understanding the therapeutic potential of α-viniferin in TNBC and supports further investigation of combination strategies.

METHODS

Cell culture

The human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, HCC-1937, SK-BR-3, MCF-7, BT-474, and human dermal fibroblasts (HDFs) were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA). MDA-MB-231, MCF-7, SK-BR-3, and HDFs were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (#LM001-05; Welgene, Gyeongsan, Korea). MDA-MB-453, HCC-1937, and BT-474 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (#LM011-01; Welgene). All cultures were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. The media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (#35–015-CV; Corning Inc., Corning, USA).

Reagents

α-Viniferin (#CFN97068) was procured from ChemFaces (Wuhan, China). SP600125 (#S1460), SB203580 (#S1076), and PD98059 (#S1177) were sourced from SelleckChem (Houston, USA). Carbonyl cyanide m-chlorophenyl hydrazone (CCCP) (#HY-100941) was obtained from MedChemExpress (Monmouth Junction, USA). Thiazolyl blue tetrazolium bromide (MTT) (#M2128) was acquired from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated by measuring the mitochondrial reduction of MTT to formazan. Cells were cultured in 6-well plates with the appropriate culture medium, treated with each drug for the specified durations, and incubated in a 1-mg/mL MTT solution at 37°C for one hour. Following incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and 2-propanol (#I9516; Sigma-Aldrich; Merck KGaA) was added to solubilize the formazan crystals. The quantity of formazan was determined by measuring absorbance at 595 nm. The results were expressed as the percentage of cell viability relative to the untreated control. IC50 values were estimated using the Quest Graph IC50 Calculator (AAT Bioquest, Inc., Sunnyvale, USA). Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Transient transfection

Control siRNA (#SN-1002); siRNA #1 (#1432-1) and siRNA #2 (#1432-2) for MAPK14; siRNA #1 (#5600-1) and siRNA #2 (#5600-2) for MAPK11; siRNA #1 (#1452-1) and siRNA #2 (#1452-2) for CSNK1A1; and siRNA #1 (#8767-1) and siRNA #2 (#8767-2) for RIP2 were purchased from Bioneer (Daejeon, Korea). Transfection was performed using INTERFERin® siRNA Transfection reagent (#101000016; Polyplus; Sartorius, Göttingen, Germany) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Microscopic observation

The cells were cultured in 6-well plates and treated with each drug for the designated times. Cell morphology was observed using an inverted microscope (#CKX53; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and representative images were captured to monitor time-dependent changes.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was assessed utilizing an FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (#556547; BD Pharmingen, San Diego, USA). The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with each drug for the specified duration. Following treatment, cells were collected by centrifugation and resuspended in 500 μL of binding buffer containing 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of propidium iodide (PI). After incubation for five minutes in the dark, cells were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (#A00-1-1102; Beckman Coulter, Brea, USA) [18].

Measurement of intracellular ROS

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with each drug for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 100 nM 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA; #8206004, Abcam, Cambridge, USA) for 30 minutes at 37°C to assess intracellular ROS. The cells were then collected and washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A total of 5,000 cells per sample were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter).

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

The cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with each drug for 24 hours. Subsequently, cells were incubated with 1 μM tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester perchlorate (TMRE) (#HY-D0985A; MedChemExpress) to assess MMP. As a positive control, cells were treated with 50 μM CCCP to induce mitochondrial depolarization. The cells were then harvested and washed with PBS. A total of 5,000 cells per sample were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter) [19].

Western blot analysis

Western blotting was conducted as previously described [20]. Briefly, the drug-treated cells were rinsed with cold PBS and lysed in 1× lysis buffer (#9803; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), which contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (#11697498001; Roche, Mannheim, Germany) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (#04906837001; Roche). Protein concentration was measured using Bradford reagent (#5000006; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, USA). Protein samples were separated by 8%–15% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred to nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for one hour at room temperature. Blot detection was performed using SuperSignal™ West Pico chemiluminescent substrates (#18633096 and #18633097; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA). Images were captured using a ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (#12003154; Bio-Rad Laboratories). The following antibodies were utilized: β-actin (#A5316; Sigma-Aldrich); Bcl-2 (#A19693; ABclonal, Woburn, USA); cleaved poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) (#9541), cleaved caspase-3 (#9661), Bcl-xL (#2764), Mcl-1 (#5453), p44/42 MAPK (ERK1/2) (#9102), phospho-p44/42 MAPK (T202/Y204) (#4370), p38 (#9212), phospho-p38 (#4511), c-Jun N-terminal kinases/stress-activated protein kinase (JNK/SAPK) (#9258) (Cell Signaling Technology); and phospho-JNK/SAPK (#ab124956; Abcam). The intensities of the protein bands were quantified with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, USA).

Isobologram analysis

Isobologram analysis was performed to assess the synergistic interaction between the two agents. The cells were treated with varying concentrations of α-viniferin (0–3 μM) and SB203580 (0–60 μM). The percentage of each agent was plotted against the corresponding level of cell viability for all combinations that achieved 50% ± 5% cell viability. The fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) was calculated by dividing the concentration of each agent in the combination by the concentration of each agent alone. A sum of FIC equal to one indicates an additive interaction, represented as a straight line, whereas values lower or higher than one indicate synergistic and antagonistic interactions, respectively, resulting in concave and convex shapes, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation from three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were performed using one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey's test, using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1; GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, USA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.01.

RESULTS

α-Viniferin preferentially reduces viability in TNBC cells

To evaluate whether α-viniferin exhibits subtype-specific cytotoxic effects in breast cancer, we analyzed cell viability across various breast cancer subtypes, including TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, and HCC-1937), luminal A (MCF-7), luminal B-like (BT-474), and HER2-enriched (SK-BR-3) cell lines, as well as normal HDFs (Figure 1A-G). Although α-viniferin reduced cell viability in multiple breast cancer subtypes at elevated concentrations, TNBC cell lines displayed significantly greater sensitivity at lower concentrations (< 5 μM), whereas non-TNBC subtypes and HDFs remained largely unaffected (Figure 1A-G). Correspondingly, the IC50 values derived from the 72-hour cell viability assays were lower in TNBC cells than in other breast cancer cell lines and HDFs (Figure 1H). Microscopic examination revealed morphological changes indicative of cell death, including cell shrinkage and loss of adherence in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells following α-viniferin treatment (Figure 1I and J). These findings demonstrate that α-viniferin exerts a pronounced and selective cytotoxic effect in TNBC cells relative to other breast cancer subtypes.

α-Viniferin induces intrinsic apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells

To ascertain whether the reduction in cell viability induced by α-viniferin in TNBC cells was mediated by apoptotic mechanisms, we conducted Annexin V/PI staining in MDA-MB-231 and MDA-MB-453 cells. Flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant increase in the Annexin V-positive cell population after α-viniferin treatment, indicating the induction of apoptotic cell death in both TNBC cell lines (Figure 2A and B). In MDA-MB-231 cells, significant increases were observed at 5 and 10 μM across all time points, with p < 0.05 at 5 μM at 24 hours and p < 0.001 under all other conditions (Figure 2A). Similarly, MDA-MB-453 cells showed significant increases at 2, 5, and 10 μM, with p < 0.05 at 24 hours and p < 0.001 at 48 and 72 hours (Figure 2B). To further confirm apoptotic cell death at the molecular level, we examined the cleavage of caspase-3 and PARP, which are hallmarks of apoptosis, using western blot analysis in MDA-MB-231 cells. Treatment with α-viniferin markedly increased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP in a time-dependent manner, and quantification showed significant increases at 5 and 10 μM (p < 0.001) (Figure 2C and D). In addition, we assessed MMP using TMRE staining. α-Viniferin induced concentration-dependent mitochondrial membrane hyperpolarization at an early time point (48 hours), with statistically significant increases at 5 and 10 μM (p < 0.001) (Figure 2E). In contrast, MMP depolarization was observed at a later time point (72 hours), which significant decreases at 5 and 10 μM (p < 0.001) (Figure 2F). Treatment with CCCP, used as a positive control, also significantly decreased MMP (p < 0.001) (Figure 2E and F). To further investigate the apoptotic features induced by α-viniferin, we evaluated intracellular ROS production. α-Viniferin increased ROS levels in a dose-dependent manner, with significant increases at 5 and 10 μM (p < 0.001) (Figure 2G). Notably, the expression levels of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Mcl-1, were also significantly decreased following α-viniferin treatment at 5 and 10 μM (p < 0.001) (Figure 2H and I). Collectively, these results indicate that α-viniferin induces intrinsic apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells, accompanied by MMP disruption and increased ROS levels.

α-Viniferin exerts synergistic effects with SB203580 in TNBC cells

Aberrant activation of MAPK signaling is implicated in the aggressive phenotype and therapeutic resistance of TNBC [21,22,23]. In this context, we evaluated the combined effects of α-viniferin with pharmacological inhibitors that target distinct MAPK pathways in MDA-MB-231 cells. Cell viability was assessed to investigate potential combined effects following co-treatment with α-viniferin and MAPK inhibitors, including SP600125 (a JNK inhibitor), SB203580 (a p38 inhibitor), and PD98059 (a MEK inhibitor). Among these, co-treatment with SB203580 enhanced α-viniferin-induced cell death, demonstrating a marked reduction in cell viability even at lower concentrations of α-viniferin (Figure 3A). This combined effect was validated at multiple time points (24, 48, and 72 hours), with significantly reductions observed in the combination treatment compared to single treatments at all time points (p < 0.001) (Figure 3B). Moreover, representative microscopy images provided further evidence of the enhanced cytotoxic effect resulting from the combined treatment (Figure 3C). To assess whether the observed combined effects were synergistic, we performed isobologram analysis, which revealed a synergistic interaction between α-viniferin and SB203580 (Figure 3D). Consistent with the enhanced cytotoxic effects, co-treatment with α-viniferin and SB203580 significantly increased the Annexin V-positive cell population compared to treatment with either agent alone (p < 0.001), whereas no significant differences were observed among the control, α-viniferin, and SB203580 single-treatment groups (Figure 3E). Furthermore, the combination treatment markedly increased the levels of cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP and reduced the expression of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Mcl-1, with statistically significant differences compared to single treatments (p < 0.001) (Figure 3F and G). These findings suggest that α-viniferin exerts synergistic pro-apoptotic effects in combination with SB203580 by enhancing intrinsic apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells.

Synergistic effect of α-viniferin and SB203580 is independent of p38 MAPK signaling

To ascertain whether the synergistic effect between α-viniferin and SB203580 is contingent upon p38 MAPK signaling, we examined p38 levels through western blot analysis. Treatment with α-viniferin did not significantly alter p38 phosphorylation (Figure 4A and B). To further investigate the role of p38 in cellular sensitivity to α-viniferin, we silenced p38α and p38β using siRNA prior to treatment with α-viniferin. Notably, the knockdown of either p38α or p38β did not significantly affect cell viability in response to α-viniferin treatment (Figure 4C). Importantly, the combined effect of α-viniferin and SB203580 on cell viability persisted following p38 knockdown (Figure 4D). In addition, the knockdown of p38α or p38β in combination with α-viniferin treatment did not markedly impact the expression levels of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins, despite the effective reduction in p38 levels (Figure 4E and F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the synergistic effect of α-viniferin and SB203580 operates independently of p38 MAPK signaling.

DISCUSSION

TNBC lacks established therapeutic targets, necessitating the development of novel therapeutic strategies to effectively target TNBC cells [1,2,3,4,5]. In this study, we assessed the effects of α-viniferin on cell viability across various breast cancer cell lines and found that TNBC cells exhibited heightened sensitivity to α-viniferin compared to other breast cancer subtypes. α-Viniferin induced intrinsic apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells, characterized by the disruption of MMP, activation of apoptotic markers, and decreased expression of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins. Notably, α-viniferin exhibited a synergistic effect with SB203580, further enhancing intrinsic apoptotic cell death and decreasing the expression of anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family proteins. Importantly, knockdown of p38α or p38β did not affect α-viniferin sensitivity, indicating that the observed synergistic effect with SB203580 occurs independently of p38 MAPK signaling. These findings underscore the potential of α-viniferin, particularly in combination with SB203580, as a promising strategy to target TNBC cells and address the absence of established therapeutic options through the induction of intrinsic apoptotic cell death.

TNBC cells are characterized by elevated basal levels of intracellular ROS compared to other breast cancer subtypes, attributable to altered mitochondrial metabolism and heightened oxidative stress signaling [24,25]. This intrinsic redox imbalance renders TNBC cells particularly susceptible to ROS accumulation [26]. α-Viniferin, a trimer of the stilbenoid polyphenol resveratrol, has been widely reported to promote ROS generation and induce DNA damage-associated senescence and apoptosis in cancer cells [27,28,29]. Consistent with prior studies, α-viniferin treatment markedly increased intracellular ROS levels in TNBC cells (Figure 2G). To determine whether ROS accumulation contributes to α-viniferin-induced apoptotic cell death, we conducted rescue experiments utilizing ROS scavengers, including N-acetylcysteine, glutathione, and Mito-TEMPO. Notably, these scavengers provided only limited protection against α-viniferin-induced cytotoxicity, suggesting that ROS accumulation may be a consequence of α-viniferin-induced apoptotic cell death rather than its initiator (Supplementary Figure 1). Therefore, the molecular targets responsible for α-viniferin-induced apoptotic cell death in TNBC cells remain unidentified, highlighting the need for further investigation.

In this study, we identified a synergistic interaction between α-viniferin and SB203580 that markedly enhances the cytotoxic effects of α-viniferin in TNBC cells. Although SB203580 is widely used as a p38 MAPK inhibitor, our findings indicate that this observed synergy is independent of p38 signaling. siRNA-mediated knockdown of p38α and p38β did not increase cellular sensitivity to α-viniferin, and the synergistic effect of SB203580 persisted even under conditions of p38 knockdown. Previous kinase profiling studies have shown that SB203580 can inhibit several kinases, including RIP2, CK1, GAK, c-Raf, PDK1, and GSK3, particularly at higher concentrations [30,31,32,33,34,35,36]. However, knockdown of either RIP2 or CK1 did not significantly enhance α-viniferin-induced cytotoxicity, indicating that these off-target effects alone are unlikely to account for the observed synergy (Supplementary Figures 2 and 3). Furthermore, despite the significant synergistic effect observed in vitro, the clinical application of first-generation p38 MAPK inhibitors, such as SB203580, remains limited due to their relatively low kinase selectivity, off-target activity, and unfavorable pharmacokinetic properties [36,37,38,39]. Therefore, identifying the specific molecular target of SB203580 that mediates its synergistic interaction with α-viniferin is crucial, as genetic or pharmacological modulation of this target, in combination with α-viniferin, may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for TNBC.

In summary, this study demonstrates that α-viniferin exhibits a pronounced cytotoxic effect in TNBC cells compared to other breast cancer subtypes. We further elucidate a novel synergistic interaction between α-viniferin and SB203580, which substantially enhances α-viniferin-induced cell death. Collectively, these findings underscore the therapeutic potential of α-viniferin in targeting TNBC.
