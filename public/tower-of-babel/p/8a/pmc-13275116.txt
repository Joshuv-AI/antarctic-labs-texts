The bench
Authors: David F Schaeffer
Published: 2026

Outside the emergency department at Vancouver General Hospital sits a bench.

For eight years, I’ve watched it from the window of my office.

When I first began working here, the bench functioned as shared space in the most ordinary way — a place for brief rest, for waiting, for a pause between destinations. Occasionally, someone would sit for a few minutes before moving on. Most days, it was empty.

That has changed as housing has grown more precarious and social supports thinner.

Most mornings now, the bench is occupied. When I arrive, someone is there, wrapped in a thin hospital blanket, belongings gathered close. Sometimes they’re asleep, sometimes awake but unmoving. Litter accumulates overnight and is cleared later in the day. The pattern repeats. The individuals change. The situation does not.

The bench sits at the boundary between hospital and street. It was never intended to hold anyone for long, but it now absorbs pressures far beyond its purpose. What was designed as a place for momentary pause — a commons — has become a place of shelter.

What was once briefly shared is now persistently occupied by those with nowhere else to go.

Perhaps the loss of a single bench is trivial when weighed against the needs of marginalized patients in an urban Canadian health care system. System constraints are far more severe in other settings. But the bench also stands for people without choice, without help. It stands for a system that has lost its buffering capacity. As inpatient beds and community supports become increasingly constrained, pressure is redistributed outward. The boundary of care extends beyond formal clinical environments into adjacent spaces — waiting rooms, corridors, and here, the threshold between hospital and street. The bench becomes a place to avoid, rather than a gift to all.

These changes are easy to overlook. What initially draws attention becomes invisible. What feels temporary begins to seem inevitable. But is it?

The bench outside my window matters because of what it reveals: a system struggling to meet demand.

What, then, is our role as physicians? It is tempting to remain at the sidelines — to focus on the encounter in front of us and regard everything beyond it as structural, inevitable, or someone else’s responsibility. A more direct and less comfortable path would be to make visible what can otherwise remain invisible. To document patterns, name fragmentation, and call out the places where care pathways fail. To engage patients and the public more explicitly in how care is used — moving beyond quiet adherence to stewardship toward active communication: explaining when tests and procedures are unlikely to change management, when waiting is appropriate, and when more is not better.

These conversations are not ancillary to care; they are part of it. In a publicly funded system, stewardship is not only a policy objective — it is a shared responsibility.

Our goal should not be to reclaim the bench, but rather to understand why it has been forced to serve this function, and to decide whether we are willing to lose the commons.
