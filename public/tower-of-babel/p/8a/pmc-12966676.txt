Uncoupled bone remodeling drives myeloma bone disease in Vk*MYC mouse model of multiple myeloma
Authors: Clarissa Schmal, Marija K. Simic, Ya Xiao, Alicia A. Catala, Millie Jiang, Thomas Levin Andersen, Marta Diaz-delCastillo, Peter Croucher, Michelle McDonald, Abbas Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma (MM), the second-most frequent hematologic malignancy, is caused by the neoplastic expansion of clonal plasma cells. Up to 80% of MM patients develop myeloma bone disease (MMBD), a hallmark of which is the development of osteolytic lesions that can lead to skeletal related events such as fractures, spinal cord compression and bone pain. In the Vk*MYC mouse model of MM, MYC is expressed in germinal center B cells, facilitating the formation of malignant plasma cells and development of major characteristics of MM, including MMBD. However, the extent of skeletal changes and underlying cellular mechanisms in the Vk*MYC model has, to date, not been comprehensively described. Here, we provide such comprehensive characterization of MMBD and the associated structural and cellular changes in the femur of mice inoculated with the Vk*MYC cell line Vk14451.Vk*MYC mice developed significant osteolytic lesions in the femur. Serum analysis revealed a notable decrease in the bone formation marker amino terminal propeptide of type I procollagen (P1NP) (−33.3%, p < 0.01) in Vk*MYC mice compared to naïve control mice, while C-telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-1) levels remained unchanged. Microcomputed tomography (microCT) analysis demonstrated significant deterioration of the trabecular bone structure in Vk*MYC as compared to naïve control mice, evidenced by reductions in bone volume fraction (−49.2%, P < 0.05), bone surface to bone volume ratio (−22.3%, P < 0.001), trabecular number (−46.3%, P < 0.0001) and connectivity density (−79.5%, P < 0.05), as well as increased trabecular separation (+86.3%, P < 0.0001) and thickness (+20.3%, P < 0.001). Cortical bone analysis indicated increased cortical porosity (+164.5%, P < 0.0001) and decreased thickness (−26.9%, P < 0.001) in the Vk*MYC mice. Histological studies revealed a decrease in osteoblast surface relative to bone surface (−49.7%, P < 0.001) and an increase in osteoclast surface relative to bone surface (+53.9%, P < 0.01).Collectively, our findings indicate significant bone loss and development of MMBD in the Vk*MYC mouse model of MM, driven by uncoupled bone remodeling characterized by decreased osteoblast activity and increased osteoclast burden. These data highlight the relevance of the Vk*MYC 14451 syngeneic model of myeloma in studies aiming to explore MM bone disease in immunocompetent C57Blk6 mice.

Introduction

Multiple myeloma (MM) is a hematologic malignancy caused by the uncontrolled, clonal expansion of plasma cells within the bone marrow (BM) (van de Donk et al., 2021). Up to 80% of MM patients develop myeloma bone disease (MMBD), which is defined by the formation of osteolytic bone lesions. These lesions can lead to severe skeletal-related events, including pathological fractures, spinal cord compression and bone pain (Abbas et al., 2025). Despite significant advancements in therapeutic strategies, MM remains an incurable disease, with its global incidence and prevalence projected to rise until 2050 (Hou et al., 2025). This trend underscores its continued, significant burden on patients and healthcare systems alike.

To study MM and MMBD pathogenesis and to evaluate novel therapeutic approaches, various animal models have been developed (Mehdi et al., 2021; Du et al., 2024). Among these, only two models established in immunocompetent mice have been described to develop MM bone disease: the spontaneously arising 5T models and the transgenic VkMYC models (Mehdi et al., 2021). As the 5T models have been well described in terms of bone disease, their utility has dominated the field of MMBD research. However, the 5T models are either limited by their environment dependence (5T2MM) or do not reproduce the typical cortical lesions (5TGM1, 5T33). They also represent a single clonal murine MM like disease, which limits their relevance to the highly heterogenous human disease (Du et al., 2024). In addition, these lines require specificity for the C57BL/KaLwRij strain, and are limited in their capacity to be modified (5T2 and 5T33). The Vk*MYC mouse model on the other hand is readily manipulated in vitro, offers extensive bone disease including cortical lesions and reflects more closely the human condition. Exploiting the physiological process somatic hypermutation (SMH) to activate a MYC transgene, the transduced Vk*MYC model is an advance on the spontaneous 5T series (Chesi et al., 2008). MYC is an oncogene that plays a critical role in regulating cell proliferation, growth and differentiation. Its dysregulation is commonly associated with various cancers, including MM (Dang, 2012). In the Vk*MYC model, the human MYC gene (exons 2 and 3) is introduced into the Vk21 gene, coding for the variable parts of the kappa light chain (V-kappa exon), a component of B-cell receptors. When germinal center B-cells begin to produce B-cell receptors, the V-kappa exon and MYC are transcribed. Initially, the translation of the MYC mRNA is blocked by an engineered stop codon. However, as SMH occurs - a process where B-cells introduce mutations to enhance receptor affinity for specific antigens - the enzyme Activation-Induced Deaminase introduces changes in the DNA, effectively reversing the stop-codon (Chesi et al., 2008). This allows for the activation of MYC in a cell-type specific, precisely timed and sporadic manner, leading to the manifestation of several features characteristic of human MM in affected mice (Chesi et al., 2008).

Follow-up studies have shown that transplantation of Vk*MYC myeloma cells leads to development of MM in the recipient mice, thereby validating the model's utility for testing anti-MM drug therapies (Chesi et al., 2012). Previous skeletal evaluations of these mice have provided evidence of trabecular bone loss in tibia (Chesi et al., 2012) and femur (Chesi et al., 2008). Another study demonstrated trabecular bone loss in Cd40lf/f control mice femora in response transplantation with the cell line Vk12653 (Xie et al., 2025). In addition, they demonstrated lesion development and histological changes in mice injected with IgG (Xie et al., 2025). However, there is to date no correlative assessment of the cellular mechanisms that govern these changes.

Given the growing interest utility of Vk*MYC model for identifying novel therapeutic strategies for MM and MMBD (Villa et al., 2022; Nakamura et al., 2018; Sokolowska et al., 2023; Nedal et al., 2024; Chesi et al., 2016; Meermeier et al., 2021; Minnie et al., 2018; Guillerey et al., 2018; Maura et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024), it is imperative to have a more comprehensive understanding of the skeletal manifestation of MM in this model to facilitate robust analysis of these novel strategies on MMBD.

In this study, we report that transplantation of Vk*MYC 14451 myeloma cells causes significant deterioration of the bone structure in the femur, leading to formation of osteolytic lesions, altered systemic bone turnover markers, decreased osteoblast surface and expansion of osteoclast surface area.

Materials and methods

Animal models

Female C57Blk6 mice were purchased from the Australian Bio Resources facility and housed in a pathogen free facility with ad libitum access standard chow and water. Bone marrow cells were extracted from Vk*MYC mice harboring the Vk14451-GFP cell line (Maura et al., 2024), provided in kind by Leif Bergsagel and Marta Chesi Department of Medicine, Division of Hematology and Medical Oncology, Mayo Clinic, Scottsdale, AZ, USA. Then, the 16 weeks-old, naïve C57Blk6 mice were intravenously injected with 250,000 of these bone marrow cells per mouse suspended in 200 μL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Mice were sacrificed nine weeks post cell inoculation. Age-matched control mice received an equivalent volume of saline. All animal experiments were approved by the Garvan Institute/St Vincent's Hospital Animal Ethics Committee (ARA 18/08) and the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purpose.

Flow-cytometry analysis

Tumor burden in the BM from the right tibia and spleen was assessed using flow-cytometry analysis. Proximal and distal ends were removed, and cells were flushed from the bone marrow cavity with 2% FCS using a 23G needle, followed by filtration through a 100 μm filter. Spleens were minced gently using a syringe tip and the resulting cell suspension was also filtered through a 100 μm filter.

Prior to flow-cytometry analysis using a FACS-Canto II (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA), erythrocytes were lysed using ammonium chloride and Dapi was used to determine live cell populations.

Micro-computed tomography

Left femora were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 24 h and subsequently stored in 70% ethanol until further analysis.

High-resolution micro-computed tomography (microCT) analysis was performed using a vivaCT40 (Scanco Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland) to assess MM-induced changes in the left femora, following current guidelines (Bouxsein et al., 2010). Left femora were aligned perpendicular to the scanning axis and scanned wrapped in parafilm without additional scanning medium. Scanning parameters included an isotropic voxel size of 10.5 μm, X-ray tube potential of 70 kVP, X-ray intensity of 114 μA, an integration time of 300 ms and 1000 projections.

Rotation to cross-sectional scans and further analysis was performed in Xming (Scanco Medical Brüttisellen, Switzerland). Trabecular bone parameters were analyzed in a 1 mm volume of interest (VOI), starting immediately at the distal end of the growth plate, where the four marrow cavities fuse. The contouring line was drawn a few pixels away from the endocortical surface to avoid inclusion of cortical structures. All bones were evaluated using a global threshold of 182.

Cortical bone parameters were evaluated in a 500 μm VOI, located 600 μm below the proximal end of the growth plate, using a global threshold of 190.

3D-reconstruction

Three dimensional reconstructed images of femora were generated using Drishti imaging software version 2.4 (ANU, Canberra, Australia). For generation of 3D images of cortical and trabecular bones, scans were re-segmented using CTAn (Bruker, Belgium).

Quantification of osteolytic lesions

Using Xming, we obtained 3D-models of the whole left femurs that were screenshotted at equal orientations. Surface area of lesions as well as number of lesions were analyzed using Image J. Data from anterior and posterior surfaces was summed. Lesions located on and between the femur condyles as well as accessory bone structures and the area below such were excluded from analysis.

Histology

For histological analysis, left femora were decalcified in 0.5 M EDTA and 0.04% PFA for a duration of 3 weeks. Paraffin-embedded samples were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) for general morphology, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) to identify osteoclasts, and RNAScope based in-situ hybridization (ISH) for Collagen type 1 alpha 1 (Col1a1) to assess Col1a1HIGH bone-forming osteoblasts following as previously described (El-Masri et al., 2024).

TRAcP-stained sections were analyzed to quantify osteoclast surface. This analysis was conducted in a defined region of interest (ROI) located between two lines: the first line was drawn perpendicular to the growth plate at a distance of 300 μm towards the diaphysis, and the second line was positioned perpendicular to the first line, extending 2 mm in the same direction. Bone surface was evaluated on endocortical and trabecular surfaces. The percentage of TRAcP+ osteoclast-covered surfaces per bone surface (Oc.S/BS) and Col1a1HIGH bone-forming osteoblast surfaces per bone surface (Col1a1HIGH Ob.S/BS) were quantified.

ELISA

Serum concentrations of the bone formation marker amino terminal propeptide of type I procollagen (P1NP) and the bone resorption marker C-telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX-1) were determined using colorimetric ELISA kits (Immunodiagnostic Systems), following the manufacturer's instructions.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed in GraphPad Prism 9.5.0, and results are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Two-tailed Student's t-tests were used to determine the statistical significance between the groups, with P-values P ≤ 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Intravenous injection of Vk*MYC cells leads to development of MM and MMBD

To evaluate the development of MM following the injection of the Vk*MYC cells, we assessed tumor burden in the spleen and BM (right tibia) of control and Vk*MYC mice. Flow-cytometry analysis of GFP+ (Vk*MYC) cells indicated establishment of MM in the spleen and bone marrow of the mice that received Vk*MYC cells (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). In addition, we observed increased spleen weight in the mice that received Vk*MYC cells compared to control mice (+115%, P < 0.01) (Supplementary Fig. 1C).

Next, we evaluated the development of MMBD by quantifying osteolytic lesions in the femur. Osteolytic lesions were predominantly located in distal and proximal metaphysis (Fig. 1A). Vk*MYC model of MM presents a significant deterioration of the trabecular bone structure

We then determined the trabecular bone parameters using microCT analysis to further characterize the phenotypic representation of MMBD in the femur of Vk*MYC cell recipient mice. The reconstructed 3D images clearly illustrated significant deterioration of the trabecular bone structure in Vk*MYC mice (Fig. 2A). Notably, Vk*MYC mice exhibited a significant reduction in bone volume fraction (−49.2%, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2B), bone surface to bone volume (−22.3%, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2C), connectivity density (−79.5%, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2D) and trabecular number (−46.3%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2E), compared to control mice, whereas trabecular separation was increased in Vk*MYC mice (+86.3%, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2F). Discussion

There has been an increasing interest in employing Vk*MYC mouse model of MM for studying pathogenesis of MM and MMBD. Recent evidence indicates that the Vk*MYC model closely replicates genomic and immunological features of human MM (Maura et al., 2024). Specifically, MYC activation in this model leads to formation of tumors with secondary mutations that mirror the genetic characteristics of human MM, including common driver gene mutations, aneuploidies, and APOBEC mutational activity (Maura et al., 2024). In addition, the Vk*MYC model reflects immunological changes of human MM such as B cell lymphopenia and expansion of CD8+ effector memory T cells (Cooke et al., 2016). Moreover, a recent study, investigating the effect of immune checkpoint blockade for treatment of MM, found that certain features of the tumor microenvironment of Vk*MYC mice resembled that of human MM more closely than the 5TGM1 model, another commonly used mouse model for MM (Xiong et al., 2025). This highlights the significance of the Vk*MYC mouse model in accurately modeling human MM, providing a platform for its use more broadly in pre-clinical research. Our data provides evidence that is lacking to date (Chesi et al., 2008; Chesi et al., 2012; Xie et al., 2025; Maura et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2024) in our capacity to assess myeloma bone disease in mouse models. These data will increase the utility of the Vk*MYC model, in the assessment of the impact of interventions on MMBD importantly overcoming the limitations that are presented by the 5T models.

Our results indicate that intravenous inoculation with Vk*MYC cells leads to development of MM, as evidenced by the presence of myeloma cells in the spleen and BM, along with an increase in spleen weight. These observations align with previous results that have reported high tumor burden in the BM and spleen (Chesi et al., 2012; Cooke et al., 2016; Bradey et al., 2022) as well as the development of splenomegaly (Jiang et al., 2024) following intravenous transplantation of Vk*MYC cells. Furthermore, we document significant trabecular bone loss and the formation of osteolytic lesions in the femur by 3D microCT analysis. These data correspond to observations made in both transgenic and transplanted Vk*MYC models (Chesi et al., 2008; Chesi et al., 2012). Notably, we observed increased cortical porosity in Vk*MYC mice compared to naïve controls, which reflects lesion development, corroborates our findings through different methodologies and offers the first quantitative evidence of cortical bone loss in Vk*MYC models. We speculate that high bone turnover in the metaphysis of the bone may explain the predominant location of lesions in these areas.

Our analysis revealed significant deterioration of the trabecular bone structures in the Vk*MYC model of MM. Our data is consistent with previous studies, that reported reduced trabecular number in the proximal femur in the transgenic Vk*MYC model (Chesi et al., 2008) and trabecular bone loss in both the proximal tibia and femur in the transplanted Vk*MYC model (Chesi et al., 2012; Xie et al., 2025). Interestingly, we observed an increase in the average trabecular thickness. This unexpected finding, alongside the observed decrease in trabecular number and increase in trabecular separation, suggests that the increase in trabecular thickness may result from the complete resorption of smaller trabecular elements, leaving only larger trabecular structures intact within the BM.

Histological analysis on both trabecular and cortical surfaces revealed a decreased percentage of bone-forming osteoblast surfaces, which correlates with the decreased serum P1NP-concentration measured in serum samples of Vk*MYC mice compared to naïve controls. This finding indicates a decrease in both osteoblast abundance and activity. In contrast, an increased percentage of osteoclast surfaces was noted in the femurs from Vk*MYC cell recipient mice, while serum CTX-1 concentrations remained unchanged. This lack of change in CTX-1 may be attributed to the timing of serum collection, which could have occurred after most bone loss had already taken place. Temporal assessment of CTX-1 throughout the study time course would determine if this was the case, but these samples were not taken in this study. In conclusion, these results indicate that bone loss and lesion formation in the Vk*MYC model of MM are primarily driven by uncoupled bone remodeling characterized by an increased burden of osteoclasts and a concurrent decrease in osteoblast abundance and activity on the remaining bone surfaces, rather than by increased osteoclast activity alone. Notably, while several prior studies have attributed Vk*MYC-associated MMBD to reduced osteoblast function and increased osteoclast burden, our data provide the first direct cellular evidence in this model, through quantitative bone histomorphometry together with serum bone turnover markers, demonstrating this uncoupled remodeling phenotype.

Early histological studies of human MMBD indicated that the formation of osteolytic lesions is primarily driven by increased recruitment and activity of osteoclasts (Bataille et al., 1991). However, subsequent studies focusing on the microanatomy of human MMBD samples have revealed that the uncoupling of bone formation and resorption, rather than heightened osteoclast activity alone, plays a crucial role in the pathophysiology of MMBD (Andersen et al., 2009; Andersen et al., 2010). Specifically, these studies demonstrated disturbances in bone remodeling compartments (BRCs) from MMBD patients, distinguishing them from the intact BRCs of healthy controls (Bradey et al., 2022). This disturbance of BRCs is caused by the disruption of a group of flat canopy cells with osteoblast-like characteristics and correlates with the occurrence of osteolytic lesions (Bataille et al., 1991). Disruption of the BRCS allows for close physical contact between multiple myeloma (MM) cells and bone cells, leading to an uncoupling effect characterized by enhanced bone resorption and inhibited bone formation (Bataille et al., 1991). Clinical studies also provide evidence of trabecular bone destruction in MM patients (Chen et al., 2021; Takasu et al., 2011).

Thus, there is significant overlap between skeletal characteristics features of MMBD in the immune intact Vk*MYC murine model and advanced human MMBD, underscoring the model's utility for research. Our findings provide valuable insights that can guide future investigations into the mechanisms underlying MM and MMBD, as well as potential therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating bone loss in affected patients.
