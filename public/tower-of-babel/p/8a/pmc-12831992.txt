Time estimation variability in delay discounting: Model comparison and considerations ☆
Authors: Brett W Gelino, Madison E Graham, Justin C Strickland, Matthew W Johnson, Derek D Reed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDelay discounting describes an organism’s sensitivity to delayed consequences. The advent of hyperboloid discounting models introduced the s discounting parameter, a fitted metric that reduces residual variance when working with human subjects. Mathematically, discounting s allows for a nonlinear relation between consequence (A) and delay (D) and could be conceptually accounted for as a psychophysical scalar that describes subjective differences in the rate of time passage. This study offers a proof-of-concept examination of hyperboloid s in relation to participant time-based experiences. A crowdsourced sample of adults (N = 211) completed tasks measuring time estimation (i.e., reporting how much time passed during a distractor task) and temporal distance estimation (i.e., reporting how temporally distant a delay feels). Participants generally underestimated durations in the time estimation task, where income and completion of a preceding day-framed discounting task (versus date-framed), but not Myerson and Green’s s (b = −0.19), and modestly Rachlin’s s (b = 0.82), exhibited a statistically significant relation with time estimation accuracy. Similarly, participant age and delay framing, but not hyperboloid s, exhibited a statistically significant relation with temporal distance estimation discrepancies. Broadly, data suggest that s may not be accounting for differences in time estimation as measured in this sample. At most, the modest effect observed for Rachlin’s s suggests limited conceptual value, meriting further consideration of the metric’s account of subject-level differences.Keywords: Behavioral economics, Delay discounting, Hyperboloid, Temporal distance, Time estimation

Introduction

Decades of operant conditioning science have documented that consequences are most effective when contacted sooner and with certainty (Ainslie, 1974; Chung and Herrnstein, 1967; Rachlin and Green, 1972). These conditioning principles underscore the operant behavioral economic framework of discounting. Delay discounting examines the extent to which delaying a consequence reduces its impact on behavior, for example, the decrease in value of a delayed reinforcer. Similarly, probability discounting presents participants with a probable outcome and a larger concurrently available option that is constrained by increased odds against contact (i.e., less-than-guaranteed outcome delivery; Rachlin et al., 1987, 1991). Across discounting frameworks, the relative subjective preference between these options is reflective of unique characteristics of the responding organism’s immediate context, learning history, and biological composition (see reviews by Gelino et al., 2024; Rung and Madden, 2018).

In the discounting sciences, participants indicate outcome valuation through a variety of tasks geared toward understanding the effect of temporal and probabilistic constraints. Researchers typically model responses across a series of discrete reinforcer assessments (e.g., “Would you rather have $500 now or $1000 in one year?”) to assemble a molar model of participant-level sensitivity to reinforcer constraint. Many such approaches rely on nonlinear modeling to account for and describe responses at each of these constraints. Notably, imposed reinforcer costs exert proportionally more influence over behavior at initial delays or odds-against, as compared to the upper end of imposed costs (Mazur, 1987; cf. Samuelson, 1937). Said differently, participants exhibit a hyperbolic-like rate of reinforcer devaluation, where lesser costs result in relatively large declines in subjective reinforcer valuation toward an eventual plateau, after which greatly escalating costs exert proportionately less effect on reinforcer value.

The added value of the s parameter is notable via the generally reduced residual variance attributable to hyperboloid descriptions of discount responding (McKerchar et al., 2009; Myerson and Green, 1995; Rachlin, 2006). Conceptually, the s parameter is theorized to correspond to unique participant-level sensitivities to amount or delay magnitude, but the specific scaling mechanism remains undetermined. In one attempt to elucidate the metric scaling interpretation, McKerchar et al. (2010); see also Mitchell et al., (2015) demonstrated that the s parameter did not co-vary with the amount of the delayed reward in delay discounting, but did co-vary with the amount of an uncertain reward in probability discounting. They thereby proposed that this asymmetrical finding was inconsistent with the psychophysical scaling interpretation of the s parameter. The intensity of an imposed delay may subjectively vary across participants, particularly if factors like biological repertoire (e.g., McCormack et al., 1999; Treisman et al., 1990) and immediate environmental context (e.g., Matthews et al., 2011) are to vary. Extant research examining delay framing effects has offered plausible conceptual value to the notion that discrepant time perception may influence discount rates (see review by Rung and Madden, 2018), but this work does not explicitly endorse the role of s as a psychophysical scalar of delay sensitivity. Thus, more work is needed to better understand what conceptual aspect of choice behavior lends practical credence to the s psychophysical scalar.

Discounting tasks vary in how they describe the relation between delay and reward valuation. For instance, in classic experimental literature, nonhuman subjects experienced real delays and, as a function of these experiences, made varied choices across concurrently available options during future trials (e.g., Ainslie, 1974). This real experience of time passing refers to time perception—the lived experience of passing time. Human studies have similarly programmed such time-experience trials (e.g., Johnson, 2012; Xu et al., 2020), but a growing body of literature instead presents simulated scenarios wherein human participants report their preference for concurrently available rewards, one of which is constrained by an imagined delay (e.g., Rachlin et al., 1991). Participants do not experience the delay in these tasks, but rather must engage in temporal distance estimation.

Psychological literature documents numerous experiments that examine the role of biological variables and human ability in estimating the passing of time (e.g., time production). For instance, a commonly used approach presents participants with a tone of a given length, followed by a secondary tone that participants must “interrupt” at approximately the length of the first tone (e.g., Fortin et al., 2005; see also Grondin, 2010). This perception mapping is efficacious for quantifying subjective differences in experience but may not effectively measure differences in participant-level estimations for future delays—those not yet experienced. Research has examined the role of time estimation in discount responding (e.g., Kim and Zauberman, 2009; Zauberman et al., 2009; see also Lu et al., 2023), with findings indicating appreciable differences between subjective temporal distance estimation and objective delay duration. Recent research suggests that individual differences in intertemporal choice may, in part, reflect variability in the subjective representation of time intervals (Agostino et al., 2017, 2019, 2021). To our knowledge, no study has yet examined differences in participant temporal distance estimation in conjunction with the added value of the hyperboloid s psychophysical scalar.

The purpose of this study was to offer a proof-of-concept for the practical correspondence between discrepancies in subjective time estimation and the best-fit psychophysical scalar s as denoted by hyperboloid discounting models. Participants completed iterations of an adjusting-amount titrating discount task randomly presented as either date- or delay-framed. Several additional measures of time estimation provided markers against which to examine the relative distribution of best-fit s values. We hypothesized that, should the hyperboloid model provide a valid conceptual interpretation of unique differences in time estimation, the distribution of s values should be largely associated with subsequent differences in these behavioral measures of time estimation.

Method

Participants

Study participants were recruited via the Prolific (www.prolific.com) crowdsourcing platform, a tool used by behavioral scientists to contact diverse and customizable sample pools. In this instance, we constrained study access to workers reporting English as a first language and having an approval rating of at least 99 % across at least 500 task submissions, with no self-reported a) history of cigarette use, b) attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, or c) color blindness. Participants received $3.00 as compensation for a completed dataset, with an average time to survey completion of approximately 15 min. Recruitment resulted in a final sample of 271 responses.

Materials

We designed and administered all study materials using Qualtrics Experience Management, an industry-leading survey design platform. Participants completed all experimental tasks prior to providing demographic information. All described procedures were approved by the University of Kansas Institutional Review Board (protocol # 20635).

Time estimation and temporal distance estimation

Study materials contained multiple brief assessments intended to serve as proxy measures for subjective time estimation and temporal distance estimation. The first of these prompted participants to closely examine a “magic eye”-type image. These images are computer generated such that no discernable shape is apparent at first glance, but a prolonged gaze can reveal a hidden three-dimensional landscape. Participants viewed this image for approximately 21 s. While onscreen, participants received a prompt to examine the image and decipher the hidden scene. Following presentation, participants were asked to report (a) the scene depicted in the image, if any, and (b) in seconds, their estimation of how much time the image was presented on screen.

A second measure gauged temporal distance estimation by presenting dates customary of the adjusting-amount delay discounting task (i.e., 1 days, 1 week, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, 5 years, 25 years) and prompting participants to report, at each delay, “How long do [they] consider the duration between today and the delay listed.” Participants logged their responses using a 100-point visual analogue scale where the left-most anchor (1) denoted “very temporally close” and the right-most anchor (100) denoted “very temporally distant.” A congruent task measured time estimation in an identical manner but instead presented delays as calendar dates yoked to the day of completion (e.g., a calendar date exactly one year from [today’s date], where [today’s date] is the day of task completion). Participants logged estimations for both delay frames (i.e., days and dates).

Adjusting-amount delay discounting task

The adjusting-amount task (see Du et al., 2002; Richards et al., 1997 for more details) presents a series of monetary values where an immediately available reward is offered concurrent with a delayed, larger magnitude reward constrained at seven delays (i.e., 1 day, 1 week, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, 5 years, 25 years). For each delay, the magnitude of the smaller-sooner reward is systematically varied until participants reach a “point of indecision” in preference between receipt of the immediately available reward and the larger delayed reward. This indifference point is said to represent the valuation of the delayed reward in terms of the immediately available reward. Indifference points at each delay can then be used to model the extent to which increasing temporal constraints result in prospective reward devaluation (i.e., discount rate).

For the present study, participants completed one version of this task where delays were presented as either (a) days or (b) calendar dates yoked to the date of completion (e.g., a calendar date exactly one year from [today’s date], where [today’s date] is the day of task completion). Participants were randomly presented with one of these task iterations yielding a between-group arrangement.

Future time perspective scale (FTP)

Participants completed the FTP as a measure of temporal horizon distance, or the degree to which participants envision the future as temporally near or distant (Carstensen and Lang, 1996). Participants used a 7-point Likert scale to indicate their agreement with statements pertaining to perceptions of future events (e.g., “Most of my life lies ahead of me.”). Higher sum scores are thought to indicate more distant temporal horizons, where values above 50 indicate relatively broad time perspectives. In a sample of adult respondents, the survey showed strong internal validity (Cronbach’s ⍺ =.92; Carstensen and Lang, 1996).

Survey Order

Participants were randomized into two groups distinct with respect to survey completion order. In each, participants completed a day- or date-framed temporal distance estimation task (within-subject) followed by an identically framed adjusting-amount discounting task (between-subject). Participants then completed the “magic eye” time estimation task, the second framing of the temporal distance estimation task (i.e., day versus date), and finally the FTP.

Data analysis

Use of the Qualtrics survey platform permitted export of the exact timing during which the “magic eye” image appeared on screen for each participant (M = 21.08 s, SD = 0.23 s). We calculated a change score for time estimation as the difference between reported time (in seconds) and actual time (in seconds), where underestimates are negative values. All data preparations and inferential tests were performed using R Studio. Code is available for access in accordance with open science initiatives.

Data quality

In matching with field analytic customs, we assessed the quality of responses to the delay discounting tasks using criteria outlined by Johnson and Bickel (2008). Specifically, we flagged responses for violating criteria of bounce (i.e., a stepwise increase in reward valuation relative to increasing delay of greater than 10 % of the greatest reported valuation) and trend (i.e., a failure to suppress reward valuation from greatest reported valuation to least reported valuation by at least 10 % of the greatest reported valuation). Responses violating these criteria were retained and incorporated in all described analyses.

General Inferential Approach

As a measure of time estimation accuracy, we first used a one-sample t-test to determine if aggregate subjective time-estimation for the “magic eye” task meaningfully deviated from a set value of 21 s (i.e., if the mean differed from a change score of 0 s). Supplemental linear models examined how Myerson and Green’s (1995) and Rachlin’s (2006) best-fit discounting s and demographic variables relate to these discrepancies in participant-level magic-eye time estimation accuracy (i.e., differences from the true time duration). We then used the R package nlme to fit linear mixed-effects models accounting for repeated-measures task administration to examine discounting s, demographic covariates, and adjusting-amount task iteration (day- or date-framed) as predictors of temporal distance estimation across day- and date-framed delays (i.e., 100-point scale). Finally, we calculated a slope depicting the relation between the objective delays as presented in the adjusting-amount task and participants’ estimated temporal distance for those delays. We generated a Pearson correlation coefficient to examine the extent to which this slope value exhibited a meaningful relation with best fit Myerson and Green’s (1995) and Rachlin’s (2006) hyperboloid discounting s values.

Time estimation

A one-sample t-test comparing subject-level time estimation accuracy (i.e., change scores) during the magic-eye distractor test (M = −2.73, SD = 11.4) against a set value of 0 (zero) revealed a statistically significant difference (t[270] = −3.96, p < .001, 95 % CI = [−4.09, −1.37]), where participants perceived durations that were less than the true duration. Table 2 presents the results of a linear model featuring Myerson and Green’s (1995) best-fit s. Participants with higher income status (b = −2.72, p < .001) and who completed day-framed discounting tasks (b = −2.11, p < .001) exhibited greater underestimation of the temporal duration. Interestingly, hyperboloid s (b = −0.19, p = .39) was not significantly related to time estimation accuracy. Table 3 presents the results of a linear model featuring Rachlin’s (2006) best-fit s. Larger best-fit hyperboloid s values were associated with more positive discrepancies (b = 0.82, p = .044), suggesting greater overprediction of the temporal duration. Furthermore, younger participants (b = −2.99, p = .001) with higher income (b = −3.03, p < .001) and greater future time perspective (b = −0.09, p < .001) exhibited greater underestimation of the temporal duration.

Discussion

The formalization of hyperboloid discounting models introduced an s parameter that improves model descriptive fits, particularly when working with human participants (e.g., Myerson and Green, 1995; Rachlin, 2006). Because this added parameter alters the relation between amount and delay thereby reducing model residuals, discounting s has been conceptually theorized as a scalar of subjective sensitivity to consequence and/or delay magnitude. The goal of this study was to examine how discounting s corresponds with participant-unique performance on time estimation tasks. Results broadly failed to support the correspondence between hyperboloid s and subject-unique differences in time estimation as measured in this sample, instead highlighting relations between time estimation and income, age, and task framing.

Time estimation

When asked to indicate the length of time for which a “magic eye” image was presented on screen, participants generally reported having perceived the duration as shorter than the “true” length of 21 s. This finding tracks with documented findings in experimental psychology. For instance, research has found that individuals who are highly distracted, as in with mathematical exercises, often judge less time to have passed as compared to less distracted counterparts (e.g., von Sturmer et al., 1968).

Data showed mixed evidence that discounting s meaningfully accounted for subject-level discrepancies in time estimation as measured in this sample. When modeling Myerson and Green’s (1995) s, we observed no statistically significant relation between best-fit s and estimation accuracy. In contrast, our model featuring Rachlin’s (2006) s suggested a modest but statistically significant association between best-fit s and time estimation. While this finding hints at possible conceptual value for s, the effect size was small, and interpretation should remain cautious.

Some covariates exhibited notable associations with discrepancies in time estimation. Individual earnings of greater than $100k annually were associated with underestimation of the temporal duration in both models. These findings may align with extant research, as in findings that increases to participant income can lessen subsequent subjective rates of discounting (e.g., Kirby et al., 2002). However, to our knowledge no perception research has yet documented a relation between participant income status and time estimation or replication tasks. Interestingly, date-framed delays were associated with underestimation of the temporal duration only when entered as a covariate alongside Myerson and Green’s (1995) s. We know of no study that has yet depicted a statistically significant relation between task framing and subsequent changes in time estimation. Discounting research has documented an effect whereby participants completing a date-framed discounting task exhibit lesser rates of discounting compared to participants completing a day-framed task (e.g., DeHart and Odum, 2015; Naudé et al., 2018), meriting greater research on how task framing interfaces with acute changes in time estimation.

Temporal distance estimation

As measured in this sample, Myerson and Green (1995) and Rachlin (2006) hyperboloid discounting s were not significantly associated with subjective temporal distance estimation. Notably, participant age showed a meaningful negative relation where older participants estimated that delays, irrespective of framing, were less far in the future. Experimental psychology (e.g., Coelho et al., 2003; Wittman and Lehnhoff, 2005) and discounting (e.g., Green et al., 1994; Kirby et al., 2002) research has documented perceptions of faster time passing and lower discounting rates as participants age. Although we did not observe this relation for time estimation, we take the presence of aging effects on temporal distance estimation as supporting the validity of our model in the context of broader psychological research.

Mixed modeling also revealed a statistically significant effect of delay framing on temporal distance estimation, and a significant interaction effect of delay framing for the temporal distance estimation task and framing on the delay discounting task. Put simply, participants estimated greater temporal distance for day-framed delays, a finding observed to a lesser extent among participants who completed the date-framed discounting task. That delays framed as dates were viewed as less temporally distant may provide conceptual support for the date-delay discounting literature wherein participants have exhibited lower discounting rates when tasks present delays as yoked calendar dates (DeHart and Odum, 2015; Naudé et al., 2018; Read et al., 2005; see also Rung and Madden, 2018). These data also support the notion that the acute effect of date-framed delays, as viewed on a discounting task, may carry forward to some extent. That is, participants who completed the date-framed discounting task would have done so (along with the date-framed temporal distance estimation task) before completing the day-framed temporal estimation task. In sum, data support a more concerted examination of date-framing as a potential mechanism for reducing discounting rates via acutely altered time perception.

Relation to hyperboloid s

Results weakly support the conceptual role of hyperboloid s in accounting for changes in time estimation or temporal distance estimation across participants as measured in this study. Myerson and Green’s (1995) best fit s value was not a statistically significant predictor in linear models of time estimation discrepancies or temporal distance estimation. Rachlin’s (2006) best fit s was modestly but significantly related to differences in time estimation, though this association was small and provides only limited evidence that s holds conceptual value beyond improving model fit. Further, neither Myerson and Green’s (1995) nor Rachlin’s (2006) best fit s exhibited a meaningful correlation with objective delay-estimation slopes. It is difficult to ascertain the variables that influence differences in time estimation, a complex that is likely dependent on interfacing biological and environmental variables. Notably, our linear mixed model had a relatively high residual value suggesting that much of the variance in subjective temporal distance estimation was unaccounted for by study variables. In considering the relative dearth of research conceptually linking hyperboloid s to differences in time estimation, and in light of this study’s findings, more work is needed to continue parsing the conceptual interpretation of the s parameter. Research has thus far failed to find evidence conceptually linking discounting s to differences in consequence magnitude sensitivity (e.g., McKerchar et al., 2010; Mitchell et al., 2015), although more work is needed to examine how environmental differences may affect this sensitivity. In the absence of a sound conceptual underpinning, discounting s may not meaningfully relate to an independent construct like time estimation. At most, the modest Rachlin effect offers tentative evidence that s might reflect some conceptual dimension, but the stronger likelihood is that it functions primarily as a scaling parameter to improve descriptive fit.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several notable strengths and limitations as a conceptual proof-of-concept of hyperboloid discounting s as a subjective time sensitivity scalar. Participants reported their time estimation in terms of actual time units. Existing perception experiments have largely required participants to listen to a pulse and, via interruption of a secondary pulse, approximate the duration of the first. This has often resulted in underestimations, where some research posits this as a methodological limitation rather than reliable function of human time perception (Riemer et al., 2012). Still, it is difficult to determine the full extent to which study measures of time estimation captured a stable and valid depiction of participant-unique psychophysical experiences. It is possible that our measures of time estimation did not fully capture the complexity of these constructs (and that the hyperboloid s may offer a better, despite uncorrelated, account of this complexity). Our measure of magic-eye time estimation is, to our knowledge, the first iteration of this task used in research and therefore may require further validation. Replication is needed to better establish the robustness of these methods and resulting data. This research also examined the potential value of discounting s as generated by two popular discounting models (i.e., Myerson and Green, 1995; Rachlin, 2006). Finally, because the scope of this experiment was limited to the delay discounting framework, our data do not offer an account of s as a scalar of subjective probability perception. Extension of this focus to probability discounting should yield a broader understanding of subject-unique discrepancies in discounting rate across the discount frameworks (see McKerchar et al., 2010).

Conclusions

Hyperboloid discounting models include an added s parameter that improves model goodness-of-fit but is difficult to conceptually reconcile. This study did not support the role of s as a psychophysical scalar of delay sensitivity as measured in this sample. Instead, data infer that income should be considered when examining time estimation and age should be considered when examining temporal distance estimation. More importantly, delay framing as days versus dates can have a notable influence over how participants perceive time and delays, warranting greater attention as a focus of discounting research.
