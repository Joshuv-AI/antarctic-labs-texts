Prevalence and patterns of congenital tooth agenesis in children: a mixed-method study on parental awareness
Authors: Merve Bilmez Selen, Nisanur Müdüroğlu, Elif Begüm Yiğit Çetiner, Şeyma Gögce, Betül Kuru
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital tooth agenesis is one of the most common developmental dental anomalies in children and may lead to functional, esthetic, and psychosocial consequences if not diagnosed and managed early. While its prevalence has been widely investigated, data regarding parental awareness and communication following diagnosis remain limited.MethodsThis mixed-method study included a retrospective radiographic evaluation and a prospective telephone survey. Panoramic radiographs of 3,550 children aged 7–15 years who attended a university pediatric dentistry clinic between 2020 and 2025 were retrospectively assessed to determine the prevalence and patterns of permanent tooth agenesis, excluding third molars. Children diagnosed with agenesis were subsequently included in a prospective telephone survey to evaluate parental awareness and prior clinical communication. Descriptive statistics and appropriate non-parametric tests were used for data analysis.ResultsPermanent tooth agenesis was identified in 155 children, resulting in an overall prevalence of 4.4%. Hypodontia accounted for 4.2% of cases, while oligodontia was observed in 0.2%. The mandibular second premolars were the most frequently missing teeth, followed by the maxillary lateral incisors. Agenesis was significantly more common in the mandible than in the maxilla (p = 0.009). Although no significant sex difference was observed in overall agenesis prevalence, maxillary lateral incisor agenesis was significantly more frequent in females (unadjusted OR = 7.5; 95% CI: 3.61–15.57; p < 0.001). Among the 127 families who participated in the telephone survey, 63% reported having been previously informed about their child’s condition; however, many described the information as insufficient. After receiving additional explanation during the survey, 83.5% of families requested further clinical evaluation or follow-up.ConclusionsThe prevalence and distribution patterns of tooth agenesis observed in this pediatric population are consistent with existing literature. However, parental awareness regarding the diagnosis remains suboptimal. Background

The development of human dentition is a complex biological process shaped by the interaction of genetic, epigenetic, and environmental factors [1]. Disruptions in this process can lead to anomalies in tooth shape, size, position, or number [2]. Among these anomalies, congenital tooth agenesis is one of the most frequently encountered conditions and is characterized by the developmental absence of one or more permanent teeth [3].

The etiology of tooth agenesis involves multiple factors, including genetic mutations, abnormalities in dental lamina development, mesenchymal tissue deficiencies, local trauma, infections, radiation exposure, and hormonal imbalances [4]. It may also occur in association with certain syndromes, such as ectodermal dysplasia, Down syndrome, and cleft lip and palate. However, most cases are non-syndromic, with commonly reported patterns including the absence of maxillary lateral incisors and mandibular second premolars [4].

Tooth agenesis can adversely affect oral health and quality of life due to its functional, esthetic, and psychological consequences. It is frequently associated with other dental anomalies, including malocclusion, arch discrepancies, impaired alveolar bone development, eruption disturbances, microdontia, and impacted teeth [5]. These alterations may increase the need for long-term orthodontic and prosthetic treatment, particularly in growing individuals [6].

The reported prevalence of congenital tooth agenesis varies according to ethnicity, age range, and diagnostic methods, with global estimates ranging from 2.7% to 14.7% [1, 7–15]. Studies conducted in Türkiye have shown similar prevalence patterns, with mandibular second premolars and maxillary lateral incisors being the most frequently affected teeth. However, despite the extensive body of research on prevalence and distribution, there is limited evidence regarding parental awareness of tooth agenesis and the effectiveness of clinician–parent communication following diagnosis. Insufficient awareness may lead to delayed diagnosis and intervention, potentially increasing the complexity of treatment in later stages. Therefore, the present study aimed not only to determine the prevalence and distribution patterns of congenital tooth agenesis in children aged 7–15 years using panoramic radiographs, but also to evaluate parental awareness and the adequacy of clinical communication following diagnosis. By integrating epidemiological findings with a structured assessment of parental awareness, this study seeks to contribute to improved early diagnosis, patient education, and long-term management in pediatric dental practice.

Methods

Study design

This study was designed as a mixed-method investigation comprising a retrospective radiographic analysis and a prospective telephone survey. The retrospective component aimed to determine the prevalence and distribution patterns of permanent tooth agenesis, while the prospective component focused on evaluating parental awareness and prior clinical communication following diagnosis.

Study population and sample selection

The retrospective phase included pediatric patients aged 7–15 years who attended the Department of Pediatric Dentistry, Faculty of Dentistry, İnönü University, between January 2020 and December 2025. Digital patient records and panoramic radiographs were reviewed. In cases where multiple records were available for the same patient, only the most recent and complete dataset was included. A total of 3,550 children with evaluable panoramic radiographs met the inclusion criteria and were included in the retrospective analysis.

Radiographic assessment and diagnostic criteria

Panoramic radiographs were evaluated by a single pediatric dentist with more than five years of clinical and academic experience. A permanent tooth was considered congenitally missing if no evidence of tooth germ formation was observed on the panoramic radiograph at an age when mineralization would be expected. Tooth identification was based on the FDI two-digit notation system, and third molars were excluded from the analysis. Based on the number of missing permanent teeth, agenesis was classified as hypodontia (1–5 missing teeth) or oligodontia (six or more missing teeth). All radiographic evaluations were performed under standardized viewing conditions by the same experienced pediatric dentist to enhance intra-examiner consistency.

Prospective phase: telephone survey and participant flow

Children diagnosed with congenital tooth agenesis during the retrospective phase were eligible for inclusion in the prospective telephone survey. Families were contacted by telephone to assess parental awareness of the diagnosis, prior information provided by clinicians, and subsequent referral or follow-up status. Of the 155 children diagnosed with tooth agenesis, families of 127 children successfully participated in the telephone survey. Eighteen families could not be reached despite repeated contact attempts, six declined participation, and four were excluded due to incomplete contact information. Verbal informed consent was obtained from all participating parents prior to the interview.

The structured questionnaire assessed parental awareness of the diagnosis, the healthcare professional who initially provided the information, the perceived adequacy of the explanation, family history of tooth agenesis, perceived functional, esthetic, or psychological impact on the child, and interest in further clinical evaluation or follow-up. The questionnaire was specifically developed by the authors for this study and was not derived from a previously published instrument. An English-language version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1. Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was reviewed by experienced pediatric dentists to ensure clarity and content relevance. A pilot evaluation was conducted with a small number of parents to assess the comprehensibility of the questions, and minor revisions were made accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic and clinical characteristics. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations or medians and ranges, as appropriate. Associations between categorical variables were evaluated using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Paired categorical comparisons were assessed using McNemar’s test, and Cramér’s V coefficient was calculated to determine the strength of associations. Unadjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess the association between sex and maxillary lateral incisor agenesis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This study contributes to the existing literature by simultaneously evaluating the prevalence, tooth- and jaw-specific distribution of permanent tooth agenesis, and parental awareness in a pediatric population. The findings are generally consistent with both national and international studies and provide important clinical and communicative implications.

The prevalence of permanent tooth agenesis has been reported over a wide range in the literature, depending on ethnicity, age group, and diagnostic methodology [1, 7–15]. Systematic reviews indicate that hypodontia prevalence in most populations is concentrated between 3% and 7% [10, 11]. Studies conducted in Türkiye largely fall within this range [3, 13, 16–19]. The prevalence observed in the present study is consistent with previously reported national data and supports the classification of Türkiye among countries with moderate prevalence.

Tooth-specific analysis revealed that the mandibular second premolars and maxillary lateral incisors were the most frequently missing teeth. This distribution pattern has been consistently reported across different populations [10–14]. The later mineralization period of mandibular second premolars has been suggested to increase their susceptibility to developmental disturbances [12, 14]. However, as the present study does not allow causal inference, this finding should be interpreted as an observation consistent with previously proposed developmental hypotheses.

In terms of jaw distribution, tooth agenesis was observed more frequently in the mandible than in the maxilla. While this finding aligns with several studies conducted in Türkiye and other regions [10, 12, 16, 18], higher rates in the maxilla have been reported elsewhere [20–22]. Such discrepancies may be attributed to differences in sample characteristics, age ranges, clinical referral patterns, and diagnostic criteria. Similar variations in jaw-specific distribution have been reported in different populations, with some studies indicating a higher prevalence in the mandible, while others have reported maxillary predominance [23–26]. These discrepancies may be influenced by population characteristics, age distribution, diagnostic thresholds, and referral patterns, highlighting the importance of population-specific evaluations when interpreting jaw-related findings.

Although no significant sex difference was observed in overall agenesis prevalence, maxillary lateral incisor agenesis was notably more frequent in females. Previous studies have reported a higher prevalence of hypodontia in females [10, 11]. This tooth-specific sex difference has been explained by Butler’s morphogenetic field theory, which suggests that teeth located at the terminal ends of the dental arch are developmentally more vulnerable [12]. The observed disparity is likely the result of a complex interaction of genetic, epigenetic, and hormonal factors [27].

One of the distinctive aspects of this study is the evaluation of parental awareness in addition to radiographic findings. The telephone survey revealed that a considerable proportion of parents were either unaware of their child’s diagnosis or perceived prior clinical information as insufficient. Notably, following structured explanation, most families requested further clinical evaluation, underscoring the critical role of effective communication in treatment engagement. Previous studies have emphasized that delayed diagnosis or insufficient communication regarding dental developmental anomalies may adversely affect treatment planning and long-term outcomes, underscoring the need for improved clinician–parent communication strategies [28]. These findings highlight the clinical importance of effective clinician–parent communication, as improved awareness may facilitate earlier diagnosis, timely referral, and more appropriate long-term treatment planning in pediatric patients with tooth agenesis.

These findings emphasize that the management of tooth agenesis should not be limited to radiographic diagnosis alone but should also include clear and structured communication with families. In pediatric patients, early information and appropriate referral are essential for optimizing long-term orthodontic, restorative, and psychosocial outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. As this was a single-center, clinic-based study, the findings may not fully represent the general pediatric population. Additionally, the use of panoramic radiographs and assessment by a single examiner may increase the risk of misclassification, particularly in younger children. Nevertheless, all evaluations were performed by an experienced pediatric dentist, which supports diagnostic consistency. Furthermore, although the questionnaire was reviewed by experienced pediatric dentists and pilot-tested for clarity, it was not a formally validated instrument, which may limit reproducibility. Future multicenter and prospective studies incorporating socioeconomic and environmental variables would provide a more comprehensive understanding of tooth agenesis.
