Exploratory clinical trial on the acoustic variability index for predicting fluid responsiveness during hepatectomy
Authors: Woo-Young Seo, Hye-Mee Kwon, Baehun Moon, Hyun-Seok Kim, Kyu-Min Kang, Chang-Hoe Heo, Gil-Chun Park, Ji-Jung Park, Sung-Hoon Kim, In-Gu Jun, Jun-Gol Song, Gyu-Sam Hwang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The acoustic variability index (AVI), derived from continuous phonocardiographic analysis of heart sounds via an esophageal stethoscope, is a novel minimally invasive method for intraoperative hemodynamic monitoring. We evaluated the ability of AVI to predict fluid responsiveness in patients undergoing hepatectomy and compared its performance with that of the conventional indices. Forty adult patients who underwent hepatectomy were enrolled in this prospective, single-center observational study. After major surgical resection and stabilization, a 500 mL crystalloid fluid challenge was administered. Hemodynamic parameters including AVI, central venous pressure (CVP), stroke volume variation (SVV), and pulse pressure variation (PPV) were recorded before and after volume expansion. Patients with ≥ 10% increase in cardiac output (CO) were defined as responders. Thirty-seven patients were included in the final analysis, of which 12 (32.4%) were classified as responders. After fluid loading, responders showed a significant decrease in AVI (11.4% ± 2.5% to 7.8% ± 2.9%, p = 0.004), while non-responders showed no significant change (7.1% ± 3.2% to 6.3% ± 3.0%, p = 0.356). AVI demonstrated predictive performance comparable to SVV and PPV, with an optimal cutoff of > 9.8% and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.743–0.967). Real-time intraoperative monitoring of the AVI shows potential as a predictor for fluid responsiveness in patients undergoing hepatectomy. AVI offers a promising, minimally invasive approach for guiding fluid therapy. Further research is warranted to validate its utility in broader surgical populations.

Introduction

Perioperative fluid management plays a pivotal role in optimizing cardiac output and ensuring adequate tissue perfusion during major abdominal surgery [1, 2]. Dynamic parameters, such as stroke volume variation (SVV) and pulse pressure variation (PPV), have been widely adopted to guide fluid administration [3–5], but they require invasive arterial catheterization and may be affected by arrhythmias and altered thoracoabdominal mechanics.

Fluid responsiveness is commonly defined as a clinically significant increase in cardiac output following fluid administration [6]. Because fluid loading improves cardiac output only in preload-responsive states, indiscriminate volume expansion may fail to enhance perfusion and may even cause harm, highlighting the importance of accurately predicting fluid responsiveness [7, 8]. Goal-directed fluid therapy (GDFT), which tailors fluid administration based on predicted responsiveness, has been shown to reduce postoperative complications and improve surgical outcomes in major surgery [1, 3, 9]. Recent consensus recommendations and meta-analyses further support the benefits of intraoperative GDFT [10, 11]. In this context, dynamic indices such as stroke volume variation and pulse pressure variation are widely used but have important physiological and technical limitations, creating a need for reliable and less invasive alternatives for assessing fluid responsiveness [12, 13].

Heart sounds (first heart sound S1 and second heart sound S2) generated by valve closure events during the cardiac cycle contain information related to myocardial contractility, afterload, and volume status. Unlike peripheral pulse-based indices, heart sound analysis directly reflects central cardiac mechanics and is less affected by peripheral vascular conditions, potentially offering more consistent performance across diverse perioperative scenarios [14–16]. However, the clinical application of heart sound-based monitoring has been limited by the lack of reliable real-time acquisition and processing systems capable of providing continuous, quantitative indices for intraoperative decision-making.

To address these challenges, we utilized a high-fidelity esophageal stethoscope system with specialized signal processing algorithms that continuously analyze heart sounds. The Acoustic Variability Index (AVI) represents an approach that quantifies the respiratory cycle-induced variability in heart sound characteristics, specifically the amplitude variations of S1 and S2 sounds and changes in systolic time intervals, to provide a minimally invasive assessment of preload responsiveness. In this exploratory study, we aimed to assess the clinical utility of AVI in predicting fluid responsiveness during hepatectomy. By comparing AVI performance with conventional hemodynamic parameters (SVV, PPV, and CVP), we evaluated whether AVI could serve as a minimally invasive surrogate for guiding intraoperative fluid therapy.

This study adheres to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) reporting guidelines.

Method

Study design and participants

This single-center, prospective, observational exploratory study was conducted between 8 February 2024 and 30 May 2024. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) and registered with the Clinical Research Information Service (CRIS), Republic of Korea. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

We enrolled adult patients aged 19 years and older undergoing elective open abdominal hepatectomy under general anesthesia with an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of 1 or 2. The exclusion criteria included: known arrhythmias, esophageal varices or strictures, pulmonary edema, valvular or structural heart disease, body mass index (BMI) > 30 kg/m2, or anticipated difficulty with arterial or central venous catheter placement. Patients were also excluded if massive bleeding (> 500 mL) occurred before fluid challenge initiation.

Anesthesia and hemodynamic monitoring

Standard anesthetic monitoring included electrocardiogram (ECG), pulse oximetry (SpO₂), and invasive arterial blood pressure monitoring. Anesthesia was induced using propofol (1.5–2.5 mg/kg), rocuronium (0.6–1.2 mg/kg), and remifentanil (0.05–0.2 µg/kg/min). Maintenance was achieved with sevoflurane or desflurane in oxygen/air mixtures. Patients were mechanically ventilated with tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 0–5 cmH₂O, maintaining end-tidal CO₂ between 35 and 42 mmHg.

Arterial and central venous lines were inserted for invasive pressure monitoring. Cardiac output (CO) and stroke volume variation (SVV) were measured using the FloTrac™/EV1000™ hemodynamic monitoring system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA). Depth of anesthesia was monitored using the Root platform (Masimo, Irvine, CA, USA) and adjusted based on vital signs and anesthetic depth indices.

Acoustic monitoring and AVI acquisition

An 18 Fr esophageal stethoscope equipped with a high-fidelity microphone was inserted and positioned approximately 28–32 cm from the upper incisors, which represents the optimal position for cardiopulmonary sound acquisition, to capture optimal heart sound signals [17, 18]. Real-time phonocardiographic data were analyzed using the proprietary mobile application SignalTAB™ (Signal House Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) [19], which synchronously recorded biometric data and calculated the Acoustic Variability Index (AVI). A schematic of the esophageal stethoscope system and real-time signal processing steps is illustrated in Fig. 1.

The AVI reflects the variability of heart sound parameters extracted from cardiopulmonary audio signals. The system separates heart sounds from respiratory sounds within the recorded cardiopulmonary audio, then analyzes heart sound data including S1 and S2 amplitude and S1-S2 intervals. The AVI quantifies the respiratory cycle-related variability in both the amplitude of the S1 and S2 heart sounds and the systolic time interval, defined as the time duration between the S1 and S2 heart sound peaks. AVI values were calculated every 5 s based on the variability of these heart sound parameters from the preceding respiratory cycle. The signal quality was ensured via built-in automated quality indices that rejected segments contaminated by electrocautery or artifact noise. In addition, the SignalTAB™ system applied real-time signal processing techniques, including band-pass filtering, envelope extraction, and time-amplitude thresholding, to eliminate intraoperative noise and isolate valid heart sound segments. These pre-processing steps allowed for stable S1–S2 detection and robust AVI calculation, even in the presence of electrocautery interference or ventilator sounds.

Fluid challenge protocol

Following major resection (after hepatectomy and hepatic vein control), and once patients were confirmed to be hemodynamically stable (no active bleeding > 200 mL and stable mean arterial pressure (MAP) for > 5 min), a standardized fluid challenge was administered: 500 mL of crystalloid infused over 10–30 min via central venous line. To ensure the validity of the uncalibrated pulse contour analysis, during the fluid challenge and data recording periods, no vasoactive agents or epidural analgesia were administered to exclude the confounding effects of pharmacological vasomotor tone alteration.

Baseline values for AVI, CO, CVP, SVV, PPV, MAP, heart rate (HR), and SV were defined as the 3-minute averaged data obtained during a stable hemodynamic state after anesthesia induction but before surgical incision. Subsequently, measurements were recorded at two timepoints: pre-fluid challenge (a 3-minute segment immediately before fluid loading) and post-fluid challenge (a 3-minute segment after infusion completion). All parameters were averaged over each 3-minute recording period for analysis.

A patient was classified as a responder if CO increased by ≥ 10% from baseline, based on previous definitions of fluid responsiveness in perioperative settings [6, 7].

Complication assessment

Any complications related to the esophageal stethoscope (e.g., sore throat and mucosal injury) were monitored daily by a dedicated research nurse until discharge. Pulmonary edema was ruled out using chest X-rays when clinically indicated.

Statistical analysis

Prior to statistical analysis, data segments with persistent noise, dislodged sensors, or signal dropout were excluded. Data from the acoustic recorder and commercial monitoring devices were synchronized based on recorded timestamps. To ensure data reliability and minimize the impact of any potential minor temporal offsets, all analyses utilized 3-minute averaged data obtained during hemodynamically stable periods.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Between-group comparisons were performed using paired or unpaired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Categorical data were analyzed using the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. The diagnostic performance of AVI, SVV, PPV, and CVP were evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The optimal AVI threshold was determined using the Youden index, and comparative AUCs were analyzed using DeLong’s test. To further evaluate the clinical utility of the indices, a grey zone approach was applied [20]. The grey zone was defined as the range of values between a cutoff yielding a sensitivity of > 90% (rule-out) and a cutoff yielding a specificity of > 90% (rule-in). This study was designed as an exploratory investigation to generate preliminary data for a novel and previously unvalidated monitoring technique. Given the absence of prior clinical data on the AVI performance, formal power calculations were not feasible. The sample size of 40 patients was determined based on practical considerations, consistent with typical sample sizes reported for feasibility and early exploratory clinical studies [21]. This sample size was deemed adequate to provide initial evidence of AVI’s clinical utility while acknowledging that larger, adequately powered validation studies would be required to establish definitive clinical efficacy and support regulatory approval for clinical use. All data analyses were performed using Python (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) with relevant statistical packages including SciPy, NumPy, and scikit-learn. A P value was considered statistically significant when it was < 0.05.

Results

Patient inclusion and baseline characteristics

Among the 40 patients screened for this study, three were excluded: two based on intraoperative decisions that precluded fluid challenge, and one due to poor signal quality from esophageal heart sound recordings. A total of 37 patients were included in the final analysis. Based on the cardiac output response, 12 (32.4%) patients were classified as responders and 25 (67.6%) as non-responders. In terms of baseline comparisons, static parameters including MAP, HR, and CVP showed no significant differences between the two groups. Conversely, baseline values for all dynamic preload indices (AVI, SVV, and PPV) were significantly higher in the responder group compared to the non-responder group.

Diagnostic Performance Analysis

The diagnostic performance of AVI, SVV, PPV, and CVP in predicting fluid responsiveness is summarized in Table 3 and illustrated in Fig. 3. The AVI demonstrated an AUC of 0.873 (95% CI: 0.743–0.967) with an optimal cutoff value of > 9.8%, determined by the Youden index. This performance was statistically comparable to that of SVV (AUC = 0.848) and PPV (AUC = 0.805), as confirmed by DeLong’s test (Table 4; P > 0.05). In contrast, CVP exhibited poor predictive value with an AUC of 0.462.

To further assess the clinical utility of the parameters, a grey zone analysis was performed using a two-cutoff method, as detailed in Table 5. The grey zone for AVI was determined to be between 8.9% and 11.6%, comprising 24.3% (n = 9) of the study population. This proportion was lower than that of SVV (27.0%) and PPV (40.5%), indicating that AVI provided conclusive diagnostic information for the largest proportion of patients (75.7%) among the dynamic indices. In contrast, CVP showed a broad grey zone including 86.5% of patients, confirming its limited diagnostic utility.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that the AVI, a novel heart sound-based parameter derived from real-time esophageal monitoring, can effectively predict intraoperative fluid responsiveness. AVI showed comparable predictive performance to established dynamic parameters (SVV and PPV), with an optimal cutoff > 9.8% and an AUC of 0.873. DeLong’s test indicated no significant differences between AVI and SVV or PPV, suggesting that AVI provides comparable discriminatory ability while offering the potential benefit of minimally invasive acquisition. Notably, the grey zone analysis further supported the clinical utility of AVI. AVI demonstrated a narrower grey zone compared to SVV and particularly PPV, indicating that it provided conclusive diagnostic information for the largest proportion of patients among the dynamic indices. All dynamic parameters significantly outperformed the static parameter CVP (AUC = 0.462), reinforcing the superiority of dynamic indices for fluid responsiveness prediction and confirming the findings from previous studies [22–24]. Consistent with these statistical findings, AVI values significantly decreased after fluid administration in responders but not in non-responders, suggesting that the index is sensitive to changes in preload conditions. These findings imply that AVI, a minimally invasive acoustic measure, can detect physiological responses to volume loading in a manner consistent with validated invasive measures.

The use of esophageal stethoscopes for intraoperative monitoring has a long clinical history dating back to the 1950 s [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of heart sound analysis for hemodynamic monitoring, showing correlations between heart sound characteristics and cardiovascular parameters including myocardial contractility, afterload, and fluid responsiveness [18, 26–33]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that heart sound-derived indices showed significant correlations (r = 0.687–0.721) with stroke volume variation (SVV) throughout extended surgical procedures in liver transplant recipients, with continuous hourly data analysis confirming reliable associations across multiple surgical phases, suggesting their potential as minimally invasive hemodynamic surrogates [34].

Building on these findings, the mechanistic basis for the AVI performance can be understood by examining its fundamental differences from conventional dynamic parameters. Unlike SVV and PPV, which are influenced by ventilatory patterns, arterial compliance, and require arterial cannulation, AVI reflects central cardiac mechanical changes through acoustic characteristics. By analyzing the beat-to-beat variability of heart sound amplitude and timing—particularly the S1-S2 interval—AVI captures hemodynamic shifts in preload with minimal reliance on peripheral vascular signals. This feature offers a unique advantage in surgical settings in which peripheral perfusion is compromised or invasive monitoring is impractical.

In terms of clinical applicability, AVI has several favorable characteristics and important practical considerations that must be addressed. As it utilizes an esophageal stethoscope, it can be integrated into routine anesthetic monitoring without the need for arterial line placement, which may be beneficial in low- to intermediate-risk surgeries where invasive access is either unnecessary or undesirable. Dynamic preload indices such as SVV and PPV have been widely adopted in perioperative settings [35] and have been shown to reduce postoperative morbidity when used as part of GDFT [1, 9]. However, their applicability is limited by various factors including mechanical ventilation requirements, cardiac rhythm, and thoracic or abdominal pressure changes, especially in the presence of low tidal volume or spontaneous breathing efforts [36, 37]. Moreover, their use requires arterial catheterization, which may be impractical in certain patient populations. AVI offers a potential advantage by avoiding peripheral vascular dependency and invasive access, while maintaining reliable predictive performance. However, several technical limitations must be considered, and its application may be limited in patients with esophageal disorders. Its feasibility in awake or non-intubated patients remains uncertain, consistent signal acquisition requires proper probe positioning and stable contact, and signal quality may be degraded by intraoperative noise or surgical manipulation.

This study had several important limitations that warrant consideration. First, this was a single-center exploratory study with a relatively small sample size (n = 37) and a limited number of responders (32.4%, n = 12), which resulted in wide confidence intervals for the diagnostic performance metrics (Table 3). Although the lower bounds of the confidence intervals for AVI still indicated clinically meaningful predictive performance, the study may have been underpowered to detect subtle differences between indices, and larger cohort studies will be required to narrow these intervals and confirm the generalizability of our findings. Second, AVI was measured only during predefined, hemodynamically stable periods, which may have overestimated its performance by excluding more dynamic or unstable intraoperative conditions. Third, cardiac output in this study was estimated using a non-calibrated pulse contour method, which may not precisely reflect absolute CO changes. This technique is known to be less accurate under hemodynamically unstable conditions [38–40]. Nevertheless, this limitation is mitigated by several factors. The variability associated with uncalibrated CO estimations was minimized by performing fluid challenge during hemodynamically stable periods after hepatic resection [41]. Supporting this, previous studies have demonstrated that uncalibrated pulse contour methods can be clinically useful and widely adopted for guiding fluid therapy in perioperative settings when applied under appropriate conditions [42, 43]. Furthermore, the AUC values of SVV and PPV in our study closely matched those reported in previous literature, lending additional support to the validity of our approach [37, 44]. Fourth, the study population consisted primarily of healthy liver donors undergoing standardized open hepatectomy procedures, which limits the generalizability of our findings to patients with significant comorbidities, hemodynamic instability, or those undergoing other types of surgery. Future studies should include larger and more heterogeneous cohorts to confirm the utility of AVI across broader clinical contexts.

In conclusion, this exploratory study suggests that AVI measured using an esophageal stethoscope may offer a potential minimally invasive approach for assessing fluid responsiveness during surgery. Its comparable performance to existing dynamic indices, along with its ease of acquisition and integration, suggests that AVI may enhance intraoperative hemodynamic management and improve patient outcomes. Because this study was conducted under controlled intraoperative conditions, including a standardized fluid challenge and the absence of vasoactive agents, further comprehensive multicenter validation studies are needed before AVI can be considered for routine clinical use, to confirm its accuracy and applicability across a broader range of surgical populations and hemodynamic conditions.
