Letter to the Editor on Generative AI-Enhanced Low-Dose CBCT for Impacted Mandibular Third Molars
Authors: Chengrui Xu, Junfeng Xu
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

I read with great interest the article titled "Impact of Generative AI-Enhanced Low-Dose Cone-Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) on Diagnosis and Treatment Planning for Impacted Mandibular Third Molars" by Zhang et al,1 recently published in the International Dental Journal. The authors present a clinically relevant investigation into the application of generative artificial intelligence (Gen-AI) for enhancing low-dose CBCT images in the evaluation of impacted mandibular third molars (M3M). Below, I offer a brief appraisal of the study's strengths and suggestions for future research.

The study's major strengths include the use of paired standard-dose and low-dose CBCT scans from the same patients, which enhances clinical validity and enables supervised model training. The CycleGAN-based model demonstrated superior performance in preserving anatomical fidelity while improving image quality. The inclusion of subjective assessments by clinicians with varying expertise adds further depth, showing that AI-enhanced images significantly improved the visibility of critical structures without altering clinical decision-making—supporting the potential for safe radiation dose reduction.

Nevertheless, several aspects warrant further investigation:

First, the sample size is relatively modest, with 302 scans from 90 patients. While the use of paired data strengthens internal validity, the single-centre design may limit the generalisability to different imaging systems and populations. Future multicentre collaborations incorporating diverse devices and patient demographics would help validate the model's robustness.2

Second, the study focused exclusively on impacted M3Ms, a specific and well-defined indication. While this narrow focus allows for controlled evaluation, it limits applicability to other diagnostic tasks. Expanding the model's scope to include other anatomical structures or pathologies—such as root fractures, periapical lesions, or temporomandibular joint assessments—would be a valuable next step.

Third, potential risks of generative models were not fully explored. Although the CycleGAN model performed well, the study did not explore potential issues such as anatomical hallucination or over-smoothing of fine details.3,4 Future work incorporating failure case analysis and adversarial testing would help ensure clinical safety.

Finally, the lack of longitudinal or outcome-based validation is a limitation. While the study assessed diagnostic decisions, it did not track whether these decisions led to improved surgical outcomes or reduced complications. Integrating Gen-AI-enhanced imaging into prospective trials with clinical endpoints would provide stronger evidence of its clinical value.5

In summary, Zhang et al1 have made a valuable contribution to the field, demonstrating that CycleGAN-based enhancement of low-dose CBCT images can improve anatomical visibility without compromising clinical decisions. Their work provides a solid foundation for future studies aiming to expand the scope, improve generalisability, and validate long-term clinical impact.
