Learning curve in off-pump coronary artery bypass graft surgery in a low-income country: a single-center experience
Authors: Anh Tuan Vo, Nguyen Thoi Hai Nguyen, Tien Cong Nguyen, An Thai Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionOff-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) remains technically demanding, especially in low-income settings. Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) continues to be one of the leading causes of death worldwide [2], accounting for a substantial burden on both patients and healthcare systems. As the global population ages and the prevalence of atherosclerosis-related conditions increases, effective long-term management strategies are becoming crucial. Although percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) has revolutionized the treatment of CAD by offering a minimally invasive alternative, it has not supplanted the role of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [6] in managing complex and multivessel disease.

CABG remains the gold standard for patients with left main coronary artery stenosis, three-vessel disease, or diabetes mellitus with diffuse lesions, based on the superior long-term outcomes demonstrated in landmark trials such as SYNTAX and FREEDOM [7]. Despite advances in surgical techniques, anesthetic protocols, and perioperative care, CABG remains a technically demanding procedure with a significant learning curve for surgeons. Mastery of the operation demands precision in vessel handling, graft configuration, and teamwork, all under time constraints. Among its various techniques, off-pump CABG offers benefits such as reduced inflammatory response, lower blood transfusion requirements, and faster recovery, but requires distinct technical skills and careful patient selection.

Cumulative Sum (CUSUM) analysis provides a robust statistical method for monitoring outcomes over time. Originating from industrial quality control, CUSUM tracks deviations from a predefined target performance and is sensitive to even minor shifts [2–4]. In the context of cardiac surgery, CUSUM allows continuous, case-by-case monitoring of outcomes, helping to pinpoint when a team or surgeon achieves consistent proficiency. Unlike binary outcome metrics, CUSUM reflects the dynamics of performance evolution, making it particularly suitable for learning curve analyses.

Several studies have successfully applied CUSUM to cardiac and thoracic surgery [9]. For instance, Holzhey et al. used this technique to evaluate robotic CABG performance [1], whereas others have extended it to valve surgery, minimally invasive procedures, and pediatric cardiac operations. Despite the increasing popularity of CUSUM in surgical education, few studies have applied it to conventional CABG in low-to-middle income countries [5]. Understanding the learning curve in these settings is essential, as resource constraints and patient complexity can affect proficiency acquisition.

This study aimed to fill this gap by evaluating the learning curve for CABG using the CUSUM approach in a Vietnamese tertiary care hospital. We analyzed key indicators, including operation time, number of anastomoses, ventilation duration, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and overall postoperative hospitalization. Our objective was to identify a performance transition point, describe its impact on perioperative outcomes, and discuss the implications for training, practice, and quality improvement in cardiac surgery.

Methods

Evaluating surgical performance requires a structured methodology that accounts for clinical complexity and temporal progression. This section outlines the retrospective study design, surgical and data collection protocols, and statistical methodology, including the application of cumulative sum (CUSUM) analysis.

Study design and patient selection

This was a retrospective cohort study analyzing 49 consecutive patients who underwent isolated coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) at Dong Nai General Hospital, a tertiary hospital in South Vietnam, between 1/2022 and 12/2024. The inclusion criteria were adult patients with confirmed multivessel coronary artery disease who underwent primary isolated off-pump CABG. The exclusion criteria included concomitant valvular procedures, reoperations, and emergency surgeries. All operations were performed by a consistent surgical team comprising one lead surgeon and supporting staff with variable levels of experience.

Institutional ethical approval was obtained for the study (Approval No. 16/CN-HDDD, Dong Nai General Hospital), and all patient data were anonymized prior to the analysis. This study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects.

Surgical technique

All patients underwent conventional off-pump coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) via the median sternotomy approach. The left internal mammary artery (LIMA) was harvested in a skeletonized manner and anastomosed to the left anterior descending artery (LAD) in all the cases. Additional grafts, including the right internal mammary artery (RIMA) and saphenous vein grafts (SVG), were employed as necessary based on preoperative angiographic assessments and intraoperative findings. The Y-graft configuration was the preferred technique when RIMA was used.

The heart was exposed using pericardial traction sutures without apical suction-positioning devices. A coronary stabilizer was used in all cases to immobilize the target vessel during the distal anastomosis. To ensure a bloodless and safe anastomotic field, intracoronary shunts were routinely inserted.

Distal anastomoses were performed using a single-needle technique, with the suture passed from inside the coronary intima to the outside to minimize intimal injury. Anastomotic targets were selected based on intraoperative findings, with a preference for noncalcified, nonatherosclerotic segments to optimize long-term graft patency.

When SVGs were used, proximal anastomoses were performed on the ascending aorta using a side-biting clamp. Epiaortic ultrasound was used prior to clamping to avoid manipulation in areas with calcifications or atheromatous plaques.

All anastomoses were completed without cardiopulmonary bypass. Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout the procedure with the assistance of inotropes as needed and by avoiding excessive cardiac manipulation during graft construction.

Surgeon Experience.

All procedures in this study were performed by a single cardiac surgeon who had previously completed over 200 cases of valvular surgery and 50 cases of on-pump CABG. To gain further expertise in coronary surgery, the surgeon underwent specialized training in coronary artery bypass grafting at a high-volume cardiac center in Ho Chi Minh City. As part of this training, the surgeon was mentored through 20 off-pump CABG procedures before performing surgeries independently.

Data were collected retrospectively from institutional medical records and operative reports. The collected variables included patient demographics, comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes, and chronic kidney disease), and intraoperative parameters. For the CUSUM analysis, a “complication” was defined as the occurrence of any of the following events: early mortality (in-hospital death), postoperative myocardial infarction, reoperation for any reason (e.g., bleeding or graft-related issues), sternal wound infection, or conversion to on-pump CABG. Each of these events was treated as a binary outcome (1 = complication, 0 = no complication), contributing to the upward or downward deviation of the CUSUM curve.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated using R (version 4.4.3). Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation and were compared using independent sample t-tests. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test, where appropriate. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

The CUSUM analysis was conducted using a binary outcome framework. The acceptable complication rate (p0) was defined as 10%, and the unacceptable rate (p1) was defined as 20%. The decision interval (h) and slope (s) were calculated based on these parameters. A linear decision boundary was drawn to establish the upper and lower control limits.

Upper Control Limit (UCL) = s × n + offset/2.

Upper Control Limit (UCL) = s × n + offset/2.

Lower Control Limit (LCL) = s × n - offset/2.

Lower Control Limit (LCL) = s × n - offset/2.

The cumulative sum was calculated as follows:

CUSUM(n) = ∑ (xi - p0), where xi is the binary complication outcome for case i.

The transition point was defined as the case in which the CUSUM curve crossed below the LCL, indicating a significant improvement beyond the expected performance.

To evaluate trends in continuous variables, we computed the average values before and after the transition case. Changes were assessed using unpaired t-tests, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Results

Patient demographics

The study comprised 49 patients who underwent off-pump CABG, with a mean age of 61.19 9.62. Males constituted 77.55% (n = 38) and females 22.45% (n = 11). Hypertension was present in 93.88% (n = 46), diabetes mellitus type 2 in 42.86% (n = 21), and smoking history in 40.82% (n = 20) of the patients. Chronic kidney disease was defined as stage IIIA or higher (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²), and affected 14.29% (n = 7) of patients, including both dialysis and non-dialysis patients. NYHA Class II (53.06%) slightly exceeded Class I (46.94%), and atrial fibrillation was uncommon (4.08%). The mean preoperative ejection fraction was 54.65%.

The mean operation time before Case 28 was 256.1 min, which decreased significantly to 180.7 min after Case 28 (p < 0.0001). This suggests improved intraoperative efficiency, likely due to increased proficiency in the anastomosis technique and team coordination.

The number of coronary anastomoses remained stable (2.86 vs. 2.93, p = 0.72), indicating that the improvement in operation time was not due to reduced surgical complexity.

ICU and postoperative times decreased significantly after case 28, whereas the complication rate did not show a difference owing to the low number of cases.

Reoperation was performed in two patients (4.08%), all due to postoperative bleeding requiring surgical intervention. No reoperations were performed for graft failure or other complications.

Figure 2 illustrates the boxplot comparison of the operation time between the pre-transition and post-transition phases. A notable narrowing of the interquartile range and reduction in median values were observed post-transition.

Operation Time Boxplot

Ventilation time showed a significant decrease from 10.66 h pre-transition to 6.52 h post-transition (p = 0.03), suggesting better intraoperative hemostasis and optimized anesthesia protocols. The ICU stay decreased from approximately 1.76 days to 0.94 days, although this change did not reach statistical significance (p > 0.05), indicating variability likely influenced by patient-specific or systemic factors beyond the surgical procedure itself, reflecting improved perioperative care, extubation strategies, and fewer early complications. The postoperative hospital stay decreased from 12.44 to 9.24 days (p = 0.02), reinforcing the overall improvements in care continuity and discharge readiness.

It is important to note that the single case of mortality after the transition point was not due to technical failure or intraoperative complications. The patient had satisfactory postoperative myocardial function but succumbed to severe pneumonia after prolonged mechanical ventilation. The patient had a long smoking history and advanced COPD (stage D), which may have predisposed him to ventilator-associated complications. This case highlights the need for individualized perioperative respiratory strategies in vulnerable subgroups of patients.

Discussion

The application of CUSUM analysis in this study allowed for real-time tracking of surgical performance and provided a clear illustration of the learning curve associated with coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) at a Vietnamese tertiary hospital. Despite the modest sample size in this study, CUSUM analysis is well-suited for detecting performance shifts, even in small datasets, as it provides sequential monitoring that accumulates evidence over time. The sensitivity of CUSUM to minor shifts in outcomes is particularly valuable when evaluating learning curves in centers with limited patient volumes.

No statistically significant differences in baseline demographics or clinical comorbidities were observed between the pre- and post-transition cohorts (Table 3). This similarity in patient profiles reduced the likelihood of confounding due to selection bias. Furthermore, the stability in preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (54.25% vs. 55.14%, p = 0.584) between the two groups confirms that improved surgical outcomes post-transition were not attributable to inherently lower-risk patients but rather reflected genuine technical and procedural advancements by the surgical team. To further contextualize the findings, future studies should evaluate outcomes across diabetic versus non-diabetic subgroups or patients with/without chronic kidney disease to determine whether performance improvements confer uniform benefits.

The transition point identified in Case 28 represents a critical inflection in the surgical team’s development, marking the achievement of technical consistency and procedural efficiency. This threshold is consistent with international benchmarks, such as those described by Mohamed Ali et al. (approximately 25–30 cases) [10] and Caputo et al. (approximately 30 cases) [11], reinforcing the credibility and generalizability of our results within the context of off-pump CABG learning curves. Furthermore, the single-center, single-surgeon nature of our study ensured a high level of precision by minimizing confounding factors introduced by variations in surgical technique, team composition, or institutional protocols. This controlled environment allows for a clear assessment of the surgeon’s progression in terms of skill and procedural efficiency, providing robust evidence to guide surgical training programs in comparable resource-limited settings.

The Vietnamese healthcare landscape presents unique challenges in surgical training. While Vietnam has made substantial progress in developing cardiac surgery programs, access to specialized surgical education and standardized performance evaluation remains variable. Restricted access to simulation tools, operating room constraints, and inconsistent preoperative optimization contribute to a steep learning curve [1, 5]. This is further compounded by the fact that many Vietnamese tertiary hospitals also function as teaching institutions, where varying levels of experience among residents and assistants can affect intraoperative flow.

Despite these limitations, our findings demonstrate that improvements in surgical outcomes are possible in this context. Although the observed mortality rate (2.04%) appears elevated for a population with preserved ejection fraction (mean EF ≈ 55%), the cause of death was unrelated to intraoperative or graft-related issues. The patient had good myocardial recovery but developed severe hospital-acquired pneumonia after prolonged mechanical ventilation. Notably, the patient had advanced COPD (stage D) and a long history of smoking, both of which contributed to respiratory compromise.

The wound infection rate (6.12%) consisting of two superficial sternal infections that responded to standard wound care and antibiotics and one deep sternal wound infection requiring VAC therapy. These outcomes, while numerically significant, are likely influenced by the small sample size and may not reflect the rates in a larger series.

There were no documented cases of postoperative stroke, and no patients experienced permanently disabling neurological complications during the study period, further supporting the overall procedural safety of OPCAB in this cohort.

The significant decrease in operation time and ventilation duration post-transition suggests enhanced technical fluency, better procedural planning, and more effective team coordination. These improvements reflect not only the increasing technical skills of the attending surgeon but also institutional adaptation in anesthesia, nursing, and postoperative care protocols. As the surgical team progressed through the learning curve, shared experience and procedural familiarity among the staff likely contributed to the reduction in time-based metrics. Although the off-pump technique is advantageous in reducing perioperative complications, it presents a unique learning curve owing to its reliance on surgical precision without cardiopulmonary bypass support.

Interestingly, although ICU time did not reach statistical significance, the absolute reduction from approximately 1.76 to 0.94 days was clinically meaningful. Reducing ICU occupancy by nearly one day per patient translates into improved patient turnover, allowing for the earlier admission of new surgical or critical care cases, thereby maximizing bed availability and enhancing workflow efficiency. In our setting, ICU turnover is a key operational metric; the observed reduction suggests fewer ventilator-associated complications, lower nursing workload, and earlier initiation of postoperative mobilization in patients. Studies from other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) have shown that optimizing ICU throughput contributes to better resource allocation, reduced nosocomial infection rates, and overall cost savings [14]. These findings suggest that even modest improvements in ICU efficiency can have significant downstream effects on institutional capacity and patient outcomes in high-demand tertiary centers. Thus, while statistical significance may be absent, the clinical and operational implications of a reduced ICU burden remain compelling.

The shorter ICU duration also suggests increased surgeon confidence in early extubation and hemodynamic stabilization, likely stemming from the procedural mastery achieved after the learning transition. While external factors, such as ICU discharge policies and bed availability, may influence this metric, the downward trend reflects positively on perioperative management improvements that coincide with growing surgical experience. Nevertheless, the clear reduction in postoperative hospital stay indicates a tangible benefit to the hospital system and patient recovery timelines, potentially easing hospital burden and increasing patient turnover in high-demand settings.

The observed reduction in ICU stays, ventilation time, and improved anastomosis efficiency identified in this study are clinically meaningful. Prior research has demonstrated that optimizing these perioperative parameters correlates positively with enhanced long-term patient recovery, graft patency, and overall outcomes following coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG). Kieser et al. reported that decreased operative times and improved perioperative efficiency significantly enhanced graft patency and patient outcomes at long-term follow-up [12]. Additionally, shorter ventilation duration and ICU stay have been associated with reduced postoperative complications, faster patient mobilization, and improved functional recovery [13,14]. In our series, the observed postoperative 100% graft patency rate indirectly supports the anticipated favorable long-term graft outcomes. Future prospective studies with explicit long-term survival and graft patency assessments are warranted to confirm these benefits.

From a broader perspective, the implementation of CUSUM in this study highlights the adaptability of this method for evaluating complex surgical performance in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [4, 7, 8]. This approach requires only basic computational tools and outcome tracking, making it accessible even when resources are limited. Its graphical format also provides intuitive feedback, which can be instrumental for early career surgeons and training programs in the future. In addition to its educational value, CUSUM can support hospital quality assurance by detecting deviations from acceptable complication rates in real time, thereby allowing timely interventions.

This study adds to the growing body of literature validating the CUSUM as a valuable tool in global surgical education [3, 7, 8]. In countries such as Vietnam, where surgical capacity building is a national priority, CUSUM provides a low-cost, data-driven strategy for continuous performance assessment. By identifying specific transition points, CUSUM can inform surgical mentorship, optimize operative scheduling, and ensure timely feedback to residents and junior surgeons.

To optimize surgical proficiency before independent operations, institutions in low-resource settings should consider implementing structured mentorship programs and standardized training curricula. These approaches allow progressive skill acquisition, promote procedural consistency, and reduce early operative risks. Mentorship by experienced surgeons ensures real-time feedback and fosters technical refinement, whereas standardized curricula provide a uniform foundation of knowledge critical for safe practice. By integrating these strategies early in surgical training, institutions can shorten the learning curve, enhance intraoperative performance, and ultimately improve patient outcomes in settings with limited margins for error.

Limitations

This was a single-center retrospective study with a modest sample size. Our analysis did not adjust for patient risk factors that could influence outcomes. Larger prospective studies using risk-adjusted CUSUM techniques would provide more generalizable results.

Conclusion

This single-center study clearly demonstrates the learning curve associated with coronary artery bypass surgery. CUSUM analysis identified a clear transition point after 28 cases, associated with improved operation time and perioperative outcomes. This method offers an effective strategy for monitoring and enhancing surgical performance and should be considered a key component of surgical education and quality assurance.
