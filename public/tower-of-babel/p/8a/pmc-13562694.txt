Alternatively spliced killer–protector system confers S19-mediated hybrid male sterility in rice
Authors: Jintao Tang, Huina Wang, Yaoming Feng, Shunmei Li, Ruiying Liu, Zixu Zhang, Shaotong Chen, Shuangchun Li, Xianrong Xie, Heying Li, Zuofeng Zhu, Yao-Guang Liu, Yongyao Xie, Letian Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHybrid sterility limits the use of strong interspecific heterosis and S19 is a major locus that confers hybrid sterility between Oryza sativa (Asian cultivated rice) and Oryza glaberrima (African cultivated rice). However, the S19 is not yet cloned and its underlying mechanism remains elusive. In this study, we identify two closely linked genes (S19A1 and S19A7) specific to African rice allele that encode a killer–protector module at the S19 locus. Two alternatively spliced transcripts expressed from the killer gene S19A1 (S19A1.1 and S19A1.2) encode mitochondria-targeted cytotoxic proteins that cause toxicity diversity for somatic and/or gametic cell death, respectively. However, S19A7 interacts with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, blocking their cytotoxic effect. Because the Asian rice S19 allele lacks S19A1 and S19A7, male gametes that carry this allele are selectively aborted in Asian–African F1 hybrids. Knockout of S19A1 can overcome S19-mediated hybrid sterility. Haplotype analysis reveals that the functional S19 allele is absent in non-AA-genome Oryza species and likely emerged in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage through a multi-step evolutionary process. Our findings provide insight into the genetic mechanisms responsible for hybrid sterility between Asian and African rice and suggest genetic and biotechnological strategies for the use of interspecific heterosis in rice.Subject terms: Plant genetics, Plant genetics, Plant reproduction

Introduction

Alternative splicing is an important post-transcriptional regulatory mechanism in eukaryotes1. Genes undergoing alternative splicing can generate multiple protein isoforms, regulating protein abundance and/or increasing protein diversity from a limited set of genes. These protein isoforms generally have undergone functional divergence in properties, such as binding affinity, post-translational modification patterns, or protein stability. To date, accumulating evidence shows that alternative splicing modulates multiple developmental processes and responses to biotic and abiotic stresses1,2. Yet, how alternative splicing contributes to species formation and maintenance is largely unknown.

Reproductive isolation acts as a general barrier to gene flow between species or subspecies, driving speciation and maintaining species identity in various organisms3. Reproductive isolation can occur before or after fertilization: pre-zygotic reproductive isolation prevents the formation of hybrid zygotes, whereas postzygotic reproductive isolation reduces the fitness of offspring through mechanisms, such as hybrid sterility and hybrid inviability (e.g., hybrid necrosis, weakness, and lethality)4. Hybrid sterility is often controlled by complex loci whose multiple, tightly linked genes function as a killer–protector (or toxin–antidote) system. A killer gene typically causes selective abortion of gametes that lack the competing protector gene, thus causing allele transmission bias in the progeny5–8. Such killer–protector systems have been widely reported at hybrid sterility loci in plants, animals, and yeast3,7–13.

Cultivated rice feeds approximately one-half of the world’s population, and hybrid rice breeding has historically been an economical and effective means of increasing rice yield. However, the yield potential of intraspecific rice hybrids has reached a plateau, inducing breeders to attempt to produce interspecific hybrids. For example, the cultivated rice species Asian rice (Oryza sativa L.) and African rice (Oryza glaberrima Steud.) were independently domesticated from Oryza rufipogon/nivara and Oryza barthii, respectively14. Asian rice includes two subspecies, japonica and indica. Compared with intrasubspecific indica–indica hybrids, intersubspecific indica–japonica hybrids and interspecific Asian–African rice hybrids exhibit stronger heterosis for yield potential15,16. However, these distant hybrids also exhibit strong hybrid sterility that limits the use of their strong hybrid vigor. The breeding of distant rice hybrids for increased food security will therefore require cloning the causal genes for rice hybrid sterility loci and dissecting their mechanisms of action.

More than 60 hybrid sterility loci have been identified and mapped in rice species, and 15 have been cloned6–8,17–33. Six of these loci (Sa, S5, S1, qHMS7, RHS12/pf12/Se, and qHMS1) contain two or three tightly linked genes that genetically interact to control hybrid sterility as described by the single-locus sporogametophytic interaction model6–8,17–20,26,27. Multiple hybrid sterility loci have been reported between Asian and African rice, but only one (S1) has been cloned and characterized27,34–39. The African rice S1 allele (S1-g) contains three tightly linked genes, S1TPR, S1A4, and S1A6 (SSP); their encoded proteins form a tripartite complex that triggers a killing signal, causing abortion of male and female gametes22,23,27. S1TPR plays the role of protector for gametes that carry S1-g, whereas gametes that carry the Asian rice S1 allele (S1-s) are selectively aborted because they lack a functional protector27.

S19, another major locus associated with hybrid male sterility (HMS) between Asian and African rice, was previously mapped to chromosome 3, but its causal gene(s) remain unknown39. In the present study, we bred a near-isogenic line (NIL) carrying S19-g from African rice in an Asian rice background and used it to map and clone S19. We found complex structural variations between the Asian rice S19-s and African rice S19-g alleles, and analysis of nine candidate genes in the S19-g allele revealed two causal genes for HMS (S19A1 and S19A7). S19A1 expresses two mitochondria-targeted cytotoxic protein variants derived from alternatively spliced transcripts, causing toxicity and somatic and gametic cell death for one isoform (S19A1.1), and gametic cell death for the other isoform (S19A1.2). S19A7 interacts with and suppresses the function of both cytotoxic protein variants, thereby acting as the protector in this pollen killer–protector system. Phylogenetic analysis showed that a locus encoding the functional killer–protector complex likely emerged in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage. These findings suggest that the S19-mediated reproductive barrier to interspecific hybrid rice breeding might be overcome by using compatible African rice lines with natural or artificial neutral S19 alleles.

Results

Map-based cloning of S19

F1 hybrids from a cross between O. sativa subsp. japonica 9522 (an Asian cultivated rice variety) and O. glaberrima CG14 (an African cultivated rice line) had greater biomass than their parents but exhibited severe male sterility and very low seed-setting rates (Supplementary Fig. 1). To clone the genes responsible for hybrid sterility between 9522 and CG14, a set of NILs was developed by using 9522 as the recurrent male parent and CG14 as the donor parent. One NIL from the BC4F2 generation (named SG72) contained an introgression fragment from CG14 on a region of chromosome 3 (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b) that overlapped with the previously reported S19 locus39. SG72 (which was homozygous for the African rice allele S19-g) was morphologically similar to 9522 (Supplementary Fig. 2c) and had normal pollen and spikelet fertility (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 2d). However, F1 hybrids of a 9522 × SG72 cross exhibited typical pollen semi-sterility (Fig. 1a).

The S19 genotypes of the 533 plants in the BC4F2 population (from which SG72 was obtained) showed severe segregation distortion: 48.8% were homozygous for the African rice allele (S19-gS19-g), 50.1% were heterozygous (S19-gS19-s), and only 1.1% were homozygous for the Asian rice allele (S19-sS19-s) (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, all plants carrying S19-gS19-s displayed pollen semi-sterility, whereas those carrying S19-gS19-g or S19-sS19-s exhibited full pollen fertility (Supplementary Table 1). This segregation distortion suggests that pollen grains with the S19-s allele were selectively aborted in the S19-gS19-s heterozygotes (Fig. 1c). We confirmed this by performing reciprocal crosses between 9522 and F1 plants. When heterozygotes (as female parents) were pollinated by 9522, the segregation ratios of S19-gS19-s and S19-sS19-s in the progeny were 48.7% and 51.3%, respectively. However, when 9522 was pollinated by heterozygotes, almost all the progeny were S19-gS19-s (98.7%), and only 1.3% were S19-sS19-s (Supplementary Fig. 2e). These genetic data confirmed that the S19-g allele shows a strong transmission advantage in Asian–African rice hybrids, acting as a typical “selfish” genetic element40. Thus, S19 behaves as described by the single-locus sporogametophytic interaction model, similar to previously cloned loci (Sa, S5, Sc, S1, qHMS7, RHS12/pf12/Se, and qHMS1)6–8,17–20,24,27.

To clone the causal genes for S19-mediated HMS, we first mapped the S19 locus using the BC4F2 population, then fine-mapped the locus using ~25,000 BC4F3 plants derived from the BC4F2 S19 heterozygotes. This approach ultimately mapped S19 to a 10-kb region of the japonica reference genome defined by the molecular markers M1509 and M1520 (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The 10-kb S19-s region contains two predicted open reading frames (ORFs) in the japonica reference annotation, S19ORF1 and S19ORF2 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Table 4). However, according to the reference genome of African rice41, the corresponding S19-g region in SG72 is 104 kb in length and contains a 94-kb genomic insertion between S19ORF1 and S19ORF2 (Fig. 1d). Transcriptome sequencing of anthers (meiosis to late microspore stage) revealed that S19ORF1, S19ORF2, and seven additional ORFs (S19A1–S19A7) in the 94-kb African-rice-specific sequence were expressed in anthers of SG72 and the SG72 × 9522 F1 hybrids (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 4a, Supplementary Table 4). Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) confirmed the expression of these genes in young spikelets (Supplementary Fig. 4b).

S19ORF1 encodes a 195-aa protein with a heavy-metal-associated domain in 9522, but harbors two alternatively spliced isoforms (S19ORF1.1 and S19ORF1.2) in SG72 due to a 24-bp insertion in the third exon (Supplementary Table 4). S19ORF1.1 encodes a 203-aa protein, and S19ORF1.2 encodes a 169-aa protein (Supplementary Table 4). S19ORF2 encodes a 456-aa protein with a calcineurin B-like interacting protein kinase domain that contains a 1-aa variation between 9522 and SG72 (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, S19A1 produced two alternatively spliced transcripts: S19A1.1 (with a lower expression level) and S19A1.2. These transcripts encode uncharacterized proteins of 645 aa and 711 aa, respectively (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Figs. 4b, 5, 6, and Supplementary Table 4). S19A2–S19A6 encode small polypeptides of 32, 153, 55, 72, and 137 aa, respectively (Supplementary Table 4), and S19A7 encodes an uncharacterized protein of 434 aa (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 4). Because these nine genes were all expressed in the anthers of SG72, we considered them to be candidates for S19-mediated HMS.

S19A1 from African rice expresses two transcripts encoding killer proteins

To assess the effects of the candidate genes on S19-mediated HMS, we attempted to knock out each gene individually in S19 heterozygotes by CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing. Knockout mutants were obtained for all the candidates except S19A7 (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9, Supplementary Table 5). Seven of these eight mutants (S19orf1, S19orf2, S19a2, S19a3, S19a4, S19a5, and S19a6) had semi-sterile pollen similar to that of the wild-type F1 plants, and the segregation ratio of the S19 locus remained distorted in their T1 generations (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9, Supplementary Table 6), indicating that they were not involved in S19-mediated HMS. However, the S19a1 mutants had fully fertile pollen and spikelets (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 10a), and the segregation distortion of the S19 locus was eliminated in their T1 generations (Fig. 2b, c). These results suggested that S19A1 is required for pollen sterility in the S19-HMS system.

Because two alternatively spliced transcripts were expressed from S19A1 (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Figs. 4b and 5), we constructed two binary vectors containing the coding sequences of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 driven by their native promoter and transferred them into 9522 to test their killer function. After many trials, we failed to obtain transgenic lines of S19A1.1 (tS19A1.1), suggesting that S19A1.1 may be cytotoxic or even lethal to somatic cells. To assess this possibility, we co-expressed S19A1.1 and GFP (tS19A1.1/GFP) in calli of 9522 and SG72. The GFP signal was really observed in SG72 calli but was diminished or absent in 9522 calli (Fig. 2d, e). These results suggested that S19A1.1 is strongly toxic to calli (somatic cells) of 9522 but that SG72 may contain another gene at the S19 locus that blocks the toxic effect of S19A1.1 on somatic cells.

We obtained multiple independent transgenic lines of S19A1.2 (tS19A1.2) in the T0 generation, all of which contained a single hemizygous insertion of tS19A1.2 and exhibited semi-sterile pollen and spikelets (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 10b). No plants with the tS19A1.2 transgene were obtained in the T1 generation (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 7). This severely distorted segregation ratio indicated that male and female gametes carrying tS19A1.2 were selectively aborted (Fig. 2h). Together, these results demonstrated that S19A1.1 acts as a strong killer in a sporophytic manner, whereas S19A1.2 functions as a killer in a gametophytic manner.

The S19A7 gene from African rice encodes a protector protein

Our failure to knock out S19A7 in S19 heterozygotes suggested that S19A7 may encode a protein that protects cells against the toxic effect of S19A1. To test this hypothesis, we attempted to knock out S19A7 in SG72 but obtained only two heterozygous S19A7-knockout mutants (KO-S19A7). Because the KO-S19A7 plants harbored the endogenous killer gene S19A1, we reasoned that if S19A7 functions as a protector, then KO-S19A7 plants would be semi-sterile, and pollen grains with the S19a7 allele would be sterile. As expected, pollen and spikelets of the T0-KO-S19A7 plants were semi-sterile (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 10c). We next analyzed segregation of the intact S19-g containing S19A7 and the mutant S19-g (S19-gm) containing S19a7 in the T1 generations derived from the two T0-KO-S19A7 plants. The segregation of the S19-g and S19-gm alleles was severely distorted: most of the T1 plants (87.1% and 97.6%, respectively) were S19-gS19-g homozygotes, the rest were S19-gS19-gm heterozygotes, and no S19-gmS19-gm homozygotes were detected (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 8). Furthermore, the heterozygous mutant of S19A7 displayed pollen, embryo sac, and spikelet semi-sterility (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Table 8). These observations confirmed that S19A7 is a protector gene that counteracts the killer effect of S19A1.

To confirm the protector role of S19A7, we constructed binary vectors containing the coding sequence of S19A7 driven by its native promoter and transferred them into S19 heterozygotes. The pollen fertility of T0 and T1 plants carrying the hemizygous S19A7 transgene (tS19A7) was restored to ~75%, and their spikelets were fully fertile (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 10d, and Supplementary Table 9). Moreover, pollen of the homozygous tS19A7 plants was fully fertile (Supplementary Table 9). The segregation ratios of the S19 (S19-gS19-g:S19-gS19-s:S19-sS19-s) and tS19A7 (77:7 –:– –) genotypes were very close to 2:3:1 in two independent T1 populations derived from T0 plants with the hemizygous tS19A7 transgene (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Table 9). Thus, both pollen grains with the S19-g allele (which contains endogenous S19A7) and S19-s type pollen grains with the tS19A7 transgene were fully fertile; only S19-s type pollen grains that lacked tS19A7 were aborted (Fig. 3e). These observations confirmed that S19A7 acts as a pollen protector in Asian–African rice hybrids.

Alternatively spliced S19A1 and S19A7 constitute a killer–protector system

If S19A1 and S19A7 constitute a killer–protector system, we speculated that homozygous or bi-allelic S19a7S19a7 mutants would be obtained when S19A1 and S19A7 were knocked out simultaneously. Indeed, in contrast to the S19A7-knockout single-gene mutants, 13 S19A1 and S19A7 knockout double-gene mutants we obtained were homozygous or biallelic double mutants (a1a1/a7a7) (Fig. 4a), all of which grew normally with full male fertility (Supplementary Fig. 10e, f). To further verify the genetic interaction between S19A1 and S19A7, we introduced genomic fragments containing S19A1 and S19A7 (tS19A1-S19A7) together into 9522, and the T0 plants with hemizygous tS19A1-S19A7 had semi-sterile pollen, mimicking the wild-type F1 plants (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Table 10). Moreover, the tS19A1-S19A7 genotypes (homozygotes and hemizygotes) segregated in a 1:1 ratio in the T1 generation (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 10). These results indicated that S19A1 and S19A7 form a killer–protector system at the S19 locus that causes HMS.

Given that S19A1 expresses two alternatively spliced variants, S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, we attempted to figure out the contribution of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 to S19-mediated HMS through co-expressing S19A1.1 or S19A1.2 with S19A7 (tS19A1.1-S19A7 or tS19A1.2-S19A7) driven by their native promoters into 9522, respectively. The pollen of plants with hemizygous tS19A1.2-S19A7 display full-fertility (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, the segregation ratio of tS19A1.2-S19A7 (carrying transgenes and non-carriers) fit a 3:1 ratio (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Table 11), indicating that S19A1.2 could not cause gamete abortion in the presence of S19A7. These observations also implied that S19A1.1 is responsible for causing the pollen sterility in the transgenic plants with hemizygous tS19A1-S19A7. However, none of the transgenic lines of tS19A1.1-S19A7 was obtained with many trials.

For that, the expression level of S19A1.2 may be higher than that of S19A1.1 (Supplementary Fig. 4), we speculated that alternative splicing confers the different dosage of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2. To validate this hypothesis, we analyzed the expression profile of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and in situ hybridization. Transcripts of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 were relatively high expression in anthers of SG72 and S19-heterozygotes, from the microspore mother cell stage to late microspore stages, and in the microspores at early and late microspore stages, and S19A7 were expressed in various tissues (Fig. 5a–c). Using in situ hybridization, we confirmed that S19A1 (total transcript of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2), S19A1.2, and S19A7 are expressed in tapetum and microspores of the anther and in the embryo sac (Supplementary Fig. 12). Although the expression level of S19A1.1 is lower than that of S19A1.2 and S19A7, we found that S19A1.1 have a long half-life period whereas S19A1.2 and S19A7 have a short half-life period when we expressed independently S19A1.1, S19A1.2 and S19A7 in rice protoplasts under the treatment of the translation inhibitor CHX (Fig. 5d–g). This characteristic may determine that S19A1.1 can function independently in the S19-s gametes of S19-heterozygotes, while S19A1.2 and S19A7 may fail to be accumulated in the S19-s gametes of S19-heterozygotes.

Consistent with the expression of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 in anthers of F1, the expression of S19A1.1 was significantly lower than that of S19A1.2 in the tS19A1-S19A7 plants (Figs. 4e, 5a, b). Moreover, when the S19A1.2 was solely expressed without the alternative splicing process, the expression of S19A1.2 in tS19A1.2-S19A7 plants is slightly higher than that in tS19A1-S19A7 (Fig. 4e), suggesting that disruption of the alternative splicing process in S19A1 increases the expression of S19A1.2. Similarly, we speculated that loss of alternative splicing regulation may also cause high expression of the S19A1.1, causing a single copy dosage of S19A7 is insufficient to inhibit the toxicity of the abundant S19A1.1 solely driven by its native promoters, and thus resulting in failing to obtain the transgenic lines of tS19A1.1-S19A7.

Based on the genetic evidence and the expression pattern of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7, we concluded that the different abundance of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 regulated by alternative splicing is essential for S19-mediated HMS, and thus displaying an alternative splicing-dependent killer–protector system.

S19A7 exerts its detoxifying effect by interacting with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2

To examine how S19A7 suppresses S19A1 function, we examined the subcellular localization of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 proteins fused with GFP in rice protoplasts. In all cases, the GFP signal overlapped with the red fluorescence signal of the mitochondrial marker, indicating that the proteins were mitochondrially targeted (Fig. 6a). Combining the genetic and molecular evidence, we reasoned that S19A7 might interact with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 to suppress their killer effect. Indeed, in vitro pull-down and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays confirmed that S19A7 bound to S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 (Fig. 6b–d), providing evidence that S19A7 interacts with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 to form a killer–protector module.

To understand the possible mechanism by how S19A7 suppresses the toxicity triggered by S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, we analyzed the three-dimensional (3D) protein structure of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 using AlphaFold3. S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 protein structures consist mainly of α-helices and disordered regions, while S19A7 consists primarily of β-sheets and disordered regions (Fig. 6e, g, and Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). Despite differing by only 71 amino acids at the C-terminus, S19A1.2 and S19A1.1 exhibit poor 3D structural alignment (Supplementary Fig. 13d), indicating that the different amino acids significantly remodel the protein architecture and potentially underly their divergent toxic activities. Furthermore, we found that S19A7 interaction with S19A1.1 or S19A1.2 converts some α-helices of S19A1.1 or S19A1.2 into disordered structures (Fig. 6f, h, and Supplementary Fig. 13e, f), suggesting that S19A7 may reduce α-helices of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 through protein interactions, thereby potentially suppressing their toxic effects.

The functional S19 locus may emerge in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage

To examine the origin and evolution of S19A1 and S19A7 in Oryza species, we obtained the genomic sequences of S19A1 and S19A7 from wild Oryza species and cultivated rice in our collection and from publicly available rice genome data at the Rice Resource Center (http://ricerc.sicau.edu.cn/)41. Full-length or partial sequences homologous to S19A1 and S19A7 were present only in some Oryza species. We then identified natural variants of S19A1 and S19A7 in the Oryza species. One major S19A1 variant detected in wild rice (e.g., Oryza barthii [AA genome] and Oryza rufipogon [AA genome]) had an additional 355-bp segment and lacks a 1187-bp sequence (covering the third to fifth exons) that caused a premature stop codon, and we named this defective allele S19da1 (Supplementary Fig. 14a).

A major S19A7 variant detected in Asian rice had a promoter/upstream region completely different from that of S19A7, as well as an additional 24-bp segment in the second exon (Supplementary Fig. 14a). To determine whether this S19A7 variant was functional as a pollen protector, we crossed SG72 with the indica variety Huanghuazhan (HHZ), which carries this S19A7 variant (and lacks S19A1 or S19da1), to generate F1 plants and their F2 progeny. The S19 heterozygote from this cross also caused segregation distortion in the F2 generation (Supplementary Fig. 14b and Supplementary Table 12), indicating that the variant allele was defective as a pollen protector, and we therefore named it S19da7. Indeed, RT-qPCR showed that S19da7 was completely silenced in HHZ (Supplementary Fig. 14c).

To trace the evolutionary origin of the S19 locus in Oryza, we identified S19 haplotypes (i.e., combinations of S19A1/S19da1 and S19A7/S19da7 gene variants) by PCR amplification of the corresponding genomic sequences in cultivated rice species, the AA-genome species Oryza meridionalis and Oryza longistaminata, and ancestral non-AA-genome wild rice species. Five major haplotypes were detected (Fig. 7a). Haplotype 1 (H1), which lacked S19A1- and S19A7-related sequences at the S19 location, was found in most ancestral wild rice species (e.g., Oryza eichingeri [CC genome] and Oryza punctata [BB genome]; Supplementary Table 13) and may therefore represent the ancestral sequence from which S19 originated. Haplotype 2 (H2) contained S19da1 but lacked S19A7 or S19da7; Haplotype 3 (H3) lacked S19A1 or S19da1 but contained S19da7; and Haplotype 4 (H4) contained S19da1 and S19da7. These haplotypes were identified in some ancestral wild rice species (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 13).

O. sativa and O. glaberrima belong to two independent lineages in the AA-genome group and were domesticated from O. rufipogon/nivara and O. barthii, respectively14. To determine the origin of the functional S19 locus in Oryza, we analyzed S19A1/S19da1 and S19A7/S19da7 sequences in the O. rufipogon–O. sativa and O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineages. In the O. rufipogon–O. sativa lineage, H1 and H3 appear to have been transmitted from O. rufipogon into O. sativa, and H1 became the predominant form of the S19-s allele in japonica rice (Supplementary Table 13). In the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage, H1 and Haplotype 5 (H5) appear to have been transmitted from O. barthii into O. glaberrima (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 13). Consistent with the detecting results in O. glaberrima, S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 are also detectable in the O. barthii carrying the H5 (Supplementary Fig. 14d), further confirming that the functional S19-g allele, H5 in O. glaberrima, is derived from O. barthii.

Since non-functional H1–H4 lacking expansion capacity are present in non-AA Oryza species, we hypothesize that the predominant H1 among these species may represent the ancestral haplotype. The intermediate variants H2 and H3 (harboring S19da1 and S19da7, respectively) may have arisen independently at the H1 location. H4 was then formed by hybridization and recombination in ancestral wild rice species carrying S19da1 and S19da7, and it gradually evolved into the functional S19 haplotype (H5) by sequence variations in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage (Fig. 7b).

Discussion

Hybrid sterility is a complex quantitative trait that maintains reproductive isolation between species or subspecies during evolution but presents a major obstacle to distant crossbreeding in rice11. Distant rice hybrids, such as indica–japonica or O. sativa–O. glaberrima hybrids, show strong hybrid vigor for biomass (Supplementary Fig. 1), highlighting the potential advantages of distant hybridization42. However, such hybrids have very low seed-setting rates because of hybrid sterility. Over the past decades, significant advances in our understanding of indica–japonica hybrid sterility have promoted the breeding of indica–japonica hybrids4, but much less is known about the genetic network that controls hybrid sterility between O. sativa and O. glaberrima. In this study, we demonstrated that two linked genes at S19, i.e., S19A1 and S19A7, encode a killer–protector module that controls HMS between O. sativa and O. glaberrima (Fig. 7c). Like that reported at the S1 locus27, the killer–protector system at the S19 locus depends solely on the O. glaberrima allele, showing an asymmetric allelic interaction in the interspecific hybrids. Whether this is a common characteristic of O. sativa–O. glaberrima hybrid sterility systems remains to be determined.

Numerous studies have shown that killer–protector or toxin–antitoxin systems have important roles in maintaining species and conferring postzygotic reproductive isolation in plants, animals, and yeasts7–13,17,43–50. These killer–protector systems are typically encoded by complex loci that contain multiple linked genes. The killer proteins expressed in sporophytic cells are generally thought to be relatively stable, remaining in the post-meiotic male or female gametes where they exert their pernicious effects. By contrast, the protector proteins may have short half-lives or be expressed mainly in gametes that carry the protector gene, and gametes that lack the protector gene are selectively aborted. In the killer–protector module at the S19 locus, S19A1 produces two alternatively spliced transcripts, S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, which encode two variant killer proteins. S19A1.1 is expressed at a lower level, but its product acts as a robust killer in both sporophytic and gametophytic cells, whereas S19A1.2 acts as a killer only in gametophytic cells (Figs. 2, 4), indicating that an alternatively spliced killer gene confers the toxicity diversity for hybrid sterility. These findings provide a new understanding of how alternative splicing contributes to hybrid sterility in rice. Additionally, S19A7 encodes a protector protein that interacts with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, suppressing their cytotoxic effects (Figs. 3–6). The protein stability analysis showed that S19A1.1 have a longer half-life than S19A1.2 and S19A7 (Fig. 5d–g). Given that the gametes with S19-s that lack S19A1 and S19A7 are selectively aborted in the S19 heterozygotes, we speculate that S19A1.1 expressed in the microspore mother cell may be retained in post-meiotic male gametes with S19-s. On the other hand, S19A1.2 and S19A7 may fail to be retained in the gametes with S19-s due to low protein stability. Instead, they may only exist in the cells or gametes with S19-g through continuous expression. Therefore, all pollen grains avoid abortion in African rice lines with homozygous S19-g alleles, because S19A7 is persistently expressed in the male gametes, preventing the harmful effects of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2. In African–Asian rice hybrids, pollen grains carrying S19-g are protected by the binding of S19A7 to S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, whereas those carrying S19-s are selectively aborted because they lack S19A7 and are not protected against the retained S19A1.1 (Fig. 7c). In addition, we noted that the tS19A1.2 transgene in the 9522 background and the heterozygous S19A7S19a7 mutants caused abortion of both male and female gametes (Figs. 2, 3), whereas the F1 plants exhibited only male sterility (Fig. 1). On the basis of these observations, we speculate that another yet unknown factor regulating S19-mediated female gamete fertility may exist in the genomic region surrounding the S19-g allele.

A previous study showed that the RHS12-encoded killer protein DUYAO disrupts mitochondrial function by interacting with COX11, which is essential for survival of somatic cells and male gametes, whereas the RHS12-encoded protector JIEYAO inhibits the DUYAO–COX11 interaction by rerouting DUYAO to the autophagosome for degradation17. Our results showed that S19A1.1 proteins impair the development somatic cells and gametes, while S19A1.2 only kill male and female gametes in the absence of S19A7, and that S19A7 specifically offsets these effects (Figs. 2, 6). We also found that S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 proteins were localized in the mitochondria (Fig. 6a). Although the detailed molecular functions of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 remain to be characterized, the effect of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 appears to be similar to that of DUYAO encoded by the RHS12/Pf12/Se locus. Additionally, the predicted protein structure of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 could not fit well (Supplementary Fig. 13), suggesting that the underlying mechanism by which they kill diverse cell types may be contributed to different targets. We also noticed that S19A7 might reduce α-helices of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 by the interaction (Fig. 6g, h, and Supplementary Fig. 13). It has been showed that the reduction of α-helices leads to the loss of protein function51. Therefore, we speculate that S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 may induce cell death by hijacking unknown factors in the mitochondrion that is essential for the development of somatic cells and/or gametes, and that S19A7 interacts with S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 to inhibit this effect by reducing α-helices of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 to prevent the hijacking interaction.

Our evolutionary analyses showed that the functional S19 killer–protector module is present only in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage, strongly suggesting that the functional S19-g locus originated in O. barthii, the ancestor of O. glaberrima (Fig. 7a and Supplementary Table 13). The S19 locus itself may have originated from a genomic location (H1) that lacked S19A1- and S19A7-related sequences through a multi-step evolutionary process, in which the initial, nonfunctional killer and protector genes, i.e., S19da1 and S19da7, arose independently and were then combined through natural hybridization and recombination, ultimately evolving into the functional S19 locus (Fig. 7b). Once the functional S19 locus had formed, the killer–protector module conferred reproductive isolation between lineages of rice with and without this module, thus limiting the use of heterosis in O. sativa–O. glaberrima hybrids. Given that H4 is also present in non-AA genome Oryza species, it may represent the possible ancestral haplotype of the S19 locus. Thus, the second possible evolutionary trajectory posits that H4 gave rise to the functional H5 in the O. barthii–O. glaberrima lineage, whereas gene loss events led to the emergence of H1–H3 in the O. rufipogon–O. sativa lineage. The artificial hybrid-compatible S19 allele created by CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of S19A1 and the H1 S19 allele found in O. glaberrima (Figs. 2 and 7a) will be valuable for overcoming S19-mediated HMS in hybrid breeding. In addition to cloning more genes that confer O. sativa–O. glaberrima hybrid sterility, future work should focus on pyramiding natural and artificial neutral alleles of these hybrid sterility loci to create widely compatible lines for O. sativa–O. glaberrima hybrid rice breeding, thus enabling the use of their interspecific heterosis.

Methods

Plant materials

A set of NILs was generated using the African rice line CG14 as the female donor parent and the Asian rice line 9522 as the male recurrent parent for successive backcrosses. The NIL SG72, containing the African rice S19-g allele in the 9522 background, was identified in the BC4F1 and BC4F2 populations by molecular-marker-assisted selection and pollen fertility phenotyping. The BC4F2 population was used for genetic analysis and primary mapping of the S19 locus, and a large BC4F3 population of over 25,000 individuals derived from S19 heterozygotes was used for fine-mapping. SG72 and S19 heterozygotes were used to generate knockout mutants of candidate genes at the S19 locus, and 9522 and S19 heterozygotes were used to generate transgenic plants with S19A1 and/or S19A7. Seedlings of the indica variety 93–11 were cultured in the dark on 1/2-strength Murashige and Skoog medium (PhytoTech Labs, Lenexa, KS, USA; Product No. M519) for isolation of leaf sheath protoplasts52. Plants used for phenotypic observations were planted in a paddy field in Guangzhou.

Observations of pollen, spikelet, and embryo sac fertility

At least five florets per plant were collected before flowering and stored in 75% ethanol. Anthers from the florets were mashed on a microscope slide and stained with 1% (w/v) KI–I2 solution for observation and imaging of pollen grains under a light microscope (Axio Observer D1; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The viable pollen grains are full and completely dark with lots of starch granules, and the inviable pollen grains are small and lightly brown with few of starch granules. Pollen grains from five independent florets were evaluated, and seed-setting rates of three or five mature panicles were recorded to obtain pollen and spikelet fertility data for two or three independent individuals. Significant differences in pollen and spikelet fertility were assessed using two-tailed Student’s t tests.

For observation of embryo sac fertility, whole-mount Eosin B staining confocal laser scanning microscopy (WE-CLSM) was performed according to previous procedures with modifications53. Mature embryo sacs were fixed in FAA (formalin-acetic acid-alcohol) solution for at least 24 h, followed by rehydration through a graded ethanol series (70, 50, 30, and 10%) and distilled deionized water (ddH2O), with 30 min at each step. The samples were mordanted with 2% aluminum potassium sulfate for 40 min and then stained with 10 mg/L Eosin B (Macklin Biochemical, China, E808595) for 13 h. After staining, the tissues were treated again with 2% potassium alum for 30 min and rinsed 2–3 times with ddH2O. Dehydration was carried out through an ascending ethanol series (10, 30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%), with 30 min per concentration, followed by two to three changes in absolute ethanol. The embryo sacs were subsequently transferred to a 1:1 mixture of methyl salicylate (Macklin Biochemical, China, M813577) and absolute ethanol for 1.5 h, and then cleared in methyl salicylate for 3 h. Finally, the cleared specimens were mounted in fresh methyl salicylate on concave glass slides, covered with coverslips, and observed under a laser scanning (Stellaris5, Leica, Germany) confocal microscope using a 561 nm excitation wavelength to examine embryo sac development.

Mapping the S19 locus

Using pollen fertility data from all individuals in the BC4F2 population and their genotypes based on simple sequence repeat (SSR) or insertion/deletion (In/Del) markers, the S19 locus was initially mapped to a region of chromosome 3 between In/Del markers M1428 and M1534. A large BC4F3 population of ~25,000 plants was then used to screen recombinants and fine-map the S19 locus to a 10-kb region between In/Del markers M1509 and M1520. Key recombinants were selfed, and the pollen phenotypes and segregation ratios of their progeny were examined to confirm the fine-mapped S19 locus. Primers used to amplify the molecular markers are listed in Supplementary Table 14. The genome sequence of S19-g in the mapping region was based on the CG14 genome sequence in the Rice Resource Center (https://ricerc.sicau.edu.cn/)41. To annotate the candidates for S19-mediated HMS, transcriptome sequencing of anthers of SG72 and F1 plants were performed. Then, the full-length coding sequences of these anther-expression candidates were amplified by RT-PCR using specific primers (Supplementary Table 15).

CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing and genetic complementation

For CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing, 20-bp single guide RNA (sgRNA) sequences were designed to target each candidate gene using CRISPR-GE (http://skl.scau.edu.cn/)54. The synthesized sgRNA sequences were fused with sgRNA expression cassettes, then subcloned into the CRISPR/Cas9 binary vector pYLCRISPR/Cas9PUbi-H55 to knock out S19ORF1, S19ORF2, and S19A1–S19A7. For genetic complementation, sequences containing the native promoter of S19A1 fused with the coding region of S19A1.1 or S19A1.2 and the genomic sequence of S19A7 (including its native promoter) were amplified by suppression thermo-interlaced PCR56. These constructs were subcloned into the pCAMBIA1300 binary vector by Gibson assembly. To confirm that S19A1-S19A7 constituted a killer–protector system, the genomic fragments of S19A1 and S19A7 were amplified and cloned into pYLTAC380H. The knockout vectors were transferred into SG72 and S19 heterozygotes, and the functional complementation vectors were transferred into 9522 by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. The target sites in the mutants were amplified by PCR, sequenced, and analyzed using DSDecode (http://skl.scau.edu.cn/dsdecode/)57. The primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table 15.

Testing the toxic effects of S19A1 on calli

Two binary vectors were constructed to investigate the toxic effects of S19A1.1. pCAMBIA1300-Ubi::GFP, which contained the GFP coding sequence driven by the Ubi promoter, was used as a control to observe the GFP signal in transgenic calli. pCAMBIA1300-NP::S19A1.1-Ubi::GFP contained two independent expression cassettes, one for GFP and one for S19A1.1 driven by its native promoter. The two constructs were transferred into 9522 and SG72 calli by Agrobacterium-mediated transformation. After 3 weeks of culture on selection medium with hygromycin B, GFP signals in the transgenic calli were observed using a stereo microscope (Axio Zoom.V16; Carl Zeiss, Germany). The number of calli with GFP signal were counted on five plates, each containing 10 individual calli.

Genotyping of tS19A7 and tS19A1-S19A7 transgenes and S19 alleles

Transgenic complementation plants carrying a single T-DNA insertion were selected for genetic analysis. The T-DNA flanking sequences of tS19A7 and tS19A1-S19A7 were identified by hiTAIL-PCR58, and the genotypes of the T-DNA insertions were determined by PCR with specific primer sets (Supplementary Table 15). The S19 alleles were genotyped using the S19-linked molecular marker M1514 (Supplementary Table 14).

Transcriptome sequencing and expression analysis

The roots, stems, leaves, anthers during different stages, and young spikelets were collected from 9522, SG72, and their F1 plants. The total RNA was extracted from each sample using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For synthesizing first-strand cDNA, 1–2 μg of total RNA for each sample was reverse-transcribed using the One-Step gDNA Removal and cDNA Synthesis SuperMix kit (TransGen Biotech, China, AT311). The transcriptome sequencing of the anthers and young spikelets was performed by Biomarker Technologies (China) using the Oxford Nanopore Technology platform. Clean reads were generated by processing raw data with MinKNOW2 and Fastp and aligned to the CG14 and Nipponbare reference genomes (http://ricerc.sicau.edu.cn/)41 using minimap2. Alignment files were visualized using the Integrative Genomics Viewer software (https://www.igv.org/). The expression of candidate genes was measured in three biological replicates by RT-PCR or RT-qPCR using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table 15) and normalized to that of the endogenous control gene UFC159. Relative expression was calculated by 2−ΔCt.

Subcellular localization and BiFC assays

For subcellular localization assays, the coding sequences of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 were amplified and subcloned into pYL322d1/(N, C)-eGFP with the GFP tag. The resulting plasmids were transferred into rice protoplasts by PEG/Ca2+-mediated transfection60. In brief, 6 μg of each GFP-fused plasmid was mixed with 6 μg of plasmid encoding Mito-mCherry (a mitochondrial marker) and co-transfected into protoplasts for assessment of subcellular localization. For the BiFC assay, the coding sequences of S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 were amplified and subcloned into the pYL322d1/(N, C)-Vn or pUC19/(N, C)-Vc vectors, which carried the N or C terminus of the Venus tag, respectively61. Ten micrograms each of plasmids carrying the N-terminal and C-terminal Venus fusions were transfected into protoplasts, and the transfected protoplasts were incubated at 28°C for 16–20 h in the dark. After centrifugation at 150 × g for 8 min, the transfected protoplasts were collected, and the resulting fluorescence was imaged by confocal microscopy (Stellaris5, Leica, Germany) using excitation/emission wavelengths of 488 nm/490–560 nm for eGFP, 561 nm/580–660 nm for mCherry, and 514 nm/520–570 nm for Venus. The primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table 15. The subcellular localization and BiFC assay were repeated three times with consistent results.

Protein pull-down assay

The coding sequences of S19A1.1 and S19A1.2 were subcloned into PGEX-4T to express the fusion proteins GST-S19A1.1 and GST-S19A1.2, and the coding sequence of S19A7 was subcloned into PMAL-c5x to express the fusion protein MBP-S19A7. The vectors were transformed into Escherichia coli Transetta (DE3) (TransGen Biotech, China, CD801-02), and the tagged fusion protein was induced by the addition of 1 mM IPTG when the OD600 of cell cultures reached 0.6–0.8, followed by incubation at 23°C, 100 rpm for 12 h. The cells containing these recombinant proteins were harvested by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 10 min, resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.2–7.4, Solarbio, China, P1032), and then disrupted by sonication (VCX150; SONICS& MATERIALS; USA) with settings 30% amplitude, 5 s pulse on/7 s pulse off, for 5 min on ice. The GST, GST-S19A1.1, and GST-S19A1.2 proteins were combined with GST-beads as bait and then incubated with lysates containing MBP-S19A7 at 4°C for 8–10 h. The beads were washed 8–10 times with PBS (pH 7.2–7.4), each time followed by centrifugation at 300 × g for 1 min and heated at 95°C for 10 min with protein loading buffer (Solarbio, China, P1040). The pulled-down proteins were detected by western blotting with anti-MBP (TransGen Biotech, China, #HT701, 1:5,000) and anti-GST antibodies (TransGen Biotech, China, #HT601, 1:5,000). The primers used for vector construction are listed in Supplementary Table 15. The pull-down assay was repeated three times with consistent results.

RNA in situ hybridization assay

To perform RNA in situ hybridization, according to the previous experimental procedures with modifications62, rice tissues were fixed under vacuum in FAA fixative (50% ethanol: 37% formaldehyde: glacial acetic acid = 89:5:6, v/v) for 20 min, followed by incubation in fresh fixative at 4°C for 24 h. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin (Paraplast Plus, Sigma-Aldrich, China, P3683). Sections of 7 µm (anthers) and 10 µm (embryo sacs) were obtained using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2245, Germany). For probe synthesis, gene-specific fragments of S19A1 (387 bp), S19A1.2 (340 bp), and S19A7 (321 bp) were fused downstream of T7 or SP6 promoters. Digoxigenin (DIG)-labeled sense and antisense RNA probes were synthesized in vitro using the DIG RNA Labeling Kit (Roche, Switzerland, #11175025910) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following hybridization, sections were incubated with an anti-DIG-AP antibody for 30 min at room temperature. Colorimetric detection was achieved by incubating with NBT/BCIP substrate (Sigma-Aldrich, China, #11681451001) in the dark for 48 h. Images were acquired using a digital slide scanner microscope (PreciPoint M8, Freising, Germany). The primers used for probe synthesis are listed in Supplementary Table 15. The RNA in situ hybridization was repeated three times with consistent results.

Protein stability analysis

The dual-expression cassette vectors S19A1.1-GFP/mCherry, S19A1.2-GFP/mCherry, S19A7-GFP/mCherry, and GFP/mCherry were constructed and transformed into isolated protoplasts from the indica variety 93–11 leaf sheaths using the PEG/Ca2+ method, respectively. After incubation at 28°C for 12 h, cycloheximide (CHX) (MedChemExpress, the USA, HY-12320) was added to a final concentration of 300 µM. The cultures were then aliquoted into four parts and further incubated. Samples were collected at 0, 3, 6, and 9 h post-treatment. Protoplasts were collected by centrifugation (4°C, 300 × g, 10 min) and lysed in 100 µL of protein extraction buffer (Beyotime, China, P0013), followed by the addition of SDS-PAGE loading buffer (Solarbio, China, P1040) and heating at 95°C for 8 min. Proteins were separated on a 10% polyacrylamide gel and detected by immunoblotting using anti-GFP (TransGen Biotech, China, #HT801, 1:5,000) and anti-mCherry (Abcam, England, ab125096, 1:5,000). Target band intensities were quantified by grayscale analysis using ImageJ software. The ratio of the experimental group to the control group was calculated for each time point and normalized to the corresponding ratio at 0 h of treatment. The protein stability analysis was repeated three times with consistent results.

Protein structure prediction and analysis

The amino acid sequences of the proteins S19A1.1, S19A1.2, and S19A7 were submitted to the AlphaFold3 server (https://alphafoldserver.com/welcome)63 for tertiary structure prediction using default parameters, respectively. For each protein, the top-ranked predicted model (i.e., the one with the highest confidence score) was retained for subsequent analysis. To analyze the structural similarity between S19A1.1 and S19A1.2, their AlphaFold3-predicted monomeric structures were superimposed using PyMOL software (v2.5.5), and the root mean square deviation was calculated. Regions with a root mean square deviation greater than 8 Å were defined as spatially divergent regions, indicating substantial local conformational differences, whereas regions with high spatial overlap were designated as structurally conserved regions. To predict the interaction between S19A7 and either S19A1.1 or S19A1.2, the amino acid sequences of the corresponding protein were submitted to the AlphaFold3 server for complex structure prediction. The resulting structure files (.cif format) were converted to PDB format using Avogadro (v1.2.0). Model reliability was assessed based on the confidence files (*summary_confidences_0.json) provided by the server, with a combined score of interface predicted template modeling score (ipTM, reflecting interface accuracy) and predicted template modeling score (pTM, reflecting overall structure quality) ≥0.75 set as the high confidence criterion63. All structural visualization and final image rendering were performed using PyMOL software (v2.5.5).

Evolutionary analysis of the S19 locus

The variants of S19A1 and S19A7 were identified in multiple Oryza species in our collection and were searched in the publicly available rice genome data at the Rice Resource Center (http://ricerc.sicau.edu.cn/)41. Specific primer pairs were designed to amplify the genomic sequences of S19A1 or S19da1 and S19A7 or S19da7. To trace the ancestral S19 alleles, leaf samples were collected from 84 wild rice accessions with non-AA genomes: 13 Oryza latifolia (CCDD genome), 19 Oryza grandiglumis (CCDD genome), 10 Oryza officinalis (CC genome), 12 Oryza rhizomatis (CC genome), 9 Oryza. eichingeri (CC genome), and 21 Oryza punctata (BB genome). To identify the origin of the functional S19 locus, leaf samples were collected from 485 accessions with AA genomes: 13 Oryza meridionalis, 25 Oryza longistaminata, 143 Oryza rufipogon, 14 Oryza barthii, 67 Oryza glaberrima, and 223 Oryza sativa. The genomic sequences of S19A1 and S19A7 were determined by PCR amplification using Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (Vazyme, Nanjing, P505). All rice samples and their haplotypes are listed in Supplementary Table 13, and the primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 15.

Statistics and reproducibility

A two-tailed Student’s t test was used to compare two groups. A one-tailed chi-square test was used to assess the goodness-of-fit to the expected Mendelian ratio. All experiments were performed at least three times independently, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5 (version 5.01), and significance is indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001.
