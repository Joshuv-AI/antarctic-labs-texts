Array of Testing Characterizes Prenatal Diagnosis of Mosaic Tetrasomy 9p24q22.3 Associated With an Unusually Mild Phenotype and Favourable Outcome
Authors: Crystle Lee, Ellen Casey, David J Amor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTetrasomy 9p is a rare chromosomal disorder with distinct clinical features, but wide phenotypic variability. Historically, tetrasomy 9p has been detected by conventional cytogenetic analysis, but newer technologies such as non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) and chromosome microarray (CMA) allow the identification of cases that would previously have gone undetected. Here we describe a girl with a mosaic isodicentric chromosome 9 (idic(9)), ascertained through NIPT, but with diagnosis not confirmed until after birth.MethodsChromosomal microarray (CMA) was performed on DNA extracted from saliva, buccal and blood samples from a clinically well newborn to confirm abnormal prenatal findings. Subsequent conventional G‐banded analysis was performed on stimulated T‐lymphocyte culture following diagnosis of mild speech delay and hearing loss.ResultsCopy number gain at 9p24.3q22.32 was identified on CMA in three independent samples. G‐banded analysis subsequently identified a supernumerary isodicentric chromosome 9 in one out of 105 cells examined, with breakpoints at 9p24 and 9q22.3, representing a partial tetrasomy of chromosome 9.ConclusionTetrasomy 9p has wide phenotypic variability due to mosaicism. This unusually mild case of tetrasomy 9p would have almost certainly gone undetected prior to the implementation of NIPT and CMA. This report highlights the value of reporting cases ascertained through high‐resolution technologies to facilitate early diagnosis and prognosis and enable a better understanding of this condition through precise breakpoint mapping.Keywords: chromosomal microarray, NIPT, prenatal, tetrasomy 9p

In recent years, the prenatal testing landscape has been transformed by new testing technologies, particularly prenatal single nucleotide polymorphism microarray (SNP‐microarray) and non‐invasive prenatal testing (NIPT). One consequence is that these techniques have led to the identification of mosaic chromosome abnormalities that previously would have gone undetected and, therefore, are not well represented in the literature. This, in turn, has created new challenges for test interpretation and genetic counselling and highlights the importance of reporting mosaic cases detected using these newer techniques.

Here we describe a girl with a mosaic isodicentric chromosome 9 (idic(9)), originally ascertained through NIPT as an increased chance of chromosome 9 copy number gain, but with diagnosis not confirmed until after birth. During an otherwise normal pregnancy of a healthy 39‐year‐old woman, NIPT identified a mosaic 84 megabase duplication of chromosome region 9p24.3q21.32. Amniocentesis was performed and SNP‐microarray analysis undertaken on uncultured amniotic fluid, confirming the same copy number gain in approximately 20% of cells; however, conventional G‐banded analysis performed on cultured amniocytes showed a normal 46,XX karyotype in all 73 cells examined, representing 22 colonies. First and second trimester ultrasounds showed normal growth and no evidence of fetal abnormality. The family opted to continue with the pregnancy, and follow‐up ultrasounds continued to show no significant abnormalities.

A healthy non‐dysmorphic female infant was delivered at 35 weeks gestation with a birth weight of 2.24 kg, length of 45 cm, and head circumference of 29 cm (all 10–50th centile). Her developmental milestones were within normal limits. She commando‐crawled at 7 months, sat unsupported at 9 months, and walked independently at 15 months. Fine motor skills were age appropriate. She spoke her first words at age 11 months, but thereafter, speech was mildly delayed and at age 2 years, she spoke around 30 words. Her receptive language and social skills were age appropriate. Her physical health was good apart from two hospital admissions for bronchiolitis, and imaging of her heart, kidneys and hips was normal. She had mild conductive hearing loss and a normal ophthalmological examination. Growth at 20 months showed height of 81 cm (25th centile), weight of 9.56 kg (17th centile) and reduced head circumference of 42.5 cm (< 2nd centile).

Postnatal cytogenetic analysis was initiated to follow up the prenatal findings. SNP‐microarray results from saliva, buccal swab and blood samples taken at two months of age confirmed the NIPT and uncultured amniocentesis results (performed prenatally by SNP‐microarray), showing the copy number gain at 9p24.3q22.32 in approximately 40% of cells (Figure 1 and Table 1). At age 14 months, G‐banded karyotyping was performed on a stimulated T‐lymphocyte culture and revealed the presence of a supernumerary isodicentric chromosome 9 in one cell out of 105 examined, with breakpoints at 9p24 and 9q22.3, representing a triplication of this region or partial tetrasomy of chromosome 9 (Figure 2A,B). Despite being present in only one stimulated T‐lymphocyte, the finding from the G‐banded analysis was not considered to be a culture artifact as it confirmed the imbalance identified by SNP‐microarray on the uncultured samples. There were, however, discrepancies in the mosaic levels between the cultured and uncultured samples, with the cultured amniocytes and stimulated lymphocyte culture showing a normal karyotype and mosaic level of 0.9% compared to 40% on uncultured samples, which may be more accurately interpreted as 20% given the nature of the isodicentric chromosome. This discrepancy may represent an inherently unstable cell line due to the presence of two active centromeres or selection bias against the abnormal cell line during culture, which has been demonstrated for tetrasomy 9p and other isochromosomes such as i(12p) (Arghir et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2014).

Tetrasomy 9p (T9p) is a broad term describing the presence of a supernumerary chromosome derived from chromosome 9, either as an isochromosome (involving the short arm of chromosome 9 exclusively) or isodicentric chromosome (involving 9p and extending into the long arm of chromosome 9) (El Khattabi et al. 2015). T9p is a clinically recognizable condition with a highly variable phenotype characterized by multiple congenital abnormalities, developmental delay and dysmorphic features such as microcephaly, low‐set ears, hypertelorism and bulbous nose. Other less commonly reported features include skeletal, cardiac, ophthalmological and renal anomalies (Kok Kilic et al. 2022; Shu et al. 2021). Phenotypically normal patients mosaic for T9p have rarely been described in the literature, and most involve the isochromosome form of the condition (Shu et al. 2021; Bellil et al. 2020; Papoulidis et al. 2012; McAuliffe et al. 2005; Chen et al. 2023). To our knowledge, only two other postnatal cases with minimal symptoms involving the isodicentric form have been described, highlighting the need for continuous reporting of such cases (Shu et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023). Another notable feature of this case is that prior to the implementation of NIPT and prenatal chromosomal microarray analysis, our patient would almost certainly never have been diagnosed, even if there had been an indication to test, given that amniocentesis G‐banded analysis was normal and postnatal chromosome analysis required more than 100 cells to be examined before an abnormal cell was identified.

The cause of the wide phenotypic variation and severity in T9p remains largely unknown; however, the size of the supernumerary marker, level and tissue distribution of the aneuploid cell line are likely determining factors. The role of tissue‐limited mosaicism on phenotypic outcome is evident from the poor prognosis of non‐mosaic cases, especially when the triplicated region extends into the long arm of chromosome 9 (Kok Kilic et al. 2022). Previously published cases of healthy individuals showed varied degrees of mosaicism ranging from 0% to 100% in peripheral blood lymphocytes, supporting the need for testing multiple tissue types to elucidate the extent of the abnormal cell line, which may serve as a more accurate prognostic marker (El Khattabi et al. 2015; Lloveras et al. 2004). Tissue‐limited mosaicism aside, a plausible explanation for the wide phenotypic spectrum could be the size of the supernumerary marker, where involvement of 9q is typically associated with a worse prognosis (El Khattabi et al. 2015). This, however, has been harder to verify, given that a large proportion of reported cases have been identified by lower resolution cytogenetic analysis. The adoption of chromosomal microarray analysis, coupled with reporting of such cases, will no doubt provide better insights into the correlation between 9q and clinical phenotype through precise breakpoint mapping.

In contrast to other cases, where the degree of mosaicism varied between tissue types and was typically higher in blood, all three uncultured samples from this proband showed similar degrees of mosaicism of the aneuploid cell line. Testing of an additional sample type may further characterise the extent of the mosaic idic(9) across different cell lines; however, given the mild features present in this patient and that additional sampling would involve invasive tissue biopsy, further testing is currently not warranted, as it is unlikely to provide additional clinical utility. Evidence of at least two other cases of clinically normal individuals reported with the additional idic(9) provides assurance that this result may be associated with a favourable outcome, acknowledging that the patient reported in Chen et al., at age 7 months, may yet present with symptoms (Shu et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2023). In addition, whilst the origin of the idic(9) is unknown, as mosaic T9p usually arises as de novo events, the recurrence risk for this family is expected to be low (El Khattabi et al. 2015).

The present report adds an additional case to the limited number of cases ascertained through higher resolution assays such as SNP‐microarray, which also have the added benefit of mitigating discordant results associated with culture biases. The influence of tissue distribution and size of the supernumerary chromosome on phenotypic outcome remains uncertain in the current case, given the unusually mild phenotype. This patient may represent one end of the wide phenotypic spectrum of tetrasomy 9p; however, to clearly establish the genotype–phenotype correlation, more cases identified through chromosomal microarray analysis need to be reported. This will allow a better understanding of this condition, which will improve genetic counselling and patient care.
