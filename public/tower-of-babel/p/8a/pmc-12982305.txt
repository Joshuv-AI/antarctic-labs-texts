Examining the underreporting of social determinants of health in randomized controlled trials published in spine journals
Authors: Michael N Megafu, Jacob T Ayers, Lance C Evans, Spencer A Newell, Michelle Uwefoh, Omar D Guerrero, Andrew C Hecht, James Lin, Robert L Parisien, Investigation Performed by the Scientific Collaborative for Orthopaedic Research and Education (SCORE) Group
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSocial determinants of health (SDOH) are nonmedical factors that influence patient outcomes. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reporting SDOH factors in their patient cohorts within several of the highest-impact spinal surgery journals. We hypothesize that SDOH factors will be infrequently reported and often miss variables such as socioeconomic status, employment status, insurance, and education level.MethodsFrom 2020 to 2024, the PubMed database was queried to analyze spine RCTs reporting SDOHs from four high-impact journals. For each study, we determined the age, sex, and/or gender, body mass index (BMI), the year the article was published, the country of origin of the senior author, and self-reported SDOH factors. Results Of the 147 included RCTs, age (95.9%), sex/gender (94.6%), and BMI (72.8%) were reported by the majority of studies. There were a total of 33 countries represented by the RCTs reporting spine outcomes, where 25 (17.0%) RCTs were conducted in the United States, 22 (15.0%) RCTs conducted in China, 11 (7.5%) RCTs in the Netherlands, 9 (6.1%) RCTs in Japan and 9 (6.1%) RCTs in Turkey. Conclusion This paper provides valuable insights into the landscape of spine research and emphasizes the necessity of consistently reporting all SDOH factors. Future research can better inform clinical practice and policy decisions by addressing these considerations, ultimately improving healthcare outcomes for spine patients worldwide.Keywords: Social determinants of health, Spine surgery, Randomized controlled trials, Orthopedic surgery

Introduction

Social determinants of health (SDOH) are nonmedical factors, such as race, ethnicity, education, and income, that significantly impact patient outcomes by influencing health behaviors and access to healthcare [1]. Research consistently shows that SDOH affects both preoperative and postoperative outcomes in orthopedics, with notable disparities among Black and Hispanic patients, who often experience reduced improvement following procedures like total knee or hip arthroplasty [2–6]. Factors such as female gender, lower education levels, and unemployment have been linked to delays in treatment and poorer disease presentation, particularly in cases of rotator cuff injuries [4, 5]. These barriers hinder patients’ access to care and adherence to lifestyle changes, resulting in significant disparities in orthopedic outcomes across diverse demographics.

While research has established the importance of SDOH in orthopedic procedures like total joint arthroplasty and rotator cuff repair, there is limited data on how these determinants impact postoperative recovery and complication rates in spine surgery. Understanding these determinants is crucial for orthopedic surgeons, particularly in spine surgery, to identify at-risk patients and address gaps in care, thereby striving to improve patient outcomes and reduce disparities within this surgical field. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of randomized controlled trials that addressed SDOH factors in their patient cohort across several leading spinal surgery journals. We hypothesize that SDOH factors will be infrequently reported and may often exclude variables such as socioeconomic status, employment status, insurance, and education level.

Methods

Search strategy

The PubMed database was queried to examine SDOH in 4 high-impact spine journals: The Spine Journal, European Spine Journal, Spine (Phila Pa 1976), and Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine. This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines with the search strategy: (((“The spine journal: official journal of the North American Spine Society“[Journal]) OR (“European spine journal: official publication of the European Spine Society, the European Spinal Deformity Society, and the European Section of the Cervical Spine Research Society“[Journal])) OR (“Spine“[Journal])) OR (“Journal of neurosurgery: Spine“[Journal]).

Exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria incorporated RCTs published between 2020 and April 2024. The exclusion criteria included articles in languages other than English, articles incorporating nonhuman subjects, editor letters, non-RCTs, and secondary analysis articles of previous RCTs (Fig. 1).

Data collection

All data used in this study was publicly available; therefore, institutional review board (IRB) approval was not required. Two authors (JA and LE) reviewed the full text of the included studies. If disagreements arose during the data collection process, the third author (SN) resolved the conflict. The following information was extracted from each article that met the inclusion criteria: (1) age, (2) sex and/or gender, (3) body mass index (BMI), (4) year published, (5) country of origin for the article, and (6) self-reported SODH factors (race and/or ethnicity, employment status, education level, insurance status, socioeconomic status, smoking status, and stress [e. g., noise, anxiety levels]). The country of the senior author (last author) was used as a metric to determine the article’s country of origin. Thus, if multiple institutions were cited for an author, the last author was used to ascertain the country of origin. While sex is a biological construct and gender a social one, for this study, sex and gender were grouped into a single category as some countries may not delineate between the two variables. Race was defined using one of the following categories: Caucasian/ White, Black/African American, Asian, Pacific Islander, Native American, or Other. We identified ethnicity as Hispanic vs. non-Hispanic. We combined race and ethnicity for this study, although they are not interchangeable. Insurance status included reporting private, public, and worker’s compensation or uninsured status. Socioeconomic status encompassed patient incomes, area deprivation index (ADI), social vulnerability index (SVI), and distressed communities index (DCI). The Area Deprivation Index measures neighborhood disadvantages that may influence healthcare outcomes. This index was created by the US federal government from Census data [7]. It is scored from 1 to 100, where 1 represents the least disadvantaged and 100 represents the most underprivileged community [7]. The social vulnerability index is a percentile ranking developed by the CDC to assess risk and target populations likely to present emergently for diseases that could typically have been treated electively [8]. The SVI is scored from least vulnerable (score of 0) to most vulnerable (score of 1) [8]. The DCI evaluates economic well-being based on zip codes, scoring from no distress (0) to severe distress (100) [9].

Statistical analysis

The distribution of social determinants of health across the United States compared to countries outside the US was analyzed using the chi-squared test with associated P values. Linear regression analysis was also used to assess the trends of publications reporting social determinants of health from 2020 to 2024. Regression analysis included a P value, regression coefficient (ß), and 95% confidence interval (CI). The frequency of publications reporting SDOH was the outcome of interest, and the year of published articles was the independent variable for the simple linear regression analysis of trends over time. All data were assessed using RStudio (version 4.2.3; Posit Software). Statistical significance was achieved when the P value was less than 0.05.

Results

Our search yielded 1,443 articles, of which 1,269 were published outside the 2020–2024 range, leaving 174 for full-text review. After assessment, 27 articles were excluded for not being randomized controlled trials, resulting in 147 included in this analysis. This included 38 articles from the European Spine Journal, 10 from the Journal of Neurosurgery: Spine, 60 from Spine (Phila Pa 1976), and 39 from The Spine Journal (Fig. 1). Over five years, 33 countries conducted RCTs on spine surgery. Notably, 25 (17.0%) of these were in the United States, 22 (15.0%) in China, 11 (7.5%) in the Netherlands, and 9 (6.1%) in both Japan and Turkey (Fig. 2). The most commonly reported social determinants of health (SDOH) were age (141 articles; 95.9%), sex/gender (139 articles; 94.6%), body mass index (BMI) (72 articles; 72.8%), and smoking status (47 articles; 32.0%) (Fig. 3). Of the 147 articles included, 25 were published in the US, while 122 originated from countries outside the US (Table 1). When comparing the reporting of SDOH between international and US-based studies, significant differences were observed in the rates of reporting for age (P < 0.01), sex/gender (P < 0.01), BMI (P < 0.01), smoking status (P < 0.01), and race/ethnicity (P < 0.01) (Table 1).

Figure 4 shows the reporting of four common SDOH factors (age, sex/gender, BMI, and smoking status) in randomized control trials (RCTs) over five years. Throughout these years, age and sex/gender were reported most frequently, consistently exceeding 90%. The reporting of BMI ranged from 67.5% to 84.6%, while smoking status was reported the least, varying from 21.9% to 39.3%. Linear regression of SDOH reported over the five years indicated no significant trend between 2020 and 2024 (P = 0.10, ß = -5.20, [95% CI, -12.09, 1.69]). No significant trends were observed in age (P = 0.08, ß = -5.30, [95% CI, -11.72, 1.12]), sex/gender (P = 0.07, ß = -5.30, [95% CI, -11.46, 0.86]), BMI (P = 0.19, ß = -2.80, [95% CI, -8.09, 2.49]), or smoking status (P = 0.22, ß = 1.80, [95% CI, -5.49, 1.89]) during the five years.

Discussion

This review examines four high-impact spine journals and highlights the varying reporting levels on social determinants of health during the study period. When analyzing spine randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that report social determinants of health (SDOH), most originated from international studies. Many studies published within the five-year study frequently reported age, sex/gender, BMI, and smoking status. However, they overlooked other factors such as alcohol use, socioeconomic status, income levels, marital status, and race/ethnicity. The hypothesis presented in this study regarding infrequently reported SDOH factors and often overlooked variables like socioeconomic status, employment status, insurance, and education level proved valid. From 2020 to 2024, we observed a consistent lack of reporting on SDOH despite a statistically nonsignificant trend. It is crucial to emphasize the importance of SDOH for patients undergoing spine procedures or involved in spine research since these factors can impact postoperative outcomes, either enhancing or jeopardizing patient care, and can act as barriers to the effective management of spine pathologies. The insufficient reporting of SDOHs in spine literature allows researchers and healthcare professionals to enhance the comprehensiveness of their studies and contribute to a more holistic understanding of patients undergoing spine surgery.

There were significant differences in the reporting of SDOH between the US, with 25 articles, and international studies, with 122 studies in our research. This disparity reflects differences in healthcare systems, data collection standards, and population diversity, all influencing patient outcomes. International studies, especially from countries with universal healthcare systems, report SDOH less frequently, as care access is more equitable and less influenced by socioeconomic factors. In contrast, US studies often include variables like insurance status, income, and race/ethnicity, driven by the fragmented healthcare system, where these factors significantly affect access to care and clinical outcomes. However, even in US studies, SDOH reporting remains inconsistent and incomplete, limiting the generalizability of findings. The underreporting of SDOH in international trials may obscure the impact of systemic inequities on patient outcomes. At the same time, inconsistent reporting in US studies prevents a comprehensive understanding of how these factors influence surgical risks and recovery. This variation can lead to biased conclusions and hinder the development of universally applicable clinical guidelines, as interventions effective in one region may not be as effective in another due to unreported sociodemographic differences.

Reporting and identifying Social Determinants of Health (SDOH) in orthopedics is crucial for understanding their root causes, as racial and ethnic disparities have been well-documented in total joint arthroplasty, with minority patients experiencing worse postoperative function, outcomes, and increased mortality rates [10–14]. Studies by Paul et al. and Zhang et al. highlight that minority patients face lower rates of procedure utilization, like total knee arthroplasty, but higher rates of adverse outcomes even after adjusting for patient and healthcare system factors [15, 16]. Despite improvements in trial diversity, minorities remain underrepresented relative to their U.S. population proportions [13]. Educational deficiencies are linked to higher readmission rates and complications following hip and ankle fractures, while economic inequality increases the risk of readmission, revision, infection, and complications [16]. Additionally, lower socioeconomic status (SES) has been associated with worse patient-reported outcomes in procedures such as anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and a greater impact on functional outcomes than fracture patterns in proximal humeral fractures [17–19]. As research on SDOH continues to grow in fields like pediatrics and internal medicine, orthopedic RCTs must improve their reporting of SDOH to enable a clearer understanding of how these factors influence patient outcomes [20–23]. Our study found that most spine studies primarily focused on age, gender, and BMI, indicating the need for a more comprehensive investigation into additional SDOH factors. Future work should examine a broader range of journals to assess how SDOHs are reported across the orthopedic literature.

To improve clinical research’s validity and applicability, US and international studies must adopt standardized frameworks for SDOH reporting, ensuring consistent measurement and analysis of socioeconomic and demographic factors across diverse healthcare settings. However, limited reporting of SDOH in clinical trials is driven by systemic barriers, including inconsistent data collection, lack of standardized reporting frameworks, and limited awareness of SDOH’s impact on surgical outcomes. Many trials prioritize clinical variables over sociodemographic data, neglecting the broader context of patient health. Without mandatory reporting guidelines for SDOH, data collection remains inconsistent and incomplete. Financial and administrative constraints further hinder the inclusion of SDOH data, as capturing and analyzing these factors requires additional resources. Biases in trial design, such as the underrepresentation of minority populations, also contribute to the invisibility of SDOH in research, limiting the generalizability of clinical findings. Healthcare journals and research institutions must implement standardized SDOH reporting frameworks to address these gaps. Clinical trials should adopt SDOH “Z-codes” (ICD-10 codes) to systematically capture financial hardship, housing instability, and food insecurity. Journals should require authors to include a dedicated SDOH section in their methods and results, specifying variables like income, education, insurance status, and race/ethnicity. Regulatory bodies like CONSORT should expand their guidelines to mandate the inclusion of core SDOH variables, ensuring consistency across trials. Funding agencies and institutional review boards (IRBs) should prioritize studies that comprehensively report SDOH data. Standardized survey tools like the Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patients’ Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE) framework, an evidence-based risk assessment survey that can streamline data collection, and journals can implement SDOH checklists during peer review [24]. Additionally, creating centralized SDOH data repositories through collaboration between healthcare systems, public health agencies, and research networks would allow access to more diverse datasets, improving the external validity of clinical trials. By adopting these solutions, the medical research community can enhance clinical outcomes’ transparency, accuracy, and equity, ultimately driving more patient-centered healthcare practices.

There is also a place for structured assessment of psychological comorbidities, as it is increasingly recognized to be an essential aspect of optimizing outcomes in spine surgery. Validated screening instruments such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) have demonstrated strong reliability and clinical relevance in surgical populations. Preoperative anxiety and depression identified by these tools are consistently associated with worse baseline pain and functional scores but do not preclude meaningful postoperative improvement [25, 26]. Implementing these inventories at baseline and longitudinally (e.g., 3-, 6-, 12-months postoperatively) enables early identification of at-risk patients, facilitates timely referral to psychological care, and improves risk stratification and reporting in perioperative management. Their integration into perioperative pathways is therefore justified and aligns with current recommendations for holistic, biopsychosocial models of spine care.

This study has several limitations. First, it focused solely on RCTs related to orthopedics. While we reviewed articles from the past five years, a more extensive search could provide a more comprehensive longitudinal analysis of SDOH trends. Additionally, while we examined SDOH in high-impact spine journals, the scope was limited to a small subset of the spine literature without comparisons to other medical fields. Lastly, the SDOH factors we considered could have been more comprehensive, capturing a broader range of social determinants.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this paper highlights the need for consistent SDOH reporting in spine research to improve healthcare outcomes. To advance this, we recommend adopting standardized SDOH reporting frameworks. Journals should require authors to report SDOH factors in dedicated sections of methods and results, and regulatory bodies like CONSORT should mandate core SDOH variables in trials. By implementing these evidence-based guidelines, future research can more effectively inform clinical practice and policy, leading to improved outcomes for spine patients globally.
