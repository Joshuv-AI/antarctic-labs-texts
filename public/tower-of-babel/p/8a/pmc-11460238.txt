Salivary microbiota composition before and after use of proton pump inhibitors in patients with laryngopharyngeal reflux: a self-control study
Authors: Xiaohuan Cui, Longlong Yin, Yanping Zhang, Xingwang Jiang, Lina Li, Xinxin Bi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIssues associated with proton pump inhibitor (PPI) usage have been documented. PPIs affect the gastrointestinal microbiome, as well as the saliva microbiota of healthy individuals. However, the alterations in the saliva microbiota of laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) patients remain unclear. This study aims to examine the composition of saliva microbiota in LPR patients before and after PPI usage through a self-controlled study.MethodsThirty-two adult LPR patients participated in the study. Saliva samples were collected before and after an 8-week regimen of twice-daily administration of 20-mg esomeprazole. The impact of PPI administration on bacterial communities was assessed using 16 S rRNA gene sequencing. The functional and metabolic changes in saliva microbial communities after PPI usage were analyzed using PICRUSt2 based on our 16 S rRNA gene sequencing results.ResultsThe alpha diversity within the salivary microbiota, as measured by the PD-whole-tree index, exhibited a significant difference between samples collected before and after PPI application (P = 0.038). Additionally, PCoA analysis of unweighted UniFrac distances (beta diversity) revealed distinct separation of saliva sample microbiota structures before and after PPI application in LPR patients, with statistical significance (Adonis test, R2 = 0.063, P< 0.010). Taxon-based analysis indicated that PPI administration increased the abundance of Epsilonproteobacteria, Campylobacterales, Campylobacteraceae, Campylobacter, and Campylobacter_gracilis, while reducing the abundance of Lactobacillaceae and Lactobacillus in salivary samples ( P< 0.050). Using LEfSe to compare bacterial abundances, Bacillaceae and Anoxybacillus were found to be enriched before PPI usage in LPR patients. Furthermore, the proportion of genes responsible for indole alkaloid biosynthesis in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients significantly increased after PPI therapy (P< 0.050).ConclusionsThese findings indicate that PPIs induce alterations in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients.Chinese clinical trial registryNo. ChiCTR2300067507. Registered on January 10,2023 retrospectively.Level of evidence4.

Background

Laryngopharyngeal reflux (LPR) is an inflammatory condition affecting the tissues of the upper aerodigestive tract (UAT), caused by the reflux of gastroduodenal contents, resulting in morphological changes in the UAT mucosa [1]. LPR symptoms are common among patients consulting otolaryngologists, with a prevalence ranging from 10–30% [2]. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) are medications that effectively inhibit gastric acid secretion. Due to their potent acid-suppressive and notable immune-modulating effects [3], PPIs efficiently alleviate LPR symptoms and are often prescribed as first-line treatment. Expert consensus typically recommends a minimum 8-week course of PPI therapy [4]. However, the potential side effects of long-term PPI use are often overlooked by otolaryngologists. While generally regarded as safe, certain side effects have been documented, including an elevated risk of infections such as enteric infections [5]. These adverse effects are believed to be linked to dysbiosis of the gastrointestinal tract induced by PPIs [6], characterized by an overgrowth of oral-associated bacteria known as oralization [7, 8]. Thus, the oral microbiota may significantly influence the extent of dysbiosis and the occurrence of PPI-related side effects. In patients with LPR, our previous study identified dysbiosis in the salivary microbiota, potentially attributable to refluxate [9]. Given that salivary pH levels can be affected by PPIs [10], the oral microbiota is likely to undergo further alterations following PPI treatment. Therefore, investigating changes in the oral microbiota following PPI treatment is crucial for better understanding the side effects of PPI use in LPR patients and optimizing treatment plans. However, previous studies examining the alterations in microbiota following PPI usage have predominantly focused on gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [11], with limited attention given to LPR.

The salivary microbiota comprises a diverse array of bacteria shed from oral surfaces, including the throat, tongue, and tonsils, which serve as the main sources [12]. Saliva serves as a convenient medium for assessing the microbial condition of the oral cavity [13, 14], as well as the upper aerodigestive tract (UAT) [10, 15]. Research has demonstrated that the composition of salivary microbiota can influence the development and progression of various diseases, such as dental caries, periodontitis, oral squamous cell carcinoma [13], glossitis [16], pancreatic cancer [17], and throat cancer [18]. Conversely, saliva plays a crucial role in maintaining oral microbiota and oral homeostasis [19]. Changes in salivary pH values following PPI administration have been observed in LPR patients [10], potentially impacting salivary microbiota and increasing the risk of other diseases. Therefore, studying alterations in salivary microbiota after PPI therapy in LPR patients may offer insights into preventing medication-related side effects. To further investigate changes in the microbiota of the oral cavity in LPR patients after PPI therapy, we aimed to conduct a self-controlled study examining the composition of salivary microbiota in adult LPR patients following PPI use. We hope that our findings will contribute to enhancing LPR therapy.

Methods

Study design and patient selection

This study was conducted as an observational, self-controlled investigation, adhering to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and in accordance with relevant laws and regulations of Chinese authorities. Outpatients presenting with Reflux symptom Index(RSI ) scores exceeding 13 and diagnosed with suspected LPR [5] were recruited from the outpatient clinic of Otorhinolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery at the Eighth Medical Center of the PLA General Hospital between February 2022 and November 2022. These patients underwent eight weeks of esomeprazole therapy (Jiangxi Shanxiang Pharmaceutical Co. LTD, H20203298), administered at a dosage of 20 mg twice daily [4]. Patients who exhibited significant improvement and achieved an RSI score below 13 after the 8-week medication regimen were included in the final experiment [4]. Each participant completed a questionnaire detailing general information [20](such as age, gender, height, weight, antibiotic or other medication usage, history of long-term smoking or alcohol abuse, etc.) and RSI score [21]. Inclusion criteria for the experimental group were as follows: (1) Patients experiencing clinical symptoms including pharyngeal foreign body sensation, persistent throat clearing, hoarseness, voice fatigue, throat pain, chronic cough, dyspnea, or other symptoms persisting for at least 3 months. (2) Patients demonstrating significant improvement after 8-week PPI therapy. (3) Patients aged 18 years or older and newly diagnosed with LPR. Exclusion criteria for both groups were: (1) Diagnosis of any other diseases affecting the pharynx and larynx, systemic diseases, or mental disorders. (2) Recent use of antibiotics, gastric acid secretion inhibitors, gastrointestinal prokinetic drugs, microecology-related products, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, hormones, or other medications potentially affecting the microbiota within the past month. (3) History of long-term smoking or alcohol abuse. (4) Significant changes in diet or consumption of caffeinated or alcoholic beverages within 24 h before sampling. (5) Female participants menstruating, pregnant, or lactating. (6) Full recovery from a recent upper respiratory tract infection less than 2 weeks. (7) Use of medications that could affect test results during the experiment. (8) Consumption of probiotics within the past month. Health education was provided to all patients, and they were monitored weekly for adverse reactions throughout the 8-week treatment period. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants in the study. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of PLA General Hospital (No. 2022110130901725) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (No. ChiCTR2300067507, registered on 10 January 2023 retrospectively).

Biological information analysis

Sample collection and 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

All patients were instructed to collect their first saliva sample between 7:00 AM and 9:00 AM after brushing their teeth and abstaining from food and drink in the morning before and immediately after 8 weeks of PPI application. Participants lightly coughed and spat approximately 1 ml of saliva into a salivary collection and preservation kit, which was then promptly stored at -80 °C.

Genomic DNA was extracted by proteinase K cleavage. The library was constructed by 2-step polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification. The steps are as follows: (1)The distinct regions of the 16 S rRNA genes (16 S V3-V4) were amplified using specific bacterial primers (357 F 5’-ACTCCTACGGRAGGCAGCAG-3’and 806R 5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’)with the barcode. PCR products were purified by gel recovery kit (Axygen USA). FTC-3000TM real-time PCR instrument (Fengling Shanghai) was used for real-time fluorescence quantitative analysis mixing according to the equimolar ratio.(2)Barcode sequence was amplified and added for sequencing and was analyzed by illumina high-throughput sequencing (Illumina USA) and bioinformatics.

Operational taxonomic units (OTU) clustering and species annotation

Each sample was distinguished based on its barcode, and subsequently, sequence quality was controlled and filtered using Trimmomatic software (Version 0.35). Flash software (Version 1.2.11) was employed for merging, and the merged sequences underwent further quality control and filtering to obtain the predominant sequences. OTU cluster analysis was conducted using UPARSE software, with clustering performed at a 97% similarity threshold. The classify.seqs command of Mothur software (Version 1.33.3) was utilized to align the representative sequences of OTUs with the silva128 database and to annotate species information.

Microbiome analysis

Alpha diversity analysis was conducted to assess the abundance and diversity of microbial communities, utilizing six indices: observed-species, Chao1, Shannon, Simpson, ACE, and PD_whole_tree. Beta diversity analysis was employed to compare differences in microbial communities between groups. Unweighted UniFrac principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was utilized to visualize differences in community structure between the two groups. Closer distances between samples indicate more similar species composition structures, with samples exhibiting high similarity in community structure clustering together, while those with significant differences in community structure tend to separate. Adonis analysis was performed to assess beta diversity between the two groups. The average composition of bacterial communities at the phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species levels was analyzed. Linear discriminant analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis was utilized to determine the magnitude of the impact of each species’ abundance on differential effects and to identify species significantly impacting sample grouping. Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States-2 (PICRUSt2) was employed to predict the metagenome from our 16 S sequencing results and analyze changes in the KEGG pathways following PPI administration. The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the National Microbiology Data Center (NMDC) repository under accession number NMDC10018644 (https://nmdc.cn/resource/genomics/project/detail/NMDC10018644).

Statistical analysis

Clinical data and general patient information were analyzed using SPSS 25.0. Quantitative indicators with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\bar x$$\end{document}± s), while those with a non-normal distribution were presented as median and interquartile range M(Q1, Q3). For comparisons between groups, the paired t-test or Wilcoxon signed rank test was employed for quantitative data analysis. A P-value < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic characteristics of patients

Initially, a total of fifty-two patients were recruited, all of whom received PPI treatment. Twelve patients discontinued medication after 2–5 weeks of treatment due to symptom improvement or resolution. Additionally, one patient failed to adhere to the medication schedule, and another did not commence medication at the start of the experiment for personal reasons. Following 8 weeks of PPI use, the RSI score of four patients remained greater than 13, and two patients did not provide saliva samples. Ultimately, thirty-two patients, comprising 21 males and 11 females, met the inclusion and exclusion criteria, with an average age of 33.906 ± 11.116 years. None of the enrolled patients exhibited apparent adverse drug reactions or abnormalities after the 8-week medication period.

16 S rRNA gene sequencing of salivary samples

A total of 3,846,541 high-quality 16 S rRNA gene sequences were identified from 64 salivary samples, with a median read count of 173,718. Rarefaction curve analyses indicated that sufficient sequencing information was obtained for all samples (Fig. 1).

PPI therapy significantly increased salivary bacterial diversity in LPR patients

Alpha diversity analysis revealed a statistically significant difference in the PD_whole_tree index ( P= 0.038) (Fig. 2), indicating increased diversity following PPI administration. However, there were no statistically significant differences in the Shannon diversity index ( P= 0.369), Simpson diversity index ( P= 0.747), Chao diversity index ( P= 0.254), ACE diversity index ( P= 0.340), and Observed diversity index ( P= 0.531) before and after PPI application. Beta diversity, as assessed by unweighted UniFrac distances using PCoA analysis of salivary microbiota structure, demonstrated clear separation between saliva samples (Fig. 3), with a statistically significant difference observed (Adonis test, R2 = 0.063, P< 0.010). These findings suggest a significant difference in salivary microbiota structure between LPR patients before and after PPI administration.

Taxono-based salivary microbiota analysis in LPR patients before and after PPI therapy

Taxon-based analysis of salivary microbiota at various taxonomic levels revealed interesting findings. Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria emerged as the top five abundant bacterial phyla in both groups (Fig. 4a). Similarly, Prevotella, Neisseria, Streptococcus, Alloprevotella, Veillonella, Haemophilus, Fusobacterium, and Porphyromonas were identified as the top eight abundant bacterial genera in both groups (Fig. 4b). At the phylum level, no statistically significant differences were observed in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients before and after PPI treatment. However, at the genus level, notable alterations were detected following PPI administration. The relative abundance of Campylobacter, Phyllobacterium, Kocuria, and Brachymonas significantly increased, while that of Pseudoalteromonas, Lactobacillus, Desulfovibrio, Faecalibaculum, Vibrio, Faecalibacterium, Anoxybacillus and Cupriavidus significantly decreased after PPI treatment (P < 0.050) (Fig. 4c). Additional taxonomic changes with statistical differences between before and after PPI therapy are listed in Table 1. Particularly noteworthy is the significant elevation in the relative abundance of Campylobacterales, Campylobacteraceae, Campylobacter, and Campylobacter_gracilis across all taxonomic levels in the saliva of LPR patients after PPI administration ( P< 0.050). LEfSe analysis revealed that only two microbes, Bacillaceae and Anoxybacillus, were enriched before PPI usage (LDA > 2) (Fig. 5).

Functions of metabolism changed in the salivary microbiome in LPR patients

To investigate functional and metabolic changes in salivary microbial communities, all OTUs were aligned with the PICRUSt2 built-in reference database. PICRUSt2 software was utilized to assess potential differences in microbiome function (Fig. 6). The analysis revealed significant alterations in gene proportions associated with various biological pathways. Following PPI administration, there was a statistically significant down-regulation in genes responsible for spinocerebellar ataxia, adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, the apelin signaling pathway, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), circadian entrainment, insulin secretion, and dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) (P < 0.050). Conversely, the proportion of genes responsible for indole alkaloid biosynthesis in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients significantly increased after PPI therapy (P < 0.050).

Discussion

Numerous studies have highlighted alterations in the gut microbiome associated with acid-related diseases following PPI medication, potentially contributing to various side effects. It has been observed that such alterations often involve the oralization of the gut microbiome, characterized by an increased presence of oral bacteria due to compromised gastric barriers. Consequently, the condition of the oral microbiome may play a pivotal role in determining the extent of dysbiosis in the gut microbiome. Given that dysbiosis has been identified in the saliva and hypopharyngeal swabs of LPR patients prior to treatment [22], the potential impact of PPI administration on the oral microbiome in this population is of particular interest. In this study, we aimed to investigate whether PPI therapy influences the oral microbiome in LPR patients through a self-control study. Our findings reveal a significant increase in alpha bacterial diversity in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients after PPI treatment compared to pre-treatment levels. Notably, dysbiosis persisted in the saliva of LPR patients following PPI use. Moreover, even short-term (8-week) PPI medication was associated with the overgrowth of pathogenic bacteria such as Campylobacter in saliva, posing potential risks of more severe systemic diseases among LPR patients.

The oral microbiome, second in size only to the gut microbiome, harbors a vast array of microorganisms that play crucial roles in pathogen resistance and immune system education [15]. Under normal conditions, these microbiota coexist with the host, forming local microecologies through mutualistic interactions [23]. Microbial diversity is fundamental for maintaining ecosystem stability and functionality. Changes in bacterial diversity, such as the overgrowth of opportunistic microbiota or the absence of key symbiotic bacteria, can disrupt the original microbiota composition, leading to microbiota imbalance and inflammation [24]. Species diversity is assessed through alpha (α) and beta (β) diversity measures. Alpha diversity quantifies the number, abundance, and evenness of species within a sample, focusing on diversity within a habitat without direct comparison. Commonly used alpha diversity indices include Chao1, ACE, Shannon, Simpson, Goods-coverage, and PD whole tree, with the PD whole tree index assessing diversity based on the phylogenetic characteristics of OTU sequences [25]. Higher microbial diversity reflects a greater variety of microorganisms within the ecosystem. Within a certain range, increased alpha diversity is associated with better physical health. However, elevated alpha diversity is also observed in some chronic inflammatory diseases, indicating the presence of infection and increased pathogen abundance [23, 26, 27]. For instance, a study by Liu et al. found significantly higher Chao1, observed OTU, and PD whole tree indices in patients with cheilitis granulomatosa (CG) compared to healthy controls [28]. This suggests greater bacterial richness and evenness in saliva samples from CG patients, potentially reflecting differences in bacterial evolution related to the condition. Regarding the α diversity of the salivary microbiota after PPI usage, few studies have explored it in a self-control study. Mishiro et al. found that the Shannon diversity index of the salivary microbiota significantly decreased after PPI usage compared to before in healthy individuals [28]. However, in our study, we observed that the PD_ whole_ tree index of the salivary microbiota increased in LPR patients after 8-week PPI therapy compared to before, suggesting a potential alteration in the diversity after PPI use in LPR considering OTU sequence evolutionary tree, or implying more phylogenetic differences in the salivary microbiota after PPI treatment in LPR patients. The differences in alpha bacterial diversity observed in these studies may be attributed to variations in the research subjects under investigation. It is known that oral pathogenic bacteria can translocate to the gastrointestinal tract through hematogenous and enteral routes, indicating a potential link between increased oral microbiota diversity and the development of gastrointestinal diseases such as gastrointestinal tumorigenesis [29, 30]. Therefore, the increased alpha diversity of the salivary microbiota after PPIs therapy may have certain health influence for LPR patients, potentially increasing the risk of gastrointestinal complications.

Another method used to evaluate species diversity is beta diversity, which compares diversity differences between groups. It utilizes evolutionary relationships and abundance information between sample sequences to calculate distances between samples, reflecting whether there are differences in microbial communities between groups. Common analysis methods include (un) weighted UniFrac analysis, PCoA, etc. Previous research has indicated a significant difference in the salivary bacterial community, distinct from feces and periodontal pocket fluid samples, between PPI users and non-users in healthy volunteers, as demonstrated by PCoA analysis using weighted or unweighted UniFrac methods [28]. Similarly, our results also revealed statistically significant differences in unweighted UniFrac distances by PCoA analysis of the microbiota structure of saliva samples in LPR patients after PPI treatment compared to before. These findings suggest that PPI usage may alter the salivary bacterial community, regardless of whether individuals have LPR or are healthy.

Campylobacter, which is sensitive to pH levels, belongs to the phylum Proteobacteria and class ε-Proteobacteria. It is recognized as the most prevalent intestinal pathogen responsible for bacterial gastroenteritis in developed countries [31]. Meta-analyses have demonstrated an increased risk of community-acquired intestinal infections following PPI use, particularly associated with Campylobacter [32]. Consequently, Campylobacter is linked to one of the adverse effects of PPIs, bacterial gastroenteritis. The underlying mechanism may be attributed to several factors. Gastric acid production is significantly diminished in patients taking PPIs due to the inhibition of H+/K + -ATPase activity, allowing oral-derived bacteria that are sensitive to low pH, including Campylobacter, to survive and translocate into the intestine. Subsequently, Campylobacter jejuni exploits its unique structural features such as a robust flagellum and helical shape to swiftly traverse the intestinal mucus layer and invade epithelial cells, thereby overcoming colonization resistance and establishing infection [33]. Furthermore, PPI usage has been shown to reduce neutrophil antibacterial activity [34], and adversely affect intestinal probiotics that help suppress pathogens [35], which potentially facilitate Campylobacter infections. In this study, we conducted a taxon-based analysis revealing that PPI administration led to an increase in the abundance of Epsilonproteobacteria, Campylobacterales, Campylobacteraceae, Campylobacter and Campylobacter gracilis while simultaneously reducing Lactobacillaceae and Lactobacillus abundance in salivary samples from LPR patients. This alteration may result from changes in oral pH levels among LPR patients following PPI treatment. It suggests that for LPR patients on PPIs, translocation of oral-derived pathogens such as Campylobacter into the intestine could be exacerbated. Overall, our findings indicate that 8 weeks of PPI treatment induced oral dysbiosis in LPR patients characterized by overgrowths of pathogenic bacteria like Campylobacter and Campylobacter gracilis alongside a decrease in probiotic populations such as Lactobacillus, potentially heightening the risk for adverse effects like enteric infections. However, the precise mechanisms driving these changes remain unclear.

Given the structural similarity of proton pumps in bacteria and human tissue [36], PPIs may not only inhibit gastric acid secretion by gastric parietal cells but also affect the growth of symbiotic bacteria in the human body [35]. Specifically, acid-producing bacteria such as Streptococci and Lactobacilli are found in both the oral cavity and gastrointestinal tract [37–39], potentially serving as secondary targets for PPIs [36]. H +-exchange pumps have been identified in rat and cockroach salivary glands [40–42], and the H+/K+-ATPase is expressed in human laryngeal submucosal glands, mucinous glands, salivary glands, and tissues of the sinus and tongue [36, 43, 44]. It is believed that non-gastric H+/K+-ATPases play a role in maintaining K + homeostasis in K + and Na+-deficient environments [45]. Therefore, the mechanism for alterations in UAT microbiota caused by PPI administration in LPR patients may involve the following processes: Firstly, PPIs might directly act on the H+/K+-ATPases of bacteria in the UAT, leading to dysbiosis of the UAT microbiota by reducing the population of acid-producing bacteria (such as lactobacillus and Lactobacillus murinus) and increasing the abundance of non-acid-producing bacteria (such as Campylobacter and Campylobacter_gracilis). Secondly, PPIs may reduce local acidity by inhibiting H+/K+-ATPases in UAT tissues, thereby altering the UAT environment and indirectly affecting the UAT microbiota.

Functional analysis using PICRUSt2 revealed significant alterations in the proportion of genes responsible for various biological pathways in the salivary microbiota of LPR patients after PPI therapy. Specifically, the proportion of genes involved in indole alkaloid biosynthesis was significantly increased after PPI therapy. Indole alkaloids are secondary metabolites produced by microorganisms, exhibiting various biological functions including antibacterial, antiviral, and antifungal activities [46]. The upregulation of genes involved in indole alkaloid biosynthesis may indicate a compensatory response from the human microbiota, potentially leading to the production of more defensive molecules in response to adverse events caused by an increase in pathogenic microflora following PPI use. Further exploration of secondary metabolites from salivary flora, such as indole alkaloids, could aid in the development of new therapeutic agents aimed at preventing and treating PPI-related side effects.

Despite the significant findings of our study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, the diagnosis of LPR patients was based on therapeutic trial with PPIs, which may introduce subjectivity. Secondly, only 32 patients were recruited in our study, which might affect the generalizability of the findings, especially when considering the diversity of salivary microbiomes. Lastly, our study was conducted at a single center, and multicenter studies are necessary to validate our findings.

In conclusion, our study revealed significant alterations in the composition of salivary microbiota after PPI administration in LPR patients. These changes may contribute to the complications associated with PPI therapy. Therefore, when treating LPR with PPIs, clinicians should consider the potential side effects of long-term PPI use, including microbiota dysbiosis and associated health risks. Further research is warranted to elucidate the specific mechanisms underlying dysbiosis of the oral microbiota in LPR patients following PPI therapy.
