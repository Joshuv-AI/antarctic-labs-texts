Mechanisms of AFB1-induced neurotoxicity and protective effect of baicalin via inflammatory and antioxidant pathways in ducklings
Authors: Pu Guo, Xinlan Ke, Hualin Zhou, Jie Zhang, Qirong Lu, Yu Liu, Chun Ye, Shulin Fu, Yinsheng Qiu, Zhongyuan Wu, Jianglin Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAflatoxin B1 (AFB1) ranks among the most hazardous contaminants in the food chain. While its hepatotoxicity is well-documented, its potential threat to the nervous system and the underlying mechanisms remain a critical but understudied toxicological issue. This study was designed to systematically investigate the neurotoxic mechanisms of AFB1 and the protective intervention of baicalin, a natural flavonoid. Using an AFB1-intubated duckling model, we provided histological and TEM in vivo evidence that AFB1 can induce substantial damage in the cerebral cortex which demonstrated that AFB1 exposure caused severe histopathological and ultrastructural alterations. IHC quantification revealed AFB1 ∼5-40 fold activated the expression of NF-κB inflammatory, and led to the upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), and simultaneously compensatory elevation of Nrf2 expression, resulting in a sharp increase in oxidative stress. Importantly, pretreatment with baicalin dose-dependently ameliorated these injuries. It effectively inhibited NF-κB activation, attenuated neuroinflammation, and suppressed the Nrf2 signaling to combat oxidative damage. Our study provides novel evidence in an in vivo duckling model that baicalin exerts its neuroprotective effects in association with reciprocal alterations between the NF-κB and Nrf2 signaling axes. These findings not only fill a significant knowledge gap in AFB1-induced neurotoxicity but also provide a solid mechanistic foundation for developing baicalin as a natural-based detoxifying strategy to mitigate AFB1 contamination, thereby enhancing safety in animal husbandry and human food consumption.Keywords: Aflatoxin B1, Baicalin, Oxidative stress, Neuroinflammation, Duckling model

Introduction

Aflatoxin B1 (AFB1) is a secondary metabolite produced by fungi such as Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus and one of the most common and most potent toxins (Li et al., 2022a). AFB1 poses a serious threat to global food safety because it heavily contaminates feed materials. Extensive reports have documented AFB1 contamination in a variety of foods and crops, including soybeans, dairy products, and cereals (especially peanuts and walnuts). Recent advances in aflatoxin detection technologies have significantly improved the sensitivity and efficiency of food-safety monitoring (Guo, et al., 2026). The European Union regulations on animal feed (Commission Directive 2003/100/EC, amending Directive 2002/32/EC) have established maximal limit for AFB1 in feed materials and complete feedstuffs: 10 µg/kg in all feed materials, and 20 µg/kg in complete feedstuffs for cattle, pigs, and poultry (excluding young animals) (EU, 2023). These limits apply to feed but not to food for human consumption. However, actual contamination often exceeds these standards. A nationwide survey of 23,003 feed samples from 2021 to 2024 showed that the AFB1 contamination rate ranged 20% to 100%, with 9.7% of raw materials and 3.5% of complete feeds exceeding the standards (Liu, et al., 2025). Such widespread excessive contamination means livestock are chronically exposed to AFB1, posing serious risks to their health, productivity, and ultimately food safety.

Excessive AFB1 contamination leads to long-term chronic exposure in livestock, resulting in growth retardation that reduces feed conversion rate, average daily gain, egg production rate, and survival rate (Olariu, et al., 2025). A meta-analysis of broiler studies reported that AFB1-contaminated feed reduced final body weight by approximately 15% and significantly increased feed conversion ratio (Putra, et al., 2024), thereby leading to substantial economic losses for the animal husbandry industry. Sporadic evidence from zebrafish models suggests that AFB1 can cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and cause neurobehavioral abnormalities (Wang, et al., 2023b). Once across the BBB, AFB1 can directly affect glial cells and alter the activation state of microglia and astrocytes, thereby promoting brain exaggerated inflammatory responses, reactive oxygen species production, mitochondrial dysfunction and further disrupting BBB integrity (Song, et al., 2024b; Zhang, et al., 2022). A recent systematic study in ducks provided critical experimental evidence that demonstrated that AFB1 disrupts BBB integrity by downregulating ZO-1 and Occludin, which induces inflammation and PANoptosis in brain tissue, and ultimately leads to neuronal necrosis and degeneration (Zhou, et al., 2026b). Existing evidence has thus begun to reveal the neurotoxic potential of AFB1.

Recent immunological insights have further highlighted the critical role of immune-related pathways in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including neurotoxicity (Zhou, et al., 2026a). However, most studies have focused on general behavioral observations or in vitro cellular mechanisms, but systematic investigations specifically targeting cognitive, behavioral, or comprehensive neurological endpoints remain largely absent. For example, an in vivo study exposing adult male Wistar rats to extremely low doses of AFB1 (25 μg/kg/week, 8 weeks orally) found that it induced abnormal neurological behaviors, including anxiety- and depression-like behaviors, along with brain tissue damage (Aytekin Sahin, et al., 2022), which may be related to hypothalamic dysfunction. Another in vitro study in primary microglia showed that AFB1 and aflatoxin FM1 (0∼31.2 ng/mL) activated NLRP3/Gasdermin D-mediated pyroptosis, causing dose-dependent cell death, necrosis, and apoptosis (Zhang, et al., 2022). Although these studies suggest that the brain may be an important potential target of AFB1, research on AFB1 neurotoxicity remains limited compared with in-depth research on organs such as the liver. It remains unclear if AFB1 directly induces structural damage in the cerebral cortex and if such damage is mediated through the Nuclear Factor kappa B (NF‑κB) inflammatory pathway and the Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor (Nrf2) oxidative stress response. Furthermore, whether natural product‑based interventions can attenuate AFB1‑induced neurotoxicity has not yet been explored.

As AFB1 pollution has many negative effects on the economic benefits of the livestock industry, researchers have been looking for ways to prevent excessive economic losses from AFB1 toxins. In terms of detoxification strategies, natural small molecule substances show potential. Plant‑derived antioxidants have been widely investigated as natural feed additives capable of improving antioxidant and immune functions in poultry (Liu, et al., 2023), and the medicinal value of phytochemical-based interventions has been widely recognized in recent years (Bagheri et al., 2024). Among them, the flavonoid compound baicalin derived from the dried root of Scutellaria baicalensis has attracted increasing attention as a natural antidote, and it has been proven to inhibit hepatocyte apoptosis (Wen, et al., 2024) and alleviate oxidative stress (Li, et al., 2022b). Baicalin has shown neuroprotective effects in some models, which provides a beneficial clue for whether baicalin can directly target the neurotoxic effects of AFB1. For example, in the pseudorabies virus model, it exhibits properties that inhibit viral replication and reduce reactive oxygen species production, suggesting that the nervous system may be protected indirectly through antioxidant pathways (Niu, et al., 2024). Neuroprotective effects are also shown in models such as Alzheimer's disease and cerebral ischemia (Putteeraj, et al., 2018). However, the exact neuroprotective mechanism under AFB1 exposure still needs experimental verification.

We hypothesize that AFB1 induces cortical damage via the NF-κB inflammatory and Nrf2 oxidative stress pathways, and that baicalin attenuates this injury by modulating both signaling axes. Meanwhile, the biological relevance of ducklings as an experimental model for studying AFB1 toxicity has been well documented (Bidura, et al., 2024). Therefore, our study uses a duckling model and aims to comprehensively utilize molecular biological technology (including histopathological analysis), systematically analyze the complex toxicity network and mechanism of action of AFB1 (with particular attention to its neurotoxicity), and focus on clarifying the intervention mechanism of baicalin, thereby providing a scientific basis for the effective prevention and control of AFB1 toxins and the development of detoxification strategies.

Materials and methods

Reagents and chemicals

AFB1 toxin (CAS: MSS1003) was purchased from Pribolab (Qingdao, China), Baicalin (CAS: BP0233) was purchased from Purifa (Chengdu, China), and Methyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was purchased from Solarbio (Beijing, China).

AFB1 toxin was dissolved in DMSO and subsequently diluted with physiological saline (final DMSO: 0.024%, v/v); the baicalin was dissolved in saline.

Animals

This study utilized 1-day-old Laced Meat duckings from Changfeng Hatchery in Xiaonan District, Xiaogan City, Hubei Province, People's Republic of China. The research was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Wuhan Polytechnic University (Approval No. WPU202404003). The ducklings were housed 2 per cage. Heating was maintained by heat lamps to ensure an indoor temperature above 32°C. During both the pre-feeding and experimental periods, all treatment groups received the same basal diet. From days 1 to 3 post-hatching, all ducks were fed identical basic feed (Fig. 1). On day 4, the ducks were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

Schematic diagram of the overall experimental design in ducklings.

Experimental Design

For the time-course study, 50 ducklings with similar body weights were randomly divided into 5 groups of 10 ducklings each, including a control group. The 4 treatment group were: 1) AFB1 exposure group (model group); 2) AFB1 exposure and administration of 25 mg/kg b.w. baicalin (25 mg/kg b.w. baicalin group); 3) AFB1 exposure and administration of 50 mg/kg b.w. baicalin group (50 mg/kg b.w. baicalin group) and 4) AFB1 exposure and administration of 100 mg/kg b.w. baicalin group (100 mg/kg b.w. baicalin group). The AFB1 dose (12 μg/kg body weight/ per day) was selected based on previous studies (Zhang, et al., 2016; Ma, et al., 2024; Lu, et al., 2025) and the Chinese feed hygiene standard-15 μg/kg (GB 13078-2017, 2018) and these baicalin doses were selected based on Lu, et al. (2025).

On day 1 of the experiment, the ducklings in the baicalin groups received a pre-administration of their respective baicalin dose via oral gavage based on equal volume per body weight (1 mL/200 g b.w.). The control and model groups received the saline-DMSO vehicle only. This pre-treatment continued for 3 d consecutively. On day 4, the control group received physiological saline only, while the model group and all baicalin groups received AFB1 administered once daily for 7 d consecutively. After daily feeding, the ducklings were observed for feeding and drinking behavior, general activity, and mental state. Daily behavioral monitoring was performed by 2 independent observers who were blinded to the experimental groups.

Histopathological analysis

All histopathological tests were performed using standard laboratory procedures. At age 11 d, all ducklings were dissected to collect cortical brain tissue. Appropriately sized tissue blocks were immediately fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution. The fixed tissues were then processed for paraffin embedding. The resulting paraffin blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were floated on a 40°C water bath to smooth the tissue, transferred onto glass slides, and placed in an oven for deparaffinization. The sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), followed by dehydration. Mounting was performed using neutral balsam to prepare the H&E-stained slides. Histopathological changes in the cerebral cortex were examined under an Olympus BX-43 optical microscope. Images were captured and analyzed to evaluate tissue morphology.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

Fresh cortical brain tissues were trimmed into pieces smaller than 1 mm³ by using a razor blade. The samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature and stored at 4°C overnight. Post-fixation was performed with 1% osmium tetroxide at 4°C for 2 h, followed by washing with ice-cold double-distilled water. Dehydration was then carried out using a graded acetone series. Thereafter, the tissues were infiltrated with resin, embedded, and sectioned into approximately 50 nm-thick slices using an ultramicrotome. After dual staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, the sections were observed under a transmission electron microscope.

Immunohistochemical analysis

The cerebral cortex tissues were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. The fixed tissues were then processed for paraffin embedding, and the resulting blocks were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval. After inactivation of endogenous peroxidases, the sections were blocked with 5% BSA. They were then incubated with the primary antibodies (1:200) anti NF κB p65 (10745-1, Proteintech Group, Inc), anti Nrf2 (Novus Biologicals Novus Biologicals), anti TNF-α (60291-1, Proteintech Group, Inc), anti IL-6 (21865-1, Proteintech Group, Inc), and anti IL-1β (26048-1, Proteintech Group, Inc) at 4°C overnight, followed by incubation with species-matched secondary antibodies. Afterwards, 3,3′-diaminobenzidine working solution was applied to the sections and the sections were incubated at room temperature in the dark for chromogenic development. Counterstaining was performed using hematoxylin staining solution. After dehydration and mounting, the slides were allowed to dry before examination under a microscope.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data was expressed as the mean ± SD. The difference between 2 groups was analyzed using the 2-tailed Student’s t-test. Compared with the control group, *=p < 0.05 to indicate a significant difference, whereas **=p < 0.01 to indicate a highly significant difference. Compared with the model group, #= p < 0.05 to indicate a significant difference, whereas ##=p < 0.01 to indicate a highly significant difference.

Results

Altered mental status

Throughout the experiment, the ducklings exhibited a progression in mental state from calmness to hyperexcitability and agitation, eventually leading to depression. Prior to the experiment, the ducklings displayed normal feeding and drinking behavior. However, 1 day after AFB1 administration, The ducklings in the experimental groups began to show abnormal psychiatric symptoms such as pronounced hyperexcitability. Specifically, these ducklings became noticeably more startled, frequently flapping their wings, running recklessly, falling, and displaying exaggerated responses to touch. This altered mental state persisted for 3 days. On day 4, the ducklings became lethargic, fatigued, and reluctant to move. By the end of the experiment, they consistently exhibited lowered heads, retracted necks, and markedly reduced responsiveness.

Histological evaluation

Distinct histopathological changes were observed in the cerebral cortex of the AFB1-treated group (Fig. 2). In the control ducklings, brain cells appeared normal with uniformly stained nuclei and intact cytoplasmic structure (Fig. 2A). Compared with the control group, the model group exhibited significant nuclear hyperchromasia, suggesting possible apoptosis of cortical cells. Prominent inflammatory responses were observed, accompanied by extensive perivascular edema (Fig. 2B). The low-dose baicalin group showed noticeable inflammatory reactions, including nuclear hyperchromasia, with the presence of multiple inflammatory lesions (Fig. 2C). In the medium-dose baicalin group, moderate nuclear hyperchromasia was observed, indicative of early inflammatory stage, along with abundant leukocytes and neutrophils (Fig. 2D). The high-dose baicalin group displayed mild nuclear hyperchromasia, significantly alleviated inflammation, and minimal perivascular edema (Fig. 2E).

Transmission electron microscopy evaluation

Transmission electron microscopy revealed severe ultrastructural damage in the cerebral cortex of 4-day-old ducklings in the model group compared with ducklings in the control group (Fig. 3). Specifically, neuronal cell membranes were discontinuous or even ruptured, nuclear membranes appeared shrunken and invaginated, chromatin was condensed and marginalized, mitochondria were swollen with disrupted cristae and vacuolation, and the rough endoplasmic reticulum exhibited dilation and degranulation. In contrast, pretreatment with low, medium, and high concentrations of baicalin resulted in a dose-dependent alleviation of these ultrastructural alterations. TEM images showed that neuronal and nuclear membranes became more intact, chromatin distribution became more uniform, mitochondrial swelling was reduced with partial recovery of cristae, and the structural integrity of the rough endoplasmic reticulum was improved with increasing baicalin concentration. These results confirm that baicalin dose-dependently mitigates AFB1-induced inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and associated ultrastructural damage in the cerebral cortex of ducklings.

Immunohistochemical evaluation

The expression of proteins related to inflammatory response and oxidative stress was significantly increased in the cerebral cortex of 4-day-old ducklings in all AFB1-treated groups (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6, Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Compared with the control group, compensatory up-regulation occurred with the expression of inflammatory response-related proteins (TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-1β), the key regulatory factor NF-κB, and the oxidative stress-related protein Nrf2 were in the cerebral cortex of ducklings in the AFB1-challenged model group. Increases in expression were 5–40-fold for inflammatory markers and ∼9.4-fold for Nrf2 (all p < 0.05).

Immunohistochemical observations revealed an increase in the number of positively stained cells and enhanced staining intensity for these proteins in the model group. Brownish-yellow granules were predominantly distributed in the cytoplasm and partially on the cell membrane, with a significant expansion of the positively stained area.

In contrast, treatment with low, medium, and high concentrations of baicalin dose-dependently down-regulated the expression of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, Nrf2, and NF-κB in the cerebral cortex. Expression in the 100 mg/kg baicalin group was not significantly different from expression in the control group. In the baicalin treatment groups, the number of positive cells gradually decreased, staining intensity weakened, and positive area reduced with increasing baicalin concentration. These results indicate that baicalin alleviates AFB1-induced inflammatory response and oxidative stress in the cerebral cortex of ducklings in a dose-dependent manner.

Discussion

The interplay between oxidative stress and inflammatory pathways via the NF‑κB/Nrf2 axes has been increasingly recognized as a critical mechanism in various pathological conditions (Huang, et al., 2025). Beyond classical cytokine signaling, inflammation‑associated molecular regulation during neural injury also involves a broader range of mediators (Zeng, et al., 2022). However, AFB1’s direct neurotoxic effects and the associated in vivo signaling within the brain remain largely unexplored. In our study, AFB1 induced significant ultrastructural and pathological damage to duckling brain tissue, accompanied by neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in association with alterations in the NF‑κB and Nrf2 signaling pathways. Baicalin treatment effectively counteracted this process. HE staining revealed morphological features suggestive of apoptotic cell death, including nuclear hyperchromasia and pyknosis, and TEM observations demonstrated pronounced mitochondrial swelling with cristae disruption and vacuolation, indicating that mitochondrial dysfunction is a prominent feature of AFB1‑induced cerebral cortex injury. These findings collectively identify the nervous system as a critical target of AFB1 toxicity and highlight the neuroprotective potential of baicalin, systematically elucidating the toxic mechanism of AFB1 and its intervention by baicalin.

As the most toxic and widely distributed aflatoxin subtype, the problem of AFB1 contamination in feed is becoming increasingly serious. In Iraq, a survey of poultry feed (n = 60) found 100% co-occurrence of AFB1 and FB1, with 38.33% of samples exceeding the European Union limit of 20 μg/kg in poultry feed (Alnaemi and Algwari, 2023). In Serbia, 40.5% of 548 maize samples exceeded the European Union AFB1 limit in 2022, with the highest concentration exceeding the limit by 26.4 times (Penagos-Tabares, et al., 2025). In Brazil, a survey of 1,749 cattle feed samples during 2017–2021 found that 97% contained at least 1 mycotoxin, with an AFB1 detection rate of 59% (Biscoto, et al., 2022).In eastern China, 9,392 feed and ingredient samples tested in 2017–2021 sad an AFB1 positive rate of 82.61% and an average concentration of 103.08 μg/kg, far above the EU limit (Hao, et al., 2023). These data consistently demonstrate that AFB1 contamination of feed has become a global challenge to animal husbandry, with widespread contamination and severe exceedances in some regions. Furthermore, AFB1 can enter the human body through the feed → animal → animal-derived food pathway (meat, eggs, milk), posing an indirect food safety risk (Khatoon A, 2025).

Based on the global AFB1 contamination status and the EU regulatory limits for AFB1 in feed, our study selected an appropriate concentration to infect ducklings. The oral LD₅₀ of AFB1 in ducks is 0.24–0.30 mg/kg body weight (Ma, et al., 2024). By simulating a real exposure scenario through 7 d consecutively of AFB1 gavage, our study selected a dose of 12 μg/kg b.w./d. This dose is below the LD₅₀, which avoids acute lethal effects, while the 7‑d exposure period, as evidenced by our histopathological and ultrastructural findings, is sufficient to induce significant and detectable neuropathological alterations in the duckling model.

AFB1 is well known for its acute and chronic toxicity, including hepatotoxicity, growth retardation, and immunosuppression (Su, et al., 2023; Li, et al., 2024; Choi, et al., 2025). However, systematic research on its neurotoxic mechanism remains limited, even though widespread feed contamination poses a threat to livestock health and production performance, and may further affect human food safety through animal-derived products. To address this gap, our study used ducks—which are highly sensitive to AFB1 (Wang, et al., 2023a) —as a model to investigate AFB1 neurotoxicity and the protective potential of baicalin, and confirmed that AFB1 directly causes brain tissue damage at histopathological, ultrastructural, and molecular levels. We found that even at doses below the LD₅₀, ducklings still exhibited significant neurological dysfunction and structural abnormalities, confirming that AFB1 has non-negligible neurotoxicity. Notably, significant inflammatory responses and morphological features suggestive of apoptosis (e.g., nuclear hyperchromasia and pyknosis) were observed in the cerebral cortex. These findings fill the gap in in vivo research on AFB1 neurotoxicity and provide direct experimental evidence for assessing its potential risks to human health.

Natural active ingredients have attracted much attention due to their multi-target and low-toxicity advantages. Several natural compounds have been reported to intervene in AFB1 induced neurotoxicity. Among these, hesperidin has been shown to protect hippocampal neurons from AFB1 induced neurotoxicity in mice (Song, et al., 2024a), while gallic acid combined with azithromycin alleviated neurobehavioral deficits in rats chronically exposed to AFB1 (Owumi, et al., 2025). Despite these promising findings, most existing evidence on natural compounds against AFB1‑induced neurotoxicity remains confined to rodent models and focuses predominantly on hippocampal pathology, leaving the protective effects in avian central nervous systems largely unexplored. In poultry models, Khashan et al. (2025) reported that nano chitosan partially improved performance and restored hematological parameters in broilers exposed to AFB1, yet its effects were attributed to mycotoxin binding rather than neuroprotection. Similarly, Koynarski et al. (2025) investigated AFB1 induced immune suppression in Moulard ducks, focusing on humoral parameters rather than neural outcomes. Unlike these strategies targeting growth performance or immune parameters, our study provides evidence that baicalin directly protects against AFB1‑induced structural damage in the cerebral cortex via NF‑κB/Nrf2 modulation, highlighting its unique neuroprotective potential among natural anti‑AFB1 interventions in avian models.

Given these anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties (Zheng, et al., 2021; Qiu, et al., 2025; Yu, et al., 2025), baicalin was selected to investigate whether it could regulate the central inflammatory and oxidative stress network against AFB1 challenge. The elevated Nrf2 in the AFB1 group reflects a compensatory response insufficient to prevent damage; its reduction after baicalin treatment indicates alleviated oxidative stress. Baicalin treatment significantly reversed AFB1 induced ultrastructural damage in brain tissue and reduced inflammatory factor expression by regulating the NF‑κB/Nrf2 pathway, thereby alleviating neural damage. This finding complements previous studies that focused on the hepatoprotective effects of various natural compounds, including baicalin, against AFB1 induced liver injury (Pauletto, et al., 2021; Zhang, et al., 2024; Lu, et al., 2025), and fills an important gap by elucidating baicalin’s neuroprotective mechanism. Thus, our work lays a foundation for developing natural detoxification strategies against AFB1 induced neurotoxicity, with practical implications for healthy animal husbandry and food safety. Further investigations into its broader pharmacological network will facilitate the optimization of such strategies for sustainable animal production.

Despite these significant findings, the present study has certain limitations that warrant consideration. Although HE staining revealed morphological features suggestive of apoptosis, confirmatory molecular markers such as TUNEL and Caspase-3 were not examined. Moreover, behavioral and cognitive endpoints were not formally assessed since the primary objective was to establish foundational histopathological evidence of AFB1-induced brain damage. Furthermore, the 7-d exposure period represents a subacute rather than chronic model, and the observed changes may not fully reflect long-term neurotoxic effects. While our IHC data demonstrated correlations between baicalin treatment and alterations in NF-κB/Nrf2 signaling, direct mechanistic interactions between these pathways were not established without specific inhibitors or genetic approaches. In addition, formal equivalence testing was not performed to confirm restoration to control levels. Future studies should incorporate molecular assays (e.g., TUNEL, Caspase-3), behavioral tests (e.g., open field, novel object recognition), extended exposure durations, pathway-specific inhibitors, and equivalence testing to definitively validate the neuroprotective mechanisms of baicalin against AFB1-induced neurotoxicity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically revealed for in vivo level the neurotoxic mechanisms of AFB1-induced brain damage, including inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and morphological features suggestive of apoptosis. From a signaling pathway perspective, this study confirmed that NF-κB and Nrf2 are key targets of AFB1-induced neurotoxicity, regulating inflammatory responses and oxidative stress processes, respectively. In addition, the study showed that baicalin effectively counteracts AFB1 neurotoxicity through synergistic regulation of the NF-κB/Nrf2 signaling axis. This finding not only highlights the urgency of researching AFB1 neurotoxicity but also provides a critical theoretical basis for deciphering its cross-barrier toxic effects and developing targeted detoxification strategies, which provides key evidence for a comprehensive understanding of AFB1’s toxicity spectrum and associated health risks, fills the gap in mechanistic research on AFB1 neurotoxicity, emphasizes that its potential neurotoxicity should be highly valued, and at the same time offers an important theoretical and practical foundation for advancing the development of natural detoxification strategies and ensuring food safety. Nevertheless, the absence of confirmatory apoptosis markers, behavioral assessments, and chronic exposure evaluation in the present study should be addressed in future investigations to fully establish baicalin's neuroprotective efficacy.
