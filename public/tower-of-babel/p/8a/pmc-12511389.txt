Association of increased physical activity with greater fat mass loss following metabolic bariatric surgery: A longitudinal observational study
Authors: Han Na Jang, Young Suk Park, Sung Hee Choi, Young Min Cho, Hak Chul Jang, Tae Jung Oh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) is an effective treatment for weight loss, but a significant proportion of patients undergoing MBS experience weight regain or muscle loss. Exercise is recommended for patients undergoing MBS, but inconsistent outcomes have been reported on the effect of exercise on weight loss. This study investigated the association between increased physical activity and the changes in weight and body composition of Korean subjects with obesity after MBS. This study prospectively analyzed 78 subjects who underwent MBS from April 2019 to April 2022. The levels of physical activity were assessed using self-report questionnaires before and one year after MBS. Physical activity-related energy expenditure (MET-min/week) was calculated by multiplying the frequency, intensity, and duration of activities. Subjects were classified into two groups based on changes in physical activity levels before and after surgery: the MET-non-increased group and the MET-increased group. Body composition was analyzed by bioimpedance analysis. The mean age of the subjects was 41.7 ± 10.2 years, 31 (39.7%) were male, and baseline weight and body mass index (BMI) were 104.6 ± 19.4 kg and 37.7 ± 5.5 kg/m2, respectively. The MET-increased group had a significantly greater reduction in weight, BMI, and fat mass (FM) after MBS compared to the MET-non-increased group (weight, -32.2 ± 13.0 kg vs. -25.6 ± 9.3 kg, P = 0.015; BMI, -11.7 ± 4.5 kg/m2 vs. -9.1 ± 3.0 kg/m2, P = 0.005; FM, -27.0 ± 11.2 kg vs. -19.4 ± 7.5 kg, P = 0.002). However, no significant difference was observed in the change in muscle mass between the two groups. A greater increase in of physical activity and exercise level was associated with a greater reduction in weight (ß = -0.263, P = 0.003) and fat mass (ß = -0.292, P = 0.003) after MBS when age, sex, BMI, HbA1c, and baseline metabolic parameters were adjusted. Increased physical activity after MBS was associated with a greater reduction in weight and body fat. Emphasizing increasing physical activity is a key component for maximizing the benefits of MBS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19383-y.

Introduction

Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) is considered one of the effective treatment modalities for weight loss1, but it is not a panacea for obesity. It has been reported that over 30% of patients undergo revisional MBS due to suboptimal weight loss2,3. Additionally, 1/3 to 1/2 of patients who undergo MBS have been reported to experience a weight regain of 15–25% from their postoperative nadir weight4,5. Moreover, MBS is known to reduce not only total weight and fat but also muscle mass6. Therefore, lifestyle changes, such as exercise, should accompany MBS to optimize outcomes7.

Generally, engaging in exercise for at least 30 min per day is recommended as part of a healthy lifestyle for patients who undergo MBS8. In fact, previous studies have shown that exercise was an important factor for achieving further weight reduction after MBS. However, the additional weight loss attributable to exercise appears to be relatively modest: 3.62 kg according to a meta-analysis of observational studies9 and 1.8 kg according to a meta-analysis of controlled studies10. Furthermore, some studies have found no significant additional weight loss from exercise interventions11,12. Several factors may have contributed to the lack of significant effect of exercise on body weight after MBS, including poor compliance with exercise interventions due to physical limitations such as joint discomfort, pain and reduced energy levels owing to calorie restriction and failure to account for baseline physical activity levels13,14.

Nevertheless, adjuvant exercise after MBS has been proposed as a strategy to address suboptimal weight loss, and weight regain following MBS. Studies involving patients with overweight and obese have demonstrated that exercise can benefit long-term weight loss maintenance after calorie restriction15 and help mitigate the loss of muscle mass associated with weight loss16. Therefore, it is important to assess not only changes in weight but also body composition to fully capture the benefits of exercise following MBS.

In this observational study, we aim to investigate the association between changes in physical activity levels after MBS with weight, anthropometric, body composition, biochemical measurements, and emotional status following MBS. We also aimed to evaluate the association between changes in the level of physical activity and changes in weight and body composition. Unlike previous studies, we consider baseline levels of physical activity, providing important insights into how physical activity can be tailored for individual patients after MBS.

Methods

Study participants

This cohort study was conducted at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH), a tertiary academic hospital. Participants were patients who underwent MBS between April 2019 and April 2022. Eligible participants were adults aged ≥ 19 years with a body mass index (BMI) of ≥ 35 kg/m2, or those with a BMI of ≥ 30 kg/m2 and obesity-related comorbidities such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, obstructive sleep apnea, polycystic ovary syndrome, or type 2 diabetes. Additionally, patients with a BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m2 and poorly controlled blood glucose levels (HbA1c ≥ 7.0%) despite standard medical care were included. Anthropometric, body composition, and chemical measurements were measured and physical activity was evaluated within one months before MBS, and measurements and evaluations were repeated about a year after MBS. Only patients with data on physical activity levels both preoperatively and at one year postoperatively were included in the final analysis. Those who had previously undergone MBS, those who had followed-up loss, and those who had already had sufficient physical activity before MBS (≥ 1200 MET-min/week) were excluded from the study8. From 2–3 days after MBS, subjects were allowed to take small snacks and protein supplements. Foods such as alcohol, sugar sweetened beverage, rice cakes, and dried fruits were restricted. During soft diet period (up to 6 weeks), caloric intake was limited to 500–700 kcal/day, and after transitioning to regular diet, 1,000–1,400 kcal/day was allowed. We just provided a general guideline for exercise but did not offer supervised programs.

Evaluation of physical activity

The level of physical activity was assessed using self-reported structured questionnaires based on the 7-day recall method17. The survey evaluated physical activity by asking about the frequency (days per week) of engaging in light-intensity activities (e.g., walking at a slow pace for more than 30 min), moderate-intensity activities (e.g., brisk walking or slow cycling for more than 30 min, tennis, golf, bowling, fitness activities), and vigorous-intensity activities (e.g., jogging or cycling at speeds exceeding 15 km/h for more than 20 min, aerobics, swimming, hiking). Light-intensity activity was assigned 2.9 metabolic equivalent tasks (METs), moderate-intensity activity 4.0 METs, and vigorous-intensity activity 7.0 METs. Total METs associated with physical activity (MET-min/week) were calculated by multiplying the frequency, intensity, and duration. Physical activity levels were categorized as < 500, 500–999, 1000–1499, and ≥ 1500 MET-min/week18. Based on these categories, subjects whose levels of physical activity increased after surgery were classified as ‘MET-increased’, while those whose levels remained the same or decreased were classified as ‘MET-non-increased’. These two groups were then compared.

Anthropometric, body composition, and biochemical measurements

Trained research nurses conducted the anthropometric measurements. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by height (m) squared. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the margin of the lowest rib and the iliac crest. Blood pressure (BP) was measured using an electronic BP monitor after 10 min of rest in a sitting position. Fat mass (FM), visceral fat area (VFA)19, and muscle mass were measured using bioimpedance analysis (InBody770, InBody, Seoul, Korea) after urination, and the water intake was restricted before the assessment.

Blood tests were conducted after an overnight fast. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured using the hexokinase method, and HbA1c levels were measured using high-performance liquid chromatography (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). Total cholesterol, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays. Insulin levels were measured using an immunoradiometric assay (DIAsource, Nivelles, Belgium). The homeostatic model assessment insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated by dividing the product of fasting insulin (μIU/ml) and FPG (mg/dL) by 40520. The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease Equation21. Subjects’ depressive symptoms were assessed using the self-administered Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ)−9, with a score of ≥ 10 classified as having depression22.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation if normally distributed, and as median [interquartile range] if not normally distributed. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to verify the normality of the variables. Changes in body composition and anthropometric parameters after MBS between the MET-increased and MET-non-increased groups were compared using Student’s t-test for normally distributed continuous variables, the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the Chi-squared test for categorical variables. Additionally, the MET-non-increased group and the MET-increased group were compared by stratifying by sex, age (< 40 vs. ≥ 40 years), presence of diabetes, and BMI (< 35 vs. ≥ 35 kg/m2). Effect size (Cohen’s d) was calculated to analyze differences between groups. Correlation analyses between changes in body composition and anthropometric parameters and changes in METs after surgery were conducted using multiple regression analysis. Covariates included age, sex, baseline BMI, HbA1c, and baseline value of each body composition, anthropometric parameters to be analyzed based on clinical relevance and univariate analysis. P-values of less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. SPSS for Windows (version 27.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analyses.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of SNUBH (No. B-1812–513-302). All participants provided written informed consent.

Correlation between changes in physical activity levels and improvements in body composition and anthropometric parameters

The relationship between changes in body composition and anthropometric parameters and changes in the levels of physical activity was analyzed. In simple regression analysis, changes in weight, BMI, waist circumference, FM, and VFA were significantly negatively correlated with changes in levels of physical activity. (Fig. 1). In multiple regression analysis, changes in weight (β = −0.263, P = 0.003), BMI (β = −0.253, P = 0.009) and waist circumference (β = −0.314, P = 0.002) were significantly correlated with changes in the levels of physical activity. Additionally, changes in FM (β = −0.292, P = 0.003) and VFA (β = −0.364, P = 0.003) were also significantly correlated with these changes. Discussion

In this study, the group that increased its levels of physical activity after MBS experienced significantly greater reductions in weight, BMI and waist circumference compared to the group that did not increase its physical activity levels. Additionally, the group with increased levels of physical activity showed significantly greater reductions in FM and VFA, while there was no significant difference in changes in muscle mass between the two groups. Increases in physical activity levels were significantly associated with decreases in weight, BMI, waist circumference, FM, and VFA. Moreover, this beneficial effect of increased physical activity was more profound in subgroups of men, those with young age, and those with higher BMI.

This study differs from previous research by examining changes in weight and FM in relation to alterations in physical activity levels from baseline after surgery. Unlike intervention studies that assess the impact of vigorous exercise after MBS, this observational study focuses on the effects of any change (increase or non-increase) in physical activity. Individuals with obesity often find exercise more challenging and typically have lower activity levels compared to those without obesity23,24. The results of this study suggest that simply increasing the amount of physical activity after MBS, regardless of intensity, can lead to additional reductions in weight and FM. Other study that analyzed body composition according to changes in the levels of physical activity for two years after MBS have similarly reported that greater excess weight loss and greater increases in fat-free mass are induced25.

In the current study, increased levels of physical activity after MBS were significantly associated with the reduction of weight, BMI, waist circumference, FM, and VFA after MBS. Exercise stimulates muscle protein synthesis and induces muscle hypertrophy, which helps attenuate the loss of muscle mass due to a hypocaloric diet26. Generally, patients who undergo MBS rapidly lose weight, which drives them into catabolic state. Therefore, maintaining muscle mass after MBS can be challenging. Nevertheless, previous studies have reported that engaging in exercise following MBS can improve muscle strength and physical activity27–29. In our study, subjects who increased their levels of physical activity did not have a significant difference in muscle mass changes compared to subjects whose levels of physical activity did not change, even though they lost more weight. Therefore, muscle mass relative to the total weight loss might be better preserved in subjects in the increased METS group. However, meta-analysis reported that exercise is effective in reducing weight and BMI in patients who underwent MBS, but does not affect fat mass or lean mass, it might be due to the fact that the timing of exercise intervention was different in the meta-analysis study, and that our study included not only exercise but also daily activity30. Future studies should explore the effects of exercise at varying intensities on both muscle mass and different aspects of muscle performance in this population.

Previous studies have reported that factors such as age, depressive symptoms, impulsivity, eating disorders, and marital status are associated with suboptimal weight loss after MBS31–33. In this study, the proportions of smokers and subjects reporting depressive symptoms were relatively higher in the group with no increased in physical activity than the group with increased physical activity. These baseline factors could be associated with suboptimal weight loss. Therefore, patients with depression or unhealthy lifestyle habits who undergo MBS may be at higher risk of not increasing their physical activity, potentially leading to suboptimal weight loss after MBS. It is crucial to emphasize the importance of increasing physical activity for these patients after MBS.

The association between METs improvement and body composition and anthropometric parameter changes was more pronounced in men, younger age, and subjects with severe obesity. Previous studies also reported that men were more likely to benefit from exercise in terms of body weight loss34,35. This may be due to differences in hormonal, appetite, and neuronal responses to physical activity in men and women36,37. Similarly, increasing the metabolic rate through exercise was easier in younger subjects38. Therefore, lifestyle intervention can be tailored based on the subject’s clinical characteristics. This study may provide valuable guidance to candidates for MBS.

This study has several limitations. First, since the levels of physical activity were assessed using self-reported questionnaires, it was not possible to objectively measure participants’ energy expenditure. Previous studies have reported differences in self-reported and accelerometer-based estimates in physical activity before and after MBS, suggesting that subjects receiving MBS tend to overestimate their levels of physical activity after MBS39. Furthermore, although sedentary time might significantly influence post-MBS outcomes, relevant data were not collected in this study. In addition, resting calorie expenditure varies depending on the age, sex, and body composition of the subject, which can affect weight changes after MBS, but this was not considered. Additionally, changes in dietary intake, calories consumed after MBS, and the amount of protein and fat consumed, which could affect weight and body composition, were not investigated. In this study, body composition was measured through bioimpedance analysis, which can vary depending on the amount of body water, and in particular, patients with obesity have relatively high amount of extracellular water and total body water, which can lead to an overestimation of fat-free mass40. Despite these limitations, the study is valuable as it highlights that increasing physical activity after MBS can aid in weight loss and fat reduction which may help prevent weight regain after MBS.

Conclusion

Increases in the level of physical activity after MBS were associated with reductions in weight and body fat, and there is a significant correlation between the increase in physical activity and the reduction in weight and body fat. For greater reduction in weight and body fat after MBS, it is crucial to emphasize the importance of increasing physical activity for patients undergoing MBS.
