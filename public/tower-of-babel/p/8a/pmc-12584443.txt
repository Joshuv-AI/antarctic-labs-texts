Risk factors associated with hepatitis B virus infection among patients attending gastroenterology outpatient department at public hospitals in Northeast Ethiopia: a hospital-based unmatched case-control study
Authors: Minwuyelet Maru Temesgen, Mengistu Legesse, Aklilu Feleke Haile, Birtukan Shiferaw, Tewodros Dessie, Nega Berhe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major global health issue. Ethiopia is among the high endemic areas, with prevalence of infection more than 8%. However, data regarding factors associated with its acquisition in the study area is scarce. Therefore, this study aimed to explain factors associated with acquisition of HBV.MethodsA hospital-based unmatched case–control study was conducted in two government hospitals in Northeast Ethiopia, both of which serve as treatment centers for hepatitis. The source population included adult patients visiting the gastroenterology outpatient departments of these hospitals. A total of 288 participants were selected consecutively from June to December 2023. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire from cases; those with hepatitis B virus infection, or hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positive and controls; those who did not have hepatitis B infection, or HBsAg negative. Data analysis was performed by STATA version 17 for description statistics, bivariate and multivariable logistic regression to evaluate predictable variables for possible risk of HBV infection.ResultsA total of 288 participants (96 cases and 192 controls) were included. The median age of participants was 30 years. The proportion of females was higher among cases than controls (71.8% vs. 52.0%). A higher proportion of cases had multiple lifetime sexual partners (36.5% vs. 26%), a family history of hepatitis (40.6% vs. 20.3%), and a history of tattoos or ear piercing (74% vs. 36%). Multivariable analysis identified multiple sexual partners (AOR = 2.07; 95%CI: 1.07-4.00), traditional practices; tattooing, ear piercing, tonsillectomy; (AOR = 6.43; 95%CI: 3.12–13.23), and family history of hepatitis (AOR = 2.83; 95% CI: 1.48–5.42) as significant risk factors. Regarding age, higher proportion of infection is at age below35 years and a downward trend was noted as age increases. Among the cases, the proportion of females is higher than that of males (71.8 vs. 22.7%; P = 0.001).ConclusionMultiple sexual partners, family history of hepatitis, and traditional practices such as tattooing, ear piercing, and sharing sharps were strongly associated with HBV infection. Public health efforts should specifically target these individuals to address the identified risk factors and halt transmission dynamics. The greater number of cases below 35 years, and females with a decreasing tendency with age may be due to early disease progression and reduced survival rates among older individuals and males. This emphasizes there should be an expansion of early screening, especially for at-risk populations, to limit the progression of HBV.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11970-8.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major global health problem affecting more than two billion people. It is the leading cause of active hepatitis and chronic liver disease, characterized by gradual deterioration of liver function, with a high risk of death from cirrhosis or hepatocellular carcinoma [1]. According to the 2023 World Health Organization (WHO) report approximately 296 million people globally are living with chronic hepatitis B virus infection with 1.2 million new infections each year and responsible for 820,000 deaths annually. The risk of chronic infection varies with age of infection, with infants and young children having the highest risk. Approximately 90% of infected infants, 30–50% of young children, infected between 1 and 5 years and 5–10% adults will remain chronically infected with HBV [2]. Only a few develop serious liver complications, including cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [3].

The prevalence of HBV infection varies in different parts of the world. In hyper endemic areas, such as China, South-east Asia, Western Pacific, and sub-Saharan Africa, the carrier rate exceeds 8% and also the active infection is higher. In endemic areas of Southern Europe, and Latin America at least 8% of the population are chronic carriers of HBV but there is stable transmission and the disease occurs at a constant predictable rate [4]. Around 63% of new hepatitis B infections worldwide occur in Africa and in 19 countries of Africa, more than 8% of the population is infected with Hepatitis B virus [5].

High prevalence of HBV among pregnant women and adult population is reported in many African and Asian countries. In Africa the prevalence of hepatitis B virus among pregnant women varied by country, ranging from 1.9% in Madagascar to 16.1% in Niger [6]. Ethiopia is among the high endemic areas and the prevalence of the infection is high at 8% in the general population [7]. Based on the 2015 survey the overall community sero-prevalence of HBsAg is 9.4% with significant regional variation, highest in the Afar Regional State at 28.8% [8]. These regional differences are related to poor health care access and vaccination coverage.

In the Amhara region, HBV prevalence ranged from 4% to 27.4% [9, 10]. The highest prevalence is found in the eastern part of the region, which may indicate the presence of a hotspot for HBV due to a variety of factors, including proximity to the Afar region, which has the country’s highest infection rate, as well as factors such as low awareness, high-risk traditional practices, and gaps in immunization efforts. Therefore, addressing HBV in the Amhara region is essential for targeted interventions and could serve as a model for broader national strategies.

Perinatal or early childhood transmission accounts for approximately 50% of the chronic cases in high endemic settings (>8% prevalence) contrary to low endemic setting (< 2% prevalence) where transmission is related to sexual exposure [11]. A number of risk factors such as demographic factors, behavioral, cultural, life style and health care service related factors [12] have been shown to be associated with HBV infection. Additionally, various factors, including host immunity, nutritional status, and co-infections with HIV in endemic areas, are frequently reported in studies as contributors to the worsening outcomes of HBV infection. Other key drivers of HBV infection in sub-Saharan African countries have also been identified, providing insights to help prioritize and strengthen HBV control programs. Among these factors, low awareness of the disease plays a significant role in its transmission [13].

In Ethiopia different strategies have been designed by the federal ministry of health (FMoH) to reduce the burden of HBV, including the screening of pregnant women, and providing vaccination for infants and high-risk groups such as health care workers [14]. However, studies indicate that the infection remains highly prevalent, with similar trends observed in the Amhara region [15]. A high prevalence of hepatitis has been reported both among pregnant women and the general population [9, 16]. Despite this, there is limited information on the risk factors contributing to HBV transmission, particularly in the eastern part of the region, where infection rates are notably high. Understanding the determinants of viral transmission in this area is crucial for identifying key risk factors, improving knowledge of transmission dynamics, and enhancing current prevention and control measures. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the factors associated with HBV acquisition.

Methods

Study settings and design

A hospital-based, unmatched case–control study was conducted during the period from June1, 2023 to December 15, 2023 at two government hospitals located in south Wollo zonal administration of Amhara regional state, North east Ethiopia namely Dessie Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, and Akesta general hospital, both of which serve as treatment centers for hepatitis. The treatment centers offer hepatitis screening, using serological tests and PCR-based molecular testing to assess viral load. They provide antiviral treatment for chronic hepatitis B and regularly monitor liver function through blood tests (e.g., ALT, AST) and imaging methods (e.g., ultrasound, elastography) to track disease progression. Alongside medical treatments, the centers also offer counseling to educate patients on the importance of treatment adherence, lifestyle changes, and preventing transmission.

Unmatched case–control design was chosen because it allows for the efficient comparison of cases to controls without requiring a strict one-to-one matching based on specific variables. This approach provides greater flexibility in selecting controls, enhances statistical power by including a larger sample size. The study aimed to evaluate the potential risk factors of HBV transmission among cases and controls in which cases are patients with hepatitis B virus infection (HBsAg positive) and controls are non-hepatic patients with no hepatitis B infection (HBsAg negative) visiting gastroenterology outpatient department (OPD). Data regarding factors associated with hepatitis B infection was collected using structured questionnaires developed for this study (supplementary file).

Study population and study subjects

The study population consisted of all patients visiting gastroenterology department during the study period and selected patients with hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positive and negative test results were our study subjects.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who were tested positive for hepatitis B surface antigen and who were in follow-up treatment for hepatitis B virus were included in the study whereas individuals who had received an HBV vaccine within the past six months and who were critically ill were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was calculated using the double population proportion method in Epi Info 7.2 Stat Calc. This calculation was based on an 80% power, 95% two-sided confidence level, a 6.7% exposure rate in the control group, and an odds ratio of 3.7, based on a study by Desalegn W. et al., from Ethiopia. The study reports that household exposure is a significant risk factor for HBV infection, with a 16.4% prevalence of HBV among household contacts of infected individuals which is also used as a parameter in our study [17]. By taking 1:2 ratio of a case to control and allowing for a non- response rate of 15%, the total sample size was 288 with 96 cases and 192 controls. Cases and controls were selected consecutively as they visited the gastroenterology outpatient department. After medical examination, they were referred to the laboratory for hepatitis testing. Then, the patients returned to OPD with the laboratory result and recruited for the study. Two corresponding controls were selected at the same hospitals where cases were identified to minimize selection bias. They were chosen randomly on the same day as the cases to ensure comparability (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Four nurse data collectors, two from each hospital and two supervisors from Amhara Public Health Institute Dessie Branch (APHI DB) were recruited and trained for the data collection. Once eligibility was confirmed, trained nurse data collectors approached the clients. After obtaining informed consent/assent, a face-to-face interview was conducted using a structured questionnaire to collect data on socio-demographic, potential risk factors, medical history, and behavioral factors associated with HBV infection. The questionnaire was developed based on literatures and before the actual data collection period, pretesting of the questionnaire was conducted on 5% of the study population, and necessary modifications have been made. Information on family history of HBV infection was self-reported and based on participants’ knowledge. To mitigate recall bias, the data collection process was also standardized with structured questionnaires to ensure consistent questioning for both cases and controls. Additionally, objective data sources (medical records) were used for variables such as laboratory results and vaccination status.

Laboratory methods

The detection of Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) was carried out using commercial lateral flow immunoassay kits (SD Bioline HBsAg rapid test kit) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. A 5mL venous blood sample was collected from each participant under aseptic conditions. The blood was then allowed to clot at room temperature before being centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min to separate the serum. A 100 µL drop of serum was applied to the test cassette, followed by the addition of a buffer solution. Results were interpreted within 15–20 min based on the appearance of colored bands: One band (control only) a Negative result and two bands (control + test band) a Positive result.

Operational definitions

Cases

Patients with various forms of liver disease or asymptomatic carriers with positive HBsAg tests during the study period.

Controls

Patients with HBsAg-negative blood test result and apparently healthy.

Adequate knowledge of HBV transmission

If a person mentions two or more of the four transmission pathways of hepatitis, namely mother-to-child, bloodborne, sexually, and contact with open sores or cuts.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered to Excel and exported to STATA version 17 for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed to assess demographic, behavioral and clinical conditions of the study subjects. Both bivariate and multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the predictable variables for risk factors of HBV infection. Odds ratio (OR) and Adjusted Odds Ratio (AOR) was used to find out the associative risk factors between cases and controls. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) and a cut-off p-value of 0.05 or less for statistical significance were used to demonstrate an association between significant factors with hepatitis infection.

Logistic regression model was used to analyze the relationship between the dependent variable, Hepatitis B virus infection, and multiple independent variables. In the multivariable analysis, potential confounding factors were identified based on their known association with both the exposure and outcome variables. These confounders were then included as covariates in the statistical model to adjust for their effects. The inclusion of independent variables was based on the model goodness of fit, which was checked by Hosmer-Lemeshow test, a p-value of > 0.05 suggests the model fits well. Multicollinearity was checked using VIF (Variance Inflation Factor) to ensure that there are no highly correlated predictors that could affect the stability of the model.

Clinical and behavioral characteristics

A slightly larger proportion of cases, 46 (47.9%), reported having been hospitalized for general medical reasons, regardless of the cause than controls, 83 (43.2%). Likewise, previous history of STI is slightly higher 3 (3.1%) in cases than controls 4 (2.1%). A comparable proportion of cases 20 (20.8%) and controls 39 (20.3%) had history of blood transfusion, but cases had higher HIV infection rate 11 (11.5%) than controls, 19 (9.9%) (Table 2). However, the proportion of study participants who had two or more life time sexual partners (at any point during their lifetime) was substantially greater in the cases, 35 (36.5%) than the controls 50 (26.1%) and the proportion of family hepatitis infection history was approximately double in the cases 39 (40.6%) than controls 39 (20.3%) (Fig. 2). Regarding age, higher proportion of infection is at age below35 years and a downward trend was noted as age increases. Among the cases, the proportion of females is higher than that of males.

Awareness and traditional practices

A higher proportion of cases, 82 (85.4%), than controls, 105 (54.7%), reported hearing about HBV transmission, with health facilities as the primary source of information for cases and electronic medias for controls. Likewise, approximately twice as many cases 33 (34.4%) as controls 33 (17.2%) had adequate knowledge of transmission. The positive attitude towards modern healthcare preference is slightly higher among cases, 40 (41.7%), than controls, 68 (35.4%); that is the majority of controls, 124 (64.6%) believe traditional health practice is better than modern health care, while this was only in half of the controls 56 (58.3%). Fourteen (14.6%) cases and 20 (10.4%) controls reported a history of sharing sharps such as common use of razors, nail clippers and scissors in the household. About two-third of cases, 71 (73.9%), had history of tattooing and ear piercing whereas among controls it was 69 (35.9%). Fewer than half of both cases and controls correctly answered the question, “Is hepatitis B vaccine preventable?” However, a slightly higher percentage of cases (47, 48.9%) provided the correct response compared to controls (77, 40.1%). Traditional medicine use was reported by 44 (45.8%) cases and 61 (31.7%) controls. (Table 3)

Risk factors associated with HBV infection among cases and controls

In a bivariate analysis, factors associated with hepatitis B infection were being female sex, urban residence, educational level below college degree, history of multiple sexual partners, sharing of sharp materials, traditional practices, and family history of hepatitis. In the multivariate logistic regression, the model fits with independent variables sex, address, educational level, life time sexual partner, sharing of sharps, traditional practice and family history of liver disease at hosmer–Lemeshow chi2 of 14.6 (P = 0.07). The value of VIF in each independent variable in our model was < 2 which is in the acceptable range of below 5, indicating low multicollinearity.

In the multivariate analysis history of multiple sexual partners, traditional practices such as tattooing/ear piercing/tonsillectomy and family history of hepatitis were found significantly associated with hepatitis B infection. Individuals with a history of traditional practices had 6.43 times higher odds (95% CI: 3.12–13.23) of infection than their counter parts. Similarly, those with an infected family member had 2.83 times higher odds (95% CI: 1.48–5.42) of contracting the virus. Likewise, participants with multiple sexual partners had 2.07 times higher odds (95% CI: 1.07–4.00) of infection (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate factors related to hepatitis B infection, including socio-demographic, behavioral, clinical and cultural practices. In our study, the socio-demographic feature in terms of age, sex, residence and educational status did not differ significantly among the cases and controls. However, interestingly, among the cases, females and urban residents composed a higher proportion than the controls. This contrasts with previous studies in countries from sub-Saharan Africa, which have generally found higher infection rates among males and urban residents [18].

Studies from Ghana highlight that urban populations often experience higher HBV prevalence [19].One possible explanation is that earlier morbidity among men could have led to their lower representation in the study sample as older age and male sex are risk factors for liver diseases progression and shorter survival [20]. Additionally, the susceptibility of women to hepatitis B due to socioeconomic vulnerabilities may have contributed to this trend. Research from Uganda indicates that while men traditionally exhibit higher infection rates, changing social and economic dynamics have led to an increasing burden among women, particularly in urban areas with evolving cultural and behavioral patterns [21]. Conversely, younger individuals are more likely to contract the virus through sexual contact, while females may be more vulnerable due to socioeconomic factors. This trend is evident in the current study, where the probability of testing positive for hepatitis decreases progressively with age. Likewise, urban populations may engage in higher-risk behaviors, such as unprotected sex or intravenous drug use, contributing to the increased prevalence observed.

Sexual transmission is a substantial risk factor for hepatitis B infection, particularly among groups with high rates of sexually transmitted illnesses and risky sexual behaviors. In high endemic regions, transmission of HBV primarily occurs during childhood, either vertically from mother to child or through household exposure [22]. In contrast, our study found that a significant proportion of young cases reported having more than two life time sexual partners indicating that sexual transmission played a larger role than anticipated. This finding is in line with a study from Ethiopia, Uganda, and Kenya which clearly linked multiple sexual partners to a higher rate of HBV infection [9, 23, 24].

The prevalence of hepatitis among the general population in Ethiopia is 4% to 5% (moderate to high endemicity) that indicates the existence of multiple factors that contributed to the high prevalence [25]. Studies have shown that transmission of hepatitis B occurs among family if there is an infected family member, especially in high endemic settings [26, 27]. In the present study the proportion of family hepatitis infection history is about double in the cases 39 (40.6%) than controls 39 (20.3%) indicating a strong prediction for the infection. In our finding, the cases were 2.83 times higher proportion of hepatitis B infected family member than controls. This finding is consistent with the study conducted in Rwanda [28], and Ethiopia [25] that showed subjects with family history of hepatitis were more likely to have HBV infection [29].

According to findings, traditional practices significantly contribute to the transmission of hepatitis B virus. The practices such as skin piercing including tatooing, tonsillectomy, and traditional circumcision can facilitate the spread due to sharing and use of unsterilized instruments [30, 31]. In our study, traditional practices such as tattoo, ear piercing and sharing sharps were found double proportion in cases than controls that confer exposure to HBV due to sharing of non-sterile materials during traditional practices and between family members. This practice is particularly concerning in rural areas with limited access to healthcare facilities which is also reported in rural Ethiopia [32]. Nonetheless, factors for HBV infection such as history of blood transfusion, HIV co-infection, and so on were not significant risk factors for HBsAg positivity although higher proportion is reported. The possible explanation could be there were few cases in our study and the sample size may have limited the study’s ability to detect a true difference or could be attributed to improvements in healthcare services, including better infection control measures and safer blood screening practices.

Adequate knowledge about hepatitis B transmission and presence of prevention methods like vaccine is considered important that could impact personal prevention practices and intern infection rate. In addition, attitude towards hepatitis diagnosis and treatment can also affect infection rate as it is believed to affect health seeking behavior [33]. In the present study, a significant proportion of cases than controls had adequate knowledge about HBV transmission and found to have a positive attitude towards hepatitis treatment. This finding is in line with a study from Gondar, Ethiopia [33], where community based study participants showed little knowledge and negative attitude toward hepatitis infection. Another study from Nigeria also found knowledge of the HBV before contacting health services for testing was inadequate [34]. This could be explained in part by the change in attitude and awareness of the cases as a result of the repeated visit to health facilities, as supported by findings indicating that health facilities serve as a source of health information.

In Ethiopia, alongside the existing health facility-based strategies, national and regional efforts should focus on expanding community-based prevention initiatives and emphasize community-targeted initiatives. These initiatives should incorporate community-based health education programs to raise awareness about transmission, traditional practices, risky sexual behaviors, and family-related exposure. Additionally, interventions should prioritize strengthening screening programs for high-risk groups.

Limitations of the study

Our study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the study population may over-represent individuals with more frequent healthcare-seeking behaviors. Additionally, data on family history of hepatitis infection were entirely self-reported, with no independent serological verification. This lack of objective confirmation could have led to either underestimation or overestimation of the association between family history and HBV infection. Despite these limitations, our study provides valuable insights into risk factors of HBV transmission.

Conclusions

This study highlights key risk factors for HBV transmission, including multiple sexual partners, family history of infection, and traditional practices such as tattooing, ear piercing, and sharing sharp objects. Public health efforts should specifically target these groups in order to address the identified risk factors and halt transmission dynamics. HBV prevention strategies should incorporate national public health policies, with a focus on addressing the specific risk factors identified in the study. Engagement of local and community leaders is crucial to promote safe practices and raise awareness, particularly in areas where traditional practices are common, to reduce the transmission of HBV. The greater number of cases below 35 years age, and females with a decreasing tendency with age may be due to early disease progression and reduced survival rates among older individuals and males. This emphasizes there should be an expansion of early screening, especially for at-risk populations, to limit the progression of HBV.

Health facilities play a crucial role in increasing awareness and shaping positive attitudes toward hepatitis treatment preference to modern healthcare. However, broader community-based education initiatives, particularly for young people, should focus on the risks associated with unsafe sexual practices, traditional practices, and familial transmission, to promote safer behaviors and reduce HBV spread.

Future studies should incorporate serological testing to validate self-reported family histories of hepatitis, which will provide more reliable insights into the transmission dynamics of HBV.
