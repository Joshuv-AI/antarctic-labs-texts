Unmasking the sacral reflex: Successful recording of the oligosynaptic component using the St. mark's electrode in an intravaginal setup
Authors: André Truffert
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The St. Mark's electrode (SMe) was designed forty-two years ago by Kiff and Swash, then active at the eponymous London hospital, for measuring the pudendal nerve terminal motor latency (PNTML) to the external anal sphincter (EAS) (Kiff and Swash, 1984a, Kiff and Swash, 1984b). Since then, its possible use for recording the sacral reflexes was not described, presumably because of the “blind spot” induced by the proximity of the stimulation and recording poles on the finger-mounted device, consisting of the stimulus artifact plus the total waveform of subsequent compound muscle action potential (CMAP) occupying the whole space of signal analysis. In this hypothesis, the “missing reflex” data in literature would be due to the earliest, monosynaptic component being drowned in the CMAP, leaving the later, polysynaptic components detectable only if the observation window is sufficiently extended and the signal sufficiently amplified.

In several female patients, just adding the basic F-waves program to the classical distal SMe CMAP program at each side, often permitted successful recordings of the bulbocavernosus reflex (BCR) in an intravaginal setup. Here is an example of such a case.

A 42-years old, 176 cm tall nulliparous woman was referred for a persistent genital arousal disorder (PGAD) of acute onset 2 months previously, after having spent several hours skiing. Symptoms of sexual arousal were felt more intense on the left, specifically triggered (or worsened) when sitting and avoided (or relieved) when crouching. Clinical examination showed a multimodal hypoesthesia in the left pudendal and right distal sciatic nerves cutaneous territories and to cold only in the left obturator nerve cutaneous territory. Pudendal nerves neurography with intravaginal SMe fixed to the right-handed examiner's right hand yielded a biphasic CMAP of 42.9% decreased amplitude and 8.3% increased latency on the right side, as compared to the left side (Fig. 1 A). Slowing by 5 times the sweep speed rendered visible late responses in the BCR temporal range, taking a burst-like aspect when superimposed. Use of split-sensitivity windows of apparatus' F-waves setup and up to 8 superimposed traces allowed simultaneous visual control of both CMAP and BCR waveforms reproducibility and exact measure of the BCR minimal latency on the right side only (Fig. 1 B, top trace), whereas on the left side, the baseline was disturbed by permanent ongoing background EMG activity that would never disappear (Fig. 1B, bottom trace). Perineal somatosensory evoked potentials recorded over the vertex showed unstructured dispersed responses on both sides and bad reproducibility during left labium major stimulation, making difficult precise determination of P1 latencies. Sural and peroneal nerves sensory potentials were normal and symmetric. Tibial nerves conduction studies showed normal, symmetric motor conduction velocities and F waves latencies with multiple late potentials of various latencies. A needle EMG examination of pelvic floor muscles was not performed. These findings were interpreted as reflecting unilateral loss of fast-conducting motor axons on the right side and bilateral loss of sensory axons, predominantly on the left side, where the persistent, unsuppressed background motor activity was attributed to proprioceptive deafferentation. Subsequently the patient did not get in touch again and was lost to follow-up.

Distal pudendal nerve evaluation using the SMe is an important step of standard electrophysiological assessment of perineum (recently reviewed by Panicker et al., 2026). This electrode was first designed in the context of faecal incontinence to record the EAS contraction after direct intrarectal nerve stimulation at the ischiatic spine (Kiff and Swash, 1984a). Its intravaginal use, which mainly records the bulbocavernosus muscle contraction was introduced later (Tetzschner et al., 1997) and reported as better tolerated than the transrectal use while providing the same measure of PNTML, even if M-wave may be suboptimal or even unobtainable by the same finger on the side opposite to examiner's handedness (Cavalcanti et al., 2006).

In our patient, left lateralized PGAD symptoms (Kümpers et al., 2023) coexisted with electrical signs suggestive of right pudendal nerve motor axons involvement. The late responses consistently observed on the right were identified as the oligosynaptic component of the BCR (Vodušek and Janko, 1990), which, in women, is usually evoked by activation of a sensorimotor circuit involving clitoral sensory fibres electrical stimulation and recording by an EMG needle inserted in the bulbocavernosus muscle (Panicker et al., 2026). This methodology, however, do not allow recording of the earliest, monosynaptic component of the sacral reflex described by Swash in 1982, which is mediated only by fusorial fast-conducting fibres inducing the stretch-reflex and translated as H-waves (Vodušek and Janko, 1990) on surface recordings. Therefore, in theory, direct SMe stimulation of the mixed pudendal nerve's trunk would activate those afferents from muscle spindles and give rise to progressive recruitment of H-waves with expected latencies around 12.0 ms, before their being extinct into the CMAP's tail. Somewhat confusingly, this monosynaptic component was initially labelled “R1”, and the following, oligosynaptic component “R2” (Swash, 1982), by analogy with the terminology used to describe the two ipsilateral responses of the blink reflex.

The main factor impeding BCR recording using SMe appears to be residual background EMG resting activity, as in our patient's left side. Hence, particular care should be given during signal acquisition to pelvic floor muscles being at maximal rest, audio feedback leaving only the amplifier blank noise to be heard between iterative stimuli and discarding from analyses traces where the short segment between M-wave tail and the BCR waves onset is not perfectly aligned with the baseline. Another solution to overcome this limitation could be long averaging of signal, which might reveal reflex responses comparable to those published in early papers using surface recordings (e.g. Fig. 4 of Haldeman et al., 1982).

Researchers and clinicians using the SMe should be encouraged to look beyond the CMAP and assess not only the M-wave characteristics but also the reflex waveforms parameters including: minimal latency; frequency; recruitment (Miguet et al., 2021); total duration; shape and BCR to M amplitude ratios, taking advantage of minimal discomfort of the procedure, that allows iterative pudendal nerve stimuli.
