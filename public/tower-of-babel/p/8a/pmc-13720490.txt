Exploring the Role of Leukocyte Telomere Length and Human Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase MNS16A Variant in Cannabis or Synthetic Cannabinoid Use Disorder
Authors: Sacide Pehlivan, Ayse Feyda Nursal, Hasan Mervan Aytac, Yasemin Oyaci, Eren Aytac, Mustafa Pehlivan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the association between the human telomerase reverse transcriptase gene (hTERT)-MNS16A polymorphism and cannabis or synthetic cannabinoid use disorder (CUD/SCUD). Additionally, the study examined leukocyte telomere length (LTL) in these individuals by comparing it with the control group.MethodsThe study included a total of 228 participants, comprised of 147 CUD/SCUD patients and 81 healthy controls. Genotyping of the hTERT-MNS16A variant was performed through polymerase chain reaction (PCR), while relative LTL was measured using real-time PCR. For analysis, the T/S ratio (telomere, T) was calculated in relation to a single-copy gene (S), allowing LTL to be expressed as the relative T/S ratio.ResultsAnalysis of the log-transformed T/S ratio indicated a significantly greater degree of LTL shortening in the patient group compared to the control group (p = 0.001). Among patients, psychotic symptoms and violent behaviors were notably more prevalent in those with a T/S ratio > 1 compared to those with a T/S ratio < 1 (p = 0.022 and p = 0.011, respectively). There was no significant difference between patients and controls in the overall distribution of hTERT-MNS16A genotypes. However, in CUD/SCUD patients, violent behavior was significantly more common in those with the hTERT L/L genotype compared to those with the L/S or S/S genotype (p = 0.005).ConclusionIn summary, although the hTERT-MNS16A variable number tandem repeat polymorphism does not appear to increase susceptibility to CUD or SCUD, LTL shortening is evident in patients with these disorders.Keywords: Substance-related disorders, Marijuana abuse, Designer drugs, Telomere, Telomerase

INTRODUCTION

Substance use disorder (SUD) is a complex condition defined by compulsive drug-seeking behavior and harmful substance use, often resulting in social dysfunction and adverse pharmacological effects. Among substances associated with SUD, cannabis is the most widely used globally after alcohol and tobacco. Its psychoactive properties are primarily attributed to its high content of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), while it contains only trace amounts of cannabidiol, a non-intoxicating compound. Adverse effects associated with acute or regular cannabis use are directly related to the concentration of THC in product [1]. Long-term cannabis use influences the endocannabinoid system at the molecular level, resulting in modifications to endocannabinoids, cannabinoid receptors, and enzymes involved in their breakdown [2]. The use of synthetic cannabinoids (SCs) has grown considerably in recent years. SCs are appealing because they produce psychoactive effects similar to cannabis, are readily accessible, and are undetectable in standard screenings. In contrast to THC, the primary psychoactive compound in cannabis that functions as a partial agonist at cannabinoid receptors, SCs act as strong, full agonists at these receptors. This distinction accounts for the stronger effects of SCs compared to cannabis, which is unsurprising [3]. Studies are showing that high levels of SCs can lead to oxidative stress, genotoxicity, and inflammation [4]. Although data on the potential interactions between cannabis use and aging are limited, both have been found to impact various neurotransmitter systems concurrently [5]. A study that tracked a representative birth cohort over a 45-year span examined the biological aging of cannabis users. The findings indicated that, by middle age, individuals who had used cannabis long-term showed signs of biological aging greater than those of non-users of the same chronological age [6].

Telomeres, found at the ends of chromosomes, play a key role in preserving the genomic structure’s integrity [7]. At telomeres, the presence of a single-stranded segment alongside a protein complex containing repetitive TTAGGG hexamer sequences shields chromosomes from degradation. This structure also safeguards against fusion and rearrangement events within eukaryotic cells [8]. Leukocyte telomere length (LTL) is regarded as a clinical marker for assessing the risk of age-related diseases and can also be observed in neurodegenerative disorders [9]. The human telomerase reverse transcriptase gene (hTERT), situated on chromosome 5 at region 5p15.33, encodes a ribonucleoprotein enzyme that plays a crucial role in elongating the ends of chromosomes. This extension counteracts the natural shortening that occurs with each cell division [10]. Mutations within the coding regions of the hTERT gene have a notable effect on both telomerase activity and the maintenance of telomere length. The MNS16A polymorphism, a functional variable number tandem repeat (VNTR), is situated downstream of the hTERT gene. The length of these MNS16A repeats appears to influence promoter activity and could be involved in regulating the expression of antisense hTERT mRNA [11]. Based on the existing literature, we hypothesized that individuals with cannabis or synthetic cannabinoid use disorder (CUD/SCUD) would exhibit shorter LTL than healthy controls and that the hTERT MNS16A polymorphism would be associated with clinical vulnerability to these disorders. Furthermore, we hypothesized that both hTERT MNS16A genotype and LTL would be associated with clinical characteristics of illness severity.

Accordingly, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the relationship between the hTERT MNS16A variant and genetic susceptibility to CUD/SCUD. Furthermore, LTL was analyzed in these individuals. In addition to these genetic factors, the study also aimed to explore the associations between the hTERT MNS16A variant, LTL, and a range of clinical parameters, including psychotic symptoms, violent behavior, suicide attempts, and various sociodemographic factors.

METHODS

Study Population

The study included 228 participants, comprising 147 individuals with CUD/SCUD and 81 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The patient group was recruited consecutively from individuals who applied to the psychiatry outpatient clinic at Bakirkoy Mazhar Osman Mental Health and Neurology Training and Research Hospital between May 2019 and January 2020.

Inclusion criteria

Diagnoses were established using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5-Clinical Version (SCID-5-CV) and confirmed through urine drug analysis at the initial visit, after which sociodemographic and clinical assessment form (including age, age of onset, sex, substance type, education, marital status, employment status, family history, smoking, violent behavior, suicide attempts, and psychotic findings) was administered. The control group consisted of healthy, unrelated healthcare workers from the same hospital without a history of psychiatric or neurological disorders, or SUDs.

Exclusion criteria

There were no women in the patient group, and individuals with psychiatric illness, intellectual disability, serious physical or neurological disorders, neurodevelopmental diseases, as well as patients with SUD other than CUD or SCUD, were excluded from the study. To minimize circadian variations, venous blood samples were drawn from the antecubital region into EDTA tubes between 07:00 and 10:00 following a 10−12 hour fasting period on the morning after the initial visit. All participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study was approved by the Istanbul Faculty of Medicine Ethics Committee (25.01.2019/02).

hTERT Genotyping

DNA was isolated from peripheral blood samples collected from each participant in EDTA tubes and stored at 4°C until it was ready for analysis. Genotyping of the hTERT MNS16A VNTR polymorphism was then carried out using the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) technique [12]. Forward and reverse primer sequences were used as 5’-AGGATTCTGATCTCTGAAGGGTG-3’ and 5’-TCTGC CTGAGGAAGGACGTATG-3’, respectively. The amplification procedure began with an initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 minutes, followed by thirty cycles that included 30 seconds at 95°C, 20 seconds at 67.5°C, and 17 seconds at 72°C. A final extension was then carried out at 72°C for 10 minutes. The PCR products were run on a 3% agarose gel with ethidium bromide for visualization. Genotypes were identified based on band sizes, with 243 bp and 272 bp classified as short (S) alleles, and 333 bp and 302 bp as long (L) alleles. Thus, the hTERT MNS16A genotypes were categorized as L/L, L/S, and S/S.

Measurement of Leukocyte Telomere Length

For each DNA sample, the following calculation was applied: The T/S ratio (telomere, T) was determined for a single-copy gene (S), with LTL expressed as the relative T/S ratio. This process measured the relative quantity of telomeric DNA, not its absolute length. The cycle threshold (Ct) value was utilized to calculate the T/S ratio, formulated as: [2Ct (36B4) / 2Ct (telomeres)]−1 = 2−ΔCt. The fold-change was then calculated using the formula: 2−ΔΔCt = 2−(DCt (group 1) −ΔCt (group 2)), where group 1 (control) average was subtracted from group 2 (experimental) average. This fold change, derived from the 2−ΔΔCt method, compared TL fold changes in case samples with those in control samples. A result of 2−ΔΔCt < 1 indicated that the DNA samples had a shorter average TL than the control DNA samples, while 2−ΔΔCt > 1 indicated that the case samples had a longer average LTL than the controls.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 21.0 (IBM Corp.). Descriptive analyses were conducted for sociodemographic and clinical variables, including age, age of onset, education level, marital status, employment status, substance type, smoking status, family history, psychotic symptoms, violent behavior, and suicide attempts. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with minimum and maximum values following assessment of normality, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. For inferential analyses, categorical variables were initially compared using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were compared using the independent-samples median test. To control for potential confounding effects, age- and sex-adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated using binary logistic regression analyses. A pvalue of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Associations of Genotype and Telomere Length with Clinical Variables

The associations between hTERT MNS16A genotype distribution and clinical variables—including psychotic symptoms, violent behavior, suicide attempts, family history, ongoing use, age of onset, and duration of use—were examined among CUD/SCUD patients (Table 3). Violent behavior was significantly more prevalent in those with the hTERT L/L genotype than in those with the L/S + S/S genotype (p = 0.005). Additionally, when clinical variables were evaluated according to LTL, psychotic symptoms and violent behavior were more common among patients with a T/S ratio > 1 compared to those with a T/S ratio < 1 (p = 0.022 and p = 0.011, respectively) (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

Chronic use or repeated exposure to psychoactive substances is recognized for triggering epigenetic changes. Addictive drugs can directly or indirectly affect numerous genes at the epigenetic level, leading to alterations in gene expression that play a role in the development of addiction-related behaviors. Chronic, heavy cannabis use can lead to addiction, often characterized by symptoms like difficulty in controlling or reducing usage, even if it doesn’t directly disrupt daily life. The lifetime risk of developing an addiction, as defined by DSM criteria, is estimated at 9% among all cannabis users. However, this risk increases to roughly 1 in 6 for those who begin using during adolescence [13]. While establishing causation from cannabinoid exposure in epidemiological research is challenging, prolonged CUD has been linked to significant neurobiological, psychosocial, and overall health concerns [14]. SCs, which are lab-made psychoactive compounds, produce effects that mimic cannabis but are often more potent due to their stronger binding affinity to cannabinoid receptors [15].

Cannabis use is closely linked to the use of other illegal substances, with alcohol and nicotine being particularly common among young users who consume cannabis [16]. Studies have also shown that cannabis use is associated with an increased risk of the early onset of psychotic disorders. Psychiatric disorders are linked to a heightened risk of severe health issues and early mortality. Research through meta-analyses indicates that people with psychiatric conditions have a greater likelihood of developing cardiovascular diseases, stroke, dementia, diabetes, and obesity. As these health conditions tend to increase in frequency with age, they are often associated with the aging process [17].

The concept of epigenetics encompasses processes that modify gene activity without altering the underlying DNA sequence, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and noncoding RNA regulation. These mechanisms collectively influence DNA accessibility, shaping patterns of gene expression. Understanding these processes offers valuable insights into how environmental and lifestyle factors contribute to aging, disease development, and overall health outcomes. DNA methylation, in particular, serves as a key regulatory mechanism by modifying gene expression through the addition or removal of methyl groups [18]. Its adaptability enables researchers to study how environmental exposures impact the epigenome and how these effects vary over time. Such methylation changes may act as flexible or consistent biomarkers [19]. Environmental and lifestyle factors, including alcohol use, smoking, physical activity, and diet, have been shown to influence epigenetic aging by either accelerating or decelerating it depending on an individual’s age [20,21]. Both cumulative and recent exposures to these factors can impact epigenetic age acceleration (EAA) differently. For instance, prolonged alcohol consumption is positively linked to EAA, while recent consumption may have the opposite effect, highlighting the distinct roles of cumulative versus recent exposures. Similarly, both long-term and recent cannabis use are thought to be connected to age-related epigenetic changes tied to lifespan [22]. These insights can enhance our understanding of the immediate and cumulative effects of cannabis on molecular and biological pathways that influence health outcomes [23].

Telomeres play a crucial role in preserving genome stability and managing gene expression [24]. Due to the incomplete replication of telomeres during each cell division, they progressively shorten over time in mitotic cells unless counteracted by telomerase, the primary enzyme responsible for telomere elongation [25]. LTL shortening has been linked to substance use in previous studies. For instance, significantly shorter LTL has been observed in individuals who use cocaine, abuse alcohol, particularly through binge drinking, or use heroin. A study investigating LTL among individuals from drug rehabilitation centers found that drug users generally exhibited reduced LTL compared to controls [26].

The telomerase complex consists of hTERT, the shelterin complex, and the RNA component of telomerase that facilitates the addition of telomere repeats to the ends of chromosomes. The expression of hTERT mRNA, which is highly regulated, is directly linked to telomerase activity, positioning hTERT as a key factor in controlling enzyme function [27]. The hTERT MNS16A polymorphism, which has short and long allele forms, is noteworthy because the short alleles are correlated with higher hTERT mRNA levels. Research has connected this polymorphism to a heightened risk of cancers, including lung cancer, nasopharyngeal carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and malignant glioma [28]. Additionally, genetic differences in the hTERT gene are believed to influence LTL, which serves as a marker of cellular aging due to its variability across the lifespan [29,30]. Shortened telomeres are linked with a higher likelihood of mortality from all causes and with age-related diseases [1,31]. Recent meta-analyses have also indicated potential links between LTL and various mental health disorders, further emphasizing the importance of telomere length in aging and disease risk [17,30,32].

Based on this information, in this study, we hypothesized that hTERT MNS16A polymorphism and LTL are associated with CUD or SCUD. The majority of our patients were those using SCs. There was no statistically significant difference in the hTERT MNS16A genotype and allele distribution between patients and control subjects. When we compare the genotype distribution and clinical findings, it was found that violent behavior was higher in those with the hTERT MNS16A L/L genotype. It was known that the hTERT MNS16A L allele is associated with decreased hTERT mRNA and telomerase activity. This suggested that low telomerase activity in individuals with the L allele may have led to violent behavior. The quantitative measurement of leukocyte LTL in patients demonstrated a significant reduction in TL compared with controls. This reduction likely results from a combination of oxidative stress, inflammation, and unhealthy lifestyle factors associated with chronic substance abuse [22,23]. However, psychotic symptoms and violent behavior were more prevalent among patients with LTL fold changes greater than 1. Given that the majority of patients were diagnosed with SCUD and had been receiving long-term antipsychotic treatment, the relatively increased LTL observed in this subgroup may be related to the anti-inflammatory and antioxidative effects of antipsychotic medications. Alternatively, higher LTL in patients with more severe psychotic symptoms and violent behavior may reflect compensatory telomerase activation, shifts in leukocyte subpopulations, or stress-related neuroendocrine responses rather than a protective biological effect. In addition, reverse causality and methodological factors inherent to relative telomere length measurement cannot be excluded.

There are limitations to this study. A significant proportion of participants exhibited violent behavior, psychotic symptoms, and suicide attempts, which may have influenced the findings. The lack of a subgroup analysis excluding individuals with these symptoms, due to the very small sample size, limits the ability to establish clearer associations. Additionally, as this is a case-control study, causality cannot be established. Furthermore, since the sample consists solely of male patients, the findings cannot be generalized to both genders. Another limitation is that nearly all participants were smokers, and the body mass index was not assessed. Despite these limitations, the study also has several notable strengths. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to simultaneously examine LTL and the hTERT MNS16A VNTR polymorphism in individuals with CUD/SCUD. The use of standardized diagnostic interviews (SCID-5-CV), objective confirmation of substance use via urine analysis, and age- and sex-adjusted statistical analyses enhances the validity of the findings. Furthermore, the inclusion of clinically relevant outcomes provides a more comprehensive understanding of the potential biological correlates of illness severity in this population. Based on the available evidence, further epidemiological studies on this subject and biological studies on the underlying mechanisms would be highly desirable.

In summary, this study demonstrates significantly greater LTL shortening in patients compared to controls, with psychotic symptoms and violent behaviors being notably more prevalent among patients with a T/S ratio fold change greater than 1. Although no significant differences were found in the distribution of hTERT-MNS16A genotypes between patients and controls, violent behavior was observed more frequently in patients with the hTERT L/L genotype compared to those with the L/S or S/S genotype. This study provides valuable insight into the relationship between telomere length and its potential role in the pathophysiology of CUD and SCUD. The findings of significantly greater telomere shortening in patients compared to controls suggest that telomere length may be a useful biomarker for understanding the biological underpinnings of these disorders. Recent years have brought significant advances in our knowledge of the genetic basis of CUD and SCUD [3,33]. Continued progress in genetic research is expected, particularly as larger sample sizes are analyzed, accounting for both genetic and environmental factors. The findings of this study may have clinically meaningful implications for the prevention and treatment of CUD/SCUD in the future. Additionally, future studies could explore the impact of cannabis use on telomere length by comparing patients treated with cannabinoid-derived medications for conditions such as epilepsy, multiple sclerosis, chemotherapy-induced nausea, AIDS-related anorexia with weight loss, and neuropathic pain to those receiving alternative treatments for these conditions.
