Multiportal Arthroscopy‐assisted Surgical Resection of Diffuse‐type Tenosynovial Giant Cell Tumors in the Ankle Joint Yields Favorable Clinical Outcomes: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Jiayao Zhang, Lei Yao, Yiyuan Sun, Junqiao Li, Yunan Hu, Kunhao Chen, Wufeng Cai, Chenghao Zhang, Jian Li, Qi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDiffuse‐type tenosynovial giant cell tumors (Dt‐TGCTs) commonly occur in the knee joint and tend to recur postoperatively. However, limited clinical data are available on ankle joint involvement especially associated multiportal arthroscopic treatment outcomes. The purpose of this study was to report the clinical results of multiportal arthroscopy‐assisted resection of Dt‐TGCTs of the ankle.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed the clinical data of 33 patients with Dt‐TGCT of the ankle who underwent multiportal arthroscopic treatment between August 2011 and December 2020. Clinical follow‐up included the visual analogue scale (VAS) score, American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) score, Kofoed score, and recurrence rate to assess surgical outcomes. The number of patients who achieved the patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) based on the AOFAS score was also examined. Additionally, the patients were categorized into two groups based on the final surgical approach: Group A who underwent multiportal arthroscopic synovectomy and Group AO who underwent combined arthroscopic and open surgical excision. Intergroup comparisons were conducted. Intraoperative characteristics, such as the number of patients with involvement of the tarsal tunnel and fibularis tendon and the Outerbridge grading of cartilage damage, were recorded to assess the selection of surgical procedures.ResultsAmong the 33 patients, 15 were assigned to Group A, and 18 were in Group AO. The median follow‐up duration for the 33 patients was 77 months (range, 28–142 months). The median VAS score was 1 (range, 0–4), the AOFAS score was 96 (range, 65–100), and the Kofoed score was 96 (range, 67–100). A total of 27 patients (82%) achieved PASS based on AOFAS scores, while five patients (15%) had recurrence. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in recurrence rate, follow‐up VAS score, AOFAS score, Kofoed score, or number of patients who reached the PASS (p > 0.05). In the AO group, 16 cases of Dt‐TGCT involved the tarsal tunnel, and 11 cases involved the fibularis tendon. All these patients exhibited extension beyond the joint. In contrast, only one patient in Group A had involvement of the tarsal tunnel. Statistically significant differences were observed between the groups (p < 0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that, with the assistance of a multiportal arthroscopic approach, surgical excision of Dt‐TGCT in the ankle resulted in favorable clinical outcomes with a relatively low recurrence rate. Additionally, patients with extra‐articular involvement were more likely to require concomitant open surgery.Keywords: Ankle, Arthroscopy, Diffuse‐type tenosynovial giant cell tumor, Recurrence, Synovectomy

Introduction

Tenosynovial giant cell tumors (TGCTs) are rare, locally invasive, and originate from the synovial lining, bursae, or tendon sheaths. They predominantly occur in the knee joint (64%); however, only 14% of cases occur in the ankle joint, and 5% occur in the foot. 1 Both inflammatory and tumorous components are involved in the pathogenesis of TGCTs. 2 , 3 Patients often present with nonspecific symptoms such as pain, swelling, stiffness, and limited range of motion, which can persist for months or even years. 4 , 5 Based on the affected location and growth pattern, TGCTs are classified into two subtypes: localized‐type TGCTs (L‐TGCTs) and diffuse‐type TGCTs (Dt‐TGCTs). Solitary nodules within or outside the joint characterize L‐TGCTs, mainly around the digits, while Dt‐TGCTs are characterized by extensive intra‐articular involvement often accompanied by extra‐articular spread. 6 The incidence of Dt‐TGCT is estimated to be only 1.8 cases per million people. 7 However, its high invasiveness, challenging surgical management, and high recurrence rate have garnered significant attention.

Currently, surgical excision is the gold standard for treating Dt‐TGCTs and can be achieved through arthroscopic, open, or combined approaches. 8 , 9 , 10 Owing to the expertise of highly skilled specialists, intra‐articular Dt‐TGCTs can be safely treated through minimally invasive arthroscopy. However, if the lesion spreads to the extra‐articular tendon sheath, complete excision is typically achieved through open surgery, which is also the most commonly employed method to date. 1 , 11 Furthermore, some studies have reported promising outcomes with the combination of arthroscopic and open surgery. 11 , 12

A review of the literature revealed that a high rate of postoperative recurrence exists, regardless of the surgical approach chosen. 9 , 13 , 14 Nonetheless, due to the relative rarity of Dt‐TGCTs in the ankle joint, most prior studies have often been limited by small sample sizes, leading to insufficient research on postoperative functional outcomes and recurrence rates. Notably, reports on arthroscopic techniques remain limited, with most studies utilizing anteromedial and anterolateral portals supplemented by posteromedial and posterolateral portals in cases involving lesions in the posterior joint. 6 , 11 , 15 These portals make it challenging to fully expose and thoroughly clear lesions around the tarsal sinus and tarsal tunnel. Incomplete excision is a significant cause of recurrence. Therefore, we supplemented the medial malleolus‐assisted and tarsal sinus portals to achieve more thorough exposure and excision of the lesions. Therefore, the aims of this study were to: (i) investigate the clinical outcomes of multiportal arthroscopic‐assisted resection of Dt‐TGCTs; and (ii) separately group patients who underwent combined open surgery, compare the lesion characteristics and clinical outcomes between patients who underwent arthroscopic resection and those who underwent combined arthroscopic and open resection, and evaluate the choice of surgical method based on lesion characteristics.

Methods

Patient Selection

The clinical data of patients with ankle Dt‐TGCT admitted to West China Hospital from August 2011 to December 2020 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) aged ≤65 years; (ii) diagnosed with Dt‐TGCT requiring surgical excision; (iii) had postoperative pathology‐confirmed Dt‐TGCT; and (iv) had a follow‐up duration of at least 24 months. Patients with a history of: (i) previous surgery on the affected ankle with restricted range of motion; (ii) coagulation disorders; (iii) severe osteoporosis; (iv) ankle infections; or (v) other conditions affecting postoperative functional recovery, such as congenital malformation of the ankle, were excluded from the study. The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (2024 Review (No. 204)). Since this study was purely observational and did not harm the patients, the Ethics Committee specifically waived the need to provide informed consent.

Surgical Techniques

All surgical procedures were performed by an experienced surgeon. Considering that anterior surgery could result in hematoma and soft tissue swelling in the posterior aspect of the ankle joint, affecting the surgical field of view, priority was given to posterior surgery. The patient was initially placed in the prone position, and a pneumatic tourniquet was applied at the root of the thigh.

Arthroscopic Posterior Approach Procedures

Initially, both posteromedial and posterolateral portals were established (Figure 1A). A 30° and 4.0‐mm diameter arthroscope was used to explore the involvement of the joint cavity. Forceps were used to grasp representative synovial lesions from multiple sites for pathological examination. A 3.0‐mm diameter shaver was used to completely remove the synovial lesions, including those around the ligaments and tendons. The focus was placed on the soft tissues surrounding the tibialis posterior tendon (TPT) and flexor digitorum longus tendon (FDLT), as well as lesions in the medial aspect between those tendons and the flexor hallucis longus tendon (FHLT). In cases where visualization through the posterior portal was challenging, an additional medial malleolus‐assisted portal (Figure 1B) was employed for thorough cleaning. To minimize risk, a polydioxanone (PDS) suture was used intraoperatively, introduced through the medial malleolus‐assisted portal and exited through the posteromedial portal. This procedure was used to retract the tibial nerve, posterior tibial vessels, FDLT, and FHLT to one side for protection.

The white arrows indicate the surface landmarks for arthroscopic portals. (A) The posteromedial and posterolateral portals were applied above the calcaneus, closely adjacent to the Achilles tendon. (B) The medial malleolus‐assisted portal was employed horizontally to the distal end of the medial malleolus, which was situated posteriorly to the sheath of the tibialis posterior tendon. (C) The tarsal sinus portal was applied at the anterior one‐third point of line ab, where point b represents the midpoint of the anterior edge of the distal fibula, line bc is the long axis of the fibula, and the angle between line bc and line bd is 30 degrees. The extension of line db to the skin prominence on the cuboid bone is point a.

Arthroscopic Anterior Approach Procedures

After thoroughly debriding the posterior ankle joint, the patient was transitioned to the supine position for a safer anterior ankle procedure. Standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals were established, and lesions within the anterior, medial, and lateral compartments of the joint were sequentially excised. When the tarsal sinus was involved, a dedicated tarsal sinus portal (Figure 1C) was employed for synovial resection. During the entire procedure, the positions of the arthroscope and shaver were repeatedly swapped to ensure that all fields of view were obtained. Radiofrequency vaporization (RFV) was employed for hemostasis. Any lesions extending beyond the ankle joint capsule or proving challenging for complete arthroscopic excision were subjected to open excision.

Open Surgical Procedures

Prior to open surgery, arthroscopic procedures were employed to remove visible lesions within the joint, aiming to reduce the number and length of open incisions. Lesions outside the joint were primarily removed through limited incisions along the extension direction.

The talar cartilage was assessed and graded via arthroscopy, with appropriate interventions applied for concomitant conditions. After surgery, examinations were conducted to assess arterial pulsation, toe flexion and extension, and ligament tension.

Postoperative Rehabilitation and Management

Postoperative rehabilitation included early mobilization. Patients commenced active or passive range of motion exercises within the first week after surgery, aiming to achieve satisfactory mobility within 8 weeks. All patients, except for those who were undergoing bone marrow stimulation (BMS) and were not allowed full weight‐bearing for the first 8 weeks, gradually increased their weight‐bearing status based on tolerance, with no specific restriction on full weight‐bearing time. 11 Patients took nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) orally as needed, and eligible individuals were advised to undergo rehabilitation training at a rehabilitation center. Additionally, all patients were advised to start localized external beam radiotherapy 4 weeks after the operation. However, the final decision was made by the patients who provided full informed consent.

Outcome Measurement

Patient‐reported outcome measure scores were employed, including the visual analogue scale (VAS), with scores ranging from 0 to 10 (lower scores indicating less pain); the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) scale score (higher scores indicated greater satisfaction, with a maximum score of 100); and the Kofoed score (higher scores indicated greater satisfaction, with a maximum score of 100). 16 Given that there were no recorded preoperative score results, we assessed the surgical effect using the AOFAS score and the patient acceptable symptom state (PASS). 17 An AOFAS score ≥ 88.5 points was considered to indicate the PASS. 18

The recurrence rate was documented, and recurrence was defined as the occurrence of new disease or progression of residual Dt‐TGCTs after synovectomy and was confirmed through repeated follow‐up magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan or subsequent surgery. 19 Recurrence time was defined as the duration between the initial surgery and the diagnosis of recurrence.

In addition, patients were classified into two groups based on the final surgical approach for comparison of baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes: the group undergoing complete synovectomy via a multiportal arthroscopic approach (Group A) and the group undergoing arthroscopic surgery combined with open surgery (Group AO). Intraoperative characteristics and postoperative outcomes were recorded and compared between groups.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Two independent samples t‐tests were applied for continuous variables that met the criteria of a normal distribution; otherwise, intergroup comparisons were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. Additionally, when continuous variables tended to be non‐normally distributed, the results were presented as median (range) rather than mean ± SD (standard deviation). Given the relatively small sample size, Fisher's exact test was used to compare categorical variables. A p value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Intraoperative Findings

A total of 33 eligible patients with ankle Dt‐TGCT were included, with 15 in Group A and 18 in Group AO. The median age of the patients was 38 years (range, 8–65 years), follow‐up duration was 77 months (range, 28–142 months), and duration of symptoms was 12 months (range, 1–144 months). Among the 33 patients, 14 (42%) had a history of trauma (13 with sprains, one with a crush injury from a car door), 30 (91%) experienced swelling, 31 (94%) complained of pain, and four (12%) had significant restrictions in flexion or extension (less than 15°). Postoperatively, 14 patients (42%) received radiotherapy.

In addition to the lesions observed in the synovial membrane in situ, other intra‐articular sites, such as the subtalar joint, crucial ligaments, tendons, and even bone structures, were also found to be affected during the surgical procedure. Extra‐articular lesions often arise from intra‐articular pathologies invading various tendon sheaths and extending along these sheaths, and the most common lesions are in the tarsal tunnel (83%, 15/18). These contents include the sheaths of the FHLT, FDLT, and TPT. Furthermore, the proportion of patients with a tendon sheath in the fibularis longus/brevis was 61% (11/18). Some lesions even extended toward the plantar aspect. The lesions compressed the tibial nerve, branches of the peroneal nerve, and dorsalis pedis artery in a minority of patients.

Under arthroscopy, 12 patients (36%) presented with talus cartilage lesions ranging from Outerbridge grade II to IV, and BMS was performed as needed. Three patients exhibited anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) injuries, two of whom healed through scarring (not treated due to a negative anterior drawer test), and one underwent ligament repair surgery. One patient had a subchondral cyst in the talus, and the other had a cyst at the distal end of the tibia; both patients received BMS after lesion clearance. Clinical Outcomes

At the last follow‐up, according to the patient‐reported outcome measures, the median VAS score was 1 (range, 0–4), the median AOFAS score was 96 (range, 65–100), and the median Kofoed score was 96 (range, 67–100). Among them, 27 patients (82%) achieved PASS based on AOFAS scores. There were no significant differences between Group A and Group AO in terms of scores or the number of patients who achieved PASS (12 vs 15, p > 0.05) (Table 2).

Among the 33 patients, five (15%) experienced recurrence, with a median recurrence time of 48 months (range, 28–84 months). These five patients did not receive radiotherapy after the initial surgery but underwent surgical intervention and radiotherapy after recurrence, resulting in successful recovery. Postoperatively, no ankle joint stiffness was observed in all patients, and no indications of wound infection, deep vein thrombosis, sensory abnormalities, or damage to nerves, blood vessels, or tendons were present. Discussion

This study is one of the few large‐scale studies reporting arthroscopic treatment of ankle Dt‐TGCTs. The most significant finding was that the clinical outcomes of arthroscopic excision of Dt‐TGCTs via a multiportal approach were favorable, with satisfactory functional scores at the final follow‐up and a low recurrence rate. Additionally, the lesion extended along the tendon sheath to the extra‐articular region, necessitating combined open excision surgery.

Advantages and considerations of multiportal arthroscopic techniques

The main aim of surgical methods used to treat TGCTs is complete resection to minimize the risk of recurrence. Despite the availability of various surgical techniques, all methods, especially those for Dt‐TGCTs, are associated with a high recurrence rate. 9 Previous studies 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 15 , 20 that included at least 10 patients who underwent Dt‐TGCT involving the ankle joint or ankle and foot revealed that, without considering postoperative treatments, the recurrence rate for open excision ranged from 13% to 64%, while for arthroscopic surgery, recurrence ranged from 0% to 64%. The overall recurrence rate for surgical interventions ranged from 0% to 64%. However, overall, the limited number of patients and high data heterogeneity compromised the reliability of the recurrence rates (Appendix Table A1 provides a table of results from the review of previous studies for reference). Spierenburg et al. 6 employed arthroscopy for the treatment of ankle Dt‐TGCTs, for which a notable postoperative recurrence rate of 64% (7/11) was observed. Owing to the high recurrence rate, open surgery was recommended as the primary treatment approach. It is essential to note that surgeons utilize a dual‐portal arthroscopic approach, which might not allow full visualization of the entire ankle joint cavity. Additionally, the chosen open surgical procedure resulted in a recurrence rate of 59% (23/39). In the latest large‐scale study conducted by Barnett et al., 10 the recurrence rate for open excision was as high as 37% (13/35). Ma et al. 21 proposed that some lesions in the mini spaces of the joint could be removed through arthroscopic excision but not through open excision; a greater recurrence rate was associated with open surgery than for arthroscopic procedures. In our study, for intra‐articular lesions, the multiportal approach provided ample exposure to nearly the entire ankle joint space, facilitating the detection of lesions. Arthroscopy allowed for a clear view, enabling precise delineation of lesion boundaries and simultaneous management of some coexisting conditions. The application of RFV effectively controlled bleeding, avoiding the potential issue of blurred lesion boundaries caused by conventional open‐incision bleeding. Furthermore, due to the initial arthroscopic excision, the number and length of open surgical incisions were reduced to some extent, minimizing trauma, alleviating postoperative pain, and shortening the recovery time. In contrast to the findings of previous studies, our recurrence rate was relatively low (15%, 5/33), and the favorable performance of our approach was indicated by patient‐reported scores, with 82% of patients achieving PASS, highlighting the advantages of the multiportal arthroscopic approach.

However, Dt‐TGCGs often involve critical structures such as neurovascular bundles and tendons. At the beginning of arthroscopic surgery, anatomical structures are often unclear, requiring very careful handling, and complete synovectomy requires considerable patience from the surgeon. Regarding the medial malleolus‐assisted portal technique introduced in the current study, although the innovative use of a PDS suture provides traction and protection for the posterior tibial neurovascular bundle, inexperienced surgeons may still pose a risk of neurovascular injury. Additionally, the number of portals needs to be carefully considered. For example, if preoperative and intraoperative evidence confirms that the lesion is located in the anterior compartment, unnecessary posterior portals should be avoided. Similarly, if evidence indicates that the lesion is located solely outside the joint capsule, arthroscopic intervention would be superficial.

Combined Limited Open‐Incision Procedure

The presence of extra‐articular lesions was identified as one of the factors contributing to the failure of arthroscopic procedures. 11 During the procedure, the contents of the tarsal tunnel (83%) and the sheath of the fibularis longus and brevis tendons (61%) were among the most commonly affected structures. The lesions extended along the tendon sheaths, sometimes reaching the plantar aspect, necessitating the choice of a combined limited open incision procedure. However, regardless of the method chosen, carefulness is imperative when attempting to protect vulnerable blood vessels and nerves. Owing to the comprehensive exposure and meticulous excision performed in both Group A and Group AO, no significant differences in clinical outcomes between the two groups were observed.

Coexisting Conditions and Management

TGCTs are thought to be related to trauma and joint hemorrhage. 22 In the present investigation, 42% of patients had a history of trauma, 93% of whom had a history of twisting injuries. Interestingly, intraoperative examination revealed evidence of ATFL injury in only three patients, with two patients showing well‐healed scars. However, 36% of the patients had Outerbridge grade II−IV cartilage injuries, and an additional two patients had subchondral bone cysts, both of which may be associated with trauma or erosive changes. Li et al. 4 reported successful outcomes of open synovectomy combined with bone grafting in patients with bone erosion and subchondral bone cysts. In our patients, the cartilage injuries and cysts were not severe, and only debridement and BMS were performed after assessment.

Postoperative Radiotherapy and Other Treatments

Postoperative radiotherapy has gained increasing attention and can be categorized into intra‐articular radiotherapy and localized external beam radiotherapy. In particular, intra‐articular radiotherapy has been gradually phased out due to the high susceptibility to severe localized full‐thickness skin necrosis observed after treatment. 23 Concerning external beam radiotherapy, Lee et al. 24 reported the outcomes of seven patients who underwent initial open surgery, and the subsequent 24‐month follow‐up examination revealed no signs of recurrence. Guo et al. 11 reported that seven patients who underwent initial arthroscopic surgery showed no signs of recurrence postoperatively (median follow‐up time of 26 months). However, among 15 patients who initially underwent arthroscopy combined with open excision, five (33%) recurred (median follow‐up time of 48 months). This phenomenon may be attributed to the more extensive invasion of the extra‐articular tissue. 12 Certainly, some researchers in the past believed that arthroscopic or open synovectomy yielded excellent outcomes, rendering postoperative radiotherapy unnecessary. 7 Nevertheless, Baniel et al. 25 recently reported that external radiation therapy provided excellent local control and good functional status, with minimal treatment‐related complications. In the current study, despite postoperative radiotherapy being recommended in all patients, only 14 patients (42%) received it, possibly due to limitations such as economic constraints or distance. Among these patients, no complications or recurrences were observed. However, among the 19 patients (58%) who did not receive radiotherapy despite undergoing thorough excision, five experienced recurrence, three of whom presented extensive extra‐articular invasion during the initial surgery. We acknowledge the significance of radiotherapy, as its integration with diverse surgical techniques has the potential to further reduce the recurrence rate. Hence, actively increasing the rate of radiotherapy in the future will be our goal.

Grounded in the principles of genetics, biological targeted therapy has yielded promising outcomes. Treatment with imatinib mesylate improved symptoms in 73% of patients, 26 while pexidartinib also offered modest pain relief for patients with TGCTs who chose not to undergo surgery or were deemed unsuitable for surgery. 27 However, the adverse reactions caused by the long‐term use of these drugs and their high cost limit the application of targeted drugs.

Limitations

Limitations of the current study include its retrospective design and an insufficiently large sample size. The insufficiently large sample size may have led to several continuous variables, including outcome scores, tending toward non‐normal distribution. Therefore, we presented the results as median (range), as done in previous studies. 11 , 19 Additionally, preoperative patient‐reported scores were not documented, which limited the assessment of the extent of the surgical impact; however, we incorporated the proportion of patients who achieved PASS as an alternative measure. Notably, only the results of Group A and Group AO are presented and briefly compared, as they differed in baseline characteristics such as the extent of involvement and incision size. Furthermore, our study did not identify specific factors associated with recurrence. Future research should involve long‐term studies with larger patient cohorts to establish the clinical efficacy of this technique.

Prospects of Clinical Application

Using multiportal arthroscopic‐assisted techniques, including the medial malleolus‐assisted portal and tarsal sinus portal, almost all the entire ankle joint space can be exposed. This approach enables the complete resection of Dt‐TGCTs confined to the ankle joint cavity. For Dt‐TGCTs extending beyond the ankle joint, initially employing multiportal arthroscopy can reduce the number of open incisions. Thus, multiportal arthroscopy is a valuable technique for patients prioritizing aesthetics and early recovery. However, care must be taken to avoid injuring the neurovascular structures around the ankle, particularly the posterior tibial nerve and vessels. Complete resection requires sufficient patience and repeated examination.

Conclusion

Our work suggested that multiportal arthroscopy‐assisted surgical resection of ankle Dt‐TGCTs is a viable method with favorable functional outcomes and a relatively low recurrence rate. When tendons are involved, the lesion is prone to spreading beyond the joint. To ensure complete lesion removal, a combination of arthroscopic and open surgery is appropriate.

Search strategy

On May 19, 2024, we conducted a search in PubMed and Web of Science using the search terms “((“Giant Cell Tumor of Tendon Sheath”[Mesh] OR “Synovitis, Pigmented Villonodular”[Mesh]) AND (“Ankle Joint”[Mesh] OR “Ankle”[Mesh])) OR ((Tenosynovial giant cell tumor OR Tenosynovial giant cell tumor OR pigmented villonodular synovitis OR TGCT OR PVNS) AND ankle)” with no time restrictions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (i) diagnosed patients with ankle (or foot and ankle) TGCT and classified them into subtypes (diffuse and localized); (ii) included at least 10 patients with diffuse‐type TGCT (Dt‐TGCT); (iii) the patients were primarily treated surgically; and (iv) reported recurrence rates. Studies were excluded if they: (i) included fewer than 10 patients with ankle (or foot and ankle) Dt‐TGCT; (ii) lacked extractable key information such as the number of Dt‐TGCT patients or recurrence rates; (iii) were published in languages other than English; or (iv) were radiological, biomechanical, anatomical, or descriptive studies related to surgical techniques. Due to the limited number of studies and the occasional infeasibility of extracting target data separately, we did not exclude studies under the following conditions: a small number of surgical subjects were recurrent patients, or a few subjects underwent partial lesion excision.

Search results

A total of 642 studies were obtained for screening. After reviewing titles and abstracts, 605 studies were excluded, and the remaining 37 studies underwent full‐text review. Ultimately, eight studies 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 10 , 11 , 15 , 20 were included. Additionally, we reviewed the references in the latest systematic review. 9
