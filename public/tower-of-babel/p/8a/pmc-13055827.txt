Fibromuscular Dysplasia and Cerebrovascular Dissection: Insights From a Multicenter Cohort
Authors: Mckay Hanna, Marianne Khoury, Qasem N. Al Shaer, Digvijaya Navalkele, Bryan Wells, Fadi Nahab, Ghada A. Mohamed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a noninflammatory arteriopathy that may result in arterial stenosis, dissection, aneurysm, and tortuosity. Data remain limited on clinical features and outcomes of patients with FMD presenting with spontaneous cervical artery dissection (SCeAD). This study aimed to describe the characteristics and long‐term outcomes of this population.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter cohort study of patients diagnosed with both SCeAD and FMD at 3 US comprehensive stroke centers (2018–2023). Patients were identified through hospital records and vascular registries. Diagnosis of FMD and SCeAD was confirmed through imaging review by vascular neurologists or cardiologists. Outcomes included recurrent dissections, cardiovascular events (ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack, myocardial infarction, subarachnoid hemorrhage) and mortality.ResultsAmong 1632 patients with SCeAD, 97 (6%) had FMD diagnosis. The cohort was predominantly female (91%) and median age at FMD diagnosis was 50 years (interquartile range, 42–63). Carotid dissections were more frequent than vertebral dissections (86% versus 27%, P<0.001). Multiple dissections occurred in 32 patients (33%): 24 (75%) had bilateral dissections, 4 (13%) had recurrent dissections in the same artery, and 7 (22%) had dissections in other vascular beds including the renal, iliac, mesenteric, and coronary arteries. Younger age (odds ratio [OR], 0.945 [95% CI, 0.908–0.983]; P=0.005) and classical FMD “beading” on imaging (OR, 3.06 [95% CI, 1.28–7.36]; P=0.012) were associated with multiple dissections. Aneurysms were detected in 27%, more frequently in patients with multiple dissections (OR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.02–2.71]; P=0.04). Most patients were discharged on single (49%) or dual (29%) antiplatelet therapy and 22% received anticoagulation with no significant differences in event rates. Over a mean follow‐up of 5±2.5 years, 13% developed recurrent dissections and 28% experienced cardiovascular events. Recurrent dissections were associated with future cardiovascular events (OR, 11.56 [95% CI, 2.22–60.07]; P=0.004).ConclusionsFMD should be considered in patients presenting with SCeAD, particularly middle‐aged women with multifocal dissections and no traditional vascular risk factors. There is an increased risk of dissection recurrence, future cardiovascular events, and harboring aneurysms. These findings highlight the need for further prospective studies that can guide surveillance and management strategies for this high‐risk population.

Fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) is a systemic nonatherosclerotic and noninflammatory disorder of the arterial wall musculature characterized by abnormal cellular proliferation and distortion of the vessel wall in small to medium‐sized arteries. 1 Although its cause remains uncertain, genetic, hormonal, environmental and mechanical factors have all been implicated. 2 In a US cohort of >4500 patients, the prevalence of FMD was estimated at 12 per 100 000, with women comprising ∼85% of cases. 3

FMD most commonly involves the extracranial cervical and renal arteries but can affect nearly any vascular bed and is classified as either multifocal or focal. 4 Multifocal FMD, which is the predominant subtype, produces the classic “string of beads” appearance on imaging characterized by alternating arterial dilations and stenoses typically in the mid to distal vessel segments. 5 Beyond stenosis, FMD is associated with arterial dissections, aneurysms, and vessel tortuosity. 6 , 7 Notably, spontaneous cervical artery dissections (SCeAD) are an important cause of stroke in young and middle‐aged adults and are linked to FMD in ∼15% to 25% of cases. 3 , 8

Early recognition of FMD is critical given the risk of severe vascular complications and the potential benefits of early evaluation and surveillance. 9 However, diagnosis is frequently delayed due to its subtle and often nonspecific clinical presentation. Many patients are asymptomatic or present with vague symptoms such as headache, tinnitus, or hypertension, which can often be attributed to more prevalent conditions. 10 As a result, FMD is frequently discovered incidentally during imaging performed for unrelated reasons. 11

Despite growing recognition of FMD as a risk factor for SCeAD, its influence on clinical management and outcomes remains incompletely defined, and existing guidelines largely treat dissections similarly regardless of FMD status. 5 , 12

This multicenter cohort study aims to address the knowledge gap by characterizing a multicenter cohort of patients with both SCeAD and FMD focusing on demographic features, dissection patterns, aneurysm burden, and identifying predictors of dissection recurrence and vascular complications.

METHODS

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

This is a retrospective multicenter cohort study conducted across three academic medical centers in the southeastern United States: Emory University Hospital, Grady Memorial Hospital, and the Medical University of South Carolina. Adult patients diagnosed with both FMD and nontraumatic SCeAD between January 1, 2018 and December 31, 2023 were eligible for inclusion.

Patients were identified through hospital electronic medical records using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes for nontraumatic carotid and vertebral arterial dissection (Table S1) and institutional vascular registries of patients hospitalized or seen and followed at stroke or vascular clinics in the participating institutions. SCeAD diagnoses were confirmed through characteristic imaging of dissection including the presence of an intimal flap, double lumen, flame shape occlusion, wall irregularity, or pseudoaneurysm in the carotid or vertebral arteries in the absence of procedural or significant traumatic causes. FMD diagnoses were confirmed based on chart‐reported diagnosis of FMD or clinical features and vascular imaging including computed tomography, magnetic resonance (MR) angiography, or catheter‐based angiography demonstrating classic findings such as a string‐of‐beads appearance, arterial tortuosity, and multifocal vascular involvement. Genetic testing for connective tissue diseases, when available, was also reviewed. All patients underwent head and neck vascular imaging (computed tomography angiography or MR angiography) as standard of care protocol. Body vascular imaging was performed at the discretion of the treating physician or based on clinical indication (n=82), with reported image features being beading, fusiform ectasia, or dissections. The presence of aneurysms or dissections in body vascular beds was documented based on the available imaging. A board‐certified vascular neurologist or cardiologist adjudicated all diagnoses. Exclusion criteria included iatrogenic dissections, known connective tissue disorders (eg, vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome, Marfan syndrome), or incomplete head and neck imaging data.

Clinical data were collected using a standardized case report form and included demographics, vascular risk factors, clinical presentation, imaging findings, inpatient procedures, treatment details and follow‐up outcomes. Outcomes of interest were recurrent dissections, vascular events including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attack (TIA), myocardial infarction, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and mortality. The treating physician made the medical management choice based on individual factors or institutional protocol regarding medical management of vascular dissection. Discharge medications were categorized as single antiplatelet therapy, dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, or combination therapy. The institutional review boards approved the study protocol of all participating institutions. Informed consent was not required given the retrospective nature of the study.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analysis was conducted as follows: Continuous variables were evaluated for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and reported as mean±SD if normally distributed, or median with interquartile ranges if nonnormally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Groups comparisons were performed using the chi‐square test for categorical variables and the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables, as appropriate. To explore potential associations between clinical/radiographic factors and the predefined outcomes, we performed univariable and multivariable analysis using Binary logistic regression, variables with a P value <0.10 in univariable analysis were included in multivariable models. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs were reported. All statistical tests were 2 sided, and significance was defined as P<0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 29; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

A total of 1632 patients with SCeAD were screened and 97 (6%) met the inclusion criteria. The cohort was predominantly female (88 patients, 91%) and White (77 patients, 75%), with a median age at FMD diagnosis of 50 years (interquartile range, 42–63). Comprehensive vascular imaging beyond the head and neck vasculature was performed in 82 patients (75%). Over a mean follow‐up of 5±2.5 years, 13 patients (13%) experienced recurrent dissections. Three of the 13 patients (23%) with recurrent dissection experienced TIAs at the time of recurrence. The remainder had asymptomatic or nonspecific presentations. These patients were significantly younger (median age 40 versus 51 years; P=0.003), and younger age remained a significant predictor of recurrence (OR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.825–0.962]; P=0.003). Twenty‐seven patients (28%) experienced major vascular events (ischemic stroke, TIA, subarachnoid hemorrhage, or myocardial infarction) and 4 patients (4%) died. Recurrent dissections were associated with subsequent vascular events (OR, 11.56 [95% CI, 2.22–60.07]; P=0.004) (Table 4). Seven patients (7%) underwent stent placement during the acute phase in the setting of impaired perfusion with no reported complications. Stents were more frequently placed in patients with multiple dissections (5/32) than in those with single dissections (2/65, P=0.003).

Among the 59 patients with available data on discharge medications, 29 (49%) were treated with single antiplatelet therapy, 17 (29%) received dual antiplatelet therapy, and 8 (14%) were discharged on short‐term anticoagulation either alone or in combination with single antiplatelet therapy followed by single antiplatelet therapy. Five patients (9%) were discharged on long‐term anticoagulation. No significant difference in event rates was observed between patients treated with antiplatelet versus anticoagulant therapy in this subgroup (P=0.18).

DISCUSSION

In this multicenter cohort, we describe the clinical, radiographic, and outcome features of patients with SCeAD and confirmed FMD. Consistent with the known demographic profile of FMD, the median age was 50 years and 91% were women. 13 , 14 , 15 Hormonal factors, including estrogen‐mediated vascular remodeling, may contribute to increased arterial fragility in women. 16 , 17

Carotid artery dissections were far more common than vertebral (86% versus 14%), aligning with literature indicating a predilection for extracranial carotid involvement in FMD‐related dissections. 18 , 19 Nearly one third of patients had multiple dissections frequently bilateral or affecting renal and mesenteric arteries similar to prior reports showing elevated rates of multifocal dissections in FMD (15% versus 4% in non‐FMD). 18 , 20 , 21 , 22 Recurrent dissections occurred in 11 cases: 4 in the same vessel and 7 in other vascular beds. Younger age and FMD in noncervical arteries were independently associated with multiple dissections, suggesting that early onset and systemic vascular involvement may indicate a more aggressive FMD phenotype. These findings support the need to evaluate for FMD in patients with bilateral dissections, even when classical angiographic beading is absent.

Although FMD predominantly affects the cervical carotid and vertebral arteries, intracranial involvement is rare and usually reflects contiguous extension. 23 Extracervical FMD may affect the renal, mesenteric, hepatic, splenic, and coronary arteries; changes include beading, fusiform ectasia, or spontaneous dissection. 24 Diagnosis is often underrecognized due to incomplete imaging and the absence of standard screening beyond the dissected territory. 25 In our cohort, one third of patients had noncentral nervous system FMD findings on body imaging, highlighting the value of comprehensive vascular screening, particularly in young women without traditional risk factors or in patients with recurrent, bilateral, or multifocal dissections. 26 Systemic imaging may also uncover alternative diagnoses such as inflammatory vasculopathies or connective tissue disorders (eg, vascular Ehlers–Danlos or Loeys–Dietz syndrome). 1

Systematic screening with body computed tomography angiography/MR angiography has proven effective in patients with FMD and SCeAD, as demonstrated in the ARCADIA (Assessment of Renal and Cervical Artery Dysplasia) and ARCADIA‐POL (High Incidence and Clinical Characteristics of Fibromuscular Dysplasia in Patients With Spontaneous Cervical Artery Dissection) studies, which detected additional dissections not identified at initial presentation. 6 , 8 Current American Heart Association guidelines recommend head MR angiography in patients with cervical artery FMD 25 but optimal imaging intervals remain undefined.

Aneurysms were found in 27% of our cohort, including intracranial aneurysms in more than one third of these cases. Prior studies have reported aneurysm prevalence in FMD ranging from 7% to >50%, depending on imaging protocols and cohort selection. Data from the US FMD Registry reported a more consistent intracranial aneurysm prevalence of 12.9%. 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Patients with multiple dissections in our cohort were more likely to harbor multiple aneurysms, suggesting shared mechanisms of vascular fragility. This is consistent with higher rates of renal and intracranial aneurysms in FMD compared with the general population, 4 , 14 , 18 though it remains uncertain whether other unique risk factors can contribute. 31 Our elevated aneurysm rate likely reflects comprehensive neurovascular imaging in a symptomatic stroke‐prone population, in contrast to cohorts where FMD is incidentally detected.

SCeAD is a leading cause of ischemic stroke in young adults and has been associated with FMD in up to 20% of cases. 3 , 32 Stroke and TIA rates vary widely in populations with FMD: 7% and 9% in the US FMD Registry and up to 19.2% in others. 13 , 22 In our cohort, 25% presented with stroke, reflecting an enriched sample of patients with symptomatic SCeAD rather than incidental FMD. Carotid dissections dominant in our cohort are more strongly associated with stroke than vertebral involvement. Nonspecific symptoms such as headache or neck pain were also common, emphasizing the need for clinical vigilance to diagnose dissections in patients with suspected or known FMD. 33

Recurrent SCeAD is uncommon in the general population (0.7%–1.9% per patient‐year) but occurs more frequently in FMD (hazard ratio, 2.36 [95% CI, 1.13–4.90]), 22 other studies report a 3‐fold increased recurrence risk in FMD and a 3.3% recurrence rate at 35 months. 34 A study reported that FMD‐related cervical artery dissection has a greater than 3‐fold increased risk of recurrent dissection compared with those without FMD. 14 Another study reported that the recurrence rate in a 35‐month follow‐up was 3.3%. 10 In our cohort, the 5‐year rate of recurrent dissection was 13%. This higher detection rate reflects the nature of our cohort of patients having both symptomatic SCeAD confirmed FMD and potential differences in screening and surveillance imaging protocols. In this cohort, TIA was a common presentation of dissection recurrence.

Medical management remains an area of uncertainty for SCeAD including in patients with underlying FMD. Antiplatelet therapy, alone or in combination, was the most common strategy, whereas anticoagulant use was limited. No significant differences in outcomes were detected between antithrombotic regimens; however, our study was not powered to assess treatment effects. In addition, antithrombotic decisions were made by treating physicians based on individual factors including the severity of the ischemic event, stent placement, preexisting indications like atrial fibrillation, or institutional protocol regarding medical management of vascular dissection. 33 A small subgroup of patients underwent endovascular carotid artery stenting during the acute phase, and it appears feasible and safe in FMD patients with favorable outcomes and no reported complications.

Stroke recurrence in our cohort was 6% at 5‐year follow‐up and comparable to rates reported in other FMD registries 15 , 22 and in studies focusing on SCeAD more broadly (2.5%–4.4%). 35 , 36 Although FMD is often considered to have a benign prognosis, our results highlight the need for vigilant follow‐up and tailored management once patients become symptomatic with dissections or ischemic events.

This study findings carry important implications for stroke systems of care and screening strategies. No guidelines currently recommend routine vascular imaging beyond the cerebrovascular territory in patients with SCeAD. However, our study shows that comprehensive vascular screening particularly in women <50 with bilateral or multifocal dissections or confirmed FMD may detect silent aneurysms and systemic disease. This supports consideration of whole‐body computed tomography angiography or MR angiography in selected patients. Early recognition of FMD may inform surveillance, guide antithrombotic strategies, and enable multidisciplinary care involving neurology, vascular medicine, and genetics. Integration of FMD screening into stroke pathways may improve recognition of systemic arteriopathies and optimize risk management. Prospective studies are needed to validate these findings and guide evidence‐based recommendations for imaging, surveillance, and treatment of FMD in SCeAD.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design introduces selection and information bias. Missing data on antithrombotic strategies may have influenced outcome comparisons. Treatment choices were not standardized and may reflect provider preferences or imaging nuances. Imaging heterogeneity also risks underdiagnosing subclinical and asymptomatic FMD. Given the rarity of FMD and absence of standardized screening protocols, vascular involvement in other territories may be underestimated. Due to the small number of incidental FMD cases we could not conduct a formal comparison of cardiovascular risk or imaging features with symptomatic cases. Finally, our regression models were exploratory and constrained by sample size. Nonetheless, this study remains among the largest to date complementing existing literature by providing a multicenter, clinically enriched perspective focused on real‐world presentation, extended imaging surveillance, and longitudinal outcomes in patients with SCeAD and FMD, offering insights into recurrence predictors and practical implications for vascular screening.

CONCLUSIONS

FMD should be considered in patients with SCeAD, even in the absence of classic imaging findings, particularly in middle‐aged women without traditional vascular risk factors. Our findings suggest that recurrence is more likely in patients who are younger and have multi‐territory vascular involvement. This population also appears to carry a higher burden of silent aneurysms and an elevated risk of future cardiovascular events. These results underscore the potential value of comprehensive vascular surveillance and systematic imaging in selected patients with SCeAD with suspected or confirmed FMD. This multicenter cohort, among the largest to date exploring the SCeAD and FMD intersection, highlights the urgent need for prospective research to refine imaging protocols, risk stratification, and therapeutic strategies in this high‐risk group.
