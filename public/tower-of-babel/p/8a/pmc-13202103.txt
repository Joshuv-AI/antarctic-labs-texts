In their own words: experiences surrounding masculinising chest surgery among transgender and gender-diverse adolescents – a secondary analysis of GENDER-Q data
Authors: Kathleen Armstrong, Ivy Quan, Shelby L Kennedy, Manraj N Kaur, Sylvie D Cornacchi, Charlene Rae, Natasha Johnson, Karine Khatchadourian, Maja Marinkovic, Gina M Sequeira, Brenda Bradley, Kevin Cheung, Shane D Morrison, Anne F Klassen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMasculinising chest surgery, also known as top surgery, is the most requested gender-affirming procedure among transgender and gender-diverse (TGD) adolescents, yet research on patient experiences remains limited. This study explored the experiences of TGD adolescents who were seeking or had undergone masculinising chest surgery.DesignQualitative secondary analysis using existing themes framework and data from the GENDER-Q (GQ) and GENDER-Q Youth (GQY) research programmes, which aim to develop comprehensive patient-reported outcome measures for gender-affirming care.SettingParticipants were sampled from five high-volume gender-affirming care clinics, three in Canada and two in the United States. Interviews were conducted online.Participants35 GQ and GQY participants aged 13–18 years who were assigned female at birth, identified as trans men or non-binary, and were pursuing (n=19) or had undergone (n=16) masculinising chest surgery.ResultsThree major themes emerged: chest appearance, health-related quality of life (HRQL) and gender practices. Most participants expected a flatter chest that aesthetically aligned with their gender identity. Presurgery participants anticipated that surgery would allow them to engage in previously avoided physical activities and would enhance their relationships. Postoperative participants reported increased physical activity, mental resilience, bodily connection and social comfort. Most reported binder use and related reliance or discomfort as motivators for pursuing surgery.ConclusionsThis study highlights the multidimensional experiences surrounding masculinising chest surgery on TGD adolescents with impacts on chest appearance, HRQL and gender practices. Centering adolescents’ perspectives, these findings underscore the importance of accessible, affirming surgical care and provide valuable insights for clinicians, policymakers and future research.

Introduction

Transgender and gender diverse (TGD) individuals have gender identities that differ from their sex assigned at birth. Some transmasculine TGD adolescents seek gender-affirming medical care to align their bodies with their gender identity, including puberty blockers, hormone replacement therapy and/or masculinising chest surgery also known as ‘top surgery’.1 2 Among TGD adolescents, the number of top surgeries has increased over the years.3 However, the number of these surgeries in TGD adolescents is relatively small: the vast majority of masculinising chest surgeries are performed in TGD adults, and a far greater number of chest-reducing procedures are performed in cisgender adolescents for other indications, such as gynecomastia or macromastia.4 Access to gender-affirming care for adolescents is increasingly shaped by shifting political and regulatory landscapes around the world. In several countries, including the USA, England and Sweden there have been recent efforts to regulate access to such care for adolescents.57 Against this backdrop, understanding the experiences of TGD youth may help inform ethical, patient-centred care and evidence-based policy discussions.

Significant gaps remain in understanding TGD patient experiences and patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are needed. To address this gap, the GENDER-Q (GQ) was developed as a comprehensive PROM for gender-affirming care.810 The GQ includes 54 independently scored scales and two checklists, including three relevant to masculinising chest surgery: satisfaction with chest appearance, health-related quality of life (HRQL) and gender practice. The GENDER-Q Youth (GQY) module, designed for individuals aged 12–25 years, has a distinct conceptual framework with some overlap with GQ scales. An important difference between the two modules is that the surgical scales are only included in the GQ study with adults aged 18 years and older. However, qualitative interviews revealed that most adolescents (35/36 eligible participants) in both concept elicitation studies directly referenced their expectations and experiences with masculinising chest surgery.

Understanding adolescents’ preoperative and postoperative experiences is essential for preoperative education, expectation setting and shared decision-making.11 While quantitative research provides valuable insights into how chest surgery impacts chest satisfaction and HRQL, qualitative research contextualises these findings by capturing lived experiences.12 This paper is a secondary analysis of broader concept elicitation interviews from the GQ and GQY, specifically exploring the preoperative and postoperative experiences of TGD adolescents who were seeking or had undergone masculinising chest surgery, offering important insights into the broader impact of gender-affirming care.

Methods

This qualitative study is a secondary analysis using the existing broader themes framework from a larger psychometric research project aiming to develop PROMs for individuals seeking gender-affirming care and services, called the GQ (18 years and older) and the GQY (12–19 years).8 9 Detailed methods and results of the GQ and GQY studies are reported elsewhere.8 9

Study sample and recruitment

For the concept elicitation interviews from the GQY study, English-speaking participants aged 12–18 were recruited through purposeful sampling at five high-volume gender-affirming care clinics (three in Canada, two in the USA), as well as through limited snowball sampling of eligible youth outside of these clinical settings.9 The research coordinator provided study details, obtained written informed consent (involving parents/caregivers when appropriate based on regional age requirements for consent), and scheduled interviews. Individuals seeking gender-affirming care for reasons other than gender dysphoria (e.g., disorder of sexual development) were not eligible. Qualitative data from interviews with adolescents as part of the GQ study for this age group was also included (n=6). This study focused on a subset of GQY and GQ participants who were aged 13-18 years who were assigned female at birth, identified as trans men or non-binary, and were pursuing (n=19) or had undergone (n=16) masculinising chest surgery. Of the 47 GQY participants, 30 were trans men or non-binary individuals assigned female at birth (AFAB); 29 of them directly referenced ‘top surgery’ in their concept elicitation interview. This study included 6 interviews from the GQ study (February 2019–February 2020) and 29 interviews from the GQY study (November 2021–October 2023).

Study procedures

An experienced qualitative researcher conducted one-on-one concept elicitation interviews with each participant using Zoom V.5.8.4. Using an interview guide (included in online supplemental file), the interviewer elicited participants’ experiences with gender-affirming care, surgical procedures, surgical recovery, appearance, voice, psychological well-being, body image, social life and physical function.8 9 All interviews were digitally recorded and transcribed verbatim with all identifying information removed. Interviews continued until thematic saturation was reached, with no new themes emerging. All interview data were anonymised prior to access by this secondary analysis team. The data were stored on a secure cloud-based drive associated with McMaster University.

Analysis

Interviews were conducted and analysed concurrently using a line-by-line approach, allowing for iterative refinement of the interview guide (available as online supplemental material).13 14 The analysis resulted in a conceptual framework and an item pool that informed the development of the GQ and the GQY.8 9 For this secondary analysis, all coded quotes mentioning ‘top surgery’ were extracted and independently reviewed by two additional researchers. The secondary analysis team included clinicians and qualitative researchers with experience in gender-affirming care and PROM development. Analytic discussions included reflexive consideration of how researchers’ clinical and disciplinary perspectives could shape interpretation of the data. We applied an interpretive description approach which serves to understand phenomena through how participants experience them.15 The existing coding structure from the GQ and GQY modules was maintained, including top-level themes and primary subthemes, while secondary and tertiary subthemes were refined as needed. To ensure rigour, the team held regular debriefing sessions to review coding decisions and establish conceptual consensus.

Chest appearance

Chest appearance emerged as a major theme in the data, with most participants sharing the expectation that their chest would aesthetically align with their gender identity. This theme encompassed three subthemes: chest contour, nipples, and scars. Most participants desired a flat chest as a surgical outcome.

“Really all I want out of it is a flat chest and if there are complications along the way, then so be it.” [Wants chest surgery (CS), Age 17]

“I decided to go with the more masculine, which means that they cut off your nipples completely, reshape them, and then stick them back on top of your skin and they do a graft. […] I want more of the appearance than the feeling.” [Had CS, Age 15]

In contrast to the consistency of chest contour goals, participants expressed varied perspectives on scarring, ranging from acceptance and indifference to concern. Some viewed their scars as tokens of their surgery journey, while others considered them an aesthetic concern.

“The scars are prominent. I don’t mind them necessarily, because to me I think that scars are kind of…the idea of scars are cool.” [Had CS, Age 17]

Health-related quality of life

HRQL was used to capture subthemes related to physical activity, social, and psychological well-being, and body image.

Physical activity/Movement

Adolescents reported feeling that their chest restricted their ability to participate in physical activities, such as gym class, personal exercise or specific sports. A common sentiment was that running, jumping, or moving freely felt difficult prior to chest surgery. For many, the desire to engage in these activities more comfortably served as key motivators for undergoing chest surgery.

“I felt really dysphoric while doing [physical activities] and I had to wear sports bras that were compact but not necessarily, quote, ‘binding’. So it was really uncomfortable to move in that way where my chest would move.” [Wants CS, Age 15]

Adolescents who participated in sports and bound their chest often found binding during physical activity challenging.

“The reason I stopped [sports] initially was because I didn’t want to go out and have people see me in a sports bra. But I also didn’t want to run in a chest binder.” [Wants CS, Age 17]

“I did track and field and I did cross country and I binded while doing that and it was a lot harder, I think, definitely than if I hadn’t been. Breathing was very hard when exercising and I would get hot and not be able to breathe but, I would still do it, I would still wear them, ‘cause I didn’t really feel like I had anything else I could do.” [Had CS, Age 16]

Many adolescents avoided swimming pre-operatively. While some attempted to use swim-specific binders, they still found them uncomfortable. Many adolescents expressed a strong desire to swim shirtless, highlighting this as a motivator for chest surgery.

“I’m hoping that I can not have to wear a binder while swimming and because binders can suck while having to do at least eight laps to try and train to become a lifeguard because that’s what I’m currently working on.” [Wants CS, Age 13]

After undergoing chest surgery, many adolescents described the experience as freeing, emphasising how it enabled them to move, exercise, and swim without restrictions.

“I’ll probably do swim team next year but I had to wear a rash guard to swim team. And I felt immense dysphoria with that, especially when I was just wearing a binder and no rash guard, you could still obviously see that my chest was not flat and it was an awful feeling. But this will be my first year where I don’t have to wear a binder.” [Had CS, Age 16]

Overall, participants described chest surgery as facilitating greater participation in various physical activities, allowing adolescents to move more freely and comfortably.

Psychological well-being

Participants who discussed psychological well-being in relation to chest surgery found that surgery enhanced their mental resilience and decreased their overall mental burden.

“I was not in a place where I was able to do trauma processing, because I was so dysphoric that I was incredibly depressed, and I wasn’t able to handle any of that. And, getting through the top surgery has stabilized my entire emotional state so much that I'm now able to actually focus on dealing with this trauma that I have and working through it and healing from it and moving on with my life.” [Had CS, Age 17]

“[Chest surgery] really makes quality of life a lot better especially with someone who already has to struggle with mental health and stuff […] There is this whole part that is not eliminated, but it’s so small now that I can do other things with my life. Like draw and work on my art and stuff. I don’t have to worry about my physical self.” [Had CS, Age 17]

Body image

Participants often described chest-specific dysphoria before surgery, with many expressing that their breasts did not feel like a part of their body. Some further explained that this disconnect made it difficult to engage with their body in everyday situations, such as looking in the mirror or showering.

“I don’t want to say that [chest surgery] itself would like complete me, but I feel like there is something on me that doesn’t feel like it should be there. When I go whoop, there is something there, it doesn’t feel like an actual part of my body. It feels like it shouldn’t be there.” [Wants CS, Age 17]

Postoperative participants reported a greater sense of ownership over their bodies, expressing that interacting with their bodies felt significantly easier and more natural after surgery. They also described how chest surgery increased their confidence. These participants highlighted the deep connection between body ownership, self-assurance and social engagement, emphasising how increased confidence enhanced their relationships and daily interactions.

“It felt like, now, if I look at my chest, I can't even imagine beforehand. It just feels like this is always how it was supposed to be and how it was supposed to look on me. This is how I’m supposed to look.” [Had CS, Age 17]

“[Chest surgery] has completely changed my perception of my body and my relationship to my body and the general way that I interact with my body emotionally. My ownership of my body has increased a lot because it, like I said, it feels like it’s mine now and as a result, all of that dysphoria vanished.” [Had CS, Age 17]

Social

Many adolescents expected that social interactions and public outings would become significantly easier after chest surgery. This expectation was driven by two main reasons: (1) an improved self-image and confidence, as discussed in the ‘body image’ subtheme and (2) a decreased mental burden, as participants often felt that the gender incongruence in appearance required constant monitoring of their posture, clothing and binders in public.

“Like, going out in public is so much easier. Like, I don’t have to constantly think about, like, is my position correct? Am I hiding my chest enough? Are other people gonna think about my chest or, ow, my back hurts; ow, my chest hurts.” [Had CS, Age 18]

A frequent expectation was that chest surgery would facilitate adolescents’ ability to be recognised as their affirmed gender, particularly in interactions with strangers or unfamiliar individuals.

“So, after I got my top surgery, I’ve noticed that it’s a lot easier to talk to people because they immediately are, like, okay, this guy’s a dude, that’s easy. So, there’s no awkwardness anymore.” [Had CS, Age 15]

Chest surgery also impacted closer relationships, including those with friends and romantic relationships. Before surgery, many avoided social gatherings and hesitated to pursue romantic or sexual relationships. After surgery, participants reported feeling more comfortable in social settings.

“I had never dated anyone before I got surgery. I don't know, everything just felt, like, weird and wrong about dating when I was a girl but now, after coming out and getting surgery, yeah, I finally got a boyfriend.” [Had CS, Age 18]

The data demonstrate the broad social impact of chest surgery on adolescents, influencing their interactions across various social circles, including public settings, peer relationships and romantic partnerships.

Gender practices

Many TGD adolescents expressed heavy reliance on their binders for daily activities such as attending school or going out in public. Some participants shared that they felt unable to leave the house or participate in everyday tasks without wearing one.

“I stay sick home from school if I can’t find my binder or if I don’t have a clean binder, because I can’t not be flat-chested. That would be the worst.” [Wants CS, Age 17]

While binding helped adolescents achieve their desired presentation in public, it was not always sufficient for concealment. For some, it was merely inconvenient, while for others, it caused significant discomfort or even pain.

“I’m very happy I got top surgery when I did, because that was awful. And it’s like … it [binder rubbing against skin] happens every day… There is nothing that I could really think of to prevent it, so I just had to deal with it. It sucked. I really hated that obviously.” [Had CS, Age 15]

For postoperative participants, not needing to bind after chest surgery was closely linked to improved HRQL. Not needing chest concealment also allowed adolescents to express themselves through clothing, contributing to a greater sense of ease and comfort in their lives.

“Being able to wear outfits that I like will be exciting for me. And, I don't know, just exist and live with my chest now is just generally exciting for me.” [Had CS, Age 18]

“If I get too hot, I can just take off my shirt or stuff which is really great.” [Had CS, Age 17]

Discussion

This qualitative study of the experiences of 35 adolescents from Canada and the USA who were seeking or had undergone masculinising chest surgery discussed impacts across three key areas: (1) chest appearance, (2) HRQL (physical activity, social well-being, psychological well-being and body image) and (3) gender practices. Many adolescents articulated the profound ways in which chest surgery shaped or was expected to shape their lives. Across many subthemes, the preoperative goals expressed by adolescents seeking chest surgery were consistent with the post-operative experiences described by those who had chest surgery. This alignment is evident in quotations throughout the study and those presented in table 2. The extensive use of verbatim quotations reflects the interpretive description approach and our commitment to centring adolescent perspectives in a field where their voices are often mediated through adult discourse.

Comparison with other studies

These findings align with previous research on TGD adolescents, which demonstrated a positive relationship between masculinising chest surgery and improvements in chest dysphoria, gender congruence, and body image.1719 This study also supports findings from broader research in adult populations, which show improvements in body image, psychosocial well-being and chest appearance after chest surgery—that are relevant to adolescents.102022 This study builds on earlier research, offering valuable insights for patients, healthcare providers and policymakers on the impacts of masculinising chest surgery for TGD adolescents. By centring the perspectives of TGD adolescents, the findings presented here bridge measurable outcomes with real-world experiences, reinforcing the lived impact of surgical interventions.

Historically, research on gender-affirming care has broadly focused on psychiatric symptoms or psychological outcomes, such as reduction in anxiety, depression or suicidality.1720 2227 While these are important considerations, this study underscores the need for a comprehensive, multidimensional approach that includes a broader range of outcomes such as chest satisfaction, body image and physical and social well-being.

Limitations

Limitations of this study include the potential for selection bias which may have preferentially drawn individuals with a positive experience with gender-affirming care, limiting the range of perspectives captured. Participants, predominantly Caucasian and from the USA and Canada, were from regions with relatively greater access to gender-affirming care. Experiences may differ in more diverse or less supportive settings. Another limitation is that this is a secondary analysis of preexisting data, and that the interviews were designed to explore gender-affirming care but not masculinizing chest surgery specifically. Future research could explore potential differences in chest surgery experiences and goals among binary transmen and non-binary-identifying individuals; and include longer term follow-up. Understanding how gender identity influences surgical expectations, satisfaction and well-being could provide further insight into the diverse needs of TGD adolescents seeking chest surgery.

Conclusions

This study highlights the important and multidimensional impact of masculinising chest surgery on TGD adolescents, documenting the impact of surgery on chest appearance satisfaction, HRQL and gender-related practices. By centring adolescents’ perspectives, these findings highlight the perceived benefits of access to gender-affirming care among participants and provides valuable insights for clinicians, policymakers and future research. Expanding an understanding of individual experiences will continue to inform best practices and ensure that gender-affirming care meets the diverse needs of TGD adolescents.
