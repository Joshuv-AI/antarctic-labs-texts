First molecular detection of Leishmania infantum in farm piglets
Authors: Ana Lesta, Lola Martínez-Sáez, Orla Ballesteros-García, Pablo Jesús Marín-García, Lola Llobat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Leishmaniasis is a zoonotic disease caused by Leishmania infantum and is endemic in different countries around the world, including Mediterranean basin. In the last years, the number of species that could act as reservoirs for the parasite has been increasing, so that the presence of L. infantum has been detected in both wild species and domestic, including livestock. The role of domestic pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) in the epidemiology of Leishmania spp. remains unexplored. This study aims to determine the prevalence of L. infantum in piglets from intensive farms in Eastern Spain (Mediterranean basin) and evaluate associated epidemiological factors. For this, 48 apparently healthy piglets from 13 commercial farms were sampled. Serum samples were tested for anti-Leishmania antibodies and molecular detection of L. infantum was performed by real-time PCR targeting kinetoplast DNA. Epidemiological data including sex, age, geographical location, and farm type were recovered and analysed. The results indicate that the prevalence of L. infantum was 22.92 % of piglets and no significant association was found between infection and epidemiological variables. This study reports, for the first time, the presence of L. infantum in farm piglets in an endemic region, which indicates that pigs could act as silent reservoir in endemic areas, emphasizing the need for broader epidemiological studies involving larger cohorts to clarify their role in disease transmission.

Introduction

Human leishmaniasis is a zoonotic disease endemic in more than 90 countries worldwide, including the Eastern Mediterranean region [12,21,22,32]. It is caused by the intracellular parasite Leishmania spp., which is transmitted by the bites of sandflies [24]. Although the dog has traditionally been considered the main reservoir of the L. infantum [11], the number of species confirmed as potential reservoirs today is more than dogs [17], including cats [2], horses [19], wild lagomorphs (Lepus europaeus) [8,29] and other wild species, such as European rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus), European wildcats (Felis silvestris silvestris), red fox (Vulpes vulpes) and pine marten (Martes martes) [1]. In the last years, detection of Leishmania spp. infection has been demonstrated in wild boars in different countries. In the New World, DNA of L. infantum has been found in the hearts of wild boards recovered from Venezuela, with a prevalence of 44 % [13], and Viettri et al. [30] indicated a prevalence of 12.9 % of Leishmania spp. in blood samples of the wild boards from Brazil. In Europe, only two studies in Italy demonstrate the presence of L. infantum DNA in wild boar, showing a prevalence ranged from 4.9 % to 42.9 % [7,28].

Related to livestock species, different studies warn of the possible role of these species in the dissemination of the parasite, so L. infantum infection has been observed in cattle [20], sheep [31], and goats [25]. However, the role of domestic pig (Sus scrofa domesticus) in the epidemiology of the L. infantum has not been studied yet. The number of studies reporting Leishmania infantum in Sus scrofa (domestic pig) is very limited [5], so it remains unclear whether the species acts as a reservoir or merely an accidental host. However, given the confirmed presence of the parasite in both wild boar and free-range pigs in the Mediterranean region—and considering the lack of vector control measures against sandflies in intensive pig farming—the potential role of farm pigs as reservoirs or incidental hosts of this zoonotic pathogen warrants further investigation.

The main objective of this study is, therefore, to analyse the prevalence of L. infantum in piglets from intensive farms located in the Mediterranean region and to study the possible epidemiological factors related to the infection.

Material and methods

Animals and sample collection

From September 2024 to April 2025, 48 farm piglets (Sus scrofa domesticus) from thirteen commercial farms operating under an intensive system in Valencia province (Eastern Spain, Mediterranean region) were included in this study. The farms were located indoors (not open-air) and protected by control measures against rodents and other pests, but not against sandflies. The presence of sandflies in this region has been reported previously [14,16]. None of the piglets exhibited clinical signs consistent with Leishmania infantum infection, and all appeared to be in good health.

For each piglet, epidemiological data were recorded, including sex (male or female), reproductive status in males (castrated or intact), age category (newborn piglets: 0–6 months; weaned piglets: 7–12 months), geographical location (six different regions, Fig. 1), and farm classification based on the production system (farrow-to-wean or finishing farms).Fig. 1Location of regions of the piglets' farms included in this study. Blood samples were collected aseptically from the ear vein by an experienced veterinarian using a 3 mL sterile syringe for routine health analysis and prior informed consent of the owner. The ethics committee determined that no specific ethical approval was necessary, since blood collection was not performed specifically for the study, but rather the collected blood samples were used for routine health analysis on the farm. The samples were then transferred into two vacutainer tubes, one with EDTA as an anticoagulant and another without an anticoagulant. The latter was left undisturbed until serum separation, after which the serum was collected in a sterile cryotube, labelled, and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

Leishmania spp. serological analysis

Serum samples were tested per duplicate for the presence of Leishmania spp. antibodies using the Leishmania Vet ELISA® test (Demeditec Diagnostic GmbH, Bonn, Germany), following the manufacturer's instructions. This assay detects anti-Leishmania-specific immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies. Animals were classified as seropositive if their ELISA titer exceeded the designated cut-off value. Each test included both positive and negative controls. As reported by the manufacturer, the kit has a sensitivity and specificity exceeding 98 %.

DNA extraction and L. infantum molecular detection

Total genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from 200 μL of whole blood collected in EDTA tubes using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA), following the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and quantity of the extracted DNA were assessed by measuring absorbance ratios using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The DNA extracted was kept at −80 °C until used.

The primers and probes for L. infantum DNA detection were selected from the conserved region of the kinetoplast DNA minicircle. Fluorogenic probes were synthesized with a FAM reporter molecule at the 5′ end, a TAMRA quencher at the 3′ end, and ROX as the internal passive reference dye. The sequences used were 5′-GGCGTTCTGCGAAAACCG-3′ for the forward primer, 5′-AAAATGGCATTTTCGGGCC-3′ for reverse primer, and 5′-AAAATGGCATTTTCGGGCC-3′ probe [9,18].

Real-time PCR was conducted in a 20 μL reaction volume, containing 10 μL of qLUMEN MasterMix (Gquence®, Labbox Labware, SL, Barcelona, Spain), 300 nM of each primer, 250 nM of the fluorogenic probe, and 50–100 ng of DNA. All reactions were performed in triplicate using the QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: an initial incubation at 50 °C for 2 min and denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Each plate included a negative control (nuclease-free water) and a positive control. The positive control consisted of a DNA sample extracted using the same protocol as the test samples, obtained from a dog confirmed Leishmania-positive by an external laboratory using the IFAT and PCR techniques.

Statistical analysis

Pearson's Chi-square test was carried out to analyse the prevalence of L. infantum infection and the epidemiological factors associated with the presence of the parasite. The confidence intervals for prevalences have been calculated by Clopper-Pearson test. he statistical program SAS (North Carolina State University, Cary, CA, USA) was used for the statistical analysis and differences were considered statistically significant at p-value <0.05.

Discussion

Our study evaluated for the first time the prevalence of L. infantum in piglets from intensive farms in the Eastern Spain region, where leishmaniosis is endemic. The prevalence found was 22.92 %. Although dogs have always been considered the main reservoir of the parasite [6], increasing studies corroborate the importance of other species, including cats, horses, wild and livestock in the dissemination of L. infantum in endemic regions [1,2,4,19,25]. The results obtained in this study support the importance of different species, including pigs, in the epidemiology of leishmaniasis, mainly in endemic regions.

The prevalence found differs significantly depending on the analysis method, this prevalence by PCR being 22.92 %, while the seroprevalence (detection of antibodies against Leishmania spp. by ELISA) was 2.08 %. Higher prevalence by PCR than serological methods has already been observed in other studies in dogs [23] and cats [3] in the Mediterranean region, indicating that PCR can detect latent or subclinical infection [26]. The results indicates that piglets may harbour the parasite without a present humoral response, in agree previous reports in other subclinical reservoirs where cellular immune response predominates over antibody-mediated immunity [3,10,15,19,27].

The farms included in the study were geographically close, with similar climatic conditions. The asymmetry in the study groups may represent a limitation and could explain to lack of significant differences between the groups.

The results found in this study emphasize the need to carry out future studies to analyse the role of livestock species such as pigs in the dynamics, including ability of transmission and contribution to the circulation of the parasite, as well as the epidemiology of L. infantum in endemic regions, in addition to studying factors such as age, sex or management conditions in the response to infection in larger cohorts of animals.

Conclusions

In conclusion, for the first time the prevalence of L. infantum in farm piglets in endemic region has been reported. These findings indicate that pigs might serve as a silent reservoir or as accidental host of parasite. The lack of association with epidemiological parameters evaluated suggests that other environmental or ecological factors may play a critical role in the transmission of L. infantum in pigs. Further studies and continued surveillance of Leishmania spp. prevalence are necessary to understand the epidemiology of the disease and the potential role of pigs and other livestock species in the spread of this parasite in endemic regions. The manuscript we present is a first approximation, as it is the first time that L. infantum has been detected in farmed white pigs using molecular methods. To determine whether piglets are competent reservoirs, further studies should be conducted, with larger sample sizes and analyzing the pig's immune response's ability to contain infection, among other reasons. Undoubtedly, and until more information is obtained in this regard, it would be interesting to include vector control measures on farms in light of the results obtained.
