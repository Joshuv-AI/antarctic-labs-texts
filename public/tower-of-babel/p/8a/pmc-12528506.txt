Evaluating the utility of growth differentiation factor 15 and fibroblast growth factor 21 as blood biomarkers for Rett syndrome
Authors: Ashley Hertzog, Adviye Ayper Tolun, Alexander D Wykes, David Brown, Samuel N Breit, Carolyn Ellaway, Gladys Ho, Wendy Gold
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRett syndrome (RTT, OMIM #312750) is a severe genetic, neurodevelopmental disorder, primarily affecting females, that occurs due to pathogenic variants in MECP2. Clinical features include loss of acquired developmental milestones, such as purposeful hand movements and communicative abilities and the onset of stereotypic hand movements. Mitochondrial dysfunction/impairment, and inflammation have been reported in individuals with RTT. Despite numerous clinical trials and medications thought to be disease-modifying, treatment often remains purely symptomatic. A significant impediment in determining treatment efficacy has been the lack of clinical biomarkers that correlate with disease state. Mitokines, such as fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), have been established as biomarkers of cellular stress and mitochondrial dysfunction that may be of clinical utility for patients with RTT. We aimed to determine the suitability of these mitokines as biomarkers for RTT where we analysed their expression levels in blood samples from individuals with RTT as well as fromthe Mecp2T158A mouse model . Our data showed higher FGF21 and GDF15 levels in female Mecp2-deficient mice compared to their wild type littermates. Median FGF21 and GDF15 levels also trended higher in the affected human cohort compared to controls; however, these elevations did not reach statistical significance and appear to be correlated with sodium valproate therapy.Keywords: Growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21), Rett syndrome, Mitokines, Blood-based biomarkersSubject terms: Genetics, Biomarkers, Diseases, Medical research

Introduction

Rett Syndrome (RTT, OMIM # 312750) is an X-linked neurodevelopmental disorder originally described by Andreas Rett in 19661. It is the second most common cause of severe intellectual disability in human females2 and occurs in around 1 in 10,000 live female births3. The majority of human males who are hemizygous for disease die in utero, although a small proportion survive to birth and develop an epileptic encephalopathy with early death. Over 95% of patients with features of classical RTT have pathogenic variants identified in the methyl-CpG-binding-protein-2 (MECP2) gene4. Despite more than 70 clinical trials to date, including ongoing gene therapy trials aiming to restore functional MECP2 expression, patient care typically remains symptomatic and supportive5. One of the main challenges in clinical trials is a lack of molecular, non-invasive biomarkers that predict disease severity and progression, and which readily translate into clinical practice6.

There is increasing literature describing how MECP2 loss-of-function leads to the clinical features of RTT. The MeCP2 protein is a transcriptional repressor, that also plays a crucial role in neurogenesis and neuronal maturation7. Animal models demonstrate that MECP2 expression is highest in the brain, predominantly in the hippocampus, cortex and cerebellum8 where MeCP2 helps to establish chromatin architecture and regulate global gene expression. Mutations in MECP2 result in dysregulated gene expression9 which is thought to underpin the clinical manifestations including altered neurodevelopment. Whilst some of the clinical features of RTT overlap with the common ‘mitochondrial disease phenotype’ of predominantly neurological/neuromuscular involvement, there are other clinical manifestations (endocrine, cardiac, microvascular)10–12 that suggests the pathophysiology is broader. Two potential explanations for the multi-systemic manifestations are mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory/immune changes.

There is evidence implicating mitochondrial dysfunction in the pathogenesis of RTT. At the histological level, individuals with RTT have skeletal muscle cell abnormalities observed by electron microscopy including swollen, dumbbell-shaped mitochondria with vacuolization, with fewer and shorter cristae13,14. These mitochondrial changes were also noted in sural nerve biopsies and in neurons where post-mortem samples were taken from the frontal cortex, cerebellar folia, and substantia nigra of individuals with RTT15,16. Additionally, a redox imbalance and morphological changes have also been reported in skin fibroblasts of patients with RTT17. At the molecular level, there is also evidence indicating changes in expression of genes associated with mitochondrial function and oxidative stress in RTT patients and animal models. These include a reduction in cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (MT-CO1) mRNA in human post-mortem brain tissue18 and decreased CO1 at both the transcript and protein level in skeletal muscle of a RTT mouse model19. The mechanism by which loss of MeCP2 function results in increased oxidative stress remains unclear but may relate to altered regulation of genes that maintain the mitochondrial membrane potential20. Other studies report on the role of immune dysfunction21 and suggest that the increased inflammation caused by MeCP2 loss-of-function leads to secondary oxidative stress22–24. Although inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction have been reported in RTT, it remains uncertain how the various changes in these pathways correlate with disease onset, progression, and severity. In the literature, there are also some studies that report on mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress observed in both blood and tissue from patients with RTT25,26. However, a larger proportion of these studies utilize samples collected by invasive procedures, limiting their applications in a clinical setting.

Mitokines, such as fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) and growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) are established blood biomarkers of cellular stress and mitochondrial disorders27,28. They are elevated in serum samples of patients with suspected mitochondrial disorders29–31 and correlate with severity of disease31,32. There is also evidence of elevation in these biomarkers in other disorders associated with secondary mitochondrial dysfunction32–34. There are several explanations for why these biomarkers are elevated in mitochondrial disease. FGF21 activates the adenosine monophosphate activated protein kinase α (AMPKα) pathway, which is crucial in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis35. The AMPKα pathway also supports release of anti-inflammatory cytokines (such as BDNF) and inhibits pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as NFκB)36. Although there is limited information on the function of GDF1534,37, some studies suggest that it is released in an effort to counteract both inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction38,39. In this context, and given the link between mitochondrial dysfunction and RTT, we aimed to evaluate FGF21 and GDF15 as potential minimally-invasive biomarkers for RTT in both a preclinical mouse model and female humans with RTT for possible future translation to a clinical trial setting.

Methods

Mice and ethical statement

These studies utilized the Mecp2T158A knock-in mouse model40 (obtained from a breeding colony at the Kids Research Transgenic Facility (The Children’s Hospital at Westmead, NSW, Australia) and originally gifted by Zhaolan Joe Zhou), which carries a missense substitution (p.Thr158Ala) in the MeCP2 methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD). Threonine 158 missense substitutions are among the most commonly reported MECP2 mutations associated with RTT4. Whilst the p.Thr158Met change is more common in humans, RTT individuals with p.Thr158Ala also present with classical RTT: therefore, the impact on deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) binding and pathogenesis underlying both genetic changes are thought to be similar41,42.

The phenotype of the Mecp2T158A mouse model has been well-established40,43. Male hemizygous and female heterozygous mice develop Rett-like symptoms at 4–6 weeks or after 17 weeks of age, respectively, including hindlimb clasping, weight dysregulation, anxiety, learning deficits, decreased motor coordination and activity levels, and seizures. Wild-type (WT) C57BL/6 littermates were also obtained from the same Mecp2T158A colony. All mice were housed and maintained in accordance with the ethical guidelines within the Kids Research Transgenic Facility (The Children’s Hospital at Westmead, NSW, Australia) at a temperature of approximately 24°C under a light-controlled schedule (12-h light/dark cycle). All mice had ad libitum access to a commercial diet (VS Irradiated Rat and Mouse; Specialty Feeds, WA, Australia) and water. All experiments were performed in strict accordance with the Australian Code of Practice for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes (National Health and Medical Research Council), were approved by the Children’s Medical Research Institute and the Children’s Hospital at Westmead Joint Animal Ethics Committee (Approval Number: 5174.02.21), and performed in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Measurement of FGF21 and GDF15 in mice

To capture potential changes in FGF21 and GDF15 serum levels associated with disease progression in the Mecp2T158A model, cohorts of male and female mice (Table 1) were analyzed at pre- and post-symptomatic ages. Male hemizygous mice develop Rett-like symptoms at 4–6 weeks and female heterozygous mice develop Rett-like symptoms after 17 weeks of age. Blood was collected from mice via terminal cardiac puncture under isoflurane anesthesia (2–3% (v/v) in oxygen). Whole blood was allowed to coagulate at room temperature for at least 30 min, then centrifuged at 3000 g, 4°C for 10 min, prior to collection of the serum supernatant. Serum samples were stored at –80°C until required and thawed once, prior to being assayed for the first time. Samples were refrozen at –80°C and thawed again if further analysis was required (e.g., dilutions or high variance between replicates).

FGF21 [Abcam (UK) (ab212160)] and GDF15 [R&D Systems (MN, USA) (MGD150)] serum biomarkers were measured by quantitative sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) analysis. Recruitment of human patients Rett syndrome

Female RTT patients in New South Wales, Australia with a confirmed pathogenic variant in MECP2 were eligible for involvement in this study and were recruited through the Multidisciplinary Rett Syndrome Clinic at the Children’s Hospital at Westmead (NSW, Australia) between October 2020 and May 2021. Informed consent was given by families, and ethics approval was provided by the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (2020/ETH02699). Approximately 1.5 mL of blood was collected into a lithium heparin tube, mixed, and then centrifuged at 3000 g, 4°C for 10 min prior to collection of the lithium heparin plasma supernatant. At the time of blood collection, information about the Rett syndrome-specific clinical severity score was not available. Information on current medication use, seizure frequency, body mass index on both the Rett syndrome and Centers for Disease Control scales, and genotype were obtained through the hospital electronic medical record (Table 2).

Recruitment of human controls

Potential control patients were identified as those with existing lithium heparin plasma samples available through the New South Wales Biochemical Genetics Service between August and December 2021. A waiver of consent was obtained by the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (2020/ETH02699) for the use of spare plasma samples referred for diagnostic testing in the New South Wales Biochemical Genetics Service as controls. Individuals referred with a suspicion of an inherited metabolic condition with abnormal plasma acylcarnitine profiles (potentially indicating a fatty acid oxidation disorder or an organic acidemia) were excluded. Clinical data from the referral was used to exclude patients with indicators of Rett Syndrome (such as microcephaly or neuroregression), as well as disease states with known associated elevations in FGF21 and GDF15 biomarkers (diabetes). Growth parameter data and medication history were unavailable for the control patients. For the purpose of this study, these control patients were classified as individuals unaffected with RTT. They were also sex- and approximately age-matched to the RTT cohort.

Measurement of GDF15 and FGF21 in humans

Lithium heparin plasma samples were collected from human females with RTT (n = 13; mean age 10 years) and age/gender matched controls (n = 12; mean age 9.9 years).

GDF15 concentrations were analyzed by quantitative sandwich ELISA as previously described44,45 and takes into account a common polymorphism46. Optical densities were measured in duplicate at 450 nm (with reference wavelength of 570 nm) on Thermo Labsystems Plate Reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a blank reduction was performed. Samples were frozen and stored at -80˚C until analysis. Samples underwent two freeze-thaw cycles prior to analysis. Concentrations were plotted against the blank-reduced optical densities for GDF15 and fitted to a logarithmic curve (r2 = 0.9976).

FGF21 concentrations were analyzed by quantitative sandwich ELISA (Biovendor, Czech Republic (RD191108200R)). Optical densities were measured in duplicate wells at 450 nm (with reference wavelength of 540 nm) using an Infinite M Nano + microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) and a blank reduction was performed. Samples were frozen and stored at –80°C until analysis. Samples underwent two freeze-thaw cycles prior to analysis. Concentrations were plotted against the blank-reduced optical densities for FGF21 and fitted to a four parameters logistics curve (r2 = 0.985). Concentrations were measured in duplicate wells with variances less than 30%.

Statistical analyses

GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA) was utilized to perform all statistical analyses, with the threshold for statistical significance set at a p value of < 0.05. Results are expressed as medians and are displayed with graphs depicting the interquartile ranges. The Mann–Whitney U Test was used to compare independent continuous variables. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used when more than two groups were being compared, with individual sub-comparisons performed by the Dunn’s Multiple Comparison test. Outliers identified using the ROUT method (false discovery rate, Q = 1%) were removed from the analysis of the murine data (FGF21: n = 10 and GDF15: n = 1) and the human data (n = 1). To investigate potential correlations between biochemical markers (FGF21 and GDF15) and continuous clinical parameters (Rett syndrome growth chart z-scores and CDC BMI z-scores), the Spearman’s rank coefficient was utilized. To investigate potential correlations between biochemical markers (FGF21 and GDF15) and dichotomous clinical parameters (seizure frequency and anti-epileptic type), the point-biserial correlation coefficient was utilized.

Results

GDF15 concentrations are increased in female Mecp2T158A mice compared to wild-type controls

Potential differences in serum GDF15 concentrations (pg/mL) between wild-type and Mecp2T158A mice presented as aggregated data, as measured by ELISA, stratified by sex (Fig. 1). In females, the median GDF15 levels in Mecp2T158A mice was 55.57 pg/mL compared to 51.81 pg/mL in the wild-type mice: this difference was statistically significant (Mann Whitney U Test: p = 0.0325). In males, there was also a significant difference (p = 0.01) in median GDF15 levels in Mecp2T158A mice (58.20 pg/mL) compared to wild-type controls (67.83 pg/mL); however, the concentrations in the wild-type mice were higher than the Mecp2T158A mice.

FGF21 outperforms GDF15 in discriminating wild-type female mice from Mecp2T158A female mice

To further assess the performance (specificity at given sensitivity thresholds) of serum FGF21 and GDF15 as potential biomarkers in the Mecp2T158A mouse model, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated (Fig. 5). Although FGF21 consistently performed better than chance (see red dashed line-of-no-discrimination) in differentiating female Mecp2T158A mice from WT females (95% confidence interval (CI) 0.61–0.8), this was not the case for FGF21 in male mice (95% CI 0.40–0.62), nor for either sex based on serum GDF15 [GDF15 female 95% CI 0.52–0.75 and GDF15 male 95% CI 0.54–0.7). The ROC curves of both FGF21 and GDF15 demonstrate poor sensitivity and specificity for distinguishing between Mecp2T158A and WT mice.

GDF15 and FGF21 concentrations are not elevated in patients with Rett syndrome

In addition to the Mecp2T158A preclinical mouse model, GDF15 and FGF21 concentrations (pg/mL) were quantified by ELISA in lithium heparin plasma from female humans with RTT (n = 13) and compared to unaffected individuals (n = 11). The median GDF15 level (Fig. 6A) in unaffected individuals was 415.5 pg/mL, compared to 509 pg/mL in the individuals with RTT: this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.13). Likewise, the median lithium heparin FGF21 concentration was also lower in unaffected individuals (60.76 pg/mL) compared to those with RTT (183.1 pg/mL), but this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.25) (Fig. 6B). Additionally, potential correlations between mitokines (GDF15 and FGF21) and clinical parameters (Rett syndrome growth chart z-scores, CDC BMI z-scores, seizure frequency, and anti-epileptic type) were investigated utilizing Spearman’s rank coefficient and the point-biserial correlation coefficient. Across the comparisons, some weak correlations were observed between GDF15 or FGF21 concentrations and the Rett syndrome growth chart z-scores (GDF15: r = −0.15, p = 0.63 and FGF21: r = −0.12, p = 0.7), the CDC BMI z-scores (GDF15: r = −0.15, p = 0.63 and FGF21: r = −0.12, p = 0.7), and seizure frequency (GDF15: r = 0.05, p = 0.87 and FGF21: r = 0.47, p = 0.13). However, strong positive correlations were observed with patients on sodium valproate and GDF15 or FGF21 concentrations (GDF15: r = 0.76, p = 0.004 and FGF21: r = 0.65, p = 0.02) when compared to the affected patients not on sodium valproate.

Discussion

In the clinical realm, there are various types of biomarkers (diagnostic, prognostic, predictive, and monitoring) that assist clinicians, scientists, and researchers in expanding knowledge of a particular disease or disorder47,48. Given the link between mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation in RTT (and the lack of a current clinical biomarker), we hypothesized that previously established biomarkers of mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammation (GDF15 and FGF21) may provide novel diagnostic and/or prognostic biomarkers for RTT.

Our study provides evidence that GDF15 and FGF21 concentrations are elevated in the serum of female Mecp2T158A mice compared to sex-matched wild-type controls. Whilst these differences in the mitokine concentrations between the Mecp2T158A and wild-type populations were statistically significant, there was substantial overlap between these cohorts. As a result, the aggregate performance of these mitokines as biomarkers in female mice was limited, despite there being statistically significant differences in the median concentrations of GDF15 and FGF21 between both wild-type and Mecp2T158A female mice. Additionally, the same mitokines were not elevated in male Mecp2T158A mice, when compared to their male wild-type counterparts, despite their hemizygous state predicting a more severe phenotype49.

While the disparity between FGF21 and GDF15 concentrations between male and female mice requires further investigation, it may be explained by hormonal differences between the sexes. Indeed, sex-dependent disparities in the expression and biology of these mitokines are well-documented50–52 and each has shown differential blood elevation between males and females in association with certain stressors or disease states53–56. Given male mouse models of RTT are often favoured over female mice in preclinical research (due to their more severe and earlier onset phenotype) this is a particularly important consideration in the context of translational biomarker discovery.

We did not observe any age-related differences in mitokine expression in both male and female mice except in the pre-symptomatic male mice (4 weeks of age) where GDF15 was significantly reduced in the Mecp2T158A. Interestingly, we observed 4-week old male and female Mecp2T158A mice to both have equivocal levels (~ 50 pg/mL) while the male counterparts had high levels (~ 70 pg/mL) and the females low levels (~ 40 pg/mL).

In our limited cohort of female patients with RTT, we observed increased GDF15 and FGF21 concentrations when compared to unaffected controls: however, this was not statistically significant. Interestingly, the majority of patients with RTT who had elevations above the medians in these biomarkers were taking sodium valproate for management of seizures (n = 5/13 of the total affected cohort and n = 5/6 for patients on valproate with GDF15 of FGF21 elevations above the medians). There is increasing evidence that sodium valproate treatment upregulates FGF21 gene expression57–59. Therefore, sodium valproate treatment is likely to explain the trend towards increased FGF21 concentrations in the patients with RTT. To our knowledge, the same confounding effect of sodium valproate has not been previously reported for GDF15: however, given our data and the impact of this drug on mitochondrial function, it could be postulated. Further studies investigating both GDF15 and FGF21 concentrations in cohorts of patients undergoing sodium therapy compared to other seizure management strategies would help to further elucidate this potential confounding effect.

Based on the results of this study there are a few possibilities to consider. Firstly, it is possible that the mitochondrial dysfunction in RTT is time-limited: if samples were not collected at this specific timepoint (for example, it is possible that samples were not taken during the period of time in which peak mitochondrial dysfunction was occurring), then their elevation may be missed. Given the various stages of disease reported in RTT7 future studies may wish to consider sample collection in the early stages of disease in humans and correlate biomarker levels to levels measured during disease plateau. or correlation with lactate measurements.

Secondly, MECP2 expression is highest in the brain (particularly mature neurons)8. It is possible that the effects of the mitochondrial dysfunction are more localized and not easily detectable in blood. Additionally, it could be postulated that the level of mitochondrial dysfunction observed may be dependent on the genetic variant (some genetic variants may cause more severe mitochondrial dysfunction). However, to our knowledge, studies of this nature have not been performed.

Overall, despite the elevated levels in our female mouse model and in the human samples, it is not conclusive if our data supports GDF15 and FGF21 to be reliable diagnostic, prognostic, or treatment-responsive biomarkers in RTT. To clearly distinguish between RTT and unaffected individuals, a reliable biomarker must be able to determine disease state and progression47,48; however, no significant differences in GDF15 or FGF21 serum concentrations were observed between male or female Mecp2T158A and wild-type mice over time.

As with most studies involving rare disorders, a limitation of our study is the small sample size of the human cohort. While we identified which individuals were on sodium valproate use, the cohort size was too small to meaningfully stratify according to their medications. In addition to larger cohorts, longitudinal studies in newly diagnosed patients may provide deeper insights. As evidenced by other studies involving neurodevelopmental disorders60–62 employing an untargeted approach (using a combination of multi-omics investigations) may prove to be fruitful in identifying a biomarker or biosignature for RTT and assisting in elucidating other mechanisms of disease.
