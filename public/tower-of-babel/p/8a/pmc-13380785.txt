ARHGAP12 loss promotes tumor budding and lymphatic metastasis in gastric cancer
Authors: Yong Ji, Xiaofeng Xu, Shihan Chen, Liqi Luo, Jian Lyu, Cong Long, Shengqiang Cai, Chuanqi Xu, Li Ding, Aihua Gao, Jing Chen, Wei Ji, Chao Huang, Wei Zhu, Feng Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIdentifying the molecular drivers of metastasis and tumor budding (Tb) is essential for improving patient outcomes. This study aimed to characterize the expression pattern and functional contribution of ARHGAP12, a Rho GTPase-activating protein, in the metastatic evolution of GC.MethodsARHGAP12 expression profiles were analyzed using TCGA_STAD and scRNA-seq (GSE163558) datasets. The spatial distribution of ARHGAP12 in Tb regions and paired lymph node metastases was validated via mIHC in a cohort of 27 GC patients. A highly metastatic cell subline (HGC-27-L) was established through in vivo selection. Functional impacts of ARHGAP12 were evaluated using siRNA knockdown, lentiviral overexpression, wound healing, Transwell migration, and transendothelial migration assays. The prognostic value of ARHGAP12 was assessed using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis.ResultsAnalysis of scRNA-seq and mIHC data revealed that ARHGAP12 expression was significantly diminished in metastatic foci and Tb cells compared to primary tumor cells (P < 0.01). The HGC-27-L subline exhibited lower ARHGAP12 levels than its parental line. Functional assays demonstrated that ARHGAP12 knockdown markedly promoted the migration and invasion of AGS cells (P < 0.01). Conversely, overexpressing ARHGAP12 in HGC-27-L and MKN-45 cells inhibited motility and transendothelial migration (P < 0.01) by stabilizing cell-cell junctions and upregulating E-cadherin. Clinically, low ARHGAP12 expression was positively correlated with higher rates of lymph node metastasis and significantly shorter OS and RFS in GC patients.ConclusionARHGAP12 serves as a pivotal suppressor of GC metastasis. Its downregulation facilitates the epithelial-mesenchymal transition and enhances the invasive capacity of tumor cells, particularly within the Tb microenvironment.

Introduction

Owing to its insidious onset and the lack of widespread early screening for GC, patients are frequently diagnosed at an advanced stage. Metastasis to multiple organs is a leading cause of high mortality in advanced gastric cancer [[1], [2], [3]]. However, there remains a paucity of effective interventions for metastatic GC. Therefore, it is critical to elucidate the molecular and phenotypic differences between metastatic lesions and primary tumors in GC, as well as to identify potential therapeutic targets—both of which are essential for improving patient survival outcomes.

RhoA GTPase activating protein (ARHGAPs) as members of the Rho GAP protein family and contains of five functional domains: SH3, WW, WW, PH, and Rho GAP. These domains collectively enhance the GTPase activity of Rho family proteins, thereby facilitating the timely inactivation of their activated forms [4]. ARHGAP plays a critical role in regulating cell signaling pathways and has been implicated in modulating cell migration and cytoskeletal dynamics [5]. Among these proteins, ARHGAP12 has emerged as a candidate regulator of gastric cancer progression. The inhibition of ARHGAP12 may promote oncogenic processes mediated by Rho GTPases, such as cell migration and invasion [4]. Recent studies have demonstrated that ARHGAP12 plays a significant role in GC. Wang et al. identified CLDN18-ARHGAP12 as a potential target for immunotherapy in GC, highlighting the immunogenicity of neoantigens derived from this fusion gene and confirming the anti-tumor activity of neoantigen-reactive T cells [6]. Another study revealed that ARHGAP-RhoA signaling exerts a suppressive effect on tumor growth by inducing homotypic adhesion in GC [7]. However, the regulatory mechanisms and expression patterns of ARHGAP12 in primary and metastatic lesions remain poorly understood. Although extensive research has focused on ARHGAP-related fusion genes in GC, the functional role of ARHGAP12 in tumor progression, particularly in relation to the immune microenvironment and metastatic behavior, has not been fully elucidated.

Tumor budding (Tb) serves as an independent prognostic factor in various types of cancer and may represent an early stage of the metastatic process [8,9]. While predominantly observed at the invasive tumor front, Tb can also occur intratumorally [10]. Despite its clinical relevance, the underlying mechanisms of tumor budding remain incompletely understood. In this study, we investigated ARHGAP12 expression in GC using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) combined with bulk RNA-seq cohort analysis. GO analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed to assess the activation or suppression of signaling pathways associated with ARHGAP12 expression. mIHC experiments revealed dynamic changes in ARHGAP12 expression in GC cells within tumor budding regions and metastatic lymph nodes. Furthermore, functional studies involving ARHGAP12 overexpression in GC cell lines were conducted to elucidate its role in tumor metastasis and patient prognosis. Finally, ARHGAP12 protein levels were evaluated using IHC scoring across clinical GC samples to assess their correlation with lymph node metastasis potential.

Materials and methods

Patient samples

All participants signed broad informed consent forms permitting the use of their samples for medical research. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health for the care and use of human subjects. Research involving human participants adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Transcriptome profiling was obtained from the TCGA database and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project. Visualization and data analysis were performed using the Sangerbox platform (http://vip.sangerbox.com/home.html). For scRNA-seq analysis, data were obtained from GSE163558 [11]. We employed the Seurat [12] R package (version 4.3.0.1) to preprocess and filter the gene expression data. To mitigate the influence of batch effects, we leveraged the Harmony [13] R package (version 1.0.3). Subsequently, dimensionality reduction was achieved through the application of a Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP). To discern gene expression differences among various subgroups, we applied the FindMarkers function in the Seurat package. Following the malignant epithelial cell clustering strategy used in the original study, CopyKAT was used to delineate clonal substructure based on the genomic copy number profiles.

Cell culture and lentiviral transfection

Human GC cell lines HGC-27 and MKN-45 were obtained from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Beijing, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

To achieve ARHGAP12 overexpression, the ARHGAP12 lentiviral vector was obtained from Vigen Biotechnology (Zhenjiang) Co., Ltd. and used in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, HEK-293T cells were co-transfected with a recombinant plasmid (Lenti-GFP-Puro-ARHGAP12) or a negative control plasmid (Lenti-GFP-Puro), along with the packaging plasmids PSPAX2 and PMD2G. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium. The virus-containing supernatant was collected, concentrated, and added to HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells at an appropriate titer for overnight infection. Transduced cells were selected with puromycin for 48 h before being used in subsequent assays. For lentivirus-mediated gene expression, HGC-27 cells were infected with the recombinant lentivirus (pLV-ARHGAP12-puro-GFP) and selected with puromycin (2 μg/ml) for one week. The transfection procedure was carried out according to the manufacturer's protocol (Vigen Technologies, China).

Transwell assay

The migration and invasion of GC cells were detected using a Transwell assay as previously described [14]. For the migration assay, HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells (1 × 105) suspended in serum-free media were added to the upper chamber and incubated for 20 or 36 h for migration assay. The number of cells in the lower chamber was counted after crystal violet staining. For invasion assay, Matrigel (BD Bioscience, San Jose, CA, USA) was diluted 4-fold, pre-plated in the upper chamber, and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells (1 × 105) were placed in the upper chamber and incubated for appropriate hours. Cells in the lower chamber were counted in at least six random fields after extensive washing, fixation, and crystal staining. For transendothelial cell migration, HUVECs were seeded in the upper chamber of 24-well migration chambers for 24 h in advance. Once HUVECs confluence reached 100%, the labeled HGC-27, HGC-27-L, and MKN-45 cells (1 × 105) were resuspended in serum-free 1640 and added to the upper chamber. The number of cells that migrated to the lower chamber was determined. Transwell and transendothelial migration experiments were performed in at least three independent biological replicates, each with technical triplicates.

Animal-related procedures and establishment of HGC-27-L cells

The high lymph node metastatic capacity of HGC-27(HGC-27-L) were kindly provided by Mei Wang's laboratory, which has been described above [15]. Briefly, 1 × 106 HGC-27 cells were injected into the footpads of BALB/c nude mice. The drained popliteal lymph nodes were collected, cut into pieces, and digested using trypsin. All single cells were harvested, suspended in RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, and plated into dishes. Animal-related procedures used to establish the HGC-27-L cell subline were conducted in compliance with institutional animal care and use requirements. No additional animal experiments were conducted in the present study. The reporting of animal-related information in this manuscript follows the applicable ARRIVE guidelines.

Western blotting analysis

Cell lysates were prepared using RIPA lysis buffer (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Protein samples were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, transferred onto PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ARHGAP12 (1:1000; pAb #PA5-51384; Thermo Fisher, USA) and GAPDH (1:4000; mAb #5174; CST, USA). Following PBST washing, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG or goat anti-rabbit secondary antibodies to detect the bound primary antibodies.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemistry was performed using an IHC kit (Boster Biological Technology, Wuhan, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. In brief, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized, incubated with 3% H2O2 at room temperature to block endogenous peroxidase activity, and subsequently blocked with 5% BSA. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody against ARHGAP12 (1:100; pAb #PA5-51384; ThermoFisher, USA). Following this, the sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature. Immunostaining was visualized using DAB chromogen, and counterstaining was performed with hematoxylin. Tissue sections were examined under a light microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Multiplex immunofluorescence

mIHC was performed using a TSA kit (Aifang Biological Technology, Hunan, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The experimental procedures prior to DAB staining were the same as those used in the IHC experiment. During the fluorescence staining phase, a tyramide signal amplification system was employed to covalently attach stable tyramide fluorescein to the tissue sample. Following this, the non-covalently bound primary-secondary-HRP complex was removed through heat-induced epitope retrieval. A second round of incubation was then carried out using the cytokeratin 19 (CK19) primary antibody (1:300; ab52625, Abcam) and an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody, followed by application of a distinct tyramide fluorescein substrate.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. The qPCR cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was performed at the end of amplification to verify the specificity of the PCR products. GAPDH was used as the internal control, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences: 5′-AAACTCCAAAGCGTTTTATGTGC-3′ (ARHGAP12 forward), 5′-AGACCAGCTACCTGCTTAACA-3′ (ARHGAP12 reverse); 5′- AGCCACATCGCTCAGACAC-3′ (GAPDH forward), 5′- GCCCAATACGACCAAATCC-3′ (GAPDH reverse). All qPCR experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates, each with technical triplicates.

Active Rho Pull-down and detection

Active Rho (RhoA/B/C) was pulled down using the Active Rho Pull-down and Detection Kit (Beyotime, P2065S). In brief, cells were lysed in Cell Lysis Buffer containing 5 mM MgCl2 and protease inhibitors. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (14,000×g, 5 min, 4 °C). For positive and negative controls, lysates were treated with 1 mM GppNHp or 1 mM GDP, respectively, in the presence of 10 mM EDTA for 15 min at room temperature, followed by addition of 5 mM MgCl2. Pre-washed Rhotekin-RBD Agarose (20 μl) was added to 200 μl lysate and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with rotation. Beads were pelleted, washed three times, and bound proteins eluted in SDS-loading buffer (95 °C, 5 min). Active Rho and total RhoA were detected by western blotting analysis.

Statistical analyses

Unpaired Wilcoxon Rank Sum and Signed Rank tests were used for ARHGAP12 expression in pan-cancer. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 3.6.2. P < 0.05 (two-tailed) was considered statistically significant. IHC staining intensity was scored as 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (medium), or 3 (strong), while the staining extent was scored as 0 (0% of the staining area), 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The IHC score was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity with the extent scores. Western blot analyses and fluorescence intensity were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software. Representative blots are shown, the mean fluorescence intensity was measured in regions of interest (ROIs) drawn around individual CK19-positive cells in the Tc and Tb regions. Quantified data, where appropriate, are presented as mean ± s.e.m. from three independent experiments.

Results

ARHGAP12 was poorly expressed in metastatic lesions in GC according to scRNA-seq dataset

We investigated the differences in ARHGAP12 expression between primary and metastatic lesions. We obtained scRNA-seq data from GSE163558 (source data included 10 samples, and one adjacent nontumoral sample was excluded). Nine human tissue samples originating from three primary GC samples and six metastatic samples were divided into two groups (Fig. 1A). We identified T (CD3D, CD3E, CD2), natural killer (KLRD1, GNLY, KLRF1), tumor cells (EPCAM, KRT18, KRT19, CLDN4), stromal (PECAM1, VWF), myeloid (CD14, CD68, LZY), B (CD79A, CD19, MS4A1), fibroblast (DCN, COL1A1), plasma (IGHG1), proliferative (MKI67, STMN1, PCNA) cells as 9 distinct lineages based on marker gene expression after dimensionality reduction and unsupervised cell clustering (Fig. 1B). We found that ARHGAP12 was mainly expressed in tumor and T cells in metastatic lesions, whereas myeloid, T and NK cells in the primary lesions. We focused on the significantly reduced tumor-cell population during metastasis. In contrast, the expression of ARHGAP12 in myeloid cell subsets was significantly upregulated compared to that in primary lesions (Fig. 1C). Molecular characteristics between the ARHGAP12 high- and low-expression groups

To validate the molecular characteristics of the ARHGAP12 low-expression group, we compared differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the TCGA_STAD cohort (N = 375). The scRNA-seq data were not used due to an insufficient number of primary samples in GSE163558, which could introduce bias caused by inter-patient variability. GC patients were stratified into ARHGAP12 high- and low-expression groups based on the median mRNA expression level of ARHGAP12 (Fig. 1D). GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were conducted to identify potential functional differences. The ARHGAP12 high-expression group showed significant enrichment in pathways related to cell-cell junctions, focal adhesions, and tight junctions (Fig. 1E and F). Given that these pathways are essential for maintaining structural integrity and inhibiting epithelial cell motility, their association with high ARHGAP12 expression supports the hypothesis that the loss of ARHGAP12 disrupts cellular adhesion and promotes gastric cancer metastasis.

Tumor budding and metastatic GC cells were correlated with lower ARHGAP12 expression

To further investigate the dynamic variations in ARHGAP12 in GC cells between primary lesions and tumor budding areas, mIHC experiments were performed. CK19 staining was used to differentiate the budding foci. Tumor cells within the central area exhibited intercellular adhesion and homogeneous morphology. In contrast, cells in the Tb regions displayed morphological heterogeneity, disrupted cell connections, and a round or spindle-shaped appearance. Notably, the fluorescence intensity of ARHGAP12 in the Tb region (indicated by yellow arrows) was notably weaker than that in the Tc region (Fig. 2A). Additionally, we assessed the expression of ARHGAP12 in primary lesions and paired metastatic lymph nodes of patients with GC. Our results revealed that within positive lymph nodes, the expression of ARHGAP12 was significantly diminished in individual tumor cells (indicated by red arrows). Conversely, tumor cells demonstrating close connectivity and homogeneity exhibited heightened levels of ARHGAP12 compared with solitary tumor cells (Fig. 2B). We quantified and conducted statistical analyses of the fluorescence intensity of ARHGAP12 to provide a thorough assessment of its mean fluorescence intensity (Fig. 2C and D). Downregulation of ARHGAP12 promoted migration and invasion in GC cells

Furthermore, we compared the expression levels of ARHGAP12 in various human gastric cancer cell lines with those in normal gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1), which served as the control. The results showed that AGS cells exhibited the highest expression level, followed by MGC-803, MKN-45, and SGC7901 cells. HGC-27 cells displayed the lowest relative expression (Fig. 3A). ARHGAP12 was subsequently knocked down in AGS cells using two specific siRNAs (#485 and #2516), and the knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blotting (Fig. 3B, Fig. S1A). RhoA pull-down assays demonstrated that knockdown of ARHGAP12 led to a marked increase in the levels of activated RhoA, while total RhoA levels remained unchanged (Fig. S1C). Notably, AGS cells with reduced ARHGAP12 expression demonstrated significantly enhanced migratory and invasive capabilities (Fig. 3C–G). Conversely, overexpression of ARHGAP12 in AGS cells resulted in a decrease in activated RhoA (Fig. S1B and S1D). The HGC-27 cell line with high metastatic capacity exhibited lower expression of ARHGAP12

We further established a highly lymphatic metastatic gastric cancer (GC) cell line (HGC-27-L) through serial in vivo passaging of HGC-27 cells (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, reduced ARHGAP12 expression was observed in HGC-27-L cells (passage 3), as determined by immunoblotting (Fig. 4B). The migratory and invasive capacities of HGC-27-L cells were evaluated and compared to those of the parental HGC-27 cell line in vitro (Fig. 4C and D). Additionally, a transendothelial metastasis model [15] was employed to assess the metastatic potential of HGC-27-L cells (Fig. 4E). Our results demonstrated that a greater number of HGC-27-L cells migrated across the endothelial monolayer and adhered to the substratum (Fig. 4F and G). Upregulation of ARHGAP12 in GC cells inhibited tumor migration

To further determine the role of ARHGAP12 in tumor metastasis, we overexpressed ARHGAP12 in HGC-27 and MKN-45 cells using lentiviral vectors and verified its expression by immunoblotting (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, as part of the EMT process, the levels of E-cadherin increased, but not N-cadherin, in HGC-27 Lv−ARHGAP12 or MKN-45 Lv-ARHGAP12 cells (Fig. 5A). The wound-healing experiment showed that HGC-27 Lv-ARHGAP12 cells had reduced migratory capacity (Fig. 5B). In vitro, the number of migrated HGC-27 Lv−ARHGAP12or MKN-45 Lv-ARHGAP12 cells was significantly decreased compared to HGC-27 Lv-vector or MKN-45 Lv-vector cells (Fig. 5C and D). Furthermore, the migration capacity of HGC-27 Lv-ARHGAP12 or MKN-45 Lv-ARHGAP12 cells through monolayer endothelial cells to the substratum was significantly reduced compared to that of the control group (Fig. 5E and F). Low ARHGAP12 expression was associated with poor prognosis in clinical GC patients

We then examined the expression of ARHGAP12 in 27 gastric cancer (GC) tissue samples using IHC (Fig. 6A). The IHC score was calculated by multiplying the staining intensity score with the extent score, which was used to quantitatively assess the expression level of ARHGAP12. Clinical characteristics of the patients, including sex, histological type, pathological stage, and presence of lymph node metastasis, were summarized and categorized (Table 1). Notably, a significantly higher proportion of patients with low ARHGAP12 expression exhibited lymph node metastasis compared to those with high-expression (Fig. 6B). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses demonstrated that low ARHGAP12 expression was associated with poorer overall survival and reduced relapse-free survival (Fig. 6C). Discussion

In recent years, great progress has been made in the treatment of GC, including surgical treatment, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy [16]. Using a gastric cancer single-cell database, we observed that ARHGAP12 expression was significantly downregulated in metastatic lesions. mIHC analysis further confirmed that GC cells located in Tb regions and lymph node metastases exhibited reduced levels of ARHGAP12. Cell proliferation assays revealed that overexpression of ARHGAP12 significantly inhibited the growth rate of HGC-27 cells (Fig. S2A and S2C). In contrast, silencing ARHGAP12 in AGS cells significantly promoted cell proliferation (Fig. S2B and S2D). In vivo and in vitro metastasis model experiments indicated that low ARHGAP12 expression was associated with a highly metastatic phenotype in GC cells. However, unlike in Tb regions or isolated GC cells, the fluorescence intensity of ARHGAP12 in metastatic GC cells that had already colonized and formed clusters (with more than five tumor cells per cluster) in lymph nodes was nearly comparable to that observed in the primary lesion. These observations led us to hypothesize that ARHGAP12 is not constitutively expressed in gastric cancer cells but rather undergoes dynamic regulation to adapt to the metastatic demands at different stages of tumor progression. While previous research has identified ARHGAP12 as a downstream effector of the Met receptor that inhibits general cellular growth and invasion in vitro [17], our study advances this understanding by revealing its specific spatial downregulation at the invasive front and Tb regions. Furthermore, we demonstrated its crucial role in lymphatic dissemination and transendothelial migration, providing a novel clinical context for ARHGAP12 deficiency in gastric cancer progression. It is conceivable that the metastatic potential of gastric cancer cells could be attenuated by restoring ARHGAP12 expression with small-molecule modulators or targeted therapeutic agents. Moreover, the potential involvement of ARHGAP12 in the formation of the pre-metastatic niche represents a promising area of investigation. Whether its immunomodulatory function contributes to immune homeostasis within this niche remains to be elucidated through further experimental studies.

The role of ARHGAP family genes in myeloid immune cells cannot be ignored. A seminal study by Yan Qi et al. demonstrated that ARHGAP4 is markedly overexpressed in human acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients and correlates with poor prognosis. Functional experiments revealed that ARHGAP4 knockdown significantly impairs AML cell viability and colony-forming capacity while inducing apoptotic cell death, establishing ARHGAP4 as a survival factor in leukemic cells [18]. Beyond ARHGAP4, other ARHGAP family members have been implicated in cancer biology more broadly, though their specific roles in myeloid cells warrant further investigation. For instance, ARHGAP18 has been shown to promote proliferation and invasion in hepatocellular carcinoma through mechanisms involving transcription factor GATA1 [19], while ARHGAP9 acts as a tumor suppressor in liver cancer by upregulating FOXJ2 and E-cadherin expression [20]. The divergent functions of ARHGAP proteins - some acting as oncogenes (e.g., ARHGAP4 in AML) and others as tumor suppressors (e.g., ARHGAP9 in HCC) - likely reflect cell-type-specific signaling contexts and interaction partners. Emerging evidence from single-cell RNA sequencing atlases (e.g., Human Protein Atlas) suggests cell-type enriched expression of certain ARHGAP genes, underscoring the need for high-resolution transcriptional profiling of ARHGAP family expression in normal and malignant myeloid cells.

Despite these findings, several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the clinical validation was conducted on a relatively small cohort of 27 patients. Although mIHC provided significant spatial insights into ARHGAP12 downregulation at the invasive front and metastatic foci, the modest sample size precluded a robust multivariate Cox regression analysis, thus limiting our ability to definitively establish ARHGAP12 as an independent prognostic factor. Second, our functional investigations focused primarily on the tumor-intrinsic role of ARHGAP12. Given its detected expression in myeloid cells via scRNA-seq, ARHGAP12 deficiency may also modulate the tumor microenvironment (TME) by altering the inflammatory landscape or promoting an immunosuppressive niche that facilitates tumor budding; however, this “extrinsic” role remains unexplored as functional validations in immune cells or co-culture systems were not performed. Future research utilizing cell-type-specific knockout models will be essential to decouple the tumor-intrinsic and microenvironmental contributions of ARHGAP12. Finally, the depth of our molecular mechanistic exploration was limited. While bioinformatic enrichment associated ARHGAP12 with focal adhesion pathways, the specific downstream effectors mediating cytoskeletal reorganization and the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) require further biochemical validation to facilitate the development of targeted therapies that mimic ARHGAP12's tumor-suppressive functions.

In this study, we utilized clinical samples from GC patients to validate the correlation between ARHGAP12 expression and lymph node metastasis. Our results demonstrated that patients with low ARHGAP12 expression exhibited a higher incidence of lymph node metastasis. However, we could not exclude the possibility that variations in tumor stage might influence the frequency of metastasis, as relatively higher ARHGAP12 levels were observed in patients diagnosed with stage I disease.
