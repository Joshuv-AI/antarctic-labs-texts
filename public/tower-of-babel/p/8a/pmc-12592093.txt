Signet‐Ring Cell Type Adenocarcinoma of the Urachus: A Rare Case
Authors: Samin Khannejad, Sevim Soleimani, Hamed Rajaii, Ali Goudarzi Karim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUrachal adenocarcinoma is a rare subset of bladder cancer originating from the urachus. We present a case of a 62‐year‐old woman with a 2‐year history of persistent dysuria, urinary frequency, and urgency. On physical examination, a firm, non‐tender suprapubic mass was detected. Cystoscopy was performed and a bladder mass was detected. After obtaining the biopsy, pathology confirmed a mucinous signet‐ring cell type adenocarcinoma of urachal origin with negative margins and no vascular or perineural invasion. Following negative tumor markers, colonoscopy and CT scans for metastasis, the patient underwent partial cystectomy with tumor resection. No adjuvant therapy was administered. At 6‐month follow‐up, the patient was asymptomatic, and imaging showed no disease progression.

Introduction

Urachal adenocarcinoma is a rare type of bladder cancer originating from the epithelium‐lined tubular structure known as the urachus, comprising only 0.17%–0.34% of all bladder carcinomas. It typically affects males, aging from 50 to 60 years [1, 2]. Mucinous urachal carcinoma is the most common non‐cystic urachal adenocarcinoma subtype. However, it accounts for < 2% of all bladder cancers. The primary sites of involvement are trigone, dome, and lateral wall of the bladder [3, 4]. Hematuria is the most commonly observed symptom, followed by mucous‐like discharge, dysuria, umbilical discharge, pelvic mass, or abdominal pain [5, 6].

To date, few cases of signet‐ring cell type urachal adenocarcinoma have been reported [7]. In the current report, we additionally report a 62‐year‐old female patient who experienced dysuria, urinary frequency, and urgency, without hematuria, and was later diagnosed with signet‐ring cell type urachal adenocarcinoma.

Case History and Examination

A 62‐year‐old non‐smoker woman visited a urology office, with persistent dysuria, urinary frequency, and urgency for the past 2 years. The patient denied mucin‐like discharge, hematuria, or abdominal pain. No other constitutional symptoms such as myalgia, weight loss, jaundice, or weakness and malaise were noted. Her medical history was only positive for hypertension, which was controlled with losartan. Her surgical history was only positive for two cesarean sections. Her family history was negative for urological morbidities. Only her grandmother passed from breast cancer at the age of 60. On physical examination, a palpable, non‐tender, firm mass was present in the suprapubic region.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

The results of the initial lab tests, including complete blood count and blood biochemistry, were normal. In addition, the urine analysis was sterile. Accordingly, serologic tests, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), carbohydrate antigen 125 (CA125), and carbohydrate antigen 19‐9 (CA19‐9) were within the normal limits.

An ultrasonography was performed, and showed a 30 × 37 mm solid mass in the dome of the bladder. Subsequently, a computed tomography (CT) scan with intravenous (iv) contrast of the pelvic area demonstrated that a calcified mass of 40 × 32 × 40 mm was reported in the left para midline area with extension to the rectus muscles around the umbilicus (Figure 1). Accordingly, the patient underwent cystoscopy, which demonstrated a 30 × 40 mm mass with transitional healthy mucosa, in the dome and the anterior wall of the bladder, possibly with an extrinsic origin. Accordingly, a biopsy was obtained.

Axial CT scan view of the pelvis with IV contrast, the lesion is marked with white arrows.

The resected specimen was properly fixed in 10% buffered formalin and sent to the pathology laboratory for evaluation. Grossly the specimen was a solid, grayish‐white mass, measuring 6 × 4 × 4 mm with a solid cystic appearance on cut sections, filled with mucinous materials. After processing, paraffin sections were stained using the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) standard staining method. The histopathology revealed features of a mucin‐producing adenocarcinoma (Figure 2).

(A) Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)‐stained section (low power) showing a malignant epithelial proliferation arising from the urachal mucosa. (B) H&E‐stained section (higher power) demonstrating signet‐ring cell type adenocarcinoma cells with mucin‐filled cytoplasm and displaced nuclei. (C) Immunohistochemical staining positive for cytokeratin (CK20) highlighting the glandular origin of the tumor cells. (D) Immunohistochemical staining with nuclear positivity for CDX2 consistent with enteric differentiation.

Colonoscopy was performed to rule out other sources of malignancy, including gastrointestinal adenocarcinoma. The colonoscopy showed normal results in the entire colon. Furthermore, the CT scan of the abdomen, and the chest revealed no findings suggesting distant metastasis.

With a suspicion for urachal adenocarcinoma, the patient underwent a partial cystectomy and tumor resection with a margin of 1 cm from the anterior wall of the bladder. The skin and fascia were opened with a Pfannenstiel incision. The connection of the umbilicus with the bladder was completely dissected. A well‐defined firm mass of 40 × 50 mm with a urachal origin was seen with a compressive effect on the anterior wall of the bladder that penetrated the detrusor of the peri‐vesical fat tissue. The mass in the path from the umbilicus to the bladder did not adhere to the surrounding tissues (Figure 3). The umbilicus and rectus fascia were preserved during, and subsequent to the surgery. The avid‐biotin‐peroxidase method was performed for immunostaining, and the panel results were positive for CK20, CKX2, Ki‐67, CD15, and β‐catenin, and negative for CK7, AMACR, P40, P63, and PAX8. Pathology reports confirmed a mucinous signet‐ring cell type adenocarcinoma, with no vascular or peri‐neural invasion, and negative margins (Figure 2).

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient underwent no adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy, and at 6 months follow‐up, the patient was doing well, without the previous symptoms. Chest, pelvic, and abdominal CT scans showed no signs of new progression of the disease.

Discussion

Clinical and Pathological Features of Urachal Carcinoma

Urachal carcinoma was initially described by Hue and Jacquin in 1863, with adenocarcinoma comprising almost 90% of urachal carcinomas [1]. The urachus, an embryological remnant arising from the urogenital sinus and allantois, establishes a connection between the bladder and the umbilicus. It is centered in the anterior wall or dome of the bladder and has a predominant invasion of muscular or deeper tissues with sharp demarcation between the tumor and surface bladder urothelium [8].

Hematuria is the most common associated symptom with urachal malignancies. Nonetheless, during the early stages, patients often remain asymptomatic, leading to diagnosis at more advanced stages [5, 6, 9]. The most common sites for metastasis are the liver, lungs, and bones. According to a study conducted by Siefker‐Radtke et al., 7 cases of 42 with urachal masses had metastasis [10]. To the best of our knowledge, only one case of metastasis and bowel obstruction has been documented. Moreover, cases of bone metastasis and ovarian metastasis have been previously reported [11, 12].

Staging and Surgical Management of Urachal Carcinoma

In addition to TNM staging and the MD Anderson Cancer Center, Sheldon et al. and Mayo Clinic suggested tumor staging systems which are now widely used [6, 13] (Table 1). In the present case, the tumor was stage II based on the Sheldon staging system. Differentiating between urachal and non‐urachal adenocarcinoma is crucial. Urachal mucinous adenocarcinoma is treated with partial rather than radical cystectomy and has a better prognosis [14]. Since this malignancy is rare and few reports exist, the comparison of partial and radical cystectomy is unsettled. Nevertheless, Molina et al. reported similar outcomes following radical surgery versus partial cystectomy with umbilicus and urachus excision [5, 15]. Furthermore, in some cases, laparoscopic surgery is suggested over open surgery [13, 16]. In this case, radical cystectomy with umbilicus preservation was performed.

Adjuvant Therapy and Prognosis in Urachal Carcinoma

The role of adjuvant chemotherapy is undetermined. Nevertheless, it has been reported that in cases of involvement of lymph nodes, or peritoneum adjuvant chemotherapy may be beneficial [17, 18]. Additionally, the tumor is not sensitive to radiotherapy regimens and is therefore not recommended [13]. The overall prognosis of bladder adenocarcinoma is poor due to late diagnosis and in advanced or metastatic cases the 5‐year survival is only up to 50% and the mean survival is up to 24 months [9, 18]. In the current study, chemotherapy was not initiated due to the absence of metastases. The prognosis of the tumor is associated with stage, marginal positivity, surgical intervention including partial or complete resection of the urachus and umbilical cord, and pathological grading [13, 14]. However, according to previously reported literature lymph node resection is not associated with a better prognosis [5, 19].

Conclusion

This case underscores the rarity, and diagnostic complexities of urachal mucinous adenocarcinoma, regarding its atypical presentation. This report highlights the importance of maintaining a keen eye for complex conditions, such as malignancies, when addressing refractory chronic urinary symptoms. It also contributes to a deeper understanding of this rare bladder cancer subtype, underscoring the need for urologists to carefully review and consider such uncommon malignancies in their practice.
