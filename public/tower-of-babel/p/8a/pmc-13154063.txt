Severe Acquired Long QT Syndrome in Childhood-Onset Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
Authors: Amy W Blasini, Aaron Scarpa, Daniel H Glaser, Jeffrey M Vinocur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) is a chronic inflammatory disease that can affect any organ system, including the heart. Cardiac rhythm abnormalities are a known complication.Case SummaryA pediatric patient presented with new-onset SLE complicated by seizures and myopericarditis. During induction immunosuppression, electrocardiogram showed severe QTc prolongation. Extensive investigation failed to identify a primary cause. Ultimately, escalation of immunosuppression led to sustained resolution of QTc prolongation.DiscussionWhile there is a known association between acute myopericarditis and QTc prolongation, this case highlights a severe prolongation of the QTc that rapidly improved only after appropriate immunomodulation and in the absence of other cardiac dysfunction.Take-Home MessagesThis case notes the importance of expedient monitoring and treatment of severely prolonged QTc with concurrent investigation into the etiology. The case also illustrates the role of immunosuppression in the treatment of acquired long QT syndrome in cSLE.Key words: acquired long QT syndrome, childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematous, electrocardiogram

History of Presentation

A 14-year-old girl with no past medical history was hospitalized after a convulsive episode in the setting of 2 weeks of fever and abdominal pain. Associated symptoms included nonbloody and nonbilious emesis, diarrhea without melena, decreased appetite, chest pain without palpitations, and myalgias. Physical examination revealed lethargy, tachycardia, regular heart rhythm without murmurs, decreased aeration of bilateral lung bases, abdominal distension without tenderness to palpation, no peripheral edema, no lymphadenopathy, normal joint examination, no rash or ulcerations, and nonfocal neurologic examination. Laboratory work-up was notable for mild pancytopenia (severe lymphopenia), hypocomplementemia, normal immunoglobulin G level, elevated ferritin, elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (peak: 8,548 pg/mL, normal: <125 pg/mL), elevated high-sensitivity troponin T (peak: 312 ng/L), and multiple positive autoantibodies (double-stranded DNA, U1-RNP, anti-SSA/Ro, and anti-SSB/La). Evaluation of new-onset seizure included normal brain magnetic resonance imaging, normal cerebrospinal fluid studies, and electroencephalography consistent with seizure activity. Computed tomography revealed bilateral pleural effusions. Echocardiogram showed normal left ventricular systolic function, mild concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, and moderate pericardial effusion without features of tamponade.

Childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus (cSLE) was diagnosed, with chief organ manifestations including bone marrow suppression, myopericarditis, and seizure. Initial treatment included ibuprofen, colchicine, furosemide, and levetiracetam, with high-dose corticosteroids added once SLE was diagnosed. Serial cardiac testing was performed.

Investigations

Electrocardiogram (ECG) on admission showed sinus rhythm with top-normal PR interval, low voltage, right-axis deviation (manifesting with high lateral rS morphology with very small r waves), widespread T-wave abnormalities, and mildly prolonged QTc at 480 ms (Figure 1A). On day 5 of admission, follow up ECG revealed extensive repolarization abnormality with severely prolonged QTc at 575 ms (Figure 1B). Repeat echocardiogram was unchanged.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for prolonged QTc includes congenital long QT syndrome (LQTS) and acquired QT prolongation due to various causes including medications, electrolyte derangements, central nervous system pathology, and other factors that affect myocardial well-being.

Management

Given the severely prolonged QTc, the patient was transferred to the pediatric cardiac intensive care unit for closer observation. Acquired LQTS was suspected given the finding of normal T-wave morphology and QTc (420 ms) on ECG obtained several weeks before the hospitalization. Electrolytes at the time of sentinel ECG were normal except for mild hypocalcemia. No QT-prolonging medications had been given in the preceding 24 hours.

Intravenous magnesium and calcium were administered, with shortening of QTc to 535 ms, and continuous magnesium infusion was maintained for several days to reduce arrhythmic risk while treating the underlying disease. Intravenous lidocaine bolus was trialed and did not result in further shortening. Mexiletine was deferred given no response to lidocaine and concurrent pancreatitis. Telemetry showed rare to occasional premature ventricular contractions with no high-grade ectopy. The PR interval peaked at 250 ms on day 3 and gradually normalized. The QRS axis gradually normalized, with high lateral rS morphology transitioning back to Rs morphology.

Given multiorgan system involvement of the lupus, including persistent myopericarditis complicated by severe acquired LQTS, the patient was treated with cyclophosphamide, rituximab, anakinra, and intravenous immunoglobulin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The QTc remained prolonged for multiple days on continuous magnesium, mainly 530 to 540 ms except if superimposed electrolyte derangement. Then, there was rapid improvement over the 24 hours after the first dose of intravenous immunoglobulin, initially to 480 ms (Figure 1C) and then gradually to normal over the next week.

Evaluation for congenital LQTS included no known family history and negative genetic testing. An in-house exome panel sequenced on the Illumina platform and evaluated via custom pipeline examined 30 genes associated with LQTS and 163 genes associated with arrhythmia syndromes. No reportable variants were identified. Since completing cyclophosphamide treatment, the patient has been maintained on low-dose prednisone, mycophenolate mofetil, and belimumab. QTc has remained normal after more than 1 year of follow-up (Figure 1D).

Discussion

This report presents a case of severe acquired LQTS in the setting of newly diagnosed cSLE that resolved with immunomodulation. Childhood-onset SLE is a rare chronic autoimmune disease, with a prevalence of approximately 3.3 to 8.8 per 100,000 children; the course of cSLE is notably more severe than that of adult-onset SLE, with higher disease activity and up to 4.4 times increased risk of myopericardial manifestations.1,2 Conduction abnormalities are a known complication of lupus-associated cardiac manifestations; ECG findings may include arrhythmias, atrioventricular heart block, ventricular conduction disturbances, ventricular hypertrophy, ST-segment changes, or QT prolongation.3

LQTS is classified into congenital or acquired, and the differentiation is important in terms of management. Acquired LQTS is typically due to offending drugs or the result of electrolyte abnormalities, but there is increased recognition that inflammation and autoimmunity play an important role.4 This patient's acquired LQTS may have been multifactorial but notably persisted despite electrolyte stabilization. The overwhelming improvement and subsequent normalization of QTc occurred after intensification of immunosuppression and improved SLE disease control. This suggests that autoimmunity was a key determinant in QTc prolongation.

The autoantibody profile of this patient, specifically positive anti-SSA and anti-SSB, is of particular interest given a growing body of research around autoantibody patterns and cardiac manifestations.3,5 Transplacental passage of maternal anti-SSA antibodies has long been associated with increased risk of congenital heart block.6,7 More recently, adult studies (in patients with and without SLE) have shown an association between anti-SSA antibodies and QTc prolongation.8 The same association has not clearly been demonstrated in cSLE, in part owing to small study size.9,10 Monitoring laboratory results during follow-up of our patient demonstrated persistent positive anti-SSA/Ro but conversion to negative anti-SSB/La titers; anti-SSB/La has not previously been reported as a risk for QTc prolongation in children or adults. The role of this patient's antibody profile in her acquired LQTS is unclear. Cardiac manifestations in cSLE, and specifically acquired LQTS in SLE, are an area for further research.

Conclusions

We report a case of severe acquired LQTS in a patient with cSLE. With prompt diagnosis of cSLE, serial monitoring of ECGs in the setting of myopericarditis, and rapid escalation of care and treatment, this patient was able to avoid life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.
