Atox1 promotes CRC progression by protecting DNA damage through interacts with a novel copper-binding protein PARP1
Authors: Mingming Sun, Xinru Zhai, Qi Yan, Chenxin Yang, Lina Yang, Xiaobo Zhai, Xinyue Geng, Yanping Li, Chunze Zhang, Tao He, Shuangping Liu, Changliang Shan, Shuai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The copper chaperone antioxidant 1 (Atox1) is a key driver in several cancers, with increased expression associated with poor survival. Its role in colorectal cancer (CRC) had been largely unexplored. Based on TCGA data and clinical samples, we found elevated Atox1 expression in CRC. Knockdown of Atox1 suppressed cell proliferation and tumor growth in vitro and in vivo, and silencing it led to DNA damage in CRC cells. Further experiments showed that Atox1 directly interacted with Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) at the copper binding site and positively regulated its activity. Mechanistic studies indicated that PARP1 is a novel copper-binding protein, and excessive copper inhibited PARP1’s enzymatic activity and weakened DNA damage repair. Atox1 relieves copper’s inhibitory effect on PARP1. Targeting Atox1 with DC_AC50 also induced DNA damage, suppressed cell proliferation, and inhibited tumor growth in CRC. The combination of DC_AC50 and Olaparib had a stronger inhibitory effect on CRC growth. This research demonstrated that Atox1 interacts with PARP1, a novel copper-binding protein, by regulating DNA damage, cell proliferation, and tumor growth in CRC. The Atox1 - PARP1 axis could be a prospective therapeutic target for CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-026-02854-8.

Background

Copper is an important trace metal element in living organisms. It exists in two ionic forms, Cu+ and Cu2+, which act as cofactors for proteins and enzymes and participate in various life activities including cancer [1]. Excessive copper within cells can be harmful and may lead to cellular toxicity, such as inducing DNA damage. Therefore, maintaining the balance of intracellular copper ions is crucial to prevent DNA damage. However, the exact mechanism remains unclear.

Copper transporter 1 (CTR1) facilitates the entry of Cu2+ into the cell. It then binds to copper chaperone proteins and is distributed throughout the cell via protein interactions, ensuring secure transport and maintaining copper balance [2, 3]. Several copper chaperone proteins exist, such as copper chaperone antioxidant 1 (Atox1), Copper chaperone for superoxide dismutase (CCS ), and Cox17, which are crucial for copper uptake and balance [3, 4]. For example, Atox1 is a water-soluble protein composed of 68 amino acids. It can transport Cu2+ to ATP7 alpha (ATP7A) and ATP7 beta (ATP7B) and then enter the secretory pathway [3, 5]. In addition, Atox1 can enter the cell nucleus due to a copper-dependent transcription factor and also transfer copper to Cysteine-rich protein 2 (CRIP2), thus inducing alterations in the secondary structure of the CRIP2 protein and eventually facilitating its ubiquitination and degradation [6]. DC_AC50 is a small molecule that inhibits the human copper-trafficking proteins Atox1 and CCS, thus offering a selective method to disrupt cellular copper transport [7]. Therefore, in-depth research on the structure and function of Atox1 is helpful for understanding the transport and homeostasis of copper within cells. Thus, identifying novel copper-binding proteins within cells and exploring their roles in modulating DNA damage remain significant challenges.

Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1), as the main executor of intracellular poly (ADP-ribose) ylation modification (PARylation), plays a core role in key cellular processes such as DNA damage repair [8–10]. This protein has three domains: the DNA-binding domain at the amino terminal contains a nuclear localization sequence and two zinc finger motifs, which are crucial for the activation of PARP1; The central self-modified domain mediates protein-protein interactions, can recruit repair factors to the damage site, and simultaneously participate in the glycosylation and dimerization processes of PARP1 itself. The catalytic domain at the carboxyl terminal catalyzes the synthesis of the PAR chain through the NAD+ binding site [11, 12]. It is worth noting that our research found that copper can inhibit the formation of PAR by binding to the zinc finger structure. Currently, PARP1 inhibitors in clinical application, such as Olaparib, Rucaparib, and Niraparib, are all NAD+ competitive inhibitors that exert anti-tumor effects by binding to catalytic domains [13, 14]. However, with the wide application of PARP1 inhibitors, the problem of drug resistance has become increasingly prominent. In this study, we innovatively discovered that targeting the copper chaperone protein Atox1 can significantly enhance the anti-tumor efficacy of PARP1 inhibitors, providing a new strategy for addressing clinical drug resistance issues.

In this study, we discovered that Atox1 is highly expressed in colorectal cancer (CRC). The growth of cells and tumors can be suppressed and DNA damage can be induced by knocking down Atox1 or treating with DC_AC50. Further exploration reveals that PARP1 is a new copper - binding protein. Excessive copper inhibits PARP1’s enzymatic activity and weakens DNA damage repair. Atox1 relieves copper’s inhibitory effect on PARP1, thereby restraining the self - modification of PAR and reducing its ability to repair DNA damage. Additionally, functional studies have found that the combination of DC_AC50 and the PARP1 inhibitor Olaparib can enhance the anti - tumor effect. This study offers new concepts and a theoretical foundation for the clinical treatment of CRC.

Methods

Clinical samples

34 paired CRC tissue microarrays which included 34 CRC tissues、34 adjacent non-tumor colorectal tissues and 34 distant normal (DN) colon tissues were collected from Tianjin Union Medical Center (Tianjin, China) after surgical resection. All the clinical CRC specimens have the written consent approving the use of the samples for research purposes from patients. The relevant characteristics are shown in the Supplementary Table.

Cell culture

HCT116, HCT8, SW620, LoVo, RKO and SW480 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, ExCell Bio, China). Normal proliferating Human normal colonic epithelial cells (NCM460) was cultured in RPMI 1640 medium with 10% FBS. 293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS.

Cell proliferation assay

The human CRC cell lines HCT8 and LoVo were seeded in 24-well plates at density of 2 × 104 cells per well. After 24 h, the cells were treated with the indicated concentrations of DC_AC50. Cell growth was determined by recording cell numbers at 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 days after being treated with DC_AC50.

Colony formation assay

The human CRC cell lines HCT8 and LoVo were seeded in 6-well plates at 1500 cells per well. After 24 h the medium was replaced with cell culture medium containing DC_AC50. The culture was continued for 10 days and then stained with crystal violet and photographed.

Transwell cell migration assay

2 × 104 cells were seeded in a Transwell insert. After a 48 h incubation under migration conditions, the cells that had passed through the Transwell insert were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 5 min, and then stained with 1% crystal violet for 25 min, and washed 4 times with PBS. The cells on the inner membrane of the insert were wiped off by using cotton swabs. The results were photographed under a microscope.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models for tumor formation

The fresh CRC tumor tissues used to establish the PDX mice model were provided by Tianjin Union Medical Center (Tianjin, China). The tumor samples were cut into pieces of approximately 1 mm3, and the tissues were pushed under the skin of mice using TROCHAR. When the tumor volumes reached approximately 50 mm3, mice were randomly divided into groups of 8 mice. To investigate the effect of Atox1 on the tumorigenicity of CRC, Atox1 shRNA or control shRNA virus was injected precisely into the center of the xenograft tumors first three days for three times. Statistical analyses were performed by comparison in relation to the control group with a paired Student’s t test.

To explore the combined effect of DC_AC50 and Olaparib on the tumorigenicity of CRC, mice received either vehicle control, DC_AC50 (20 mg/kg) alone, Olaparib (30 mg/kg) alone, or DC_AC50 and Olaparib together. They were administered every two days via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection. After the inhibitor treatments, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumors were excised, imaged, and weighed. Statistical analyses were performed by comparison with the control group using an unpaired Student’s t test.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemistry was performed on paraffin-embedded sections. Tissue sections were dewaxed and rehydrated following standard protocols. Antigen retrieval was carried out by boiling the samples in citrate buffer for 45 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was inhibited with 3% hydrogen peroxidase. The sections were blocked in 3% BSA dissolved in PBS and incubated with primary antibodies (Ki67, Atox1) overnight at 4 °C. The sections were rinsed in PBS and developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB). The sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed with NP40 lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 10 mM HEPES [pH = 7.0], 1%NP40, 5 mM Na4P2O7, 5 mM NaF, 2 mM Na3VO4) on ice for 20 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein samples were separated by 10% or 15% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto 0.2–0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA) according to protein molecular mass. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h. Then the membranes incubated overnight at 4 °C (or 2 h at room temperature) with the primary antibody. The membranes incubated 1 h at room temperature with secondary antibody. Lastly, the signals were detected using Luminol substrate solution (Millipore, USA).

Measurement of Cu2+ and ROS levels

Copper concentration was measured with the Copper Assay Kit (Elabscience, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Intracellular ROS levels were analyzed using the ROS Assay Kit (Beyotime, China).

Immunoprecipitation or Flag-pull down assay

Cells were lysed in NP40 lysis buffer on ice 20 min and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Subsequently, the cell lysates were subjected to immunoprecipitation using different antibodyconjugated beads (or Flag-conjugated beads), which were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Immune complexes were extensively washed for three times with cold TBS and analyzed by western blotting with specific antibodies.

Cellular Thermal Shift Assay (CETSA)

Mixing 1 µg recombinant protein PARP1 with 140 µL TBS, then divided them into two aliquots. Treat one aliquot with CuCl2 (100 µM) and the other aliquot with the vehicle. After incubating 10 minutes at room temperature, divide the respective lysates into smaller (20 µL) aliquots. Heat each aliquot at different temperatures (50 °C, 55.2 °C, 61.8 °C, 64.8 °C, 70 °C) for 10 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 3 min. Then, analyze the samples by western blot.

Microscale thermophoresis (MST) assay

The MST assay was conducted using Monolith NT.115 (Nano Temper, Germany). A concentration of 2 µM PARP1 and 2 mM DLG/ETGE were incubated with various concentrations of CuCl2 (ranging from 0.076 to 625 µM). Subsequently, the samples were loaded into a hydrophilic silicon capillary (K0012). Data analysis was conducted utilizing the Monolith software.

In vitro PARylation assay

To investigate the activity of PARP1, poly(ADP-ribose)ylation was measured after activation of PARP1 by a nicked plasmid. PARP1 substrate was generated by nicking a pLVX3-based circular plasmid, containing a single EcoR1 restriction site, with EcoR1 for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by heat inactivation of the enzyme at 65 °C for 20 min. After 10 min preincubation of PARP1 (100 ng) with CuCl2 or Atox1 in preincubation buffer (83 mM HEPES, 10 mM MgCl2, 11 mM KCl, 0.2 mM EDTA, 0.11 mM DTT, 5.6% v/v Glycerol, pH 7.9) at 25 °C, the PARP1 reaction was carried out for 5 min at 37 °C in reaction buffer (50 µM β-NAD+, 2 ng/µL PARP1, 0.2 ng/µL nicked plasmid, 41 mM HEPES, 5 mM MgCl2, 4 mM KCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.04 mM DTT, 2% v/v glycerol, pH 7.9). The reaction was terminated by adding 1× SDS loading buffer. The PARylation of targeted proteins were then detected using an immunoblot assay with an anti-PAR antibody [12, 15].

TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) anlysis

The RNA-seq data of COAD patients from the TCGA database website (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) was downloaded, and the data was divided into two groups according to types of the patients’ tissue (normal or tumor). Then, the mRNA level of Atox1 was compared between tumor group and the normal group by t-test.

Survival curves were analyzed by GEPIA plotter

GEPIA Plotter is a widely used online tool for survival analysis. This platform integrates multiple large-scale databases including GEO, EGA, and TCGA, and enables assessment of the correlation between gene expression levels and patient survival rates across various tumor types. On the website homepage (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn), users can enter a gene name, choose Methods and Group Cutoff, select the target cancer type (e.g., colorectal cancer), and then click “Plot” to generate the corresponding survival curve. The tool automaticall provides the log-rank P-value. Finally, the resulting plot can be downloaded in PDF format for use in this study.

KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) analysis

The RNA-seq data of COAD patients was downloaded on TCGA database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov). Variance analysis was performed between normal group and tumor group and differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were found. Then, “ClusterProfiler” R Package was used to perform KEGG pathway enrichment analysis using the DEGs. The bubble chart was drawn by “ggplot2” R Package.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prims 8 software was used to analyze data. Statistical details of experiments including statistical tests, mean ± SD. The differences between two groups with similar variances were analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t test. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software. A p value lower than 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Atox1 expression is elevated in CRC

To investigate the potential role of Atox1 in CRC tumorigenesis and chemotherapy response, we systematically analyzed Atox1 expression across clinical samples and cell lines. Analysis of The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) data revealed significantly elevated Atox1 expression in CRC tissues compared to normal controls (Fig. 1A). To validate these findings, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on a CRC tissue microarray (Cohort 1: n = 34, including tumor (T), adjacent normal (AN), and distant normal (DN) colon tissues). Strikingly, the expression of Atox1 was markedly upregulated in tumor tissues, with 29.41% (10/34) of cases showing positive staining and 11.76% (4/34) exhibiting strong positivity (Supplementary Table 1). Quantitative assessment confirmed that Atox1 levels were significantly higher in CRC tissues than in paired normal counterparts (Fig. 1B−C). However, no significant association was observed between Atox1 expression and tumor grade (Supplementary Table 2). We further examined Atox1 expression in a panel of human CRC cell lines (HCT116, HCT8, SW620, LoVo, RKO, SW480) relative to normal colonic epithelial cells (NCM460). Consistent with our clinical data, Atox1 was highly expressed in most CRC cell lines (Fig. 1D). Importantly, elevated Atox1 expression was correlated with poor prognosis in CRC patients (Fig. 1E), underscoring its potential clinical relevance. In summary, our findings demonstrate that Atox1 is frequently overexpressed in CRC and associated with adverse outcomes, suggesting its potential as a novel therapeutic target in CRC.

Atox1 promotes CRC cell proliferation and migration

To investigate Atox1’s role in CRC, we established stable Atox1-knockdown cell lines in HCT8 and LoVo cells using shRNA-mediated silencing. To confirm the knockdown, we first measured Atox1 expression levels by qRT-PCR (Fig. S1A). Atox1 depletion significantly suppressed cell proliferation and colony formation (Fig. 2A and C), and pharmacological inhibition with DC_AC50 had similar effects (Fig. 2B and D). We also found that Atox1 knockdown or inhibition reduced the migratory capacity of CRC cells (Fig. 2E− F and Fig. S1B−C), suggesting its role in metastatic potential. To validate these findings in vivo, we employed a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model generated from fresh CRC tumor tissues. Intratumoral delivery of Atox1-targeting shRNA significantly attenuated tumor growth (Fig. 2G), resulting in smaller tumor volumes (Fig. 2H) and reduced tumor weight (Fig. 2I). Consistent results were obtained in an independent PDX model (Fig. S1D−F). The expression of Ki67, a marker of proliferative activity, was diminished in Atox1-knocked down tumors (Fig. 2N). When evaluating the therapeutic potential of DC_AC50’s in the PDX model, intraperitoneal administration (40 mg/kg, every two days) led to a pronounced reduction in tumor growth (Fig. 2J and K), tumor mass (Fig. 2L), and Ki67 levels (Fig. 2O) compared to the DMSO-treated control group. And with no significant effect on mice’s body weight (Fig. 2M). This result was further validated in an additional PDX model (Fig. S1G−J). In summary, our data show that Atox1 drives CRC progression, and its genetic or pharmacological inhibition suppresses oncogenic phenotypes, highlighting Atox1 as a promising therapeutic target in CRC.

Atox1 regulates DNA damage

As a key copper transporter, Atox1 significantly influences intracellular Cu²⁺ homeostasis in CRC cells. Our experiments revealed that the knockdown of Atox1 resulted in marked Cu²⁺ accumulation compared to control cells (Fig. 3A), and a similar effect was observed with DC_AC50 treatment (Fig. 3B). This copper dysregulation triggered elevated intracellular ROS levels, mirroring the oxidative stress induced by CuCl₂ treatment (Fig. 3C–E). To elucidate the underlying mechanism, KEGG pathway analysis of TCGA data identified Atox1’s involvement in DNA replication pathways (Fig. 3F). Functional studies confirmed that both Atox1 depletion and pharmacological inhibition exacerbated DNA damage, as evidenced by increased DNA tailing and γH2A.X expression (a marker of DNA damage) - phenotypes recapitulated by CuCl₂ treatment (Fig. 3G–M). These findings collectively demonstrate that Atox1 deficiency disrupts copper homeostasis, leading to DNA damage through copper accumulation.

PARP1 is a novel copper-binding protein

PARP1, which comprises three N-terminal zinc finger domains, a BRCT domain, a WGR domain, and a C-terminal catalytic domain (Fig. 4E), is crucial for repairing single-strand breaks (SSBs) and double-strand breaks (DSBs). Since Atox1 expression does not influence PARP1 levels, we hypothesized a potential physical interaction between these proteins. Indeed, co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays conducted both in vivo and in vitro, along with microscale thermophoresis (MST) analysis, confirmed the direct binding between PARP1 and Atox1 (Fig. 4A–C), with a binding affinity of Kd = 13.99 µM (Fig. 4C). Domain mapping experiments indicated that the zinc finger domains of PARP1 mediate this interaction (Fig. 4D). Previous studies have demonstrated that depleting Atox1 causes DNA damage without altering the protein expression level of PARP1. This led us to hypothesize that Atox1 might modulate DNA damage by regulating the activity of PARP1. Intriguingly, Atox1 enhanced PARP1-mediated poly(ADP-ribose) (PAR) formation in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 4F), and PARP1 activity remained unactivated when Atox1 was inhibited by DC_AC50 (Fig. 4G). Given that DC_AC50 binds to the copper transport interface of Atox1, we questioned whether Cu²⁺ could affect PARP1 activity. Significantly, Cu²⁺ suppressed PARylation (Fig. 4H), while Atox1 could counteract the inhibitory effect of Cu²⁺ on PARP1 activity (Fig. 4I), In addition, consistent findings were observed in vivo (Fig. 4J). Collectively, These results reveal a regulatory interplay where Atox1 and Cu²⁺ jointly modulate PARP1 function.

Given Atox1’s role in copper transport, we investigated the Cu²⁺-dependency of this interaction. Notably, the PARP1-Atox1 binding was impaired when using Atox1 mutants that were defective in metal binding (MBS mutants) or upon treatment with the Cu²⁺ chelator bathocuproine sulphonate (BCS), demonstrating that Cu²⁺ was essential for the aforementioned molecular interaction (Fig. 4K and L). Based on the above data, we speculate whether PARP1 is an novel copper-binding protein. Our research has shown that both cellular PARP1 and recombinant PARP1 (rePARP1) possess the ability to bind Cu²⁺, as evidenced by metal affinity chromatography (Fig. 4M). In addition, the thermal shift assays and MST assays have also confirmed this interaction, demonstrating a strong binding affinity between PARP1 and Cu²⁺ (Kd = 12.02 nM) (Fig. 4N and O). To investigate whether the interaction between PARP1 and metal ions is driven by specific coordination rather than non-specific electrostatic attraction, we tested PARP1 binding to a panel of metal ions. The data showed no detectable binding to K+ or Mg2+, thereby ruling out a purely charge-dependent mechanism (Fig. 4P). In addition, when PARP1 deletes the Zn-binding domain (PARP1ΔZn), it cannot bind to Cu2+ either (Fig. 4Q). The above data indicate that PARP1 is a new copper-binding protein.

Atox1 affects DNA damage and proliferation through PARP1

To determine whether Atox1 modulates DNA damage through PARP1 regulation, we conducted rescue experiments in Atox1-knockdown cells. Treatment with the BCS significantly restored cell proliferation, reduced γH2A.X levels, and decreased ROS accumulation in Atox1-deficient cells (Fig. 5A–C). Importantly, reconstitution with wild-type Atox1 (Atox1 WT), rather than the metal-binding defective mutant (Atox1 MBS), reversed these phenotypes (Fig. 5D–F). Furthermore, ectopic expression of wild-type PARP1 (PARP1 WT) similarly rescued the proliferation defect and γH2A.X accumulation in Atox1-depleted cells (Fig. 5G and H). These findings demonstrate that Atox1 regulates the DNA damage response through a copper-dependent modulation of PARP1 activity.

Targeting Atox1 can enhance chemotherapy sensitivity

To further evaluate the broader therapeutic potential of DC_AC50 in combination with standard chemotherapy, we examined its synergistic effects with additional chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-FU, Oxaliplatin, Camptothecin, and Etoposide. Colony formation assays revealed that DC_AC50 significantly augmented the anti-tumor activity of all tested drugs (Fig. 6A and B). Olaparib, a well-characterized PARP1 inhibitor, is widely used as a chemotherapeutic agent for CRC treatment. Given its clinical relevance, we sought to investigate whether DC_AC50 could enhance the sensitivity of CRC cells to Olaparib. Our results demonstrated that the combined treatment of CRC cells with DC_AC50 and Olaparib led to a more pronounced suppression of tumor cell proliferation and colony formation compared to either treatment alone (Fig. 6C and D), indicating that DC_AC50 potentiates the efficacy of Olaparib in CRC. To validate these findings in vivo, we established a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) model in nude mice. While DC_AC50 or Olaparib monotherapy exhibited only modest inhibitory effects on tumor growth, their combination resulted in a marked reduction in tumor volume and mass (Fig. 6E–G). Consistent with these observations, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis revealed a significant decrease in Ki67 expression in the combination treatment group (Fig. 6I), further supporting the enhanced anti-tumor efficacy of the dual therapy. Notably, the combined treatment did not adversely affect the body weight of the mice (Fig. 6H), suggesting a favorable safety profile. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that DC_AC50 significantly enhances the anti-tumor effects of multiple chemotherapeutic agents, including Olaparib, in CRC models.

Discussion

Copper stands as one of the vital trace elements in living organisms. Nevertheless, an overabundance of Cu2+ may also bring about irreversible harm to DNA and trigger the emergence of diseases, including cancer. PARP1 detects SSBs and DSBs in DNA via the base excision repair pathway and takes part in their repair. In this context, we explored whether Cu2+ is implicated in the repair function of PARP1 on DNA damage. For maintaining the equilibrium of Cu2+ inside cells, copper that enters the cytoplasm attaches to copper transporters like Atox1 and CCS, and then is conveyed to targeted proteins through ligand exchange among proteins to play its role. Our prior research verified that DC_AC50 can specifically suppress tumor cell proliferation by binding to the copper transport interface of Atox1 and CCS, hindering the transport of Cu2+ within cells [7]. Moreover, recent studies have discovered that copper - binding protein CRIP2 interacts with Atox1 in the cell nucleus. Atox1 conveys copper to CRIP2, inducing changes in its secondary structure and ultimately boosting ubiquitin - mediated proteasome degradation [6]. Here, we discovered that excessive copper can restrain the activity of PARP1, and Atox1 mitigates the suppression of PARP1 activity by copper through interaction with PARP1, and modulate the repair of DNA damage. Moreover, our results show that PARP1 is a new copper - binding protein. Furthermore, DC_AC50 can augment the anti - tumor activity of the PARP1 inhibitor Olaparib, supplying a novel theoretical foundation for the clinical treatment of CRC.

Besides its typical copper chaperone role, the Atox1 protein can be highly expressed as a copper-dependent transcription factor in various tumors [16]. For example, as a transcription factor, Atox1 regulates the expression of MDC1, a crucial protein in DNA damage response, playing a part in tumor drug resistance and promoting DNA damage repair [17]; CCNB1 facilitates the transfer of copper from Atox1 to CDK1, thus activating its kinase function [18]. Here, we demonstrated that Atox1 was highly expressed in CRC, and knockdown of Atox1 markedly suppressed the proliferation of CRC cells and the growth rate of transplanted tumors. The same effect can be attained by using DC_AC50. Signal pathway analysis using the TCGA database showed that Atox1 regulates DNA damage repair. Consequently, we further explored the impact of Atox1 on DNA damage repair. Knocking down Atox1 or treatment with DC_AC50 will result in an increase in the levels of Cu2+ and ROS within cells, while also inducing DNA damage and an increase in the level of γH2A.X. And an overly high Cu2+ level will also produce the same outcome. These suggest that Atox1 influences DNA damage repair by regulating the level of Cu2+ within cells.

The transfer of copper is mediated by the ligand exchange between copper transporters and targeted proteins. In this study, we found that Atox1 can bind to the DNA binding domain of PARP1. Next, we confirmed through the PARP1 self-modification experiment that Atox1 can activate the PAR modification of PARP1, while Cu2+ and DC_AC50 can inhibit the activation of PARP1’s PAR modification by Atox1. Since PARP1 expression was unaffected by Atox1, DC_AC50, or Cu2+, our data suggest that the modulation of PARP1 activity by Atox1 and DC_AC50 is mediated through Cu2+. The amino acid sequence of PARP1 in NCBI shows that there are three metal-binding domains (MBS)-CXXC in the DNA binding domain of PARP1, namely the amino acids at positions 21–24, 125–129 and 295–299. We speculate that the three metal-binding domains can bind copper. PARP1 was confirmed to be a copper-binding protein through copper column binding assay and MST assay, no binding to Cu2+ was observed for PARP1 when deletion of the DNA binding domain. Our study identifies PARP1 as a novel copper-binding protein and demonstrates the regulatory role of copper in its activity.

PARP1 inhibitor Olaparib is a first-line chemotherapy drug, but with the expansion of its clinical application scope, its drug resistance has gradually emerged. To expand the application scope of Olaparib, we explored whether targeting Atox1 could increase the sensitivity of CRC to Olaparib. The experimental results show that the combined treatment of DC_AC50 significantly increases the killing effect of the chemotherapy drug Olaparib on CRC cells. Based on the above results, targeted intervention of Atox1 not only inhibits the growth of CRC but also enhances the anti-cancer effect of the chemotherapy drug Olaparib.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that Atox1 is highly expressed in CRC and regulates DNA damage repair. Further mechanism research revealed that PARP1 is a novel copper-binding protein, and Atox1 alleviates the inhibition of Cu2+ on its activity by binding to PARP1. In addition, targeting Atox1 or DC_AC50 can enhance the effect of Olaparib in CRC ( Fig. 6J). This work not only provides a new understanding of the relationship between Cu2+ and DNA damage repair, but also provides important guidance for overcoming chemotherapy resistance in CRC.
