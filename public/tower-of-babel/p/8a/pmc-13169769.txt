Quit journey lessons: qualitative insights from men in Qatar who formerly vaped
Authors: Mohammed Al-Hamdani, Rana Abouzoor, Ahmad AlMulla, Hanan Abdul Rahim, Noor ElNakib, Silva Kouyoumjian, Karam Turk-Adawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionVaping among in Qatar is a growing public-health concern, especially among men. Evidence on effective cessation strategies and relapse triggers—especially among older adults in the Middle East—is limited. This qualitative study noted the motivations, quitting strategies, and relapse drivers among Young Adults (YAs, 18–30 years old) and Older Adults (OAs, 31 years or older) who successfully stopped vaping.MethodsBetween May and June 2025, purposive recruitment used Qatar University email lists and paid social media ads. Thirteen males abstinent for ≥ 30 days completed semi-structured online interviews (30–45 min) in Arabic or English. A phenomenological approach guided analysis; transcripts were coded in ATLAS.ti and themes developed using Braun and Clarke’s framework.ResultsSaturation was reached at interview 12 (n = 13). Three themes emerged: social influences (inside and outside the workplace), health-centered decisions, and intrapersonal factors. YAs reported stronger peer influence, immediate social pressure at gatherings, sensory and experiential motives (flavors, vapor clouds, nicotine “boost”), and greater price sensitivity. OAs emphasized cumulative health concerns, spousal encouragement, and workplace stress as primary drivers to quit. Across groups, facilitators included family/spousal support, exercise, willpower/self-restraint, and step-down nicotine strategies (including leveraging fasting). Common relapse triggers were negative emotions (stress, anxiety, depression), sensory/ritual cues, unsupportive peer or household environments, and the discreteness of vaping enabling covert use.ConclusionMale YAs and OAs in Qatar share cessation facilitators but follow age-differentiated pathways. Interventions maybe age-tailored: YAs may benefit from reducing product appeal (pricing, flavor limits), peer-resistance skills, and sensory substitutes; OAs from health-focused messaging, spousal and workplace supports, and clinically guided nicotine tapering or NRT. Family engagement, exercise promotion, sleep-hygiene, and culturally congruent strategies (e.g., Ramadan-based tapering) could strengthen cessation across age groups. The comparisons by age group are to be interpreted with caution due to the small study sample.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-26847-x.

Background

E-cigarette use, commonly known as “vaping,” has increased among youth in the past several years, reaching a global prevalence of 9.2% [1], and continues to be a notable public health issue in the Middle East [2]. In a study involving university students in Qatar, the prevalence of vaping was 14% [3]. Vaping has direct negative effects on psychiatric, respiratory and cardiovascular health—it is addictive [4], damaging to lung tissue and respiratory health [5], and poses cardiovascular health risks [6]. Regular vaping in the Middle East is often reported to have negative effects, including respiratory, oral, and gastrointestinal effects, with 43% of users reporting compulsive use (taking their device out 16 or more times per vaped day) and 50.3% reporting binge vaping (taking 10 or more puffs per vaped episode) [7]. Vaping also poses a serious indirect public health concern by virtue of its relation to smoking; The odds of smoking initiation among youth are 4.6 times higher if they currently vape compared to those who do not vape [8]. Vaping also poses renormalization concerns [9]. Approximately 21.8% of cigarette smoking initiation among youth who vape is associated with vaping [10]. Furthermore, vaping is associated with increased risks for smoking relapses, as individuals who previously smoked and currently vape are more likely to return to smoking than those with no history of vaping [11]. Binge and daily vaping patterns increase the likelihood of other substance use [12]. While the majority of evidence suggests that vaping is damaging to public health because of its direct and indirect effects, including the transition of youth to smoking following vaping [as summarized in [13], few studies have shown otherwise [14].

Youth and young adults who vape cite experimentation as a primary reason for starting to vape [15]. Furthermore, they cite flavor, nicotine rush, and tricks (creating shapes such as circles through exhaling vape clouds) as motives for continued use [16]. Product discreteness, i.e., the ease with which a device can be used and hidden, is an important characteristic that appeals to young users [17]. There are important differences in vaping motives by age and gender. For example, male youth report nicotine rush, the enjoyable feeling from the psychoactive effects of nicotine, as a main reason. Female youth report tricks, creating shapes when blowing out vape clouds, as an important positive aspect of use [16]. Reasons for discontinuing use include losing interest and experiencing negative effects from vaping [18]. Young people who vape specifically report health concerns and take breaks to avoid building tolerance as reasons for discontinuing use [19].

In terms of strategies used by youth and young adults who manage to quit, will-power tops the list, followed by deliberate attempts to restrict oneself from the product and healthy alternative behaviors [20]. Social influences seem to be the most frequently reported trigger in the journey toward quitting vaping, followed by substance use and mental health triggers [20]. However, female youth are more influenced by social influences relative to both female and male young adults and male young adults are more influenced by substance use triggers relative to male and female youth [20]. Willpower and the elimination of social triggers are associated with long-term quitting, and the experience and substance use, as well as sensory cues for vaping, are less likely to be experienced by long-term quitters [21]. Using social support systems which include professional help through counselling and nicotine replacement therapy are also commonly reported as quit strategies for people who formerly vaped [20]. Evidence to date shows support for pharmacotherapy, education and text-based digital support showing increased abstinence with these interventions [22] although the evidence has been characterized as having low certainty due to risk of bias [23].

Despite advances in research on reasons for use and discontinuation of use, evidence on the strategies used for quitting vaping lags behind. Additionally, there is a dearth of evidence on quit strategies used by adults who are 30 years or older. Although most people who vape belong to the 24-year-old or younger category, those who are older may need support if they choose to quit vaping. Furthermore, research on the factors that facilitate quitting and those that serve as barriers to quitting is lacking in the Middle East. To this end, our research aimed to capture these factors in a sample of people from Qatar with a history of past vaping. Differences between YAs and OAs were noted where applicable.

Methods

Sample

From May to June 2025, we recruited via Facebook ads and Qatar University email lists. Of 75 respondents, the first 13 eligible candidates who consented to participating were interviewed. Eligibility: age 18–60, past regular vaping (vaping once a week at least, every week within a 30-day period), currently abstinent ≥ 30 days, resident of Qatar, and English or Arabic proficiency. Sample size planning considered qualitative saturation guidelines. We characterized YAs as those 18–30 years and OAs as those who are 31 or older in line with the Society for Adolescent Health and Medicine’s general characterization for YAs which extends to 30 years old in some past studies [24].

Data collection

Interested participants completed an online pre-interview survey (10–15 min) for those who consented electronically. Interviews (estimated 30–45 min) were scheduled and conducted online to protect confidentiality given the social stigma of vaping in Qatar. Digital informed consent was obtained prior to interviews; each interview began with an opportunity for participant questions.

Study instrument

The survey and interview questions were used from prior youth and young adult studies [7, 20, 21, 25], then pilot tested in English and translated and tested in Arabic. Pre-interview survey questions included age, gender, employment, monthly income, highest education level, marital status, past vaping (days, episodes, and puff per episode as used in a past study [20]), and smoking status and tobacco related questions (see supplementary file for details). The responses for past vaping were categorized for day was categorized to daily/non-daily for days of use per week, regular (1–16 episodes per day) vs. compulsive (17 or more episodes per day) for episodes per day and moderate (1–9 puffs) and binge (10 or more puffs) for puffs per episode in line with [7]. See the supplementary file for the questionnaire and interview questions with details on questions adopted from past studies and modifications made to some questions. Note that one interview question was adopted from [20] verbatim and two questions were also adopted verbatim from [25] with added examples to the questions to increase participant clarity.

Despite multiple attempts, no female with a history of vaping participated. Interviews asked about supports for quitting, helpful/unhelpful strategies, and triggers for relapse. Interviews were conducted in English or Arabic. The email lists from Qatar University sent reached both male and female participants; it was evident from incomplete pre-interview surveys that were discarded, that all eligible female participants answered “no” to being contacted to set up an interview. For Facebook ads, despite targeting both males and females, only males completed the pre-interview survey and consented to be contacted for an interview.

Approach

The current study used a phenomenological approach where the aim was to understand the strategies used by people who formerly vaped to quit and the relapse triggers they faced along their quit journey. By focusing on the subjective perspectives of the people who quit vaping, the approach provides insight into the process of quitting and relapse triggers. The approach is centered on uncovering the meaning of strategies and triggers from the lived experiences of those who formerly vaped.

Analysis

The analysis was done by the first and second authors of this research collaboratively. ATLAS.ti (version 23.2.1) was used for coding. After reading the interview scripts line by line we coded quotes and grouped them into categories [26]. Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six-step guide was used as a guide for conducing the thematic analysis [27]. We began by familiarizing ourselves with the data to observe the strategies and relapse triggers faced, assigning them to subthemes, then grouped them into themes and reviewed them for logical connectedness. The first and second authors engaged in reflexivity throughout the process of conducting the research—both have public health backgrounds and the first author particularly has expertise in vaping control research and practice. They independently coded quotes, to reduce individual bias, and then reconciled differences after meeting. This ensured that the analysis is grounded in the participants’ experiences.

Trustworthiness

The research process was carefully documented, including the coding procedures, to enhance auditability and transparency. Credibility was supported by the use of rich, verbatim quotations, which ensured that participants’ perspectives were represented accurately. To further strengthen the findings, member checking was conducted with two participants, who reviewed the interpretation of their quotations to confirm that their lived experiences of quitting vaping were faithfully reflected, thereby representing their worldview. These steps, combined with collaborative coding and reconciliation of differences between the first and second authors, contributed to the overall rigor and trustworthiness of the study.

Results

Sample characteristics and saturation

Saturation occurred by the 12th interview; one additional interview confirmed saturation. All 13 participants were males who formerly vaped: five young adults (YAs) and eight older adults (OAs). Most had undergraduate degrees, were married and employed. Participants came primarily from Arab countries outside Qatar; a few were from Southeast Asia, Africa, or Qatar. Smoking histories varied: most were formerly smoked, a few currently smoke, and many had used other tobacco products (hookah, cigars, snus). Patterns of vaping revealed that the majority (76.9%) reported daily use, while a smaller proportion (23.1%) vaped between one and six days per week. Regarding episodes, 69.2% engaged in regular use (1–16 episodes), whereas 30.8% reported compulsive use (17 or more episodes). In terms of intensity, most participants (76.9%) consumed a moderate number of puffs per episode (1–9 puffs), while 23.1% reported high-intensity use (10 or more puffs). Theme 1: social influences inside and outside the workplace

Avoiding social influence (N = 3)

Both YAs and OAs described deliberately avoiding other individuals who vape early in the quit process because initial self-control felt limited: “For the first short period, like the first week or two, I used to avoid sitting with people who vape.” (YA_P9). OAs similarly avoided public places with visible vaping to reduce temptation: “I started avoiding places where there’s a lot of vaping. For example, I used to go to a café where there was vaping; I would not go there anymore, so I would not feel that way.” (OA_P10). The difference is that YAs emphasized avoiding peers and social gatherings where direct offers and pressure occurred, whereas OAs highlighted avoiding public venues and routines where exposure was frequent.

Supportive family and friends (N = 4)

Family encouragement was widely cited by both groups but was particularly influential for OAs, who often described sustained family reminders and living in smoke-free households as catalysts for gradual reduction: “From a family perspective, it was my father, mother, and sisters. No one in the house smoked except me… my father was the one who encouraged me the most to quit.” (YA_P9); “Little by little, with the support of my family… I discovered that I was capable of quitting e-cigarettes, and I gradually decreased.” (OA_P10).

Wife support (N = 3)

Spousal support emerged as a powerful motivator across ages but was emphasized more by OAs. Some YAs credited partners with initiating change: “It was not a personal decision. My wife helped me. She told me that you would harm me before you harm yourself… This motivated me to quit.” (YA_P2). OAs more frequently framed spousal input as the decisive turning point or sustained pressure for behavior change: “The big push was from my wife’s side… That is why I took that decision.” (OA_P3). For several OAs, marriage itself marked a life transition that supported cessation: “…the fact that I got married, I wanted to take a step, change myself…” (OA_P10).

Unsupportive family and friends (N = 2)

Exposure to household or peer vaping normalized use and provoked cravings, but the patterns differed: YAs often described immediate peer pressure and parental modeling that produced acute cravings—“I would go somewhere and sit with my dad, and my dad would take out his e-shisha and use it in front of me, so I craved e-shisha” (YA_P2)—whereas OAs described social gatherings and friend groups as chronic relapse risks that eroded abstinence over time: “…the thing that made me go back was friends or gatherings with them… if you see this one… vaping in front of you… Definitely I will feel vulnerable….” (OA_P3).

Negative work environment (N = 4)

Workplace stress and unsupportive colleagues were prominent relapse risks for both groups, but OAs emphasized this factor more strongly, linking job stress and negative coworkers directly to frequent vaping episodes: “Work and friends at work are an influential factor because the work environment is full of negative people who complain about work….” (OA_P3). YAs acknowledged work-related vulnerability but tended to cite it alongside social stressors: “So if anything, if I will ever be tempted to go back, it would be because of work” (YA_P12).

Ease of use and discreteness at work (N = 2)

The smokeless, odorless nature of vapes facilitated discreet workplace use and frequent consumption: “in the office and all we cannot use the cigarettes… and we can use this vaping in washrooms or anywhere… there will be more tendency to get addicted to the vape….” (YA_P13). OAs framed vaping as a practical workplace alternative to cigarettes, making abstinence more difficult when smoking bans applied: “I used to work in my office, so smoking was prohibited there, so it was the only alternative.” (OA_P7).

Theme 2: health-centered decisions to quit

Health consciousness (N = 12)

Health concerns motivated nearly all participants; OAs more often cited concrete physiological symptoms, abnormal labs, and declines in fitness as decisive reasons to quit: “I discovered that they [vaping products] have many harmful effects, such as chest effects, coughing…. After discovering their harmful effects, I quit… Using e-cigarettes affects the respiratory system.” (OA_P6). OAs recounted fatigue and observable biomarker/vital sign changes : “I discovered I was experiencing increased stomach problems, elevated liver enzymes, ALT, AST, and elevated cortisol levels. I’m exhausted (OA_P7). YAs reported health worries too but frequently emphasized sleep quality and immediate functional improvements after quitting: “So I when I quit…. My cardio is better… I do not get too far out of breath already, like from walking. Or jogging .…” (YA_P12). A YA noted switching to NRT as supportive: “What helped as well is switching to nicotine gums and patches” (YA_P12).

Fear of addiction (N = 3)

Concern about developing or transferring addiction motivated quitting in both groups. OAs often framed vaping as creating a new, entrenched dependence after attempting to quit cigarettes (“I became addicted to vaping. Now I have another problem” (OA_P3)). YAs described very frequent daily use and tolerance development (“When I start the vape, I am very addicted… using the vape for many times, I do not remember actually the count” (YA_P13); “Over time… you increase the amount.” (YA_P9)), underscoring immediacy of dependence among younger users.

Using exercise to quit (N = 3)

Both groups used physical activity to manage cravings and improve mood. YAs described exercise as a practical replacement during busy schedules: “I used other things, which were work and exercise” (YA_P2). OAs emphasized structured exercise routines that restored function and supported long-term abstinence: “I started exercising, running, and walking a lot. ” (OA_P10). Both noted mood benefits: “Sports is the biggest contributing factor… It relieves depression…” (YA_P9); “It helped me a lot” (OA_P10).

Desire to sleep better (N = 2)

Sleep disruption motivated cessation across ages. OAs stressed being awakened by cravings and the resulting daytime fatigue: “I will wake up… I was waking up three times during my sleep sometime… I’m feeling sleepy and dizzy and tired… it was giving me more headache …” (OA_P3). A YA reported severe insomnia as the proximate reason to quit: “I could not sleep at all. So because of that, only I quit that one.” (YA_P13).

Theme 3: intrapersonal factors influencing quitting

Cost savings (N = 5)

High cost and limited availability in Qatar discouraged continued use—especially among YAs, who reported price sensitivity clearly: “The price is very expensive” (YA_P8); “It was the expenses… thinking about money makes quitting easier.” (YA_P12). OAs noted that inaccessibility and premium pricing reduced consumption: “The nice thing about Qatar is that the vape is not accessible… you’re forced to kind of pay a premium for it.” (OA_P4). OAs additionally framed future medical costs resulting from vaping as deterrents: “The main motivation is financial, because the health risks later on will be financially costly…. God forbids you develop high blood pressure or diabetes, which would cost more… Cancer treatment is very expensive…” (OA_P14).

Willpower and self-restraint (N = 6)

Internal conviction and deliberate removal of the device were common strategies across ages. OAs emphasized sustained self-regulation over time: “The conviction of the person who quit vaping… try to leave the vape … at home or somewhere else and not to keep it accessible at all times” (OA_P3). YAs described acute acts of resolve and self-encouragement: “I started to encourage myself. It is the idea that you yourself push yourself away from it.” (YA_P9); “I just threw that vape outside; I didn’t use that vape again” (YA_P13).

Step-down of nicotine intake (N = 2)

Gradual nicotine reduction and leveraging fasting periods supported cessation. A YA described step-down dosing: “I reduced my nicotine level…It was 60%, then 50%, then 30%, then 10%, and then I spent a month at 5%…” (YA_P2). An OA used Ramadan as a structured opportunity to taper: “I took advantage of the Ramadan period… Little by little… I discovered that I was capable of quitting e-cigarettes, and I gradually decreased.” (OA_P10).

Negative emotions (N = 7)

Stress, anxiety, depression and anger were commonly reported relapse triggers across ages. YAs linked academic and short-term stressors to vaping urges: “When I get angry, I go and vape… When you’re sad, or depressed… you vape” (YA_P2); “I get stressed easily, especially during the last two semesters and my final year… it’s a stress factor” (YA_P8). OAs emphasized chronic life and work pressures: “The most likely thing that can make someone turn to vaping is stress” (OA_P3).

Psychological and sensory motives (N = 6)

Cravings, flavor enjoyment, nicotine rush and ritualized cues sustained use and provoked relapse. YAs emphasized the immediate nicotine “boost”: “I have tried all smoking products, but nothing gives you a pure nicotine boost like the e-shisha” (YA_P2). OAs more often described habitual sensory attachments to flavors, vapor clouds and routines: “It was more about having fun… the flavors and the smoke… I used to enjoy the clouds that I used to make” (OA_P4). Some OAs used habit substitution (e.g., changing drinks) to break cue-linked routines “The hardest thing I faced was the habit I usually do while vaping… for example, drinking coffee. I started changing my drinks… so I wouldn’t have that urge (OA_P10).

Discussion

This study identified three interrelated domains—social influences, health-centered decisions, and intrapersonal factors—that shaped vaping cessation among male who previously vaped in Qatar. Social dynamics were central, but their form and weight differed by age group: YAs were more influenced by peer norms, immediate social pressure at gatherings, and sensory/experiential motives, whereas OAs were more influenced by spousal encouragement, workplace stressors, and cumulative health concerns. The strategy of avoiding pro-vaping settings reflected differential patterns: YAs emphasized avoiding peers and social gatherings where direct offers and teasing occurred, while OAs emphasized avoiding public venues and routine exposures that sustained habitual use. Evidence that exposure to people who smoke decreases quitting success and that social norms influence quit attempts supports these distinctions [28, 29]. Family and peer support improved cessation outcomes across ages, yet OAs described more sustained family reminders and smoke-free household dynamics that facilitated gradual reduction, aligning with literature linking social support for adults who smoke and youth who vape to higher quit intentions and success [30–32]. Yet our study shows that the finding applies to OA who vape as well. Spousal involvement frequently acted as a decisive turning point for OAs, while for YAs it sometimes initiated a change; this age-differentiated role of partners aligns with prior work on partner support and smoking cessation outcomes [33, 34]. Our study extends current literature by demonstrating that the same is true for vaping.

Unsupportive home or peer environments undermined abstinence for both groups, but mechanisms varied: YAs reported acute parental modeling that produced immediate cravings, whereas OAs described chronic exposure in friend circles and workplace contexts challenged their abstinence journey over time. These patterns resemble research on parental modelling effects on young individuals and workplace influences on vaping in the past literature [35, 36].

Workplace stress and the use of nicotine as a coping strategy were particularly salient for OAs, who linked job strain and negative coworkers to frequent vaping episodes; YAs referenced workplace-related vulnerability alongside academic and social stressors. Prior studies tying job stress to elevated tobacco use and the use of nicotine-based vaping products in young adults for coping support these findings [25, 37]. Our study add to the literature by showing that OA vaping, like tobacco use, is influenced by the workplace. In congruence with past research, in our study we found that discreteness of vaping—allowing covert use in restricted settings—facilitated frequent consumption and hindered cessation [38, 39] but our study finds discreteness to be a relevant aspect for OA as well.

Health-centered motives were universal but more decisive for OAs who reported concrete symptoms (respiratory problems, fatigue), abnormal biomarkers, and declines in athletic capacity that precipitated quitting. YAs reported health concerns as well but tended to emphasize more immediate functional changes such as sleep quality and improved cardio performance after cessation. These age patterns are consistent with literature showing older adults prioritize cumulative health risks as motivators while younger users often perceive lower long-term risk [39–42]. Fear of developing or transferring addiction appeared in both groups: OAs often framed vaping as a new entrenched dependence after switching from cigarettes, which was also seen in past research [43], whereas YAs described rapid tolerance and very frequent daily use, underscoring more immediate dependence dynamics among younger users. Our study adds to the literature by delineating the nicotine dependence perceptions that differ by age group.

Intrapersonal drivers also diverged by age. Cost sensitivity and limited product availability in Qatar disproportionately motivated YAs, who explicitly cited price and accessibility as primary reasons to quit—consistent with evidence that younger users are more price elastic [44–46]. OAs, while noting cost and accessibility, more often framed financial considerations as future health-care burden deterrents. Both groups relied on willpower and self-restraint, but OAs emphasized sustained self-regulation over longer periods, and YAs described acute decisive acts and substitution strategies. Gradual nicotine step-down and culturally timed opportunities (e.g., Ramadan) were practical aids reported across ages in our study, though clinical guidance for NRT in vaping cessation is emerging [47]. Negative emotions and sensory cues were cross-cutting relapse risks; YAs linked vaping to short-term stressors (academic pressure, immediate mood states) and sensory/experiential reward (flavors, nicotine “boost”), whereas OAs linked relapse more to habitual rituals and chronic life or work stress, a distinction partly supported by prior qualitative and experimental work on age-differentiated motives and reward responses [48–52].

Cautioned implications based on these age-differentiated findings include tailored approaches due to the small sample size in this study: for YAs, policies and interventions that reduce product appeal (pricing, flavor restrictions), which has been echoed by past research [16], strengthen coping skills for acute stressors, given that past research has shown stress to be associated with vaping in YA [53]. For OAs, interventions that foreground health-risk communication, involve spouses and family, provide workplace stress interventions, and offer clinically guided nicotine tapering or NRT may better address habit-based dependence and cumulative harms. Cross-cutting supports—family engagement, exercise programs, sleep-hygiene interventions, and culturally congruent strategies such as leveraging Ramadan—could improve acceptability for both groups. Future research prioritize age-diverse and gender-inclusive longitudinal studies and rigorously evaluate NRT/tapering protocols specific to vaping to inform evidence-based, age-sensitive cessation programs. Interestingly, the respondents in our study did not mention receiving medical support, counselling or cessation, despite studies in other contexts showing that such supports are part of the strategies used by people who have forgone vaping [20, 21]. This calls for further studies to determine reasons behind either not disclosing such supports or understanding whether receiving supports is atypical for those attempting to quit vaping.

Limitations

This study included only male participants, limiting generalizability to women; cultural stigma likely impeded female participation [54]. A past study with individuals who vape showed that in Qatar, social unacceptability perceptions about vaping are stronger than those in Egypt, and females that vape, specifically hold higher social unacceptability perceptions towards vaping [54]. Therefore, it is likely that females did not feel comfortable sharing their experiences about past vaping behaviour in an interview which is potentially due to gender norms that deem addictive product use disproportionally unacceptable for females and interviews are a bit more detailed then surveys, making participation in such format even less likely. Although there were some females that filled out the pre-survey interview, they refused to be contacted to be scheduled to participate in the interview. This suggests that interviews about sensitive topics may be challenging to conduct in light of the gender norms and perceptions of unacceptability towards vaping. Some interviews were conducted in Arabic and required translation, introducing potential subtle meaning shifts despite careful transcription and coding. The small purposive sample and qualitative design preclude predicting long-term cessation outcomes; longitudinal and larger studies are needed to assess durability of strategies and age-related trajectories. Finally, the participants in this study quit for a month or longer so there insights as successful strategies should be interpreted with caution given that relapse is likely within six months of quitting.

Conclusion

Males who previously vaped in Qatar identified social influences, health concerns, and intrapersonal resources as key determinants of cessation and relapse. YAs were particularly influenced by peer norms, sensory enjoyment and price sensitivity, while OAs emphasized health harms, spousal support and workplace stress. Shared facilitators included family support, willpower, exercise, and nicotine tapering; shared barriers were negative emotions and sensory/ritual cues. Findings may support the development of age-targeted cessation programs that combine social-environmental strategies, health-focused education, behavioral substitution, and culturally congruent supports to strengthen quit attempts and reduce relapse. Further research should include female perspectives and longitudinal designs to inform sustained, generalizable interventions.
