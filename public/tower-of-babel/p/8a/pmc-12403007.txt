The rise of diversity terminology in biomedical research
Authors: Brandon L. Kramer, Catherine Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUsing a computationally driven matching technique on curated dictionaries made publicly available, we examine quantitative trends and regression analyses of the use of diversity and population terminology in a sample of nearly two million biomedical abstracts spanning 30 y. We find growth in diversity, sex, gender, life course, and socioeconomic terms as well as national, continental, and subcontinental population labels but a plateau or decline in racial and ethnic terms, beginning in the mid-2000s. We argue that diversity’s use has grown to encompass concepts beyond its historical origins in race-based programs. Despite some critics’ claims regarding the role of diversity in research, we do not find evidence that its use signals retreat from or commitment to equity and inclusion efforts.

Across fields, ranging far and wide, from education to entertainment, diversity is touted as vitally important. Used alongside celebratory language, diversity can refer to legally protected categories like race and sex or to differences in life perspectives and personality traits (1). This range of meanings is the basis of “diversity projects”—programs or plans of action focused on promoting or celebrating ideas of diversity—which have grown over the past four decades (2–7). Attention to the concept of diversity has also grown in biomedical research—a broad area of research focused on identifying and treating disease, illness, and health. Diversity projects in biomedical research may refer to diversifying the research topic, study population,* or the workforce doing the research (8, 9). Successful efforts to diversify in these various ways are often heralded as vital for scientific progress (10–12) and for addressing social problems such as racial disparities in health (13, 14). Not diversifying research is characterized as a moral or ethical failure and cited as a hindrance to confronting health inequities (15–17). Despite these pronouncements, it is unclear what diversity means in biomedical research, specifically to what type of difference the concept may reference. For example, given the history of association between race and diversity, is diversity a shorthand for race or ethnicity? This article unpacks the meaning of diversity and also explores what may be shaping its reference in biomedical research by examining their published abstracts from 1990 to 2020.

As in other fields, diversity can have a range of meaning in biomedical research. The term is polysemic, that is, it has multiple meanings. Diversity may be used as a shorthand to refer to sociopolitical constructs like race or ethnicity as well as stand for the meaning of heterogeneity. Both ideas may be elided into one when diversity signals the variety of a subject population that is ethnically or racially identified (18). Such notions are rooted in the history of civil rights programs like race-based affirmative action, specifically in education and employment law (6, 19–21). For example, in 1978, the Supreme Court banned racial quotas in higher education admissions while it allowed race-conscious policies in the service of creating a diverse student body, before outlawing race as a factor for admissions in 2023 (22). For 45 y, diversity was a legally acceptable goal. Race-based affirmative action policies, which had previously focused on tackling racial inequality, became centered on the educational benefits of a diverse student population and diversity goals that are often abstractly conceived. Sociologists argue that this shift highlights how symbolic celebratory framing has overtaken the challenging work of fighting racism (1, 2, 6, 23).

In biomedical research, the push for diversity became an institutional focus as demand for greater representation of women and racial minorities in clinical research, begun in earnest in the 1970s, resulted in several key developments at the NIH in the 1990s (8). The decade began auspiciously with the start of the Human Genome Project and the Office for Research on Minority Health at the NIH in 1990 (8, 24, 25). Most significantly, Congress passed the NIH Revitalization Act in 1993, which led to the NIH issuing guidelines for an inclusion mandate (26, 27). The mandate required the inclusion of women and “members of minorities and their subpopulations” in all human subject research and their inclusion in Phase III clinical trials in sufficient numbers such that “valid analyses” of differences in intervention effects can be accomplished. Today, scientific investigations such as the NIH’s All of Us project, launched in 2018, join a growing list of biobanking initiatives around the world aimed at recruiting “people of different backgrounds” (28).

What “different backgrounds” means and to which characteristics diversity is supposed to capture have been tied to not only calls for greater racial or ethnic representation but also standardization in data collection related to race and ethnicity. Science studies research on standardization have highlighted the critical role it plays in establishing authority and enabling collaboration and other coordinated action in science (29, 30). Classification systems often work invisibly behind the scenes, helping to create “objects that are able both to travel across borders and maintain some sort of constant identity” (32, p. 16). As such, the standardization of race enables coordinated action across fields (31).

Standardization dynamics are apparent in the move to coordinate racial and ethnic data collection in biomedical research following the NIH inclusion mandate. Throughout the late 1990s, government health agencies, including the NIH and Federal Drug Administration, moved to harmonize data collection on race and ethnicity and to require the use of racial and ethnic categories outlined in the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Directive 15, which states the official standards for classifying federal data on race and ethnicity (32, 33). Thus, researchers seeking grant support from U.S. science and health agencies, including the NIH, have to use the official OMB Directive 15 racial and ethnic categories when describing their human subjects. These racial and ethnic categories (White, Black, Asian American and Pacific Islander, American Indian, and Hispanic or Latino, also found in the U.S. Census) make up what the historian David Hollinger (34) has termed the United States “ethno-racial pentagon.” Despite criticism about the use of these terms in research, social scientists have long documented their prevalence in a wide range of biomedical studies (35, 36), from health outcomes (37) to pharmaceutical development (38, 39) and genome biobanks (25, 40). Thus, given the standardization in collection and reporting of racial and ethnic data and institutional requirements for doing so, the use of racial and ethnic categories outlined in the OMB Directive 15 may have grown alongside diversity terminology in biomedical abstracts.

Despite the standardization of racial and ethnic categories and formal policies that may encourage their use, there is also a growing body of literature that suggests scientists are moving away from what Shim et al. call “typological notions of race to statistical notions of difference among populations” (41, p. 583). For example, in their investigation of racial and ancestry terminology in genetics research spanning seven decades, Byeon et al. (42) state that authors have transitioned away from explicitly racial terms and toward more ancestry and continental labels. Nevertheless, Fujimura and Rajagopalan (43) report that scientists in their study often defaulted to racialized conceptions of geography, human history, or continental origins as they referred to genetic ancestral lines. Their findings suggest biomedical researchers may be more likely to use terms like “genetic ancestry” as well as national and continental terms to reflect the concept of race in lieu of racial and ethnic terms (44).

The use of population-specific terms in genetics research to describe race may speak to the notion that there are continually multiplicitous forms of race and population terminology (45). Panofsky and Bliss argue that geneticists’ blending of racial schemas with geographical terms illustrate the polyvocality of race (46). Genomic scientists can summon the idea of race, with various population terms, capitalizing on the ambiguity of the latter. Although scientific research may demand specificity, Panofsky and Bliss argue that ambiguity around population definitions enables geneticists to collaborate with other scientists, challenge bureaucratic oversight of their work, and build accountability with study populations, all of which help them to claim authority across scientific fields (46). The ambiguity of population and polyvocality of race allows for population terminology such as national, continental, and directional labels to evoke racialized meaning without explicit use of racial or ethnic terms. Thus, diversity may refer to racialized meaning that appears alongside national, continental, and directional labels.

By focusing on the meaning and use of diversity as well as which terms or concepts get mentioned or disregarded, this investigation can help illuminate how biomedical researchers are conceptualizing group differences. Understanding diversity’s meaning and use can also provide insight into whether diversity projects in science and medicine can address health inequities, most critically, racial health inequities (47, 48). Many scholars now identify structural racism as a fundamental cause of health inequities (49–51). In response, some health activists and investigators have advocated for both greater diversity of the individuals conducting the research as well as the individuals whose bodies are included in the studies addressing inequities (52–54). Large, nationally and internationally funded research initiatives have focused on the concept of diversity as both the means and goal of doing good science, which will yield better health outcomes for more people, particularly those who have been previously overlooked by researchers (10, 55). However, these projects often cost hundreds of millions of dollars of public funding and may not yield the kinds of results to make a difference in tackling disparities rooted in larger structural inequities (56, 57). This suggests that there may be potential costs to ignoring race-based programs focused explicitly on reducing racial inequities in favor of diversity projects. Thus, understanding how researchers conceptualize diversity in relation to race and ethnicity is vitally important for the field of biomedicine and beyond (58).

To investigate how researchers refer to diversity in biomedical research, we draw from methods in the field of computational social science. Over the past decade, social scientists have employed novel computational methods to explore cultural trends and stereotypes in large-scale text corpora. Supervised text mining is the most straight-forward application of computer-assisted analysis, allowing researchers to examine trends in word frequencies and conduct hypothesis testing with a predefined dictionary of relevant terms (59). More recently, Garg et al. rely on unsupervised computational methods to examine historical shifts in how racial, ethnic, and gender stereotypes change relative to common occupational terms (60). Charlesworth et al. show these trends are relatively stable across text sources, including adult and children books, television, and movies scripts (61). More recently, Kozlowski et al. draw on word embeddings to uncover the ways that textual dimensions of social class change relative to large-scale economic and industrial transformations (62).

Building on this emerging literature, we leverage a computationally driven matching technique to identify quantitative patterns in how investigators use the concept of diversity across biomedical research. We evaluate a sample of nearly 2 million biomedical abstracts from 1990 to 2020 to examine the overall growth of diversity, populations, and terminology often associated with those concepts. Given the history of the concept and its polysemic quality, we do not begin with an a priori definition of diversity. Instead, we start by examining the rise of diversity terminology in biomedical research, matching words that arise in our corpus to a curated dictionary of diversity-related terminology, finding that most of these terms have grown over time. We then explore whether the standardization of racial and ethnic data in the field has led to the rise of racial or ethnic terms or other population terminology.

The standardization of racial categories, enabled in part by OMB Directive 15, creates a unique opportunity for computational analysis. Because these categories represent an official, bounded vocabulary for describing race and ethnicity in federally funded research, we can systematically track their usage alongside alternative terminologies. Our nested dictionary approach, explained in greater detail below, allows us to capture not only direct usage of OMB terms, but also the geographic and ancestry-based alternatives that may be replacing them. This methodological strategy enables us to test empirically whether the shift away from typological notions of race that scholars have described is occurring at scale across biomedical research.

Computational methods are essential for this investigation, because the shifts in racial and ethnic terminology we hypothesize may be subtle, gradual, and unevenly distributed across biomedical subfields. Manual content analysis, while valuable for understanding context, cannot capture patterns across the massive scale of biomedical publishing necessary to detect field-wide trends. Moreover, the polysemic nature of diversity—and the strategic ambiguity that may characterize contemporary racial discourse—requires systematic analysis of term co-occurrences and contextual usage that computational approaches can provide.

We show how the use of racial and ethnic terms has stagnated and, in some cases, has even declined since 2005 while national, continental, and subcontinental-specific terms have shown pronounced growth over the same period. We argue that these divergent trends reveal researchers are shifting from the practice of using race and ethnicity to delineate populations to relying more on geographic labeling strategies to do the same work. To understand what may be fueling the rise in diversity-related terminology, we present a series of logistic regression models, examining the effects of the various terms, year of publication, and journal subdisciplines in biomedical research. We show that the co-occurrence of most terms, apart from sex/gender, are associated with a greater likelihood of diversity being included in an abstract. In contrast, sex/gender terms are more likely to be found in abstracts with equity/inclusion terms.† We argue that the use of diversity has grown to encompass concepts beyond its historical origins in race-based programs as it has in fields like higher education and employment. Our findings suggest that this development is not necessarily associated with a move away from equity or inclusion, nor is it indicative of researchers’ goal of addressing health inequities through diversity.

Although our study of the use of diversity in biomedical research may provide crucial insight into how the overall field of biomedicine is tending to these related issues of difference, representation, or equity, we recognize that the field of biomedicine extends beyond biomedical research. Thus, our findings cannot address whether the conceptualization and use of diversity we found operate the same across other areas of biomedicine, such as healthcare delivery, or fields beyond it, such as education. Nevertheless, we root our inquiry in broad and interconnected field developments in biomedicine to ask our empirical and theoretical questions. In doing so, our investigation may help guide future inquiry. Furthermore, we provide publicly available code, datasets, and curated dictionaries to assist such efforts.

Results

The Growth of Diversity Terminology.

Given the significance of diversity in fields in and outside of biomedicine (2, 3, 5, 6) and the growth of policies promoting greater representation of women, children, and racial or ethnic minorities in clinical research (8, 24, 33), we expect to see diversity-related terminology increase in biomedical research publications. We hypothesize that diversity-related terminology has been rising in biomedical research over the past 30 y (H1) and deploy a computationally driven matching technique to examine the frequency and proportion of term usage in each of the 12 categories ‡ associated with diversity from 1990 to 2020 (Table 1). Fig. 1 A and B illustrate a dramatic growth of diversity-related terminology over the three decades. More specifically, we find that life course, which refers to stages of aging like adolescence and adulthood, as well as sex/gender terms, increased six to seven-fold, demonstrating a more pronounced gain relative to the other categories of terms. SI Appendix, Table S2 shows that life course and sex/gender terminology are the most frequently referenced terms overall and regularly surface alongside terms such as patients, risk, disease, treatment, and therapy, suggesting their importance in designating study populations within clinical trials. We include life course in our analysis to reflect its significance in health outcomes study as well as NIH and FDA guidelines related to research of individuals across the life span. The frequent use of age and sex/gender comes as little surprise, as the NIH inclusion mandate strongly incentivized clarity around this language in addition to the eventual inclusion of women, children, and the elderly in clinical trials (8).

Fig. 1B shows the log-transformed proportional change in categorical usage over time. These plots show more clearly that terms from the sex/gender, life course, socioeconomic, diversity (all), cultural, equity/justice, and diversity (social) categories have risen notably over time. In contrast, terms in the race/ethnicity, sexuality, disability, and ancestry categories have either stayed flat or grown very little over the past 15 y. Most importantly, the percentage of abstracts with terms in the race/ethnicity category nearly doubled from 1990 to 2000 before stagnating after 2005. Although our analyses do not speak definitively to which cultural, political, or economic factors may have contributed to these effects, it is worth noting that the initial period of growth roughly coincides with the first decade of the NIH inclusion mandate and related diversity initiatives while the plateau effect follows the completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003. Below, we discuss how the latter may have enabled researchers to move away from race-based terminology in favor of geographic labeling practices.

Racial/Ethnic Labels vs. Geographic Population Labeling.

The standardization of racial schemas in general, and the application of racial and ethnic categories outlined in the OMB Directive 15 across multiple domains in particular, suggest that we might see an increased incidence of these terms in biomedical research (8, 29, 32, 33). We hypothesize that institutional requirements regarding racial and ethnic data collection and reporting have led to greater prevalence of U.S.-based racial and ethnic terms found in the OMB Directive 15 and the U.S. Census (H2). To analyze trends in their usage, we drew from our curated dictionary of OMB/Census terms to analyze how often researchers referred to these concepts across our study period (Table 1).

Fig. 2 shows that OMB/Census terms increased in the first decade of our analysis, but this growth is smaller than the increase of the social diversity category in H1. The most prominent increases appear in the race/ethnicity category (i.e., terms such as race, racial, or ethnic), which rose 11-to-18-fold, while Black and White went up 3-to-5-fold. However, when we look at the trends as proportions in Fig. 2, we find that the race/ethnicity terms stagnated while the Black and White terms declined after 2005. Looking at the remaining categories, we see that only Asian and social diversity (i.e., the use of diverse category terms alongside other categorical terms from the H1 dictionary) grew as proportions over time while Hispanic/Latinx, Native American, and Pacific Islander terms evinced no change after 2005. Overall, these results lead us to weakly reject H2. We see growth in the OMB/Census terms over time, but most of the categories remained stagnant or, in some cases, even declined after 2005.

Longitudinal trends in the proportional use of OMB/Census and race-related terms in scientific abstracts, 1990–2020. This figure presents the proportion of PubMed abstracts containing terms from official OMB/Census categories (Black, White, Hispanic/Latinx, Asian, Native American, Pacific Islander), broader race/ethnicity terms (e.g., race, racial, or ethnic), and social diversity terms (defined as uses of diversity that co-occur with other social category terms; see Materials and Methods). Race/ethnicity terms rose sharply through the early 2000s but plateaued after 2005 and declined thereafter. Among the OMB/Census terms, only Asian and social diversity continued to grow proportionally in the later years of the study period while other categories remained flat or declined.

Although the decline of racial and ethnic terminology was contrary to our expectations in H1 and H2, it is not necessarily at odds with recent science studies literature, which report researchers using geographic and genetic ancestry terms more often than race and ethnicity (43, 46). Building on this finding, we hypothesize that these population labels have increased (H3). To test how often population labels are deployed, we aggregated a dictionary of over 6,600 terms clustered into continental, subcontinental, national, subnational, directional, OMB/Census, and racial/ethnic categories.

In Fig. 3, growth trends demonstrate that the prevalence of population terminology has grown tremendously since 1990 (H3). In the top trend line, we observe that when the population labels are combined together, the growth trends increase by more than 11-fold over our analysis period. The majority of this development is tied to national terms being used more often, rising nearly 17-fold (from around 5% of abstracts to more than 19% of abstracts). The next largest growth trend is in continental terms, swelling 14-fold from 2% to around 6% of all abstracts. We also find expansion in subnational, subcontinental, and directional terminology. Although each of these categories roughly doubled in usage, they never exceeded 6% of total abstracts in a given year. As we find above, the incidence of OMB/Census and race/ethnicity terms plateaued around 2005 and declined thereafter. Similarly, the category with directional terms declined in the early portion of our analysis period before returning to its baseline of around 5% of all abstracts in 2020.

Reasons for Rise in Diversity Terminology.

To investigate potential reasons for the rise in diversity terminology, we ran a series of logistic regression (Table 3). We use logistic regression in Models 1 to 4 to examine the influence of race/ethnicity, sex/gender, and geographic terms on the likelihood of diversity appearing in biomedical abstracts. In Model 1, we observe that the inclusion of race/ethnicity and socioeconomic terms, having received funding from a U.S. governmental organization, and being published in genomics journals increase the likelihood of diversity occurring in biomedical abstracts by as much as 5 to 7% in their combined effect. Somewhat surprisingly, the inclusion of OMB/Census terms and being published in public health journals are associated with either nonsignificant or a weak likelihood of diversity being present. Model 2 shows that the inclusion of sex/gender terms is associated with a decreased likelihood that diversity is referenced in the abstract. In Model 3, we add continental, subcontinental, national, subnational, and directional terms and find that all but national terms have a strong, positive effect on the likelihood of diversity turning up in biomedical abstracts. Given the audience reach that PNAS has, we include PNAS as a variable in Model 4. This model indicates that being published in PNAS also strongly increases the likelihood that diversity is mentioned in our sampled abstracts. Taken together, we find biomedical abstracts with race/ethnicity terms, especially when the studies have U.S. government funding or are published in PNAS or genomics journals, are associated with an increase in the likelihood that diversity is present in an abstract by as much as a 7 to 10% combined effect.

Given the surprising findings that mentions of sex/gender terms or publication in public health journals are not associated with a higher likelihood of diversity’s usage, we decided to complement our analyses with an additional model (Model 5) that examines the use of equity/inclusion as the outcome variable. Model 5 shows that the inclusion of racial/ethnic and socioeconomic terms and being published in public health journals all significantly and robustly increase the likelihood of equity/inclusion appearing in biomedical abstracts. Diversity, sex/gender, OMB/Census, sub/national, and sub/continental terms also are associated with a significant, though a more modest, positive likelihood of equity/inclusion occurring in the text. In contrast, directional terms, having received funding from a U.S. governmental organization, and being published in PNAS or genomics journals are all associated with either a negative or nonsignificant likelihood of equity/inclusion being present in biomedical abstracts.

Discussion

Diversity is celebrated in science, heralded in the pathbreaking findings that are generated by diverse researchers or diverse subjects. However, the push for diversity is not without costs, which includes, for example, a federal allocation of $1.5 billion to fund the All of Us research program. In higher education and employment, researchers argue that symbolic celebration of diversity is edging out the more sobering yet necessary work of addressing social, particularly racial, inequities (2, 6, 23). This development in higher education, for example, has been one reason for questioning the value of diversity projects in biomedicine as a solution for addressing racial disparities in health. Thus, it is critical that we understand what diversity means and how it is used in biomedical research.

Using a computationally driven matching technique that links abstract content to curated dictionaries of relevant terms, we examined the prevalence of diversity and population terminology in nearly two million biomedical abstracts published between 1990 and 2020. Our results, which demonstrate notable growth in diversity across biomedical research, mirror many of the changes that have occurred in higher education and employment. Diversity’s usage has grown substantially and is not necessarily equivalent with social representation or inclusion of underrepresented groups. Our findings indicate that diversity is increasingly referenced in biomedical research publications regardless of whether its usage connotes the general meaning of heterogeneity or refers specifically to social representation and inclusion for diverse human groups, what we call social diversity. Although social diversity’s frequency grew the most of all categories of terms we examined, it nonetheless accounted for only 10% of all diversity-related occurrences in 2020. Our findings highlight the fact that researchers investigating diversity terminology must work to understand the polysemic nature of diversity across the various contexts in which it is used, preferably through a combination of quantitative, qualitative, and computational strategies. Our publicly available resources and open-source software aim to help future research in this area.

As it has in higher education and employment, diversity has broadened its association beyond race/ethnicity, most significantly to include sex/gender and life course terms. These results suggest that science policies such as the NIH inclusion mandate might have played a role in shifting the growth of certain term usage, especially age and sex/gender, in addition to race/ethnicity. While sex/gender and life course terms continued to rise over the study period, race/ethnicity terms began to stagnate in 2005. Furthermore, despite the rise of standardization in the collection and reporting of racial and ethnic data, we found that racial and ethnic terms derived from the OMB Directive 15 mostly declined after 2005, contradicting our initial hypothesis and standing in contrast to predictions of a rise in racial terminology made early in the postgenomic era (63). Contrary to our expectations, we do not observe evidence of racial terminology being used more often in biomedical research since the unveiling of the human genome in 2003. Of course, the shift away from explicitly racial language does not mean that discourses are not racialized in more discrete or veiled forms (64, 65), as numerous ethnographic and other qualitative accounts of scientific research have shown (24, 44, 66, 67).

Like many recent science studies literature (42, 43, 46, 68), we see evidence that biomedical researchers are turning more toward population terminology that relies on national, continental, and directional labeling practices. Our work expands upon prior findings by documenting these trends over a longer time period with a larger, more varied sample. Moreover, we note that this shift began directly following the completion of the Human Genome Project in 2003. This trend may be biomedical researchers’ response to calls that shortly followed in which science journals and working groups recommended that scientists extricate race from genetic studies and their publications (69–71).

These results could be interpreted as evidence that diversity rhetoric may be replacing race-conscious discussions as scholars of higher education have also observed (2, 6, 23). However, our logistic regression analysis results suggest a more complex picture in biomedical research. Diversity is more likely to be mentioned alongside race/ethnicity, especially when the research is supported by funding from a U.S. governmental organization or is published in PNAS or a genomics journal. This observation is not surprising given the overall growth of diversity beyond biomedicine. Our analysis of what terms are associated with equity adds nuance to the role diversity plays. We found the inclusion of diversity, sex/gender, OMB/Census, sub/national, and sub/continental terms is associated with a significant, yet modest, positive likelihood of equity/inclusion appearing in biomedical abstracts. We believe that these findings lend some support to the view that diversity in biomedical research can be directed at addressing health inequities as some scholars have argued (13–15). However, the inclusion of directional terms, receiving U.S. governmental funding, and being published in PNAS or genomics journals all have either a negative or nonsignificant effect on the likelihood of equity/inclusion being mentioned in biomedical abstracts. These results do not prove that large government-funded projects designed to enroll previously underrepresented groups in clinical research and to address health inequities are failing to tackle such inequities. Nevertheless, our results provide quantitative evidence to support recent calls for genomic studies and other biomedical data collection projects to better address issues of equity (16, 17, 57). Considered in sum, our study helps demonstrate the point that the rise of diversity in biomedical research may not be, in and of itself, a reason for celebration nor fear.

Although our study was able to show the use of and relationship between terms related to diversity using novel methods across a broad range of biomedical research domains, there are important limitations to our investigation. First, our dataset was limited to scientific abstracts rather than the full content of scientific publications. Although we would have preferred to analyze the full content of these papers, doing so would require considerably more resources to manually download publications, extract and store their content, and examine trends on an exponentially larger sample of textual data. We are confident that the trends revealed in our analysis of abstracts would also be found in full-text publications. Second, because we did not conduct systematic content analysis of a subset of the articles, we are unable to provide a qualitative account of the context and application of diversity and other related terms. Additional qualitative research such as interviews with study authors could help illuminate the ways in which scientists conceptualize diversity to represent ideas ranging from heterogeneity to identity, justice, and equity. Third, we were not able to systematically control for more expansive international collaboration tendencies in biomedical research over the course of our study period. Although we suspect that having researchers from more geographically varied areas would increase diversity and population-specific terminology, we did not have clear and consistent author affiliation data. Even so, such data would fail to account for authors’ personal backgrounds, variation in past and current coauthors, or the demographics of the study populations that researchers are investigating. Fourth, following the work of Fox and colleagues (72), we looked at a range of biomedical journals that published research from disciplines as varied as cell biology and epidemiology. Nevertheless, future studies could examine the different disciplinary backgrounds of the journals, using content analysis as the method to enrich contextual understandings of diversity in biomedical research. Our study does show that genomics research is associated with a robust likelihood of the use of diversity terminology while public health research is less likely to reference these terms. We expect similar variance to exist between journal topics when studied in more detail. Finally, our investigation was limited to biomedical research and not other areas of biomedicine. Although diversity may operate differently, we expect the institutional field changes we document have been similarly important for biomedical areas such as healthcare, drug development, and health policy (8).

Despite these limitations, our investigation makes important contributions that can be expanded upon by future studies. Our dataset, code, and curated dictionaries of diversity and population-related terms are all publicly available, enabling researchers to explore additional questions about diversity. Thus, our methodological approach and empirical findings can provide avenues for investigating the use and meaning of diversity in other fields. The current study deepens our knowledge, offering empirical insight into diversity’s meaning and use over a crucial three-decade period. For biomedical scientists, health equity advocates, research funding agencies, and scholars of diversity, continued critical investigation of what diversity means is necessary for understanding the many hopes and expectations we have attached to the concept.

Materials and Methods

A key motivation for our methodology was to foster open innovation in diversity-related social science research by using public data sources and reproducible software code. In addition to using PubMed/MEDLINE data, our computational matching technique relies on several nested dictionaries that help researchers identify a) the growth of the term diversity and its metonyms, including cultural, disability, diversity, equity/justice, life course, migration, minority, race/ethnicity, sex/gender, and sexuality; b) terms related to race/ethnicity as derived from the U.S. OMB Directive 15 and U.S. Census categories; and c) continental, subcontinental, national, and directional terms that relate to population classifications. Although future researchers will undoubtedly identify shortcomings of using these dictionaries, these resources support several practical use cases. Our approach leverages these dictionaries to measure raw and proportional changes in term usage over time, but future studies may, for example, employ these dictionaries for sampling techniques (e.g., filtering large samples of social media by specific domains) or understanding relationships in and across text corpora (e.g., co-occurrences of diversity and population terms). While the following sections provide brief overviews of each dictionary, the full collection of terms, the resources and logic used for their construction, and full access to our software code can be found on our supplementary website (https://riseofdiversity.netlify.app/), accompanying GitHub repository (https://github.com/brandonleekramer/diversity), and an open-source software package (https://github.com/brandonleekramer/diverstidy) we hope will help bootstrap researchers’ efforts to innovate on this project.

PubMed Dataset.

The dataset used in this paper derives from the PubMed/MEDLINE database—a free and publicly available collection of scientific abstracts provided by the U.S. National Library of Medicine. PubMed contains metadata of more than 27 million publications, including author, abstract, and publication source information for articles published between 1990 and 2020. To download the data, we relied on an open-source Python package named PubMedPortable to automate the extraction of metadata from PubMed’s source files into a PostgreSQL relational database (73). We filtered this dataset to contain only 250 highly ranked biomedical journals based on Elsevier’s 2019 CiteScore rankings (74), reducing the dataset to 2,510,266 distinct abstracts. Next, we developed two binary indicator variables that detected “animal” and “human” abstracts based on matching 1,124 keywords from a curated dictionary. More specifically, abstracts that reference terms like man, woman, participants, and patients, among others, were deemed human and retained in our final sample of 1,929,467 entries while abstracts that mention terms related to animals, insects, plants, astronomy, and geological research without any indication of human terminology were removed from our final sample.

Hypothesis 1: Diversity Dictionary and Analysis.

To test Hypotheses 1–3, we developed a computationally driven matching technique to measure the frequency of diversity-related terms in biomedical abstracts. This was done using a supervised text mining approach that relies on nested dictionaries—a strategy that entailed curating three dictionaries with a set of terms that are allocated into several categories (Table 1). Our main objective was to count all relevant terms in our dataset based on whether they match the terms in these dictionaries. The terms can be counted independently (i.e. the number of times the word sex is referenced) or can be grouped into categories like those discussed below (i.e. the number of times sex, gender, and all associated terms in the sex/gender category are mentioned). Although Table 1 provides an example of select terms included in our dictionaries, our supplementary website provides visualizations and interactive tables for exploring these terms in greater detail.

The H1 dictionary contains 460 total terms that fall into 12 diversity-related categories: ancestry, cultural, disability, diversity (all), diversity (social), equity/justice, life course, migration, minority, race/ethnicity, sex/gender, sexuality, and social class. For each category, we compiled the terms based on our empirical and theoretical knowledge of research fields related to diversity in biomedicine, higher education, and employment. In the case of sex/gender and sexuality terms, we referenced the Oxford Dictionary of Gender Studies (75), for racial/ethnic terms we rely on the OMB Directive 15 and U.S. Census categories, and for the disability category we leaned on the National Center on Disability and Justice’s Style Guide (76) to inform term inclusion. We conducted extensive sensitivity analyses of each category, which revealed that the inclusion of some terms yielded a substantial number of false-positive results. For example, when we included “blind” in the disability category, we found that the majority of results related to “double-blind” studies, which are not indicative of the meaning of diversity we are interested in evaluating. We dealt with false-positive results largely by removing terms that were too general to provide precise results or by reclassifying certain word combinations during our preprocessing stage to minimize inflated count totals. This strategy proved effective in generating accurate results, which we validated by manually spot-checking a random sample of abstracts to ensure that we captured valid occurrences of the terms.

The social diversity category was critical to the validity of our findings since diversity is polysemous (i.e., it has multiple meanings). To estimate cases in which the term diversity is referring to minority representation rather than heterogeneity more generally, we iteratively developed a reproducible computational strategy to differentiate these two meanings of diversity (SI Appendix). This algorithm classifies all references of social diversity as those that a) co-occur in the same sentence as terms from any of the other H1 categories, b) co-occur along common group labels (e.g. cohort, participant, etc.), and c) do not co-occur alongside other common word sequences known to be attached to diversity’s meaning of heterogeneity. To validate this approach, we extracted 1,000 abstracts from each side of the social diversity and general diversity samples to manually verify their accuracy. This validation step yielded high scores across our performance metrics (Tables 2 and 4), including an F1 Score of 84.8%. Although this procedure may lead to some false positives in the classification of social diversity, the performance metrics surpass the 80% threshold for what most statisticians consider a quality classification model.

Hypothesis 2: Racial/Ethnic Dictionary and Analysis.

To test H2, we constructed a second dictionary that contains 57 terms allotted into 8 categories: Asian, Black/African American, ethnicity, Hispanic/Latinx, Native American, Pacific Islander, race, and White/Caucasian. These terms derive from the 1997 Office of Management and Budget (OMB) Directive 15 categories discussed above and are also crucial to the race and ethnicity questions in the U.S. Census. Although this resource required less curation than the other two dictionaries, we took two extra steps to ensure its validity. As we state above, all compound terms (e.g., Native American, African American) were converted into single tokens in our counting process to minimize count inflation. Second, we took extra precautions to validate that the terms Black and White were not generating false-positive results, especially on common biomedical phrases like “white blood cell.”

Hypothesis 3: Populations Dictionary and Analysis.

To test H3, we used a third dictionary of 6,679 population labels, including continental, subcontinental, national, subnational, directional, OMB/Census, and race/ethnicity categories. Our main objective was not only to determine whether population terms, as a whole, are increasing over time, but to better understand which categories are most likely to be employed in scientific abstracts. Tracking changes in these categories helps us to understand how scientists’ efforts to stratify human populations through different sets of labeling practices have varied over time. These dictionaries were not designed to be an exhaustive list of concepts that could be included in each category. Instead, we intend these to be publicly available resources that researchers can adapt for future studies based on their project objectives.

The H3 dictionary was the most complicated dictionary construction, which involved an abductive data collection approach using the public knowledge repository Wikipedia as our primary source. Although Wikipedia is not without its faults, the site has long been among the top-10 most frequented websites on the internet; is an active historical document crowdsourced by millions of online users; is subject to continuous peer review and editing that includes an accessible editing history; and, in some cases, may be more comprehensive than any single national governing apparatus, which may exclude the representation of select or historically marginalized populations.

To generate the H3 nested dictionary, we started by collecting all of the proper, adjectival, and demonymic forms of continental, subcontinental, and national terms from Wikipedia. Next, following Panofsky and Bliss (46), we also collected terminology related to cardinal directions, terms from the OMB Directive 15 and United States Census categories, as well as terms related to (genetic) ancestry, race, and ethnicity. As a point of clarification, racial and ethnic terms refer simply to variations of race and ethnicity and not terms like Asian, Black, or White. When applicable, those terms fall under the OMB/US Census category.

In what proved to be the most complicated aspect of this curation process, we decided to collect all of the ethnic, tribal, and caste terms we could find from around the world and classify them under the umbrella of the subnational category. This process began with a catalog of all indigenous peoples from around the world—a directory that was separated by continent. The point of this exercise was not to classify populations into hierarchical categories. In fact, we explicitly chose to create the umbrella term subnational to circumvent further contributing to the violent history of hierarchical classification into races, classes, or castes. Instead, this process was meant to compile the largest, most inclusive list of population terms we could logically manage, knowing that this list will likely never be exhaustive, because the enactment of population differences depends on ambiguity, polyvocality, and dynamism.

After constructing the three dictionaries, we used the tidyverse and tidytext packages written in the R programming language to conduct several preprocessing steps that standardized our text data (77, 78), including converting text to lower case, removing special characters and numbers, recoding select compound and hyphenated terms, and then classifying each term into the H1, H2, and H3 categories. Using this procedure, we report the total proportion of articles in which these terms are used. This proportion was calculated by using a binary indicator based on whether a term is discussed in the article, taking the sum of articles that include those terms, and dividing it by the total number of articles in each year across our sample.

After testing our three hypotheses, we conducted logistic regression analyses to investigate what may be driving a rise in diversity terminology. We examined the effects of the race/ethnicity, sex/gender, and geographic terms on the likelihood of diversity being included in biomedical abstracts. We also looked at the effect of OMB/Census racial and ethnic categories and having U.S. government science funding to examine whether the NIH inclusion mandate and related racial and ethnic data collection harmonization efforts have been associated with rising diversity terminology. Finally, we considered journal discipline, including the construction of binary indicators for genomics and public health research more specifically, as well as whether the abstract was published in PNAS. The inclusion of PNAS was to inform potential policy implications on biomedical research on diversity published in this journal.
