Common electrophysiological signatures of relative magnitude in both space and time
Authors: Estelle Mieulet, Kamila Smigasiewicz, Inga Korolczuk, Laurence Casini, Franck Vidal, Jennifer T Coull
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuration processing can be indexed by the amplitude of two electrophysiological potentials, the contingent negative variation (CNV) and the late positive component (LPC). When a currently elapsing event becomes longer than that of a memorized standard, CNV amplitude begins to resolve back to baseline even if the event is not yet over, and LPC amplitude, evoked by event offset, is reduced. Using electroencephalography in 30 human participants, we examined whether these patterns were unique to explicit duration judgements or might also be observed during spatial judgements. Two dynamic visual stimuli were presented consecutively on each trial. The relative duration (temporal task) or distance (spatial task) of the second stimulus was compared to the first. Confirming prior findings, CNV dynamics and LPC amplitude reflected the relative duration of the two stimuli. However, this occurred only when duration was task-relevant during the temporal task, not when it was irrelevant during the spatial task. Moreover, CNV and LPC activity indexed relative distance only when distance was task-relevant in the spatial (not temporal) task. Together these results suggest that CNV and LPC dynamics reflect domain-general mechanisms for processing the magnitude of task-relevant stimulus features, whether in the temporal (duration) or spatial (distance) dimension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-23391-3.Keywords: Time perception, Timing, Magnitude, Decision-making, P300, SMASubject terms: Decision, Perception

Introduction

Several electrophysiological markers of time perception have been identified. Prime among these is one event-related potential (ERP) in particular, the contingent negative variation (CNV). The CNV is characterized as a slow, negative-going component with a fronto-central distribution1, initially thought to reflect attention, arousal, expectancy or motor preparation1,2. However, subsequent research revealed a link between CNV amplitude and the perception of time. During presentation of a stimulus whose duration must be compared to that of a previously memorized standard, the CNV gets steadily more negative as stimulus duration increases, indicating a link between CNV amplitude and stimulus duration3–6. However, if the duration of the stimulus is longer than that of the memorized standard, the CNV begins to resolve back to baseline before the stimulus has even terminated. The moment at which the CNV begins to resolve corresponds to the duration of the memorized standard7–10 or, at least, to the time at which a temporal decision can be made11–14. Even when temporal judgements are not explicitly required by task instructions CNV dynamics still convey information about durations that have been learned implicitly. For instance, in oddball paradigms, stimuli most often appear after the same fixed interval, apart from occasional “oddballs” that appear after shorter or longer intervals. Participants implicitly learn to expect the stimulus to appear after the most probable interval and CNV dynamics reflect this memorised temporal expectation15,16 or any deviation from it17.

One remaining question is whether the specific time at which the CNV begins to resolve reflects a process specific to duration judgements, or whether it might reflect the time at which judgements about other forms of magnitude, such as size or number, could be made. One way of addressing this issue is to compare patterns of electrophysiological activity in tasks for which the task goal is to compare either temporal (e.g. duration) or non-temporal (e.g. size) features of exactly the same stimuli. If the CNV resolves prior to stimulus offset selectively in the timing task, the moment at which it resolves would reflect comparison of the currently elapsing task-relevant stimulus duration to a memorized (or expected) standard. By contrast, if the CNV also resolves early in the non-temporal task then the moment at which it resolves could reflect processing of this non-temporal feature. It has already been shown that CNV amplitude is higher when participants make decisions about the duration rather than the number18, spatial extent19 or colour20 of visual stimuli. However, it is not yet known whether the time at which the CNV resolves represents a similarly task-selective signature of temporal decision-making.

We aimed to shed light on this question by comparing electroencephalographic (EEG) dynamics during visual discrimination of temporal versus spatial stimulus features. Participants estimated either the duration or spatial extent of an initial (Sample) stimulus and compared it to that of a subsequent (Probe) stimulus. Since CNV dynamics have long been known to vary as a function of motor preparation1, we used a perceptual discrimination, rather than motor reproduction19, paradigm. As a further safeguard against motor preparation confounds, we varied the button-press associated with a particular decision (shorter/equal/longer) on a trial by- trial basis so that participants could not begin to prepare the appropriate response effector until after the Probe stimulus had disappeared. Finally, we aimed to equate the cognitive demands of the spatial task to those of the temporal task21–23, by using stimuli for which either the initial moment in time, or location in space, had to be retained and updated in memory in order to make accurate decisions. Specifically, participants compared the relative duration (temporal task) or distance (spatial task) of the trajectory of a dot that moved across the computer screen24,25. A prior functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) study using these stimuli revealed selective activation of Supplementary Motor Area (SMA) for duration versus distance judgements, with SMA activity increasing as a function of stimulus duration24. In the current study, we harnessed the high temporal resolution of EEG to examine how neural dynamics fluctuate throughout the elapsing duration. We used exactly the same stimuli in both temporal and spatial tasks to be able to directly compare how activity evolved as participants processed either the duration or distance of our dynamic stimuli.

The CNV indexes duration-related activity during stimulus presentation. However, information about stimulus duration is also conveyed in a late positive component (LPC) of EEG activity26–29, which is evoked around 300ms after the stimulus ends. It is akin to either the P300/P3b, strongly evoked by unexpected events30,31 and related to decision-making32, or to the CPP (centro-parietal positivity), thought to reflect the accumulation of evidence during decision-making33. In timing studies, the amplitude of the LPC is lower for stimuli whose duration was objectively, or even subjectively, longer than that of a memorized standard5,20,34,35. Lower LPC amplitude could reflect a lower degree of surprise for longer versus shorter durations: the longer we wait for an event the more likely it is to appear, meaning that events appearing later than expected (or which have a longer duration than that memorised) are less surprising than those appearing earlier (or which have a shorter duration). Amplitude differences in potentials evoked by the offset of stimuli whose durations are shorter or longer than a standard therefore provide an electrophysiological signature of relative duration12,34. We extend and refine recent research20,34 by directly comparing electrophysiological activity during estimation of relative duration versus relative distance of identical visual stimuli. Importantly, both tasks required a judgement about the relative magnitude of stimulus features (i.e. a “prothetic” judgement) with the task-relevant magnitude being either temporal (duration) or spatial (distance).

We hypothesize that during presentation of the Probe stimulus CNV amplitude should resolve back to baseline as soon as the duration of the memorized Sample stimulus has been reached and that the amplitude of the LPC evoked by stimulus offset will vary inversely with relative stimulus duration. Concretely, when Probe duration is longer than Sample duration the CNV should resolve early, prior to stimulus offset7,8,12,14, and LPC amplitude should be relatively low20,34,35. Importantly, if these electrophysiological signatures are unique to the explicit and goal-driven estimation of duration, they should be observed selectively in the temporal, not spatial, task.

Methods

Participants

31 participants (22 women and 8 men) aged 20 to 36 years old (mean ± standard deviation = 24.07 ± 3.54) participated in this study. This sample size is equivalent or even greater than similar EEG studies of duration processing in healthy participants5,6,18–20. A power analysis for sample size estimation, conducted using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 and based on data from a very similar EEG study of temporal and spatial processing19, indicated that a minimum of 15 participants would be required to detect an effect.

Participants’ vision was good or corrected if necessary. Written, informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the experiment and the procedure was approved by the French Sud-Est III ethics committee (2020-A00358-31). Research was performed in accordance with relevant regulations and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Stimuli and procedure

The cognitive task was adapted from that used by Coull et al.24 for fMRI. The task was programmed in Python and run with Psychopy 3.2 software. Our experimental protocol consisted of two tasks: a temporal task and a spatial task. The two tasks were visually identical, only the instruction given to the participant differed between the two conditions. During the task (Fig. 1), two stimuli were presented one after the other and the participant was asked to decide whether the second stimulus (Probe) was shorter, the same or longer than the first (Sample), in terms of either duration (temporal task) or distance (spatial task).

Trial details for both temporal and spatial tasks. Participants judged whether a probe stimulus was shorter (S), equal (=), or longer (L) than the previous Sample stimulus, either in terms of duration in the temporal task or in terms of distance in the spatial task. The stimulus was a dot that moved smoothly from left to right on the screen. Responses were provided with index/middle/ring finger button presses, and stimulus-response associations varied across trials to minimize motor preparation confounds. Sample, Probe and response screen stimuli were separated by a variable inter-stimulus interval (ISI) of 750-950ms. Intertrial intervals (ITI) lasted 800ms. ISI and ITI consisted of a black screen.

Each trial began with an 800ms blank inter-trial interval (ITI), followed by the consecutive presentation of the two visual stimuli (“Sample” then “Probe”). Each stimulus was a white dot moving from left to right on a black screen for one of four durations (800, 1200, 1600, 2000ms) and one of 4 distances (1.6°, 2°, 2.8°, 3.2° visual angle). The Sample and Probe were each followed by a variable (750 to 950ms) inter-stimulus interval (ISI), which allowed the electrophysiological signal to return to baseline after presentation of each of the two stimuli. Finally, a response screen appeared for 1500ms, consisting of the characters “S”, “L” or “=”. For the temporal task, the participant was asked to judge whether the duration of the Probe was shorter (S), longer (L), or equal (=) to the duration of the Sample. For the spatial task, the participant was asked to decide whether the distance covered by the Probe was shorter, longer, or equal to that covered by the Sample. Participants provided their decision with the index, middle or ring finger of the right hand, which were positioned on one of the 3 response buttons located before them. The positions of the buttons corresponded to the location of the characters on the response screen. Importantly, the position of the response characters on the screen varied from one trial to the next (S = L, LS=, or = LS) to minimise the possibility that participants would begin preparing their motor response during stimulus presentation, which would confound electrophysiological activity related to motor preparation with that related to timing. As soon as the participant had responded, the next trial began.

The temporal and spatial tasks were presented in two different blocks, counterbalanced across participants. Each task lasted approximately 40 min and participants could take a short rest every 10 min or so. There were 256 trials per task, comprising 64 trials of each of the four possible durations/distances of the Sample or Probe stimuli. The duration and distance of the stimuli were varied orthogonally, resulting in different stimulus speeds. To maximize the number of trials per Sample-Probe pairing (32 trials per trial-type), we partially orthogonalized the relative magnitude of Sample and Probe stimuli (Table 1). In the temporal task, there were 32 repetitions of each of the eight trial-types listed in Table 1: 800ms Samples could be followed by Probes of either equal (800ms) or longer (1600ms) duration (i.e. 800–800 or 800–1600 trials), 1200ms Samples could be followed by equal (1200ms) or longer (2000ms) Probes (1200–1200 or 1200–2000 trials), 1600ms Samples by equal (1600ms) or shorter (800ms) Probes (1600–1600 or 1600 − 800 trials), and 2000ms Samples by equal (2000ms) or shorter (1200ms) Probes (2000–2000 or 2000 − 1200 trials). Correspondingly, in the spatial task, there were 32 repetitions each of the eight trial-types listed in Table 1: a 1.6° Sample could be followed by Probes of equal or longer (2.8°) distance, a 2° Sample could be followed by equal or longer (3.2°) Probes, a 2.8° Sample by equal or shorter (1.6o) Probes, or a 3.2° Sample by equal or shorter (2.0o) Probes. Importantly, the duration of stimuli in the spatial task was matched to that in the temporal task to allow for direct comparison of identical stimulus durations that were either task-relevant (temporal task) or not (spatial task). Similarly, stimulus distances in the temporal task were the same as those used in the spatial task. To encourage participants to attend selectively to stimulus duration in the temporal task, or to stimulus distance in the spatial task, the relative magnitude of the task-relevant feature did not covary with that of the task-irrelevant one. For instance, if the Probe had a longer duration than the Sample, it would not also have a longer distance (Table 1). This meant participants could not provide a correct answer based on the task-irrelevant feature. Finally, the starting location of the stimulus on the screen was varied from one trial to the next (centre or left/right of centre) so that participants could not estimate stimulus distance simply from its final position on the screen. This manipulation served to further equate the temporal and spatial tasks: in both cases, the duration or distance of the stimulus could only be estimated by reference to a memorized starting point in either time or space. Prior to EEG acquisition, participants completed a training block of 10 trials for each task to become familiar with the paradigm.

EEG acquisition and pre-processing

EEG activity was recorded with the 64-channel Biosemi ActiView system at a sampling rate of 1024 Hz. Pre-amplified Ag/AgCl electrodes were arranged on the recording cap according to the 10/20 system. To detect artifacts related to eye blinks the electro-oculogram (EOG) was collected by positioning two electrodes at the outer canthi of the eyes to detect horizontal movements and above and below the right eye to detect vertical movements. All electrodes were off-line re-referenced to the average of left and right mastoids.

Pre-processing steps were performed with BrainVision Analyzer software. To ensure that EEG data was most likely to reflect selective processing of either temporal or spatial stimulus features, we processed data from correct trials only. Continuous EEG data were re-referenced to the averaged signal from mastoid electrodes and were filtered with a 0.01 Hz high pass filter and a 30 Hz low pass filter. Independent Components Analysis (ICA) was applied to EEG data to detect and remove data containing eye movements. Data from one participant was excluded from all further analysis due to poor signal quality. From the remaining datasets, we excluded data from 4 particularly noisy electrodes (FP1, AF7, AF8 and FP2). Any remaining artifacts not previously accounted for e.g. ocular artifacts, muscle artifacts, non-physiological artifacts, or slow drifts, were then marked and eliminated by a semiautomatic method (detected by an algorithm and then visually inspected).

We then created two epochs (segments) of interest from the continuous EEG recording of each task. Epochs were time-locked to presentation of the Probe (comparison) stimulus since this was the moment at which temporal or spatial decision-making occurred. Note that although participants could make a perceptual decision during presentation of the Probe, they could not yet begin to prepare the appropriate motor response since the response screen varied from trial to trial. First, to explore how CNV activity evolved during Probe presentation, we defined an epoch time-locked to stimulus onset that began 200ms prior to Probe onset and ended 200ms after its offset. Signal amplitude in each epoch was baseline corrected with reference to the period 200ms before stimulus onset. 13.2% of these epochs were removed from further analysis due to noisy data. Second, to investigate how decision-making was reflected in LPC activity after the stimulus had ended, we defined an epoch time-locked to Probe offset that began 50ms before offset and ended 600ms after it. Signal amplitude in each epoch was baseline corrected with reference to a 100ms period, from 50ms pre-offset to 50ms post-offset34. 12.53% of these post-stimulus epochs were removed due to noise. Signal amplitude was measured either during or after stimulus presentation within a region of interest (ROI) centered on frontocentral electrodes (comprising FCz, FC1, FC2, C1, C2 and Cz electrodes), consistent with previous EEG studies of timing5,12,14,19,20,34. A Python script was used to average the amplitude of the signal across all six electrodes of the ROI, within each time window of interest. Prior to statistical analysis, relevant epochs were averaged across all participants to allow initial visualization of EEG dynamics. Although LPC activity was analysed within a frontocentral ROI to allow for comparison with previous timing studies20,29,34,35, the P3b or CPP component typically exhibits a more parietal scalp distribution. We therefore replicated LPC analyses within a more posterior ROI, comprising CPz, CP1, CP2, Pz, P1, and P2 electrodes (see Supplementary Information, Figure S1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with Jamovi software. Shapiro’s test was used to evaluate the normality of the data.

Behavioural performance

To determine whether tasks were matched for difficulty paired Samples t-tests were used to compare the percentage of correct responses or errors (under- versus over-estimates) in the temporal and spatial tasks.

CNV dynamics

We first aimed to confirm prior findings that the slope of the CNV is steeper for shorter memorized (sample) durations9,36,37 .We restricted analysis to Probes whose duration was equal to that of the memorized Sample (i.e. 800–800, 1200–1200, 1600–1600 and 2000–2000 trial-types) to minimize the possibility that temporal decisions would be made prior to stimulus offset. For each of these four Probes, we calculated the slope of mean signal amplitude in the temporal task from 400ms (CNVonset) to 800ms (longest timepoint common to all four Probes) after stimulus onset. We analysed the effect of Probe duration on CNV slope in a one-way ANOVA.

Second, we aimed to clarify prior findings5,7–12,14 that CNV amplitude resolves back to baseline (i.e. starts to become more positive) prior to stimulus offset only when Probe duration is longer than Sample duration (P > S trials), not when it’s equal (P = S trials). We examined whether this effect depended upon the instruction to explicitly estimate duration, by comparing how CNV amplitude evolved throughout Probe presentation when duration was either the task-relevant stimulus feature (temporal task) or not (spatial task). Specifically, we measured activity during 1600ms Probes that had been preceded by either a shorter or equal duration Sample (800–1600 or 1600–1600 trials), and during 2000ms Probes that had been preceded by either a shorter or equal duration Sample (1200–2000 or 2000–2000 trials), in both the temporal and spatial tasks. We calculated mean signal amplitude in 25 sliding time windows of 100ms (50ms overlap) during the last 1200ms of Probe presentation. Timebins for the 1600ms and 2000ms Probes were aligned such that the duration corresponding to the Sample duration fell into either the 9th timebin (for P > S trials) or the final timebin (for P = S trials). To compare the dynamics of CNV resolution in the temporal versus spatial tasks, we used a 25 × 2 × 2 repeated measures ANOVA to compare the evolution of mean CNV amplitude during these 25 timebins during presentation of the two longest Probes (1600 and 2000ms) during either task (temporal/spatial).

We further quantified these effects by calculating the slope of the change in activity across timebins during presentation of the 2000ms Probes. Importantly, the orthogonalization of duration and distance in the 1200-2000ms and 2000-2000ms trials (Table 1) allowed us to identify electrophysiological signatures of feature-specific processing. In both temporal and spatial tasks, the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of duration in 1200-2000ms trials but Sample and Probe distance were equal. Conversely, for 2000-2000ms trials, the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of distance but Sample and Probe durations were equal. For 1200-2000ms and 2000-2000ms trials, we calculated slopes before and after the 1200ms timebin. This timebin corresponds to the memorized Sample duration for 1200-2000ms trials in the temporal task and to the memorized Sample distance for 2000-2000ms trials in the spatial task. Slopes were calculated from 400ms (CNV onset) to 1200ms, and from 1200ms to Probe offset. We used 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVAs to explore the effects of task (temporal, spatial), relative duration (P > S, P = S), and time window (before/after 1200ms) on these slopes.

In an analogous manner, we also plotted CNV amplitude as a function of the elapsing distance (visual angle), rather than duration (milliseconds), of the Probe stimulus. In the spatial task, for which distance was the task-relevant feature, slopes of activity were calculated for 3.2o Probes whose distance was either longer (2.0o-3.2o trials) or equal (3.2o-3.2o trials) to that of the preceding Sample. Conveniently, in the 2.0o-3.2o trials, the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of distance but their durations were the same. By contrast, in 3.2o-3.2o trials, Probe and Sample distances were the same but their durations differed. By way of comparison, we also calculated slopes during the 3.2o Probes of the temporal task, for which distance was not task-relevant. Specifically, we calculated slopes for the 2.0-3.2o and 2.8-3.2o trials of the temporal task. We averaged CNV amplitudes in overlapping bins of 0.08o visual angle from Probe onset (0o) to Probe offset (3.2o). For both tasks, we calculated slopes during two distinct segments of the Probe’s trajectory, either before or after the bin corresponding to the critical visual angle of 2.0o. We then conducted 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVAs on the resulting slope values to explore the effects of task (temporal, spatial), relative Sample-Probe distance, and visual angle bin (before/after 2.0o).

LPC amplitude

Based on previous studies34,38, we hypothesized that during the temporal task the amplitude of the LPC at stimulus offset would be smaller when Probe duration was longer than Sample duration (P > S trials) than when Probe duration was shorter (P < S trials). To extend these prior findings, we then examined whether LPC effects were dependent upon the instruction to explicitly estimate duration, by comparing LPC amplitude when duration was the task-relevant stimulus feature (temporal task) or not (spatial task). Specifically, we measured mean LPC amplitude in a 200ms temporal window from 300ms to 500ms after stimulus offset34 in both temporal and spatial tasks. Signal amplitude was extracted from trials in which the duration of the Probe was longer than the Sample (800-1600ms and 1200-2000ms trials) and trials in which it was shorter (2000-1200ms and 1600-800ms trials). We used a 2 × 2 repeated measures ANOVA to test the effects of relative duration (P > S; P < S) and task (temporal/spatial) on LPC amplitude. Moreover, to explore whether this pattern of effect is selective to temporal decision-making, we examined whether LPC amplitude might also reflect decisions concerning the relative magnitude of a different stimulus feature, namely spatial distance. We therefore calculated mean LPC amplitude for trials in which the distance travelled by the Probe was longer than that of the Sample (i.e. 3.2°-2.2° ; 2.8°-1.6°) versus trials in which Probe distance was shorter than Sample (i.e. 2°-3.2° ; 1.6°-2.8°). A 2 × 2 repeated measures ANOVA was used to test the effects of relative distance (P > S; P < S) and task (temporal/spatial) on LPC amplitude.

Results

Behavioural performance

Task performance was slightly better in the temporal task (67.4% correct) than the spatial task (62.4% correct) but this difference was not significantly different (t(29) = 1.90, p = 0.067, Cohen’s d = 0.347, two-tailed test). There was no significant difference in the relative proportion of under- and over-estimates of the task-relevant feature in either the temporal (t(29)= -0.427, p = 0.673, Cohen’s d= -0.078) or spatial task (t(29) = 0.820, p = 0.419, Cohen’s d = 0.15).

CNV

Effect of absolute Sample duration on initial CNV slope in the temporal task

We first analysed CNV dynamics for P = S trials, in which the duration of the Sample and Probe were identical (800-800ms, 1200-1200ms, 1600-1600ms, 2000-2000ms trials). Confirming prior findings, CNV slopes during the first 800ms of Probe presentation in the temporal task (i.e. the initial period common to all four Probe durations) varied as a function of the Sample duration to which it was being compared (Fig. 2a). Specifically, CNV slopes were significantly steeper for shorter memorized Samples F(3,87) = 4.46, p = 0.006 (Fig. 2b).

Effect of relative Sample duration on CNV resolution in temporal and spatial tasks

We then compared CNV dynamics for Probes preceded by a Sample of equal duration (P = S) to Probes whose duration was longer than that of the Sample (P > S). Grand averaged frontocentral ERPs indicated that the CNV resolved early, prior to stimulus offset, for P > S trials in the temporal task (Figs. 3a, b), but not in the spatial one (Figs. 3c, d). A repeated-measures ANOVA with 4 within-subject factors of task (temporal, spatial), relative duration (P > S, P = S), duration range (800/1600, 1200/2000) and timebin (25 sliding time windows during the final 1200ms of stimulus presentation) revealed significant three-way interactions between task, relative duration, and timebin (F[24,696] = 6.571, p < 0.001, η2p=0.185), and between relative duration, duration range and timebin (F[24,696] = 4.425, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.132), which were further qualified by the full four-way interaction between all factors (F[24,696] = 4.614, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.137).

Conversely, in 2000-2000ms trials, the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of distance but their durations were matched. Breaking this interaction down by task revealed that during the temporal task, there was a significant interaction between relative duration and timebin (F[24,696] = 3.826, p < 0.001, η2p=0.117). As hypothesized, the CNV resolved back to baseline more steeply when the Probe had been preceded by a Sample with a shorter duration (P > S), than by one with the same duration (P = S). There was also a significant interaction between duration range and timebin (F[24,696] = 9.797, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.253), which revealed that the timebin in which the CNV resolved differed between 800-1600ms trials and 1200-2000ms trials. As hypothesized, the deflection point for 1200-2000ms trials was in the timebin corresponding to the memorized 1200ms Sample (Fig. 3b, dashed line). Intriguingly, the deflection point for the 800-1600ms trials was not in the timebin corresponding to the 800ms Sample (Fig. 3a, dashed line) but, instead, appeared also at 1200ms.

The 3-way interaction between relative duration, duration range and timebin was not significant for the temporal task (F[24,696] = 0.473, p = 0.986 η2p=0.016). By contrast, this 3-way interaction was significant in the spatial task (F[24,696] = 7.12, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.197) which reflected early resolution of the CNV when Probe duration matched that of the Sample (F[24,296] = 2.428, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.077) and, more specifically, when both stimuli were 2000ms (Fig. 3d) but not when both were 1600ms (Fig. 3c) (F[24,696] = 9.199, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.241). Together, these analyses reveal that the CNV resolved early when Probe duration exceeded that of the Sample duration (P > S) in the temporal task but resolved early when Probe and Sample duration were equal (P = S) in the spatial task (specifically when both were 2000ms). Although this early CNV resolution in the spatial task might seem surprising, it is important to remember that during the spatial task the task-relevant feature is distance. In the 2000-2000ms trials of the spatial task, the Sample distance (2o) was shorter than the Probe distance (3.2o) (Table 1). Therefore, the time at which the distance of the Sample stimulus would have been bypassed during presentation of 2000ms Probe was (2000ms/3.2o)*2o i.e. 1250ms. This corresponds closely to the moment at which the CNV began to resolve in the 2000–2000 trials of the spatial task (Fig. 3d).

Effect of Sample duration or distance on CNV slope in temporal and spatial tasks

CNV slope as a function of relative Sample-Probe duration

We further quantified these effects by analysing the slope of the CNV during presentation of the 2000ms Probes specifically (Fig. 3e). In 1200-2000ms trials, the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of duration (P > S duration) but Sample and Probe distance were matched (P = S distance). Conversely, in 2000-2000ms trials the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of distance (P > S distance) but their durations were matched (P = S duration). For 1200-2000ms and 2000-2000ms trials of both temporal (Fig. 3b) and spatial (Fig. 3d) tasks, we calculated slopes before and after the 1200ms timebin, which corresponds to the moment of early CNV resolution for task-relevant duration or distance judgements. A 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between task (temporal, spatial), relative duration (P > S, P = S), and time window (before/after 1200ms) (F1,29 = 26.61, p < 0.001, η2p=0.478). After the critical 1200ms timepoint (Fig. 3e, right-hand graph), the CNV slope was significantly more positive (i.e. resolved back to baseline) in the temporal versus spatial task for 1200-2000ms trials, in which the Probe was longer than Sample in terms of duration but not distance. Conversely, in 2000-2000ms trials, in which the Probe was longer than the Sample in terms of distance but not duration, the CNV was significantly more positive in the spatial than the temporal task.

These analyses also revealed that before the critical 1200ms timepoint (Fig. 3e, left-hand graph) the negative slope of the CNV in the temporal task was significantly steeper for 1200-2000ms trials than 2000-2000ms trials, (t(29)= -2.49, p = 0.009, Cohen’s d=-0.454). This result corroborates our initial analysis of the P = S trials (Fig. 2) and confirms that that the initial ramping phase of the CNV is steeper when the previously memorized duration was relatively short (1200ms) rather than long (2000ms). Compellingly, a corresponding pattern was found in the spatial task. The initial CNV slope was significantly more negative for 2000-2000ms trials, in which the memorized distance of the Sample was shorter (2o) and therefore reached sooner (1250ms) than in 1200-2000ms trials, in which Sample distance was equal to the Probe (1.6o) and therefore not reached until Probe offset (2000ms) (t(29)= -2.27, p = 0.015, Cohen’s d= -0.415).

CNV slope as a function of relative Sample-Probe distance

To clarify and further quantify these spatial effects, we plotted CNV amplitude as a function of the elapsing distance (visual angle), rather than duration (milliseconds), of the Probe stimulus, and performed analogous analyses on CNV slopes. Specifically, we compared slopes during presentation of 3.2o Probes (Fig. 4a) to examine whether CNV amplitude would resolve prior to stimulus offset during trials in which the Probe was preceded by a Sample of shorter distance (2.0-3.2o trials) rather than equal distance (3.2-3.2o trials) in the spatial, but not temporal, tasks. A 2 × 2 × 2 repeated-measures ANOVA of CNV slope revealed a significant 3-way interaction between task, relative Sample-Probe distance, and visual angle bin (F1,28 = 51.30, p < 0.001, η2p=0.647). Breaking this interaction down by visual angle bin (before/after 2.0o) showed that the initial slope of activity before 2.0o ((Fig. 4b, left) was significantly more negative for the shorter Sample distance in the spatial, but not temporal, task (F[1.28] = 11.00, p = 0.003, 0.282). This result confirms that the initial ramping phase of the CNV is steeper for shorter memorized distances when distance is the task-relevant feature.

In addition, after the critical distance of 2.0o had been reached (Fig. 4b, right) there was a significant task-specific divergence in the direction of the CNV slope depending on the Sample distance that had been memorized (F(1,28) = 45.70, p < 0.001, η2p=0.620). In the spatial task, CNV slope remained negative until stimulus offset if the Probe had been preceded by a Sample of equal distance (3.2o-3.2o trials) but began to resolve (became more positive) if the preceding Sample distance had been shorter than that of the Probe (2.0o-3.2o trials) (t(29) = 6.69, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.22). Moreover, the CNV began to resolve during these 2.0o-3.2o trials only during the spatial, not the temporal, task (t(28) = 6.61, p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.23). Taken together, these findings indicate that the CNV began to resolve as soon as the Probe had exceeded the memorized Sample distance only when distance was task-relevant in the spatial task, not when it was task-irrelevant in the temporal task.

LPC

To explore whether LPC amplitude carries information about both temporal and spatial decision-making, we measured activity during trials in which either the duration (Figs. 5a, b) or the distance (Figs. 5c, d) of the Probe was longer (P > S) or shorter (P < S) than that of the Sample in the temporal (Fig. 5, top) or spatial (Fig. 5, bottom) tasks. In the temporal task, grand averaged frontocentral ERPs indicated that activity 300-500ms after Probe offset (LPC) was lower when the duration of the Probe was longer (P > S trials) rather than shorter (P < S) than the Sample (Fig. 5a). Intriguingly, during the spatial task grand averaged ERPs ndicated that LPC amplitude was similarly lower in the same time window when the distance of the Probe was longer (P > S) rather than shorter (P < S) than the Sample (Fig. 5d).

Topographies display activity from 300ms to 500ms after the Probe offset (grey shaded area). A late positive component (LPC) was observed from 300ms to 500ms after Probe offset. LPC amplitude was significantly lower when either (e) the duration of the Probe was longer (P > S) rather than shorter (P < S) than the Sample in the temporal task or (f) when Probe distance was longer, rather than shorter, than the Sample in the spatial task. (***p < 0.001). Error bars represent standard error.

These observations were first quantified by a repeated-measures ANOVA of LPC amplitude showing a significant interaction between Task (temporal/spatial) and relative duration (P > S or P < S) (F[1,29] = 25.41, p < 0.001, η2p=0.467). LPC amplitude was smaller when Probe duration was relatively longer than the Sample than when it was shorter, but only in the temporal, not spatial, task (Fig. 5e). This pattern confirms previous findings of lower LPC amplitude for longer durations34 and, furthermore, reveals that these duration-related changes are observed only when participants are explicitly making a temporal decision in the temporal task. A second repeated-measures ANOVA showed a significant interaction also between Task (temporal/spatial) and relative distance (P > S or P < S) (F[1,29] = 53.70, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.649). Specifically, LPC amplitude was smaller when Probe distance was relatively longer than the Sample than when it was shorter, but only in the spatial, not temporal, task (Fig. 5f). Therefore, in parallel to the duration-related results described above, LPC amplitude was lower for longer distances but only when these decisions were being made explicitly during the spatial task.

Discussion

We examined how the dynamics of two electrophysiological potentials evoked either during (CNV) or after (LPC) stimulus presentation, varied as a function of the temporal (duration) or spatial (distance) magnitude of a visual stimulus. In the temporal task, duration was task-relevant and distance was task-irrelevant, while the opposite was true in the spatial task. The use of identical dynamic stimuli in both temporal and spatial tasks not only controlled for incidental working memory and attentional demands between tasks but also allowed us to examine whether CNV and LPC dynamics varied with relative duration or distance, when that feature was either task-relevant or not.

Confirming our hypotheses, the CNV resolved back to baseline before stimulus offset when Probe duration was longer than Sample duration selectively in the temporal, not spatial task. In other words, early resolution of the CNV, thought to index decision-making based on a memorized temporal template7,9,10,12,20, was observed only when (temporal) task goals required participants to attend selectively to stimulus duration17. In parallel, the CNV resolved early during the spatial task when the distance covered by the Probe was longer than that of the Sample, i.e. when (spatial) task goals required participants to attend selectively to stimulus distance. Together, these results demonstrate that early resolution of the CNV reflects explicit, goal-driven estimation of the task-relevant stimulus feature. Our results therefore extend prior findings in the timing literature11,12,19 by suggesting that CNV dynamics reflect the time taken to make a decision about the magnitude of any task-relevant feature, and are not specific to the processing of stimulus duration. Specifically, we suggest that early resolution of the CNV, prior to stimulus offset, identifies an internal model of the moment at which the magnitude of the memorized task-relevant feature would be reached, whether that feature is temporal (duration) or spatial (distance).

Indeed, parallels in temporal and spatial processing were also observed during the initial ramping phase of CNV activity. Confirming prior findings in humans9,19,36 and animals37,39–42, the initial slope of the CNV during the Probe of the temporal task was steeper when the previously memorized Sample duration had been shorter (Fig. 2). The steepness of this slope reflects the internal temporal template to which the current stimulus is being compared. Moreover, the initial CNV slope was also steeper in the spatial task when the distance of the memorized Sample was shorter (Fig. 4). Therefore, the initial CNV slope was steeper whenever the memorized magnitude was reached sooner, whether that task-relevant magnitude was duration or distance. Incidentally, differences in initial CNV ramping were unlikely to simply reflect the speed with which the Probe stimulus was presented. CNV slopes were steeper for the 2000ms Probe that travelled at the slower speed in the temporal task but at the faster speed in the spatial task.

CNV dynamics, both before (initial ramping) and after (CNV resolution) the moment at which the magnitude of the memorized Sample had been reached, are therefore similar for the goal-driven processing of temporal or spatial stimulus features. Our findings complement and extend those of Robinson and Wiener19 who found that CNV dynamics varied as a function of the duration, but not distance, of a memorized visuospatial trajectory during motor reproduction of a visuospatial trajectory. By using a perceptual discrimination task, we demonstrated that CNV dynamics in fact represent an amodal internal model of the time needed to reach the memorized magnitude of a sensory stimulus, be it magnitude in time (duration) or space (distance). Nevertheless, during the 800-1600ms trials of our temporal task, the CNV unexpectedly resolved around 1200ms, and not 800ms as would be the case if participants were comparing the Probe to the memorized Sample. Participants might have instead been comparing the Probe stimulus to a temporal heuristic that represented the approximate average of the four durations used in our experiment, a kind of a temporal central tendency43. Indeed, CNV resolution around the central value of the duration distribution has already been observed in temporal bisection tasks5,14 in which two standard durations were memorized at the start of the experiment. In our experiment, by contrast, different standards were presented on each trial and we had hypothesized that participants would base their decisions on the duration of these Sample stimuli. It appears instead that participants binarized stimuli into short (800ms and 1200ms) and long (1600ms and 2000ms) categories, and used the longer of the short durations (1200ms) as a critical threshold for making their temporal decision (see also12,44.

Compellingly, the LPC evoked at stimulus offset also appears to reflect decisions about the relative magnitude of stimulus features in both temporal and spatial dimensions. Consistent with previous findings5,26,34, LPC amplitude in the temporal task was greater when Probe duration was shorter than the Sample. This probably reflects the greater degree of surprise evoked by Probes that end sooner than expected (i.e. sooner than the memorised Sample). We refined these findings by demonstrating that LPC amplitude reflected relative stimulus duration only when duration was the task-relevant feature in the temporal task, not when duration was incidental to task requirements in the spatial task. Moreover, we extended existing findings by demonstrating equivalent effects in the spatial dimension: LPC amplitude was greater when Probe distance was shorter (and therefore more surprising) than Sample distance and that this was observed selectively during the spatial task, not the temporal one. Bueno & Cravo20 have also demonstrated previously that LPC amplitude (and CNV dynamics) changed as a function of duration when duration was task-relevant in a temporal discrimination task, but not during a color discrimination control. However, their stimuli did not allow investigation of similar effects of task-relevant color processing, partly because color is not a quantifiable (or “prothetic”) stimulus feature, like duration or distance. Together, our collected findings suggest that LPC amplitude and CNV dynamics do not reflect temporal processing selectively. Instead, they appear to represent comparison of the magnitude of a task-relevant stimulus feature to a memorized internal model, in either the temporal or spatial dimension.

A simple explanation of these data would be that LPC amplitude simply varies as a function of Probe duration: the longer the duration, the greater the variability in the latency of individual neural responses and so the lower the average amplitude. Alternatively, our results are consistent with the model of temporal decision-making proposed by Balci and Simen45, based on a Drift Diffusion Model (DDM). The DDM consists of two stages: the first is information accumulation, where temporal information is accumulated until a threshold is reached, while the second stage is the decision-making process in which a temporal decision is made depending on when this threshold was reached. The threshold represents the temporal template against which the currently elapsing duration is compared, with shorter temporal templates corresponding to faster (steeper) rates of information accumulation. The data from our study confirm observations made by others6,35,46 that the first stage of the model appears to be reflected by CNV dynamics. We found that the initial slope of the CNV was steeper for shorter Sample durations, consistent with the threshold-setting mechanism. The CNV then developed gradually until this threshold was reached. Once this threshold had been surpassed, the CNV resolved. A temporal decision could then be made depending on whether the threshold was reached prior to stimulus offset. If so, the decision would be that Probe duration was longer than that of the template. If not, the decision would be that it was equal to or shorter than the template. Moreover, Ofir and Landau34 recently demonstrated that the extent to which stimulus duration is shorter than that of a template is coded in the amplitude of the LPC evoked at stimulus offset. In other words, LPC amplitude reflected the temporal distance from the decision threshold, potentially representing the decision mechanism described in the second stage of the DDM model. Specifically, Ofir and Landau34 showed that if the duration of a stimulus is shorter than that of a memorized template, LPC amplitude varied inversely with duration i.e. LPC amplitude was higher for shorter durations. However, if stimulus duration exceeded that of the template, the amplitude of the LPC measured after stimulus offset did not get any lower as a function of increasing duration because the threshold had already been reached during presentation of the stimulus. In line with these observations, we found that LPC amplitude after stimulus offset was higher whenever the Probe was shorter than the Sample, and confirmed that this mechanism was observed only when stimulus duration was task-relevant i.e. when duration was being estimated explicitly in the temporal task, not when it might have been processed only incidentally in the spatial task. Moreover, and in line with our CNV results, we show that LPC amplitude was also higher when Probe distance was shorter, rather than longer, than that of the spatial template, and that this occurred selectively during the spatial task when stimulus distance was the task-relevant feature. LPC amplitude might therefore reflect the surprise evoked by the magnitude of a task-relevant stimulus, in either the temporal or spatial dimension. Since LPC amplitude is known to vary as a function of expectancy, these variations could reflect the degree of temporal or spatial prediction error from the memorized (expected) standard.

Our findings, consistent with previous observations6,34,35,46,47, indicate that CNV dynamics could represent the initial setting, and accumulation of information up to, a decision threshold, whereas the amplitude of the LPC at stimulus offset represents the difference between current stimulus magnitude and the memorised internal model. Such electrophysiological activity over frontocentral electrodes might reflect activity in the SMA3,48–50. fMRI data consistently demonstrate that SMA is a region activated by judgements about the relative duration of events51–55 and single-cell recordings in monkey SMA37,56,57 have identified duration-specific patterns of neural activity. ATOM (A Theory Of Magnitude) is an influential theoretical framework proposing the existence of a brain network that processes the relative magnitude of a variety of prothetic stimulus features, including size, duration and number58. The most widely proposed anatomical candidate for ATOM has been the parietal cortex, but a recent meta-analysis of neuroimaging studies also identified SMA as being systematically activated by processing of both space and time59. Although SMA is preferentially activated by temporal, rather than spatial, processing59, particularly when duration is task-relevant24, our data invite further investigation as to whether activity in this region might also carry information about the relative distance or size of a stimulus, as well as its duration.

In summary, our results indicate that CNV and LPC dynamics reflect a domain-general mechanism for goal-driven processing of the magnitude of task-relevant stimulus features, be they relative duration judgements during temporal tasks or relative distance judgements during spatial tasks. Nevertheless, even though activity across an entire population of cells within a region might be consistent with a domain-general representation, it’s still possible that individual neurons within that region could differentially code for duration versus distance60. Multivariate pattern analysis of EEG activity during different forms of magnitude judgements could shed light on this issue.
