Bilateral erector spinae plane block by multiple injection for pain control in pseudomyxoma peritonei surgery: a single-blind randomized controlled trial
Authors: Shuang Yu, Guangya Gao, Ruiqing Ma, Liangyuan Lu, Yaoping Zhao, Zhanmin Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveCurrently, the primary surgical treatment for pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) is cytoreductive surgery (CRS) combined with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC). The perioperative period is frequently accompanied by severe pain. Erector spinae plane block (ESPB) can enhance analgesia for abdominal surgery. The purpose of this study was to compare the analgesic effects of bilateral multiple-injection ESPB in patients with PMP.MethodsFifty patients with PMP were randomly divided into two groups: the ESPB combined with general anesthesia group (Group E) and the general anesthesia alone group (Group C). Prior to the induction, patients in Group E underwent ESPB at the T7 and T11 levels. The primary outcome was the visual analog scale (VAS) scores during rest at 6 h post-extubation. Secondary outcomes included intraoperative and postoperative opioid consumption, time for first rescue analgesia, frequency distribution of rescue analgesia, incidence of nausea and vomiting, adverse events associated with ESPB.ResultsThe Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scores in Group E were significantly lower compared to Group C at immediate post-extubation (1.6 ± 0.9 vs. 2.4 ± 1.2, P = 0.008), and at 2 (1.9 ± 1.2 vs. 3.2 ± 1.1, P < 0.001), 4 (2.4 ± 1.5 vs. 3.7 ± 1.0, P = 0.001), and 6 h (2.7 ± 1.1 vs. 3.8 ± 1.4, P = 0.004) post-extubation during rest. Similarly, the VAS scores in Group E were significantly lower than those in Group C at immediate post-extubation (3.0 ± 1.4 vs. 4.6 ± 1.2, P < 0.001), and at 2 (3.8 ± 1.7 vs. 4.9 ± 1.4, P = 0.019), 4 (3.5 ± 1.3 vs. 5.3 ± 1.5, P < 0.001), and 6 h (3.9 ± 1.8 vs. 4.9 ± 1.3, P = 0.004) post-extubation during movement. In Group E, the intraoperative remifentanil administration (2319.3 ± 1089.5 vs. 2984.6 ± 796.1, P = 0.017) and the amount of rescue analgesia within 2 h post-extubation (0 vs. 4, P = 0.037) were significantly less than in Group C, and the first rescue analgesia time was shorter as well (231.4 ± 147.5 vs. 668.8 ± 416.7, P < 0.001).ConclusionCompared to general anesthesia alone, bilateral multiple-injection ESPB with 0.2% ropivacaine can enhance analgesia and reduce opioid administration in patients with PMP. However, the duration of analgesia with ESPB is relatively short due to the low concentration of the local anesthetic used.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry, ChiCTR2300069504, 20/03/2023.Keywords: Erector spinae plane block, Postoperative analgesia, Pseudomyxoma peritonei, Regional anesthesia, Ultrasound-guided

Introduction

Pseudomyxoma peritonei (PMP) is a rare malignant tumor of the abdominal cavity [1]. The primary clinical features are diffuse progressive mucinous ascites and extensive intraperitoneal tumor metastasis. The primary surgical protocol for PMP is cytoreductive surgery (CRS) combined with hyperthermic intraperitoneal chemotherapy (HIPEC). CRS is performed to remove visible tumor cells in the abdominal cavity (which may include hysterectomy and adnexectomy, greater omentectomy, splenectomy, right hemicolectomy, limited hepatic resection, and partial resection of the abdominal diaphragm), and HIPEC is administered to remove as many residual tumor cells as possible [2]. Surgical trauma is severe, and patients frequently experience severe postoperative pain, which hinders their speedy recovery [3]. Perioperative pain management in PMP is still a challenge for anesthesiologists.

Currently, multimodal analgesia is advocated as a method for perioperative analgesia, with regional anesthesia playing a significant role [4]. Traditional analgesic techniques, such as epidural analgesia and ultrasound-guided paravertebral block, can provide effective analgesia for trunk surgery [5]. However, these two techniques can result in adverse effects such as intercostal hematoma, epidural hematoma, epidural abscesses, and hypotension [6, 7]. Patients with abdominal pseudomyxoma experience increased intraoperative bleeding, which can result in perioperative coagulopathy, hemodynamic instability, and other sudden conditions, as well as increase the risk of epidural analgesia and ultrasound-guided paravertebral block, hence it is not widely used in clinical practice.

Recently, erector spinae plane block (ESPB) has become a prominent technique for interfascial plane block of the trunk. Since ESPB was first described in 2016, it has been reported that ESPB can be used not only in thoracic surgery but also in liver surgery, gastrointestinal surgery, and gynecological surgery, among others [8–10]. It can provide effective perioperative analgesia and enhanced the recovery of patients. An increasing number of anesthesiologists have applied ESPB in perioperative analgesia as it is a simpler and safer technique of analgesia administration. Therefore, we hypothesized that bilateral multiple-injection ESPB may be beneficial for perioperative pain management in patients with PMP and reduce the use of perioperative analgesics. To date, no research has been published on the application of ESPB in PMP. The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the perioperative analgesic efficacy of bilateral multiple-injection ESPB in patients with PMP, and the secondary aim was to explore the influence on the application of opioid drugs.

Methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Aerospace Center Hospital (IRB 2023-010) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry prior to patient enrollment (ChiCTR2300069504, 20/03/2023). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Fig 1 depicts a CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) flow diagram for this study.

Flow diagram of participant enrollment and analysis. ESPB erector spinae plane block

The duration of this prospective, randomized, single-blinded, controlled clinical trial was between March 2023 and September 2023. Patients with PMP, 18–65 years of age, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–III, and undergoing elective surgery for the first CSR combined with HIPEC, were enrolled. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) allergy to local anesthetic drugs; (2) coagulation disorders; (3) presence of infection in the region designated for ESPB; (4) prolonged usage of opioids; (5) refusal to participate in this study; (6) prior partial removal or chest operation involving the diaphragm due to its involvement; and (7) failure to remove the endotracheal tube within two hours after transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU).

All patients who participated in this study were randomly divided into two groups using a computer-generated random number table: the ESPB combined with general anesthesia group (Group E, n = 25) and the general anesthesia alone group (Group C, n = 25). On the day of surgery, an anesthesiologist performing the ESPB was given a sealed envelope containing the group allocation by a nurse anesthetist who was unaware of the study design. After every patient entered the operating room, their electrocardiogram (ECG), SpO2 (oxygen saturation), and non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) were routinely monitored. Prior to induction of general anesthesia, ESPB is administered by an experienced anesthesiologist. Anesthesiologists who administered general anesthesia and follow-up were blinded to group allocation and did not participate in ESPB.

Bilateral multiple-injection ESPB

In Group E, bilateral multiple-injection ESPB was administered in the lateral decubitus position, with continuous monitoring in place. The ESPB puncture sites were designated at right T7, right T11, left T7, and left T11. The specific spinal segments were located through palpation, starting from C7 and moving caudally. T7 and T11 segments were precisely identified and marked on the skin for the procedure. The low-frequency curved ultrasound probe was positioned 2.5 to 3 cm lateral to the posterior midline in the sagittal plane. The transverse process and erector spinae muscles were identified by ultrasound (Fig. 2). Following the sterilization of the puncture site’s skin, a needle was inserted using the in-plane method in a caudal-to-cephalad direction. Upon contact with the transverse process, 1–3 ml of saline was injected to ascertain the needle tip’s placement. Following the ultrasound confirmation of saline spread between the erector spinae and the transverse process, local anesthetics were administered. The dosage of ropivacaine was calculated based on the patient’s body weight: the total dosage was 3 mg/kg, and the ropivacaine concentration was diluted to 0.2% with normal saline and placed in a 20 ml syringe. At each injection point, one-fourth of the total local anesthetic volume was administered.

General anesthesia protocol

Before the induction of anesthesia, all patients underwent continuous invasive blood pressure monitoring of the radial artery. The same general anesthesia protocol was used in this study. Sufentanil 0.3 µg/kg, propofol 1–2 mg/kg, and cisatracurium 0.15 mg/kg were used to induce anesthesia. After the drug was fully effective, a a wire-reinforced tube (7.5 for men and 7.0 for women) was inserted by video laryngoscope. Inhalation of 1% sevoflurane was used to maintain anesthesia and prevent intraoperative awareness. The administration involved a continuous intravenous infusion of propofol at 2–4 mg/kg/h, remifentanil at 0.1–0.5 µg/kg/min, and cisatracurium was administered intermittently to maintain muscle relaxation. The bispectral index (BIS) was maintained between 40 and 60 by adjusting the propofol infusion rate. The infusion rate of remifentanil was adjusted according to mean arterial pressure (MAP) and heart rate (HR) levels during surgery, so that MAP and HR were maintained at 30% of baseline value. All patients were administered sufentanil (5 µg) during abdominal wall closure. After surgery, patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) was administered with sufentanil 2 µg/kg, tropisetron 10 mg, diluted to 100 ml with normal saline, a background dose of 1 ml/h, a bolus of 0.5 ml, and a lockout time of 15 min. If the patient’s resting VAS score was > 4 points or if rescue analgesia was required, tramadol 1 mg/kg was administered. Patients in both groups were transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) for recovery if the extubation criteria were met. If patients in both groups were not extubated in the operating room, they were transferred to the ICU. As soon as the patient met the indications for extubation, the tube was extubated.

Outcome

The primary outcome of this investigation was the VAS scores (visual analog scale) during rest at 6 h post-extubation. The secondary outcomes included VAS scores during rest and movement immediately and 2h, 4h, 12 h, and 24 h after post-extubation (VAS score: 0–10 points; 0 points: no pain, 10 points: severe pain) [11], intraoperative and postoperative opioid consumption, time for first rescue analgesia, frequency distribution of rescue analgesia at 2 h, 4 h, 6 h, 12 h, and 24 h post-extubation, and incidence of nausea and vomiting. Nausea and vomiting assessments were conducted by anesthesia physicians and nurses independent of the research team, unaware of the patients’ group assignments. Nausea was defined as an unpleasant sensation in the stomach, while vomiting referred to the forceful expulsion of stomach contents. Any occurrence of nausea or vomiting from extubation to 24 h postoperatively was documented. Evaluators underwent training and adhered to uniform assessment criteria to ensure consistency and accuracy of recorded events. Local anesthetic systemic toxicity, hematoma, infection, and so on, were recorded as adverse events associated with ESPB. If the patient experienced dizziness, tinnitus, oral numbness, drowsiness, seizures, arrhythmias before induction of anesthesia, it was recorded as LAST. All anesthesiologists and nurses performing follow-up were unaware of the study group assignments.

Statistical analysis

All recorded data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 25.0. The normal distribution of variables was determined using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables are expressed as the means ± standard deviations, and the independent sample Student’s t test was used to compare groups. Skewed data are expressed as the median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U tests. The Pearson’s chi-squared test was used to compare differences between groups based on categorical variables expressed as numbers and percentages. The two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare repeated measures variables between the two groups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant between both groups.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated based on the primary outcome of this study. In the preliminary pilot study involving 10 patients, the VAS scores during rest at 6 h post-extubation were 3.1 ± 0.9 and 3.8 ± 0.7 for Group E and Group C, respectively. We calculated a minimal sample of 22 patients in each group for a research power of 90% (𝛼 = 0.05, 𝛽 = 0.1). Due to the potential loss of follow-up during the study, we enrolled a total of 25 patients in each group.

Results

Initially, 63 patients met the inclusion criteria for the study, but 13 patients were excluded (2 patients had tumor metastasis in the diaphragm, and part of the diaphragm was resected; 6 patients did not extubate the tracheal tube at 2 h after transfer to the ICU due to massive intraoperative bleeding; 5 patients declined to participate in this study). Finally, a total of 50 patients were included for analysis in this study, and no participants were lost during the follow-up period. Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that preoperative administration of 0.2% ropivacaine for bilateral multiple-injection ESPB in patients with PMP can significantly reduce the intraoperative consumption of remifentanil, effectively relieve postoperative pain within 6 h post-extubation, reduce the frequency distribution of rescue analgesia, and lengthen the time for first rescue analgesia. There were no cases of local anesthetic toxicity, hematoma, infection, or other complications related to regional anesthesia in patients who underwent ESPB. However, administration of sufentanil, pain scores at 12–24 h after extubation, and the frequency distribution of rescue analgesia were similar between the two groups. The results of our study demonstrated a reduced duration of postoperative analgesia compared to other published studies of ESPB in abdominal surgery (cholecystectomy and hysterectomy) [12, 13]. This result may have been caused by two different factors. First, it is pertinent to note that PMP surgeries entail an open abdominal approach, characterized by a substantial incision spanning from the xiphoid process to the pubic symphysis, covering a considerable surgical area [14]. Therefore, in this study, ESPB was performed at bilateral T7 and T11 transverse process levels to provide extensive abdominal analgesia. However, to prevent the occurrence of LAST, the total amount of ropivacaine used in the implementation of ESPB did not exceed 3 mg/kg, and the concentration of ropivacaine was 0.2%; consequently, the postoperative analgesia time was short. In published literature, it has also been reported that the effect of postoperative analgesia with 0.2% ropivacaine is significantly shorter than that with 0.5% ropivacaine [15]. Second, in our study, the operation time (435.8 ± 97.4 min) of CRS combined with HIPEC for PMP was significantly longer than that of other abdominal surgeries reported (laparoscopic cholecystectomy 78.2 ± 20.1 min, laparoscopic hysterectomy 155.2 ± 7.2 min) [16, 17].

In 2016, Forero et al. described ESPB as a novel treatment modality for chronic chest pain [18]. In recent years, numerous articles on the clinical application, diffusion mechanism, and extent of blocking diffusion of ESPB have been published [19–21]. However, there is still much controversy about ESPB. Presently, most scholars concur that the primary dispersion of local anesthetic occurs within the fascial space between the transverse process and erector spinae muscles. A minor portion spreads ventrally, reaching the ventral branch of the spinal nerves and occasionally even the paravertebral space. This distribution can effectively block the sympathetic trunk, thereby alleviating visceral pain [22, 23]. However, a few studies have shown that local anesthetic (LA) cannot spread ventrally after the implementation of ESBP and that postoperative pain cannot be significantly reduced [24, 25]. While Schwartzmann et al. [26](2020) observed gadolinium spreading into the neural foramina and intercostal space using in vivo MRI, it is crucial to note significant patient-to-patient variability in these observations. The objective of our study is to attain optimal perioperative abdominal analgesia through comprehensive exploration and understanding of these variations. As per earlier studies, Mounika applied bilateral ESPB at the T7 level for gallbladder surgery, and Frassanito applied bilateral ESPB at the T10 level for gynecological surgery. These approaches were adopted to provide analgesia in the regions of upper abdominal and pelvic surgery, respectively [27, 28]. In this study, we therefore chose puncture sites at T7 and T11 levels. PMP is typically caused by a perforated epithelial tumor of the appendix and requires a right hemicolectomy [29, 30]. The risk of gastrointestinal ulcers and even anastomotic leakage is increased by the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in order to prevent abnormal liver function and intraoperative bleeding [31]. In our study, NSAIDs were not utilized for perioperative analgesia. Instead, intravenous tramadol was administered as a rescue analgesic regimen when patients experienced a VAS score greater than 4 during rest or exhibited a strong need for rescue analgesia. Our results revealed that the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting and the administration of sufentanil within 24 h after extubation were reduced in patients with preoperative ESPB, but there was no significant difference when compared with general anesthesia alone. The reasons for this finding may be due to the relatively small sample size included in this study as well as the relatively brief duration of block caused by the application of 0.2% ropivacaine in ESPB, which was incapable of providing prolonged postoperative analgesia. Therefore, patients in Group E continued to require opioid supplementation for pain relief, leading to the results of this investigation.

This study has several limitations. It is challenging to provide effective analgesia for a long duration with 0.2% ropivacaine after ESPB. To extend the block duration, future research endeavors could explore increasing the dosage of ropivacaine and LA concentration. Additionally, it is important to note that the measurement of sensory block segments was not conducted in some patients, mainly due to significant preoperative abdominal swelling and, in certain cases, bowel obstruction. These factors could have potentially influenced the accuracy of the measurements. Finally, this was a single-blind investigation, and patients were aware of whether or not they would undergo ESPB block. This may have increased the bias of the results.

Conclusion

Bilateral multiple-injection ESPB can be applied to CRS combined with HIPEC for perioperative analgesia. This approach has the potential to decrease the requirement for opioid administration and effectively alleviate postoperative pain. However, the concentration of 0.2% ropivacaine used in this study was insufficient to provide long-term analgesia. Further research is essential to ascertain the safety and efficacy of increasing the dosage of LA in bilateral multiple-injection ESPB to achieve optimal analgesia.
