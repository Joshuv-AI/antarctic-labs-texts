Biomimetic Copper‐Doped Nano‐Aluminum Adjuvant Potentiates Therapy in Chemoresistant Acute Myeloid Leukemia
Authors: Chao He, Lingxiao Zhang, Yue Xiong, Yu Wang, Yuqing Zeng, Dandan Su, Ran Wang, Ying Li, Hongbo Chen, Fang Cheng, Zhi Ping Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a hematologic malignancy with frequent resistance to first‐line cytarabine‐based chemotherapy, primarily driven by heightened mitochondrial function. Here, we develop a copper‐doped nano‐aluminum adjuvant (CuNA) to overcome this resistance by targeting mitochondrial vulnerability. Upon internalization, CuNA releases Cu2 +, leading to intracellular Cu2+ overload, which disrupts mitochondrial function and induces ferroptosis in drug‐resistant AML cells, thereby markedly enhancing cytarabine sensitivity. Moreover, CuNA downregulates cholesterol biosynthesis and antioxidant defense pathways, including suppression of HMG‐CoA reductase and glutathione peroxidase 4, further amplifying ferroptosis in combination with cytarabine. In drug‐resistant AML mouse models, CuNA coated with AML cell membranes demonstrates efficient tumor targeting, robust suppression of leukemia progression, and prolonged survival. This study highlights CuNA as a promising tool to overcome chemoresistance mediated by mitochondrial reprogramming in AML.Keywords: copper overload, cytarabine‐resistant acute myeloid leukemia, ferroptosis, heightened mitochondrial function, nano‐aluminum adjuvant

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly aggressive and refractory hematological malignancy [1]. Standard‐of‐care involves a “7 + 3” induction chemotherapy regimen, which combines cytarabine (AraC) with an anthracycline [2]. However, resistance to chemotherapy, particularly to AraC, remains a major barrier to achieving effective and sustained treatment responses in AML patients. Restoring the chemosensitivity of AraC‐resistant (AraC‐R) leukemia cells thus represents a clinically valuable and economically feasible strategy to improve patient outcomes. Currently, extensive studies have revealed that chemotherapy resistance in AML is closely associated with heightened mitochondrial function [3, 4, 5]. Drug‐resistant AML cells exhibit enhanced mitochondrial‐endoplasmic reticulum contacts (MERC) and elevated oxidative phosphorylation [6, 7]. This metabolic rewiring indicates that chemotherapeutic stress drives AML cells to rely heavily on mitochondrial activity for survival [8]. As such, targeting this mitochondrial reprogramming offers a promising avenue to overcome chemoresistance and restore responsiveness to AraC.

Mitochondria are the primary sites of cellular energy production, with oxidative respiration playing a central role in ATP generation [9]. Notably, AraC treatment has been reported to enhance mitochondrial metabolism, leading to elevated production of mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) that may overwhelm the antioxidant defenses of drug‐resistant AML cells. Transition metal ions such as Cu2+ and Fe2+ can further promote ROS accumulation through Fenton‐type reactions, thereby exacerbating oxidative stress. Increasing evidence highlights the pivotal role of metal homeostasis in regulating leukemic cell fate and therapeutic response. For instance, iron chelators or inhibitors targeting iron metabolism‐related proteins can induce differentiation, apoptosis, and cell‐cycle arrest, whereas excessive intracellular iron triggers ferroptosis [10, 11]. Although iron‐based therapeutic strategies have shown promise in leukemia treatment, their clinical application is constrained by iron's dual functions, potential impairment of normal hematopoiesis caused by prolonged chelation, off‐target toxicity arising from non‐selective iron overload, and the difficulty of precisely modulating intracellular Fe2+ levels in vivo due to stringent systemic iron homeostasis [12, 13]. In contrast, copper offers distinct advantages as a therapeutic trigger for redox‐mediated cell death. A recent study has demonstrated that cells highly dependent on mitochondrial respiration are approximately 1000‐fold more sensitive to copper‐induced cell death than cells relying less on this pathway [14]. Moreover, downregulation of ALR and COX17—proteins involved in mitochondrial copper homeostasis—leads to increased levels of free Cu2+ and impairs leukemia stem cell self‐renewal [15]. Moreover, copper‐induced ROS generation is highly dependent on its redox cycling between Cu2+ and Cu+, a process critically facilitated by the FDX1 protein [16, 17]. Therefore, these findings suggest that inducing intracellular Cu2+ overload represents a more potent strategy for targeting AraC‐R cells. However, efficient Cu2+ delivery to AraC‐R cells remains challenging, as cellular uptake is largely mediated by the high‐affinity copper transporter SLC31A1 (CTR1) [18], which limits rapid intracellular accumulation of free Cu2+. Therefore, innovative delivery systems are needed to bypass this bottleneck and achieve effective Cu2+ overload in AraC‐R cells.

Nano‐aluminum adjuvant (NA), re‐engineered from clinical AlOOH and Mg(OH)2 and sharing a similar chemical composition to the anti‐gastric disease Talcid, has been widely employed for cancer immunotherapy [19, 20, 21]. Upon internalization by tumor cells, NA's inherent weak alkalinity blocks autophagic flux by inhibiting autophagolysosome acidification, thereby inducing tumor cell apoptosis [22, 23]. Importantly, the metal ion composition of NA is highly tunable, allowing substitution with other divalent or trivalent metal ions [24, 25]. For instance, Zn2+‐doped NA increases cytosolic Zn2+ levels, promoting mitochondrial ROS accumulation and dysfunction, which triggers immunogenic cell death via activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway [26, 27]. A similar effect has been observed with Mn2+‐doped NA, which also activates the STING pathway, leading to enhanced type I interferon secretion [28]. NA also exhibits efficient endosomal escape and mitochondrial localization, making it an ideal platform for quick Cu2+ delivery and mitochondrial enrichment [29, 30].

This study aims to overcome AraC resistance in AraC‐R cells by targeting heightened mitochondrial function via inducing mitochondrial copper overload. As shown in Scheme 1a, we developed a Cu2+‐doped NA (CuNA) by partially replacing Mg2+ with Cu2+, and further coated it with AML membranes (CuNA@M) to enable selective targeting of AraC‐R cells in vivo [31]. During internalization by AML cells, CuNA@M effectively dissolves in acidic endosomes/lysosomes and then delivers Cu2+ to the cytosol via endosomal/lysosomal escape. This process quickly enables cellular Cu2+ overload, which disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential, elevates ROS levels, and leads to mitochondrial dysfunction (pathway I in Scheme 1B). Notably, combination therapy with AraC synergistically amplifies ferroptosis by downregulating HMG‐CoA reductase (HMGCR) and glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) (pathway II in Scheme 1B). This dual‐targeting strategy positions CuNA@M as a promising therapeutic platform for restoring chemosensitivity and improving outcomes in drug‐resistant AML.

The IV injected CuNA@M nanoparticles target AML tumor cells and are endocytosed via endosomal/lysosomal escape, in which the nanoparticles are partially dissolved to quickly release a high level of free Cu2+ in the cytosol. (I) Overloaded Cu2+ ions in mitochondria increase ROS production and cause mitochondrial dysfunction; (II) Overloaded Cu2+ ions in cytosol downregulate the expression of HMGCR and GPX4; both sensitize the drug‐resistant AML cells to AraC chemotherapy.

Results

Cu2+ Increases Chemotherapy Sensitivity of Drug‐Resistant AML Cells

The transcriptome of AraC‐R cells (which survived AraC treatment) in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database was analyzed, and the gene sets related to mitochondrial functions were examined by comparing with AraC‐S cells (without AraC treatment). As shown in Figure 1A, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) reveals significant enrichment of mitochondrial‐related pathways in AraC‐R cells, as previously reported [32]. Previous studies have demonstrated that cells relying on mitochondrial respiration are approximately 1000‐fold more sensitive to Cu2+‐induced cell death [14], which may be attributed to excessive Cu2+ ions that induce the accumulation of mitochondrial ROS and cause mitochondrial dysfunction [33]. Notably, GSEA results also revealed that the cellular homeostasis, response, and binding capacity of Cu2+ in AraC‐R cells were significantly increased (demonstrated by increased enrichment score), while the detoxification of Cu2+ in AraC‐R cells was significantly decreased (Figures 1B–E). Supportively, the expression of mitochondrial respiratory protein COXIV in AraC‐R was found to be significantly higher than that in AraC‐S cells (Figure 1F). Concurrently, the expression of mitochondrial [2Fe‐2S] ferredoxins ferredoxin 1 (FDX1, a key regulator of cuproptosis) and the transmembrane protein SLC31A1 (regulating copper homeostasis) in AraC‐R cells was significantly upregulated when compared to AraC‐S cells, whereas ATP7B (encoding copper‐transporting ATPase 2) expression remained unchanged (Figures 1G–I). These data suggest that AraC‐R cells may have higher sensitivity to Cu2+, and thus elevation of Cu2+ in cells may serve as a potential strategy to alleviate the drug‐resistance of AraC‐R cells.

To verify this hypothesis, both AraC‐S and AraC‐R cells were treated with Cu2+ at concentrations from 1 to 100 µg mL−1. As shown in Figure 1I, the cell viability analysis revealed that AraC‐R cells (IC50 = 6.5 µg mL−1 of Cu2+) exhibited higher sensitivity to Cu2+ than AraC‐S cells (IC50 = 30 µg mL−1 of Cu2+). More AraC‐R cells started to die at 5 µg mL−1 of Cu2+ and continued until 40 µg mL−1 in comparison with AraC‐S cells (Figure 1I). On the basis of this, the impact of Cu2+ on cell viability was further analyzed in the presence of AraC. As shown in Figure 1J and Figure S1, AraC treatment (0.5 µm) dramatically decreased the cell survival rate of AraC‐S cells to 58.7%, and the value decreased to 31.8% in the presence of Cu2+ (20 µg mL−1). In contrast, 71.3% of AraC‐R cells survived in a higher concentration of AraC (1.0 µm), but the addition of Cu2+ (20 µg mL−1) dramatically decreased the cell viability to 22.0%, showing a good synergistic effect (Figure 1K; Figure S1 and Table S4). Therefore, Cu2 + ion is an ideal candidate to reverse the drug‐resistance of AraC‐R cells and amplify the chemotherapy of AML.

CuNA Amplifies the Chemotherapy of AraC‐R Cells

Although the free Cu2+ ion has been shown to enhance AraC‐R cell sensitivity to chemotherapy to a certain degree, increasing the cellular Cu2+ level using appropriate vehicles is believed to further amplify this efficacy. To achieve this aim, NanoAlum (NA) was selected as a template to carry different amounts of Cu2+ ions, as NA can be effectively internalized by tumor cells, escape from endosomes/lysosomes, release Cu2+ ions, and target mitochondria [25, 34]. As shown in Table S1, all CuxNA nanoparticles were successfully prepared with the amount of Cu2+ from 4.3% to 16.1% (w/w). Doping Cu2+ slightly impacted the particle size and morphology of the resultant CuxNA, with Z‐average particle size ranging from 25 to 30 nm (Figure 2A,B; Table S1). Meanwhile, all nanoparticles exhibited a positively charged surface (zeta potential of 35–38 mV) and maintained good dispersity (Figure 2B; Table S1). The XRD patterns confirmed that all CuxNA nanoparticles were well crystallized with a high purity of NA‐type phase (Figure 2C). Further, the elemental composition and spatial distribution of the as‐synthesized NanoAlum were investigated by transmission electron microscopy coupled with energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (TEM‐EDS). Elemental mapping images clearly demonstrated the homogeneous co‐localization of Cu, Mg, and Al (Figure S2A); The spectrum exhibited distinct characteristic X‐ray emission peaks at approximately 8.04 keV (Cu Kα), 1.25 keV (Mg Kα), and 1.49 keV (Al Kα), confirming the presence of Cu, Mg, and Al (Figure S2B). The full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the (003) peak was wider for Cu3NA and Cu4NA, as a high portion of Cu2+ incorporated into the hydroxide layers may distort the lattice and thus reduce crystallinity [35].

Next, the cellular uptake of CuxNA was evaluated by using FITC‐labeled Cu1NA. As depicted in Figure 2D, the AraC‐R AML cells took up Cu1NA nanoparticles (containing 5 µg mL−1 of FITC) very quickly, with sustained uptake over an extended period, reaching a peak at 48 h. Quantitatively, the intracellular Cu2+ concentration upon Cu1NA treatment (100 µg mL−1 of Cu1NA containing 4 µg mL−1 of Cu2+) was about 3.2‐fold higher than that following free Cu2+ treatment (4 µg mL−1 of Cu2+) for 12–24 h in HL60‐R cells (Figure 2E), which was consistent with data collected for THP‐1‐R and MV4‐11‐R cells (Figure S3A,B). Furthermore, a 24‐h treatment of Cu1NA significantly induced the depletion of FDX1 protein, compared to the control and free Cu2+ group (Figure S4), indicating that Cu1NA effectively caused rapid intracellular overload of Cu2+ ions.

Given that increased cellular Cu2+ level greatly elevated the sensitivity of AraC‐R cells to AraC (Figure 1J,K), the survival of AraC‐R cells was further evaluated after treatment with CuxNA in the presence of AraC. Notably, CuxNA treatment alone only slightly induced cell death (approximately 5%–11%) in a Cu2+ concentration dependent manner (i.e., 4 to 16 µg mL−1 of Cu2+ in CuxNA) (Figures 2F‐G). In sharp contrast, a combination of CuxNA and AraC (5 µm) significantly increased the apoptosis of AraC‐R cells, particularly for Cu3NA‐ and Cu4NA‐treated groups, as reflected by the combination index (CI) of 1.27 and 2.15 (Table S5). However, the combination of CuxNA with 2 µm AraC just generated additive inhibition effects on drug‐resistant AML cells (Figure S5A). Consistently, CuxNA synergized with AraC to induce greater cell death in the drug‐resistant MV4‐11 cell line as well, with CI values being 1.61 and 1.70 for Cu2NA and Cu4NA, respectively (Figure S5B). These data collectively indicate that CuxNA nanoparticles efficiently sensitize drug‐resistant cells to AraC by delivering much more Cu2+ into the cells. Based on the result that higher Cu2+ overload is correlated with stronger anti‐tumor effects (Figure 2F), we selected Cu4NA (briefly as CuNA below otherwise specified) to do the tests in the following experiments.

CuNA Enhances AraC Inhibition of AraC‐R Cell Growth in Mouse Models

To achieve active targeting to tumor cells, the surface of Cu4NA was coated with cell membranes derived from AraC‐R AML cells (CuNA@M) according to our previous reports [31, 36]. As shown in Figure S6A, the prepared CuNA@M had an average particle size of approximately 100 nm and a zeta potential of −22.5 mV. The transmission electron microscopy image further confirmed that the nanoparticles retained the typical hexagonal morphology of NA (Figure S6B). FTIR spectroscopy confirmed membrane coating via detection of characteristic ‐OPO3 2− vibrations at 1089 cm−1 (Figure S6C). Notably, despite the increase in particle size, the membrane coating significantly enhanced long‐term colloidal stability in biological media, keeping the hydrodynamic diameter consistently around 100 nm (Figure S6D,E). Furthermore, CuNA@M nanoparticles were taken up by HL60‐R cells more efficiently than uncoated CuNA (Figure 3A; Figure S7), due to their enhanced targeting capability, which thus more readily causes cellular Cu2+ ion overload. CuNA@M exhibited minimal toxicity to bone marrow stromal cells and macrophages at the concentration of 100 µg mL−1, but significantly induced HL60‐R cell death (Figure 3B; Figure S8), particularly in combination with 1 µm of AraC, resulting in nearly total HL60‐R cell death within 48 h.

On this basis, the therapeutic efficacy of CuNA@M was further validated in tumor‐bearing mice established by subcutaneous inoculatiion of HL60‐R cells. The treatment regimen consisted of two courses (Figure 3C). The first course was started with intravenous (iv) injection of CuNA@M at day 0 and followed by iv injections of 3 consecutive doses of AraC at day 1, 2, and 3, and the second one was repeated from day 7–10. Monitoring the tumor volume revealed that the combination of AraC and CuNA@M significantly inhibited tumor growth compared to the groups treated with CuNA@M or AraC alone (Figure 3D; Figure S9A,B). The combination also conferred notable therapeutic benefits, including the reduction of spleen swelling (Figure 3E), decreased infiltration of immune cells into the spleen (Figure 3F). Further analysis of tumor proliferation marker Ki67 and apoptosis marker Caspase‐3 revealed that the combined treatment significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis (Figure 3G,H). The body weight indicated that these treatments had no significant effect on the body weight of the tumor‐bearing mice (Figure S10).

Furthermore, an orthotopic AML model was also established using HL60‐R cells to evaluate the treatment efficacy of drug‐resistant AML. The combination treatment significantly improved the survival of tumor‐bearing mice at day 16 (Figure 3I). The detection of the proportion of tumor cells in the peripheral blood of tumor‐bearing mice after one course of treatment also showed that combination treatment significantly reduced the proportion of hCD45‐positive cells, much lower than treatment with AraC alone (Figure 3J‐K). The combination treatment group also significantly reduced splenic tumor infiltration and attenuated splenomegaly (Figure 3L,M; Figure S11), and did not cause substantial damage to the major organs (Figure S12). Notably, in vivo distribution analysis revealed that CuNA@M showed greater accumulation in splenic tissue than CuNA@BSA, which may be crucial for reducing splenic tumor burden (hCD45‐positive cells) (Figure S13A). Furthermore, long‐term monitoring of healthy mice indicated that CuNA@M circulated in the blood for extended periods, yet its injection does not induce significant alterations in complete blood count parameters (Figure S13B–E). These findings indicate that combining CuNA@M with AraC enhances chemotherapeutic efficacy against drug‐resistant AML cells in both subcutaneous and orthotopic mouse models.

CuNA Sensitizes AraC Cytotoxicity by Regulating Ferroptosis

We then conducted RNA sequencing analysis on cells treated with CuNA in combination with AraC to elucidate the potential mechanisms underlying synergistic effects. Principal component analysis revealed significant transcriptomic differences across treatment groups (PBS, NA, CuNA, AraC, and AraC + CuNA combination), enabling subsequent identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Figure 4A,B). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis based on these DEGs further identified that significantly enriched pathways included mineral absorption, ferroptosis, and phagosomes in the CuNA or combined treatment groups (Figure 4C–F). This finding suggests that CuNA significantly regulated lipid peroxidation and cellular phagocytic activity. KEGG enrichment analysis comparing the NA and PBS groups also revealed enrichment of phagosome‐related genes in the NA‐treated cells (Figure S14A,B). This indicates that NA‐mediated regulation of phagosome activity may be linked to its ability to neutralize lysosomal acidity, as NA nanoparticles have been reported to disrupt the acidic lysosomes [37]. In contrast, no significant changes in ferroptosis‐related gene expression were observed in the NA‐treated group, suggesting that CuNA had a distinct role in regulating ferroptosis, attributable to the role of overloaded Cu2+.

We further evaluated the effect of CuxNA on ferroptosis in HL60‐R cells. Treatment with Cu x NA elevated the mitochondrial ROS levels and depleted intracellular GSH (Figure 5A,B; Figure S16), leading to a reduction in the quantity of functional mitochondria (Figure 5C; Figure S15) and loss of mitochondrial membrane potential (Figure 5D,E). Furthermore, we assessed the mitochondrial metabolic state of the cells by measuring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) following Cu4NA treatment. The results showed that Cu4NA treatment significantly reduced both OCR and ECAR in drug‐resistant cellscompared with the control and NA‐treated groups (Figure S17A–D), indicating that Cu4NA simultaneously suppressed mitochondrial respiration and glycolysis in drug‐resistant cells. Lipid peroxidation (LPO) assay further showed that Cu4NA treatment increased cellular LPO levels, and this increase was more than 2‐fold in the presence of AraC compared to that with AraC treatment alone (Figure 5F). Furthermore, the inhibition of lipid peroxidation by Ferrostatin‐1 (Fer‐1) significantly attenuated cell death induced by the combination of Cu4NA and AraC (Figure 5G,H). Interestingly, regardless of whether Cu4NA was added or not, the intracellular ferrous ion concentration increased significantly under AraC treatment (Figure 5I). Together with the observation that AraC alone cannot cause a significant increase in LPO, we speculate that Cu4NA may have a unique, undiscovered pro‐ferroptosis mechanism, as discussed shortly. Collectively, these data indicate that the combination of Cu4NA and AraC amplifies ferroptosis in AraC‐R AML cells.

CuNA Downregulates HMGCR‐GPX4 Expression

To clarify the distinct mechanisms for inducing ferroptosis, we conducted a comparative analysis between the CuNA and NA treatment groups. GSEA was applied to investigate the molecular mechanisms triggered by CuNA. Compared to NA, Cu4NA activated gene sets associated with copper homeostasis and ferroptosis, while inhibiting mitochondrial complex IV assembly (Figure S18A). CuNA also inhibited cholesterol biosynthesis and metabolism (Figure S18B). Furthermore, trend analysis based on the mFuzz R package revealed the gene expression trends in PBS, NA, and CuNA groups. As shown in Figure 6A, the genes in Cluster 2 were significantly downregulated in the CuNA group but not changed in PBS and NA groups, indicating that these genes may be specifically regulated by CuNA. Enrichment analysis of these genes (Cluster 2) further showed that mitochondrial function and cholesterol biosynthesis may be the main targets of CuNA (Figure 6B).

HMGCR is the rate‐limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, and its overexpression is positively correlated with AraC resistance in AML [38, 39]. Studies have shown that HMGCR upregulation elevates GPX4 expression, thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in cells [40]. Consistently, gene correlation analysis using an AML gene expression dataset confirmed a positive correlation between HMGCR and GPX4 expression (Figure 6C). As further shown in Figure 6D,E, AraC‐R cells expressed higher levels of HMGCR compared to the corresponding sensitive cells, and GPX4 levels were also significantly elevated. Remarkably, CuNA treatment downregulated both HMGCR and GPX4 levels (Figure 6F; Figure S19A,B). When free Cu2+ ions were used to treat AraC‐R cells, we observed that low concentrations of copper ions promoted the upregulation of HMGCR and GPX4, while high concentrations inhibited the expression of both proteins (Figure 6G). Concurrently, low concentrations of Cu2+ ions minimally affected ROS production, whereas higher concentrations (8 and 16 µg mL−1) significantly increased ROS generation (Figure S20A,B). These results further support that it is Cu2+ overload that induces the suppression of HMGCR and GPX4, thereby promoting ROS production. Treating with the HMGCR inhibitor simvastatin significantly inhibited GPX4 expression (Figure 6H) and also sensitized drug‐resistant cells to AraC cytotoxicity, including HL60‐R, THP‐1R, and MV4‐11‐R cells (Figure 6I–K; Figure S21). These observations collectively indicate that CuNA induces the sensitivity of resistant AML cells to ferroptosis by inhibiting the expression of HMGCR‐GPX4 (Figure 6L).

Discussion

Prior studies have highlighted the potential of targeting mitochondria to overcome chemotherapy resistance in acute myeloid leukemia (AML). However, the development of effective mitochondrial targeting drugs remains a significant challenge. In this study, we demonstrate that CuNA offers a novel strategy to counter AraC resistance in AML by inducing cellular Cu2+ overload. Utilizing the acid‐responsive and rapid cellular uptake properties of NA nanomaterials [23, 41], we achieved cellular Cu2+ overload via quick release of Cu2+ ions from endosomes/lysosomes during endocytosis. This strategy effectively induces mitochondrial dysfunction and amplifies ferroptosis in drug‐resistant cells, thereby sensitizing them to the cytotoxic effects of AraC, effectively inhibiting the AML proliferation and promoting AML apoptosis in both in vitro and in vivo tests.

The pan‐programmed cell death‐related genes index based on machine learning and the predictive model of AML cell drug sensitivity have found that drug‐resistant cells have a stronger cuproptosis/ferroptosis index [42]. Thus, combining a traditional chemotherapy drug with an agent that promotes cuproptosis/ferroptosis could enhance the killing of resistant AML cells. Consistent with the results of our reanalysis of RNA‐seq data, AraC treatment enhanced the cells’ binding to Cu2+ and reduced the detoxification of Cu2+ (Figure 1C). Thus, causing Cu2+ overload in drug‐resistant AML cells may have the ability to restore chemosensitivity to AraC. However, under normal physiological conditions, Cu2+ homeostasis is tightly controlled by transporters, like CTR1, ATP7A, and ATP7B, making it challenging to achieve therapeutic cellular Cu2+ overload through free Cu2+ supplementation alone, particularly in vivo models. To meet this demand, we prepared CuNA by incorporating Cu2+ ions into the hydroxide layers. CuNA can be rapidly engulfed by cells through receptor‐mediated endocytosis [43, 44]. Thereby causing a burst of Cu2+ overload in cells after endosomal/lysosomal escape [45]. Our results showed that within 12–24 h, CuNA nanoparticles were quickly taken up by AML drug‐resistant cells, readily causing 4‐fold higher intracellular copper accumulation compared to free Cu2+ ions (Figure 2D; Figure S3). Note that the intracellular copper accumulation may be even higher when CuNA was coated with homotypic targeting AraC‐R cell membrane (Figure S5). This membrane coating not only improved targeting efficiency but also reduced CuNA cytotoxicity toward hematopoietic support cells, such as bone marrow stromal cells and macrophages, without significant adverse effects on normal hematopoiesis (Figure S13). As is well known, FDX1 is a key regulator of cuproptosis, primarily through its role in reducing Cu2+ to the more toxic Cu+ form [46, 47]. Conversely, excessive intracellular Cu2+ can rapidly deplete FDX1 protein within a short time frame. Our data showed that CuNA induced a marked reduction in FDX1 protein (Figure S4), suggesting that CuNA effectively triggers acute intracellular Cu2+ overload. Based on our collected data (Figures 1F and 2D,F; Figure S3), it is estimated that the free Cu2+ overload threshold in AML cells would be around 50 fg/cell at 24 h, and Cu3NA and Cu4NA could well maintain this overload in AML cells for a long time through sustained dissolution and continuously cause mitochondrial damage, as explained in (SD2).

Interestingly, CuNA showed low cytotoxicity to normal cells, but cells with high mitochondrial metabolism are highly sensitive to Cu2+, which together endows CuNA with low toxic side effects. The CCK‐8 assay, which measures cell proliferation via mitochondrial activity, revealed that although copper ions inhibited the proliferation of AML cells, they did not induce significant cell death in the absence of AraC (Figure 1I–K). Consistently, CuNA alone was not markedly cytotoxic to drug‐resistant AML cells (Figure 2E,F). An important mechanistic insight from these results is that copper ions primarily function as cytostatic agent, arresting proliferation rather than directly inducing cell death. However, when combined with AraC, CuNA significantly increased the death of AraC‐resistant cells (Figure 2E,F). This synergistic effect is mainly resulted from intracellular Cu2+ overload, which significantly induces mitochondrial damage and copper‐dependent ROS generation (Figure 5B–E), ultimately leading to ferroptosis (Figure 5F,G). This unique mechanism effectively accounts for the low toxicity profile of CuNA, while enabling targeted eradication of drug‐resistant AML cells. Thus, our findings provide a promising and relatively safe therapeutic strategy for mitochondria‐enhanced diseases based on copper‐containing materials.

Mechanistically, our study demonstrates that CuNA is critical in modulating ferroptosis through inhibiting HMGCR‐GPX4 expression. High cholesterol levels are implicated in tumor progression and enhance the resistance to ferroptosis by promoting GPX4 activity [48, 49]. HMGCR, as the rate‐limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, is reported to be upregulated in AraC‐R AML cells [39]. Moreover, HMGCR positively regulates GPX4 protein expression [50, 51]. RNA‐sequencing data reveal that CuNA downregulates some key enzymes for cholesterol synthesis, including HMGCR, which may directly impact ferroptosis resistance by influencing cholesterol biosynthesis. This downregulation, in turn, reduced the expression of GPX4, a key enzyme that protects cells from lipid peroxidation. The simultaneous suppression of HMGCR and GPX4 (e.g., by CuNA) effectively induces ferroptosis, thereby amplifying the cytotoxic effects of AraC. Interestingly, our results revealed that AraC alone significantly increased the intracellular accumulation of ferrous ions, yet it failed to induce ferroptosis in drug‐resistant cells (Figure 5G), likely due to the elevated expression of GPX4 in these cells, as schematically explained in Figure 6K. CuNA internalization disrupted the ferroptosis resistance maintained by GPX4 and enhanced the cytotoxicity of AraC to AML drug‐resistant cells. As a result, the in vivo efficacy of CuNA was significantly improved in a subcutaneous and an orthotopic tumor mouse model using AML‐derived cell membrane‐coated CuNA for homotypic targeting. Our findings thus suggest that CuNA represents a promising candidate for overcoming drug resistance through the combined mechanisms of mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis. It is worth noting that this work highlights the therapeutic potential of copper‐based multifunctional systems for treating malignancies characterized by mitochondrial dependency [52]. In the future, systematic investigations into the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of the CuNA@M system will be essential. Considering the specific pathological features of the AML model and the rapid sequestration of most nanomedicines by macrophages [53], data regarding the impact of CuNA@M on T cell responses and the crosstalk between systemic immune cells remain limited. Addressing these aspects in subsequent studies will be instrumental in advancing and refining the preclinical evaluation of the CuNA@M platform.

Conclusion

In summary, our study developed CuNA nanoparticles as a dual‐action therapeutic agent that potentiates AraC efficacy by simultaneously promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis in AML drug‐resistant cells. CuNA nanoparticles are quickly taken up by AML cells, readily achieving intracellular Cu2+ overload. This continuous overload causes mitochondrial dysfunction and regulates lipid peroxidation, providing a unique advantage over conventional therapies. By downregulating cholesterol biosynthesis and disrupting antioxidant defenses, CuNA offers a multifaceted approach to overcoming chemotherapy resistance in AML treatment.

Materials and Methods

Materials

MgCl2·6H2O, AlCl3·6H2O, NaOH, and CuCl2·2H2O were purchased from Aladdin Chemicals (Shanghai, China). RPMI medium 1640 basic, fetal bovine serum (FBS), bovine serum albumin (BSA), and penicillin streptomycin were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). Cytarabine, Ferrostatin‐1, HKSOX‐1m, and phorbol 12‐myristate 13‐acetate (PMA) were purchased from MCE (NJ, USA). MitoTracker Deep Red was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (MA, USA). JC‐1, FITC, and Calcein‐AM/PI kit were purchased from Solarbio (China). Phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Servicebio (China). Busulfan was purchased from MeilunBio (China).

Preparation and Characterization of CuNA

CuxNA nanomaterials were prepared via a co‐precipitation [54]. Briefly, a mixed salt solution (10 mL) containing 1 mmol of AlCl3·6H2O, (3.0‐0.2x) mmol of MgCl2·6H2O and 0.2x mmol of CuCl2·2H2O (x = 0, 1, 2, 3, and 4, Table S1) was added to 40 mL of NaOH solution (8 mmol) to precipitate under vigorous stirring in an iced bath. The range of x was determined according to the result reported previously [54]. After stirring for 30 min, the suspension was centrifuged at 4750 g for 5 min, and the slurry was washed twice with deionized water. The precipitate was then dispersed in 20 mL of deionized water at room temperature. After a standstill for a few days with occasional shaking, a homogeneously dispersed CuxNA suspension was obtained.

The hydrodynamic particle size and zeta potential of CuxNA were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS; Malvern, UK). For transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging, a diluted suspension of CuxNA was dropped onto a copper grid and allowed to air‐dry. The images were captured using a JEOL JEM‐F200 TEM. The X‐ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of CuxNA were collected using a Rigaku MiniFlex 600, scanning the 2θ range from 5° to 90° at a scan rate of 5° min−1 with Cu Kα radiation. To determine the Cu2+ content in CuxNA, 0.1 m HCl was added to the CuxNA solution (1 mg mL−1) to release all bound Cu2+ ions, which were then quantified using a Copper (Cu) Colorimetric Assay Kit (Elabscience, E‐BC‐K300‐M).

Cell Uptake and Apoptosis Assay

Cell Lines

Human acute myeloid leukemia cell lines HL60 (RRID: CVCL_0002), THP‐1 (RRID: CVCL_0006), and MV4‐11 (RRID: CVCL_0064) were purchased from CellCook (Guangzhou, China) and cultured in RPMI‐1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Human bone marrow stromal cell line HS‐5 (RRID: CVCL_3720) was purchased from CellCook (Guangzhou, China) and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% non‐essential amino acid. All cell lines used in this study were authenticated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling and confirmed to be mycoplasma‐free. AraC‐R AML cell lines, including HL60 (HL60‐R), THP‐1 (THP‐1‐R), and MV4‐11 (MV4‐11‐R) cells were gradually induced by culturing under stress at the sublethal drug concentration for at least 8 weeks. All cells were cultured in a humidified incubator at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Cell Uptake

Cells (HL60‐R) were seeded in 6‐well plates with 5 × 105 cells per well. FITC‐CuNA nanoparticles were added to a final concentration of 50 µg mL−1 and allowed for cell uptake for 4–24 h. Then, cells were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min), and the cell uptake was evaluated by flow cytometry through the FITC channel.

Cell Apoptosis Assay

Following the manufacturer's instructions, cell death was detected using the Annexin V‐FITC/PI kit. Cells were seeded at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells per well in 6‐well plates overnight. The seeded cells were treated with Cu2+ (4 µg mL−1), NA (100 µg mL−1) and CuxNA (100 µg mL−1) for 4 h, respectively, and the drug (AraC) was then added to the system to a final concentration of 5 µm. After 24 h incubation, the cell death assay was performed with flow cytometry.

Detection of Mitochondrial Functions

Mitochondrial ROS Detection

Twenty‐four hours after treatment with CuxNA or NA, cells cultured in 6‐well plates were collected by centrifugation at room temperature and resuspended in 1 mL of 1 × PBS buffer. Cells were incubated with HKSOX (MCE, USA) at the working concentration (5 µm) for 15 min at 37°C, followed by another centrifugation. The cells were then washed twice with 1 × PBS buffer. Mitochondrial ROS levels were detected and analyzed using flow cytometry.

Mitochondrial Quality Detection

Similar to the cell treatment for the mitochondrial ROS detection, the quality of mitochondria was detected by MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher, USA) using flow cytometry according to the instructions, with the working concentration of 50 nm.

Mitochondrial Membrane Potential Detection (MMP)

For MMP detection, cells were analyzed using JC‐1 according to the manufacturer's instructions. First, cells pretreated with CuxNA were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) at room temperature and resuspended in 0.5 mL of culture medium. The JC‐1 working solution (5 µm) was then added, and the cells were incubated at 37°C for 20 min. After incubation, cells were collected by centrifugation (300 g, 5 min) and washed twice with JC‐1 staining buffer. Finally, cells were resuspended in 0.5 mL of JC‐1 staining buffer and analyzed by flow cytometry.

Analysis of Transcriptome Using RNA Sequencing (RNA‐Seq)

RNA Library Construction and Sequencing

Total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by purification through poly‐T oligo–attached magnetic beads. The mRNA was then fragmented into smaller pieces using divalent cations. Subsequently, the cleaved RNA fragments were reverse‐transcribed to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA), following the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation v2 protocol (catalog numbers RS‐122‐2001 and RS‐122‐2002, Illumina, USA). The resulting paired‐end libraries had an average insert size of 300 ± 50 base pairs (bp). Finally, paired‐end sequencing was conducted on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 at LC Sciences, USA, in line with the manufacturer's guidelines.

Sequence and Primary Analysis

A cDNA library was constructed from pooled RNA of HL60‐R cells with the indicated treatment and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Using the Illumina paired‐end RNA sequencing (RNA‐seq) approach, we obtained a transcriptome sequence output. Prior to mapping, low‐quality reads were filtered out, including reads containing sequencing adaptors, sequencing primers, and nucleotides with a quality score below 20. After filtering, the high‐quality paired‐end reads per sample were retained.

RNA‐Seq Reads Mapping

Reference genome and gene annotation files were downloaded from genome websites. An index of the reference genome was built using Hisat2 v2.0.5, and paired‐end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Hisat2 v2.0.5. Hisat2 was selected as the mapping tool for that Hisat2 can generate a database of splice junctions based on the gene model annotation file, and thus provides a better mapping result than other non‐splice mapping tools. Following the generation of this final transcriptome, StringTie and edgeR were applied to quantify transcript expression levels.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) Enrichment Analysis

To investigate the biological functions and pathways associated with differentially expressed genes (DEGs), KEGG enrichment analysis was performed. DEGs were identified using edgeR with a significance threshold of |log2(fold change)| ≥ 1 and adjusted p‐value < 0.05. Gene identifiers were converted to KEGG Orthology (KO) terms using the clusterProfiler R package. Enrichment analysis was conducted via a hypergeometric test, and pathways with a Benjamini‐Hochberg‐adjusted p‐value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Results were visualized using dot plots and pathway topology maps to highlight enriched pathways.

Trend Analysis

To investigate the effect of CuNA on AraC‐R cells, we used the Mfuzz R package (version = 2.66.0) to visualize the gene expression trends in the PBS, NA, and CuNA groups. Six profiles were identified based on the gene expression patterns by analyzing the gene expression matrix.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

Through a search in the GEO database, the dataset GSE231914 was acquired in association with AraC resistance. Then, the gene set enrichment analysis on this dataset was conducted using GSEA software (version 4.3.2) based on gene sets retrieved from the molecular signature database available on the GSEA website (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp).

Preparation of Tumor Cell Membrane and Camouflage of CuxNA

Collection of Cellular Membrane (CM)

The AML cell membrane was extracted from HL60‐R cells according to the previous report [55]. Briefly, the cultured HL60‐R cells were collected by centrifugation at 1200 rpm for 5 min and washed with cooled PBS. Then, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of HM lysis buffer (1 mm EDTA, 0.25 m sucrose, 20 mm Hepes‐NaOH, and protease inhibitor cocktail) and incubated for 30 min in an ice bath. Afterward, the cells were lysed by ultrasound, and the lysis solution was centrifuged at 700 g for 10 min at 4°C twice to remove the cell nucleus and unbroken cells as the precipitate. After further centrifugation of the supernatant at 14 000 g for 30 min, the precipitate was collected as cell membrane fragments. The cell membrane fragments (AMLm) were stored in a −80°C refrigerator or directly used in subsequent experiments.

Camouflaging CuxNA with AMLm

First, we prepared the CuxNA@BSA nanoparticles, as previously reported [54, 56]. In brief, CuxNA (2 mg mL−1, 1 mL) was added dropwise to a stirred BSA solution (10 mg mL−1, 1 mL). After completing the dropwise addition, the system was placed in an ice bath and sonicated for 10 min. Subsequently, the BSA‐coated CuNA suspension was then mixed with the pre‐prepared cell membrane (AMLm, 1 mg mL−1, 100 µL) and sonicated in an ice bath for another 15 min. Finally, the AMLm‐cambouflaged CuxNA@BSA nanoparticles (CuxNA@M) were collected through centrifugation at 15 000 g for 10 min and re‐suspended in 0.5 mL saline.

Quantification of Lipid Peroxidation (LPO)

Levels of lipid peroxidation (LPO) in AML cells following CuxNA pretreatment were determined using a commercial LPO assay kit (Solarbio, BC5245, China) following the supplier's protocol. Briefly, cell lysates were prepared and combined with the detection reagent, followed by incubation at 100°C for 60 min in a heating block. Subsequent to ice‐cooling, a 200 µL aliquot of the supernatant was pipetted into a 96‐well plate, and absorbance readings at 532 nm and 600 nm were then obtained using a BioTek Epoch2 microplate reader (USA). Final LPO values for the cell samples were derived by referencing a standard curve plotting concentration against optical density (OD).

qRT‐PCR Analysis and Western Blotting

qRT‐PCR Analysis

Gene expression was quantified using a qRT‐PCR detection kit (TranGen Biotech, China). According to the manufacturer's instructions, total RNA was extracted from AML cells using TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, China). The RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (TranGen Biotech, China), following the protocol provided by the manufacturer. The qRT‐PCR reaction was set up in a 96‐well plate with 1 µL of cDNA, 10 µL of SYBR Green Master Mix (AQ601, TranGen Biotech, China), 1 µL of forward and reverse primers, and 7 µL of nuclease‐free water for a final volume of 20 µL per well. The plate was loaded into a real‐time PCR machine (Roche). The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the 2^‐ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as the internal control. The primary information is listed in Table S2.

Western Blotting

For protein expression detection, cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Bio, China). The cell lysates were then mixed with loading buffer, loaded onto an SDS‐PAGE gel, and transferred to a PVDF membrane (Millipore). The PVDF membrane was blocked with 5% non‐fat milk to prevent non‐specific binding and incubated with the indicated primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. HRP‐linked secondary antibodies were applied to bind to the primary antibodies and amplify their signals. Finally, the blot signals were visualized using Omni‐ECL (Epizyme, China) with a chemiluminescence analyzer (Baygene, China). The information of regents used in Western blotting is listed in Table S3.

Detection of Oxygen Consumption Rate (OCR) and Extracellular Acidification Rate (ECAR)

S.NEST (CYTENA BPS, TW), a high‐throughput culture system integrated with a real‐time metabolism analyzer, was used for real‐time dissolved oxygen (DO)/pH monitoring and OCR/ECAR calculation. HL‐60‐R cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well in a 24‐well S.NEST sensor plate (#PX24A005 and #PX24A006, CYTENA BPS, TW). After treatment with NA or Cu4NA (100 µg/mL), the S.NEST lid was placed onto the sensor plate and transferred to S.NEST and DO/pH monitoring at the mixing rate of 50 s cycle−1. The fluorescent signals of the pH and DO sensor spots in the microplate were detected by the camera module equipped in the S.NEST system, with which the data were collected every 10 min. All experiments were conducted under controlled conditions at 37°C and 5% CO2 to ensure an optimal cell culture environment. The method of analysis can be found in the Supporting Information.

In Vivo Study

Mice were maintained in individual ventilated cages and fed with autoclaved food and sterile water at the Laboratory Animal Center, Shenzhen Campus, Sun Yat‐sen University. The approved animal ethical number was SYSU‐IACUC‐2024‐002553.

For the subcutaneous tumor model, SCID mice were injected subcutaneously with HL60‐R cells (1 × 106) into the groin to establish the model. The modeled mice were then randomly divided into four groups, i.e., the saline group (Ctrl), CuNA@M group (25 mg kg−1), AraC group (50 mg kg−1), and combined treatment group (AraC+CuNA@M). The treatment contained 2 courses for 2 weeks. The tumor size was measured every day, and the growth rate (R) was calculated by comparing the tumor volume at any day (V = (length × width [2])/2) with that at day 0. The tumor and spleens were harvested at the end of the second course (D13 post treatment) and subjected to further H&E or Immunohistochemistry analysis. For the orthotopic model, SCID mice were intraperitoneally injected with Busulfan to further deplete immune cells, and 24 h later, HL60‐R cells (1 × 106) were injected into the tail vein to establish the model. On the seventh day post injection of tumor cells, the number of human CD45‐positive cells in the blood was measured by flow cytometry to confirm successful model establishment. The model animals were then randomly divided into 4 groups, followed by the same treatments used in the subcutaneous model for one course. The major organs, including spleen, kidney, liver, heart, and lung, were harvested at the end of the second course (D7 post treatment) and subjected to PFA for a 48 h fixation. Then these organs were sliced and stained with H&E to detect the potential pathological changes.

Statistical Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad PRISM version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc, California). To compare differences between groups, parametric tests (One‐way ANOVA with Bonferroni's multiple comparison test) were performed after confirmation of a normal distribution, or, where a normal distribution could not be confirmed, or sample size was small, non‐parametric Mann‐Whitney U tests or the Kruskal‐Wallis tests with a post‐hoc test were performed. For in vitro experiments, all n values refer to three independent experiments (n = 3), unless otherwise stated. In animal studies, n refers to the number of independent repeated samples, i.e., the number of different animals used. For evaluating the effect of drug synergy, we used the previously reported combination index (CI) [57]. A CI being around 1.0 means an additive effect of two therapeutics, and a CI > 1.2 means that these two therapeutics have a strong synergistic effect on cell death.
