Microcanonical Rate Constants With Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus in Eyringpy
Authors: Aura Gómez‐Heredia, Eugenia Dzib, Gabriel Merino
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus (RRKM) theory was implemented in Eyringpy to enable the computation of microcanonical rate constants, densities of states (DOS), and sums of states. Input can be provided either as experimental data or directly from electronic‐structure output files, requiring minimal user specification. The Stein–Rabinovitch algorithm was employed for accurate state counting, and tunneling corrections were introduced through the Eckart model to account for light‐particle transfer and narrow barriers. To validate the implementation, the DOS of hydrogen peroxide was evaluated with anharmonic‐vibrational corrections, and three gas‐phase reactions were analyzed: thermal decomposition of digermane, bromine‐atom loss from CH2BrO·, and hydrofluoric‐acid elimination from CF3CH3. The validation confirms that Eyringpy provides accurate and efficient RRKM calculations, providing reliable kinetic analyses of unimolecular reactions across a broad energy range.

Introduction

Unimolecular reactions involve the transformation of a single molecule without direct participation of other species, as exemplified by thermal decomposition or isomerization processes [1]. Early investigations sought to understand how such reactions could occur in the absence of intermolecular collisions. Two key questions emerged: how the required energy is acquired and how it is distributed within the molecule. In 1919, Perrin proposed infrared radiation as an activating source [2], but Langmuir showed these reactions also occur without light, ruling out this hypothesis [3]. Kinetic studies established that unimolecular gas‐phase reactions follow first‐order kinetics. However, early collision‐based theories predicted second‐order kinetics, contradicting experiments and failing to explain first‐order kinetics.

To address this inconsistency, Lindemann [4] introduced a model based on the steady‐state approximation (SSA), in which collisions transfer energy while preserving first‐order kinetics. This framework became the basis for the kinetic theory of unimolecular reactions. Hinshelwood later refined the model by introducing critical energy (i.e., the minimum energy required for a reaction) and vibrational degrees of freedom [5].

Building on these developments, the Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel (RRK) theory described molecules as sets of oscillators vibrating at a common frequency [6, 7] and introduced the microcanonical rate constant, kE. Marcus subsequently incorporated transition state theory (TST), formulating the RRKM (Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus) theory, which provides a more complete description of unimolecular reactions [8]. RRKM extends TST by accounting for the statistical distribution of internal energy among molecular degrees of freedom. While TST yields thermal rate constants, kT, RRKM is formulated in terms of internal energy and allows direct computation of kE. Importantly, from kE, both kT and the pressure‐dependent rate constants, kT,P, can be derived [9]. Accurate determination of kE is therefore crucial for reliable estimates of rate constants under varying pressure and temperature conditions.

RRKM theory is particularly relevant for systems in which reaction dynamics depend strongly on internal energy distribution or pressure. Drougas and Kosmas [10] analyzed the decomposition and isomerization of the CH2BrO· radical and found that the Br–O bond cleavage dominates at low energies, while H2 elimination becomes favored at higher energies, yielding more stable products. These results, consistent with experimental evidence, highlight the ability of RRKM theory to predict the energy‐dependent selectivity of competing pathways. Later, Dobbyn and coauthors [11] showed that RRKM theory also enables kinetic analysis over a wide pressure range, as in the dissociation of the HO2· radical. Because measurements at high pressures are often inaccessible, RRKM provides reliable rate constants under both high‐ and low‐pressure conditions.

Several programs are now available to compute kE in the gas phase using RRKM theory (Table 1). These implementations, developed in different programming languages, often incorporate tunneling corrections, typically through the one‐dimensional Eckart model. The most common methodologies for computing kE are direct‐state counting (DSC) algorithm and its extension, the Stein‐Rabinovitch algorithm (SR) (currently, the most accurate), and the inverse Laplace transform (ILT).

RRKM refers to a calibration with respect to the microcanonical ensemble.

In this work, RRKM theory was implemented in Eyringpy [12, 13, 25, 26] to compute microcanonical rate constants. Eyringpy is a modular Python‐based software package for computing thermochemical properties and reaction rate constants (in both gas‐phase and solution environments). It supports unimolecular and bimolecular reactions, using canonical partition functions, and computes rate constants via TST with Wigner, Eckart, and multidimensional tunneling corrections. In solution, it incorporates diffusion‐limited corrections (Collins–Kimball), Marcus theory for electron transfer, and pH‐dependent models. More advanced features (e.g., canonical variational TST and hindered rotor corrections) have been integrated in recent versions. The software is freely available for download and use at the official website: https://www.theochemmerida.org/eyringpy.

Microcanonical rate constants were computed using the SR algorithm [27]. The Eckart tunneling model was also included to account for reactions involving light‐particle transfer or narrow barriers, particularly at low temperatures. Consistent with the general design of Eyringpy, these calculations require very minimal input, namely electronic structure outputs of the reactant and transition state.

Rice–Ramsperger–Kassel–Marcus Theory

For a molecule to undergo fragmentation or isomerization, it must acquire sufficient internal energy distributed among its vibrational modes to enable dissociation or structural rearrangement. The RRKM theory provides a statistical framework for describing how internal energy is distributed across vibrational and rotational states in unimolecular reactions, allowing for the quantitative determination of energy‐dependent rate constants. Therefore, RRKM theory builds upon the SSA and collision theory [9].

The Lindemann mechanism postulates that a molecule X, after colliding with another molecule M (either another X or an inert gas), forms an energized molecule X* (I). This scheme accounts for the first‐order kinetics observed experimentally. In this model, an energized molecule can undergo deactivation through collisions (II) or, if it retains sufficient energy, decompose to form product Z (III) [4]. (I) X+M→k1X*+M (II) X*+M→k−1X+M (III) X*→k2Z

The rate constants for these steps are k1, k−1, and k2, respectively. The SSA assumes that the concentration of activated molecules X* (Equation 1) remains constant, leading to: (1) v=dZdt=k2X*, (2) X*=k1XMk−1M+k2.

From these relations, the general expression for the unimolecular rate constant, kuni, and its dependence on pressure P is obtained: (3) kuni=k∞1+k∞/k1P (4) k∞=k1k2k−1 where k∞ is the rate constant when the rate of collisional deactivation greatly exceeds that of decomposition (k2). Although this mechanism forms the basis of unimolecular reaction theory, it shows deviations when compared with experiment (Figure 1a).

Hinshelwood improved the Lindemann mechanism by introducing the concept of critical energy, E0, and vibrational degrees of freedom, s. Incorporating these into the expression for k1 gives: (5) k1=Z1s−1!E0RTs−1exp−E0RT, where Z1 is the collision rate, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature [6].

This modification provides a closer approximation to experimental data (Figure 1b). However, it requires empirical adjustments of the parameter s, leading to a trial‐and‐error approach. These limitations motivated the development of more refined models, such as the RRK theory [6].

In the RRK theory (Figure 1c), a molecule is represented as a set of s identical oscillators vibrating at the same frequency. The rate constant for product formation, previously denoted k2 and now expressed as kE, depends on the internal energy of the molecule. The energized molecule X* has total energy E=nhν, where n is the principal quantum number, h is the Planck's constant, and ν is the vibrational frequency. Dissociation requires that the critical oscillator (corresponding to the bond to be broken) accumulate at least no quanta, equivalent to the critical energy. The expression for kE is as follows: (6) kE=Dn−nons−1, where D is a proportionality factor. At the high‐pressure limit, the rate constant k∞ becomes: (7) k∞=Dexp−E0/kBT, which is consistent with the Arrhenius equation. However, the Arrhenius pre‐exponential is typically much larger than experimental values. To address this discrepancy, TST was incorporated, ultimately leading to the RRKM theory [8].

In RRKM theory (Figure 1d), the activated molecule X* can proceed through a transition state (TS), X‡, before forming products. X‡ is the fraction of quantum states of X* that result in reaction. So, the corresponding kE is as follows: (8) kE=χ‡χσextσext‡ge‡ge1hW‡E−E0ρE, where W‡E−E0 is the sum of the quantum states of the TS, and ρE is the density of states (DOS) of the reactant. The expression accounts for reaction path degeneracy [28] and includes corrections from rotational symmetry (σ, σ‡), optical isomers (χ, χ‡), and electronic degeneracy (ge, ge‡). These factors allow for the treatment of multiple energetically and kinetically equivalent reaction pathways.

RRKM theory is well‐suited for unimolecular reactions in which kinetics depend on the redistribution of internal energy. Its application, however, relies on several assumptions. First, it presumes rapid intramolecular vibrational energy redistribution, which may yield inaccurate rate constants when the energy is not statistically distributed. Second, it neglects non‐adiabatic effects, such as interactions between potential energy surfaces during electronic transitions [29]. Finally, it requires a well‐defined TS, which reduces accuracy for weakly bound TSs [30]. These limitations should be considered when applying the theory.

In Eyringpy, RRKM theory was implemented following the methods of Robinson and Holbrook [9]. Only pathways with a well‐defined first‐order saddle point on the potential energy surface (PES) are considered (i.e., tight TS) [31].

Densities and Sums of States

The sum of states, WE, is the number of quantum states with energy less than or equal to a given value E. The ρE, or DOS, corresponds to the number of quantum states per unit energy and is given by dW/dE. At low energies, WE and ρE show discrete behavior due to separated quantum levels, where at high energies both WE and ρE approach continuous and smooth functions because of increasing degeneracy.

Eyringpy employs the Stein–Rabinovitch (SR) extension of the Beyer–Swinehart (BS) algorithm to obtain exact counts of states under the separable degrees of freedom approximation. The BS algorithm was originally developed to compute the number of integer partitions, that is, the distinct ways an integer can be expressed as the sum of other integers [32]. In chemical applications, it is used to calculate the sum of vibrational states, ∑eυ=0EPeυ, where Peυ denotes the number of configurations in which a vibrational energy eυ is distributed among the vibrational modes of a polyatomic molecule, assuming harmonic oscillators.

The SR extension incorporates rotational as well as vibrational degrees of freedom, thus broadening the applicability of the method to unimolecular reaction kinetics [27]. Eyringpy computes both WE and ρE for systems treated with harmonic and anharmonic oscillators and rigid rotors. The corresponding quantum‐mechanical expressions are summarized in Table 2.

The maximum principal quantum number, nmax, defines the maximum energy level, where m is the particle mass and L the box length. The maximum energy is denoted as Emax. For the vibrational model, xe is the anharmonicity constant, μ the reduced mass, and μd2 the moment of inertia. In Eyringpy, the density and sum of states are divided into two regimes: a low‐energy region, where the finer spacing between energy levels enhances the precision of state counting, and a high‐energy region, optimized for computational efficiency. This partitioning allows accurate and efficient evaluation of kE across a broad energy range [18]. For large molecules or high energies, WE and DOS values can increase rapidly, potentially exceeding the numerical range of conventional data types. To prevent overflow, these quantities are calculated in logarithmic space, ensuring numerical stability and reliable results across all energy regimes.

Eyringpy supports two approaches: manual input of experimental or theoretical data, and automatic extraction from Gaussian 09 or 16 output files. For kE, calculations require data for the reactant, TS and products when using automatic extraction from electronic structure output files.

Manual input can be provided with the keywords “FREQ_x,” “DEG_FREQ_x,” “MOM_INERT_x,” “ANHARM_CONST_x,” and “PATH_DEG,” corresponding to vibrational frequencies, vibrational frequency degeneracies, moments of inertia, anharmonic constants, and path degeneracy of the reaction, respectively, where x indicates the species (REACT1 and TS). For tunneling effect in kE calculations, the TS imaginary vibrational frequency (negative frequency) must also be specified in the keyword “FREQ_TS”.

Automatic data extraction uses keywords that specify the output filenames of the reactant “REACT1,” transition state “TS,” and products “PRODn,” where n denotes the product number. To apply anharmonic corrections ‘ANH’ and rotations “ROT” corrections, the keyword “DEGREES_OF_FREEDOM” must also be included (Figure 2). These corrections require that both the reactant and TS output files contain the corresponding moments of inertia and anharmonic constants.

Further details on keywords and usage are available in the Eyringpy user manual.

This implementation enables accurate calculation of DOS for molecules and kE for unimolecular gas‐phase reactions. At present, hindered rotations are not supported, although this functionality is planned for future versions. In addition, the applicability of RRKM theory itself is restricted to reactions with a well‐defined transition state, sufficiently high barriers, and reaction times that are not excessively short. When these conditions are not met, the statistical assumptions of the theory may break down, leading to significant deviations in the calculated rate constants.

Tests

The performance of Eyringpy was evaluated first by computing the DOS of hydrogen peroxide using manually entered experimental data. Once the DOS was accurately reproduced, kE was evaluated for three unimolecular gas‐phase reactions: (a) thermal decomposition of digermane (Ge2H6), (b) decomposition of the bromomethoxy radical (CH2BrO·), and (c) elimination of hydrofluoric acid (HF) from 1,1,1‐trifluoroethane (CF3CH3). For these reactions, results obtained with Eyringpy were compared to those from Multiwell 2023 [36]. Vibrational and rotational contributions were included in all calculations. An energy spacing of 10 cm−1 was used in the low‐energy regime, except for Ge2H6 and H2O2, where a finer spacing of 5 cm−1 was applied. Electronic structure calculations were performed with Gaussian 16 Rev. C.01 [37].

Hydrogen Peroxide

H2O2 was used to validate the accuracy of DOS calculation from experimental data, including anharmonic vibrational corrections, but neglecting rotational contributions. Vibrational frequencies, degeneracies, anharmonicity constants, and bond lengths and angles (Figure 3) were taken from Dorofeeva et al. [38] Table S1 lists all data used for the DOS calculation. Anharmonic corrections were evaluated using a maximum energy of 8.0 × 104 cm−1, an energy spacing of 4.0 × 103 cm−1, and 3.0 × 103 points in the low‐energy regime.

In the low‐energy range (< 30 kcal·mol−1), DOS values differ by 0.5 to 1 unit compared to Multiwell results. Here, the discretization of vibrational states produces more pronounced jumps, especially in small systems, making fine energy spacing advisable to capture states accurately. At higher energies, the DOS levels approach a continuous function, reducing discrepancies to less than 0.2 units.

Overall, the comparison confirms that DOS calculations in Eyringpy are consistent with Multiwell across both energy regimes (Figure 3), validating the methodology. This establishes a reliable basis for subsequent kE calculations for chemical reactions.

[39], who performed calculations with Gaussian 94 at the MP2/6‐311G(d,p) level of theory (Figure 4).

In this work, the moments of inertia were calculated at the same level and incorporated into the kE calculations, allowing rotational contributions to be included. These calculations were performed with both Eyringpy and Multiwell. The data are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. Microcanonical rate constants were obtained using 500 points (300 in the low‐energy region) and a maximum energy of 8.5 × 104 cm−1.

Figure 4 shows the DOS of Ge2H6 and the corresponding kE values. The DOS computed with Eyringpy agrees closely with Multiwell, with discrepancies of less than 0.1 units across both low‐energy (< 10 kcal·mol−1) and high‐energy regimes. Compared to H2O2, Ge2H6 has more degrees of freedom, which reduces discontinuities in the low‐energy regime, and the DOS curve approaches continuity before reaching the high‐energy regime.

For the calculation of kE, a critical energy of 37.0 kcal·mol−1 was used to evaluate the sum of states of the TS. The resulting kE values differed by less than 2 units, representing a negligible deviation in the rate constants. DOS and kE values remained within the same order of magnitude.

Br‐Atom Extrusion From Bromomethoxy Radical

Full geometry optimizations and frequency analysis were performed at the MP2/6‐31G(d) [40] level. Open‐shell systems were treated with unrestricted methods. Local minima and TS were identified based on the number of imaginary frequencies (0 and 1, respectively). To confirm that each TS connected to the correct reactants and products, intrinsic reaction coordinate (IRC) [41, 42] calculations were carried out using the local quadratic approximation (LQA) [43, 44]. The DOS and kE were calculated with 1000 points (500 in the low‐energy regime) and a maximum energy of 1.0 × 105 cm−1.

For CH2BrO·, two main reaction channels (thermal decomposition and isomerization) have been reported [10]. The present analysis considered the Br‐atom extrusion pathway, CH2BrO·→Br·+CH2O which dominates the decomposition at low energies, has the lowest critical energy, and yields the largest kE. The geometrical parameters, vibrational frequencies, and moments of inertia of reactant and TS (Tables S4 and S5) show good agreement with previously reported theoretical data [10]. The calculated critical energy of 9.0 kcal·mol−1 is in close agreement with the reported value of 9.1 kcal·mol−1.

Comparisons of the DOS curves show deviations of less than 1 unit in the low‐energy regime (< 25 kcal·mol−1) and between 2 and 7 units in the high‐energy regime, although the overall order of magnitude remains consistent (Figure 5a). For kE, both programs yield discretized values near the critical energy. At low and high energies, this behavior resembles that of the DOS (Figure 5b), with discrepancies arising from the level of precision in the ab initio calculations.

Furthermore, the tunneling contribution was also examined. The barrier height relative to the products was determined to be 37.6 kcal·mol−1, with the critical energy consistent with the value reported earlier. To capture tunneling probabilities at low energies, the range was extended to include energies below the barrier. Figure 6c shows the TS sum of states corrected with the Eckart tunneling approximation, which increases from 1025 to 1026 at high energies and confirms the existence of accessible states below the critical energy.

Figure 6d compares kE values computed with and without tunneling. Inclusion of tunneling substantially enhances kE at low energies (< 100.0 mol−1), yielding non‐zero probabilities below the barrier. At 70.0 kcal·mol−1, neglecting tunneling underestimates the rate constant by nearly two orders of magnitude, with discrepancies reaching four orders at lower energies. As higher energies, the kE curves converge and tunneling contributions become negligible.

Summary and Outlook

In this work, RRKM theory was implemented in Eyringpy to compute microcanonical rate constants, densities of states, and sums of states for unimolecular reactions. The Stein–Rabinovitch algorithm provides reliable quantum state counting, and the Eckart tunneling model appropriately captures light‐particle transfer and narrow‐barrier effects, particularly at low energies. Comparison with Multiwell calculations shows excellent agreement for densities of states and generally close agreement for k(E). Tunneling contributions were found to significantly increase low‐energy rate constants. The current implementation of RRKM theory in Eyringpy requires minimal input data and provides flexible input options, making it well‐suited for unimolecular reactions where kinetics depend on internal energy redistribution. So, the results indicate that Eyringpy is a reliable and efficient tool for kinetic studies of unimolecular reactions, capable of providing consistent rate constant predictions across a wide range of energies and supporting pressure‐ and energy‐dependent kinetic analyses.

Nonetheless, further developments are needed, particularly to better treat coupled degrees of freedom, which are critical for non‐RRKM approaches in systems with non‐statistical energy distributions. Future work will focus on incorporating these improvements and extending compatibility with additional quantum chemistry packages, such as Orca, to enhance flexibility and broaden applicability. Furthermore, efforts will focus on the treatment of barrierless reactions, a persistent challenge in unimolecular kinetics. Parallel to this, multidimensional tunneling correction models are being implemented to refine the computation of microcanonical rate constants.
