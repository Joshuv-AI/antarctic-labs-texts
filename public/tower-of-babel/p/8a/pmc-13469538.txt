Regional variations in diabetes prevalence and their associations with obesity and dietary habits: An ecological cross-sectional study using the National Database of Japan
Authors: Fumitake Yamaguchi, Takumi Kiyonaga, Shiori Nitta, Naoki Yoshinaga, Sayaka Akieda-Asai, Yukari Date, Hirotake Sawada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundType 2 diabetes (T2D) is a major global health issue. Many Asian individuals with T2D are non-obese, suggesting that factors beyond body mass index (BMI) may be relevant.Design and methodsThis ecological cross-sectional study examined correlations between diabetes prevalence and lifestyle-related factors using age-standardized, prefecture-level data from the 2021 National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan Open Data. Variables included diabetes prevalence (glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] ≥ 6.5%), obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2), other obesity-related indicators, and self-reported dietary habits. Associations were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction.ResultsAfter FDR correction, prefecture-level diabetes prevalence was positively correlated with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, elevated waist circumference, and weight gain in both sexes, and with alanine aminotransferase in women. Among dietary variables, the proportion of individuals with impaired masticatory function was positively correlated with diabetes prevalence in both sexes, whereas daily snacking was inversely correlated only in women; breakfast skipping, late-night dinner, and fast eating were not significantly correlated in either sex. However, after adjustment for BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, none of the dietary habit variables showed a significant association with diabetes prevalence in either sex.ConclusionsPrefecture-level diabetes prevalence in Japan correlated with several obesity-related indicators and impaired masticatory function in both sexes, although impaired masticatory function was not robust to BMI adjustment. Sex-specific patterns included a positive correlation with alanine aminotransferase and an inverse correlation with daily snacking in women. These findings warrant future individual-level studies.Keywords: body mass index, diabetes, obesity, regional disparities, Japan

Significance for public health

This ecological cross-sectional study provides population-level evidence on regional variation in diabetes prevalence in Japan. At the prefectural level, diabetes prevalence was ecologically correlated with BMI, waist circumference, weight gain since age 20, and impaired masticatory function, and some additional sex-specific patterns were also observed. However, dietary associations were not observed after controlling for BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. These findings highlight the value of examining regional metabolic and behavioral patterns beyond BMI alone, while also underscoring the need for future individual-level studies.

1. Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is a leading global health concern. According to the International Diabetes Federation, approximately 537 million adults were living with diabetes worldwide in 2021, and this number is projected to rise to 643 million by 2030 and 783 million by 2045. 1 Diabetes is broadly classified into two types: type 1 diabetes, in which autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells leads to insulin deficiency, and type 2 diabetes (T2D), characterized by insulin resistance and impaired insulin secretion, driven by genetic and/or dietary factors. 2 T2D accounts for over 90–95% of all diabetes cases3,4 and has traditionally been associated with overnutrition and obesity.5,6

Adiposity contributes to insulin resistance through mechanisms such as increased free fatty acid flux, low-grade inflammation, and ectopic lipid accumulation.7,8 However, the relationship between body mass index (BMI) and diabetes risk is not identical across populations. 9 Obesity is conventionally defined as BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 by the World Health Organization (WHO), whereas the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity uses a lower threshold of BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.10,11 In a clinical survey of patients with diabetes in Shiga Prefecture, 30.9% were overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2). 12 Furthermore, a retrospective analysis of the Japan diabetes comprehensive database project based on an advanced electronic medical record system reported that 64.0% of Japanese patients with type 2 diabetes had BMI < 25 kg/m2. 13 In contrast, U.S. studies, including analyses based on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, have reported lower proportions of individuals with BMI < 25 kg/m2 (12.7%–17.1%) and higher proportions with BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 (46.9%–58.1%) among adults with diabetes. 14 Taken together, these findings suggest that non-obese diabetes is relatively common in Japan and other Asian populations. This pattern highlights the limitations of using BMI alone to characterize diabetes-related population patterns and suggests the need to examine additional metabolic and behavioral indicators at the population level.

Indeed, accumulating evidence suggests that waist circumference, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and triglycerides (TG) are superior to BMI in capturing metabolic risk (e.g., Cibičková et al. 15 ; Moriyama 16 ; Wu et al. 17 ). In addition, a weight gain of ≥ 10 kg since the age of 20 years has been linked to elevated risks for metabolic syndrome and abnormal cardiometabolic markers, even among individuals with similar current BMI. 18 These markers may provide practical and informative alternatives for evaluating T2D risk beyond simple anthropometric indices.

Eating habits associated with diabetes encompass a wide range of factors. While current clinical guidelines in Japan emphasize general dietary strategies—such as caloric restriction, limiting saturated fat intake, and maintaining regular meal timing—they do not address chewing ability, dietary texture, or snacking behaviors. 19 However, these factors may play a significant role in the development of T2D, particularly among non-obese individuals.

Impaired mastication and frequent snacking have been associated with poorer glycemic control and increased metabolic risk in individual-level studies, including Japanese studies on masticatory performance and self-reported chewing difficulty.20,21 Experimental studies have also examined the metabolic effects of soft-textured, easily absorbable foods in non-obese settings.22–24 Although these studies do not establish that such factors explain regional diabetes prevalence, they provide background for considering qualitative aspects of eating—such as mastication, eating frequency, and dietary texture—when examining population-level metabolic patterns.

Although obesity is an important correlate of diabetes, BMI alone may not fully capture regional metabolic risk patterns in Japan. Therefore, this ecological study examined whether prefectural diabetes prevalence was associated with obesity-related indicators and selected lifestyle-related variables, while recognizing that the design is exploratory and does not permit causal inference.

2. Design and methods

2.1. Study design and data source

This ecological cross-sectional study was conducted using the 9th release of the National Database of Health Insurance Claims and Specific Health Checkups of Japan (NDB) Open Data, accessed July 30, 2025. 25 The NDB, operated by the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (MHLW) of Japan, is one of the most comprehensive national-level healthcare databases in the world. We analyzed aggregated prefecture-level health checkup data for adults aged 40–74 years in fiscal year 2021 (April 1, 2021 to March 31, 2022), a period that overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic in Japan; therefore, the findings should be interpreted in this temporal context. According to the official results reported by medical insurers through the MHLW data (https://www.mhlw.go.jp/stf/newpage_03092.html), a total of 30,240,302 individuals underwent specific health checkups out of 53,801,976 eligible persons across Japan’s 47 prefectures in fiscal year 2021, corresponding to an implementation rate of 56.2% (SD = 4.4%). For each variable, the dataset includes the number of participants stratified by sex and 5-year age groups (40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, and 70–74 years). Available indicators include anthropometric measures (e.g., BMI, waist circumference), laboratory tests (e.g., ALT, TG, glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], other lipid profiles), blood pressure, urinalysis, and questionnaire-based lifestyle factors. The questionnaire includes 22 items covering dietary and behavioral habits (e.g., masticatory function, eating speed, breakfast skipping, snacking, and late-night dinner). 25

2.2. Variables and definitions

The dataset provides stratification by sex (men/women) only, and gender-related aspects were not available. The primary outcome was diabetes prevalence, defined as the proportion of individuals with HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, based on the 2021 NDB Open Data Japan.

Explanatory variables included the following:

● Obesity-related indices: BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2, as defined by the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity 11 ; waist circumference (≥ 85 cm for men and ≥ 90 cm for women), based on Japanese metabolic syndrome criteria 26 ; ALT > 30 U/L, based on a recent Japanese health checkup-based study showing that ALT > 30 U/L is a reasonable rule-in cut-off for identifying metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease 27 ; TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, based on the contemporary hypertriglyceridemia guidelines 28 ; and self-reported weight gain ≥ 10 kg since age 20. 18

● Obesity-related indices: BMI ≥ 25.0 kg/m2, as defined by the Japan Society for the Study of Obesity 11 ; waist circumference (≥ 85 cm for men and ≥ 90 cm for women), based on Japanese metabolic syndrome criteria 26 ; ALT > 30 U/L, based on a recent Japanese health checkup-based study showing that ALT > 30 U/L is a reasonable rule-in cut-off for identifying metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease 27 ; TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, based on the contemporary hypertriglyceridemia guidelines 28 ; and self-reported weight gain ≥ 10 kg since age 20. 18

● Eating habits: Eating habit variables were assessed using self-reported items from the standard questionnaire of the specific health checkup program administered by the MHLW. 25 Masticatory function was assessed based on the question, “How would you describe your ability to chew food?” and was categorized as impaired if participants responded, “I can hardly chew” or “I have some difficulty chewing due to teeth, gums, or bite issues.” Breakfast skipping was defined as skipping breakfast three or more times per week. Daily snacking was defined as responding “every day” to the question on whether snacks or sweet beverages were consumed in addition to the three main meals. The item did not distinguish sugar-sweetened beverages from artificially sweetened or zero-calorie beverages in the aggregated NDB Open Data. Late-night dinner was defined as eating dinner within 2 hours before bedtime three or more times per week. Fast eating was defined as responding “fast” to the question asking whether the participant’s eating speed was fast compared with others.

Because the NDB Open Data provides only aggregated proportions within predefined categories, rather than continuous summary measures such as means, the study variables were analyzed using clinically meaningful thresholds or standard questionnaire categories, rather than as continuous measures.

2.3. Statistical analysis

To adjust for differences in age distribution across prefectures, age-standardized rates were calculated by the direct method using the 2015 Japanese standard population as the reference population, 29 , based on 5-year age groups (40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, and 70–74 years) (see Supplementary Formula S1 and Table S1 for details of the standard population).

Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients (ρ) were calculated to assess the associations between diabetes prevalence and each explanatory variable across Japan’s 47 prefectures. As an additional analysis, partial correlation analyses were performed separately for men and women to examine the associations between diabetes prevalence and dietary habit variables while controlling for the proportion of individuals with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. Multiplicity was addressed across the 20 prespecified sex-stratified comparisons using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure at Q = 5%, based on two-sided p values from the initial correlation analyses. To further account for obesity-related regional patterns, exploratory sex-specific multivariable linear regression models were fitted to examine whether dietary habit variables remained associated with diabetes prevalence after adjustment for obesity-related indicators. Each dietary habit variable was entered separately into the model. Variance inflation factors (VIFs) were examined to assess multicollinearity. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients, partial correlations, and exploratory multivariable regression analyses were conducted using JMP Pro, version 17.2 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA), and FDR correction and graph generation were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Choropleth maps visualizing regional variations were created using MANDARA (Kenji Tani’s Laboratory, Saitama, Japan).

2.4. Ethical considerations

This study utilized publicly available, anonymized secondary data provided by the MHLW of Japan. As no individual-level data were analyzed, ethical review and informed consent were not required, in accordance with relevant national guidelines.

3. Results

3.1. Prevalence of diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) and obesity (BMI ≥ 25)

Figure 1 shows the prefecture-level distribution of obesity (BMI ≥ 25) and diabetes prevalence (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%), visualized using MANDARA 10. Overall, the prevalence of diabetes (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) and obesity (BMI ≥ 25) was approximately 1.5–2 times higher in men than in women across all prefectures.

Among men, the highest obesity prevalence was observed in Okinawa Prefecture at 48.2%, markedly higher than that in any other prefecture. This was followed by Hokkaido (40.9%), Aomori (40.0%), Miyagi (39.4%), Iwate (39.3%), Fukushima (39.3%), and Akita (39.0%), indicating that high obesity prevalence was prominent in northern Japan and the Tohoku region (Figure 1(A)).

In contrast, the highest diabetes prevalence among men was observed in Miyagi Prefecture (12.3%), followed by Kumamoto (11.9%), Ishikawa (11.8%), Saga (11.7%), Ibaraki (11.6%), Toyama (11.5%), Kagoshima (11.4%), Wakayama (11.4%), Okinawa (11.4%), and Aomori (11.3%). Prefectures with high diabetes prevalence were concentrated along the Pacific coast of the Tohoku region (e.g., Miyagi, Fukushima, Ibaraki), in southern Kyushu (e.g., Kumamoto, Kagoshima), and in the Hokuriku region (e.g., Ishikawa, Toyama). The geographic distribution of diabetes did not fully overlap with that of obesity, which was particularly high in Okinawa and northern Japan (Figure 1(B)).

Among women, the highest obesity prevalence was also observed in Okinawa Prefecture at 32.1%, a similarly outstanding level as that observed in men. This was followed by Aomori (27.0%), Fukushima (26.9%), Iwate (26.8%), Akita (26.0%), Kagoshima (25.6%), and Yamagata (25.0%), again showing a tendency toward higher obesity prevalence in the Tohoku–northern Japan area and southern Kyushu (Figure 1(A)).

The highest diabetes prevalence among women was observed in Okinawa (5.9%), Kagoshima (5.8%), Miyagi (5.8%), Saga (5.8%), Kumamoto (5.7%), and Ibaraki (5.6%). These high-prevalence prefectures were concentrated in southern Kyushu (Okinawa, Kagoshima, Kumamoto) and along the Pacific coast of the Tohoku region (Miyagi, Ibaraki). Although the absolute values were lower than those observed in men, Okinawa consistently ranked high for both obesity and diabetes, and the geographic pattern of elevated diabetes prevalence along the Pacific Tohoku coast was largely consistent between men and women (Figure 1(B)).

3.2.2. Association between diabetes and dietary habits

The proportion of individuals with masticatory dysfunction was positively correlated with HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, showing a weak correlation in men (ρ = 0.330, p = 0.024) and a moderate correlation in women (ρ = 0.478, p < 0.001), and these associations remained significant after FDR correction (Figure 3(A), (B)). After FDR correction, no significant association was observed between skipping breakfast ≥ 3 times per week and HbA1c ≥ 6.5% in either sex (men: ρ = 0.293, p = 0.046; women: ρ = 0.260, p = 0.077) (Figure 3(C), (D)). An inverse association between daily snacking and HbA1c ≥ 6.5% remained significant after FDR correction in women (ρ = –0.519, p < 0.001) (Figure 3(E), (F)).

4. Discussion

This nationwide ecological study using age-standardized prefecture-level data from Japan’s National Database identified significant positive correlations between diabetes prevalence and several obesity-related indices. After FDR correction, BMI, waist circumference, and weight gain since age 20 remained significantly correlated with diabetes prevalence in both men and women, whereas ALT remained significant only in women. Among dietary variables, the proportion of individuals with impaired masticatory function remained significantly correlated with diabetes prevalence in both men and women, whereas the proportion of individuals reporting daily snacking remained significantly correlated only in women. However, these dietary associations were not observed in additional partial correlation analyses controlling for BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.

Among the obesity-related indices analyzed, waist circumference showed the strongest correlation with diabetes prevalence in both sexes. Although BMI was also significantly correlated with diabetes prevalence, waist circumference showed a higher correlation coefficient than BMI, particularly in men. The correlation between BMI and diabetes prevalence is not inconsistent with previous epidemiological findings linking adiposity to diabetes. At the same time, several prefectures, particularly among men, showed relatively high HbA1c ≥ 6.5% prevalence despite lower proportions of individuals with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2. This pattern suggests that BMI may not fully reflect regional patterns of diabetes prevalence at the population level. This interpretation is not inconsistent with the findings of Chen et al., who reported in a prospective cohort study of 3,461 Chinese adults that waist circumference, but not BMI, was significantly associated with incident diabetes after multivariable adjustment. 30 In addition, the consistently high BMI observed in Okinawa was broadly consistent with previous regional reports. However, because the present study was ecological and such measures were limited in the current dataset, further individual-level studies are needed.

Sex-specific ecological patterns were also evident. Correlations between diabetes prevalence and BMI, waist circumference, and weight gain since age 20 were numerically higher in women than in men (men: ρ = 0.389–0.482; women: ρ = 0.571–0.673), and ALT was correlated with diabetes prevalence only in women after FDR correction. Previous studies have reported associations between ALT-related liver markers and diabetes, although evidence specifically addressing sex differences remains limited.31–33 Thus, the ecological patterns observed in the present study differed between men and women, although the present design does not allow direct evaluation of the biological or social mechanisms underlying these differences. As a rough descriptive index, these obesity-related coefficients in men corresponded to approximately 15%–23% shared variance, suggesting that these statistically significant associations represented modest prefecture-level signals rather than strong explanatory relationships.

At the prefectural level, the proportion of individuals reporting impaired masticatory function was positively correlated with diabetes prevalence in both sexes, with a stronger correlation observed in women (men: ρ = 0.330; women: ρ = 0.478). As a rough descriptive index, these coefficients corresponded to approximately 11% and 23% shared variance, respectively, indicating modest to moderate prefecture-level signals. This ecological finding is noteworthy because it is broadly consistent with previous individual-level studies conducted in Japan, although the level of inference differs. Yamazaki et al. 21 conducted a cross-sectional study of nearly 7,000 residents aged 40–74 years in Nagahama City, Shiga Prefecture (2,283 men and 4,544 women) and reported that masticatory performance was associated with diabetes prevalence, with a significant association remaining in men after adjustment. An individual-level cross-sectional study by Iwai et al. found that self-reported chewing difficulty was independently associated with HbA1c ≥ 6.5% after adjustment for confounders. 20 Thus, the present ecological finding is not inconsistent with the existing individual-level literature on mastication and glycemic status. However, because impaired masticatory function in the present study was defined using a self-reported questionnaire item, it should be interpreted as a proxy indicator rather than an objective measure of oral function.

Although the present ecological analysis does not allow causal inference, previous individual-level and experimental studies may offer possible biological explanations for the observed ecological correlation. In a study of healthy Japanese adults, reduced chewing was associated with higher postprandial glucose responses. 34 In addition, animal studies have shown that prolonged intake of soft-textured diets—requiring less mastication—can induce insulin resistance, exacerbate postprandial glucose elevations, and promote hepatic glucose dysregulation and lipid accumulation, even without obesity.23,24 Accordingly, while the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent causal effect of mastication on regional diabetes prevalence, they may be consistent with biological pathways previously suggested in individual-level and experimental studies.

An inverse ecological correlation between daily snacking and diabetes prevalence was also observed in women in the primary analyses. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously because previous individual-level studies on eating frequency and diabetes-related outcomes have shown inconsistent results. Although some cohort data suggest that more frequent eating occasions may be associated with lower incident diabetes risk, 35 recent systematic review evidence does not consistently support a clear beneficial effect of higher meal frequency on glycemic control.36,37 Rather, the inverse ecological correlation observed in the present study may reflect broader regional clustering of dietary behaviors, adiposity-related characteristics, or other unmeasured lifestyle factors. In addition, this questionnaire item did not distinguish sugar-sweetened beverages from artificially sweetened or zero-calorie beverages, further limiting interpretation of the inverse association. Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as evidence of an independent causal effect of snacking on diabetes prevalence.

However, this study’s ecological cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as individual-level relationships. The potential for ecological fallacy is a primary limitation of the present study and constrains the interpretation of all observed correlations. In the primary analyses, several dietary variables showed bivariate ecological correlations with diabetes prevalence. However, in additional partial correlation analyses controlling for the proportion of individuals with BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2, none of the dietary habit variables remained significantly associated with diabetes prevalence in either sex. Furthermore, exploratory multivariable regression analyses with adjustment for obesity-related indicators did not provide consistent evidence that dietary habit variables were independently associated with diabetes prevalence. Although late-night dinner ≥3 times/week was significantly associated with diabetes prevalence in one female model, this variable was not significant in the primary correlation analysis. These models should be interpreted cautiously, particularly in women, because the number of prefectural units was limited relative to the number of covariates, and several obesity-related indicators were conceptually and clinically related. Although VIF values did not exceed 10, these factors may have increased the risk of overfitting and affected the stability and interpretability of the regression coefficients. Taken together, these findings suggest that the observed dietary associations may partly reflect confounding by obesity-related factors rather than independent associations. Moreover, as previous nationwide and cohort studies have shown that the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with reduced physical activity, increased overweight, and worsening cardiometabolic risk in Japan,38,39 the 2021 NDB data may reflect lifestyle and metabolic patterns under pandemic conditions rather than those of a typical year. For example, reduced physical activity may have shifted obesity-related indicators and HbA1c-based diabetes prevalence upward in some prefectures, whereas disruption of daily schedules may have altered eating habits such as breakfast skipping, late-night dinner, and snacking. In addition, if health checkup attendance during the pandemic was selective, prefectural estimates may have been affected by selection bias. Therefore, these findings should be regarded as ecological and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further longitudinal and individual-level studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms.

Additional limitations should be noted. First, diabetes prevalence was operationally defined as HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, which is a widely used indicator of medium- to long-term glycemic status.2,40 However, this definition does not fully correspond to clinically diagnosed diabetes. In particular, individuals already undergoing glucose-lowering treatment may have had HbA1c values below 6.5% at the time of assessment and therefore may not have been captured by this definition, leading to potential underestimation of diabetes prevalence. The magnitude of this underestimation may also have varied across prefectures according to differences in treatment access, healthcare utilization, and adherence. In addition, this definition does not distinguish type 1 diabetes from T2D. Because type 1 diabetes is not primarily related to lifestyle factors, its inclusion may have introduced some degree of misclassification. Nevertheless, because more than 95% of diabetes cases in Japan are T2D, the present findings are likely to predominantly reflect prefecture-level patterns related to T2D in the Japanese population. Second, although HbA1c, BMI, waist circumference, ALT, and TG are originally continuous measures, the NDB Open Data provided these variables only as aggregated categorical proportions based on predefined thresholds. Therefore, the present analyses used prefectural proportions meeting these predefined thresholds rather than continuous values. This categorization may have reduced information and limited adjustment for adiposity and metabolic status. Future studies should examine the robustness of these findings using continuous adiposity and metabolic measures where available. Third, lifestyle variables, including dietary behaviors and masticatory function, were self-reported and susceptible to recall bias and misclassification. Because the NDB Open Data does not provide information on formal validation of these questionnaire items against objective measures of masticatory function, these measures should be interpreted as proxy indicators rather than direct assessments. Fourth, individuals not undergoing specific health checkups were excluded, and regional variation in participation rates may also have affected the observed geographic patterns. Although these rates were not significantly correlated with key indicators (HbA1c ≥ 6.5%) at the prefectural level, selection bias cannot be ruled out. Fifth, although multiple testing was addressed using FDR correction, the limited number of analytical units (47 prefectures) restricted statistical power and constrained our ability to adequately control for confounding. Accordingly, the observed correlations should be interpreted as ecological and hypothesis-generating rather than as fully adjusted associations. Finally, the NDB data also lacked gender-related variables, limiting generalizability with respect to gender identity and roles.

In summary, the present ecological findings do not contradict the existing individual-level literature supporting the importance of obesity-related factors in diabetes. At the same time, they suggest that regional variation in diabetes prevalence in Japan may involve broader obesity-related and oral-health-related patterns, and that some sex-specific population-level patterns warrant further study. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory ecological observations. Future individual-level studies, particularly those focusing on non-obese populations, are needed to clarify the mechanisms underlying regional and sex-specific variation in diabetes prevalence.

5. Conclusion

At the prefectural level, diabetes prevalence was ecologically correlated with BMI, waist circumference, weight gain since age 20, and impaired masticatory function. Some additional sex-specific ecological patterns were also observed for ALT levels and daily snacking in women. Dietary associations were not observed after controlling for BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2.
