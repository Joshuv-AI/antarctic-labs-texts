Exploring Tobacco Vendor Perspectives on Tobacco Vendor Licensing in Lucknow, India: An Exploratory Mixed-Methods Study
Authors: Aman Rajput, Vinay K. Gupta, Anil Kumar, Nishita Kankane, Gaurav Mishra, Sumit Kumar, Mohit K. Kanoujia, S. Deepak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAim:Tobacco vendor licensing (TVL) under India’s Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act is intended to regulate tobacco sales and reduce youth access; however, implementation gaps at the local level may limit its impact. This study explored, in a purposively selected sample, tobacco vendors’ awareness, perceptions, and experiences regarding TVL in Lucknow, India.Materials and Methods:An exploratory concurrent triangulation mixed-methods design was used. A structured survey of 150 purposively selected vendors assessed demographics, awareness, and perceptions of TVL, while six in-depth interviews captured lived experiences. Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics to identify exploratory patterns, and qualitative data were examined thematically using Braun and Clarke’s framework. Integration occurred at the interpretation stage. Given the non-probability sampling, findings are not statistically inferential or generalizable.Results:Only 26.7% of vendors reported awareness of TVL regulations, indicating a substantial information gap. Vendors who were aware of TVL tended to report more favorable views of licensing and its intended regulatory role. Qualitative analysis identified five interrelated themes: lack of awareness, positive perception of TVL’s intent, challenges in implementation, unclear enforcement mechanisms, and suggestions for improvement. Vendors highlighted financial burden, procedural complexity, and limited official communication as key barriers to compliance.Conclusion:These exploratory findings suggest that information deficits, procedural opacity, and weak enforcement constrain the effectiveness of TVL in Lucknow. Strengthening vendor-focused awareness, simplifying licensing processes, and ensuring consistent, transparent enforcement are essential to better align policy intent with on-ground practice.

INTRODUCTION

India faces one of the world’s largest tobacco epidemics, with an estimated 1.35 million deaths annually attributed to tobacco-related illnesses—a figure that underscores the urgent need for comprehensive control measures.[1,2] Nationally, tobacco use is prevalent among diverse populations, including the youth and socioeconomically disadvantaged, who are particularly vulnerable due to the widespread, unregulated availability of tobacco products. Uttar Pradesh (UP), the country’s most populous state, exemplifies these challenges: according to the Global Adult Tobacco Survey, nearly 35.5% of adults in UP use tobacco in one form or another, fueling a persistent public health crisis.[2] Easy accessibility to tobacco—often through informal vendors—has been identified as a critical factor perpetuating both initiation and continued use, especially among school-aged children and lower-income groups.[3,4]

To address these challenges, India instituted the Cigarettes and Other Tobacco Products Act (COTPA), which includes crucial regulatory interventions such as restrictions on advertising and bans on tobacco sales within 100 yards of educational institutions.[5,6] A recent and significant addition to this regulatory environment is tobacco vendor licensing (TVL), a measure mandating all retailers to obtain licenses to legally sell tobacco products.[7] TVL aims to formalize the retail sector, create mechanisms for monitoring sales, and, most importantly, prevent illegal sales to minors. Experience from international settings, like Spain, suggests that vendor licensing can be a pivotal tool for reducing youth exposure and strengthening compliance with tobacco control laws.[8]

Implementation of TVL in UP began in earnest in 2021, with explicit government orders requiring local authorities to license all tobacco retailers in both rural and urban regions.[7] However, the retail environment in UP, and particularly in urbanizing districts such as Lucknow, is vastly unorganized, with thousands of small-scale, informal vendors operating outside formal regulatory frameworks. According to the Economic Times, the UP government issued directives to urban local bodies to begin vendor registration, yet a substantial proportion of vendors remain unlicensed—indicating enforcement and awareness gaps.[7] Furthermore, departmental reports indicate that while vendor sensitization programs have started, confusion persists among vendors about the licensing process, its benefits, and penalties for noncompliance.[2,9]

The problem is further exacerbated around educational institutions. Despite the COTPA ban, a 2024 study surveying over 61,000 schools in four Indian states found that 15.4% of schools in UP reported tobacco shops operating within 100 yards—a clear violation.[3] This proximity increases the likelihood of tobacco initiation among students and highlights the need for more robust monitoring and enforcement in so-called tobacco-free zones. Lucknow, as the state capital, has been a focus area for pilot interventions, with efforts made to map vendors near schools and create buffer zones in line with national policy, supported by the Department of Education and the National Tobacco Control Programme (NTCP).[3,6,10]

Alongside regulatory controls, cessation support is critical. UP has established Tobacco Cessation Centers (TCCs) in each district, including multiple facilities in Lucknow, to provide behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy for users seeking to quit. However, reach and utilization remain limited, with only a small fraction of tobacco users and a negligible proportion of vendors accessing these services.[11,12,13]

Tobacco vendors themselves often operate in an information vacuum, with little understanding of new regulations or access to compliance resources. Studies from India show the majority of vendors remain unorganized, lack awareness of legal requirements, and are unsure of how licensing affects their business or community standing.[9] Recognizing these obstacles, recent government initiatives have proposed innovative measures: simplifying licensing with digital forms, conducting targeted awareness drives, and incorporating feedback from vendor associations.[2,10] Such efforts are vital not just for regulatory compliance but also for reducing tobacco initiation and use among youth.

However, little empirical work has explored the perspectives of vendors themselves, particularly in settings where the retail structure is informal and fragmented. Existing research largely examines enforcement perspectives, leaving a gap in understanding the lived realities and practical challenges faced by vendors. To address this, this study adopts an exploratory mixed-methods approach using concurrent triangulation, integrating descriptive quantitative findings with qualitative narratives to provide contextual insights rather than statistical inference. Given the absence of a reliable sampling frame and the highly unorganized nature of tobacco retail in Lucknow, purposive sampling was used to capture diverse vendor types. The findings are intended to be exploratory and non-generalizable, offering preliminary guidance for future policy refinement and implementation strategies.

By examining awareness, perceptions, and experiences among tobacco vendors in Lucknow, this study seeks to generate practical, evidence-informed insights to improve engagement, compliance, and the on-ground success of TVL in UP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

STUDY DESIGN AND SETTING

This mixed-methods study complies with the Good Reporting of a Mixed-Methods Study framework, addressing all six recommended criteria, including justification, design detail, integration strategy, limitations, and insights from mixed-method triangulation. An exploratory concurrent triangulation design was adopted, giving equal emphasis to quantitative and qualitative components. Integration occurred during interpretation through a side-by-side comparison of findings to enhance contextual understanding. The study was conducted in Lucknow, UP—an urban district with a dense concentration of both formal and informal tobacco vendors.

ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethics Committee of King George’s Medical University, Lucknow (Approval No.: 2074/Ethics/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Participants were assured of confidentiality and informed of their right to withdraw at any time without consequence.

SAMPLING AND SAMPLE SIZE

For the quantitative component, 150 tobacco vendors were purposively selected. This approach was adopted because the tobacco retail sector in Lucknow is highly unorganized and lacks a reliable sampling frame, making probability-based selection infeasible. While purposive sampling ensured inclusion of diverse vendor types (fixed shops, kiosks, and mobile vendors), it limits representativeness and therefore restricts any statistical inference.

For the qualitative component, six vendors were purposively chosen for in-depth interviews to capture variation across vendor types. The sample size was guided by pragmatic considerations of data saturation and feasibility. Saturation was deemed reached when no new themes emerged, though the small number remains a limitation for broader transferability.

DATA COLLECTION

QUANTITATIVE COMPONENT

Structured, face-to-face interviews were conducted using a pretested questionnaire with seven sections: demographics (age, gender, education level, and years of experience), awareness of TVL (knowledge, licensing status, and information sources), perceptions (impact on business, necessity, and population-level accessibility), compliance (knowledge of legal provisions and challenges), opinions on licensing regulations (fees, location restrictions, and harm reduction), suggestions for improvement, and closing remarks.

QUALITATIVE COMPONENT

Semi-structured interviews were conducted using a pretested guide to collect detailed narratives on participants’ backgrounds, motivations for entering the tobacco business, types of products sold, awareness and understanding of licensing, perceived impacts of TVL on sales and accessibility, compliance challenges, and suggestions for improvement. Rapport was built through introductory questions about birthplace and schooling. All interviews were audio-recorded with consent and transcribed verbatim.

DATA ANALYSIS

QUANTITATIVE ANALYSIS

Data from the quantitative component were analyzed using descriptive statistics only (frequencies and percentages) to summarize vendor responses. Although chi-square tests were initially computed to explore patterns, these results are interpreted purely as exploratory associations and not as inferential evidence, given the purposive nature of sampling. No claims of statistical significance or population generalizability are made. Analysis was performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 24.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

QUALITATIVE ANALYSIS

Thematic analysis was conducted following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase framework.[5] Emergent themes were identified to capture the depth and diversity of vendors’ experiences and perceptions toward TVL. Integration of findings from both strands occurred at the interpretation stage to develop a comprehensive exploratory understanding.

RESULTS

A total of 150 tobacco vendors participated in the study. The majority were male (97.3%) and predominantly uneducated (90.7%). The mean age of the respondents was 35.06 years (standard deviation [SD] = 10.24), and the mean duration of experience in tobacco vending was 9.32 years (SD = 8.24). DISCUSSION

This exploratory mixed-methods study highlights the gap between the intent of India’s TVL policy and the practical realities of its implementation at the vendor level. Integrating descriptive quantitative data with qualitative narratives provided a contextual understanding of vendors’ challenges beyond numeric awareness patterns.

Despite TVL’s potential to regulate retail environments, limit youth access, and strengthen compliance with national tobacco control laws, vendor awareness was found to be low (73.3% unaware), and financial as well as procedural barriers remained dominant. These findings align with evidence from other low- and middle-income settings, where retailer engagement and compliance are often hindered by socio-economic constraints and limited institutional support.[14,15]

TVL systems enable governments to monitor vendor density and proximity to sensitive locations such as schools and healthcare facilities. Comparative experiences demonstrate mixed success. In Himachal Pradesh, for example, stricter licensing enforcement and bans on loose-cigarette sales reduced retail density and promoted livelihood diversification among vendors.[3] Similar interventions in Maharashtra and Noida, involving layered awareness and enforcement mechanisms, were associated with improved compliance and reduced youth initiation.[16] Internationally, Spain, the United Kingdom, and the United States have also reported declines in youth tobacco use following well-implemented vendor-licensing frameworks coupled with retailer education.[15,17]

In this study, descriptive patterns indicated that vendors who were aware of TVL tended to hold more favorable perceptions of its impact on business, fee structure, and location-based restrictions. However, these observations are exploratory in nature and should not be interpreted as statistical associations. Qualitative data further revealed persistent procedural complexity, confusion regarding responsible authorities, and inconsistent enforcement—echoing previous studies from Himachal Pradesh, Odisha, and Siliguri that noted similar implementation gaps.[18]

The continued sale of tobacco near educational institutions indicates ongoing noncompliance with COTPA Sections 6a and 6b, corroborating national reports documenting illegal sales within 100 yards of schools.[19]

The dense vendor network, limited awareness of cessation support, and weak enforcement collectively sustain youth access to tobacco. TCCs established under the NTCP remain underutilized, both by consumers and vendors, due to inadequate outreach—reinforcing the need for integrated demand- and supply-side interventions.[20,21]

Addressing these multifaceted barriers requires a coherent national and state-level framework. The recent directive of the UP government to mandate vendor licensing is an important step; however, clear operational guidelines on fee structures, enforcement roles, and procedural transparency are still lacking. Future policy measures should prioritize vendor-friendly mechanisms such as digital licensing platforms, phased implementation, financial subsidies for low-income vendors, and structured awareness campaigns.

Furthermore, zoning laws ensuring zero tolerance for tobacco sales near educational and healthcare institutions, coupled with inter-sectoral collaboration among education, health, and law enforcement authorities, are critical. A participatory approach that incorporates vendor feedback and dialogue can foster trust and ownership, thereby promoting sustainable compliance.[22,23,24]

LIMITATIONS

This study’s findings should be interpreted as exploratory rather than inferential. The purposive (nonprobability) sampling of 150 vendors limits representativeness; therefore, proportions such as the 26.7% awareness rate describe only this sample and cannot be generalized to all vendors in Lucknow. Additionally, only six qualitative interviews were conducted. While data saturation was reached—no new themes emerged after the sixth interview—the small qualitative sample may not capture the full diversity of vendor experiences across the city.

Despite these limitations, the concurrent use of quantitative and qualitative data enhanced interpretive depth and triangulated insights into vendor perspectives and the implementation realities of TVL.

CONCLUSION

TVL remains a crucial regulatory instrument supporting India’s tobacco control objectives and the World Health Organization Framework Convention on Tobacco Control Article 16 mandate. However, policy success depends on converting regulatory intent into inclusive, context-specific action. Strengthening vendor awareness, simplifying procedures, and building sustained enforcement capacity are essential to bridging the policy–practice gap in UP and beyond.[25]
