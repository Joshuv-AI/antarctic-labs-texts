Enhanced okara bioconversion and protein recovery through Aspergillus luchuensis fermentation and ultrasound processing
Authors: Weimin Meng, Miao Hu, Shanshan Wang, Jianghua Cheng, Xianglong Wu, Fengzhong Wang, Shuying Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Fungal protein is a promising sustainable alternative protein because of its relatively low environmental burden and efficient use of resources [1], [2]. Filamentous fungi can convert agricultural by-products into edible protein, thereby increasing their value. Okara is an abundant, low-cost soybean by-product rich in protein and dietary fiber, making it a promising substrate for fungal protein production [3], [4]. Therefore, fungal fermentation of okara could reduce by-product waste and promote sustainable food production.

Aspergillus species are widely used in agricultural-waste degradation, food fermentation, and biomass conversion because they secrete diverse enzymes and adapt well to complex plant-based substrates [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]. Aspergillus luchuensis has a long history of use in East Asian fermented foods, including Korean soju, Japanese awamori, glutinous rice wine, and Chinese Pu-erh tea [12], [13], [14]. It produces enzymes such as cellulases, amylases, and thermostable pectinases, which degrade plant cell-wall polysaccharides, starch, and pectin and support growth on fiber-rich substrates [15], [16], [17], [18]. Its acidification, antimicrobial activity, and contribution to aroma formation may also improve fermentation stability and product quality. These traits make A. luchuensis a promising organism for degrading the fibrous okara matrix and enriching fungal biomass protein.

However, the efficient conversion and recovery of protein from fermented okara remain challenging. The dense okara fiber network and the chitin- and glucan-rich walls of filamentous fungi both restrict nutrient release, substrate utilization, and protein extraction [19], [20], [21]. Green process-intensification technologies are therefore needed to improve fermentation efficiency and protein recovery.

Ultrasound technology has been widely used for protein extraction and modification. It can disrupt complex matrices and enhance mass transfer through cavitation, mechanical shear, and localized transient effects [22], [23], [24]. Ultrasound pretreatment disrupts the compact fibrous matrix of okara, increases its specific surface area, and improves substrate accessibility to microorganisms. [25], [26]. For filamentous fungal biomass, ultrasound may also help weaken the mycelial cell-wall barrier and promote protein release [23]. Thus, ultrasound has potential for upgrading filamentous fungi-fermented agricultural by-products.

Previous studies of ultrasound-assisted protein extraction have focused mainly on native plant materials, in which ultrasound disrupts plant tissues and releases endogenous proteins. The present study instead examined a biologically transformed okara–mycelium composite containing both residual soybean protein and newly formed fungal biomass protein. Protein recovery from this composite is limited by both the okara fiber network and fungal cell walls. Accordingly, ultrasound was applied before fermentation to improve substrate accessibility and during extraction to enhance protein release and modify the recovered protein. This dual application strategy distinguishes the present approach from conventional ultrasound-assisted extraction (UAE) of native plant proteins.

Our previous work showed that MW-1 adapts well to okara and promotes protein enrichment (Fig. S1; Tables S3 and S4), but the formation, structure, and functionality of the resulting protein fractions remain unclear. This study therefore characterized the nutritional composition, safety, structural evolution, and functional properties of okara during MW-1 fermentation. Ultrasound pretreatment was then evaluated as a means of improving substrate accessibility and fermentation efficiency. Finally, conventional alkaline extraction and ultrasound-assisted alkaline extraction were compared in terms of protein recovery and the structural and functional properties of MW-1-fermented okara protein (MOP).

Materials and methods

Materials and strain

Fresh okara, a wet fibrous residue containing approximately 80 % moisture, was obtained from a local market in Shandong, China. MW-1, an A. luchuensis possessing a rich repertoire of carbohydrate-active enzymes, was isolated in our laboratory and preserved at − 80 °C. The molecular identification result is presented in Fig. S2.

Analysis of the fermentation characteristics of MW-1

Preparation of fermented okara

MW-1 was inoculated onto potato dextrose agar (PDA) and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. A mycelial plug was then transferred to potato dextrose broth (PDB) and cultivated at 28 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h. The mycelia were collected by filtering the liquid culture through sterile gauze. Okara fermentation was conducted with 15 g of fresh okara, 60 mL of distilled water, and 1.8 g of mycelia at 28 °C and 200 rpm. Samples were collected on days 2, 3, 4, and 5. Three independent fermentation batches were prepared for each sampling time and used as biological replicates. All collected samples were immediately stored at − 80 °C for no longer than 2 days before centrifugation, ultrasonic treatment, and freeze-drying to obtain MW-1-fermented okara protein powder (MOP) and subsequent analysis.

Analysis of nutritional components

Mycelial biomass, chemical composition, and mycotoxin levels of fermented okara were determined as follows. For mycelial biomass determination, two 5 g portions were uniformly collected from the fermentation substrate. One portion was mixed with 50 mL of distilled water, and the sample was washed and filtered to recover the mycelium, which was subsequently freeze-dried and weighed (M1). The other portion was directly freeze-dried to determine the dry weight of the whole fermented substrate (M2). Mycelial transformation efficiency was calculated using the following equation. The protein of okara and MW-1 was determined using a Dumas nitrogen analyzer (SER14816, VELP, Italy) with a nitrogen-to-protein conversion factor of 6.25. Soluble proteins, peptides, and amino acids were determined according to the method of Meng et al. [27]. Dietary fiber and crude lipid contents were determined according to AOAC methods 991.43 and 960.39, respectively. The concentrations of aflatoxins, ochratoxins, fumonisins, and citrinin were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (MM-0593O1, MM-1089O1, MM-1584O1, and MM-95411O1; Jiangsu Enzyme Immunoassay Co., Ltd.).(1)Mycelial transformation efficiency(%)=M1M2×100

Structure characteristics

The surface morphology of okara before and after fermentation was analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (S-3400 N, Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The spectral characteristics were examined using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrometer (FTIR) (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany). X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed using an X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan), and thermal stability was evaluated using a thermogravimetric analyzer (Pyris Diamond TG/DTG, Perkin Elmer, USA). The specific procedures followed the methods described by Meng et al. [27].

Processing characteristics

The water-holding capacity (WHC), oil-holding capacity (OHC), swelling capacity (SC), and emulsifying activity index (EAI) of okara before and after fermentation were determined according to the method described by Meng et al. [27].

Ultrasound pretreatment of okara and its fermentation application

Fifteen grams of fresh okara was dispersed in 60 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 1 mmol/L, pH 7.0) and magnetically stirred at 25 °C for 1 h to ensure complete hydration. The hydrated suspension was then subjected to ultrasonic treatment using a JY92-T ultrasonic cell disruptor (Shanghai Haozhuang Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) equipped with a 6 mm flat-tip probe, which was immersed approximately 2.0 cm below the liquid surface. The ultrasonic processor was operated at a frequency of 20 kHz, with output powers of 0, 150, 300, and 450 W. Sonication was performed for 10 min in pulse mode (5 s on and 5 s off). To minimize temperature increases during treatment, the sample was placed in an ice-water bath, manually stirred every 1 min, and supplemented with ice as needed to maintain the system temperature at 25 °C throughout the process. The polypeptide, free amino acid, and soluble dietary fiber contents of the samples were determined according to the methods described in Section 2.2.2. The cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin contents were measured following the method reported by Meng et al. [28]. Samples from the 0 and 450 W ultrasonic treatment groups after 96 h of fermentation were selected for structural characterization, and the analytical procedures were carried out as described in Section 2.2.3.

Optimization of the protein extraction process

Experimental design

To optimize protein extraction from okara fermented with MW-1 for 96 h, the fermented samples were dispersed in distilled water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL). The pH of the suspension was adjusted to 12.0 with NaOH to solubilize the protein. The effects of ultrasonic time (5, 10, 15, 20, and 25 min) and ultrasonic power (150, 300, 450, and 600 W) on protein extraction efficiency were then investigated. Ultrasonic treatment was performed using a JY92-T ultrasonic cell disruptor (Shanghai Haozhuang Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The instrument was equipped with a 6 mm flat-tip probe, which was immersed approximately 2.0 cm below the liquid surface. The ultrasonic frequency was 20 kHz, and the treatment was conducted in pulse mode (5 s on and 5 s off). During treatment, the sample was placed in an ice-water bath, manually stirred every 1 min, and supplemented with ice as needed to maintain the system temperature at 25 °C. After extraction, the suspension was centrifuged to collect the supernatant. The pH of the supernatant was then adjusted to 4.5 with HCl to precipitate the protein. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed with distilled water, neutralized, and freeze-dried to obtain MW-1-fermented okara protein (MOP). The control group was extracted under the same alkaline extraction and acid precipitation conditions without ultrasound treatment.

Protein recovery efficiency

One gram of MW-1 fermented okara for 96 h was used as the raw material to prepare protein. After freeze-dried, the protein powder was weighed to obtain M2. Structural characterization of MOP

ζ-potential and size

One gram of MOP from various preparation methods was dispersed in 5 mL of PBS to form a uniform protein suspension, and measurements were carried out at room temperature using a Zetasizer Nano Zs analyzer (Malvern, UK).

Protein flexibility

Protein flexibility was measured following Zhu et al. [29] with minor modifications. The protein solution (1 mg/mL, Tris–HCl buffer, pH 8.0) was mixed with trypsin at a 16:1 vol ratio and incubated for 10 min. The reaction was terminated with an equal volume of 10 mg/mL TCA, followed by centrifugation at 5,000 × g for 25 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 280 nm.

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

Surface hydrophobicity was determined using 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid (ANS) as a fluorescent probe. Protein samples were diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 0.01 mol/L, pH 7.2) to concentrations of 0.02–0.10 mg/mL [30]. Then, 40 μL of ANS solution (8 mM) was added to 4 mL of each sample and incubated in the dark for 10 min. Fluorescence intensity was measured at 390 nm excitation and 470 nm emission. The initial slope of fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration was defined as the surface hydrophobicity index (H0).

FTIR

The wavenumber range for testing was set at 4000–500 cm⁻1. Initially, the MOP sample was ground into a fine powder using a mortar. The powder was blended with dried KBr crystals at a 1:50 ratio, finely ground, dried, and compressed into pellets for analysis [31].

The infrared spectrum of the protein was processed (including baseline correction and linear smoothing) and then analyzed using PeakFit V4.12 for baseline correction, deconvolution, and second-derivative fitting (R2 > 0.9999). By identifying the correspondence between each sub-peak and the secondary structure, the proportion of each secondary structure element was determined by calculating the percentage area of its corresponding sub-peak.

XRD

The XRD analysis of MOP was performed with an X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IV, Rigaku, Japan). The samples were evenly spread on glass slides, and the measurements were carried out at a scanning speed of 2°/min, with a tube current of 40 mA, over a 2θ range of 5° to 50°.

Ultraviolet spectroscopy

The MOP sample was dissolved in a 0.02 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH = 7.2), and the absorbance of the protein sample was measured against the buffer as a blank control within a wavelength range of 200–600 nm, followed by plotting the curve.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

A total of 1 mg of MOP was dissolved in 50 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 0.01 M, pH 7.2) and subjected to fluorescence spectroscopic analysis using an F97 spectrophotometer (Lengguang, China). The excitation wavelength was set at 290 nm, and fluorescence was recorded over a range of 300 to 500 nm. Both excitation and emission slit widths were maintained at 2.5 nm [32].

Thermogravimetric analysis (TG/DTG)

Thermogravimetric (TG) and derivative thermogravimetric (DTG) analyses were conducted according to the method described in Section 2.2.3.

Processing characteristics of MOP

Protein solubility was determined according to the method of Tang et al. [33]. Briefly, 1 g of sample was suspended in 100 mL of distilled water and continuously stirred at 25 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 10 min. The supernatant was collected, and its protein content was measured using a Bradford protein assay kit. The determination of WHC and OHC was the same as that described in Section 2.2.4. Foaming ability (FA) and foam stability (FS) were determined according to the method of Yang et al. [34] with slight modifications. Briefly, 10 mL of sample solution (20 mg/mL) was transferred into a 50 mL graduated glass cylinder and homogenized using a high-shear homogenizer (IKA T18, Werke GmbH, Germany) at 10,000 rpm for 1 min.(3)FA(%)=V0VL×100

Here, V0 represents the initial foam volume at 0 min, and VL denotes the total volume of the sample solution (10 mL). Subsequently, the foam volume at 30 min (V30) was measured, and foam stability (FS) was determined using the following equation:(4)FS(%)=V30V0×100where V30 is the foam volume at 30 min, and V0 is the initial foam volume. The above method allows for a systematic evaluation of the sample's foaming ability and stability.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate for each biological sample. Results are presented as mean values ± standard deviation (SD) and were visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.34 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Statistical analysis was carried out using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, with significance set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Bioconversion of okara during MW-1 fermentation

Protein utilization and transformation

Protein utilization during fermentation was evaluated based on mycelial conversion efficiency and the protein contents of residual okara and mycelia. Mycelial conversion efficiency increased rapidly to 48.5 % at 48 h and then more gradually to 55.1 % at 120 h (Fig. 1A). Meanwhile, the protein content of fermented okara increased and then stabilized, reaching 27.55 % at 96 h, whereas the residual protein content in okara continuously decreased from 17.49 % to 6.73 % by 120 h. Mycelial protein increased initially and stabilized at 47.33 % at 96 h (Fig. 1B). Soluble protein and peptide contents peaked at 7.50 and 68.13 mg/g, respectively, at 48 h and subsequently declined, whereas free amino acid content increased from 1.22 to 6.11 mg/g by 96 h (Fig. 1C). These results demonstrated efficient okara protein bioconversion by MW-1. Dynamic monitoring carbohydrate and fat changes of okara during fermentation

The transformation of carbohydrate and lipid components during fermentation was further evaluated by analyzing dietary fiber and fat contents. Total dietary fiber (TDF) decreased throughout fermentation, whereas soluble dietary fiber (SDF) initially increased and then declined (Fig. 1D). Insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) decreased from 67.32 % to 29.81 %, while SDF increased from 2.37 % to 13.34 % at 48 h before decreasing to 10.45 %. These results indicated that MW-1 promoted dietary fiber degradation and transformation fiber during fermentation. The transient accumulation of SDF was likely attributable to the enzymatic hydrolysis of polysaccharides by MW-1, which promoted the breakdown of complex carbohydrates into more soluble low-molecular-weight fractions [36], [37]. As fermentation progressed, these soluble fractions were further metabolized, resulting in a subsequent decline in SDF content. Crude lipid content changed little during the first 48 h but increased from 4.96 % to 8.77 % by 120 h (Fig. 1E). The limited change in fat content at the early stage may be attributed to the relatively weak lipid-utilization capacity of MW-1 and its low lipase activity [38], [39]. In contrast, the pronounced increase at the later stage was mainly associated with rapid mycelial proliferation, indicating effective lipid accumulation in the fungal biomass.

Mycotoxin safety assessment of fermented okara

The contents of citrinin, aflatoxin, ochratoxin, and fumonisin were 2.13–3.26 ng/g, 1.98–2.67 ng/g, 1.63–2.42 ng/g, and 2.00–2.89 ng/g, respectively, all well below the limits cited by the World Health Organization (WHO) (Fig. 1F). Mycotoxin analysis is essential for assessing the safety of fermented okara, as filamentous fungi may produce toxic secondary metabolites during fermentation. These results indicate that, under the conditions tested in this study, MW-1 fermentation did not lead to significant mycotoxin accumulation, thereby demonstrating the good safety profile of the fermentation process.

Structural analysis of okara before and after fermentation

SEM, FTIR and XRD analysis

The microstructure and structural changes of okara during fermentation were characterized by SEM, FTIR, and XRD. As shown in Fig. 2A, unfermented okara exhibited a highly aggregated and flaky morphology, whereas fermented samples contained smaller particles, more fragmented surfaces, and greater fungal biomass. With increasing fermentation time, the surface became progressively fractured and more porous, which may have increased substrate accessibility and exposure of functional groups. FTIR analysis further revealed pronounced changes in the major functional groups of okara after MW-1 fermentation (Fig. 2B). In particular, the broad absorption band at 3000–3700 cm⁻1 shifted to lower wavenumbers and increased in intensity, suggesting alterations in hydrogen bonding interactions and the microenvironment of hydroxyl groups. [40]. In addition, the characteristic peaks at 2930 cm⁻1 and 1730 cm⁻1 were enhanced, indicating structural rearrangements involving aliphatic chains and carbonyl-containing groups [27]. The strengthened absorption peaks at 1648 cm⁻1 (amide I) and 1536 cm⁻1 (amide II) indicated changes in protein-associated structures [41]. Meanwhile, the peaks at 1053 and 893 cm⁻1 were assigned to the CO stretching vibration of cellulose polysaccharides and the characteristic absorption of β-glycosidic linkages, respectively [42]. XRD analysis showed a distinct diffraction peak at 21.6° in both unfermented and fermented okara, corresponding to the crystalline region of cellulose I. (Fig. 2C). Thermal stability analysis

TG and DTG analyses revealed three main stages of thermal degradation (Fig. 2D and E). The initial stage (30–120 °C) primarily corresponds to moisture evaporation and loss of volatile components [43], the intermediate stage (200–400 °C) reflects the thermal degradation of dietary fiber [44], and the final stage (400–500 °C) represents the carbonization decomposition process. It is noteworthy that fermentation significantly extends the thermal degradation range of the initial stage, fully demonstrating the effectiveness of fermentation in modifying the structure of key components in okara. In the intermediate degradation stage, the characteristic peak shifts significantly toward lower temperatures. This change indicated that the fermentation promotes significant degradation of IDF and transformation of SDF. Notably, the residue content of fermented okara at 550 °C increases significantly from 17.81 % to 28.32 %. This change directly reflected the increase in mycelium, indicating that the fermentation effect of MW-1 significantly altered the thermal degradation behavior of okara.

Processing performance of okara

WHC and OHC analysis

WHC and OHC influence the texture and stability of food products. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, the WHC continuously increased with prolonged fermentation time, rising significantly from 3.17 g/g to 4.23 g/g after 96 h of fermentation. Fermentation also markedly altered the OHC of okara, with its value increasing from 2.47 g/g to 5.88 g/g after 96 h (Fig. 3B). The WHC and OHC of okara are significantly influenced by its chemical composition and physical structure [45]. Specifically, the proportions of SDF and protein notably increased at 48 h. These changes led to a higher proportion of components with excellent water-holding capacity, while the structure of okara transitioned from a dense state to a looser and more crumbly texture.Fig. 3Changes in WHC(A), OHC(B), SC(C), EAI(D) during fermentation of okara.

Changes in WHC(A), OHC(B), SC(C), EAI(D) during fermentation of okara.

SC analysis

The SC is one of the key indicators characterizing the hydration properties of food [46]. As shown in Fig. 3C, the SC value of okara exhibited a continuous upward trend during fermentation, reaching 10.80 mL/g at 120 h. This change may be related to alterations in physicochemical properties, such as the dissociation of cellulose-protein complexes, the dissolution of soluble substances, the decrease in particle size and the breakdown of surface morphology.

EAI analysis

Okara with high emulsifying capacity is widely used in meat, baked, and dairy products, improving texture, extending shelf life, reducing oil loss, and enhancing processing efficiency and quality [47]. The EAI was measured using the classical turbidimetric method, and the results are shown in Fig. 3D. The EAI value of unfermented okara was 7.06 m2/g. During the fermentation process, the EAI value of okara increased significantly, reaching a peak of 39.47 m2/g at 96 h, and then stabilized. The enhancement of the emulsifying properties of fermented okara may be attributed to smaller particle size and a higher content of soluble components resulting from fermentation [48].

Effect of ultrasonic pretreatment on the composition and structure of okara

Analysis of the nutritional composition of okara after ultrasonic treatment

To clarify how ultrasound promotes nutrient release and dietary fiber conversion in okara, the major chemical components were analyzed after treatments at different ultrasonic power levels (Fig. 4). The peptide content initially increased and then decreased, reaching a maximum of 39.32 mg/g at 150 W, 31.06 % higher than that of the untreated control. In contrast, free amino acid and soluble dietary fiber contents increased continuously with ultrasonic power, reaching 2.09 mg/g and 5.41 g/100 g at 450 W, respectively, corresponding to increases of 64.10 % and 46.61 % compared with the control. Cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin decreased significantly under 450 W treatment, by 13.04 %, 24.39 %, and 11.31 %, respectively. These changes were mainly attributed to ultrasonic cavitation, mechanical shear, and transient localized high temperature and pressure, which disrupted the dense cell-wall structure and fibrous network of okara [26], [49]. These effects facilitated protein degradation and peptide bond cleavage, promoted the release of bound amino acids, and enhanced the conversion of IDF into SDF. Overall, ultrasound treatment transformed okara from a dense, intact, and poorly soluble matrix into a loose, fragmented, and more soluble structure, thereby reducing protein molecular size and increasing the solubility of insoluble fibers. This structural remodeling improved the bioavailability of nutritional components in okara and provided a more suitable substrate for subsequent fermentation, bioprocessing, and protein extraction.Fig. 4Changes in peptide (A), free amino acid (B), SDF (C), cellulose (D), hemicellulose (E), and lignin (F) contents of okara under different ultrasonic power treatments.

Changes in peptide (A), free amino acid (B), SDF (C), cellulose (D), hemicellulose (E), and lignin (F) contents of okara under different ultrasonic power treatments.

FTIR analysis

FTIR analysis showed that ultrasonic treatment significantly altered the functional group structure of okara (Fig. 5A). The broad absorption band at 3500–3250 cm⁻1 was assigned to O–H stretching vibration, while the weak peaks at 2925 and 2854 cm⁻1 corresponded to C–H stretching vibrations in polysaccharides [33]. The increased intensities of these peaks after ultrasonication indicated changes in hydrogen-bonding interactions among hydroxyl groups. The absorption peaks within 1200–1800 cm⁻1 were mainly associated with hemicellulose, pectin, lignin, and cellulose structures [51]. The peaks at 1745 and 1651 cm⁻1 were related to esterified C=O groups and lignin aromatic structures, respectively, whereas those near 1421 and 1375 cm⁻1 were characteristic peaks of cellulose [51]. In the carbohydrate fingerprint region (950–1200 cm⁻1), the decreased intensities of the peaks at 1161, 1056, and 892 cm⁻1 indicated the disruption and degradation of hemicellulose and polysaccharide structures [50]. Overall, ultrasonic treatment promoted the depolymerization of okara fiber and partially disrupted its amorphous regions.Fig. 5Effects of ultrasonic pretreatment on the FTIR (A), XRD (B), thermogravimetric (C: Weight (%)), DTG (D: Weight loss rate (%/min)) and SEM (D) structural characteristics of okara and its subsequent fermentation properties (F, G).

XRD analysis

XRD was used to characterize the molecular aggregation state of okara (Fig. 5B). A distinct diffraction peak at approximately 34.6° was observed both before and after ultrasound treatment, indicating the presence of crystalline cellulose regions [52]. After ultrasonication, the intensity of this peak decreased, suggesting partial disruption of the crystalline structure, possibly due to cavitation-induced cleavage of β-1,4-glycosidic bonds and polysaccharide degradation [53]. In addition, a diffraction peak at approximately 21.1°, corresponding to the amorphous region, was also observed [54], [55]. This peak became weaker after ultrasound treatment, indicating partial degradation of the amorphous structure. Overall, ultrasound caused more pronounced disruption in the amorphous region than in the crystalline region of okara fiber.

Thermal degradation behavior analysis

As shown in Fig. 5C and D, ultrasound treatment altered the thermal degradation behavior of okara. Compared with the untreated sample, the ultrasound-treated sample showed a higher residual mass, indicating increased char yield and enhanced thermal stability [56], [57]. The thermal weight loss of okara mainly occurred in three stages: moisture evaporation, thermal decomposition of fibrous polysaccharides, and final carbonization, with the intermediate-temperature region representing the major weight-loss stage [57]. The DTG curves showed that the main thermal decomposition peak shifted to a higher temperature after ultrasound treatment, suggesting delayed thermal degradation and a higher temperature requirement to reach the maximum weight-loss rate [58]. The increase in SDF may also have contributed to the improved thermal stability [59].

SEM analysis

As shown in Fig. 5E, the morphology of okara changed markedly after ultrasound treatment. Untreated okara exhibited a dense, intact block-like structure with a relatively smooth surface and few visible fragments. After ultrasound treatment, the original compact structure was disrupted and transformed into exfoliated, stacked lamellar structures with a more pronounced honeycomb-like morphology. These results indicate that ultrasonication disrupted the structural integrity and fiber network of okara, reduced particle size, loosened the matrix, and increased the effective contact area for microorganisms, thereby facilitating nutrient release and substrate utilization during subsequent fermentation.

Analysis of the protein enrichment capacity of MW-1 fermentation with ultrasonic pretreatment of okara

Although ultrasonic pretreatment did not alter the overall trend of protein enrichment during MW-1 fermentation of okara, it significantly improved fermentation efficiency and shortened the fermentation period. Among the treatments, the 450 W pretreatment showed the most pronounced effect, reducing the required fermentation time from 120 h to 72 h. At 96 h, the crude protein content of the 450 W-pretreated okara was 2.09 g/100 g higher than that of the control (Fig. 5F). Meanwhile, the levels of four mycotoxins in the fermented okara under this treatment condition were all below the regulatory limits (Fig. 5G), indicating good fermentation safety. The combined chemical composition and structural characterization results showed that ultrasonic pretreatment increased the contents of peptides, free amino acids, and soluble dietary fiber, while reducing cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin contents. It also disrupted the original dense block-like structure of okara, loosened the fiber network, reduced particle size, and increased the specific surface area. These changes promoted nutrient release and improved substrate accessibility, thereby enhancing the fermentation efficiency and protein-enrichment capacity of MW-1 in okara.

Optimization of the extraction process for MOP

Ultrasonic power and treatment time were selected as the key parameters for optimizing protein extraction. As shown in Fig. 6, protein recovery initially increased and then decreased as ultrasonic power and treatment time increased. The optimized condition was 450 W for 15 min, under which the protein recovery reached 67.32 %. Overall, ultrasonic power had a more pronounced effect on protein recovery than treatment time. This behavior may be related to the structural characteristics of the fermented okara–mycelium complex. At low ultrasonic intensity or short treatment time, cavitation and mechanical shear were insufficient to disrupt the dense okara matrix and fungal cell walls, thereby limiting protein release [60]. As ultrasonic power and treatment time increased, enhanced cavitation promoted disruption of the fungal biomass–substrate matrix and improved mass transfer, thereby facilitating protein dissolution and increasing recovery [61]. However, excessive ultrasonic power or prolonged treatment may induce protein conformational changes, denaturation, or aggregation through intense cavitation and localized transient high temperature and pressure, ultimately reducing protein solubility and recovery [62].Fig. 6Effects of ultrasonic power and treatment time on protein recovery efficiency.

Structural characterization of MOP

Microstructure of MOP

SEM showed that UAE at different power levels markedly altered the microstructure of the protein samples. The 0 W group (Fig. 7A, B) exhibited a relatively intact and compact lamellar structure with a smooth surface and tightly aggregated particles. After treatment at 150 W (Fig. 7C, D), the samples displayed a certain degree of fracture, wrinkling, and loosening, accompanied by the formation of localized pores, indicating that moderate ultrasound weakened the aggregation among protein molecules. In contrast, the 450 W group (Fig. 7E, F) showed much more pronounced structural disruption, with the dense lamellar structure transforming into a loose, porous network- or honeycomb-like morphology, characterized by a substantial increase in pores and a more open structure. These results suggest that high-power ultrasound promoted the dissociation of protein aggregates and structural unfolding through stronger cavitation and mechanical shear effects, thereby increasing the specific surface area and interfacial contact opportunities of the samples and providing a structural basis for the improved emulsifying and foaming properties [63], [64].Fig. 7Microstructure of protein components in fermented materials under different ultrasonic power treatments (A, B: 0 W; C, D: 150 W; E, F: 450 W).

ζ-potential and size analysis of MOP

Z-potential reflects the surface charge of particles and is an important indicator of dispersion stability. A higher absolute ζ-potential generally indicates stronger electrostatic repulsion and greater resistance to aggregation [65]. In this study, UAE increased the absolute ζ-potential of MOP from 12.85 to 17.50 mV, suggesting enhanced surface charge repulsion (Fig. 8A). This change may be attributed to ultrasound-induced protein unfolding, which exposed more negatively charged groups [66]. Ultrasound significantly reduced the particle size of MOP from 1388.09 nm in the untreated samples to 613.56 and 412.45 nm after 150 and 450 W treatments, respectively (p < 0.05) (Fig. 8B). This reduction may be attributed to cavitation, microstreaming, and turbulence generated during ultrasound treatment. Surface hydrophobicity and protein flexibility analysis

Surface hydrophobicity (H0) reflects the exposure of hydrophobic groups on the protein surface [68]. Protein flexibility was evaluated by trypsin hydrolysis, because hydrolysis degree reflects molecular chain mobility and enzyme accessibility [69]. As shown in Fig. 8C, the H0 of MOP first increased and then slightly decreased with increasing ultrasound power. The increase in H0 at moderate ultrasound power suggests that cavitation and mechanical shear promoted partial protein unfolding. This exposed hydrophobic regions that were originally buried inside the protein structure. This result was consistent with the FTIR analysis. Ultrasound treatment increased the β-sheet content and reduced the α-helix and random coil contents, indicating secondary-structure rearrangement (Table 1). These changes suggest that protein unfolding was followed by molecular reorganization. The exposed hydrophobic patches could strengthen intermolecular hydrophobic interactions and promote protein association. The lower H0 at 450 W compared with 150 W may be related to excessive ultrasound-induced aggregation. Under high-power ultrasound, some exposed hydrophobic groups may be re-embedded within aggregates, thereby reducing the measured surface hydrophobicity. A similar trend was observed for protein flexibility (Fig. 8D). Moderate ultrasound treatment, especially at 150 W, increased protein flexibility by improving molecular chain mobility and enzyme accessibility. Secondary structure analysis of MOP

FTIR spectroscopy was used to evaluate changes in protein structure and functional group microenvironments. The amide I band (1600–1700 cm⁻1), mainly associated with C=O stretching, is sensitive to protein secondary structure, while the broad band at 3100–3600 cm⁻1 reflects O–H and N–H stretching related to hydrogen bonding [70]. As shown in Fig. 8E, the peak near 3300 cm⁻1 shifted to a higher wavenumber after ultrasonication, suggesting weakened hydrogen bonds and changes in the protein microenvironment. Variations in the amide I and II bands further indicated that ultrasound modified protein functional group vibrations and promoted secondary-structure rearrangement [71]. As shown in Table 1, ultrasonic treatment increased β-sheet content while reducing α-helix and random coil contents, implying protein unfolding, reorganization, and formation of β-sheet-rich aggregated structures. These structural changes agreed with the results of flexibility and surface hydrophobicity, and may further influence the solubility, emulsifying properties, and foaming properties of MOP.

XRD analysis of MOP

XRD analysis was used to characterize the crystalline structural features of proteins, thereby providing a basis for revealing their structural changes and functional properties. Fig. 8F showed the XRD patterns of MOP before and after UAE. Characteristic diffraction peaks were observed at around 2θ = 8° and 19° in all samples, which are generally attributed to the α-helix and β-sheet structures of proteins, respectively, consistent with previous reports [72]. Compared with the untreated group, the crystallinity of MOP increased after UAE. In particular, the diffraction peak intensity at 19°, corresponding to the β-conformation, was markedly enhanced in the 150 W and 450 W treatment groups, indicating a greater degree of protein ultrasound-induced structural rearrangement, which was consistent with the FTIR results.

UV and fluorescence spectroscopic analysis of MOP

UV absorption spectroscopy can be used to monitor protein structural changes because aromatic amino acid residues contain chromophoric groups that absorb light at specific wavelengths. In particular, tryptophan (Trp) showed a typical absorption band around 285 nm [73]. Changes in protein conformation can modify the microenvironment of aromatic residues, thereby affecting UV absorption. As shown in Fig. 8G, the UV absorption intensity of MOP increased markedly with increasing ultrasonic power, suggesting that ultrasound promoted protein unfolding and exposed more buried hydrophobic regions.

Fluorescence spectroscopy is also widely applied to evaluate protein conformation. The intrinsic fluorescence of proteins mainly originates from Trp, tyrosine (Tyr), and phenylalanine (Phe), among which Trp contributes most strongly [74]. As presented in Fig. 8H, the fluorescence intensity of MOP first increased and then decreased as ultrasonic power increased. This trend indicates that appropriate ultrasound treatment disrupted intramolecular hydrophobic interactions and enhanced molecular unfolding. However, excessive ultrasonic power may induce protein aggregation or structural rearrangement, which could reduce fluorescence intensity.

TG and DTG analysis of MOP

Fig. 8I and J showed the TG and DTG curves of proteins extracted under different ultrasonic powers. The results indicated that different ultrasonic treatments significantly affected the thermal stability of MOP. During the 50–200 °C stage, the 450 W-treated sample showed the greatest mass loss, suggesting that it was more prone to water evaporation and loss of low-molecular-weight compounds. This may be related to the looser protein structure and weakened water-binding capacity induced by high-power ultrasound. During the 200–300 °C stage, the 450 W-treated sample exhibited a more pronounced decrease in mass, indicating more intense thermal decomposition in this temperature range. This may be attributed to ultrasound-induced protein unfolding and weakened intramolecular interactions, thereby reducing structural stability [75]. In contrast, during the 300–500 °C stage, the 450 W-treated sample showed the lowest mass loss, suggesting that high-power ultrasound may promote molecular rearrangement or aggregation, leading to the formation of more compact thermal degradation residues. Overall, UAE exerted a stage-dependent effect on the thermal degradation behavior of MOP, and high-power ultrasound significantly affected the composition and thermal stability of the protein components.

Functional properties of MOP

Solubility analysis

Protein solubility is an important indicator of protein functionality [76]. As shown in Fig. 9A, UAE significantly affected the solubility of MOP. The untreated group showed the highest solubility of 63.72 %, whereas the solubility decreased to 51.11 % after treatment at 450 W. This decrease was closely related to ultrasound-induced structural changes. High-power ultrasound promoted protein unfolding and exposed buried hydrophobic groups, as indicated by the UV and fluorescence spectra. The increased crystallinity observed by XRD further suggested molecular rearrangement into a more ordered structure. These changes likely strengthened hydrophobic interactions and promoted protein aggregation, thereby reducing the availability of hydrophilic sites and lowering solubility. In addition, cavitation-generated free radicals may have induced sulfhydryl oxidation and disulfide bond formation, further enhancing protein cross-linking. WHC and OHC analysis

WHC and OHC are important indicators of protein functionality. These properties affect texture regulation, flavor retention, and processing stability [77]. As shown in Fig. 9B and C, WHC increased progressively with ultrasound power and was 28.8 % higher at 450 W than in the untreated group. OHC decreased from 1.87 g/g oil in the untreated sample to 1.43 g/g oil at 150 W, but then increased to 2.75 g/g oil at 450 W. The loose, porous structure produced at high power facilitated water penetration and retention, thereby increasing WHC. The initial decrease in OHC may reflect partial unfolding without sufficient pore formation or favorable rearrangement of hydrophobic sites. At 450 W, greater exposure of hydrophobic groups and formation of a porous network enhanced oil entrapment. Thus, ultrasound improved WHC throughout the tested range and improved OHC only at high power.

Emulsifying properties analysis

The emulsifying activity index (EAI) of MOP increased with ultrasound power (Fig. 9D). The EAI increased from 38.12 m2/g in the untreated group to 43.64 and 47.64 m2/g in the 150 W and 450 W groups, respectively. This result indicates that UAE improved the emulsion-forming ability of MOP. Although the solubility of MOP decreased after high-power ultrasound treatment, EAI is not determined solely by protein solubility. Particle size, specific surface area, and interfacial adsorption also play important roles. As shown in Fig. 8B, ultrasound markedly reduced the particle size of MOP. Smaller particles have a larger specific surface area and more interfacial contact sites. This may promote protein diffusion and adsorption at the oil–water interface. In addition, ultrasound-induced protein unfolding and the release of interfacially active mycoprotein components may expose more hydrophobic groups. This could improve the affinity of MOP for the oil phase. Therefore, the higher EAI of ultrasound-treated MOP may be mainly attributed to its smaller particle size, larger surface area, and enhanced interfacial adsorption ability. In contrast, UAE had a limited effect on emulsion stability (Fig. 9E). The emulsion stability values of the 0 W, 150 W, and 450 W groups were 83.33, 85.65, and 82.87 min, respectively. The 150 W group showed the highest value and was significantly higher than the 450 W group. However, the untreated group did not differ significantly from either ultrasound-treated group. These results suggest that UAE improved the emulsifying activity of MOP while maintaining relatively good emulsion stability. The slight decrease in emulsion stability at 450 W may be caused by excessive protein unfolding and partial aggregation. Although ultrasound promoted protein adsorption at the oil–water interface, high-power treatment may weaken the compactness and mechanical strength of the interfacial film. This could make the emulsion less resistant to droplet aggregation during storage. Therefore, moderate ultrasound treatment improved emulsion formation, whereas high-power ultrasound slightly reduced emulsion stability.

Foaming properties analysis

In terms of foaming properties (Fig. 9F and G), UAE showed different effects on the foaming ability (FA) and foam stability (FS) of MOP. The FC increased from 16.25 % in the untreated group to 17.95 % and 20.25 % in the 150 W- and 450 W-treated groups, respectively [34]. This result indicates that ultrasound improved the FA of MOP. The increase in FA may be related to protein unfolding, particle-size reduction, and the release of interfacially active mycoprotein components. These changes could promote protein diffusion and adsorption at the air–water interface, thereby facilitating foam formation. In contrast, FS decreased after UAE. This suggests that ultrasound improved initial foam formation but weakened foam retention during storage. FA and FS are controlled by different mechanisms. FA mainly depends on the rapid adsorption and rearrangement of proteins at the air–water interface, whereas FS depends on the compactness and mechanical strength of the interfacial film [78]. High-power ultrasound may induce excessive protein unfolding, partial aggregation, and weakened intermolecular interactions. As a result, the interfacial film may become looser and less cohesive. Such a film is less effective in resisting liquid drainage, bubble coalescence, and foam collapse. Therefore, UAE enhanced the initial FA of MOP, but reduced its long-term foam stability to some extent.

Conclusion

In this study, Aspergillus luchuensis MW-1 fermentation combined with ultrasound-assisted processing was developed as an integrated strategy for okara valorization and protein functionality improvement. MW-1 fermentation promoted the degradation of macromolecular components in okara and increased the accumulation of protein, peptides, and soluble dietary fiber, while maintaining acceptable mycotoxin safety. Ultrasound pretreatment disrupted the dense fibrous matrix of okara through cavitation, mechanical shear, acoustic streaming, and localized transient effects, thereby improving substrate accessibility and shortening the protein enrichment period during fermentation. Moreover, UAE significantly enhanced protein recovery, reaching 67.32 % under the optimized condition of 450 W for 15 min. Ultrasound treatment also modified the structural characteristics of okara protein by reducing particle size, increasing the absolute value of ζ-potential, enhancing surface hydrophobicity, and inducing secondary-structure rearrangement, which contributed to improved emulsifying activity and foaming capacity. However, excessive ultrasound treatment may induce protein aggregation and reduce solubility and foam stability. The main novelty of this study lies in establishing a systematic connection among ultrasound-induced okara matrix disruption, enhanced fungal fermentation, improved protein extraction, and protein functionality regulation. These findings indicate that ultrasound can serve not only as an extraction-assisted technique but also as a process-intensification strategy for upgrading agricultural by-products. Nevertheless, this study mainly focused on the recovery and modification of protein fractions, while the changes and integrated utilization of other valuable components, such as dietary fiber and polysaccharides, require further investigation. In addition, the present work was conducted using a fixed ultrasonic frequency of 20 kHz and at the laboratory scale; therefore, future studies should further evaluate frequency effects, energy efficiency, process stability, scale-up feasibility, equipment adaptability, and cost-effectiveness. Overall, this study provides a promising fermentation–ultrasound strategy for the high-value utilization of okara and the development of sustainable alternative protein resources.
