IRF‐1 modulates hepatic ferroptosis and aggravates liver ischemia/reperfusion injury via DYRK1α
Authors: Jinping Zhang, Jinming Zhang, Siyuan Song, Mingyang Li, Liying Sun, Zhijun Zhu, Dongdong Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe purpose is to define the contribution of the interferon regulatory factor‐1–dual‐specificity tyrosine phosphorylation‐regulated kinase 1α (IRF‐1–DYRK1α) axis to hepatocellular ferroptosis during liver ischemia/reperfusion injury (LIRI).MethodsFerroptosis was induced in AML12 hepatocytes with erastin. After gain‐ or loss‐of‐function manipulation of IRF‐1 and DYRK1α, we quantified glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (ACSL4), reactive oxygen species (ROS), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH). In vivo, wild‐type (WT) mice, IRF‐1 knockout (Irf1−/−) mice, and WT mice treated with the DYRK1α inhibitor harmine underwent a 70% warm LIRI model. Liver and blood were collected for histology, serum transaminases, cytokine ELISA, protein analyses, and flow cytometry.ResultsIRF‐1 knockdown attenuated LIRI, accompanied by suppression of hepatocellular ferroptosis. DYRK1α inhibition reversed IRF‐1–mediated decreases in GSH and GPX4 and prevented increases in MDA, ROS, and ACSL4 in AML12 cells, indicating DYRK1α participation in IRF‐1–driven ferroptosis. In mice, pharmacologic inhibition of DYRK1α with harmine reduced hepatic ferroptosis and ameliorated LIRI.ConclusionIRF‐1 aggravates LIRI, at least in part, by promoting hepatocellular ferroptosis via DYRK1α. Targeting the IRF‐1–DYRK1α axis may offer a therapeutic strategy to limit perioperative liver injury.Keywords: ACSL4, DYRK1α, ferroptosis, GPX4, interferon regulatory factor‐1 (IRF‐1), liver ischemia–reperfusion injury, oxidative stress

INTRODUCTION

Liver ischemia–reperfusion injury (LIRI) is the most common complication of liver surgery and markedly influences postoperative outcomes. 1 Owing to the unique complexity of liver transplantation, ischemia–reperfusion injury is especially severe during transplantation. 2 Elucidating the pathophysiology of LIRI and developing strategies to mitigate hepatic damage therefore remain central goals in hepatobiliary surgery. 3

Ferroptosis is a regulated form of cell death driven by iron‐dependent lipid peroxidation, first defined by Brent R. Stockwell in 2012. 4 As the principal organ for iron storage and metabolism, the liver is particularly susceptible to ferroptotic injury, which contributes to multiple hepatic diseases. 5 Importantly, ferroptosis is a key effector in LIRI. 6 Unlike apoptosis, necrosis, or autophagy, ferroptosis is characterized by mitochondrial oxidative injury that amplifies reperfusion damage. Prior studies show that ferroptosis inhibitors (e.g., Fer‐1) and iron chelators attenuate LIRI, positioning ferroptosis as a promising therapeutic target and motivating deeper mechanistic investigation. 7

Interferon regulatory factor‐1 (IRF‐1) is a transcription factor that binds interferon‐stimulated response elements (ISREs) and orchestrates interferon‐induced signaling. 8 IRF‐1 has been implicated in the pathogenesis of diverse liver disorders and in aggravating ischemia–reperfusion injury during transplantation, 9 , 10 via mechanisms that include promoting hepatocyte apoptosis and inducing autophagy. 11 , 12 IRF‐1 has also been linked to ferroptosis across disease contexts, including cancer, 13 , 14 , 15 prompting us to examine whether IRF‐1 drives ferroptosis in LIRI.

Dual‐specificity tyrosine phosphorylation‐regulated kinase 1α (DYRK1α) autophosphorylates its activation‐loop tyrosine and phosphorylates serine/threonine residues on substrates. 16 DYRK1α has been associated with Down syndrome phenotypes—intellectual disability, early neurodegeneration, and congenital heart defects 17 , 18 —and participates in cell proliferation, transcriptional control, stress responses, DNA damage repair, apoptosis, and angiogenesis, thereby contributing to tumor progression. 19 Emerging evidence indicates that DYRK1α modulates ferroptosis and is involved in myocardial infarction pathogenesis. 20 , 21 , 22 However, how DYRK1α regulates ferroptosis in the liver and its role in LIRI remain unclear.

Here, we show that IRF‐1 regulates ferroptosis after LIRI. DYRK1α expression increased after LIRI but was reduced in IRF‐1–deficient mice. Genetic or pharmacologic inhibition of DYRK1α suppressed iron‐dependent ferroptosis and alleviated liver injury in LIRI models. These findings suggest that IRF‐1 promotes ferroptosis, at least in part, by upregulating DYRK1α. Collectively, our data identify an IRF‐1–DYRK1α axis that accelerates ferroptosis and worsens hepatic damage, offering a potential therapeutic avenue for the prevention and treatment of LIRI.

METHODS

Animal models

Male wild‐type (WT) and IRF‐1 knockout (KO) mice (C57BL/6 background; 18–20 g) were obtained from Beijing Shenrui Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed in a temperature‐controlled facility on a 12‐h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to standard chow and sterile water.

To induce liver LIRI, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the portal vein branches, which were protected with saline‐soaked gauze. The left portal branch was occluded with a microvascular clamp for 60 min while mice were maintained on a heating pad to preserve normothermia. The clamp was then removed to allow reperfusion, and the abdomen was closed in layers. At predefined time points, animals were euthanized, and liver tissue was harvested.

For DYRK1α inhibition studies, mice were administered harmine (50 mg/kg) or vehicle (phosphate‐buffered saline, PBS) via tail vein once daily for five consecutive days prior to LIRI. 23 , 24

Histology and immunohistochemistry

Liver tissue was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned. Hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining was performed to assess histopathology. For immunohistochemistry, sections were probed with primary antibodies against acyl‐CoA synthetase long‐chain family member 4 (ACSL4), glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), ferritin heavy chain (FTH), IRF‐1, and DYRK1α (Abcam, Cambridge, UK), followed by appropriate secondary antibodies, and developed per standard protocols.

Transmission electron microscopy

After being rinsed with PBS, liver samples were fixed overnight in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/4% paraformaldehyde, then washed in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer containing 4% sucrose (pH 7.2). Post‐fixation was performed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OsO4) in 0.1 mol/L phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) for 90 min. Samples were dehydrated, embedded in epoxy resin, and polymerized at 60°C for 72‐h. Ultrathin sections (70–80 nm) were stained with 2% uranyl acetate and lead citrate (Renner) and examined on a Hitachi H‐7700 transmission electron microscope (Hitachi High‐Technologies, Tokyo, Japan).

Cell culture

The normal mouse liver cell line AML12 (Procell, Wuhan, China) was cultured in DMEM/F12 (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 1% 10 × insulin‐transferrin‐selenium (ITS‐G) (MedChemExpress, New Jersey, USA) supplement, and 40 ng/mL dexamethasone. To establish an in vitro hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model, AML12 cells were transferred to glucose‐free, serum‐free medium and incubated in an anoxic chamber (94% N2, 5% CO2, 1% O2). After 6‐h of anoxia, the glucose‐free, serum‐free medium was replaced with standard culture medium containing 10% FBS, and the cells were transferred to a normoxic incubator for reoxygenation. To induce ferroptosis in vitro, cells were exposed to 10 μmol/L erastin (MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) for 48‐h before further analysis.

Transfection

AML12 cells were seeded in a 12‐well plate and allowed to grow until they reached 40%–50% confluence, at which point viral infection was initiated. The culture medium was replaced with 500 μL of half‐strength complete medium. Subsequently, 20 μL of adenoviral vectors (HanBio, Shanghai, China) solution was added to each well, along with Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) reagent to enhance transduction efficiency. After 12‐h of incubation, an additional 500 μL of complete medium was added to each well to restore the full culture volume. At 24‐h post‐infection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were maintained under standard culture conditions. Fluorescence microscopy was performed 48‐h post‐infection to assess transduction efficiency. Given that the virus carries both GFP and a puromycin resistance gene, the presence of a green fluorescent signal indicated successful infection. Once cell morphology stabilized, selection was initiated by replacing the medium with puromycin‐containing complete medium. Based on preliminary dose–response experiments, a concentration of 15 μg/mL puromycin was determined to effectively eliminate non‐infected cells. After 48‐h of puromycin selection, successfully transduced cells were collected and passaged for further expansion.

Approximately 3 × 105 AML12 cells were seeded into a pre‐prepared 24‐well plate, and transfection was carried out when the cell confluence reached 30%–50%. siRNA negative control (NC), siRNA IRF‐1(Ribobio, Guangzhou, China), or siRNA DYRK1α (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) (approximately 2500 ng) was diluted with 30 μL of 1× riboEFECTTM CP buffer (Ribobio), followed by gentle mixing. Subsequently, 3 μL of riboEFECTTM CP reagent solution containing the siRNA complex was added to the cells, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. The transfection mixture was then added to 465.75 μL of antibiotic‐free culture medium to achieve a final RNA concentration of 50 nmol/L. Cells were cultured under standard growth conditions in this medium for 24‐h, and successfully transduced cells were collected and passaged for further expansion.

Real‐time reverse transcription‐polymerase chain reaction

Total mRNA was extracted from AML12 cells and liver tissues using TRIzol reagent (Solarbio, Beijing, China). For quantitative analysis, the high‐capacity cDNA reverse transcription kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China) was used for reverse transcription, and cDNA amplification was performed with SYBR Green reagent (Tiangen). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted on an Applied Biosystems 7300 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and data were analyzed using the threshold cycle (Ct) method.

Western blot

Proteins were extracted from mouse liver tissues and AML12 murine hepatocyte cells using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with a commercially available cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Solarbio). Primary antibodies against ACSL4 (1:1000), GPX4 (1:1000), FTH (1:2000), prostaglandin‐endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2) (1:1000), IRF‐1 (1:1000), DYRK1α (1:1000), and glyceraldehyde 3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (1:1000) (all from Abcam) were used, along with corresponding secondary antibodies (1:2000; Keshun Bio, Shanghai, China). Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Rockford, USA).

Malondialdehyde and glutathione content detection

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced glutathione (GSH) concentrations in mouse liver tissues were quantified using commercially available colorimetric assay kits (Solarbio), following the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were mixed with the working solution and incubated for 60 min, followed by centrifugation at 10 000 × g (MDA) or 80 000 × g (GSH) for 10 min. The supernatants were collected for absorbance measurement, and the concentrations of MDA and GSH were calculated accordingly.

ELISA

Fresh mouse blood was centrifuged at 2000–3000 rpm for 20 min to collect the supernatant. The supernatant was diluted and added to an enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plate pre‐coated with T‐bilirubin(Biolab, Beijing, China) or alanine aminotransferase (ALT; Solarbio) or aspartate aminotransferase (AST; Solarbio) antibody, along with proportionally diluted standard solutions. The plate was sealed with a sealing film and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After incubation, the washing solution was added, allowed to stand for 30 s, and then aspirated. Except for the blank wells, 50 μL of enzyme conjugate reagent was added to each well. The plate was resealed and incubated again at 37°C for 30 min. After another wash step, the chromogenic substrate was added, and the plate was incubated at 37°C in the dark for 15 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of stop solution to each well. Absorbance values were measured at 450 nm, and the concentrations of ALT and AST were calculated based on the standard curve.

Reactive oxygen species fluorometric assay

The 2,7‐Bis(chloromethyl)fluorescein protein acetate (DCFH‐DA) assay kit (Thermo Fisher, Massachusetts, USA) was used to measure intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) fluorescence intensity. AML12 cells were harvested from the incubator and detached using 0.25% trypsin–0.53 mmol/L EDTA solution. Cells were incubated at 37°C for 1–2 min to achieve partial dissociation, followed by addition of 3–4 mL pre‐warmed culture medium containing 10% FBS to quench trypsin activity. Subsequently, cells were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 800 μL of DCFH‐DA working solution (10 μmol/L) for the experimental group. For the negative control, the pellet was resuspended in 800 μL of PBS. For the positive control, the pellet was resuspended in 800 μL of DCFH‐DA working solution (20 μmol/L) supplemented with 10 μL of ROS donor. All tubes were incubated at 37°C for 60 min in the dark. After incubation, samples were centrifuged again at 1000 × g for 5 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was carefully aspirated. Cell pellets were gently resuspended in an appropriate volume of PBS and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry using FITC fluorescence detection settings. Data were processed using FlowJo, version 10.8.1 software. Single‐cell populations were first gated based on forward scatter area (FSC‐A) versus side scatter area (SSC‐A). Subsequently, FITC‐A fluorescence intensity was quantified via histogram analysis, and the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was extracted as the primary readout—reflecting intracellular oxidation of DCFH to fluorescent DCF. A standardized gating and analysis strategy was applied uniformly across all samples to ensure comparability.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The statistical significance of differences between two groups was assessed using the Student's t‐test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's multiple‐comparison test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001; ns, not significant).

RESULTS

LIRI is associated with ferroptosis

In the murine model of LIRI, the group subjected to 12‐h of ischemia–reperfusion (IRI 12‐h group) exhibited the most extensive areas of hepatic necrosis when compared with the sham group, the IRI 6‐h group, and the IRI 24‐h group (Figure 1A). H&E staining further corroborated that the extent of liver damage in the IRI 12‐h group was significantly greater than that observed in the other groups (Figure 1B). Additionally, serum levels of ALT, AST, and bilirubin were markedly elevated in the IRI 12‐h group (Figure 1C). These findings suggest that liver injury reaches peak severity at 12‐h post‐reperfusion in our murine LIRI model. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that hepatocytes from the IRI 12‐h group displayed significantly shrunken mitochondria with a pronounced decrease or complete loss of mitochondrial cristae compared with the other groups (Figure 1D). Furthermore, a marked increase in lipid peroxidation and a significant depletion of GSH were observed in the IRI 12‐h group, indicating that LIRI is accompanied by ferroptosis (Figure 1E). To further validate the occurrence of ferroptosis, we assessed the expression of iron‐dependent proteins. The levels of ACSL4 and PTGS2 were significantly upregulated during LIRI, peaking at 12‐h post‐reperfusion. In contrast, the levels of protective proteins such as GPX4 and ferritin heavy chain (FTH) exhibited a marked decrease (Figure 1F). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed the upregulation of ACSL4 and the downregulation of GPX4 and FTH in injured liver tissue (Figure 1G). Collectively, these results indicate a strong association between LIRI and ferroptosis.

IRF‐1 is involved in the regulation of ferroptosis after LIRI

To elucidate the relationship between LIRI and ferroptosis, we employed a hypoxia–reoxygenation model using AML12 hepatocytes. Consistent with findings from the murine LIRI model, hypoxia–reoxygenation in AML12 cells induced ferroptosis, as evidenced by the upregulation of ACSL4 and PTGS2 alongside a reduction in GPX4 (Figure 2A,B). Notably, the expression of IRF‐1—a multifunctional transcription factor implicated in both LIRI and ferroptosis—was also elevated following hypoxia–reoxygenation (Figure 2A,B). To investigate the role of IRF‐1 in hypoxia–reoxygenation‐induced ferroptosis, we used siRNA to downregulate IRF‐1 in AML12 cells and subsequently reconstructed the hypoxia–reoxygenation model. Both quantitative PCR (qPCR) and Western blot analyses revealed that siRNA targeting IRF‐1 significantly decreased ACSL4 expression and reversed the hypoxia–reoxygenation‐induced downregulation of GPX4 (Figure 2C,D). These findings suggest that IRF‐1 plays a critical role in mediating hypoxia–reoxygenation‐induced ferroptosis in AML12 cells. To further validate the role of IRF‐1 in promoting ferroptosis, we induced ferroptosis with erastin while simultaneously manipulating IRF‐1 expression via adenoviral overexpression and siRNA knockdown. As illustrated in Figure 2E–G, overexpression of IRF‐1 markedly increased ACSL4 and ROS while concurrently reducing GPX4 in AML12 cells compared with empty‐vector controls. Conversely, knockdown of IRF‐1 significantly reversed these effects (Figure 2E–G). Additionally, lipid peroxidation and GSH levels exhibited similar trends in AML12 cells (Figure 2H). Collectively, these results demonstrate that IRF‐1 accelerates ferroptosis in erastin‐treated AML12 cells. Subsequently, we established an LIRI model with a 12‐h reperfusion period in IRF‐1 knockout mice. H&E staining revealed that liver injury in the IRF‐1 knockout group was significantly attenuated compared with the wild‐type group (Figure 3A). Serum ALT, AST, and bilirubin were also significantly reduced in the IRF‐1‐deficient group—consistent with previous studies 23 —indicating that IRF‐1 knockout can ameliorate LIRI (Figure 3B). Furthermore, mitochondrial damage in hepatocytes from IRF‐1 knockout mice subjected to 12‐h of ischemia–reperfusion was less severe than that observed in WT mice (Figure 3C). Both qPCR and Western blot analyses indicated that IRF‐1 knockout suppressed the LIRI‐mediated increase in hepatic ACSL4 and restored the levels of protective proteins GPX4 and FTH (Figure 3D,E). In line with our in vitro findings, IRF‐1 knockout LIRI mice exhibited higher hepatic GSH and decreased lipid peroxidation compared with wild‐type LIRI mice (Figure 3F). Immunohistochemical staining further confirmed the alterations in ferroptosis‐related proteins in injured livers following IRF‐1 knockout (Figure 3G). These results demonstrate that the depletion of IRF‐1 partially alleviates LIRI by suppressing ferroptosis.

DYRK1α promotes ferroptosis induced by LIRI

During LIRI, we observed a significant increase in DYRK1α expression, which peaked at 12‐h post‐IRI in affected liver tissues (Figure 4A). Hypoxia–reoxygenation likewise led to elevated DYRK1α expression in AML12 cells (Figure 4B). To elucidate the role of DYRK1α in LIRI, we employed the specific DYRK1α inhibitor harmine to inhibit its activity. Given that DYRK1α is implicated in ferroptosis, we hypothesized that DYRK1α may exacerbate LIRI by promoting ferroptosis. Indeed, following DYRK1α inhibition, ACSL4 expression significantly decreased in hypoxia–reoxygenated AML12 cells, whereas GPX4 was markedly upregulated (Figure 4C,D). Furthermore, ROS levels in harmine‐treated AML12 cells were lower than those in cells with intact DYRK1α activity after hypoxia–reoxygenation (Figure 4E). Using siRNA to knock down DYRK1α yielded similar alterations in ferroptosis‐associated markers (ACSL4, GPX4, and ROS) in hypoxia–reoxygenated AML12 cells (Figure 4F–H). These findings indicate that the inhibition of DYRK1α expression and activity attenuates ferroptosis in AML12 cells during hypoxia–reoxygenation.

Subsequently, we administered harmine to specifically inhibit DYRK1α activity in mice. Consistent with our in vitro observations, LIRI mice treated with harmine exhibited reduced hepatic ACSL4 and increased GPX4 compared with PBS‐treated LIRI mice (Figure 4I,J). Furthermore, harmine‐treated mice displayed significantly less liver damage than untreated controls (Figure 5A). In addition, livers from harmine‐treated mice showed elevated GSH and lower lipid peroxidation compared with PBS‐treated mice (Figure 5B). Immunohistochemical analysis further corroborated these findings; LIRI mice treated with harmine exhibited reduced hepatic ACSL4 and increased GPX4 compared with PBS‐treated LIRI mice (Figure 5C). These results demonstrate that inhibiting DYRK1α partially alleviates LIRI by reducing hepatic ferroptosis.

Inhibition of DYRK1α restrains IRF‐1‐mediated ferroptosis

The similar role of DYRK1α in ferroptosis induced by LIRI led us to hypothesize that IRF‐1 and DYRK1α may act in concert to exacerbate injury. Indeed, knockout of IRF‐1 significantly reduced DYRK1α expression in injured liver tissue (Figure 6A,B). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed that DYRK1α expression in livers from IRF‐1 knockout (IRF‐1−/−) mice subjected to LIRI was markedly lower than that in WT mice (Figure 6C).

To investigate the relationship between IRF‐1 and DYRK1α in ferroptosis, we induced ferroptosis in AML12 cells with erastin and manipulated IRF‐1 expression using adenoviral vectors and siRNA. Notably, DYRK1α expression was significantly upregulated in AML12 cells overexpressing IRF‐1, whereas a contrasting decrease in DYRK1α was observed in IRF‐1 knockdown cells (Figure 6D). These findings suggest that IRF‐1 may regulate DYRK1α expression during ferroptosis.

To further elucidate the role of the IRF‐1–DYRK1α axis in ferroptosis, we treated IRF‐1‐overexpressing AML12 cells with harmine or siRNA targeting DYRK1α. As anticipated, suppression of DYRK1α reversed the alterations in ACSL4 and GPX4 induced by IRF‐1 overexpression (Figure 6E,F). Additionally, the increase in ROS associated with IRF‐1 overexpression was effectively blocked by both harmine and siRNA‐DYRK1α (Figure 6G). Furthermore, the elevation of MDA and depletion of GSH induced by IRF‐1 overexpression were also mitigated by DYRK1α inhibition (Figure 6H). Collectively, these results indicate that DYRK1α participates in—and is required for—IRF‐1‐mediated ferroptosis.

DISCUSSION

The role of ferroptosis in tissue IRI has been extensively recognized across critical organs, including the heart, brain, kidneys, and liver. 7 , 25 , 26 , 27 IRF‐1, a pivotal transcription factor in interferon‐induced signaling, substantially influences IRI through multiple mechanisms—activation of immune cells, promotion of apoptosis, and induction of autophagy. 28 Our previous work demonstrated that IRF‐1 enhances autophagy by upregulating high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and activating the c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase (JNK) and P38/P62 signaling pathways, thereby exacerbating hepatic IRI. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 In addition, prior studies have implicated IRF‐1 in the regulation of cellular ferroptosis. 14 , 15

In this study, we show that IRF‐1 exacerbates LIRI in part by accelerating ferroptosis. In parallel, the suppression of DYRK1α inhibited LIRI‐induced ferroptosis. These observations prompted us to explore a potential relationship between IRF‐1 and DYRK1α in LIRI‐associated ferroptosis. Indeed, we found that manipulating IRF‐1 consistently altered DYRK1α levels in hepatic cells, whereas the inhibition of DYRK1α did not affect IRF‐1 expression. Moreover, pharmacologic inhibition of DYRK1α with harmine or silencing of DYRK1α with siRNA partially reversed the augmented ferroptosis observed with IRF‐1 overexpression. Together, these data suggest that DYRK1α functions downstream of IRF‐1 within the ferroptosis pathway and contributes to the progression of LIRI.

Our prior study initially identified the IRF‐1/HMGB1/autophagy axis as a critical regulatory pathway in hepatic IRI. Specifically, IRF‐1 was shown to transcriptionally upregulate HMGB1 expression by directly binding to the interferon‐stimulated response element (ISRE) within the HMGB1 gene promoter, thereby triggering autophagic activation and contributing to hepatocyte apoptosis. 30 Subsequent mechanistic investigations demonstrated that IRF‐1 promotes the phosphorylation‐dependent interaction among JNK, p38 mitogen‐activated protein kinase (MAPK), and the selective autophagy adaptor protein p62/SQSTM1, culminating in lysosome‐dependent autophagic hyperactivation. 31 In parallel, elevated DYRK1α expression has been implicated in pathological tau and amyloid‐β protein processing, 32 , 33 , 34 and p38 MAPK is known to phosphorylate tau at multiple pathogenic sites. Notably, hyperosmotic stress induces inhibitory phosphorylation of caspase‐9 in a p38α‐ and DYRK1α‐dependent manner, suggesting functional crosstalk between these kinases in cellular stress response signaling. 35 Literature review reveals potential indirect functional crosstalk between IRF‐1 and DYRK1α within broader cellular signaling networks. Notably, both proteins may converge on shared upstream stimuli—particularly interferon‐γ (IFN‐γ) and DNA damage responses. IFN‐γ is a potent inducer of IRF‐1 transcription via JAK–STAT signaling; upon receptor engagement, it activates STAT1/STAT2, which dimerize with IRF‐9 to form the ISGF3 complex and drive the expression of interferon‐stimulated genes (ISGs). 28 Although no direct evidence currently links IFN signaling to DYRK1α transcriptional or post‐translational regulation, emerging studies implicate DYRK1α in immune modulation and viral pathogenesis: for instance, DYRK1α functions as a kinase‐independent host factor facilitating coronavirus replication, 36 and the TRAF3–DYRK1α–RAD54L2 complex stabilizes ACE2 expression to enhance SARS‐CoV‐2 infectivity. 37 These findings suggest that DYRK1α may operate downstream—or in parallel—to IFN‐responsive pathways, warranting investigation into context‐dependent functional intersections with IRF‐1. Furthermore, both molecules exert regulatory influence over cell cycle progression, albeit through distinct mechanisms: IRF‐1 modulates G1/S and G2/M checkpoints in response to stress and DNA damage, 38 whereas DYRK1α phosphorylates key regulators—including p27Kip1 and components of the Rb/E2F axis—to control cardiomyocyte proliferation. 39 Critically, DYRK1α inhibition has been shown to promote epigenetic reactivation of cell cycle genes and enhance cardiac repair after myocardial infarction, 40 raising the possibility that coordinated or compensatory regulation between IRF‐1 and DYRK1α may occur in stress‐activated parenchymal cells. Collectively, these observations support a model of indirect, pathway‐level convergence rather than direct molecular interaction—highlighting an important avenue for future mechanistic study.

IRF‐1 is a master transcriptional regulator critically involved in interferon signaling, major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I antigen presentation, Th1 lineage commitment, macrophage activation, and apoptosis control. 28 , 41 , 42 During LIRI, coordinated activation of diverse immune cell populations and inflammatory cascades drives hepatocellular damage and amplifies tissue injury. 43 , 44 , 45 However, global IRF‐1 knockout introduces significant interpretative challenges: its systemic deletion perturbs multiple immunological compartments, thereby obscuring the cell‐intrinsic function of IRF‐1 in hepatocytes. First, IRF‐1 is indispensable for the development and effector functions of key immune cells—including CD4+ and CD8+ T lymphocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and macrophages. 41 Consequently, constitutive IRF‐1 ablation may impair IFN‐γ production and downstream JAK–STAT signaling in these cells, 44 , 46 confounding interpretation given IFN‐γ's dual pro‐inflammatory and cytoprotective roles in LIRI. Second, IRF‐1 governs macrophage polarization toward the pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype 42 and modulates the expression of chemokines and adhesion molecules that regulate leukocyte recruitment. Systemic IRF‐1 deficiency thus likely alters the kinetics, composition, and functional polarization of bone marrow–derived infiltrates—such as neutrophils, inflammatory monocytes, and dendritic cells—in the injured liver. 47 , 48 Notably, cross‐talk between IRF‐1 and redox‐sensitive pathways has been reported in macrophages 48 ; therefore, indirect modulation of antioxidant responses in non‐parenchymal cells may further complicate the attribution of observed phenotypes to hepatocyte‐autonomous mechanisms. Third, IRF‐1 directly transactivates genes encoding pivotal inflammatory mediators—including interleukin‐12 (IL‐12) p40, tumor necrosis factor‐alpha (TNF‐α), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 10 (CXCL10). 49 Global loss of IRF‐1 consequently reshapes systemic and intrahepatic cytokine/chemokine landscapes, potentially masking or mimicking hepatocyte‐specific effects—for instance, altered CXCL10 expression could disrupt immune cell homing independent of intrinsic hepatocyte dysfunction. 49 Collectively, these systemic immunological confounders represent a key limitation of our current model. To rigorously define the hepatocyte‐autonomous role of IRF‐1 in LIRI, future studies should employ conditional, hepatocyte‐specific IRF‐1 knockout mice, thereby isolating IRF‐1 function within parenchymal cells while preserving systemic immune homeostasis.

In summary, our findings indicate that IRF‐1 modulates the ferroptotic response after LIRI, with DYRK1α playing a significant mediating role. We propose that IRF‐1 regulates DYRK1α to drive LIRI‐induced ferroptosis, thereby exacerbating hepatic injury. Nevertheless, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying this interaction warrant further investigation in future studies.
