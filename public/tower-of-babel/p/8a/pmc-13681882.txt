Serum androstenedione levels are associated with dysmenorrhea and pain severity among women without endometriosis: an exploratory cross-sectional study
Authors: Tingting Li, Sihui Yu, Peipei Shi, Sheng Yin, Jiarong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDysmenorrhea is a common gynecological condition with heterogeneous etiologies. Endometriosis is an important condition associated with dysmenorrhea, but the serum steroid and reproductive hormone profiles related to dysmenorrhea, and whether these associations differ by endometriosis status, remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the associations of serum hormone profiles with dysmenorrhea and pain severity.MethodsThis exploratory cross-sectional study was conducted from October 2022 to January 2024 at Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. A total of 139 premenopausal participants were classified into dysmenorrhea (n = 73) and non-dysmenorrhea (n = 66) groups and further stratified by endometriosis status. Serum steroid and reproductive hormones were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry and immunoassays. Group comparisons were performed according to data distribution, and false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was applied for multiple hormone comparisons. Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between androstenedione (A4) and dysmenorrhea.ResultsIn the overall cohort, women with dysmenorrhea showed higher A4 levels and a higher luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone (LH/FSH) ratio, although these differences did not remain significant after FDR adjustment. After stratification by endometriosis status, these differences were mainly observed among women without endometriosis. In this subgroup, A4 levels were significantly higher in women with dysmenorrhea than in those without dysmenorrhea (0.85 ± 0.36 vs. 0.65 ± 0.28 ng/mL, P = 0.004; FDR-adjusted P = 0.024), as was the LH/FSH ratio (2.11 ± 1.47 vs. 1.44 ± 1.02, P = 0.013; FDR-adjusted P = 0.047). Among women without endometriosis, A4 was modestly correlated with visual analogue scale score (Spearman r = 0.285, P = 0.006) and remained associated with dysmenorrhea after multivariable adjustment (odds ratio = 8.07, 95% confidence interval: 1.60–40.65, P = 0.011). A4 and the LH/FSH ratio showed modest discrimination, with area under the curve values of 0.660 and 0.655, respectively.ConclusionsSerum A4 levels were associated with dysmenorrhea and pain severity, particularly among women without endometriosis. The LH/FSH ratio showed a weaker supportive association. Background

Dysmenorrhea is a common gynecological condition characterized by cramping lower abdominal pain before or during menstruation, often accompanied by systemic manifestations such as nausea, fatigue, headache, diarrhea, and lower back pain [1]. Its reported prevalence varies widely among reproductive-aged women, and moderate-to-severe menstrual pain can substantially impair daily activities, work productivity, psychological well-being, and quality of life [2]. Clinically, dysmenorrhea is heterogeneous and may occur without identifiable pelvic pathology or in association with gynecological conditions such as endometriosis, adenomyosis, uterine fibroids, or pelvic inflammatory disease. Endometriosis is one of the most important gynecological conditions associated with secondary dysmenorrhea and chronic pelvic pain [3]. However, dysmenorrhea cannot be fully explained by endometriosis alone. Therefore, characterizing endocrine features related to dysmenorrhea may help improve understanding of this common condition.

The pathophysiology of dysmenorrhea involves complex interactions among endocrine, inflammatory, vascular, and neural mechanisms. Excessive production of prostaglandins and other inflammatory mediators can induce uterine hypercontractility, local ischemia, and pain sensitization [4, 5]. Beyond prostaglandin-mediated mechanisms, estrogen and progesterone may also contribute to menstrual pain by regulating endometrial function, inflammatory responses, uterine contractility, and pain-processing pathways [6, 7].

In addition to estrogen and progesterone, several steroid hormones and their metabolites have neuroactive properties and may participate in pain modulation. Some steroid hormones and metabolites with neuroactive properties can affect neuronal excitability, neuroinflammation, and the balance between excitatory and inhibitory signaling in the nervous system [8, 9]. Altered levels of dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate have been associated with chronic pain in women, while pregnenolone supplementation has shown potential analgesic effects in chronic low back pain [10, 11]. Studies of menstruation-related migraine have also reported alterations in allopregnanolone, pregnenolone sulfate, pregnanolone, progesterone, testosterone, and estradiol, suggesting that menstrual-cycle-related changes in steroid hormones may be linked to pain susceptibility [12, 13]. In young patients with endometriosis, presurgical blood metabolites, including pregnenolone sulfate, have been associated with postoperative pelvic pain outcomes [14]. Together, these findings suggest that steroid-related endocrine changes may be relevant to pain susceptibility, including menstrual pain.

However, few studies have comprehensively evaluated serum steroid and reproductive hormone profiles in relation to dysmenorrhea. It also remains uncertain whether hormone-dysmenorrhea associations are influenced by endometriosis status, or whether specific hormones are related to pain severity. Androstenedione (A4), a key androgen precursor in steroidogenesis, may reflect androgen-related endocrine features, but its relevance to dysmenorrhea has not been well characterized. Therefore, this exploratory cross-sectional study investigated serum steroid and reproductive hormone profiles in women with and without dysmenorrhea. We further assessed whether these associations differed by endometriosis status and evaluated the relationships of key hormone markers, particularly A4 and the luteinizing hormone/follicle-stimulating hormone (LH/FSH) ratio, with dysmenorrhea occurrence and pain severity.

Methods

Study design and participants

This exploratory cross-sectional study was conducted at the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology, Zhongshan Hospital from October 2022 to January 2024. Premenopausal women who underwent gynecological evaluation during the study period were enrolled. The study aimed to investigate serum steroid and reproductive hormone profiles in relation to dysmenorrhea and pain severity. Women were excluded if they had received hormone therapy or medications potentially affecting steroid hormone levels within the previous three months, including oral contraceptives, glucocorticoids, opioid analgesics, or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors. Additional exclusion criteria included psychiatric comorbidities, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), pregnancy, hyperprolactinemia, and abnormal liver or kidney function. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University (Approval No. B2022-500R2). All participants provided written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of dysmenorrhea and endometriosis

Dysmenorrhea severity was assessed using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), a 10-cm scale ranging from 0, indicating no pain, to 10, indicating the worst possible pain [15]. Patients with a VAS score of 0 were assigned to the non-dysmenorrhea group, whereas those with a VAS score greater than 0 were assigned to the dysmenorrhea group. Dysmenorrhea severity was further classified into mild, moderate, and severe categories based on scores of 1–4, 5–7, and 8–10, respectively. Endometriosis status was determined according to surgical findings and/or pathological confirmation.

Sample collection and hormone measurement

Demographic information, clinical history, menstrual characteristics, dysmenorrhea status and severity, and endometriosis status were recorded using a standardized clinical form at enrollment. The time interval from the last menstrual period to blood sampling was recorded when available. Fasting venous blood samples were collected in the morning. Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 1 h and then centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. After centrifugation, serum samples were subjected to routine laboratory measurement without long-term storage.

Serum levels of pregnenolone (PREG), 17α-hydroxypregnenolone (17-Preg), 17α-hydroxyprogesterone (17-OHP), androstenedione (A4), and estrone (E1) were measured using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). A Waters® Xevo™ TQS MS ACQUITY UPLC® System was employed to separate and measure each analyte. Details of the LC–MS/MS methods have been described previously [16]. Other reproductive hormones, including estradiol (E2), progesterone (PROG), luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS), testosterone (T), and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), were measured using routine clinical immunoassays in the laboratory of Zhongshan Hospital, Fudan University. The LH/FSH ratio was calculated by dividing the serum LH concentration by the serum FSH concentration for each participant.

Because blood samples were not uniformly collected at a fixed menstrual-cycle phase, menstrual-cycle-related variables were considered in exploratory analyses. The time interval from the last menstrual period to blood sampling was recorded and analyzed as a supplementary indicator of menstrual-cycle timing. Ovulatory status was clinically inferred based on menstrual history and serum progesterone levels. Participants with indeterminate ovulatory status were excluded from analyses involving ovulatory status.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Normally distributed variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test, as appropriate. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median and interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Serum hormone levels were compared between women with and without dysmenorrhea in the overall cohort and after stratification by endometriosis status. False discovery rate (FDR) adjustment was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to account for multiple hormone comparisons. FDR adjustment was applied within each prespecified set of hormone comparisons, as described in the table legends; for endometriosis-stratified analyses, adjustment was performed separately within each endometriosis-status subgroup. Correlations between hormone levels and VAS score were evaluated using Spearman’s correlation analysis. Group comparisons across dysmenorrhea severity categories were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test.

Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between A4 and dysmenorrhea. Models were adjusted for age, body mass index (BMI), endometriosis status, menstrual cycle regularity, and clinically inferred ovulatory status in the overall cohort. Models restricted to women without endometriosis were adjusted for age, BMI, menstrual cycle regularity, and clinically inferred ovulatory status. Sensitivity analyses were further performed by additionally adjusting for AMH. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminatory performance of A4 and the LH/FSH ratio for dysmenorrhea among women without endometriosis. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses and figure generation were performed using Python 3.13.5.

Hormone differences stratified by endometriosis status

Because endometriosis is an important gynecological condition associated with dysmenorrhea and may also influence endocrine profiles, hormone comparisons were further stratified by endometriosis status (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 1). Among women with endometriosis, serum A4 levels and the LH/FSH ratio did not differ significantly between those with and without dysmenorrhea. In contrast, among women without endometriosis, serum A4 levels were significantly higher in women with dysmenorrhea than in those without dysmenorrhea (0.85 ± 0.36 vs. 0.65 ± 0.28 ng/mL, P = 0.004; FDR-adjusted P = 0.024; Fig. 2C). The LH/FSH ratio was also higher in women with dysmenorrhea without endometriosis (2.11 ± 1.47 vs. 1.44 ± 1.02, P = 0.013; FDR-adjusted P = 0.047; Fig. 2D). AMH levels were also higher in this subgroup after FDR correction, whereas other measured hormones showed no significant differences after multiple-comparison adjustment. Associations with pain severity and multivariable analysis

Given that the main hormone differences were observed among women without endometriosis, we further evaluated the relationships of A4 and the LH/FSH ratio with dysmenorrhea severity in this subgroup (Fig. 3). A4 showed a modest positive correlation with VAS score (Spearman r = 0.285, P = 0.006; Fig. 3A). The LH/FSH ratio also showed a weak positive correlation with VAS score (Spearman r = 0.220, P = 0.036; Fig. 3B). When participants were classified according to dysmenorrhea severity, A4 levels differed significantly across severity groups (Kruskal–Wallis P = 0.037; Fig. 3C), whereas the difference in the LH/FSH ratio showed a borderline trend (Kruskal–Wallis P = 0.055; Fig. 3D).Fig. 3Associations of A4 and LH/FSH ratio with dysmenorrhea severity among women without endometriosis. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed to evaluate the association between A4 and dysmenorrhea after adjustment for potential confounders (Table 4). To further assess the internal consistency of the measured steroid hormone profile, Spearman correlation analysis was performed among measured steroid hormones (Supplementary Fig. S1). PREG, an upstream precursor in steroidogenesis, showed positive correlations with several downstream steroid hormones, including 17-Preg, PROG, 17-OHP, A4, and DHEAS. Positive correlations were also observed among androgen-related hormones, including A4, T, and DHEAS, and between E1 and E2, consistent with their close metabolic relationship. These correlation patterns were broadly consistent with known steroidogenic pathways and supported the internal consistency of the hormone measurements.

Discussion

In this exploratory cross-sectional study, we investigated serum steroid and reproductive hormone profiles in relation to dysmenorrhea and pain severity. The main finding was that serum androstenedione (A4) levels were associated with dysmenorrhea, particularly among women without endometriosis. In the overall cohort, A4 levels and the LH/FSH ratio were higher in women with dysmenorrhea, although these differences did not remain significant after false discovery rate correction. After stratification by endometriosis status, the differences in A4 and LH/FSH ratio were mainly observed among women without endometriosis. In this subgroup, A4 was also modestly correlated with pain severity and remained associated with dysmenorrhea after multivariable adjustment. However, A4 and the LH/FSH ratio showed only modest discriminatory performance, suggesting that they should not be interpreted as standalone diagnostic markers.

A4 is a key androgen precursor in steroidogenesis and can be converted downstream to testosterone and estrogens [17]. Therefore, elevated A4 may reflect broader androgen-related steroidogenic activity rather than an isolated alteration of a single hormone. Previous studies regarding reproductive hormones and dysmenorrhea remain limited and inconsistent, and most have focused on estrogens, progesterone, gonadotropins, or prostaglandin-related mechanisms rather than androgen precursors [18, 19]. Evidence from other pain conditions also suggests that androgen-related hormones may be linked to pain regulation, although the direction of association varies across populations and pain phenotypes. In a population-based study of older women, lower sex hormone levels, including A4, were associated with chronic musculoskeletal pain [20]. Conversely, higher A4 and testosterone concentrations have been reported in men with chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome [21]. Experimental studies have shown that A4, as an aromatase substrate, can activate transient receptor potential ankyrin 1, a nociceptive ion channel involved in aromatase inhibitor-induced pain-like responses [22]. Although these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to dysmenorrhea, they provide mechanistic context for considering A4-related endocrine variation in pain research. In the present cross-sectional study, however, the observed association between A4 and dysmenorrhea should be interpreted as a correlation and hypothesis-generating finding rather than evidence of a direct causal mechanism.

The endometriosis-stratified analysis is important for interpreting the clinical meaning of these findings. Dysmenorrhea may occur without identifiable organic disease or secondary to gynecological pathology. Endometriosis is one of the major gynecological conditions associated with secondary dysmenorrhea and should be considered particularly in women with severe or persistent menstrual pain [23]. In our cohort, endometriosis was more frequent among women with dysmenorrhea, supporting its relevance as a source of clinical heterogeneity. After stratification, the A4-related difference was mainly observed among women without endometriosis. This subgroup pattern may reflect differences in clinical heterogeneity rather than a specific endocrine mechanism. Endometriosis-associated pain may involve lesion-related inflammatory, neuroangiogenic, and sensitization mechanisms [24], which could obscure or modify systemic hormone–pain associations. This interpretation remains speculative and requires further validation. However, women without endometriosis in this study should not be regarded as equivalent to women with primary dysmenorrhea, because other causes of secondary dysmenorrhea were not systematically excluded.

The concurrent elevation of A4, LH/FSH ratio, and AMH among women with dysmenorrhea without endometriosis may reflect subtle differences in reproductive endocrine regulation. LH and FSH are central components of hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis activity, and an increased LH/FSH ratio may be related to follicular development, ovulatory function, or androgen-related endocrine activity [25]. AMH reflects the ovarian follicle pool and is often linked to follicular dynamics and androgenic endocrine features [26]. Clinically diagnosed polycystic ovary syndrome and hyperprolactinemia were excluded in this study; however, the available data were insufficient to determine whether subclinical PCOS-like features contributed to the observed hormone pattern. Importantly, the association between A4 and dysmenorrhea remained significant after additional adjustment for AMH, suggesting that the A4 association was not fully explained by AMH differences. In contrast, the LH/FSH ratio showed only a borderline association after multivariable adjustment and should be interpreted as a supportive endocrine signal rather than an independent marker.

The practical implication of this study is not that A4 or the LH/FSH ratio can currently be used as diagnostic markers for dysmenorrhea, because their discriminatory performance was modest. Rather, these findings are consistent with the possibility that dysmenorrhea may include endocrine subphenotypes, particularly among women without identified endometriosis. Future studies should validate these findings in larger cohorts, distinguish strictly defined primary dysmenorrhea from different forms of secondary dysmenorrhea, and standardize blood sampling using rigorously defined menstrual-cycle phases with prospective ovulation confirmation. Longitudinal and mechanistic studies are also needed to determine whether A4 elevation precedes menstrual pain and to clarify whether A4 is directly involved in dysmenorrhea pathophysiology or serves as a marker of broader steroidogenic activity.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, this was a single-center study involving women who underwent gynecological evaluation, which may limit generalizability to community populations. Third, blood samples were collected at non-standardized time points during the menstrual cycle. Although clinically inferred ovulatory status and the time interval from the last menstrual period were considered in supplementary and multivariable analyses, ovulation was not confirmed by ultrasound monitoring or luteinizing hormone surge testing, and only a single progesterone measurement was available. Therefore, physiological hormonal fluctuations across the menstrual cycle may have introduced measurement variability and may have attenuated, exaggerated, or obscured hormone–dysmenorrhea associations. In addition, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, including age at menarche, duration of dysmenorrhea, analgesic use, stress or psychological status, insulin resistance, and adrenal activity, cannot be excluded. Therefore, the multivariable findings should be interpreted as adjusted associations based on available covariates, rather than as fully controlled estimates. Fourth, endometriosis status was determined according to surgical findings and/or pathological confirmation, but other potential causes of secondary dysmenorrhea, such as adenomyosis or uterine fibroids, were not systematically excluded in all women without endometriosis. Therefore, this subgroup cannot be strictly defined as primary dysmenorrhea. Fifth, the sample size was limited, particularly in the endometriosis subgroup and severe dysmenorrhea category, which may reduce statistical power and contribute to wide confidence intervals. Finally, this study measured circulating hormones but did not assess local endometrial hormone metabolism, prostaglandin levels, inflammatory mediators, or nociceptive pathway activation.

Despite these limitations, this study evaluated a broad panel of serum steroid and reproductive hormones using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry and routine clinical immunoassays, incorporated endometriosis-stratified analyses, and examined pain severity in addition to dysmenorrhea status. The use of false discovery rate correction, multivariable adjustment, AMH sensitivity analysis, and menstrual-cycle-related supplementary analyses strengthened the robustness of the exploratory findings. Overall, our results suggest that serum A4 is associated with dysmenorrhea and pain severity, particularly among women without endometriosis. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify the biological role of androgen-related endocrine features in dysmenorrhea.

Conclusions

In this exploratory cross-sectional study, serum A4 levels were associated with dysmenorrhea and pain severity, particularly among women without endometriosis. The LH/FSH ratio showed a weaker supportive association, whereas A4 remained associated with dysmenorrhea after multivariable adjustment and AMH sensitivity analysis. However, A4 and the LH/FSH ratio showed only modest discriminatory performance and should not be interpreted as standalone diagnostic markers. These findings suggest that androgen-related endocrine features may be associated with dysmenorrhea without identified endometriosis and warrant further validation in longitudinal and mechanistic studies.
