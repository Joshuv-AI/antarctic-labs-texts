Carbonate-capped seamount subduction accelerates CO2-rich arc magma ascent
Authors: Xiaohan Huang, Alexandra Yang Yang, Mingdao Sun, Wei Tan, Yoshihiko Tamura, Siyu Zhao, Taiping Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubduction transports carbon into Earth’s interior, yet how subducted carbon influences arc magma ascent dynamics remains unclear. Here we combine volatile compositions of olivine-hosted melt inclusions with olivine diffusion chronometry at Pagan volcano (Mariana arc) to reveal a direct link between carbonate-capped seamount subduction and rapid magma ascent. Melt inclusions record high CO2 contents (2000-6000 ppm), and magma storage near Moho depths (~20 km). Olivine zoning profiles constrain magma ascent from near-Moho to the surface on timescales of weeks to months. Elevated CaO/Al2O3 ratios (>1) in erupted volcanics, heavy δ13C values (~0.5‰) in monitored volcanic gases, and the occurrence of seamount carbonate in the forearc mud volcano collectively point to subducted seamount carbonates as the dominant CO2 source feeding Pagan magmatism. We propose that early CO2 exsolution near the Moho generates overpressure that enables hydrous arc magmas to bypass crustal stalling. These results identify CO2 as a key driver of rapid magma ascent in arcs and highlight carbonate-capped seamount subduction as an efficient pathway for deep carbon cycling.Subject terms: Volcanology, Geochemistry

Introduction

Volcanic rocks from arcs are typically more evolved than those from oceanic islands, as evidenced by their notably lower MgO contents (Supplementary Fig. 1), consistent with prolonged magma storage within the crust1. Such extended crustal residence obscures primary mantle signatures through fractionation, assimilation, and magma mixing2,3, complicating efforts to link mantle sources with surface magma eruptions. Crystal zoning provides a time-integrated record of magma evolution and offers a window into the dynamics of deeper magmatic systems. Primitive basaltic magma commonly takes decades to thousands of years to ascend from the mantle to the surface, undergoing significant compositional evolution during prolonged crustal residence4. In contrast, the preservation of high forsterite (Fo) olivines with sharp element gradients reveals that some basaltic magmas can ascend from the crust-mantle interface (Moho) to the surface within a year, thereby retaining their primitive signatures5–10. Such rapid ascents, predominantly observed in intraplate volcanoes, have been linked to low-degree, CO2-rich partial melts7,11, where CO2 exsolution enhances magma buoyancy when gas bubbles are held within the magma11,12.

Although dissolved H2O reduces melt viscosity, hydrous arc magmas are prone to early H2O degassing during ascent13. If the exsolved gas phase escapes, decompression-driven degassing promotes crystallization and increases the viscosity of the magmatic mixture, potentially leading to deep crustal stalling2,13. This viscous stalling would likely lead to longer residence times and the generation of evolved arc magmas, in contrast to intraplate settings2 (Supplementary Fig. 1). Yet primitive, high-MgO arc magmas hosting high-Fo olivines have erupted at several arc volcanoes, including Pagan volcano in Mariana14, Etna volcano in Italy8, Klyuchevskoy volcano in Kamchatka15, Irazú volcano in Central America5, and Ambae volcano in the New Hebrides16. Whether a hydrous arc magma stalls or bypasses crustal storage ultimately depends on the competition between buoyancy gain associated with volatile exsolution and gas retention, and viscosity increase driven by melt degassing and crystallization10,12. When buoyancy dominates, rapid magma ascent can occur. Although CO2 has been invoked as a driver of rapid ascent in some arc settings10, an integrated framework linking mantle source signatures to exceptionally rapid transcrustal ascent of hydrous arc magma remains poorly explored. Establishing this source-to-crust chain helps clarify the mechanism by which magma bypasses crustal storage and, critically, its implications for deep carbon cycles.

Pagan volcano in the Mariana arc provides an exceptional natural laboratory to examine the role of subducted carbon in arc magma ascent. Pagan has erupted unusually primitive basalts containing high-Fo olivines and exhibits geochemical signatures distinct from other Mariana volcanoes (Fig. 1). Moreover, the Mariana forearc east of Pagan hosts subducted carbonate-rich seamount materials, indicating an efficient pathway for carbonate contribution into the mantle wedge of the Mariana subduction zone17,18.

In this study, we integrate olivine-hosted melt inclusion analyses with diffusion chronometry studies of high-Fo (>89) olivine phenocrysts from Pagan volcano to quantify rapid transcrustal magma ascent in an arc setting. Our results demonstrate that CO2 derived from subducted carbonates plays a critical role in enabling rapid transcrustal ascent of hydrous arc magmas, thereby linking deep carbon cycling to volatile-driven eruption dynamics.

Results and discussion

Magma storage at Moho depth inferred by melt inclusion volatiles

Pagan, the largest and one of the most active volcanoes in the Mariana Islands (Fig. 1a, d), provides a rare opportunity to investigate deep magma storage and rapid magma ascent in an intra-oceanic arc, as it erupts unusually primitive basalts (MgO >10 wt.%) containing olivine phenocrysts with Fo up to ~9414. Olivines analyzed in this study were hand-picked from a magnesian clinopyroxene–olivine basalt collected from the northeastern submarine flank of Pagan, where primitive compositions are best preserved14.

High-Fo olivine crystals in the Pagan volcano provide critical insights into both their crystallization locations and how long they have been stored within the crustal magma reservoir. The volatile contents of melt inclusions trapped in these olivines are key indicators of their crystallization depth19. Most melt inclusions in high-Fo olivine crystals from Pagan contain glass and a single vapor bubble (Fig. 2; Supplementary Figs. 2–5) after being heated at one atmosphere.

To accurately determine volatile concentrations in both the vapor bubble and glass, we employed multiple analytical techniques20. The volume proportion of vapor bubbles, quantified using 3D X-ray micro-computed tomography (see “Methods” section), predominantly ranges from 6 to 13 vol.% in melt inclusions (Fig. 2, Supplementary Fig. 6d). Strong linear correlations between melt inclusion volumes and bubble volumes, as well as between CO2 concentrations in the bubbles and the bubble volume fractions in their host melt inclusions (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicate that most inclusions originally trapped melt only, with bubbles forming after entrapment21,22. Consequently, the volatile systematics of these inclusions can reliably record entrapment pressures21,22. In contrast, melt inclusions hosted in two olivine crystals (Fo94 and Fo91) deviate from these trends and are thus excluded from the subsequent entrapment pressure calculations (Supplementary Fig. 6c, d). One inclusion contains a large vapor bubble (~28 vol.%) with exceptionally high CO2 concentrations of up to 1.2 wt.%, consistent with co-entrapment of melt and an exsolved CO2 gas bubble21,22. The other inclusion also contains a large bubble (~16 vol.%) but low CO2 concentrations (~2000 ppm) and the lowest bubble CO2 density (Supplementary Data 1), likely recording syn-eruptive bubble growth22.

Total CO2 concentrations (bubble + glass) of melt inclusions in Fo89–92 olivines were estimated to range from 2200 to 5900 ppm after correcting for post-entrapment crystallization and Fe loss23 (Supplementary Data 2). Notably, 78–95% of the total CO2 resided within the vapor bubble phase (Supplementary Data 1), confirming the necessity to include the bubble phase for CO2 measurements21. These CO2 contents are among the highest reported in primitive olivines from Mariana arc volcanoes (Fig. 3a). The positive correlation between CO2 in melt inclusions and Fo in host olivines at Pagan (Fig. 3a) indicates progressive CO2 degassing during olivine crystallization. Furthermore, the melt inclusions analyzed in this study, which plot at the most primitive end of the H2O–K2O linear trend (Supplementary Fig. 8), preserve the highest water contents (2.47–3.45 wt.%) observed at Pagan, suggesting limited H2O diffusion loss24 (details shown in the “Methods” section).

H2O and CO2 concentrations can be used to calculate vapor saturation pressure with solubility models—MagmaSat19 and MafiCH25, which yield minimum entrapment pressures of melt inclusions (Supplementary Data 3). MagmaSat yielded consistently higher pressures (375–676 MPa) than MafiCH (237–451 MPa) and was ultimately adopted given its broader calibration range and better compatibility with the compositions of Pagan melts (see “Methods” section). The entrapment pressures of 375–676 MPa correspond to depths of 15–24 km based on regression fits26. The median entrapment depth (n = 11) is 20 km (+1.33/−2.13 km) (Fig. 3b), closely matching the Moho depth beneath Pagan as determined by seismic studies27.

Magma ascent timescales inferred from diffusion profiles

Diffusional element zoning in these olivine phenocrysts constrains the timescales of olivine entrainment and magma transport from the last chemical/thermal perturbation to the surface28,29. Olivines exhibit element zoning patterns (Supplementary Figs. 10–13), with decreasing Fo (91–92 in cores to 85–86 in rims) and Ni (1909–2564 ppm in cores to 820–1286 ppm in rims), and increasing Mn (929–1246 ppm in cores to 1634–2067 ppm in rims) from core to rim. These olivines lack complex zonings in slowly diffusing elements (e.g., Cr and P, Supplementary Fig. 14), which is typically associated with dendritic/composite crystal growth30.

Using diffusion coefficients for Ni, Mg, Fe, and Mn, we applied Diffusion Process Analysis (DIPRA)31 and Finite Elements and Nested Sampling (DFENS)32 (see “Methods” section) to model the diffusion timescales, yielding comparable durations within a year (Fig. 4b; details shown in the “Methods” section and Supplementary Figs. 10–13, 15). Although derived from a single sample, the dataset captures the dominant population of primitive olivine, with cores of Fo90–93 and rims of Fo85–88. These olivines occupy the most primitive end of the NE Pagan olivine compositional spectrum14 (Fig. 5a; Supplementary Fig. 16a–d), and display low Ni/Cr ratios across the Fo range (Supplementary Fig. 16e), indicating crystal fractionation rather than storage and remobilization in crustal cumulates33. The high-Fo cores (Fo91–92) suggest equilibrium with mantle peridotite, whereas the lower-Fo rims formed as the crystals were entrained into a carrier melt during ascent from the Moho-depth magma reservoir to the surface34. The predominance of highly primitive olivines further suggests that the analyzed crystals were entrained shortly after melt extraction from the mantle, minimizing residence and re-equilibration within the crust. The timescales are predominantly on the order of weeks to months (Fig. 4b), and, together with the simple core–rim zoning patterns (Supplementary Figs. 10–13), indicate that these olivine crystals record a major magma mixing event34, followed by rapid ascent from the mantle to the surface.

Reconstructing the magma ascent history beneath NE Pagan volcano

Olivine diffusion timescales constrain the interval between the last chemical perturbation and eruption to weeks to months (Fig. 5b, c). Although this perturbation may not strictly coincide with the magma storage depth recorded by melt inclusion volatiles (Fig. 5b, c), multiple independent observations indicate that both signals most likely originate near the Moho beneath NE Pagan.

First, the olivines analyzed here record the highest Fo values reported for Pagan and lack a subordinate low-Fo population (Fig. 5a), suggesting residence in a deep, near-Moho reservoir35 rather than repeated cycling through shallow storage. Second, interstitial glasses within olivine aggregates overlap in major-element composition with erupted NE Pagan glasses36, whereas melt inclusions in high-Fo olivines display higher CaO/Al2O3 ratios characteristic of carbonate-influenced melts37. Intermediate CaO/Al2O3 compositions preserved in Fo88–90 olivines define hybrid melts (Fig. 5a), implying mixing of low CaO/Al2O3, slightly evolved magma with the primitive olivine-bearing magma mush near the Moho, where olivine with Fo >88 is expected to form5. Third, the preservation of delicate olivine mush textures (Fig. 1c) supports entrainment of pre-existing mush fragments.

Taken together, these observations support a model in which a pre-existing olivine-dominated mush straddling the Moho beneath NE Pagan was destabilized by magma recharge (Fig. 5b, c). This recharge is more consistent with the preservation of mush aggregates than wholesale entrainment into a large, shallow evolved reservoir, which would likely have disrupted the mush fabric and would leave a considerably low-Fo olivine population, as shown in other Pagan samples (Fig. 5a).

Origin of the rapid ascent of primitive magma beneath arc volcanoes

In typical arc volcanic systems, water has long been known to play the dominant role in magma ascent through the crust. Magmas with higher water contents tend to degas water earlier and crystallize at deeper depths, resulting in viscosity increases that lead to long residence in deeper crustal reservoirs38 (Fig. 6), referred to as viscous death2,39 or viscous stalling13. Such a mechanism has been frequently used to explain the genesis of more evolved intermediate and silicic magmas at arcs2,12. The role of CO2 during pre-eruptive magma ascent, on the other hand, has received comparatively less attention than that of H2O.

However, eruption of primitive basalts with high-Fo olivines has been frequently reported at Shiveluch6 and Los Hornitos10, Etna8, Klyuchevskoy15 as well as Pagan. Notably, despite containing typical arc-like water contents (3.6 wt.% at Pagan, 5.21 wt.% at Klyuchevskoy40, 6.35 wt.% at Etna8; Fig. 6), their primitive magmas did not seem to be affected by viscous stalling in a shallow crustal reservoir. Instead, their estimated storage depths, derived from both CO2 and H2O contents in melt inclusions and coexisting bubbles8,40–42, are significantly deeper than the predicted water-saturation depths for those magmas (Fig. 6). Such a discrepancy implies that magma ascent dynamics in these systems are not governed by H2O alone.

CO2, instead of H2O, likely triggers fast ascent of primitive magma from deeper crustal reservoirs10,22,43. Primitive arc magmas are estimated to have a CO2 content of ~1.4 wt.%44, which would make it easy to achieve CO2 saturation and exsolution during ascent. CO2 plays a fundamental role by triggering early H2O exsolution and the generation of a buoyant gas phase, which might outweigh viscosity increases associated with degassing and crystallization45–47. For example, intraplate magmas are often rich in CO2 but poor in H2O, which avoids water-degassing-induced viscous stalling10 and promotes magma movement via significant overpressure resulting from CO2 extraction into bubbles22. At Pagan, MagmaSat calculations19 based on volatile contents of the majority of melt inclusions in olivines with Fo89–92 indicate that melt inclusions at the time of their entrapment were in equilibrium with a vapor phase containing only minor H2O (averaging ~28 mol%) and dominated by CO2 (averaging ~72 mol%) (Supplementary Data 3). Additionally, the melt inclusion in the most primitive olivine (Fo94) contains a co-trapped bubble, providing direct evidence for the presence of an exsolved CO2 vapor phase at melt inclusion entrapment22. This CO2-rich vapor phase was likely retained in the magma in a deep reservoir beneath Pagan, and thus facilitated rapid magma ascent22.

Two lines of evidence indicate that Pagan’s magma source is rich in carbon and that this carbon is primarily derived from subducted carbonate. First, primitive melt inclusions and whole-rock data from Pagan exhibit elevated CaO/Al2O3 (0.97–1.3) ratios (Fig. 1g), significantly higher than those from other Mariana arc volcanoes. These values are inconsistent with CO2-poor peridotite-derived melts (CaO/Al2O3 < 1, Fig. 7, e.g., ref. 48), but match experimental melts from CO2-bearing mantle sources (Fig. 7, mostly with CaO/Al2O3 > 1, e.g., ref. 37), suggesting a carbon-rich source. Second, the δ13C values of the volcanic gas (−1.1‰, −0.5‰) at Pagan49,50 are heavier than those typical of arc volcanoes (−12 to −2‰)51, pointing to a considerable contribution from subducted carbonate. Isotopic mixing models based on CO2/3He and δ13C values50,52 estimate that ~90% of the carbon in Pagan’s mantle source is derived from subducted carbonate material (Supplementary Fig. 17). This is consistent with prior geochemical evidence for carbonate and sediment melt contributions beneath Pagan14.

The source of this subducted carbonate likely lies in the subduction of seamounts capped with thick carbonate platforms, such as the Magellan Seamounts on the Pacific Plate (Fig. 1a), which contain carbonate deposits up to 900 m thick53. The presence of limestone fragments at the Asùt Tesoru mud volcano in the forearc of Pagan (Fig. 1a) and clustered seismicity beneath it provide strong evidence for the ongoing subduction of seamounts17,54. Together, these features suggest that carbonate-bearing seamounts have reached the forearc and are likely influencing the volcanic arc system (Fig. 8). Furthermore, slab surface temperatures beneath Pagan (~870–900 °C)55 are high enough to generate calcium-rich, hydrous carbonatitic liquids from the slab56 (Fig. 8a), which subsequently metasomatized the mantle wedge and induced CO2-rich melt generation (Fig. 8b). This contribution from subducted seamount carbonate may also partially explain the genesis of CO2-rich, high δ13C vent fluids at NW Rota-1, Nikko, and Daikokus57.

We estimate that carbon influx from subducted seamount carbonates in the Mariana arc reaches ~0.29 Mt/yr—equivalent to nearly half of the carbon flux from subducting sediments (0.59 Mt/yr; see “Methods” section and Supplementary Data 9 for calculation details). This previously overlooked contribution likely explains the unusually high CaO/Al2O3 ratios and CO2 contents in Pagan magma relative to other Mariana arc volcanoes. The elevated CO2 contents promote volatile overpressure during ascent, enhancing buoyancy and facilitating rapid magma transport through the crust. These observations establish a direct link between subducted slab carbon inputs and the crustal ascent dynamics of volatile-rich arc magmas.

This link between subducted carbonate and rapid magma ascent may extend to other arcs. At Etna (Aeolian arc), melt inclusions exhibit exceptionally high CO2 contents (up to 9600 ppm)8 and elevated CaO/Al2O3 ratios (1.18–1.67), even higher than those observed at Pagan, which were previously attributed to a similar carbon-driven ascent mechanism8. At Klyuchevskoy, where a vertically continuous conduit from the mantle to the volcano has been imaged43,58, volcanic gases exhibit high δ13C values, indicating that ~86% of the total carbon budget in the mantle source is derived from subducted carbon59. Melt inclusions from this system contain up to ~7000 ppm CO240. Despite low CO2 content in subducted sediments at Kamchatka60, the contribution of carbon may come from seamounts with thick carbonate deposits that have already been subducted, similar to the un-subducted Meiji Seamount, which has a ~100 m calcareous layer61. Similarly, rapid magma ascent has been observed at Ambae volcano (New Hebrides)16, where extensive CO2 degassing has been attributed to subducted sedimentary carbon62.

Taken together, these observations highlight the underappreciated role of carbonate-rich sediments in global subduction carbon fluxes. Their contribution not only modulates melting dynamics in the mantle wedge but also facilitates the rapid ascent of primitive magmas. Such processes are likely widespread in arc settings where carbonate-rich inputs—particularly from subducting seamounts capped with carbonate—are present, offering valuable insights into mantle carbon cycling and arc magmatism.

Methods

Sample selection and preparation

Samples used in this study were collected in 2010 (cruise NT10-12), using ROV Hyper-Dolphin and R.V. Natsushima. Dive HPD1147–R21 sampled a primitive clinopyroxene–olivine basalt. Olivine crystals (0.5–1.5 mm) were hand-picked after being crushed and sieved using the SelFrag High Voltage Pulse Fragmentor at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GIGCAS). This highly selective fragmentation minimizes mineral crystal destruction. Only crack-free olivine crystals were selected for analysis. We selected melt inclusions (MIs) with a diameter of over 40 μm without cracks.

One inclusion-bearing olivine crystal was heated using the Linkam TS1400XY Microscopic heating stage to determine the precise homogenization temperature (dissolution of all solids) for olivines (1195 °C) at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, GIGCAS. The heating time at temperatures above 1000 °C was less than 10 min to prevent possible loss of water from the MIs63. Others were heated rapidly to 1195 °C for no more than 10 min in a one-atmosphere vertical furnace at the State Key Laboratory of Isotope Geochemistry, Guangzhou Institute of Geochemistry, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

The heated olivine crystals, which have melt inclusions with bubbles, were individually mounted on epoxy resin and then doubly polished without exposing them using sandpapers and an abrasive pad (1 µm diamond suspension) until the target MI was sufficiently close to the surface of the crystal for Raman analysis. Then, the final polishing was performed using a 0.3 µm alumina suspension. During polishing, we ensured that none of the vapor bubbles were breached21.

In situ mineral orientation measurements

Diffusion along the [001] crystallographic orientation is around six times faster than along [100] or [010] for Fe–Mg in olivine64. Therefore, it is essential to determine the crystal orientation relative to the compositional traverse. To prepare the samples for Electron Back-scatter Diffraction (EBSD) analysis, thin sections were polished with 0.05 μm colloidal silica for 3 h. Subsequently, a carbon coating approximately 12 nm thick was applied to prevent electrical charging. Imaging and EBSD analyses were performed at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, GIGCAS, using a Thermo Scientific Helios 5 CX equipped with the OXFORD ULTIM Max100 and Aztec Symmetry II system. The EBSD measurements utilized an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a beam current of 11 nA, a working distance of 10–20 mm, and a tilt angle of 70°.

To ensure that crystal orientation was not affected by cracks or deformation, orientation maps consisting of hundreds of point measurements were obtained for each compositional profile. The data were plotted on a lower-hemisphere equal-area projection, and the angles between crystal axes and compositional traverse directions were measured using Oxford Instruments CHANNEL 5 post-processing software. Measured Euler angles were then converted into angles between the electron microprobe traverses and the crystallographic a-, b-, and c-axes in olivine, and used to determine their coordinates on the lower-hemisphere equal-area projection65.

Element compositions and mapping for olivine and melt inclusions

Electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA) was conducted on a JEOL JXA-8230 at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, GIGCAS.

For major (Mg, Fe and Si), minor (Ni, Mn, Na, Ca, Co and Cr) and trace element (Al) profiles in olivine and the olivines hosting melt inclusions, an accelerating voltage of 15 kV was applied with a working current of 20 nA for elements (Mg, Fe, Si and Na) and 200 nA for minor and trace elements (Ni, Mn, Ca, Co, Cr and Al). On-peak count times of 20 s were used for elements (Mg, Fe, Si, and Na) and 120 s for minor and trace elements, with half-count times off-peak. A spot size of 1 μm was selected, with point spacing of profile 1–3 varying from 5 to 10 μm (typically close to the transitional change in concentration and the crystal edge), and 20 μm (within the homogenous core), and point spacing of other profiles (3–13) of 6 μm to ensure both high spatial resolution and analytical precision. Calibration was carried out using a mixture of natural and synthetic minerals and oxides66. The calibration standards for olivine are olivine (Si, Mg), magnetite (Fe), rhodonite (Mn), Ni-metal (Ni), Co-metal (Co), Cr-Oxide (Cr), Cr-diopside (Ca), Albite (Na), and almandine (Al). The ZAF calibration procedures were utilized for reducing matrix effects. The MongOL Sh11–2 olivine was used to monitor the accuracy of measurements66. Most elements of our reference materials were reproduced to better than 3% (Si: 1%, Ni: 1.4%, Al: 2.9%, Cr: 11%, Fe: 0.6%, Mn: 1.6%, Co: 10%, Mg: 0.7%, Ca: 3.5%, Na: 30%).

For major elemental abundances in glass matrix from melt inclusions enclosed in olivines, we use a 10 nA beam current and a 15 kV accelerating potential with a 5–10 μm diameter beam. Count times on peak for major elements varied between 20 s (Mg, Al, Fe, Ca, Ti, Si, Mn, Cr, P, Cl) and 10 s (Na, K). Background count times for the elements were 50% of their peak counting times. The calibration standards for melt are olivine (Mg), magnetite (Fe), rhodonite (Mn), Cr-diopside (Si, Ca), rutile (Ti), Cr-Oxide (Cr), Orthoclase (K), Albite (Na), Apatite (P), almandine (Al), Tugtupite-1 (Cl), and Pyrite-1 (S). The ZAF calibration procedures were utilized for reducing matrix effects. The replicate analysis of a basaltic glass checks standard (BCR-2G, Jochum et al.67) yielded an average of 2 standard deviations (2 SDs) of less than 6% for major elements SiO2, Al2O3, FeO, MgO, and CaO. The average 2 SDs for K2O, TiO2, and Na2O fell within the range of 5%–8%, while those for MnO and P2O5 ranged between 18% and 25%. Additionally, the 2 standard error percentages (2SEs) for sulfur (S) and chlorine (Cl) were ±0.59% and ±0.33%, respectively. Standard errors of the chemical compositions of melt inclusions and their host olivines are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

High-resolution X-ray elemental intensity mapping of Mg, Al, Cr, Ni, and P was conducted on a carbon-coated thin section containing olivine grains. X-ray mapping was performed using a JEOL JXA-8230 electron microprobe at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, GIGCAS, Chinese Academy of Sciences. Mapping was carried out at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV, a beam current of 150 nA, and a focused beam diameter of 3 μm. Elemental maps were acquired with a step size of 3 μm and a dwell time of 65 ms per pixel.

CO2 contents in bubbles of melt inclusions

The CO2 contents in the melt inclusion can be derived from a series of analyses20. First, we measure the CO2 density in the bubbles using Raman Spectroscopy. Next, the olivine grains are mounted in epoxy, and 3D X-ray tomography is used to measure the volumes of the bubble and melt inclusion, from which the CO2 mass in the bubble is calculated. Finally, we polish the mount to expose the glass of the melt inclusion and measure the CO2 contents in the glass using Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry. A WiTec Alpha 300 R Raman spectrometer at the State Key Laboratory of Deep Earth Processes and Resources, GIGCAS, was used, equipped with a 531.885 nm (green) laser focused through a 50× objective. Each analysis was conducted at 30 mW with five accumulations of 30 s integration time, using an 1800 grooves/mm grating to achieve a spectral resolution of 0.54 cm⁻¹. All collections were done in a single spectral window (1121.5–1661.5 cm−1). Benzonitrile68 spectra were collected immediately before and after each analysis using 5 s integration and two accumulations to correct for non-linearity of the Raman shift axis. To avoid coexisting liquid and vapor CO2 in the bubble, each analysis was maintained at a supercritical temperature of 37 °C using the CONGTICAL thermo stage (TS120) (31.1 °C is the critical temperature of pure CO2), and the thermometer was accurate to ±0.5 °C. In addition, for comparison of how well different densimeters agree with our sample datasets, we also conducted 24 °C temperature measurements with the thermo stage. Peak positions were found using a peak-fitting program, Origin. For comparison, we also used a Lorentzian-Gaussian least-squares minimizing peak-fitting procedure in MATLAB69, and found similar results to our Origin peak fits. The average relative standard deviation of density is ~11%. Results are shown in Supplementary Data 1.

Melt inclusion 3D X-ray tomographic analysis

High-resolution 3D X-ray micro-computed tomography was performed using a ZEISS Xradia 620 Versa at the Institute for Advanced Study, Central South University. Olivine grains were securely mounted and scanned for ∼3 h at 80 kV and 10 W. The pixel resolution ranged from 0.91 to 1.22 µm. The 3D images were reconstructed using TXM3DViewer, and the volumes of melt inclusion and vapor bubble were calculated using Dragonfly software. Note that the volumes of melt inclusions determined by 3D X-ray micro-computed tomography are commonly lower than those measured by microscopy, and the volume was calculated assuming ellipsoidal shape (Supplementary Fig. 6a), indicating that melt inclusion volumes may be overestimated by previous approaches in which the dimensions of melt inclusions were measured by microscopy70. The surface of the bubbles and glass in melt inclusions was smoothed using a Gaussian filter by convoluting 3 × 3 × 3 neighborhood voxels in the Dragonfly. The associated errors for volumes are less than 10%70. Data are shown in Supplementary Data 1.

CO2 and H2O contents in glass and CO2 in melt inclusions

Following Raman analysis, samples were prepared for nanoscale Secondary Ion Mass Spectrometry (NanoSIMS) analysis of the glass in the melt inclusions (MIs). The olivine phenocrysts were polished further (using a lapping film with 0.3 μm alumina suspension) until the glassy part of the target MI was exposed at the surface. After polishing, the olivine phenocrysts were removed from the epoxy target and then mounted on a 1″ round Metal tin mount in preparation for NanoSIMS analysis.

Volatile (H2O and CO2) contents of the MI glasses were measured with a CAMECA NanoSIMS 50 L at the Institute of Geology and Geophysics, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The samples were coated with gold, loaded in sample holders together with the standards, and baked overnight at about 60 °C in the NanoSIMS airlock. Each location was pre-sputtered for 60 s using a ~ 1 nA current on a 10 μm × 10 μm area to remove surface contaminants. After pre-sputtering, the raster size was reduced to 5 μm × 5 μm, and a beam intensity of 200 pA was used for analysis, with 49.4% electronic gating (beam blanking) used to discard data from the crater edge, such that the reported data represent an area within the sputter crater measuring 2.5 μm × 2.5 μm. Standard samples used for H2O calibration included 1833-11, MORB, KOV, and DAP; for CO2, we used 1833-11, FJ-G2, EPG-G3, and IND-G2. Two-sigma relative standard deviations (RSDs) for the calibration standards are reported in Supplementary Data 1.

CO2 contents in ppm of melt inclusion were determined using a mass-balanced approach using the equations:

M as the mass of CO2 in the MI glass or bubble, XCO2glass as the mass fraction of CO2 in the glass, and C as the CO2 content (wt.%) in the glass or MI. ρglass is determined from DensityX calculations71. ρCO2bubble is calculated from the corrected Fermi diad splitting and the equations69. The average error in the total CO2 content for most of MI is ~8%. Results are listed in Supplementary Data 1.

Assessing volatile saturation and post-entrapment modification of melt inclusions

Major and volatile element data for all melt inclusions hosted in olivine were corrected for the effects of post-entrapment crystallization and Fe–Mg diffusion processes by MIMiC23. The melt Fe2+/Fe Total ratios were calculated36, assuming a constant fO2 (ΔQFM + 1.23). Temperatures of olivine-melt equilibrium were calculated using the olivine-saturated melt thermometer26, corrected for the effect of water on olivine liquidus temperature72. CO2 and H2O contents in melt inclusions, after correction for PEC and Fe loss, are slightly lower than the uncorrected values, likely due to enrichment associated with recrystallization in the uncorrected melt inclusions (Supplementary Fig. 7). An exception is observed in the melt inclusion hosted in Fo94 olivine, which exhibits a substantial difference (Supplementary Fig. 7).

Assessing water loss in melt inclusions

Diffusive H⁺ re-equilibration can affect melt inclusions, causing H2O contents to reflect the last stage of equilibration with the surrounding melt rather than the original entrapment conditions73.

Several observations suggest that H2O loss in our dataset is within 1 wt.%. Relative to melt inclusions from olivine in centimeter-scale lapilli to fist-sized bombs74, <2 cm tephra clasts36, and bombs75, the inclusions analyzed here define the primitive end of the H2O–K2O trend (Supplementary Fig. 8) and record the highest water contents (2.47–3.45 wt%). Within the same K2O range, the Pagan melt inclusions exhibit a systematic trend shifted toward ~1 wt% lower H2O on average (Supplementary Fig. 8), consistent with a diffusive loss of up to ~30% H2O—similar to the core-to-rim variation observed in bomb-sized samples24. On the other hand, our analyses were restricted to inclusions in large (>500 µm) olivine crystals without internal fissures, further minimizing diffusive exchange76.

Thus, although H2O loss cannot be ruled out, the combined evidence indicates that our measurements capture near-maximum pre-eruptive water contents. Importantly, any diffusive loss would only shift our volatile saturation pressures to lower values, meaning that the reported storage depths should be regarded as minimum estimates.

Calculating saturation pressures

We calculate saturation pressures for our MI using two solubility models (Supplementary Data 3): MagmaSat19 and MafiCH25. MagmaSat includes the broadest range of compositions in its experimental suite and is applicable at pressures up to 3 GPa. Additionally, although the model MafiCH is calibrated up to 700 MPa, it was designed to model CO2–H2O solubility within alkaline mafic compositional ranges (44–53 wt.% of SiO2 and 2–9 wt.% of total alkali), which is not consistent with the compositional range of Pagan melts (1.5–2 wt.% of total alkali). Therefore, we use the saturation pressures calculated by MagmaSat for the melt inclusions studied.

Diffusion modeling

Timescales for Fe–Mg interdiffusion, Ni, and Mn along EPMA traverses were modeled using DFENS32 and DIPRA31, based on the one-dimensional concentration-dependent diffusion equation. This approach integrates finite element diffusion models with nested sampling Bayesian inversion via pyMultiNest77, allowing robust uncertainty estimation by accounting for covariance between diffusion parameters. By incorporating observations from multiple elements, this method better constrains individual timescales and reduces uncertainties compared to assuming parameter independence. Diffusion coefficients for Fe–Mg calculated by DFENS depend on Eq. (2)64, and then Ni and Mn element diffusion were modeled. Furthermore, anisotropy-corrected diffusivities were calculated for each traverse based on the angles between the analytical profile and the olivine crystallographic axes measured by EBSD (α, β, and γ, respectively; Costa and Chakraborty28). The boundary condition was completed as shown in the Supplementary Data 8. The initial temperature derived from the olivines and their melt inclusions were calculated as 1210 °C with an uncertainty of ±29 °C using equation (22)26. In addition, the average data 5.08 × 108 Pa for entrapment pressures of melt inclusions19 is used as initial pressure. Average magmatic Log fO2 (NNO + 0.71) was calculated from glass compositions and Fe3+/Fetotal of melt inclusions36. Note that the possible effect of H2O on Fe–Mg diffusion is not considered. Although Fe–Mg interdiffusivity is generally higher under hydrous conditions compared to anhydrous ones78,79, their significance under the moderate H₂O contents typical of arc magmas remains unclear and is not addressed here.

For initial or boundary conditions, we used either a step function or a homogeneous profile. The initial condition depends on the compositions of well-defined core concentration plateaus, and the boundary condition was the measured element content of each olivine rim shown in Supplementary Data 8. 12 profiles exhibited simple Fo zoning, which could be modeled with a single timescale. 8 profiles exhibited complex zoning (for example, LINE 7, 8, 9, 20), thus we assume that this zoning had a non-homogeneous initial condition and time-dependent Fo concentrations at the boundaries31,34.

Based on core-to-rim variations in Fo, Mn, and Ni, 70 one-dimensional diffusion timescales were calculated (Supplementary Figs. 10–13 and Data 8). Both DFENS and DIPRA yield comparable timescales on the order of days to weeks (Supplementary Fig. 15). Discrepancies between different lines in the same olivine profile may result from sectioning effects80, and variations in local boundary conditions (e.g., differences between interstitial and external melt). For example, profiles LINE7 and LINE11 calculated by DFENS are taking across section corners, resulting in large time overestimates80 (149 days, 169 days, respectively).

Estimation of the slab C influx in the Mariana subduction zone

The carbon influx of sediments was calculated using Eq. (5). This equation accounts for the C content (Ci), thickness (hi), and density (ρi) of the specific layer of the slab, along with the trench length (L) and the convergence rate (v). The carbon flux from subducted seamount carbonate layers was estimated using an average carbonate layer thickness, average summit diameter, subduction rate (5 seamounts/Myr estimated from the number of seamounts encountered per unit length of subducting plate per million years), limestone density, and carbon content. The resulting carbon input flux of carbonate-capped seamounts is 2.91 × 1014 kg/Myr, equivalent to 0.29 Mt/yr. Detailed parameters are provided in Supplementary Data 9.
