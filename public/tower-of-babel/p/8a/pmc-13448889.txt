Synthesis of alumina ceramic meta-fibers with tensile super-plasticity
Authors: Jiawei Wu, Yongshi Guo, Soon Hyung Hong, Hongti Zhang, Wang Hay Kan, Yanyan Ma, Tanglong Bai, Zheng Yang, Xiaohua Zhang, Kai Dong, Jie Sun, Jianhua Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCeramics are prized for exceptional strength, but their inherent brittleness has long been a fundamental bottleneck. Here, we report tensile plastic Al2O3 meta-fibers that achieve a combination of high tensile strength (1.94 GPa) and large plastic strain (10.01%), with micro-strain reaching 194%. This is enabled via a phase-regulation strategy that constructs high-purity unsaturated [AlO5] coordination with disordered oxygen lattices in Al2O3 fibers. The metastable [AlO5] fundamentally alters the deformation mechanics by reducing the energy barrier for bond breaking and reformation, while the disordered oxygen lattice facilitates extensive atomic displacement, collectively enabling intrinsic plastic behavior. This local plasticity accumulates in high-purity networks, ultimately achieving macroscopic tensile plasticity of fibers, challenging conventional understanding of ceramic deformation. These plastic fibers can be twisted and woven into high-strength, stretchable refractory ceramic fabrics. Our work establishes metastable phase customization as a universal paradigm, opening a promising pathway for designing superplastic ceramics.Subject terms: Ceramics, Mechanical properties

Introduction

Strength and strain are fundamental yet often mutually exclusive properties in structural materials1. Polymers typically exhibit high strain but low strength, whereas ceramics possess high strength but suffer from limited strain tolerance, usually below 1%2,3. For decades, the pursuit of materials that combine both high strength and large strain has been a central goal in materials science4,5. Given the intrinsic strength of ceramics, significant efforts have been directed toward imparting substantial deformability to these materials, aiming to realize plastic ceramics as a new class of structural material6. Recent studies have demonstrated that plasticity in ceramics can be activated through deliberate defect engineering, such as introducing high-density dislocation motion7,8. However, such plasticity remains rare and is typically confined to nanoscale regions under compression9,10. In particular, tensile plasticity—essential for practical applications—has been realized in only a few specialized systems, leaving a general design strategy still lacking11.

To date, only two types of oxide ceramics have been reported to exhibit tensile plasticity. One approach used superhigh-temperature pulsed laser deposition to fabricate an ultra-thin (~40 nm) amorphous Al2O3 film, which leveraged the free volume of amorphous oxides and a high fraction of metastable [AlO5] phase to enable ductility12. Another strategy achieved tensile plasticity in La2O3 by transferring dislocations from a contacting metallic Mo substrate13. These studies highlight two promising pathways—metastable phase and dislocation transfer—to unlock tensile deformation in ceramics. However, scaling these approaches to achieve macroscopic plasticity in high-strength bulk ceramics remains a formidable challenge14,15. Processing bulk amorphous oxides with metastable coordination bonds is difficult due to their tendency to crystallize at high temperatures, while low-temperature processing often results in weak, porous materials16,17. Likewise, uniform dislocation transfer throughout a ceramic bulk is experimentally demanding18–20. Despite these challenges, the two pioneering studies offer a crucial insight: if directional metal doping can be used to generate dense, high-purity metastable phase—enabling dislocation-like atomic displacement reinforcement—it may be possible to concurrently achieve high strength and tensile plasticity in ceramics21,22.

Here, we report a scalable phase-modulation strategy that enables the controlled synthesis of Al2O3 meta-fibers containing a high-purity metastable [AlO5] coordination network, realizing simultaneous tensile super-plasticity and high strength. We designed a precursor fiber embedded with dispersed aluminum nanoparticles (NPs) and carefully regulated the annealing process. Conventionally, Al2O3 formation begins with the development of oxygen lattices containing voids, followed by Al3+ diffusion into octahedral or tetrahedral sites to form coordination-saturated [AlO6] or stable [AlO4] units. In our metal-enriched precursor fibers, however, Al diffusion promotes the formation of a metastable [AlO5]-dominated network (Fig. 1a). Combined experimental and computational analyses confirm that the metastable [AlO5] provides atomic-scale displacement space, while the metastable structure reduces the energy barrier for bond conversion, activating a metal-like dislocation mechanism. As [AlO5] transforms into stable [AlO4] and [AlO6], a local tensile strain of up to 194% is achieved, accompanied by a remarkable toughness of 128.4 MJ/m3—far exceeding conventional limits (Fig. 1b). This localized plasticity is integrated into a macroscopic response through the high-density network of plastic units, endowing the fibers with a tensile strain of 10.01% and a strength of 1.944 GPa, surpassing other oxide ceramics to the best of our knowledge (Fig. 1c).

Results

Phase modulation engineering to form a high-purity [AlO5] network in Al2O3 fibers

We developed an inorganic sol spinning route to synthesize amorphous Al2O3 fibers with a high-purity [AlO5] architecture and controlled diameters. The process began with the reaction of aluminum chloride (AlCl3) and aluminum powder in deionized water to form a stable sol, which was then spun into precursor fibers and subsequently annealed (Fig. S1). Sol stability was governed by Al3+ hydrolysis, influenced by the AlCl3/Al molar ratio. Through systematic analysis of colloidal particle size, dispersion behavior, cluster concentrations, pH, viscosity, and total dissolved solids, an optimal AlCl3/Al molar ratio of 1:5 was identified (Figs. S2–S5). We further engineered precursor fibers incorporating dopamine-coated (5-8 nm thick) Cu or Al NPs (Figs. S6, 7). Here, Cu and Al were selected as dopants due to their distinct ionic valences and diffusion kinetics. With these two models, we systematically investigate how metal diffusion influences the formation of [AlO5] architecture. This synthetic approach yielded three types of ceramic fibers: pure Al2O3, Al-doped Al2O3 (Al-Al2O3), and Cu-doped Al2O3 (Cu-Al2O3).

Thermogravimetric analysis indicated that precursor clusters can transform into amorphous Al2O3 above 600 °C, with crystallization onset at 710 °C (Fig. S8). X-ray diffraction confirmed that pure Al2O3 fibers remain largely amorphous below 700 °C, transitioning to γ- and α-Al2O3 crystalline phases between 800 °C and 1100 °C (Fig. S9). Accordingly, fibers annealed at 600 °C, 800 °C, and 1000 °C—denoted Al2O3−600, Al2O3−800, and Al2O3−1000—were selected for detailed study. TEM imaging revealed that Al2O3−600 consists of amorphous domains alongside incipient crystalline nuclei (Fig. 2a). Al2O3−800 exhibited coarse γ-Al2O3 grains with minor α-Al2O3 content, while Al2O3−1000 was fully crystalline with coarse γ-/α-Al2O3 grains (Fig. S10).

In Cu-Al2O3 fibers, crystallization commenced at 621 °C, whereas Al-Al2O3 fibers remained amorphous up to 800 °C (Fig. S11). Uniform metal infusion altered fiber coloration from white to orange (Cu-Al2O3) and silver-gray (Al-Al2O3) (Fig. S12). Cu doping significantly refined grain structure and reduced the fiber diameter from 1.16 µm to 800 nm (Fig. 2b). Cu-Al2O3 retained a CuAl2O4 spinel structure below 1100 °C, with average grain sizes increasing from 2 nm (Cu-Al2O3−600) to 32.4 nm (Cu-Al2O3−1000) (Fig. S13). In contrast, Al doping did not alter fiber diameter, and Al–Al2O3−600 remained fully amorphous (Fig. 2c). Crystallization to η-Al2O3 occurred at 900 °C, progressing to a mixed η-/α-phase at 1000 °C (Fig. S14). Although Al-Al2O3−800 was largely amorphous, cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed NP agglomeration and a semi-coherent η-Al2O3 interfacial layer (~9 nm thick), as confirmed by fast Fourier transformation (FFT) analysis (Figs. S15–S16). Inverse FFT indicated that oxygen diffusion drives the formation of this crystalline interface (Fig. S17), which exhibits a higher energy barrier than the amorphous matrix, thereby inhibiting further Al diffusion.

To elucidate the formation mechanism of [AlO5], we propose three models of metal diffusion in Al2O3 based on ionic radius, crystal field stabilization energy, and electrostatic energy18–21. During annealing, oxygen atoms initially form tetrahedral and octahedral lattices with interstitial voids. In pure Al2O3, Al3+ preferentially occupies octahedral sites at high temperatures and both octahedral/tetrahedral sites at low temperatures to maximize electrostatic stabilization. This leads to a mixed [AlO6]/[AlO4] coordination environment, resulting in brittle crystalline Al2O3 (Fig. 2d). In Cu-doped fibers, Cu2+ diffuses much faster than Al3+ and preferentially occupies tetrahedral sites, forming [CuO4] units. This induces Jahn–Teller distortion, thereby stabilizing the CuAl2O4 spinel structure (Fig. 2e). This tetrahedral diffusion does not disrupt the oxygen lattice and thus also leads to a mixed [AlO6]/[CuO4] coordination, refining a denser fiber network to enhance both strength and stretchability. In contrast, Al-doping disrupts the oxygen lattice and reduces the oxygen coordination number, favoring the formation of metastable [AlO5] units (Fig. 2f). Raman spectra confirmed that Al-Al2O3 fibers annealed at 600 °C exhibit a high-purity [AlO5] network. However, increasing the annealing temperature promotes the ordering of the oxygen lattice at the expense of [AlO5] (Fig. 2g), as the slow Al diffusion kinetics favor thermodynamically stable coordination bonding at high temperatures23,24. Consequently, 600 °C was identified as the optimal condition for maximizing [AlO5] content.

In-situ scanning electron microscopy (SEM) tensile testing reveals that only Al diffusion—which disrupts the oxygen lattice—produces plastic fibers. Pure Al2O3 fibers showed limited strength (1.02 GPa), strain (4.85%) and Young’s modulus (21.6 GPa). Cu–Al2O3 exhibited ultrahigh strength (2.04 GPa), modulus (32.6 GPa), and moderate strain (6.22%). Remarkably, Al–Al2O3 fibers achieved high strength (1.94 GPa), large modulus (24.0 GPa) and exceptional ductility, with a tensile strain of 10.01% (Fig. 2h, Supplementary Video 1). The similar modulus values for Al2O3 and Al‑Al2O3 fibers arise from their predominantly amorphous structures, while Cu‑Al2O3 fibers exhibit a significantly higher modulus due to the crystalline spinel CuAl2O4 phase. Post-fracture analysis revealed a 6.46% reduction in fiber diameter (Fig. S18). In-situ TEM further probed the deformation mechanism of Al-Al2O3 fibers (Supplementary Video 2). Under loading, the fiber transitioned from uniform to localized plastic deformation. The amorphous region was categorized into Zone I (localized deformation) and Zone II for comparative analysis (Fig. S19). During uniform deformation (0–24.01% strain), pore nucleation dominated in both zones, with the pore count scaling linearly with strain—a direct manifestation of stress dissipation through bond rupture—and an average pore size of ~10 nm (Figs. S20–S21). The formation of these nanopores serves to release accumulated stress during stretching, allowing the fiber to sustain further plastic deformation without premature fracture. Notably, upon unloading at 16.42% strain in Zone I, we observed 13.62% plastic in addition to 2.80% elastic recovery (Fig. S22). Beyond 24.01% strain, crack propagation initiated in Zone I. At fracture, Zone I experienced a dramatic strain surge to 194.75%, while Zone II remained at 37.38% (Fig. S23). This progressive accumulation of damage in the localized plastic zone, together with the pore-mediated stress release, underlies the fatigue mechanism under cyclic loading. The evolution of crack morphology from linear to circular further attested to the high toughness of the [AlO5]-rich fibers.

Mechanisms of super-plasticity in Al-Al2O3 fibers

While prior theoretical studies predicted that amorphous Al2O3 can exhibit plasticity exceeding 100%, we reported direct experimental observation of local tensile plasticity reaching 194.75%25. To uncover the origin of this plasticity, we performed simulations of Von-Mises stress distribution, deliberately excluding the direct contribution of Al NPs (Supplementary Video 3). These simulations show that tensile fracture occurs in two distinct stages: Stage I (0-42 fs) and Stage II (42-600 fs) (Fig. S24). In Stage I, stress concentrates intensely at the interface between the amorphous matrix and the Al NPs, leading to failure at 1.549 GPa. In Stage II, failure of this semi-coherent η-Al2O3 interface precedes macroscopic plastic strain, while Al NPs experience negligible stress—consistent with our in-situ TEM observations.

Then, we simulated the tensile behavior of Al-Al2O3 fibers to elucidate the plastic deformation mechanisms mediated by metastable [AlO5]. Unlike metallic Al, which exhibits plasticity via de-/reconstruction of Al-Al bonds and dislocation-mediated atomic mobility, the rigid Al2O3 lattice constrains atomic movement due to dense oxygen packing that elevates energy barriers for bond reorganization. Crucially, the [AlO5] coordination possesses structural instability that permits low-energy Al-O bond de-/reconstruction under stress. This behavior parallels dislocation motion in metals, where a high-density network of such plastic units enables localized microscopic plasticity to accumulate, ultimately manifesting as macroscopic ductility. Our simulations reveal exceptional mechanical properties: 6% elastic strain, 24% plastic deformation, and 1.5 GPa tensile strength (Fig. 3a). Tracking [AlOx] coordination evolution under increasing strain confirms this mechanism—the [AlOx] distribution remains stable during elastic deformation (Fig. S25), while [AlO5] content decreases substantially throughout plastic flow (Fig. 3b), mainly occurring as 2[AlO5] → [AlO4] + [AlO6]. Additional analysis demonstrates that [AlO5] transforms into [AlO4] via bond rupture rather than significant elongation (Fig. S26), highlighting its intrinsic instability and role in reducing energy barriers for atomic rearrangement (Fig. S27). Meanwhile, the oxygen species released from this bond rupture can coordinate with adjacent [AlO5] to form [AlO6], completing a dynamic bond‑rearrangement cycle that further facilitates local structural relaxation.

To experimentally validate the deformation, we conducted in-situ SEM creep and stress relaxation tests, which confirmed that activation of the bond conversion mechanism in [AlO5] units requires sustained tensile loading. Under prolonged stress, the total fiber strain of 5% was resolved into 2.5% plastic and 2.5% elastic components (Fig. S28), consistent with the 2.8% elasticity observed via in-situ TEM. Long-duration cyclic tensile tests further revealed evolving mechanical behavior: over four cycles, elastic strain decreased from 3.95 to 2.56%, while plastic strain increased to 5.68% (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Video 4). Post-fracture analysis indicated a 7.5% reduction in fiber diameter (corresponding approximately to the plastic strain of 7.5%, Fig. S29), collectively demonstrating that the intrinsic elasticity of Al-Al2O3 fibers remains below 3%. The transformation of [AlO5] units involves time‑dependent bond rearrangement, which is fully activated under the prolonged loading conditions of in‑situ TEM and in‑situ SEM cyclic tests, thereby allowing the full expression of plasticity in these measurements.

Notably, the bond-rupture-mediated reduction of the energy barrier for deformation is irreversible. As [AlO5] is progressively consumed, the fiber’s plasticity diminishes. Spatial mapping of coordination environments via electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) revealed a pronounced depletion of [AlO5] units near the fracture surface following plastic failure (Fig. 3d). This finding was corroborated by pair distribution function (PDF) analysis (Fig. 3e and S30) and solid-state NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 3f), which confirmed that [AlO5] content drops to nearly zero after fracture. Together, these results underscore that a high-density network of [AlO5] units is essential for the macroscopic plasticity of the fiber, with an estimated threshold of at least 25% content required to sustain observable plasticity under SEM tensile testing. Below this level, the fibers fail to exhibit significant plastic deformation. Moreover, these metastable units are progressively consumed during plastic flow. Critically, during tensile deformation, neither bond lengths nor angles within the [AlOₓ] units exhibited significant change. Instead, plasticity arises from the rupture of Al–O bonds and the resulting reconfiguration of Al–O coordination environments, which facilitate the formation of pores or microcracks. These insights confirm that [AlO5]-enabled bond conversion underpins the unique plastic deformation behavior of Al-Al2O3 fibers.

Topological Al-Al2O3 yarns and fabrics with macroscopic tensile plasticity

Conventional rigid ceramics have historically been excluded from applications requiring deformation tolerance. The advent of plastic ceramics now disrupts this paradigm, offering an alternative to polymer-based systems. Here, we demonstrate the intrinsic twistability of plastic Al-Al2O3 fibers into yarns, which are subsequently woven into fire-resistant and electrically insulating fabrics (Fig. S31). While this showcases promising potential—particularly in terms of strength and processability—we acknowledge that further improvements in cyclic fatigue performance are still required to meet the stringent long-term durability demands of real-world fire-resistant textiles.

The mechanical integrity of these ceramic fabrics critically depends on yarn strength. Through twist optimization, we fabricated aligned 20-μm Al-Al2O3 yarns that mitigate the degradation caused by random fiber orientation. By further plying five and fifteen 20-μm yarns, we produced 50-μm and 100-μm yarns (Fig. 4a and S32). Cross-sectional analysis revealed the influence of ply number on mechanical performance (Fig. S33). The 20-μm yarn exhibited 74.41% porosity and delivered the highest tensile strength (257.4 MPa) but a low strain (3.8%), including a plastic plateau of 2.688%. Increasing yarn diameter to 50 μm reduced porosity to 63.74% (Fig. S34), enhancing tensile strain to 4.45% while retaining substantial strength (229 MPa) (Fig. 4b and S35)—properties competitive with commercial carbon and polymer yarns (Fig. 4c). In contrast, 100-μm yarns showed both high porosity (81.07%) and strain but diminished strength due to fiber slippage (Fig. S36). Dynamic mechanical analysis under creep and stress-relaxation conditions further confirmed the yarns’ plastic behavior (Fig. S37).

Notably, 50-μm yarns can be over-twisted into coarse spiral structures with diameters of 1–2 mm, readily integrable into practical ceramic fabrics (Fig. 4d). The resulting textile exhibits mechanical performance on par with commercial alumina felt (Fig. 4e), while retaining exceptional flexibility and fireproof properties. The fabric displays ultra-low thermal conductivity—0.032 W/m·K at room temperature, approaching that of air (0.026 W/m·K), and only 0.052 W/m·K at 500 °C (Fig. 4f). In thermal protection tests, a mere 0.2-cm-thick layer of our fabric surpassed the insulation performance of 5-cm-thick cotton and goose down (Fig. 4g), underscoring its superior heat resistance in compact geometries.

Discussion

We have established that the metastable [AlO5] phase within the disordered O lattice is the primary origin of the tensile super-plasticity and high strength observed in Al2O3 fibers. The content and, crucially, the distribution uniformity of the Al NPs are fundamental in governing the formation of this unique structure. To decouple the contributions of the ceramic and metallic phases, we first prepared bulk Al-Al2O3 composites with varying Al content via hot-pressing. As the Al content decreased to 70 wt.%, the tensile strain plummeted to 1.12%, accompanied by the complete disappearance of the plastic plateau. A further reduction to 60 wt.% drove the tensile strain down to 0.81%, while the tensile strength concurrently dropped from 508 to 285 MPa (Fig. S38). Fractography analysis revealed that failure consistently initiates at the metal-ceramic interface before the metal can undergo plastic deformation, precipitating this simultaneous collapse in both strength and ductility. These results confirmed that the macroscopic plasticity of our fibers is intrinsically derived from [AlO5] phase, not the metallic Al NPs.

Subsequently, we investigated the influence of NP dispersion uniformity on the [AlO5] network. We fabricated Al-Al2O3 fibers via dry spinning with diameters spanning from 5 to 300 μm. Given that the sol viscosity in dry spinning is high, achieving a homogeneous dispersion of Al NPs is challenging. In thinner fibers (5–20 μm), agglomerated Al NPs were clearly visible on the surfaces. As the diameter increased to 50-300 μm, surface cracks became prevalent (Figure S39). Although a quantitative correlation between NP dispersion and [AlO5] content was not established, a clear qualitative trend emerged: fibers with larger diameters exhibited progressively poorer dispersion, which directly led to a marked decrease in [AlO5] content and the complete loss of plasticity. Owing to this non‑uniform dispersion, all these fibers crystallized, exhibited brittle fractures, and their mechanical properties progressively deteriorated with increasing diameter (Fig. S40). These findings confirm that uniform NP dispersion is critical. Agglomeration leads to localized enrichment of [AlO5], which prevents the effective percolation and accumulation of local micro-plasticity, thereby hindering its translation into fiber plasticity. Consequently, fabricating bulk plastic Al2O3 necessitates precise control over the spatial distribution of Al NPs within the matrix.

In summary, we have developed a phase-modulation strategy that enables the fabrication of Al2O3 meta-fibers with unprecedented tensile plasticity and high strength. The key breakthrough lies in the disordered oxygen lattice, which unlocks additional atomic displacement pathways. Within this framework, metastable [AlO5] functions as a fundamental plastic element by significantly reducing the energy barrier for atomic rearrangement. Crucially, the coordinated deformation of these [AlO5] units through a high-density network allows localized microscopic plasticity to accumulate coherently, ultimately manifesting as macroscopic tensile plasticity. The practical significance of this discovery is demonstrated by the ability to directly twist these fibers into robust spiral yarns, thereby helping to overcome long-standing brittleness limitations that hinder ceramic fiber applications (Supplementary Video 5). This work establishes a general paradigm for designing tensile-plastic oxide ceramics, effectively bridging the gap between atomic-scale deformation mechanisms and macroscopic mechanical performance.

Methods

Preparation of water-based inorganic aluminum sol

First, 24.15 g of AlCl3·6H2O (ALADDIN, 99.99%) were dissolved in 100 ml of deionized water to prepare a solution with a concentration of 1 mol/L, which had a pH of 0.83. Next, 13.5 g of Al-metal powder (RHAWN, 200–300 mesh) was added to the AlCl3 solution. The molar mass ratio of Al to AlCl3·6H2O was 5:1. Then, the mixture solution was heated with stirring to ensure a uniform and stable chemical reaction. Specifically, the solution was first heated to 75 °C and reacted violently for 1 h, then heated to 90 °C for 10 h, and finally cooled. The entire phase reaction process took over 12 h to ensure that more than 99% of the Al-metal powder was dissolved. The reaction occurred in a condensation reflux device, to remove the generated hydrogen and recover water vapor. It was easy to filter out the undissolved Al-metal powder to obtain a clear and transparent aluminum sol, which exhibited great fluidity. Preparation of dopamine-coated metal NPs

The Al NPs (MAO GUO), with a diameter of 50 nm, were coated with Dopa (ALADDIN, 98%) in a Tris buffer solution with a pH value of approximately 7. Specifically, 50 g of Al NPs were added to 500 ml of a self-polymerized dopamine solution, and after 24 h of reaction, they were removed, filtered, and washed with alcohol. The obtained Dopa-coated Al NPs exhibited a uniform morphology, similar to that of Cu NPs.

Electrospinning and dry spinning of the prepared aluminum sol

The prepared and modified Al NPs were introduced into the aluminum sol at a molar ratio of 5 wt.% of the sol. To achieve a comparable effect of metal NPs on the electrospinning sol, the molar ratio of Cu NPs to Al NPs was kept at 1:1. The mass ratio of the polyethylene oxide additive (MACKLIN, Mv ~600,000) was set at 0.3 wt.% of the total solid content. After thorough grinding and mixing, the aluminum sol was successfully obtained. The electrospinning parameters were established as follows: the voltage was set to ±13–14 kV, and the sol propulsion speed was 1 ml/h per needle. The yarn, with a diameter of 20 μm and high orientation, was produced using a collecting disc speed of 50 revolutions/min and a collecting rod speed of 60 cm/min. Regarding the dry spinning parameters, the settings were as follows: the drying temperature was 35 °C, the humidity was 40%, and the length of the drying tunnel was 1 m. The speed of the collecting rod was adjusted from 1 m/min to 30 m/min. The metal NPs were dispersed by vigorous stirring followed by prolonged ultrasonication for 1 h to minimize agglomeration.

Fabrication of Al2O3 yarns

Before annealing, the electrospun Al2O3 yarn precursor should be drawn and twisted. The tension during drafting was approximately 2-3 cN, and the twisting rate was 60 revolution/min. Following additional drafting and twisting, the orientation and strength of the Al2O3 yarn were significantly enhanced. Subsequently, the annealing process for the Al2O3 yarns was divided into two stages. In the first stage, the heating rate was 1 °C/min, and the annealing temperature was held at 300 °C for 1 h. In the second stage, the heating rate remained at 1 °C/min, and the annealing temperature was increased to 600 °C for another hour. A specific tension was applied to the yarns throughout the annealing process.

Fabrication of Al2O3@Al bulk

Micron-sized Al2O3 particles and Al-metal particles were applied. The mass ratios of Al2O3 to Al were 0:100, 10:90, 30:70, and 40:60, respectively, to obtain different mixed particles. The mixed particles were ball milled for 1 h, and the ball milling parameters were similar to those used for obtaining Dopa-coated Al-metal NPs. Afterward, the mixed particles were pressed into sheets and placed into a high-temperature vacuum furnace set at 600 °C for 2 h, during which a certain pressure was applied during the annealing process.

Obtaining the cross sections of Al2O3 fibers and yarns

The yarn was initially soaked in liquid paraffin, then dried to stabilize the internal fibers, subsequently frozen in liquid nitrogen for 1 h, and finally cut along the cross-section. The paraffin was ultimately removed through simple heating. The cross-section of the Al2O3 yarn was thus obtained. The cross-section of the Al2O3 fiber was prepared using a FIB. Initially, the yarn was dispersed into individual fibers in alcohol. Subsequently, a suitable fiber was selected for the TEM test, and the sample was obtained by applying a Pt protective layer. The protective layer was then trimmed using an argon ion beam to reduce its cross-sectional thickness to less than 100 nm.

Materials characterization

The chemical properties of the aluminum sol were tested using NMR (AVANCE III HD 600 MHz), the colloidal particle size within the sol was measured via dynamic light scattering (BI-200SM), and the sol’s viscosity was assessed with an intelligent viscosity measuring instrument (DV2TLV). The sol’s surface tension and electrical conductivity were measured using a surface tensiometer (DCAT11) and a conductivity meter (DDSJ-308F), respectively. SEM (SU8010) and TEM (JEM-2100f) were employed to observe the morphology of fibers and yarns, with ImageJ software utilized for analyzing the cross-sections of fibers and yarns. TG (TG209F1 Libra) analysis and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, DS8500) under an air atmosphere were conducted to determine the annealing temperature. The Brucker Escalab 250Xi, equipped with a Cu Kα wavelength, was used to ascertain the materials’ crystallinity, while X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Escalab 250Xi) was employed for elemental analysis. An automatic fast specific surface and porosity analyzer (Autosorb-iQ) was utilized for porosity testing and simulation analysis. Optical photographs were captured using a stereomicroscope (NIKON SMZ745T). For obtaining stress-strain curves, yarn samples were equilibrated at 25 °C under a preload of 0.1 cN, and the force was increased at a rate of 6 cN·min–1 by a single fiber strength tester (ST200C). A dynamic mechanical analyzer (DMA850) was used to evaluate the creep and stress relaxation properties of the yarns. The values for tensile strength, strain, toughness, and modulus were averaged from over ten measurements. The Electronic Energy Loss Spectroscopy (EELS) test was conducted by the Shanghai Institute of Ceramics, Chinese Academy of Sciences.

Semi-quantitative calculation of XRD testing

According to the semi-quantitative calculation of XRD test, the interface is composed of 81.18% of η-Al2O3 and 18.82% of α-Al2O3, and the formula for mismatch of two contact surface is δ=αβ−αααα, where αα and αβ are atom distances. First, we calculate the mismatch of Al and the interface, where αβ is the atom distance (2.85 Å) in the Al phase, and αα is the atom distance in the η-phase. The atom distanceαα is 1.97 Å when there is 100% of η-Al2O3, and the average atom distance is 2.49 Å (1.97 Å*0.8118 + 4.76 Å*0.1882) when there are 81.18% η-phase and 18.82% α-phase. Therefore, the mismatch δ between Al/η-Al2O3 was calculated as 0.1445, and the value was increased to 0.4426 when the interface contained 100% of η-Al2O3. Then, we calculated the mismatch of the amorphous region and the interface, where αβ is the atom distance in the η-phase, and αα is the atom distance in the amorphous. Considering that the atomic distance in amorphous Al2O3 is greater than crystalline Al2O3, we set the atomic distance in amorphous distance to be consistent with α-Al2O3 (4.76 Å). Similarly, the value of mismatch was 1.416 when the interface contained 100% of η-Al2O3, while the value decreased to 0.9116 when there were 81.18% η-phase and 18.82% α-phase.

Dynamic mechanical analysis and tensile test of the Al2O3 fiber

The tensile test was conducted in accordance with GB/T 3362-2017, and two elastic polymer blocks (Ecoflex) measuring 1 × 1 × 1 cm were prepared for the test. Initially, the yarn was cut to the length specified by the test standard. Subsequently, the two ends of the Al2O3 yarn were placed on two Ecoflex blocks, and two drops of epoxy resin solution were applied. The epoxy resin solution was prepared by uniformly mixing 10 ml of acetone, 3 g of epoxy resin, and 0.9 g of triethylenetetramine. Ecoflex was utilized to enhance the instrument’s grip on the yarns, given that the smallest diameter of the yarn was only 20 μm. The epoxy resin solution served to prevent fiber slippage within the yarn during the tensile test. In the dynamic mechanical analysis, only the yarn with a diameter of 20 μm was used as the test subject, as the twist during the tensile test could affect the yarn’s plasticity. A pre-tension ranging from 0.5 cN to 1 cN was applied to straighten the yarns, and the experiment was conducted for a duration of 30 min. Al2O3 yarns with a 1% constant elongation were prone to breaking. Consequently, the maximum constant tension and stress for Al2O3 yarns were set at 0.5% and 40 MPa, respectively.

Molecular dynamics for the tensile property of the Al-Al2O3 fibers.

By referencing the amorphous Al2O3 film, we employed the general empirical potential developed by Streitz and Mintmire (S-M) to describe the interactions between Al2O3 and oxygen atoms. This model is effective across various phases and local structures encountered during strain simulations. All simulations were conducted using LAMMPS simulation software with a 1 femtosecond time step and periodic boundary conditions (PBC). A Nosé-Hoover thermostat and barostat, as implemented in LAMMPS, were utilized for temperature and/or pressure control. Cell deformations were achieved by incrementally changing the cell dimensions every 100 fs to prevent artifacts from abrupt changes. The strain direction changes were pre-defined, while the changes in perpendicular directions were regulated by the barostat, maintaining ambient pressure in those directions.

The amorphous base structure was generated using a process similar to that described by Gutiérrez et al. Initially, a random Al2O3 structure with a density of 2.8 g/cm3 was equilibrated at 5000 K for 1 ns under NVT conditions to erase any structural memory. The structure was then cooled down to 300 K over 1 ns and equilibrated at that temperature for another 1 ns. The density was subsequently adjusted to 3.1 g/cm3. Following this, we employed Atomsk to model the Al2O3-Al system with an embedded Al-ball. The model was relaxed once more at 300 K for 1 ns to achieve a balanced structure. Due to atom deletion and insertion operations, the system often lost charge balance. To address this issue, the crystal was cut into specific sizes to minimize charge imbalance, and additional atoms in the glass were randomly selected and deleted to maintain charge neutrality in the final sample. Finally, the model was deformed at a constant strain rate of 1×109 s−1. During the deformation process, the stress in other directions was controlled at zero using the Berendsen barostat. The open visualization tool (OVITO) was utilized to visualize the simulation results.
