Severe Macrocytic Anemia Uncovering Pernicious Anemia and Early-Stage Gastric Adenocarcinoma: A Case Report
Authors: Admire Hlupeni, Jeffrey Melnick, Adebisi Adejola, Tarig Mohemmed, Elie J Chahla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pernicious anemia (PA) is an autoimmune cause of vitamin B12 deficiency, often secondary to chronic atrophic gastritis, which increases the risk of gastric malignancies. Early signs, like mild macrocytosis, are frequently overlooked, especially in patients with other risk factors such as long-term metformin use. We present a case illustrating the full clinical trajectory from long-standing, unexplored macrocytosis to severe anemia, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of PA and detection of early-stage gastric adenocarcinoma on surveillance esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). Informed consent was obtained from the patient to publish the case.The patient was a 59-year-old man with type 2 diabetes, treated with metformin 2 g daily for over four years, who presented with severe symptomatic macrocytic anemia after approximately one year of unexplored macrocytosis on routine laboratory testing. Evaluation revealed profound vitamin B12 deficiency with evidence of hemolysis, and serologic testing confirmed PA. Surveillance EGD identified a gastric polyp, which was resected for histopathologic evaluation. Examination of the lesion revealed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with associated high-grade dysplasia and submucosal invasion. Random gastric biopsies demonstrated chronic inflammation with focal atypia and were negative for Helicobacter pylori. Subsequent staging investigations confirmed stage I disease (cT1b, cN0, and cM0). The patient underwent partial gastrectomy and continued intramuscular vitamin B12 therapy, with excellent clinical recovery and no evidence of recurrent disease on follow-up.Our case highlights that persistent macrocytosis should not be overlooked, particularly in patients with risk factors for vitamin B12 deficiency such as long-term metformin use. Early evaluation may uncover important underlying diagnoses such as PA, prevent severe hematologic complications, and facilitate detection of associated gastric malignancies at a potentially curable stage.

Introduction

Vitamin B12 deficiency is a common cause of macrocytic anemia, presenting with a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations ranging from subtle fatigue to severe neurologic dysfunction. Causes include nutritional deficiencies, malabsorption syndromes, and certain medications [1]. For example, metformin is associated with vitamin B12 deficiency by impairing the calcium-dependent absorption of the vitamin B12-intrinsic factor (IF) complex in the terminal ileum, particularly at doses exceeding 1.5 g daily and with prolonged use [1-4].

Another important cause is pernicious anemia (PA), an autoimmune disorder characterized by destruction of gastric parietal cells and the production of autoantibodies against IF [1]. These processes lead to impaired vitamin B12 absorption and, ultimately, vitamin B12 deficiency. The associated chronic autoimmune gastritis and gastric atrophy also increase the risk of gastric adenocarcinoma and gastric neuroendocrine tumors [5,6]. Diagnosis of PA involves serologic testing for IF-blocking and parietal cell antibodies. Once confirmed, lifelong vitamin B12 replacement therapy is essential, and surveillance endoscopies are recommended to detect gastric malignancies early, given the well-established association between PA and gastric tumors [5-7].

In this report, we present a case of potentially delayed recognition of PA that culminated in severe anemia and a surveillance-detected stage I locoregional gastric adenocarcinoma, illustrating the classical presentation of PA and its well-established association with gastric cancers. This case also highlights how severe vitamin B12 deficiency can lead to significant hemolysis, further complicating the clinical picture. Importantly, it demonstrates how early recognition and intervention can enable favorable outcomes, underscoring the need to thoroughly investigate even mild, persistent macrocytic anemia to identify and manage potentially serious underlying conditions.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old African-American man with hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus (on metformin 1 g twice daily for four years), obesity, and obstructive sleep apnea presented with a two-week history of progressive dyspnea on exertion and extreme fatigue. He also reported a dry cough but denied chest pain, leg swelling, gastrointestinal bleeding, or changes in bowel habits.

He had two prior outpatient encounters that showed early evidence of macrocytosis. Approximately 12 months before the current presentation, he was evaluated after a mechanical fall. Routine laboratory testing at that time revealed very mild anemia (hemoglobin 13.5 g/dL; reference range, 13.6-16.5 g/dL) and macrocytosis (mean corpuscular volume (MCV) 103.6 fL), but no further evaluation was pursued. At a subsequent annual primary care visit, seven months before presentation, repeat laboratory work demonstrated worsening anemia (hemoglobin 12.6 g/dL) and persistent macrocytosis (MCV 100.9 fL), without additional workup. Two prior colonoscopies, performed at ages 44 and 55 years, were unremarkable. He was a non-smoker, did not consume alcohol, and lived with his wife.

On arrival at the hospital Emergency Department (ED), the patient was tachycardic (heart rate, 103 beats per minute) and markedly pale, but cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations were otherwise unremarkable. Electrocardiogram and chest X-ray revealed no significant acute abnormalities (Figure 1).

Based on these results, PA was suspected. IF blocking antibodies (74.6 AU/mL, normal range 0.0-1.1) and parietal cell antibodies (57.5 units, normal range 0.0-20.0) confirmed the diagnosis. He underwent a bone marrow biopsy and aspirate, which demonstrated a moderately hypercellular marrow with erythroid and megakaryocytic hyperplasia, mild erythroid dyspoiesis, and megaloblastic myeloid and erythroid maturation, without evidence of malignancy (Figure 3).

Bone marrow biopsy and aspirate findings.

Panel A (hematoxylin and eosin stain, 40×) shows a bone marrow core biopsy specimen demonstrating a moderately hypercellular marrow with increased hematopoietic cellularity.

Panel B shows a bone marrow aspirate smear (Wright-Giemsa stain, 1000× oil immersion) with findings interpreted by hematopathology as erythroid and megakaryocytic hyperplasia (yellow arrows), with mild erythroid dyspoiesis and megaloblastic myeloid and erythroid maturation.

The patient was transfused with packed red blood cells and started on intramuscular vitamin B12 injections, with rapid clinical and hematologic improvement. He was discharged on hospital day 10 on intramuscular vitamin B12 1000 μg daily, with plans for indefinite supplementation. Neither colonoscopy nor esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) was performed during the initial admission, as there was no clinical or laboratory evidence of gastrointestinal bleeding. His stool occult blood test was negative; therefore, vitamin B12 deficiency from PA was considered the primary cause of his anemia.

A baseline EGD performed two weeks after discharge, in accordance with contemporary guidelines recommending evaluation for gastric malignancy in patients with PA [7], revealed a 30-mm semi-pedunculated polyp at the incisura with spontaneous bleeding (Figure 4). Although the gastric polyp demonstrated spontaneous surface bleeding, its contribution to the patient's profound anemia was considered unlikely, given the severe vitamin B12 deficiency, longstanding macrocytosis, and absence of clinical or laboratory evidence of significant gastrointestinal blood loss.

The lesion was completely resected endoscopically and submitted for histopathologic evaluation. Histopathologic examination demonstrated a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma arising within the polyp with high-grade dysplasia and invasion into the submucosa (Figure 5).

Histopathologic examination of the resected gastric polyp (hematoxylin and eosin stain).

Panel A demonstrates high-grade glandular dysplasia (200×). Panel B shows moderately differentiated intramucosal adenocarcinoma characterized by infiltrating atypical glandular structures (black arrow, 200×). Panel C demonstrates submucosal invasion by adenocarcinoma (black arrow, 100×).

These findings confirmed a moderately differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma with submucosal invasion arising in a gastric polyp with associated high-grade dysplasia, consistent with the final pathologic stage of pT1b disease.

HER2 and PD-L1 were negative. Random gastric biopsies showed fragments of antral-type mucosa with chronic inflammation and focal atypia, indeterminate for high-grade glandular dysplasia, and Helicobacter pylori testing was negative.

Approximately three weeks after the diagnostic EGD, the patient underwent endoscopic ultrasound (EUS) and positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) as part of the staging work-up. At the site of the previously resected gastric lesion in the incisura, EUS and endoscopic examination demonstrated heaped-up tissue consistent with the prior endoscopic resection site. No regional lymphadenopathy was identified in the gastrohepatic ligament, celiac, or perigastric regions. PET/CT demonstrated no evidence of distant metastatic disease (Figure 6).

Collectively, these findings were consistent with localized disease without regional nodal or distant metastatic spread. Accordingly, the tumor was staged as clinical stage I (cT1b, cN0, cM0) according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition staging system [8].

Per the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines in effect at the time, upfront surgery was indicated for T1b lesions because of the increased risk of lymphovascular invasion and incomplete endoscopic resection [9]. The patient subsequently underwent partial gastrectomy with Roux-en-Y gastrojejunostomy. Histopathologic examination of the resected specimen demonstrated a mucosal/submucosal scar consistent with the prior endoscopic resection site, patchy chronic inflammation, intestinal metaplasia, and no residual carcinoma or dysplasia. Seven perigastric lymph nodes were identified and were negative for malignancy (0/7) (Figure 7).

Combined with the initial endoscopic resection specimen demonstrating submucosal invasion, the final pathologic stage was Stage IA (pT1bN0M0) according to the AJCC 8th edition staging system.

The case was discussed at a multidisciplinary tumor board. Given the absence of residual tumor and negative nodes, no adjuvant therapy was recommended. Surveillance was initiated per NCCN guidelines, including scheduled EGDs and imaging. At the 18-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with normalized blood counts and vitamin B12 levels (Table 1). Surveillance CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis at six months post-gastrectomy showed no evidence of recurrence or metastasis (Figure 8). He continues to follow up regularly for nutritional monitoring and surveillance.

Discussion

This case illustrates the clinical progression from previously unexplored mild macrocytic anemia to severe symptomatic anemia due to PA, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of early-stage gastric adenocarcinoma. Mild macrocytic anemia can be an early sign of an underlying condition such as PA, and persistent macrocytosis, as in our patient, warrants further evaluation [6]. When vitamin B12 deficiency is identified, a thorough workup is essential to differentiate nutritional deficiency from autoimmune causes such as PA [10]. In our patient, comprehensive diagnostic evaluation confirmed PA and incidentally uncovered a moderately differentiated gastric adenocarcinoma, an uncommon but classical complication of chronic gastritis associated with PA [11]. This underscores the importance of performing surveillance EGD in patients with PA, even in the absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, as it enables early detection and treatment of gastric malignancies [7].

The patient also had evidence of hemolysis with reticulocytosis, which can itself raise MCV and potentially mislead clinicians into attributing macrocytosis solely to hemolysis. However, it is essential to recognize that severe vitamin B12 deficiency leads to ineffective erythropoiesis, driving both intramedullary hemolysis and macrocytosis [12]. In this case, the long-standing, progressively worsening macrocytic anemia, predating the acute presentation, and the patient's chronic metformin use were important diagnostic clues pointing toward vitamin B12 deficiency.

This case also highlights the value of multidisciplinary care. Coordination among hematology, gastroenterology, pathology, oncology, surgery, and primary care was crucial to achieving a timely diagnosis, curative treatment, and structured surveillance. Although our patient did not have thyroid dysfunction or autoimmune diabetes at the time of presentation, ongoing monitoring is warranted, given the increased risk of autoimmune endocrinopathies in patients with PA [6,13].

Conclusions

Persistent mild macrocytosis may represent an early manifestation of clinically significant vitamin B12 deficiency and should not be attributed solely to medications such as metformin without further evaluation. This case illustrates how delayed recognition of PA can culminate in severe hematologic complications and emphasizes the importance of timely diagnostic workup, particularly in patients with progressive macrocytic anemia. Importantly, recognition of PA has implications beyond hematologic management, as appropriate surveillance may facilitate early detection of associated gastric malignancies and improve long-term clinical outcomes.
