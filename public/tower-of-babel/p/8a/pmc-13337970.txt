ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimEarly‐life nutrition plays a critical role in long‐term health. Although complementary feeding has been widely studied, the influence of portion size during this stage on subsequent overweight risk remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate whether portion size during complementary feeding is associated with overweight risk in childhood and preadolescence.MethodsSecondary analysis of the European Childhood Obesity Project, a randomised clinical trial across five European countries. Dietary data was collected at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months using 3‐day food records and food portions standardised as internal z‐scores; anthropometry was assessed at 2, 8 and 11 years. Associations between early portion size and later overweight were examined using logistic regression (adjusted for feeding type, country, parental education, birth weight, meal frequency and energy intake) and structural equation modelling.ResultsLarger portion sizes at 6 months were associated with more than a twofold higher risk of overweight at 2 years (OR 2.36, p = 0.031). Portion size at later complementary feeding stages showed positive trends with overweight at 2, 8 and 11 years and indirect associations with overweight at 8 and 11 years mediated by portion size at 8 years.ConclusionLarger portion sizes early in life are associated with increased risk of overweight later in childhood. These findings highlight portion size guidance during complementary feeding as a potential early target for obesity prevention.

Introduction

Childhood obesity remains one of the major public health challenges worldwide, with long‐term consequences for physical and metabolic health [1]. Effectively preventing childhood obesity from early‐life is therefore a top priority [2, 3]. Available evidence indicates that dietary choices in early life can shape future health outcomes, including body weight and obesity risk [4, 5].

Within the theory of metabolic programming, the complementary feeding (CF) period from about 6 to 24 months of age has been described as a critical period in early life [6, 7]. During the first 2 years of life, infants are primarily fed with breastmilk or infant formula, with a progressively more diversified diet as they grow. Breastfeeding has been consistently associated with a reduced risk of obesity [8, 9, 10, 11].

Toward the end of the first half year of life, infants are introduced to complementary foods with a wider variety of new tastes, textures and food groups [6]. To optimise complementary feeding introduction, several key practices should be considered, including the timing of solid food introduction, food quality and diversity, preparation methods, portion size, feeding frequency, responsiveness to infant hunger and satiety cues and the safe storage of food [12].

Regarding diet quality, dietary patterns characterised by high intake of ultra‐processed foods with an undesirable nutritional profile, for example, with a high energy density and/or high sugar content, have been associated with a greater likelihood of rapid weight gain and an increased risk of overweight or obesity in infants aged 6–12 months [13]. A comprehensive meta‐analysis examining the impact of protein intake during complementary feeding found that higher protein intake—particularly from animal sources—in children ≤ 18 months of age are likely associated with subsequent increased body mass index (BMI) [4, 14, 15]. Additionally, higher energy intake during the complementary feeding period has been associated with higher BMI in childhood [16, 17]. Evidence suggests that total daily energy intake from complementary foods in healthy infants during CF stage increases with both total energy density and feeding frequency [18]. Syrad et al. suggested that young children, from 21 months of age, gain more weight by eating larger portions than by eating more frequently; specifically, every 10 kcal increase in meal size was associated with a 1.5 g/week (4%) faster growth rate [19].

Although the complementary feeding period is increasingly recognised as a critical window for shaping long‐term health outcomes, the specific role of portion size during this stage remains largely unexplored. While some studies have described portion size during this period, they have not examined its potential long‐term health effects [20, 21, 22, 23]. A cross‐sectional study analysing data from three datasets (CSFII 1994–1996/1998, NFCS 1977–1978 and a longitudinal sample) found a positive association between portion size and body weight in infants aged 12–24 months [17]. However, there is limited evidence on whether portion size during complementary feeding influences the risk of overweight or obesity later in childhood.

Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate whether portion size during the complementary feeding period is associated with an increased risk of overweight (including obesity) later in childhood.

Material and Methods

Study Design

This is a secondary longitudinal analysis from the EU Childhood Obesity Project (EU CHOP). The CHOP study was a longitudinal, multicentre, double‐blind and randomised clinical trial testing whether reducing protein supply during the first year of life could reduce later obesity risk.

The trial used two non‐hydrolyzed cow's formula milks with different protein content. The protein content of the infant formulas was 1.8 g/100 kcal vs. 2.2 g/100 kcal for the lower and the higher protein formula and the content of the follow‐on formulas was 2.9 g/100 kcal and 4.4 g/100 kcal, for the lower and the higher protein formula respectively [15]. Formulas were nutritionally complete, according to the European Commission Directive [24].

The study was conducted in hospitals from 5 European countries: Belgium (Brussels and Liege), Germany (Munich and Nuremberg), Italy (Milano), Poland (Warsaw) and Spain (Tarragona and Reus). More details of the clinical trial were previously published [15].

Study Population

Participants were enrolled within the first 8 weeks of life at the participating centres. Inclusion criteria for study eligibility were apparently healthy, singleton, term infants who were born between October 2002 and July 2004. Participants had to reside in the study area and caregivers had to be fluent in the local language. Infants whose mothers ingested drugs during pregnancy that could influence infant growth or mothers with hormonal and/or metabolic diseases, were excluded. Participants were randomly assigned to receive one of the two formula types, which they consumed during the first year of life. A group of breastfed infants was also recruited as a reference group.

Data Collection

Dietary data were collected at 6‐, 12‐, 18‐ and 24 months of life using means of weighed and/or estimated 3‐day food diaries [25, 26]. Anthropometric measurements taken at ages 2‐, 8‐ and 11‐years visits were considered as outcome measures.

Dietary Assessment

Food intake was recorded by parents or caregivers on three consecutive days: including 2 weekdays and 1 weekend day. Food records were specifically designed for different age ranges (e.g., beginning of complementary feeding, toddlerhood and childhood) to improve the accuracy and quality of all the collected dietary data across the different feeding stages. Food records were retrospectively checked by trained nutritionists‐dietitians, by using standardised operating procedures, during the face‐to‐face study visits at all ages (except for the 18‐month visit, which was done by telephone). Dietary data were entered into a dedicated software developed as a part of EU CHOP project. The database was mainly based on the German BLS II.3 food composition table and it was complemented and extended by adding local food items using national food composition databases or food labels, especially in case of commercial complementary foods. A Standard Operation Procedures (SOP) manual was created to ensure a harmonised interpretation and quantification of data food records [25]. During the food record data entry, a quality check of the reported data was applied. The accuracy of the food records was assessed according to a standardised procedure previously described by Verwied‐Jorky et al. [25]. Briefly, records were evaluated considering the quality of food description, completeness of recipe ingredient information and the reporting of quantities offered and consumed. Children who were still breastfeeding at the analysed timepoint were automatically excluded when applying the accuracy criteria, ensuring that variables such as meal frequency were not affected. Once children had stopped breastfeeding, they were included in the analyses. Food records with poor accuracy and those collected during days when the child was reported to be ill where excluded from the analyses.

Calculating Portion Size

Each portion size was expressed in grams. Portions were also classified in 33 food groups (see Table S1). When multiple food items from the same food group were consumed within a single meal, their gram amounts were summed (e.g., carrots and tomatoes eaten in the same meal were combined and considered a single portion of vegetables). For each participant a mean portion size for each food group was calculated based on all meals of the 3‐day food records in which the child consumed that food group at least once. Thus, food portions described do not represent the overall consumption but the size of the portion when consumed. To normalise the mean food portion size of the different food groups, those were converted into internal‐for‐age portion size z‐scores. The mean of all these z‐scores was calculated to obtain a unique portion size z‐score per child. An example of the calculation procedure is illustrated in Figure S1.

Meal Frequency

The average number of meals per day over the 3‐day collection period was calculated for each participant at each timepoint.

Anthropometry

All anthropometric measurements were performed by trained dietitian‐nutritionist and study nurses, who received instructions on standardised procedures and completed 4 h of training sessions four times throughout the study. The same equipment, periodically calibrated, was used in all the centres.

Weight (kg) was measured using SECA 336 baby scale [precision ±10 g] at 2‐year visit and SECA 702 was used at 8‐ and 11‐year visit. Height (m) was measured using SECA 242 stadiometer [precision ±1 mm] at 2, 8‐ and 11‐year visit. All the measurements were made twice and recorded with a precision of 50 g for weight and 0.1 cm for height. The mean of the two values was used in subsequent analyses. BMI was calculated as weight/height2 (kg/m2).

At 2 years children were classified as normal weight if their weight‐for‐height was between ≥ −3 Standard Deviations (SD) and ≤ 2SD and classified as overweight and/or obesity category if their weight‐for‐height > 2SD, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) Child Growth Standards [27]. At 8 and 11 years, children were classified as normal weight if their BMI was between ≥ −2SD and ≤ 1SD and were classified as overweight or at risk of obesity category if their BMI was > 1 SD according to the WHO Child Growth References [28].

At 2 years, no children were classified as underweight according to WHO criteria. At 8 and 11 years, 7 children (1.2%) and 12 children (2.1%) were classified as underweight (< −2 SD), respectively and were included in the normal weight category for analytical purposes. For facilitating the readiness of the results, when referring to children with overweight and/or obesity it will be summarised as ‘children with overweight’.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean and SD. Normality was assessed by visualisation. To compare food portion sizes between weight status groups, a t‐test for independent samples was applied. For anthropometric variables (weight, height/length and BMI), which did not meet normality assumptions, the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test was applied.

Pearson correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between portion size z‐score and either meal frequency or total energy consumed (kcal/day).

Binary logistic regression analyses were conducted to assess the association of portion size consumed at different ages with overweight risk. Portion size was analysed as a continuous variable and entered into the logistic regression models as such. Portion sizes at 2 and 8 years, along with the interaction between earlier intake and both later time points, were included as predictors, as later portion sizes may be conditioned by earlier feeding practices. Models were fitted using a combination of forced entry and forward stepwise selection (Wald statistic). Portion size z‐score was included using forced entry (enter method), while early feeding (breastfeeding, higher protein and lower protein formula), country, weight at birth, mother's and father's education level and meal frequency and energy intake at the time that food portions were analysed were considered using a forward stepwise procedure.

Structural equation modelling (SEM) was performed to analyse the mediating role of portion size measured at the time of weight status assessment in overweight risk, without imputation of missing values (maximum likelihood estimation from cases with full information). In the case of overweight risk at 11 years, portion size at 8 years was used as a mediator due to lack of dietary intake data at 11 years.

Linear mixed‐effects models were used to examine the associations between meal frequency and energy intake with portion sizes across timepoints (from 6 months to 8 years). The models included timepoint, either energy intake or meal frequency (in separate models) and their interaction as fixed effects, along with a random intercept for participant ID to account for repeated measurements. In addition, correlation analyses were conducted to examine the relationships between meal frequency and portion size and between energy intake and portion size.

Sensitivity Analyses

To assess whether the associations varied according to food type, food groups were classified into energy‐dense discretionary foods and nutrient‐dense core foods. Logistic regression models were repeated using these category‐specific portion size (z‐score).

Nutrient‐dense core foods group includes fruits, vegetables, leafy vegetables, soups and vegetable dishes, ready‐to‐eat infant foods, cow milk and regular yoghurt, grains, potatoes, pulses, red meat, white meat, eggs, fish, lean seafood and fatty fish. Energy‐dense discretionary foods group includes flavoured milks, processed milk products, processed cereal products, processed potatoes, processed meat, processed fish, saturated spreads, added sugar, cakes, biscuits, pastries, confectionary, savoury snacks, soft drinks and other sauces (not oil based). Food groups that could not be clearly classified into any of the categories or that could fit into both categories, were excluded from the sub‐analysis to ensure analytical clarity.

Statistical analysis was carried out with SPSS Statistics (Version 31.0.1.0) and RStudio (Version 4.5.2).

Assessment of Attrition Bias

To evaluate potential attrition bias over the follow‐up period, we compared baseline characteristics between participants included in the analysis and those lost to follow‐up. Continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test and categorical variables were compared using the Chi‐square test.

Baseline characteristics of included participants are presented in the main manuscript, while those of participants lost to follow‐up are provided in the Supporting Information S1. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 and values between 0.05 and 0.10 were considered indicative of borderline differences (p‐values not shown).

Ethics

The study was submitted to and approved by the local ethical committees of each hospital/study center (Hospital Universitari Joan XXIII de Tarragona, Hospital Universitari Sant Joan de Reus, the Children's Memorial Health Institute Warsaw, the Medical Faculty, Ludwig‐Maximilians‐Universität Munich, the Medical Faculty—University of Milan, the CHU de Liège and Höpital Universitaire des Enfants Reine Fabiola). The study was carried out in compliance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The EU CHOP was registered at clinicaltrials.gov as NCT00338689. Parents or caregivers received written information about the project and signed the consent for their child to participate in the study.

Results

Study Sample

A total of 1679 infants were recruited between 0 and 8 weeks of age (median age = 14 days, Interquartile range: 3–30 days). Dietary intake was collected at 6, 12, 18 and 24 months of age and linked to anthropometric measurements taken at 2, 8 and 11 years. At 6 months, 1202 infants attended the study visit and 1009 remained in the study at 24 months. Dietary intake was provided at each time point by around 80%–90% of the participating families. Correlation analyses (presented in Table S3) indicated that portion size was inversely correlated with meal frequency from 6 months up to 8 years of age. Sensitivity Analysis

According to food type, associations differed across food categories (Table S4 and Table S5). For nutrient‐dense core foods, larger portion size z‐score at 6, 12 and 18 months were associated with overweight risk at 2 years, whereas only portion size at 18 months was associated with long term overweight risk (8 years). For energy‐dense discretionary foods, significant associations were observed only between portion size at 6 months and overweight risk at 2 years.

Discussion

This study examines the relationship between portion size during the complementary feeding period and long‐term overweight risk in a European paediatric population. Our findings suggest that the portion size of complementary feeding during the first 2 years of life might be important for childhood obesity prevention.

Portion size has been associated with energy intake in adult populations [30]. Previous studies in preschool and school‐aged children consistently showed that larger portions are associated with a higher daily energy intake [31, 32, 33, 34]. Our findings support a similar association at younger ages, indicating that portion size is positively correlated with energy intake even during infancy and the second year of life.

Recently, Sani et al. published a systematic review and meta‐analysis showing that offering larger portions increases energy intake in children aged 5–17 years, although evidence on long‐term obesity outcomes is limited and unclear [35]. Other longitudinal studies indicate that even small daily differences in energy intake (46–77 kcal/day) can contribute to weight gain, highlighting the relevance of early consumption habits [36, 37]. Nevertheless, most of these studies are observational and causality cannot be inferred. Interventional studies providing guidance or educational strategies on complementary feeding portion sizes are lacking and are needed to evaluate also the contribution of other predictors such as eating frequency, parenting styles or energy intake [38].

Our work extends the analysis to infants and toddlers, providing evidence that portion size during complementary feeding may influence obesity risk years later. These findings support the metabolic programming hypothesis during the first 1000 days of life, which posits that early exposure to specific dietary factors can influence the risk of later obesity risk [39, 40].

Our findings may be particularly relevant because complementary feeding might serve as a foundational window for children to develop effective self‐regulation of food intake through internal hunger and satiety cues. Although infants in the first months of life may naturally regulate intake, exposure to appropriate portion sizes during this early phase may help consolidate healthy eating habits. Evidence indicates that from 4 years of age, children become increasingly sensitive to larger portion exposures, largely due to social and environmental influences [41]. Therefore, promoting appropriate portion size experience during complementary feeding may support the development of skills that protect against overeating later in childhood [42, 43].

Regarding health effects, McConahy et al. reported in their cross‐sectional study that portion size among children aged 1–2 years was positively associated with body weight [17]. This was the only published study to date that included portion size data during complementary feeding and explored its association with overweight [17]. However, no longitudinal studies have assessed whether early portion size predicts later overweight risk. Our results, although based on different analytical approaches, align with these findings.

However, the observed associations between portion size and subsequent overweight risk should be interpreted with caution. Although models were adjusted for birth weight, later weight variables were not included to avoid overadjustment. Early growth patterns may influence both dietary intake and later weight status [44]. Consequently, portion size may partly reflect underlying growth patterns as children with faster growth or larger body size are likely to consume larger portions and have higher subsequent weight status. Although standardised z‐scores limit direct comparability with guidelines, larger portions likely reflect intake exceeding age‐appropriate norms. Furthermore, portion size may still be influenced by child appetite and parental feeding practices, such as pressure to eat or encouragement to finish meals [45, 46], which also may affect amounts consumed and weight outcomes. The lack of information on caregiver feeding behaviour and children's responsiveness to satiety cues limits the interpretation of portion size as an independent exposure and should be considered a source of residual confounding.

Sensitivity analysis findings should be interpreted in the context of overall dietary patterns, as higher intakes of nutrient‐dense core foods may reflect overall intake and underlying feeding practices, rather than detrimental effects of these foods themselves. Although these findings do not imply causality, they suggest that portion size, alongside food quality, may contribute to subsequent weight outcomes [47].

We observed an inverse correlation between meal frequency and portion size, a pattern that persists from 6 months to 8 years of age, but no effect of meal frequency on overweight risk. This aligns with results from the Feeding Infants and Toddlers Study (FITS) showing that infants and toddlers who eat more frequently tend to consume smaller portions [20]. They also did not show any independent effect of meal frequency on overweight risk, an observation also made by Syrad et al. in 21 months old toddlers [19].

Previous behavioural research suggests that repeated exposure to larger portions may recalibrate perceptions of what constitutes ‘normal’ amount to eat [30]. While our findings don't directly assess repeated exposure or behavioural mechanisms, they are consistent with the hypothesis that early portion size experience may be relevant for later eating and weight‐related outcomes. Given that portion size is a universal driver of energy intake [37], interventions that promote appropriate portion ranges could have substantial public health implications.

While in clinical practice it is often not feasible to quantify energy intake or macronutrient composition on a child's diet, assessing portion size may serve as a powerful and accessible tool for guiding families and health professionals in the prevention of childhood obesity [31]. Educational programmes should consider targeting both parents and children to promote healthy portion habits from early life, mainly based on offering food portions in the adequate range for age, without limiting infant's intake and not exerting pressure to eat, thus allowing infants self‐regulation.

Strengths and Limitations

A key strength of our study is its longitudinal design, the large sample size and the standardised measurement of anthropometry and diet across multiple European countries. However, it is important to acknowledge that dietary data were collected using 3‐day food records, a self‐reported technique that may be subject to bias, despite standardised procedures to minimise errors. Notably, the use of 3‐day dietary records provides an estimate of portion size at consumption occasions rather than cumulative or long‐term exposure to larger portion sizes.

Additionally, some socio‐demographic differences between included participants and those lost to follow‐up may indicate potential selection bias. Due to missing dietary data at 11 years, SEM models used portion size at 8 years as potential mediators. Another potential limitation is that causality cannot be inferred due to the observational design.

Although we adjusted our results for birth weight, growth trajectories and body size when portion size was assessed were not included in the models, which may have resulted in residual confounding. Moreover, we lacked information on child appetite and parental feeding practices, factors that may influence both portion size and later weight outcomes.

Conclusions

Consumed portion size during complementary feeding is associated with the risk of overweight and obesity throughout childhood and pre‐adolescence. Given the limited evidence available for infants and toddlers, further research is needed to clarify the potential mechanisms linking early portion‐size exposure to later obesity, including effects on satiety regulation, eating behaviours and parental feeding practices. Future studies should also assess whether providing portion‐size guidance during complementary feeding can influence long‐term energy intake and key health outcomes such as weight status, thereby informing clinical guidelines and public health policy.
