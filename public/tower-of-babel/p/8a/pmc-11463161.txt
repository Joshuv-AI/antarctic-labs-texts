CT-guided microcoil versus hook-wire localization of pulmonary nodule prior to video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery without fluoroscopic guidance
Authors: Xuedong Sun, Jinxin Fu, Chao Ma, Zhenfei Song, Siwei Yang, Long Jin, Feng Duan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth microcoils and hook-wires are commonly utilized for preoperative pulmonary nodule localization due to their convenience, but it remains unclear which one should be prioritized for recommendation.AimsTo compare the safety and efficacy of microcoils and hook-wires for pulmonary nodule localization.MethodsFrom January 2021 to December 2021, 310 consecutive patients (113 males and 197 females) with 341 pulmonary nodules who underwent CT-guided microcoil or hook-wire localization prior to video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) at our center were retrospectively included in this study. There were 161 patients in the microcoil group and 149 patients in the hook-wire group. The successful localization rate, complication rate, radiation exposure, and medical costs were compared between the two groups.ResultsA total of 341 pulmonary nodules were localized, with a success rate of 99% (180/184) in the microcoil group and 93% (146/157) in the hook-wire group, respectively. All patients successfully underwent VATS. Multivariate analysis revealed that hook-wire localization, shorter needle depth into the lung tissue and the longer waiting time from localization to VATS were the risk factors for the localization failure. The incidences of pneumothorax in the microcoil group and hook-wire group were 34.8% (56/161) and 34.9% (52/149), respectively (P = 0.983). The incidences of pneumorrhagia were 13% (24/184) and 46.5% (73/157), respectively (P = 0.000). Multivariate analysis revealed that hook-wire localization and greater depth of needle penetration into lung tissue were risk factors for pneumorrhagia.ConclusionMicrocoil localization of pulmonary nodules is superior to hook-wire localization in terms of efficacy and safety. This finding provides insight into priority and broader promotion of microcoil localization.

Introduction

The incidence and mortality rates of lung cancer are the highest of all malignant tumors [1]. The 5-year survival rate of patients with early-stage lung cancer after radical resection is over 90%, whereas the 5-year survival rate of patients with advanced lung cancer is less than 5% [2]. Therefore, early screening and diagnosis of lung cancer are highly important.

Currently, the widespread implementation of low-dose spiral chest computed tomography (CT) has increased the detection rate of small pulmonary nodules (SPNs) rising [3]. The high risk SPNs on radiologic imaging required further intervention. Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) remains the primary approach for patients with SPNs as a minimally invasive method, with some advantages sucn as fewer complications, lower cost, and shorter hospitalization period [4, 5]. However, nearly half of SPNs cases cannot be well identified in VATS procedures, especially those distant from the pleural surface [5]. Consequently, an expansion of the resection scope and, in some cases, conversion to thoracotomy becomes necessary [6].

To this end, several techniques have been developed to localize pulmonary lesions preoperatively, improving the accuracy and safety of VATS [7–12]. Among these techniques, localization with metallic materials, including hook-wire and microcoil, has been commonly utilized in clinical practice due to their convenience [13–20]. However, mixed results have been reported in comparisons between hook-wire and microcoil localization [15, 18, 20–23]. Although the incidence of complications with microcoil use was reported to be lower than that with hook-wire use, most studies found no statistically significant difference in the localization success rate between these two methods. Moreover, the small sample size and limited outcome events hindered statistical efficiency [15]. Therefore, further studies are needed to identify the optimal preoperative localization method for SPNs.

In this study, we aimed to compare preoperative localization techniques using microcoils and hook-wires in terms of efficacy and safety for the treatment of SPNs.

Patients and methods

Patients

The study procedures adhered to the ethical guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, and this retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board (Approved Number: 2022-P2-290-01). All patients signed informed consent for CT-guided percutaneous localization before the operation. Thoracic surgeons determined the necessity of surgery, while interventional radiologists evaluated the feasibility of pulmonary nodule localization before VATS.

The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (I) age 18–80 years; (II) SPNs with a maximum diameter of ≤ 20 mm, suitable for wedge resection; (III) SPNs with malignant signs indicated by CT imaging; (IV) an applicable puncture path (without any bone or apparent blood vessel tissues obstructing). The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) SPNs>2 cm, unsuitable for wedge resection; (II) Coagulation disorders or cardiopulmonary dysfunction; (III) severe pneumothorax, pleural effusion, and pleural adhesions; and (IV) incomplete medical records.

The flowchart of patient selection is shown in Fig. 1. From January 2021 to December 2021, 310 consecutive patients (113 males and197 females) with 341 pulmonary nodules were retrospectively collected in this study. Two nodules were located in 28 patients, and four nodules were located in 1 patient. All patients underwent CT-guided microcoil or hook-wire localization prior to VATS at our center. From January 2021 to June 2021 only the hook-wire was used, while from July 2021 to December 2021 only the Microcoil was employed.

The localization procedures were performed by two interventional radiologists (F.D. and X.D.S., with 15 and 9 years of interventional radiology experience, respectively) on the same day as the VATS resections, which were conducted by two thoracic surgeons. A total of 161 patients who underwent CT-guided microcoil localization were classified into the microcoil group, and 149 patients who underwent CT-guided hook-wire localization were classified into the hook-wire group.

Data on pulmonary nodule duration, smoking history, nodule characteristics (including nodule location, type, size, and the shortest vertical distance between the nodule and pleura), as well as localization procedure and VATS-related data, were collected from the electronic medical records system. The nodules were classified into three types based on the CT scan: solid, sub-solid, or ground-glass opacity (GGO) [3, 24].

Localization techniques

The patient was trained to breathe consistently to ensure a uniform inspiratory amplitude before each breath-hold CT scan. First, an appropriate body position was selected based on the preoperative chest CT scan, which indicated the lesion’s location. A locator was placed on the body surface, and the first CT scan was performed. The optimal lung puncture point was identified by locating the vertical projection of the lesion on the lung surface. The best puncture point, angle, and depth (avoiding bone and vascular tissue) were determined and marked on the body surface. The puncture sites were disinfected, sterile drapes were applied, and local anesthesia was administered using lidocaine.

For microcoil localization (Fig. 2), a 21G × 15 cm Chiba needle was used to puncture the patient within 1 cm of the pulmonary nodule. A second CT scan was performed to measure the intrapulmonary depth (length A) of the needle. A microcoil (0.018” × 14 cm, Nester, Cook Medical) was then loaded into the needle stylet and advanced with the pushrod. A minor crease was made 15 cm from the pushrod’s end. The microcoil was pushed into the lung parenchyma during a breath-hold, stopping at (A + 2) cm from the crease. The needle and pushrod were then withdrawn simultaneously. Approximately 2 cm of the microcoil’s tail was left on the pleural surface or in the chest wall as a marker for VATS. A third CT scan confirmed the microcoil’s position and checked for complications (pneumothorax and hemorrhage). If the microcoil was misplaced, an additional coil was placed with the end on the pleural surface or in the chest wall.

For hook-wire localization (Fig. 3), a 20G puncture trocar (LW 0107, DuaLok localization wire, Bard Medical) loaded with a hook-wire was used to puncture the lung parenchyma near the pulmonary nodule under CT guidance. The hook-wire was released, and the trocar was withdrawn.

Definition

The definition of localization success was the successful placement of the microcoil or hook-wire next to the lesion, with the tail exposed on the pleural surface, allowing the lesion to be recognized during VATS. Localization failure was defined as a situation where the microcoil or hook-wire was removed from the lung tissue or the tail was not exposed on the pleura surface, making identification of the lesion impossible during VATS. Complications were classified as major if they resulted in prolonged hospitalization, hospital readmission, required surgery, or resulted in death, based on the Society of Interventional Radiology criteria. Otherwise, they were considered minor, such as a small amount of pneumothorax and intrapulmonary hemorrhage in this study [25] .

Study objective

The primary outcome of the study was the successful localization and resection of the pulmonary nodules. The secondary outcomes included complications, medical costs, and total radiation dose during the localization procedure.

Statistical methods

Continuous variables were compared using the Student’s t test. The categorical parameters were compared using the chi-square test. Whenever the distribution was not nominal, the Kruskal‒Wallis test was used. The occurrence of the locating device detachment and complication were set as major outcomes in this study, respectively. A stepwise binary logistic regression model was used to determine the risk factor for outcomes. P values less than 0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. SPSS (version 20, IBM) was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Patient and nodule characteristics

The lobar distribution of the nodules was similar. The distance of the nodule from the lung surface did not significantly differ, with the mean depth of nodules in the microcoil group measuring 9 mm, compared to 8 mm in the hook-wire group. Most of the nodules in this study were GGOs, accounting for 60% (205/341) of the nodules. The baseline characteristics of the patients and nodules are summarized in Table 1. There were no significant difference in sex, age, smoking history, SPNs duration, etc., between the two groups. The common histopathological types of the nodules were invasive adenocarcinoma (53%, 181/341) and minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (23%, 79/341) (Table 2). Malignant nodules accounted for 77% (262/341), while benign nodules accounted for 23% (79/341). Moreover, there was no significant difference in the pathological results between the two groups.

Localization success rates

The localization success rates were 99% (180/184) and 93% (146/157) in the microcoil and hook-wire groups, respectively (P = 0.028). In one patient from the microcoil group, the length of the microcoil exposed on the pleura surface was too short, leading to retraction of the microcoil into the lung tissue while awaiting surgery. As a result, the microcoil could not be identified on the pleura during VATS. In eleven patients from the hook-wire group, the hook-wire was found to have dislodged from the lung tissue during VATS. However, all patients successfully underwent VATS. For those who experienced localization failure, the operator located the nodule by identifying the area of hemorrhage caused by pleural puncture and processed with VATS pulmonary segmentectomy. Histopathological examination of all specimens confirmed that the tumor cells were negative at the resection margin.

The time between localization and VATS in the microcoil group was significantly longer than in the hook-wire group (5.2 ± 3.3 h vs. 3.6 ± 1.8 h, P = 0.000). The intrapulmonary depths of puncture needle were 1.7 ± 0.6 cm and 2.6 ± 0.9 cm in the microcoil group and hook-wire group, respectively (P = 0.000). The localization procedure time were similar between the two groups (16 ± 5.7 min vs. 15 ± 7.1 min, P = 0.163).

Multivariate analysis suggested that hook-wire localization (P = 0.000, OR = 66.672, 95% CI 6.871-646.953), shorter depth of needle penetration into lung tissue (P = 0.010, OR = 0.741, 95% CI 0.590–0.930), and longer duration from localization to VATS (P = 0.004, OR = 1.612, 95% CI 1.166–2.230) were risk factors for detachment of the localization device from the lung.

Complications

No major complications occurred in either group. Pneumothorax and pneumorrhagia were common minor complications in both groups, and both could resolve on their own without additional treatment. The incidences of pneumothorax in the Microcoil and Hook-wire groups were 34.8% (56/161) and 34.9% (52/149), respectively. The incidences of pneumorrhagia (based on localized pulmonary nodules) in the Microcoil and Hook-wire groups were 13% (24/184) and 46.5% (73/157), respectively. The risk of pneumorrhagia in the Microcoil group was significantly lower than that in the Hook-wire group (P = 0.000), but there was no significant difference in the incidence of pneumothorax between the two groups (P = 0.983). Multivariate analysis suggested that greater needle penetration depth into lung tissue (P = 0.000, OR = 1.072, 95% CI 1.037–1.109) and hook-wire localization (P = 0.000, OR = 3.211, 95% CI 1.764–5.846) were risk factors for bleeding, while no risk factors for pneumothorax were identified in this study.

Volume of the lung wedge resection

The comparison of lung wedge resection volumes between the two groups was conducted only for patients with a single pulmonary nodule. The lung wedge resection volumes for 141 patients who underwent hook-wire localization and 140 patients who underwent microcoil localization were 62.5 ± 41.8 cm3 and 55.2 ± 38.6 cm3, respectively (P = 0.131).

Dose length product (DLP)

A comparison of DLP between the two groups was conducted only for patients with a single pulmonary nodule. A total of 141 patients who underwent hook-wire localization and 140 patients who underwent microcoil localization had DLP doses of 521.9 ± 181.5 mGy * cm and 376.8 ± 172.4 mGy * cm, respectively (P = 0.000).

Costs of the localization

A comparison of the localization costs between the two groups was conducted only for patients with a single pulmonary nodule. The localization costs of 141 patients who underwent hook-wire localization and 140 patients who underwent microcoil localization were 1087.6 ± 111.9 yuan and 1593.0 ± 118.1 yuan, respectively (P = 0.000).

Discussion

Localization of small pulmonary nodules before VATS resection has become a common technique to enhance the efficiency of lung nodule resection and minimize damage to normal lung tissue. An increasing number of localization techniques have been adopted in clinical practice, but the most commonly used are microcoil and hook-wire localization [23].

In this study, we compared the localization success rate, incidence of complications, radiation dose and medical costs between microcoil and hook-wire. Compared to hook-wire, we found that microcoil had a higher success rate in localization, a lower incidence of pneumorrhagia, and reduced radiation exposure. However, hook-wire localization was associated with lower costs.

Our microcoil localization success rate was 99%. Only one patient experienced the tail end of the microcoil retracting into the lung tissue, and no patient had microcoil detachment. The time from localization to VATS in the microcoil group was significantly longer than that of the hook-wire group (5.2 ± 3.3 h vs. 3.6 ± 1.8 h, P = 0.000). This suggests that the microcoil allows for longer waiting periods between localization and surgery without the risk of dislodgment, providing convenience for thoracic surgeons in scheduling VATS. Consistent with Hu’s large-sample research findings, the technical success rate of the microcoil localization procedure was 98.4% (1310/1331 nodules) [8]. However, in our study, the detachment rate of the hook-wire was 7%. Our data were similar to those reported in Hwang’s [20] and Song’s studies [7], which showed hook-wire detachment rates of 9% and 8.7%, respectively, with no case of microcoil detachment [20]. Rostambeigi et al. reported a hook-wire detachment rate as high as 46%, while the microcoil detachment rate was only 5% [15]. Given the superior localization success rate of the microcoil compared to the hook-wire, it is recommended to use the microcoil for preoperative localization.

The microcoil localization technique, initially described by Mayo et al. [26], was modified in our study. We did not observe microcoil detachment, likely due to our simplified localization procedure. Approximately 2 cm of the tail end of the microcoil was left in the chest wall, eliminating the need for an additional CT scan to confirm that the needle tip and the microcoil were located in the pleural cavity, as described in a previous study [18, 26]. This modification reduced the risk of needle displacement caused by patient respiratory movements. Occasionally, respiratory movements and inaccurate control of the needle’s withdrawal distance can push the microcoil completely into the lung tissue, impacting the positioning of pulmonary nodules during VATS. However, as most of the microcoil is pushed into the lung tissue, forming loops with strong anchoring force, while the portion in the chest wall remains linear, leaving the tail end of the microcoil in the chest wall does not result in detachment from the lung tissue or increase patient discomfort. Moreover, with the relative movement between the lung tissue and the chest wall during breathing, or the contraction of lung tissue during VATS, the tail end of the microcoil in the chest wall easily detaches and adheres to the surface of the pleura, facilitating detection during thoracoscopy [27].

Our modified simplified microcoil localization procedure also reduced the number of CT scans, thereby lowering the radiation dose, which was less than that reported in previous studies [15] (DLP: 377 mGy*cm vs. 497 mGy*cm). This reduces the risk of radiation damage to patients and effectively enhances the safety of the procedure. Although the costs of the microcoil localization was higher than that of hook-wire localization, the actual cost was influenced by local medical insurance policies, and our cost analysis is provided for reference only.

In this study, multivariate analysis suggested that hook-wire localization, shorter depth of needle penetration into lung tissue, and longer duration from localization to VATS were risk factors for device detachment from the lung. This is easy to understand: if the penetration depth of the hook-wire into the lung tissue is too shallow and the time between surgery and localization is too long, the risk of detachment increase as the patient’s respiratory movement continue. Although there was no significant difference in nodule depth between the microcoil group (9 mm) and the hook-wire group (8 mm), the hook-wire needed to penetrate deeper into the lungs than the microcoil group (2.6 ± 0.9 cm vs. 1.7 ± 0.6 cm) to maintain stability.

Xu et al.‘s study showed that out of 279 nodule microcoil localizations, 28 (10%) failed, with 4 patients not having the microcoil exposed from the lung surface and 24 patients experiencing detachment from the lung tissue [13]. The depth of penetration into the lung tissue associated with of microcoil positioning failure was 19.1 ± 14.9 mm (1.0–44.8 mm) (the length of the microcoil used in their study was 7 cm), and a microcoil intrapulmonary depth ≤ 20 mm was identified as an independent risk factor for localization failure. However, in our study, the microcoil intrapulmonary depth was 1.7 ± 0.6 cm, and no detachment occurred. This may be related to the longer microcoils (length 14 cm) we used, which were more often anchored in the lung and left only approximately 2 cm outside the lung tissue. Sui et al. [28] reported a microcoil detachment rate was 2.9%, and multivariate analysis revealed that a microcoil intrapulmonary depth ≤ 10 mm and the presence of pneumothorax were the main factors affecting microcoil positioning. In another study, the median intrapulmonary puncture depth of the microcoil was 1.9 cm, with a localization failure rate of only 1.6% [8]. These research findings suggest that for microcoil localization, a needle intrapulmonary puncture depth of 1–2 cm can achieve a higher localization success rate.

In our study, the main complications were pneumothorax and hemorrhage, consistent with previous research [8]. The incidence rates of pneumothorax were 34.8% (56/161) in the microcoil group and 34.9% (52/149) in the hook-wire group (P = 0.983). Although the incidence of pneumothorax was high in both groups, it involved a small amount of air that could be absorbed naturally without requiring special treatment. In a recent study, researchers performed microcoil localization on 1331 lung nodules, resulting in pneumothorax in 302 cases (22.7%), which was lower than our findings [8]. Chu et al. reported a lower pneumothorax incidence rate of 9.6% in hook-wire localization [10]. We believe this difference may be related to the use of modified hook-wire in their study. Hu et al. showed that hook-wires were more likely to cause pneumothorax than other types of localization devices [18]. They also found that lesions located in the lower lobes were more prone to pneumothorax during localization. Considering the large amplitude of respiratory movement in the lower lobes of the lungs, the puncture needle is more likely to cut and damage the lung tissue. The lower lobes experience greater respiratory motion, causing more torsional stress around the pulmonary parenchyma, which may explain why nodules in the lower lobes are associated with an increased incidence of pneumothorax.

The incidence rates of pneumorrhagia were 14.9% (24/161) in the microcoil group and 49.7% (73/149) in hook-wire group (P = 0.000). The microcoils used for localization were soft and flexible, causing minimal damage to the lung parenchyma. These devices did not cause any additional impairment to physiological respiratory movements. Furthermore, animal experiments [29] have demonstrated that microcoils coated with hemostatic synthetic nylon fibers can induce peripheral pulmonary parenchyma coagulation and fill puncture channels, thereby reducing the incidence and severity of pulmonary hemorrhage. Multivariate analysis suggested that deeper needle penetration into lung tissue and hook-wire localization were independent risk factors for pneumorrhagia. The penetration depths into lung tissue for the microcoil and hook-wire were 1.7 ± 0.6 cm and 2.6 ± 0.9 cm, respectively (p = 0.000). Hook-wires require deeper penetration to stabilize in the lung, significantly increasing the risk of vascular injury.

This study has several limitations. Although the sample size of this study was not small, it cannot be denied that it was a retrospective study. The time interval between the localization procedure and VATS was not controlled. The operator’s experience increased with the accumulation of cases.

In the future, hook-wire localization technology will require further improvement to minimize the impact of respiratory movement, making it more suitable for the preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules. Additionally, new convenient localization devices should be explored. Large-sample, prospective, randomized controlled trials are needed to determine the most effective and safe localization methods for pulmonary nodules prior to VATS.

In conclusion, both microcoil and hook-wire could be used for preoperative localization of SPNs. However, microcoil localization has been found to be associated with a higher localization success rate and lower complication rate, making it worthy of clinical promotion.
