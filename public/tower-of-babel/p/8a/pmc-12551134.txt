Green synthesis, characterization and antibacterial potential of zinc oxide nanoparticles with naringenin
Authors: Leirika Ngangom, Kunal Sharma, Neha Pandey, Shabaaz Begum JP, Divya Venugopal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn comparison to physical and chemical fabrication of metal nanoparticles, green synthesized nanoparticles are non-toxic, biocompatible, and biodegradable; they undergo a single-step bioreduction process. This research was primarily driven by the goal of creating an environment friendly, efficient, and sustainable process for synthesizing ZnO NPs using naringenin, a naturally occurring flavonoid with well-established medicinal benefits. The growing need to replace traditional chemical synthesis methods which frequently involve energy-intensive processes and toxic reducing agents with environment friendly, biocompatible, and scalable alternatives served as the impetus for the study. The synthesized ZnO NPs were characterized using different analytical techniques such as UV-Vis, FTIR, XRD, FESEM-EDS and HRTEM. The UV-Vis spectra of the synthesized zinc oxide nanoparticles showed a strong absorption peak at 374 nm. The XRD pattern exhibits prominent and precise diffraction, which indicate good crystallinity. Both FESEM and HRTEM confirmed that the synthesized ZnO NPs were compactly organized and polycrystalline-looking particles with an average size 25 to 31 nm. EDS analysis provided further evidence of the product’s purity. Disc diffusion method was used to examined the antibacterial potential of the synthesized ZnO NPs against pathogenic bacterial strains namely S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and E. faecalis. The zones of inhibition of 11 ± 0.1 mm, 10 ± 0.1 mm, 9 ± 0.1 mm and 9 ± 0.1 mm was observed in E. faecalis, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and S. aureus respectively.Graphical abstract Keywords: Trifolium repens, Naringenin, Zinc oxide nanoparticles, Characterization, Antibacterial potential

Introduction

Nanotechnology is one of the fastest-growing scientific disciplines, offering transformative potential across biological, environmental, and industrial domains. It is poised to drive the next industrial revolution. Among various nanomaterials, metal oxide nanoparticles stand out for their unique chemical, physical, and biological properties, including high surface area, solubility, structural stability, and strong interfacial interactions. However, conventional synthesis methods often rely on toxic reducing and capping agents, which pose environmental and biological risks. In this context, plant-based extracts and purified phytochemicals provide an eco-friendly, cost-effective, and a sustainable alternative for nanoparticle synthesis. This green synthesis approach minimizes hazardous byproducts and enhances the biocompatibility of the resulting nanomaterials, aligning with principles of green chemistry and safe nanotechnology. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO NPs) have gained significant attention due to their broad spectrum of applications, including in food, cosmetics, and biomedical fields. Notably, Zinc oxide (ZnO) is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), supporting its use in consumer and biomedical products [1–3]. This designation reflects its low toxicity at regulated concentrations and reinforces its suitability for use in antimicrobial formulations, drug delivery systems, and food packaging. The nanoscale form of ZnO further enhances its surface reactivity and biological functionality, making it a promising candidate for green nanotechnology applications. Given the GRAS status of ZnO and its widespread application, there is growing interest in advancing safer, more reproducible synthesis methods. In this context, the use of pure bioactive compounds including flavonoids, phenolics, and alkaloids offers a precise and consistent alternative to crude plant extracts in the green synthesis of metal nanoparticles. These bioactive compounds function as bioreducing and capping agents, facilitating the formation of stable nanoparticles while minimizing variability and impurities [4].

Recent research has emphasized the potential of ZnO NPs as effective agents in biomedical and dental applications due to their unique physiochemical and biological properties. In a related investigation, both chemically and biologically synthesized ZnO NPs were explored for potential use in dental applications. The biologically derived nanoparticles, synthesized using Murraya paniculata leaf extract, exhibited strong antibacterial activity against common dental pathogens such as S. mutans and E. coli. Depending on the synthesis method, the particles displayed diverse morphologies ranging from nanorods to spherical and cubical forms. These findings highlight the potential of ZnO NPs as eco-friendly and biocompatible alternatives in dental care, particularly for use as durable tooth filling materials [5]. Another study reported similar findings, demonstrating the successful green synthesis of ZnO NPs using Calendula officinalis petal extract, resulting in spherical particles sized 16–27 nm with notable antioxidant activity. Their study highlighted the suitability of these biogenic nanoparticles in mitigating oxidative stress, suggesting their promising role in therapeutic and pharmaceutical formulations [6]. One of the most potential inorganic oxides, ZnO has recently drawn the interest of numerous researchers for the process of biosynthesis of nanoparticles because of its distinctive characteristics and numerous uses, including photocatalytic degradation, and drug delivery. Green synthesis of ZnO NPs using bioactive compounds offers a sustainable and eco-friendly approach that integrates natural resources for nanoparticle fabrication. By improving the nanoparticles’ biocompatibility and reducing their environmental impact, this technique makes them appropriate for a range of applications, especially in agriculture and healthcare. ZnO NPs have been synthesized from a variety of plant extracts, including Viscum album [7], and Mangifera indica [8]. The fabrication of plant-mediated copper oxide nanoparticles (pm-CuO NPs) from Annona squamosa aqueous seed extract for efficient antibacterial activity and subsequent use of this as a platform for electrocatalytic measurement of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) for applications in agriculture. The study also noted that the bacterium that causes rice bacterial blight, Xanthomonas oryzae, was significantly inhibited by pm-CuO NPs [9].

White clover, or Trifolium repens (T. repens), is a multipurpose herbaceous perennial with important uses in ecology, agriculture, and traditional medicine. Because of its flexibility, it can flourish in a variety of settings, such as turfgrass combinations and pasture legumes [10]. Its therapeutic qualities are highlighted in the current research, demonstrating its beneficial significance. On account of its bioactive compounds, T. repens is utilized for the treatment of a number of illnesses, such as inflammation and skin conditions. Antibacterial, antifungal and anti-inflammatory properties are displayed by compounds such as quercetin and kaempferol, suggesting their potential for use in medications development [11, 12]. Naringenin is bioactive member of the flavonoid class of polyphenolic compounds. The thoroughly studied bioactive substance naringenin is extensively found in helpful medicinal plants including T. pratense [13], T. repens (quantified in the present study by HPLC), tomatoes and other fruits, as well as citrus fruits like grapefruits and oranges. Free radicals can be countered by naringenin since it possesses antioxidant properties. Unstable chemicals called free radicals can harm cells and induce oxidative stress, which can lead to a number of chronic illnesses [14]. According to reports, naringenin is an effective anti-inflammatory medication that can be used in pain relief therapy and efficiently relieves pain [15]. The possibility of naringenin as an inhibitor of the COVID-19 virus, RNA Polymerase SARS-CoV-2, has been investigated. When compared to other antiviral medications, it had more hydrogen bonds and produced encouraging results in molecular dynamics simulations [16]. Among the bioactive compounds that have been identified for our latest computational investigation, naringenin and trifolin demonstrated the highest binding affinities with the targeted proteins of the pathogenic bacterial strains (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Enterococcus faecalis, and Acinetobacter baumannii) [17].

Previous studies strongly support the concept that surface modification through compositing with different metals or metal oxides and coating with functional groups significantly enhances both biocompatibility and antibacterial targeting capabilities of nanoparticles. For instance, the green synthesis of Ag (silver) and ZnO NPs using Rauvolfia tetraphylla seed extract resulted in composites with improved antibacterial performance compared to individual components, due to synergistic effects and optimized surface charge interactions [18]. Similarly, functionalized with phytochemicals from Elaecarpus ganitrus or blushwood berry extracts introduced active surface groups (such as alkaloids, flavonoids, polyphenols), which acted as natural capping agents, enhancing biocompatibility and selective membrane disruption [19–21]. These surface-bound biomolecules not only stabilize the nanoparticles but also facilitate targeted interactions with microbial cells, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and improving antimicrobial potency. Thus, deliberate surface engineering via biogenic routes enables safer and more effective antimicrobial nanomaterials.

Based on these insights, the present study explores a refined biogenic strategy by employing pure naringenin, a naturally occurring flavonoid for the green synthesis of ZnO NPs as a reducing and stabilizing agent. Unlike previous studies that relied on crude plant extracts, this approach provides a more scalable and reproducible method. The resulting ZnO NPs exhibited notable antibacterial activity and were thoroughly characterized, highlighting their promise as eco-friendly antimicrobial agents. This research presents a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical synthesis by uniquely integrating the principles of nanotechnology and phytochemistry.

Methodology

Materials and chemicals

Standard antibiotics (kanamycin sulphate, amikacin sulphate, and ciprofloxacin hydrochloride monohydrate), metal salt (zinc nitrate hexahydrate), bioactive compound (naringenin), and media (Muller-Hinton agar) were all purchased from HiMedia.

Bacterial strains and growth condition

The bacterial strains used for the study were Staphylococcus aureus (MTCC 737), Pseudomonas aeruginosa (MTCC 2582), and clinical strains (Enterococcus faecalis and Klebsiella pneumoniae). Bacteria were cultivated in Muller Hinton Broth (MHB) and kept at 37 ºC overnight. The bacterial culture of 0.5 McFarland standard was used for antimicrobial susceptibility study with a working cell suspension of 1.5 × 105 CFU/ml.

Collection and preparatory method of plant sample

The leaves of T. repens were collected from Mussoorie, Uttarakhand, India. The plant samples were shade dried for 4–7 days and were coarsely ground using a grinder. The extraction of the plant samples was done using the maceration technique. 10 g of the samples were weighed and mixed with 50 ml distilled water and macerated on a platform shaker for 3 days. Subsequently, samples were then kept in a water bath and heated to 60 °C to allow the solvents to evaporate. The samples were filtered with Whatman No. 1 filter paper and syringe filter respectively. The filtrate was then collected and kept at 4 ºC for further use.

HPLC

The filtered extract was subjected for HPLC (High-performance liquid chromatography) analysis to identify the natural bioactive compounds present in the leaves extract. The high-performance liquid chromatography was performed using Shimadzu, LC 2030 Plus equipped with Prominence Diode Array Detector (PDA). The separation was carried out using a reverse-phase C18 column (Shimadzu- pack solar, 5 µM, 4.6 × 250 mm). The mobile phase for phenolic acid used was gradient mode with methanol (A) and 0.1% ortho-phosphoric acid (B) at 40: 60 with a flow rate 1 ml /min and injection volume 20 ml for total run time of 16 min and mobile phase for flavonoids used was isoelectric mode methanol: acetonitrile: water (40:15:45) with 1% acetic acid with a flow rate 1 ml /min and injection volume 20 ml for total run time of 12 min. The standard bioactive compounds used were gallic acid, catechin, cholorogenic acid, vallinic acid, p-coumaric, ferulic, m-coumaric, naringenin, kaempferol-3-o-galactoside and quercetin [22].

Biosynthesis of ZnO NPs

The green synthesis of ZnO NPs was carried out using naringenin served as both stabilizing and reducing agent. With constant stirring, a 0.01 M aqueous solution of zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.297 g in 100 mL deionized water) was made. Separately, a clear solution was obtained by dissolving 0.136 g of pure naringenin (0.5 mmol) in 10 mL of 70% ethanol while it was mildly heated. After that, the zinc nitrate solution was heated to 60–80 °C for one to two hours while the naringenin solution was added dropwise while being constantly stirred. The formation of ZnO NPs was indicated by the appearance of a white turbidity. 0.1 M NaOH was used when needed to keep the reaction’s pH close to neutral (pH 7). The mixture was then subjected for reduction in a preheated muffle furnace at a temperature of 400 ºC for 20 min. The resulting ZnO NPs appeared as a fine yellowish powder and were kept in airtight containers for additional characterization [23–25].

Characterization

For confirmation of the size, shape, and crystalline structure of the synthesized nanopowder, analytical techniques such as UV-Visible Absorption Spectroscopy (UV-ABS, Shimadzu), X-ray − 6000 diffractometer (XRD, Shimadzu Japan), Fourier Transform Infra-Red spectroscopy (FTIR, Perkin Elmer), Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FESEM, VEGA TESCAN-JEOL/EO JSM-5600) and High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HRTEM, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) have been employed. Band gap analysis was performed using UV-Vis Spectroscopy with light wavelengths between 300 and 800 nm [26], analysis of the crystalline material is carried out using XRD with Cu Kα1 of wavelength 0.154 nm [27], to assess the vibrational stretching and bending modes of the chemical components and other residues on the outside of ZnO NPs, FTIR was used [28]. To determine the elemental composition of the ZnO NPs, the synthesized nanoparticles were morphologically analysed using FESEM that was integrated with an energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and the size of the synthesized ZnO NPs were examined using HRTEM with SAED (selected area electron diffraction) [29].

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial efficacy of the synthesized ZnO NPs was assessed against both gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria, specifically E. faecalis, P. aeruginosa MTCC 2582, S. aureus MTCC 737, and K. pneumoniae. The response range of antibacterial activity was identified using broth microdilution method. The effective concentration was then analysed using the disc diffusion method. The test strains of bacteria were cultured in MHB and incubated for 24 h at 37 ºC in a shaking incubator. For the experiment, the microbial seeding density of 1.5 × 105 CFU/ml was used and as per CLSI guidelines (NCCLS, 2002). The effective concentrations of ZnO NPs (80 µg/ml, 60 µg/ml, 40 µg/ml, and 20 µg/ml) obtained from broth microdilution were further used for the disc diffusion method [30]. The standard antibiotics amikacin, ciprofloxacin, and kanamycin were taken as the positive controls whereas MHA as the negative control.

Statistical analysis

For the statistical analysis, OriginPro 8.5 (OriginLab software, Northampton, Massachusetts, USA) was employed. The results of the antibacterial potential are represented by the mean ± standard deviation. A p-value of less than 0.05 was deemed significant.

Results and discussion

HPLC analysis

One of the techniques that is most commonly used to assess the bioactive compounds found in plants is the HPLC analysis. Using HPLC coupled with diode array detection, the specific bioactive compounds of T. repens were identified and quantified by comparing the retention times of the observed peaks with those of the pure compounds. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the plant extract’s HPLC chromatograms were recorded at 210, 280, and 330 nm wavelengths and were identified by comparison with standards. The bioactive compounds that been identified include three flavonoids (naringenin, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside, and quercetin) and seven polyphenols (gallic acid, catechin, chlorogenic acid, vallinic acid, p-coumeric, ferulic, and m-coumeric). Table 1 displays the concentration of the bioactive compounds found in the analysed sample. In the aqueous extract of T. repens, kaempferol-3-O-galactoside (145.05 ± 0.10 mg/L) was the dominant constituent followed by gallic acid (25.48 ± 0.41 mg/L) and naringenin (9.13 ± 0.54 mg/L). The quantified bioactive compounds have therapeutic uses such as kaempferol-3-O-galactoside has a significant antimicrobial, anticancer, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties [31–33]. Similarly, naringenin shows antioxidant, antimicrobial, anticancer, antidiabetic, and anti-inflammatory properties [14, 15, 34, 35].

UV-Visible spectroscopy analysis

As illustrated in Fig. 2, the green-synthesised ZnO NPs using naringenin showed a clear and sharp absorption peak at about 374 nm in the UV-visible spectroscopy analysis. This is typical of the intrinsic band-gap absorption of ZnO NPs. This absorption, which results from the electronic transition from the valence band to the conduction band, validates that ZnO was successfully synthesized at the nanoscale. The high purity of the produced nanoparticles, devoid of unreacted zinc precursors or organic contaminants, is indicated by the absence of additional peaks in the spectrum. The peak position, which is somewhat blue-shifted in comparison to bulk ZnO, which normally absorbs at 385 nm, further indicates that the particles are in the nano-size range. The quantum confinement effect could be the cause of this blue shift, which would support the nanoparticles’ uniformly small size. Additionally, naringenin’s stabilizing function, which probably capped the nanoparticle surfaces to inhibit aggregation and enhance optical clarity, is reflected in the peak’s sharpness. Overall, the UV-Vis result validates the production of stable, monodisperse ZnO nanoparticles, with naringenin functioning as an efficient capping and reducing agent in environment friendly synthesis conditions.

XRD analysis

The XRD pattern of the biosynthesis ZnO NPs is shown in Fig. 3, exhibiting characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 31.76º, 34.41º, 36.24º, 47.52º, 56.60º, 62.86º, 66.38º, 67.96º, and 69.09º, which correspond to the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201) crystal planes of the hexagonal wurtzite phase of ZnO, as per JCPDS card no. 00-036-1451. The Y-axis labelled “Intensity (cps)” refers to counts per second, indicating the rate at which diffracted X-rays were detected during the scan. Prior to plotting, background correction and baseline subtraction were performed using the Shimadzu XRD-6000 software to remove unwanted signals, primarily noise and background scattering.

FTIR interpretation

Figure 4 illustrates the FTIR spectral profile of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs, highlighting several characteristic absorption bands that correspond to various functional groups originating from naringenin, the capping and stabilizing agent. The functional groups corresponding to there absorption peaks are detailed in Table 3. A prominent peak at ~ 510.83 cm-1 corresponds to the Zn-O stretching vibration, confirming the formation of the wurtzite ZnO lattice and consistent with previously reported ZnO structures synthesized using plant-based compounds [36]. The broad band at 2656 cm-1 is attributed to O-H stretching vibrations, which likely originate from hydroxyl groups present in the naringenin structure. These hydroxyl groups are known to participate in the bioreduction of Zn2+ ions and aid in the stabilization of the resulting nanoparticles through hydrogen bonds [37, 38]. Additionally, a medium intensity peak at 2324 cm-1, assigned to O = C = O asymmetric stretching, may arise from residual carbon dioxide adsorbed or from decarboxylation products of phenolic compounds during the heating of the plant extract, a phenomenon noted in other phytochemical-assisted nanoparticles syntheses [39]. The absorption band at 1636 cm-1 corresponds to C = O stretching or aromatic C = C bending variations, which can be attributed to the flavonoid backbone of naringenin. The presence of this band suggests that the Π-conjugated systems of naringenin interact with Zn2+ ions during nucleation and growth of the nanoparticles, supporting its dual role as capping and reducing agent [40].

FESEM-EDS analysis

FESEM was used to evaluate the surface morphology and structural features of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs. As shown in Fig. 5, the particles exhibit a predominantly spherical to slightly irregular shape, with moderate aggregation likely due to van der Waals interactions and the high surface energy of nanoscale particles, which promote clustering during post-synthesis heating [41–43]. The particle size distribution observed ranged approximately from 30 to 60 nm, consistent with the sizes estimated by HRTEM and XRD analyses.

ZnO NP analysis using FESEM

The surface morphology indicates that the particles are densely packed and polycrystalline in nature, which aligns with the strong diffraction patterns observed in XRD and the lattice fringes seen in HRTEM. Similar morphological characteristics spherical or near-spherical ZnO NPs with minor agglomeration have been reported in other green synthesis studies using phytochemicals such as Euphorbia hirta and Acalypha fructicosa, where particle sizes were reported in the range of 20–70 nm [4, 44].

In addition, the EDS spectrum (Fig. 6) confirms the presence of elemental zinc (Zn) and oxygen (O) with no detectable impurities, affirming the successful formation of high-purity ZnO NPs. The compositional purity further supports the effectiveness of naringenin as a bioreducing and stabilizing agent in the green synthesis route.

HRTEM analysis

HRTEM was employed to analyze the detailed morphology, size and crystallinity of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs. The HRTEM micrographs revealed that the nanoparticles were predominantly exhibit a spherical to slightly oval in shape, with smooth edges and uniform contrast, suggesting monodispersity as clearly shown in Fig. 7. The particles were well-dispersed with minimal agglomeration, suggesting effective stabilization by naringenin during synthesis. The particle size ranged from 25 to 31 nm, and a particle size distribution histogram generated from image analysis showed an average particle diameter of 23.86 nm, confirming the nanoscale dimension of the synthesized ZnO NPs. This narrow size range reflects a controlled synthesis process and consistent particle formation.

HRTEM micrographs and SAED pattern of ZnO NPs, with the histogram indicating an average particle size

Furthermore, the SAED pattern exhibited distinct concentric rings, characteristic of a polycrystalline structure. The presence of well-defined lattice fringes in the HRTEM images further supports the high crystallinity of the nanoparticles, consistent with the XRD results. Overall, the HRTEM findings confirm the successful synthesis of uniformly shaped, crystalline ZnO NPs at the nanoscale, highlighting the effectiveness of naringenin as a green reducing and stabilizing agent.

Antibacterial potential of synthesized ZnO NPs

The synthesized ZnO NPs demonstrated significant antibacterial activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains. This is consistent with earlier findings on metal-based nanoparticles, where their antimicrobial potential has been attributed to mechanisms such as cell membrane disruption, generation of ROS and release of metal ions that interfere with bacterial metabolic processes [45, 46]. The smaller particle size and high surface reactivity of ZnO NPs enhance their interaction with microbial membranes, improving bacterial efficacy. ZnO NPs, in particular, have gained attention due to their GRAS status, biocompatibility, and ability to release Zn2+ ions, which aid in bacterial inhibition and wound healing. Additionally, the incorporation of such nanoparticles into biodegradable matrices (like PPLA nanosheets) allows for controlled ion release at physiological pH, enhancing their applicability in biomedical environments [47]. Ag NPs have also demonstrated size-dependent antibacterial properties, with smaller particles offering stronger inhibition due to increased surface area and reactivity. Environment friendly green synthesis methods using biological extracts (such as fungi, plant phytochemicals) are now enabling sustainable, low-cost production of such nanoparticles [46].

Relevant research findings highlight that the antibacterial and photocatalytic activities of nanoparticles are strongly influenced by their physical and chemical properties. Titanium dioxide nanoparticles (TiO2 NPs) with high surface area and mixed-phased crystallinity showed efficient dye degradation and hydrogen production [48]. Ag-based nanocomposites synthesized using chamomile extract or cow urine exhibited strong photocatalytic and antibacterial activity due to their small size and bioactive surface [49, 50]. ZnO structures with controlled morphologies (nanocombs, thin films) demonstrated enhanced photocatalytic performance, emphasizing that tailoring particle size, shape, and crystallinity boosts their reactivity and effectiveness [51, 52].

The antibacterial efficacy of the biosynthesized ZnO NPs was evaluated against S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, K. pneumoniae, and E. faecalis using the disc diffusion method. At a concentration of 80 µg/mL, ZnO NPs demonstrated measurable zones of inhibition of 9 ± 0.1 mm (S. aureus), 9 ± 0.1 mm (P. aeruginosa), 10 ± 0.1 mm (K. pneumoniae), and 11 ± 0.1 mm (E. faecalis). For comparison, standard antibiotics at 10 µg/mL exhibited significantly higher inhibition zones: Ciprofloxacin showed 17 mm (S. aureus), 16 mm (P. aeruginosa), 16 mm (K. pneumoniae) and 18 mm (E. faecalis); Amikacin produced 11 mm, 12 mm, 10 mm, and 11 mm, respectively; while Kanamycin showed varied sensitivity with 12 mm (S. aureus), resistance in P. aeruginosa, 8 mm (K. pneumoniae), and 9 mm (E. faecalis). These findings, summarized in Fig. 8; Table 4, demonstrate that although ZnO NPs exhibited relatively moderate inhibition compared to synthetic antibiotics, their consistent antibacterial performance, combined with biocompatibility and eco-friendly synthesis, highlights their potential as alternative antimicrobial agents, particularly for applications where antibiotic resistance or chemical toxicity is a concern.

Given their biocompatibility and GRAS status, these nanoparticles hold strong potential for future applications in wound healing, antimicrobial coatings, controlled drug delivery systems and food packaging, offering a sustainable alternative for next-generation antimicrobial formulations.

Impact of particle size on functional properties

The nanoscale dimensions of the synthesized ZnO NPs confirmed through HRTEM (25–31 nm), FESEM (30–60 nm), and XRD (~ 52.8 nm crystallite size) play a crucial role in determining their physiochemical and biological behavior. As particle size decreases, the surface-area-to-volume ratio increases significantly, which enhances surface reactivity and interaction with biological targets. This is particularly important in antibacterial applications, as smaller nanoparticles exhibit greater surface contact with microbial membrane, leading to more effective ROS (reactive oxygen species) generation, membrane disruption, and bacterial inhibition. Moreover, the observed blue shift in UV-Vis absorption (peak at ~ 374 nm) further supports the quantum confinement effect, a size-dependent optical property seen in nanoscale ZnO. These effects collectively contribute to the high antibacterial efficiency observed in the present study, even at moderate concentrations. Thus, the precise control of nanoparticle size during green synthesis not only ensures structural integrity but also optimizes functional performance, reinforcing the importance of size as a design parameter in nanobiotechnology.

Conclusion

The present research offers a sustainable, non-toxic, and reproducible approach for synthesizing ZnO NPs with naringenin, a distinct flavonoid derived from plants. The synthesized nanoparticles are shown to be highly crystalline, nanoscale in size, and significant antibacterial activity. The elimination of toxic chemicals and use of a defined phytochemical improves both the reproducibility and biocompatibility of the synthesis process. These findings are particularly valuable to nanotechnologists, material chemists, pharmaceutical researchers, and biomedical engineers interested in developing eco-friendly nanomaterials. The use of a pure phytochemical enhances consistency, which benefits researchers aiming for reproducible synthesis protocols, and supports the pharmaceutical and cosmetic industries seeking biocompatible alternative for antimicrobial agents or tropical formulations. In addition, environmental and agricultural sectors may apply these nanoparticles in the development of non-toxic antimicrobial coatings, nanofertilizers, or plant protection agents.

By integrating principles of green chemistry, nanoscience, and bioengineering, this research contributes to the advancement of safe and scalable nanotechnology and offers a platform for sustainable innovation in both academic and industrial domains.
