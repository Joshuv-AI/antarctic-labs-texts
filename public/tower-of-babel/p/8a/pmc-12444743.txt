Refractive Index of Benchmark Polystyrene Nanoplastics by Optical Modeling of UV–Vis Spectra
Authors: Mattia Andrini, Stefania Federici, Luca Gavioli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optical recognition and identification of nanoplastics such as polystyrene nanobeads (PSbs), a widely used polymer and an actual source of environmental pollution, is a challenging task relying on knowledge of the PSbs’ refractive index (RI) and its relation to the PSbs’ morphology. This is, however, lacking for PSbs’ sizes lower than 1 μm. Here, we bridge this gap by measuring UV–vis spectra of PSbs deposited on a sapphire substrate via spin coating and by connecting the experimental data to the RI, PSbs’ morphology, and optical transitions through a new optical model based on the Mie theory. Specifically, the new model allows us to fit the total and diffuse light components considering the particles’ size distribution, the superficial density, and the substrate effects, thus correlating the PSbs’ wavelength-dependent RI features to the experimentally validated specific sample morphology. Two critical model assumptions, i.e., Mie theory and substrate effects, are also discussed employing discrete dipole approximation simulations. Moreover, we identify the optically allowed molecular electronic transitions in nanometric PSbs as potential fingerprints for nanoparticle characterization in more complex matrixes.

Introduction

The nanoplastic environmental dispersion and interaction with any ecological matrix are a potential harm on human health and ecosystems. In particular, polystyrene (PS) beads (PSbs) are extensively studied due to the PS abundance in the environment, the evidence of its effects on living organisms, and their application as optical analytical markers. − As an example, hypoxia and disruption of key metabolic pathways in aquatic animal embryos are observed for PS particles below 300 nm. PSbs also affect blood flow speed and melanin production, with potential chronic toxicity from long-term exposure. As a benchmark system in optical analytical markers, the PSbs are applied as phantoms in biomedical optics, , as carriers of biomedical agents, in calibration of flow cytometers, and in optical sensing and space optics. In this framework, a key challenge is the understanding of the light scattering by PSbs to correlate the particles’ size, shape, structure, and composition with the refractive index (RI). In particular, the environmental degradation modifies the nanoobject’s physical properties, thus making the optical in situ quantification and characterization of PSbs very challenging. − At present, optical fingerprinting relies on the calculation of scattering and absorption cross sections computed from the Mie theory, assuming the PSbs’ RI is known. Hence, two main gaps are present. The first is a methodological gap for particles smaller than 1 μm, since at this scale the scattered component can be prominent if the PSbs assume a monolayer configuration. The second is the lack of data about PSbs’ RI below 400 nm wavelength range and with a radius as small as 55 nm. Indeed, the RI dispersion in the UV–vis range was determined only for bulk PS by reflection and transmission spectra inversion. − This method overlooks the scattering phenomena inherent to nanoscale PSbs, which may affect the RI, and does not provide any information about the PSbs’ morphology. Mie theory-based Monte Carlo simulations were employed to reproduce diffuse transmission and reflection spectra in the 370–1600 nm range without fully addressing the UV–vis spectrum. Only at a single wavelength (441.6 nm), a size-dependent investigation of PSbs determined the RI by fitting experimental scattering data to Mie theory predictions. Other works analyzed PSbs arranged in monolayers, accounting for multiple scattering and areal density effects, but without providing the RI or addressing the 200–400 nm range. Finally, the molecular optical transitions (π → π* and σ → σ*) were linked to the RI dispersion only for bulk PS. ,,− Thus, a model to compute the RI of nanometric PSbs and the expected transitions, particularly in the UV–vis range, is lacking.

This work aims to bridge these gaps by correlating the PSbs’ morphological characteristics with their optical response in the 200–800 nm range. Using monodispersed PSbs deposited on a sapphire substrate via spin coating, we measured four distinct optical components: total transmission, total reflection, diffuse transmission, and diffuse reflection. The data were reproduced by a new model exploiting Mie theory to account for scattering phenomena. The model accounts for the experimentally measured substrate effects and particle’s superficial density and size distribution, retrieving the PSbs’ wavelength-dependent complex RI by fitting the reflection and transmission spectra, thus linking the RI to the specific sample morphology. Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) and optical microscopy images were used to evaluate the sample morphology and support the model parameters. Moreover, the main model assumptions employed to treat the substrate contribution and the presence of PSb aggregates were tested against discrete dipole approximation (DDA) simulations. The model addresses limitations in existing inversion techniques by directly incorporating scattering and morphological effects and focusing on the 200–400 nm range, where data about PSbs’ RI are scarce. Additionally, we discuss the RI relation to the molecular optical transitions, providing a framework for interpreting nanometric PSbs’ optical properties in relation to their electronic structure, as a potential tool to find PSbs’ optical fingerprints for its characterization in more complex matrices.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Section

All the experiments were performed on 0.5 mm thick single-crystal sapphires cleaned with acetone and ethanol and sonicated in isopropanol to remove superficial stains and further sonicated for 30 min in H2O2 (30% (w/w) in H2O) to improve hydrophilicity. To deposit isolated PSbs (Sigma-Aldrich LB1, nominal radius 55 ± 8 nm), spin coating was chosen as it does not require any particular solvent or emulsifier. The PSbs were dispersed in a Milli-Q water/ethanol 1:1 v/v solution and sonicated for 2 h. An 80 μL droplet of this solution was casted on top of the substrate placed inside the spin-coating instrument (SPSSPIN 150i-NPP Desktop VersionPOLOS). The sample was accelerated for 40 s up to the cruise rotation speed (2000 rpm for 180 s) and decelerated for 40 s. The surface morphology was studied by an optical microscope (Leica DM2500 M coupled with a Renishaw MS20 Encoded Stage 100 nm) and by an atomic force microscope (Park NX10) operated in noncontact mode using a silicon nitride PPP-NCHR tip (NANOSENSORS, nominal radius less than 10 nm). Optical data were acquired with a PerkinElmer Lambda950 spectrophotometer in the 200–800 nm wavelength range, with a 150 mm Spectralon integrating sphere to split four optical components as shown in Figure .

The total reflection (R tot, Figure a) is obtained by collecting all of the light reflected by the sample. The total transmission (T tot, Figure b) is composed by all the light transmitted and scattered in the [−π/2; π/2] angles range with respect to the impinging light direction (magenta arrow). Results and Discussion

The New Optical Model

The new Mie-based optical model links the light transmitted, reflected, and absorbed by an array of monodisperse PSbs resting on a flat substrate with the PSbs’ physical parameters.

We consider first a single nanoparticle of size r. Reflection is separated from transmission by splitting Q sca (eq ) in two angular domains (see Figure b), θ ∈ [0,π/2] for the forward scattering (eq S1a of the Supporting Information) and θ ∈ [π/2,π] for the backward scattering (eq S1b of the Supporting Information). Accordingly, the scattered intensity becomes Isca = I sca for + I sca back. Hence, the light scattered forward by a single particle is 5a Tdiff(r,n̂)=IscaforI0=Qscafor4

Similarly, the light scattered backward direction is 5b Rdiff(r,n̂)=IscabackI0=Qscaback4

Since the PSbs’ size distribution is log-normal with center in R fit and standard deviation σfit (see below), the model computes Tdiff(r,n̂) , Rdiff(r,n̂) , and Q̃ext(r,n̂) in the range R fit ± 3σfit, as explained in Section 2 of the Supporting Information, providing the weighted averages Tdiff(Rfit,σfit,n̂) , Rdiff(Rfit,σfit,n̂) , Q̃ext(Rfit,σfit,n̂) . Moreover, since the PSBs cover only a fraction of the substrate, part of the impinging is interacting directly with the substrate. To account for this, we introduce the particle surface density f fit as the ratio between the sphere projected cross section (blue area) and the substrate area (Figure a). f fit ranges from 0 (complete absence of PSbs) to π/2√3 ∼ 0.91 (monolayer hexagonal lattice of nondeformable spheres). The model also accounts for the sapphire contribution. The light transmitted and reflected by the substrate is named T sub and R sub, while the constant terms T sub,diff const and R sub,diff const take care of diffusion. The multiple PSbs-substrate reflections are approximated to the first order; i.e., the light scattered from the PSBs can be reflected or transmitted by the substrate only twice. Multiple scattering between spheres is neglected since the AFM-measured surface density of PSbs is less than 10%. We also assume that the scattered light/substrate interaction does not depend on the incident angle, since the light is scattered mainly at small angles and the cone defined by θ < 30° includes more than 60% of the energy. The justification and limits of this assumption will also be treated in detail in the discussion section. Finally, the sample-light detector distance is much larger than the PSbs’ size r. The different light components included in the new model are explained in eq S3a–d and Figures S1–S4 of the Supporting Information, where T diff, R diff, and Q̃ext are the weighted averages defined in eq S2a–c of the Supporting Information.

The model calculations and the data fitting have been done with an original Python code, the Determination of Effective Multilayer Optical constants for Nanotechnology-Mie expansion (DEMON-Mie). The crucial difference with the original DEMON version is that the experimental data T tot, R tot, R diff tot, and T diff tot are calculated from eq S3a–d of the Supporting Information. We summarize here the critical quantities employed: T sub and R sub are calculated from the sapphire dielectric function, which is determined from a separate fit of the experimental bare substrate reflection and transmission data employing an arbitrary sum of oscillators. R diff, T diff, and Q̃ext are computed using Mie theory (eqs , , and ) through the amplitude functions S1(θ,r,n̂) and S2(θ,r,n̂) , which are calculated exactly exploiting an open-source code written by Prahl and verified by Wiscombe.

For each wavelength λ, the output Mie functions depend on the scattering angle θ, the PSbs’ radius r, and the complex RI n̂ . The θ dependence is eliminated as Q̃ext which is a function of S 1(θ = 0) = S 2(θ = 0) and both R diff and T diff are calculated from an integration over the suitable subsets of θ (eq S1a,b). As indicated above, we employ a log-normal radius distribution R fit ± σfit instead of a single radius.

Considering the PSBs’ surface density, the resulting user-dependent fitting parameters are then n̂ , R fit, σfit, and f fit. We compute through DEMON-Mie the theoretical prediction for R tot, T tot, R diff tot, and T diff tot as a function of the wavelength λ, starting from a dummy PSb’s complex RI n̂ , a radius R fit with standard deviation σfit and a coverage density f fit. The latter three quantities are fixed based on the experimental AFM data; hence, the complex RI n̂ is the sole unknown quantity.

Surface Morphology

The PSbs’ distribution on the substrate was studied by using both optical microscopy and AFM images to cover a field of view from 1 × 1 cm2 to 1 × 1 μm2. At the optical microscope scale (Figure S5 in the Supporting Information), the PSbs are homogeneously distributed on the surface thanks to the spin-coating deposition. The morphology at the particle scale is reported in the AFM images of Figure S6a,b (30 × 30 μm2), c (10 × 10 μm2), and d (5 × 5 μm2) of the Supporting Information, respectively, taken at different sample positions. The PSbs are mainly separated, as the amount of the superimposed, aggregated, or close-packed particles is lower than 30%. Moreover, there are no visible stains derived from the substrate treatment or PSbs’ solution, demonstrating the spin-coating potentiality for depositing separated PSbs onto a substrate. These data support the single-scattering assumptions of the model.

The PSBs’ sizes were obtained from the AFM data with the Gwyddion watershed algorithm. Figure shows the distributions of the vertical radius (r Z) and the equivalent horizontal radius (r eq) as sketched in the inset, together with the log-normal fits (eq S5a–c of the Supporting Information). The fitting z-height curve (black line) has (md ± SD) r Z = 41 ± 14 nm, in agreement with the nominal manufacturer value of 55 ± 8 nm. The lower r Z distribution center may be due to the PSbs’ deformation induced by the SiN AFM tip during scanning, coupled with capillarity effects and humidity.

Optical Spectra

Figure a–d shows the total and diffuse optical spectra, the red and black dots representing the bare substrate prior to deposition, and the PSbs’ data, respectively. The total reflection spectrum (Figure a) shows a clear PSb-related peak at λ ∼ 231 nm and a dip at λ ∼ 223 nm, which are not visible in the sapphire spectrum. The total transmission (Figure b) shows two PSb-induced slope changes at 238 nm and 223 nm and a downward shift of the spectrum for λ < 400 nm. PSbs’ optical features are most evident in the diffuse data since the amount of light scattered by the bare substrate is almost flat. Diffuse reflection (Figure c) increases from the visible to the UV region with three structures at λ ∼ 212 nm (dip), λ ∼ 231 nm (peak), and λ ∼ 268 nm (shoulder). Diffuse transmission (T diff, Figure d) is more intense (up to 5% of the incident beam intensity) and shows almost the same trend of the diffuse reflection (R diff, shoulder at λ ∼ 268 nm and peak at λ ∼ 230 nm), even though at λ ∼ 212 nm a shoulder is present instead of a dip. This difference suggests that backward and forward scatterings provide different outcomes. All of the observed features can be attributed to the presence of PSbs on the surface. The λ < 350 nm region of the spectrum is crucial to understand PSbs’ optical properties, thus great care was taken to correctly reproduce the main spectra features in this range, using the following fitting parameters: beads’ radius distribution centered in R fit with standard deviation σfit, surface density f fit, and PSbs’ complex dielectric function ε̂fit . The scattering background noise, noticeable in Figure c,d, was taken into account by adding the terms T sub,diff const = 0.65% and R sub,diff const = 0.22%. Without these constant terms, Mie-DEMON would predict T diff tot and R diff tot exactly equal to zero for λ > 400 nm, leading to a bias in the fitting process. The simulated spectra, obtained by the simultaneous fitting over the four experimental optical data, are plotted in green in Figure and the fitting results for the PSbs’ radius R fit ± σfit and the particle surface density f fit are reported in Table . The data-fit residuals are represented by the blue dots in Figure a–d. While the modeled distribution R fit ± σfit is consistent with the experimental data, f fit is partly differing from the experimental estimated f AFM. Anyhow, considering the uncertainty in f AFM, the discrepancy is not deemed significant.

For the total reflection (Figure a), our proposed model correctly reproduces both the 223 nm dip and the 231 nm peak and the descending trend with upward concavity in the visible-IR range. Total transmission spectra (Figure b) are also well reproduced, as the fit exhibits the two experimental measurable slope changes at 220 nm and 238 nm. The total optical response when λ > 400 nm is mainly related to the substrate, as the PSbs do not absorb nor scatter in this range. In the R diff (Figure c), the mean peak at λ ∼ 231 nm and the 212 nm dip are correctly reproduced in both shape and intensity, with discrepancies less than 4%. Even though the slope change is fitted at 250 nm instead of the experimentally measured 268 nm, the agreement between data and fit is very good between 200 nm and 400 nm (threshold limit of 5%). In T diff (Figure d), the model fails to replicate the 212 nm shoulder, as the fit calculates the same dip characteristic of diffuse reflection. This discrepancy suggests that the mechanism inducing diffuse reflection and transmission in the UV cannot solely be related to the parameters involved in our treatment; thus, a more detailed study is suggested to investigate the possible scattering sources. In any case, the 231 nm peak and trend for 400 > λ > 250 nm are well reproduced, with discrepancies less than 5%, thus the fitting results are considered satisfactory. The details about the absorption spectrum and the complete list of the oscillator used to model the dielectric function are found in the Paragraphs 7 and 8 of Supporting Information, respectively. The resulting PSbs’ complex RI, calculated from the dielectric function, is plotted in Figure e,f. The real part of the RI n (Figure e) shows a distinct peak at λ = 230 nm and a dip near the 200 nm experimental limit. A shoulder is visible at λ = 260 nm, while in the visible-IR range, it decreases with a normal dispersion trend. The complete understanding of these structures is out of the scope of this paper, and it will be treated in future works. The imaginary part of the RI (Figure f) has the main peaks at 220 nm and ∼260 nm. Discrepancies with other authors’ results ,, on bulk PS are within 1% when λ > 350 nm for both n and k and within 1.5% of Ma et al. calculation about PSbs’ n, suggesting that in the visible range the PSbs’ optical properties are similar to bulk PS. For λ < 350 nm, larger differences with respect to French et al.’s results are noted. Indeed, the latter are based on the direct inversion of the total reflection and transmission spectra, while here an effective model considering the PSbs’ morphology is used to determine the RI by a fitting process of the experimental data. The shape and scale of our system would prevent getting n̂ through a direct numerical inversion of the data, , which has no unique solution and should be applied only in bulk-like systems. ,− Moreover, the RI presented here is bridging the gap for dispersed PSbs with a radius between 40 and 50 nm.

Model Assumptions

We now discuss our assumption on the particle/substrate interaction in the scattering of light by using the Amsterdam Discrete Dipole Approximation (ADDA) software to perform different simulations of T diff and R diff. ADDA calculates the light scattering from a system of an arbitrary voxelized morphology (cubes of 1.2 nm size here, each representing a dipole responding to the incident electric field), including the particle–substrate interaction through the substrate wavelength-dependent RI (see the details in the Supporting Information). We stress that the PSbs’ refractive index is that determined in this work, if not otherwise specified.

Figure a shows the results for R diff (T diff is reported in Figure S9b of the Supporting Information) for PSbs in touch with the substrate, with log-normal size distribution (I), for two attached PSbs (II), for a weighted combination of I and II (III) and with log-normal distribution using French’s RI (IV). None of the simulations is reproducing correctly the experimental data (black circles), and the same is happening for T diff as shown in the Supporting Information. In particular, curve I does not match the 232 nm shoulder, as shown in the inset of Figure a, and the trend for λ > 350 nm. The II and III curves are failing above 230 nm and even using French’s RI (curve IV) the 250–350 nm range is not matched.

To understand the reason for discrepancies, we report in Figure b the contribution of each model component as defined in eq S3b and following the notation of the Figure S2a–c of the Supporting Information for a single particle size. While the S2b components (blue curves) do not show relevant differences between the two methods, it is the interplay between the S2a and S2c Mie components that allows us to correctly address the experimental peak at 232 nm and the shoulder at 260 nm, while the corresponding ADDA-based curves fail in this respect. It is also evident that in both cases, the higher order terms (purple curves) are not contributing to the scattered intensity. These results support our model assumptions of negligible substrate/PSbs’ interaction in the scattering of light for the current PSbs’ size. This also entails the use of the presented model and the DEMON-Mie calculation, which provides a better data fit and is about 3 orders of magnitude faster with respect to including PSbs’ substrate interaction through the DDA method.

The red curve in Figure is the ELF calculated from the data presented in this work. Both functions show a main peak around 5.8 eV, a broad structure near 4.75 eV and approach zero for E < 4 eV, where the PSbs do not absorb nor scatter. Nevertheless, the 5.8 eV peak is reproduced slightly differently: the maxima are indeed separated by 0.05 eV and the shape is different, as the 4.75 eV peak appears sharper and separated with respect to the ELF. To assign these structures, we compared our results with other authors’ calculations: French et al.’s (J cv and ELF for bulk polystyrene), Buck et al.’s and Inagaki et al.’s works (bulk polystyrene ELF), and Ritsko and Bigelow , and Rocco et al. simulations or measurements of electronic transitions. Ritsko and Bigelow and French et al. report the π→π* aromatic transition of phenyl rings at 6.3 eV (E 1), while French et al. , also find a shoulder at 5.8 eV (E 1 ′) in the J cv. We note that our model reproduces a peak at 5.74 eV that thus can be related to the π → π* aromatic transition of phenyl rings (E 1 ′). ,, The broad structure in the J cv between 5.1 eV (240 nm) and 4 eV (310 nm), weakly appearing inside French works, was not attributed to any optical or electronic transition in bulk polystyrene. This prompts a more detailed study to discriminate between a possible electronic transition or a PSbs’ specific optical property. Indeed, Rocco et al. measured styrene optical transition at 5.3 eV, while Ritsko and Bigelow , calculated the ethylbenzene excitation (π → π*) at 4.75 eV. The latter would be in principle forbidden by the benzene symmetry but becomes weakly allowed in the presence of the alkyl substitution characteristic of polystyrene. ,

In the ELF, we obtain a peak at 5.8 eV lying in the same energy range of the E 1 ′ transition assigned by French et al. and of the shoulder retrieved by Buck et al. and Inagaki et al. This strengthens the assignment of our peak at 5.8 eV to the E 1 ′ aromatic transition of phenyl rings. ,, On the other hand, the 4.75 eV peak in the ELF has not been observed by other authors ,, for bulk polystyrene, and the least energetic structure in the ELF is computed around 5.3 eV. Nevertheless, some transitions, like the styrene excitation at 5.3 eV and the ethylbenzene excitation π → π* at 4.75 eV, , are reported in this energy range. This deviation may also be related to the fact that other authors calculate the ELF using the dielectric function resulted from the direct inversion of the reflection spectrum through Kramer–Kronig relations. , This approach, however, cannot consider scattering and morphology effects accounted for by our model. Moreover, due to the weakly allowed nature of the ethylbenzene excitation, we may also propose that the 4.75 eV structure in the ELF and J cv could be related to a specific PSb’s optical feature not emerging in bulk polystyrene. If this hypothesis were confirmed, this feature could be used as a marker for polystyrene recognition and characterization, enabling novel methods for nanoplastic identification.

Conclusions

We measured the optical properties in the 200–800 nm range of PSbs homogeneously deposited on a sapphire substrate via spin coating, correlating the nanoparticle morphology to the RI by a new model based on Mie theory. The model, including the experimentally determined PSbs’ morphology through the PSbs’ radius and the particle surface density, fits the four measured light components using the Mie scattering of isolated PSbs, obtaining the PSbs’ complex RI. The validity of the Mie scattering for PSbs in the presence of the substrate was discussed in comparison to ADDA simulations. The analysis supported the use of our new model with the Mie theory for the current PSbs’ size, allowing a 3 orders of magnitude gain in computational time. We showed that light scattering is playing a key role giving rise to some peculiar RI features appearing between 200 and 280 nm. The J cv and the ELF obtained from the RI allowed us to attribute the 5.8 eV (214 nm) peak to the styrene aromatic transition (π → π*) of phenyl rings. Furthermore, the peak at 4.75 eV could be related either to the forbidden benzene excitation (but weakly allowed for polystyrene) or to a PSb’s specific optical feature not emerging in bulk polystyrene and not reported in the literature to date. In the latter case, this feature could potentially be used as a marker for nanoplastic identification and recognition, widening our model application range. Further studies are suggested in this direction to investigate the root of this structure aiming at creating a novel reference method for nanoplastic quantification.
