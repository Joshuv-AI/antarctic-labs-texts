Municipal multidimensional poverty increases the risk of acute myocardial infarction: a retrospective cohort study with sex-stratified analyses in Chile’s public health system
Authors: Natalia Bello-Escamilla, Paulina Pino, Carolina Nazzal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo estimate the association between municipal multidimensional poverty (MMDP) and the risk of a first hospitalised acute myocardial infarction (AMI) among users of the Chilean public health system, and to examine whether this association differs by sex.DesignMultilevel analysis of a retrospective cohort study.SettingPrimary level of care across 138 municipalities in Chile.ParticipantsA total of 137,162 individuals aged ≥45 years were included, comprising 83,598 women and 53,564 men, all enrolled in the cardiovascular health programme of the Chilean public health system due to the presence of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia or tobacco use. Individuals with a prior history of AMI were excluded. Participants were clustered by municipality and followed from 1 January 2015 to 30 April 2019. Sociodemographic and clinical data at the individual level were obtained from electronic health records and linked to municipal-level poverty indicators from the National Socioeconomic Characterization Survey.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was the time to first hospitalised AMI . Cox proportional hazard models with a shared frailty term were used to assess the association between MMDP (defined as being in the upper quartile of the index) and AMI incidence. Models were adjusted for individual sociodemographic characteristics, behavioural risk factors (eg, smoking, diet and physical activity), and biological conditions (eg, hypertension, type 2 diabetes and dyslipidaemia). Sex-stratified analyses were conducted to explore potential differences in the association.ResultsMMDP was significantly associated with an increased risk of AMI (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.64) after adjustment for individual-level risk factors. In stratified models, the association remained significant among women (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.68), but not among men (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.31).ConclusionResiding in municipalities with high levels of multidimensional poverty is associated with an increased risk of AMI among individuals with cardiovascular risk factors, particularly women. These findings underscore the need for intersectoral policies to address the structural determinants of cardiovascular health.Keywords: Myocardial infarction, Cardiovascular Disease, EPIDEMIOLOGY, Health Equity, PUBLIC HEALTH

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, with projections estimating 22.2 million deaths by 2030.1 According to the World Heart Report, they currently cause approximately 20.1 million deaths each year, around one-third of all global deaths.2 In Chile, they are also the leading cause of mortality, with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) being the most frequent diagnosis among ischaemic heart diseases.3 According to the 2016–2017 National Health Survey, the lifetime prevalence of AMI is approximately 3.3%, with incidence increasing after age 45 years and significantly higher rates in men.4 The national response has largely focused on secondary care strategies targeting individual risk factors through monitoring or self-management.5 However, 27.6% of people aged >15 years have high blood pressure,6 and fewer than 20% of those with diabetes receive adequate care7—figures that highlight the limitations of individually focused interventions. Tackling CVD more effectively requires examining the role of contextual and structural determinants.

The socioeconomic environment, characterised by factors such as unemployment, poverty and violence, has been shown to significantly influence cardiovascular risk.8 9 People living in economically disadvantaged communities face an elevated risk of CVD,10 along with increased rates of morbidity and mortality.11,13 Furthermore, they are prone to engaging in cardiovascular risk behaviours, including smoking, poor dietary habits and a lack of physical activity.814,16

Vulnerable environments involve not only low income but also other forms of deprivation, such as inadequate housing,17 insecurity18 and weak social cohesion due to poor neighbourhood organisation,19 which collectively defines multidimensional poverty. In these settings, access to resources that protect cardiovascular health—such as healthy food20 or facilities for physical activity,21 and infrastructure for active transportation22—is often limited. High crime rates further restrict outdoor activity by creating a persistent sense of insecurity.23

Chronic exposure to violence and unsafe environments may activate the body’s stress response, increasing heart rate, blood pressure and glucose release.23 Over time, repeated activation leads to overexposure to neural, endocrine and immune stress mediators, contributing to adverse effects across peripheral biological systems, including the cardiovascular and metabolic domains.24

Environmental disadvantage has been consistently linked to the risk of AMI, particularly through socioeconomic and neighbourhood-level factors. Previous reviews have shown that low income, limited education, unemployment and neighbourhood disadvantage are associated with increased AMI risk.25 26 However, most studies rely on composite indices or isolated deprivation variables—such as income, social cohesion or safety—which may not fully capture the multidimensional nature of contextual disadvantage.14 19 27 28

In Chile, the Ministry of Social Development has implemented a multidimensional poverty index since 2014, based on the Alkire and Foster methodology.29 This index complements income-based measures by incorporating additional dimensions of deprivation. In this study, we apply the municipal multidimensional poverty (MMDP) index, which provides an aggregated measure of social vulnerability at the municipal level. A detailed description of the five dimensions and 15 indicators used to construct the index is provided in the online supplemental materials S1.

In the context of individual cardiovascular risk, studies have consistently shown that men and women differ in physiological profiles, risk perception and attitudes toward behaviour change.30 Beyond biology, the social burden of gender roles has also been proposed as a relevant predictor of heart disease.31 However, little is known about how these gender-based differences interact with multidimensional poverty and their combined impact on AMI risk.

In summary, CVD remains a major global health challenge. While most prevention efforts have focused on individual-level factors such as treatment adherence, growing evidence supports the inclusion of social and community determinants. In Chile, no studies have assessed the role of MMDP in the occurrence of a first myocardial infarction, nor have they explored whether this relationship differs between men and women.

This study aimed to estimate the association between MMDP and the risk of a first hospitalised AMI in users of the Chilean public health system, and to examine whether this association varies by sex.

Method

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study, following individuals from cohort entry in 2015 through to the occurrence of AMI, death or the end of follow-up in 2019. Participants were identified from longitudinal electronic health records from the Chilean public health system. The cohort was established on 1 January 2015, and included all individuals aged ≥45 years, with documented cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia or tobacco use, and enrolled in the cardiovascular health programme (PSCV in Spanish). Eligible participants had at least one clinical record in 2015 and no previous history of AMI. Participants were identified from anonymised electronic health records provided by Rayen Salud and facilitated by the Chilean Ministry of Health (MINSAL in Spanish). This longitudinal database covers over 5000 primary healthcare centres nationwide and includes hospital discharge records for cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality.

Each participant was retrospectively followed over time from the date of cohort entry (1 January 2015) until the occurrence of a first AMI, death from other causes or the end of the study period (30 April 2019), whichever came first. The follow-up time in months was recorded for each individual.

The final analytical sample included 137,162 users from 138 municipalities represented in the 2015 National Socioeconomic Characterization Survey (CASEN in Spanish), who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study. Of these, 83,598 were women and 53,564 were men.

Variables and measures

Dependent variable: hospitalisation due to a first AMI, coded in International Classification of Diseases-10: I21-I22. This variable was dichotomised based on the occurrence of a fatal or non-fatal AMI between 2015 and 2019, coded as 0=absent and 1=present.

Independent variable: MMDP. This variable captures deprivations in five key dimensions: education, health, work and social security, housing and environment, and social networks and cohesion. These dimensions are represented by 15 indicators reflecting household-level deficiencies in critical aspects of well-being.

Multidimensional poverty is defined as the accumulation of weighted deprivations that exceed a threshold of 22.5%. This cut-off is based on the Alkire and Foster methodology, which establishes a minimum share of weighted deprivations to classify individuals or households as multidimensionally poor. In Chile, this definition has been officially adopted in national poverty measurement and applied in surveys such as CASEN since 2015.32 33

The 2015 CASEN database provides the percentage of multidimensional poverty at the municipal level (percentage of households per municipality) and is publicly available on the official website of the Chilean Ministry of Social Development.34 This study’s variable was dichotomised using the upper quartile as the cut-off point (≤75th percentile=0, low MMDP; >75th percentile=1, high MMDP), thereby ensuring a sufficient sample size in the high-poverty group while also classifying the majority of municipalities in the low-poverty group to create a reasonable definition of ‘high’ exposure. The decision to categorise the variable is consistent with prior research in environmental and area-level deprivation, where continuous indices (eg, neighbourhood socioeconomic status or deprivation scores) are commonly categorised into quartiles to capture threshold effects of contextual influences on health outcomes.35 36 Moreover, by distinguishing municipalities in the top 25% of MMDP, we focus on areas with comparatively high levels of deprivation, which aligns with public health strategies that prioritise ‘high-risk’ environments where interventions are most urgently needed.

Covariates: the geographic zone of the municipality was included as a contextual (municipal-level) variable. At the individual level, covariates included gender, age, health insurance, insurance bracket (used as a proxy for socioeconomic status, where Fonasa A affiliates represent the most socioeconomically disadvantaged individuals and Fonasa D affiliates the least), risk factors prevalence as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, obesity, abdominal obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, diet adherence and medication adherence. A detailed description of these variables and their operationalisation is provided in the online supplemental materials S2.

Statistical analysis

Given the hierarchical structure of the data, a two-level multilevel modelling approach was applied,37 where individuals (level 1) were nested within municipalities (level 2). The model included both individual-level and contextual-level variables to assess the association between MMDP and AMI occurrence. Subscript notation was used to represent data hierarchy: j indicates individuals and i municipalities. For example, Xij refers to the value of variable X for individual j in municipality i. This approach captures variability at both levels and aligns with multilevel statistical modelling standards. The table illustrating the multilevel diagram has been included in the online supplemental materials S3.

Initially, the variables were described using frequencies, medians and IQRs, depending on their nature. Univariable survival analysis was conducted using Cox proportional hazards regression models to estimate the crude associations (HR±95% CI) between each covariate and the time to first AMI. This method accounts for censoring of individuals who either died from other causes or reached the end of the study period without experiencing the event.

To assess the effect of MMDP on AMI risk over time, multivariable multilevel Cox proportional hazards models were fitted, including a random intercept for each municipality specified through a gamma shared frailty term. This approach accounted for the hierarchical structure of the data (individuals nested within municipalities) and allowed for heterogeneity across municipalities. Results are presented as HRs with 95% CI.

The multivariable model included sex, age and type of health insurance, variables frequently used for adjustment in cardiovascular research. In addition, established cardiovascular risk factors—hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia, smoking and sedentary lifestyle—were incorporated, as they are recognised in the literature as potential confounders of the association between socioeconomic environment and AMI risk, and were statistically significant in the univariable analysis (p<0.05). Municipality area, dietary adherence and medication adherence were also included, as they met the same statistical criterion. Model 1 included municipal-level variables; model 2 additionally incorporated sociodemographic characteristics; and model 3 further adjusted for biological and behavioural cardiovascular risk factors, as well as other individual clinical variables. Analyses were performed on the total sample and stratified by sex. All statistical analyses were conducted with Stata statistical software, release 16 (Stata Corporation, College Station, Texas, USA).38

Ethical statement

The Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine of the University of Chile reviewed and approved this project (Act Aug064/114-2020). The study used secondary data from two sources: (1) the 2015 CASEN survey database and (2) the electronic clinical records of PSCV patients enrolled in primary care facilities. According to Law 19.628,39 electronic clinical records are considered sensitive data; therefore, the database—provided by the Ministry of Health’s Primary Care Division, which authorised the study on behalf of the patients—was anonymised before being delivered to the researcher.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

AMI incidence was 3% overall. Men in communities with a high level of MMDP were the most affected (4.4% vs 2.1%).

Baseline characteristics differed significantly by sex. Women had higher rates of obesity and sedentary lifestyle, while men showed a greater prevalence of smoking and diabetes. Additionally, women were more frequently classified in the most socioeconomically disadvantaged insurance category (Fonasa A; table 1).

Other individual-level predictors associated with increased AMI risk included presence of diabetes, dyslipidaemia and smoking. On the other hand, regular medication adherence was found to have a protective effect. In this context, medication adherence refers to the regular intake of prescribed drugs for cardiovascular risk management, including antihypertensives, lipid-lowering agents and antidiabetic medications, according to the clinical condition of the patient.40

Discussion

As the main finding, this study supports the hypothesis that residents of municipalities with a high level of MMDP have a higher risk of experiencing a first hospital admission for AMI, even after adjusting for individual differences, consistent with previous studies.25 26

The findings may be explained by various contextual characteristics encompassed within the MMDP index, which could influence previously proposed mechanisms. For example, dimensions such as limited access to education, transportation and inadequate housing conditions may hinder community organisation, increasing vulnerability to environmental and health-related challenges.20,23 Additionally, exposure to environmental pollution—whether air, drinking water or waste accumulation in public spaces—has been identified as a risk factor for CVD.23 41

This study also sought to identify higher-risk groups, including stratification by sex. The findings show that aspects of the residential environment relevant to health vary according to sex. Women residing in communities with a high level of MMDP had a higher risk of AMI compared with men in similar conditions, consistent with previous research.30 31

These differences in responses to the environment by sex could be explained from a gender perspective. Women may spend more time in the residential area, being more exposed to adverse environmental factors.42 In addition, they may perceive and report adverse aspects of their environment in greater detail because of their social roles, such as parenting and home maintenance, which link them closely to local characteristics.43 These roles may limit their access to healthy lifestyles, physical activity, self-care and specialised medical care.44 In Chile, women often assume responsibility for home and family care, which may lead them to underestimate their health problems or delay seeking medical care to prioritise family well-being.45

This study has some limitations. The occurrence of AMI was obtained from hospital discharge records in public hospitals, which excludes cases of persons who did not seek hospital care, either because they did not perceive the severity of the event or died outside the system. This selection bias may lead to under-reporting of AMI cases and, consequently, to an underestimation of the true incidence.

Although the independent variable MMDP is a comprehensive indicator that considers dimensions such as education, health, work, housing and social networks, it does not capture all the contextual factors relevant to health and CVD, such as the availability of healthy food sources or the full extent of air pollution. Moreover, disaggregated data by dimension of the MMDP index are not available at the municipal level, limiting the ability to explore the specific contribution of each domain.

The measurement of individual socioeconomic level was limited, as only health protection data were available, with no records on income, occupation or schooling, which could generate residual confounding. Nevertheless, health protection provided a reliable socioeconomic status gradient and was considered in the analyses, along with a wide range of potential confounders: sociodemographic characteristics (age and sex), behavioural risk factors (eg, diet and sedentary lifestyle) and risk criteria defined in the technical guidelines of the PSCV, including diagnoses of hypertension, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidaemia and tobacco use in individuals aged ≥55 years. These variables were incorporated to explicitly address their potential role as confounding factors. However, we acknowledge that residual confounding from other unmeasured factors—both individual and contextual—cannot be entirely ruled out and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Despite these limitations, this study is a pioneer in Chile and Latin America in examining the effect of MMDP on the risk of a first AMI and the role of community factors in this occurrence, using massive public registries. Multilevel methods allowed us to address autocorrelation within clusters and estimate how area characteristics affect health, providing a more detailed understanding of the phenomenon.

The strong association observed between the level of MMDP and the occurrence of a first AMI suggests that the effects of the residential environment should be addressed. In a context where the burden of chronic diseases is increasing, these inequalities have important implications for public health. Reducing AMI-associated morbidity and mortality will require coordinated interventions that integrate individual, community and multisectoral components. These findings highlight the importance of implementing intersectoral social policies, a responsibility held by municipalities in Chile, which can significantly contribute to advancing public health objectives.
