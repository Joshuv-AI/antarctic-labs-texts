Multi-modal single-cell platform for nanoparticle-enhanced time-series metabolic profiles of CD8+ T cell exhaustion in tumor immunosurveillance
Authors: Chenjie Yang, Mingxia Gao, Xiangmin Zhang, Hailong Yu, Chunhui Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCytotoxic T cells (CD8+) play a pivotal role in immunosurveillance by identifying and eliminating tumor cells. However, the onset of CD8+ T cell exhaustion, characterized by overexpression of immune checkpoint receptors, impairs their function, allowing tumor cells to evade immunosurveillance. Single-cell metabolic profiles hold the promise in characterizing intrinsic cellular metabolic heterogeneity of exhausted CD8+ T cells, even at ultra-early time points. Herein, we developed a multi-modal single-cell platform that integrates nanoparticles-enhanced laser desorption/ionization mass spectrometry and protein number counting platform to elucidate the temporal dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion. A comprehensive time-series analysis was conducted, with nearly 3000 single cells performing metabolic profile extraction and checkpoint receptors quantification. Our results demonstrated that the onset of exhaustion was as early as 3 h post-stimulation and upon cessation of stimulation, a degree of reversibility was observed in these exhausted cells. Using deep learning algorithms, the discrimination of the different exhausted CD8+ T cell subpopulations from the control achieved an area under the curve value of more than 0.904, even to 1.000 with 100% sensitivity and specificity. Our work presents a robust, high-throughput, and scalable system for multi-modal single-cell analysis, offering valuable insights into the dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03774-4.Keywords: Nanoparticles, LDI-MS, Single-cell, Metabolic profiles, CD8+ T cell exhaustion

Introduction

The development of cancer is normally repressed by the immune system, a process referred to as immunosurveillance [1]. Cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) play a critical role in immunosurveillance by eliminating tumor cells via the release of cytotoxic molecules such as perforin and granzymes [2, 3]. However, prolonged exposure to chronic infection and tumor antigens leads to a progressive loss of CD8+ T cell effector functions, a phenomenon referred to as ‘exhaustion’ [4, 5]. Exhausted CD8+ T cells are characterized by heightened expression of immune checkpoints, including programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), among others [6–9]. Despite these advances, the ability to precisely track the ultra-early stages of CD8+ T cell exhaustion remains hindered by the results of population-level analysis, which provides the average view of cellular behavior [5]. In contrast, single-cell analysis has the ability to uncover cellular heterogeneity, precisely identify distinct cell subpopulations, and monitor cellular dynamics over time [10, 11].

Metabolites, stemming from genomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic processes, directly reflect the real-time state of the cellular [12]. Emerging evidence suggests metabolic alteration, [13] including metabolic reprogramming that affects energy metabolism and metabolic pathways [14], dramatically affects the activation and function of the exhausted CD8+ T cells during the exhaustion process [15, 16]. For instance, mitochondrial damage and decreased oxidative phosphorylation capacity are the most commonly observed metabolic changes during T cell exhaustion, which not only affect the energy supply of CD8+ T cells but also impair their ability to release cytotoxic molecules, further exacerbating T cell exhaustion and leading to tumor immune evasion [16–18]. Recently, Guo and colleagues demonstrated that up-regulation of mitochondrial pyruvate carrier-dependent oxidative phosphorylation remodeled mitochondrial function in exhausted T cells through the use of IL-10 [19]. These findings highlight that the metabolic alterations hold the huge promise to reveal the changes of the functions of exhausted CD8+ T cells.

Mass spectrometry has emerged as a state-of-the-art analytical technique for single-cell metabolic analysis, offering unparalleled depth of understanding across various scientific domains [20–22]. Secondary ionization mass spectrometry (SIMS), electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI-MS), and matrix-assisted desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-MS) have revolutionized the exploration of cellular metabolism at the single-cell level [23, 24]. Each of these techniques has its strengths and limitations. SIMS, for instance, offers exceptional spatial resolution and subcellular imaging capabilities, but it is hindered by low throughput, complex sample preparation, restricted coverage, and high costs [25, 26]. Similarly, while ESI-MS provides high sensitivity and broad coverage, it suffers from challenges related to throughput and the complexity of workflows in single-cell analysis [27–29].

In contrast, MALDI-MS stands out for its speed, with analysis times in seconds, minimal sample pretreatment, and the potential for high throughput and broad coverage [30, 31]. These features make MALDI-MS a promising tool for single-cell metabolic heterogeneity investigation since 2010 [30, 32, 33]. More recently, the integration of MALDI-MS with immunocytochemistry has successfully identified lipid heterogeneity between single astrocytes and neurons, revealing seventeen significant differences [34]. Whereas LDI-MS usually requires organic matrices for energy and electron/proton transfer during desorption/ionization, the accuracy of metabolic detection is heavily limited by disintegrated fragments from the organic matrices in the low mass range.

In the past decade, nanoparticles have gained huge attention as alternative matrices due to their superior desorption/ionization capabilities, advanced optical properties, enhanced thermal stability, and cost-effectiveness [35–37]. Notably, ferric-enhanced nanoparticles assisted LDI-MS (NPLDI MS) have demonstrated their ability to improve the charge transfer and reduce heat dissipation during the photon-induced desorption/ionization process, improving the LDI-MS performance for analyte detection [38, 39]. Notably, this approach has been particularly successful in elucidating the metabolic heterogeneity of hematopoietic stem cells at single-cell resolution [40].

Despite these advancements, high-throughput living single-cell metabolic analysis platforms that are scalable for multi-modal analysis have not been introduced. Herein, we developed a high-throughput multi-modal single-cell platform that integrates single-cell NPLDI MS (scNPLDI MS) with single-cell protein number counting (scPNC) to investigate the CD8+ T cell exhaustion process (Fig. 1A&B). Additionally, an automated air-bubble-assisted single-cell sampling system (auto-SiCS) ensures rapid and stable single-cell sampling for multi-modal analysis. In this regard, the scNPLDI MS platform can efficiently trap metabolites from a single CD8+ T cell (SCD8+TC), facilitating high-throughput and high-quality extraction of single-cell metabolic profiles. Similarly, the scPNC platform allows for precise quantification of immune checkpoint receptors, such as PD-1 and TIM-3, along with B-lymphocyte-induced maturation protein 1 (BLIMP-1). By following a predefined time series, we collected large-scale metabolic profiles and uncovered the expression changes of PD-1, TIM-3, and BLIMP-1. The multi-modal data revealed critical dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion (Fig. 1C). Our work introduces a robust, high-throughput, and scalable multi-modal system for single-cell analysis, offering valuable insights into the temporal dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion.

Results and discussion

The establishment of the multi-modal single-cell platform

Precise, reliable, and efficient single-cell sampling is essential for conducting high-throughput single-cell analysis. Our auto-SiCS system has undergone substantial enhancements, building on the previous design [41, 42] to include automation capabilities, a three-dimensional operating platform with infinitely switchable speeds, and improved stability with low vibration, securely fixed to the optical table. Notably, auto-SiCS achieved the precise manipulation of over 100 target single cells per hour under direct microscope visualization without interference from non-target cells. Additionally, the sampling apparatus has been upgraded with a stepping motor system, enabling the sampling capillary tip to move with a precision of up to 0.16 μm per step. The technique guarantees 100% confirmation of single-cell sampling, as validated through simulations on transparent slides (Fig. S1&S2). The scPNC platform was accomplished using single-cell single-molecule fluorescence imaging integrated with our auto-SiCS system. Target SCD8+TCs were captured and then transferred to the pre-marked locations on antibody-functionalized slides. These single cells were lysed in situ to release exhaustion-related biomarkers, forming monomolecular layers on the slide surface. Quantum-dot-conjugated antibody fluorescent tags were subsequently applied for imaging and number-counting of these biomarkers. Characterized by its extraordinary sensitivity, the scPNC platform can acutely observe the expression changes of PD-1, TIM-3, and BLIMP-1.

High-throughput scNPLDI MS platform utilizes a stainless-steel microchip with a capacity of up to 1526 sample spots, each with a diameter of 1 mm (approximately 0.785 mm2), specifically tailored for single-cell analysis. Notably, a 3D-printed sampling adaptor was designed to enable the efficient transfer of SCD8+TC cells from the sample pool to the microchip for subsequent LDI-MS analysis (Fig. 2A and Fig. S3). Immediately after sampling, the microchip was submerged in liquid nitrogen for 10 min to quench metabolic activity, followed by thawing at room temperature to facilitate cell lysis (Fig. S4) [40, 43]. We designed the scNPLDI MS platform for sensitive and reproducible single-cell metabolic profiles extraction using ferric nanoparticles (FeNPs). Specifically, FeNPs were synthesized via a cost-effective hydrothermal method, with each 1 g FeNPs supporting up to 107 single-cell analyses.

To clarify the crystal structure of the material, we conducted a powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) analysis. As shown in Fig. S5a, the XRD pattern exhibits characteristic diffraction peaks at 31.1°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.5°, 57.0°, and 62.2°. When compared with the standard Fe₃O₄ card (PDF#19–0629), these peaks correspond to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystal planes, demonstrating the Fe₃O₄ phase in the FeNPs. Subsequently, FeNPs were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and the results revealed the uniform spherical morphology with a rough surface, which can promote the size-selective trapping of metabolites (Fig. 2B and Fig. S6a) [44]. The exact elemental composition of FeNPs was characterized by energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy with 67.12% of oxygen and 32.88% of ferrum (Fig. S5b). From the results of Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis, a strong absorption peak at 3430 cm⁻¹ attributed to O-H stretching vibrations can explain the high content of elemental oxygen may be due to the adsorption of H2O on the surface of FeNPs. The Transmission electron microscopy further confirmed the uniform size of FeNPs, ensuring consistent and reliable results during the LDI-MS process (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, dynamic light scattering demonstrated good water dispersity for evenly mixing the suspension without precipitation, as well as a consistent nanoparticle size with an average diameter of approximately 260 nm (Fig. 2D). The strong absorption at 355 nm of the ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectra indicates the efficient energy transfer and desorption/ionization capability during the LDI-MS process (Fig. 2E).

The LDI-MS performance of FeNPs was estimated in terms of detection reproducibility and sensitivity, compared to traditional organic matrices like a-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) and 2,5-dihydroxybenzoic acid (DHB). The homogeneous co-crystallization of analytes with FeNPs underscored excellent reproducibility, in contrast to the coffee ring effect observed with CHCA and DHB (Fig. 2F). Further, FeNPs demonstrated a superior ability to detect a broader spectrum of metabolites with significantly enhanced intensity and signal-to-noise ratios. Notably, FeNPs successfully identified a variety of metabolites, including glucose (Glc), arginine (Arg), aspartic acid (Asp), glutamic acid (Glu), histidine (His), methionine (Met), phenylalanine (Phe), valine (Val), taurine (Tau), and mannitol (Man) (Fig. 2G). The corresponding metabolite adducts (Na+/K+/H+) showed high reliability, with most of the coefficients of variation (CVs) for intensity measurement being below 15% (Fig. 2H). The limit of detection (LOD) of FeNPs was also evaluated, with the LOD for Glc, His, Arg, and Met ranging from 1 ng/mL to 100 ng/mL, validating its effectiveness for single-cell analysis (Fig. S7). Significantly, in the analysis of SCD8+TC, Glc was used as a representative metabolite, and metabolic profiles successfully detected the peak of [Glc + Na]+ (203.05) (Fig. 2I), which is not observed in the spectrum when using CHCA (Fig. 2J) and DHB (Fig. 2K) as the matrix.

Time-series monitoring the dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion

We utilized an in vitro system in which activated T cells were expanded in the absence or presence of persistent antigenic stimulation from the MCF-7 cell lineage to investigate the dynamic of the CD8+ T cell exhaustion (Fig. 1A). We collected metabolic profiles and expression of exhaustion biomarkers of the SCD8+TC samples at eight distinct time points (0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h) (Fig. 3A). Notably, the 0 h time point (designated Co-c0h, equivalent to Con0h) served as the baseline control in all comparative analyses. A total of 926 SCD8+TC from eight different cell populations were analyzed: 90 SCD8+TC from Co-c0h, 95 SCD8+TC from Co-c3h, 92 SCD8+TC from Co-c6h, 90 SCD8+TC from Co-c12h, 138 SCD8+TC from Co-c24h, 138 SCD8+TC from Co-c48h, 144 SCD8+TC from Co-c72h, and 139 SCD8+TC from Co-c96h.

Mass spectrometry data within the m/z range of 80 Da to 500 Da were recorded, and following peak extraction, alignment, and normalization, 613 features with a signal-to-noise ratio (S/N >3) were obtained for each SCD8+TC metabolic profile. Representative metabolic profiles for each cell population were depicted (Fig. 3B). Subsequently, we utilized the t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) algorithm to evaluate the metabolite distribution across eight SCD8+TC populations [45]. Notably, the Co-c0h, Co-c3h, and Co-c6h cell populations overlapped into a cluster, and the other five cell populations representing Co-c12h, Co-c24h, Co-c48h, Co-c72h, and Co-c96h were successfully distinguished (Fig. 3C&D). Through the Pearson correlation analysis, an integrated metabolic profile dataset was generated, which indicated strong correlations among the eight CD8+T cell populations, demonstrating time-dependent metabolic alterations (Fig. 3E). To validate these findings, we conducted parallel bulk SCD8+TC exhaustion experiments by culturing CD8+ T cells alone as controls. Notably, this includes 26 SCD8+TCCon3h, 26 SCD8+TCCon6h, 26 SCD8+TCCon12h, 29 SCD8+TCCon24h, 22 SCD8+TCCon48h, 27 SCD8+TCCon72h, and 30 SCD8+TCCon96h (Fig. S8). Interestingly, t-SNE analysis suggested a uniformly mixed distribution from Con3h to Con96h populations, SCD8+TC tended to form a uniformly mixed distribution, exhibiting no significant differences (Fig. 3F).

Using the scPNC platform, we evaluated the expression of the checkpoint proteins PD-1 and TIM-3, as well as the transcription factor BLIMP-1, in individual SCD8+ T cells throughout distinct time points. Absolute counts of the three exhausted biomarkers were listed in Tables S1-S8. Our scPNC platform analysis revealed a consistent upregulation of PD-1, TIM-3, and BLIMP-1 compared to the Con group (Fig. 3G-I). Notably, an initial upregulation of PD-1 (P = 0.001) and TIM-3 (P = 0.001) was detected as early as 3 h, with statistically significant increases compared to the Con group (Fig. 3J). By 6 h, PD-1 (P = 0.006), TIM-3 (P = 0.02), and BLIMP-1(P = 0.03) exhibited significant upregulation relative to their controls (Fig. 3K), with more pronounced enhancements observed from 12 h onwards (Fig. S9). These findings suggest that our multi-modal SCD8+TC analysis system can detect the early exhaustion signals well before the traditionally recognized 48–72 h window. Moreover, 3 h marks an ultra-early exhaustion time point, while 6–12 h intervals are critical for exhaustion progression.

Metabolic features screening during CD 8+ T cell exhaustion

Identifying key metabolic features is essential for comprehending the exhaustion process of CD8+ T Cells. We systematically analyzed SCD8+TC samples at different time points (Co-c3h, Co-c6h, Co-c12h, Co-c24h, Co-c48h, Co-c72h, and Co-c96h) compared to the control group (Con0h). By applying rigorous statistical methods (t-test with a Bonferroni-corrected P value < 0.05 and fold change threshold (FC = 2), substantial metabolic alterations were identified at various time points (Fig. 4A). The corresponding volcano plots highlighted both upregulated and downregulated features across these time points. Notably, metabolic changes were observed as early as 3 h, but fewer alterations were evident during the early stage (0 h ~ 6 h). In contrast, a substantial increase was detected from Co-c12h to Co-c96h, signifying a critical metabolic transition at 6 h, which is consistent with the results of scPNC analysis (Fig. 4B-H).

To deepen our understanding, we concentrated on the critical time points of 6 h. Initially, a Venn diagram showed 39 shared metabolic features between samples at Co-c3h and Co-c6h (Fig. 4I). Another Venn diagram displayed 258 common significant features among samples at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h (Fig. 4J & Fig. S10). These results led to the screening of 12 key features spanning the entire exhaustion process from 3 h to 6 h, and 258 features most relevant to the exhaustion process from 12 h to 96 h (Fig. 4K). Clustering heatmaps further demonstrated the strong correlations of these features across eight cell populations (Fig. 4L-M).

To validate the distinguishing performance of these significant metabolic features, we divided the eight SCD8+ TC populations into training (70%) and validation (30%) sets. Twelve key features were used to distinguish the groups in the early stage, while 258 features were used for the later stage. Utilizing a neural network machine learning algorithm, we successfully distinguished cell populations from the control group. Notably, 12 features achieved an area under the curve (AUC) value of 0.904 (95% CI of 0.819–0.989) with 77.8% sensitivity and 89.7% specificity, and 0.971 (95% CI of 0.924–1.000) with 96.3% sensitivity and 89.3% specificity for co-c3h and co-c6h cell populations, respectively (Fig. 4N). Meanwhile, we employed the 258 individual significant features to distinguish the remaining populations, and remarkably, the 258 features achieved the overall AUC value of 1.000 with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity for all the SCD8+TC cell populations from 12 h to 96 h (Fig. 4O and Fig. S11). These results underscore the key points at 6 h and the key metabolic features in the early and later stages.

Reversible state of CD8+ T cell exhaustion

Studies have demonstrated that exhausted CD8+ T cells can undergo a partial recovery away from the tumor microenvironment [46, 47]. To investigate this point, we conducted experiments that co-culturing CD8+ T cells with MCF-7 cells for various durations (6 h, 24 h, 48 h, 72 h, and 96 h), followed by a 24-hour recovery period in individual culture conditions (Re-c) to assess metabolic alterations and exhaustion biomarkers (Fig. 5A). Firstly, metabolic profiles were extracted and encompassed 655 SCD8+TC from seven populations: 90 SCD8+TCcon0h, 96 SCD8+TCRe−c6h, 94 SCD8+TCRe−c12h, 92 SCD8+TCRe−c24h, 94 SCD8+TCRe−c48h, 96 SCD8+TCRe−c72h, and 93 SCD8+TCRe−c96h. The representative metabolic profiles were presented in Fig. S12. Utilizing t-SNE clustering, distinct metabolic states were observed across these populations, reflecting their different metabolic alterations due to the different co-culture durations (Fig. 5B). Concurrently, exhaustion biomarkers PD-1, TIM-3, and BLIMP-1, evaluated via the scPNC platform, showed significantly reduced expression in the Re-c group compared to the Co-c group (Fig. 5C-E and Table S9-S13), indicating the exhaustion state is recoverying to some extent compared to the Co-c group.

Ongoing T cell receptor and inflammatory signaling in disease states such as cancer or chronic viral infection progressively drive CD8+ T cells into exhaustion, resulting in numerous metabolic defects or alterations evident in exhausted CD8+ T cells. We identified four key metabolites from the significant features highlighted in Fig. 4L-M and Table S14, offering some critical understanding of CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Specifically, stearoylcarnitine (SCA, m/z 466.39, [M + K]+) was identified in the early exhaustion stage (3 to 6 h of co-culturing). Three additional metabolites, namely 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE, 195.08, [M + K]+), glucose 6-phosphate (G6P, 261.07, [M + H]+), and linoleic acid (LA, 281.20, [M + H]+) were identified during the later stages of exhaustion (12 h to 96 h of co-culturing).

To further explore the metabolic features identified, we performed KEGG pathway and enrichment analysis (Fig. S13). The results revealed that LA and G6P are significantly enriched in several metabolic pathways, emphasizing the central role of lipid and carbohydrate metabolism in CD8+ T cell exhaustion (Table S15-S16). The significant enrichment of LA in its own metabolism pathway (P = 0.00649) and in the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids (P = 0.0463) suggests that lipid metabolism dysregulation is a key factor in CD8+ T cell exhaustion. This dysregulation can lead to fatty acid accumulation, impairing mitochondrial function.

Lipid peroxidation (LPO) plays a crucial role in driving terminal T cell exhaustion and curbing anti-PD-1 efficacy [48, 49]. As an omega-6 polyunsaturated fatty acid, LA is critical in inflammatory responses and cellular metabolism [50]. During fatty acid metabolism, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are produced, leading to oxidative stress, which impairs T cell function and drives them toward exhaustion [48, 49]. As shown in Fig. 5H, the abundance of LA in the Co-c group is gradually upstream regulated as the culturing duration increases compared to the Con group, indicating that CD8+ T cells may elevate LA levels to resist exhaustion [51]. However, after a 24 h recovery, LA levels in the Re-c group decreased, but remained higher than in controls, suggesting LA’s role in mitigating the exhaustion state. 4-HNE, a product of lipid peroxidation, was elevated in co-cultured cells (P < 0.05), particularly after 6 h of co-culturing, consistent with previous studies [52]. Interestingly, after 24 h of recovery, 4-HNE levels approached those of the control group (P >0.05), indicating the reversible nature of CD8+ T cell exhaustion.

SCA, also known as acylcarnitine C18:0, is a fatty ester lipid formed by the conjugation of the long-chain fatty acid stearic acid with carnitine and facilitates the transport of long-chain fatty acids to mitochondria for β-oxidation [53, 54]. However, previous studies indicate that exhausted CD8+ T cells accumulate specific long-chain fatty acids, impairing their antitumor functions by reducing mitochondrial activity and hindering fatty acid oxidation [55]. As shown in Fig. 5F, the level of SCA in the Co-c group exhibited a gradual increase with the co-culturing duration from 0 h to 96 h. Conversely, after a 24 h recovery, the level of the SCA in the Re-c group significantly decreased, suggesting its potential as an early exhaustion biomarker for monitoring the reversible exhaustion state.

G6P, enriched in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway (P = 0.0233), indicates a shift in glucose utilization during exhaustion. G6P, a pivotal intermediate in glycolysis and glycogenolysis, plays a crucial role in energy metabolism and the generation of NADPH and nucleotides via the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP). In the tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T cells often rely on glycolysis due to hypoxia and high energy demand, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect. In the tumor microenvironment, CD8+ T cells are under high energy demand and hypoxic conditions, and cells may preferentially use glycolysis, a phenomenon known as the Warburg effect. Thus, glucose converting to G6P as the start step of the glycolysis pathway resulted in the accumulation of G6P. Moreover, under conditions of exhaustion, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation is impaired, preventing G6P from entering the PPP and thereby inhibiting the production of reduced NADPH, which is indispensable not only for sustaining metabolic processes but also for modulating signaling pathways in CD8+ T cells. As displayed in Fig. 5G, the level of G6P in the Co-c group showed a significant and progressive regulation with the co-culturing duration from 0 h to 96 h when compared to the Con group. In parallel, following the 24 h recovery, the level of the G6P in the Re-c group is comparable to the Con group.

Further analysis of other enriched pathways, such as neomycin, kanamycin, and gentamicin biosynthesis (P = 0.0026), and inositol phosphate metabolism (P = 0.0386), suggests broader metabolic reprogramming during T cell exhaustion that warrants further investigation.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a high-throughput multi-modal single-cell platform that integrates the scNPLDI MS platform with the scPNC platform to investigate the dynamics of CD8+ T cell exhaustion. By systematically monitoring a defined time series from 0 h to 96 h, we extracted over 1539 metabolic profiles from SCD8+TC, and exhaustion-related protein biomarker number counting (PD-1, TIM-3, and BLIMP-1) was performed on over 1260 SCD8+TC, facilitating a comprehensive characterization of the temporal dynamics underlying CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Our findings reveal critical time points in the exhaustion process. Notably, we observed the onset of CD8+ T cell exhaustion as early as 3 h post-stimulation, significantly earlier than previously reported initial time points. The intervals between 3 and 6 h were identified as indicative of early exhaustion states. However, after stimulation for 12 h, the analysis results revealed a significant explosion in the metabolic alterations and exhaustion-related protein biomarkers. Leveraging the extensive metabolic datasets generated, we applied a neural network machine learning algorithm to accurately classify distinct exhaustion clusters, yielding promising results. Additionally, we investigated the reversible nature of CD8+ T cell exhaustion. Following the cessation of stimulation, exhausted CD8+ T cells presented a reversible state to some extent. Importantly, we identified four significant metabolite, including stearoylcarnitine, glucose 6-phosphate, linoleic acid, and 4-hydroxynonenal, which were up-regulated with the occurrence of exhaustion and down-regulated with the recovery of the exhaustion. While the key metabolic alterations were revealed, the underlying pathway mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated and will require future studies with higher-resolution metabolite identification. In summary, scNPLDI MS provides a high-throughput and rapid platform for single-cell metabolic analysis, aligning with the scPNC platforms, enabling early monitoring of exhaustion states and presenting potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment.

Methods

Peripheral blood mononuclear cell isolation

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital of Fudan University (B-2020-085R). Blood samples were obtained from healthy volunteers within 3 h before the peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) isolation process. Informed consent from all participants was signed and obtained. The PBMCs were isolated in a density-gradient separation method with Ficoll-Paque PLUS (GE Healthcare) [56]. The whole blood sample was first diluted with PBS and then added on top of the Ficoll solution along the wall of the centrifuge tube, both softly and slowly. The final ratio of these three components was 1:1:1. The liquid level of the mix was kept layered and then centrifuged for 30 min at 400 g at 4 ℃. The narrow band of all layers (the second layer from above) containing PBMCs was kept for further processing. The PBMCs obtained were washed with PBS three times and ACK lysing buffer was added for the lysis of red blood cells for 15 min at 4 ℃. Then PBMCs were washed again with PBS and resuspended with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin.

In-vitro CD8+ T cells exhaustion model

The T-cell simulation mix (0.5 µg/mL anti-CD3 and 5 µg/mL anti-CD28 in ultimate concentration) was first added to 24-well cell culture plates and incubated overnight for plate-coating at 4 ℃ following the manufacturer’s manual. The uncoated antibodies were washed with PBS three times. Then the fresh PBMCs were added into the pre-coated wells and simulated for 24 h at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Then the simulated PBMCs were transferred into new, uncoated wells, and the cells-medium was replaced and maintained with a complete growth medium (RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 0.5 ng/mL IL-7, and 100 ng/mL IL-2) for further culture and proliferation [56, 57]. The MCF-7 cell line was cultured and passaged with RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, which was prepared for the co-culture process. The stimulated PBMCs and MCF-7 cells were co-cultured in a ratio of 5:1, with the PBMCs cultured in a Transwell-insert (upper compartment) and MCF-7 cells in the lower compartment.

Target CD8+T cell fluorescence labeling and sampling

10 µL of PBMCs co-cultured were obtained when needed, and PBS was added to make the total volume of 100 µL. Then 20 µL PE Mouse Anti-Human CD8 antibody (BD Pharmingen) was added and incubated at 4 ℃ for 30 min in the dark. Excessive fluorescent labels were washed with PBS three times. Then the CD8+ T cells labeled were sampled in the dark with the air-bubble adhesion sampling method mentioned above, while the excitation wavelength was set at 538 nm.

Preparation of quantum-dot-based (QD-based) fluorescent labels

The QD-antibody fluorescent labels were prepared as reported [41]. To obtain the quantum-dot-dibenzocyclooctyne (QD-DBCO), 10 µL of 1 µM QD-NH2 solution was mixed with 1 µL of 2 mM DBCOPEG4-NHS ester for 1 h at 37 °C. Then, an excessive DBCOPEG4-NHS ester was removed with 0.5 mL centrifugal filters (100 K). Meanwhile, to obtain the protein/antibody-azide, 10 µL of 1 µM antibody solution was mixed with 1 µL of 100 µM azido-PEG4-NHS ester at 37 °C for 1 h. As mentioned above, excessive azido-PEG4-NHS ester was also removed with centrifugal filters (10 K). Finally, the purified QD-DBCO and the purified antibody − azido were mixed in a ratio of 1:1 at room temperature for 1 h. The synthesized QD-antibody labels were kept at 4 °C, and their reactivity can be preserved for 1 month.

Manipulation of target single-cell samples

Target single-cell samples were manipulated using the auto-SiCS system, which is updated based on the previously reported bubble-assisted single-cell sampling technique [42]. Briefly, at first, the tip of the sampling capillary was adjusted to aim at the target single cell with specific fluorescent labels. Then, a small amount of air is expelled from the capillary to form a bubble at the tip, and the bubble is used to adhere to the target single cell. After that, the bubble was immediately sucked back, and then the target cell will be sucked into the capillary along with both the bubble and solvent to complete the sampling process. The single-cell sampling was accomplished under the visualization conditions throughout the entire process, and the accompanying liquid volume of the target single cell was obtained in a single pass, controlled by observing the height of the gas-liquid plane. After sampling, single-cell samples were rapidly transferred under direct vision to the designated sample site on the target plate.

Number counting of biomarkers in single CD8+T cells was performed according to our previous work [41, 42]. Briefly, counting the number of BLIMP-1 in single T cells as an example, the anti-BLIMP-1-functionalized coverslips covered with perfluoropolyether (PFPE) oil and quantum-dot-anti-BLIMP-1 fluorescent labels were prepared in advance. Then, the single-CD8+T-cell sampling and single-cell lysis in situ procedures were performed successively. After BLIMP-1 from target single cells was bound to the functionalized coverslip, QD-anti-BLIMP-1 fluorescence labeling was implemented. After all these, under-oil-layer washing of excessive fluorescent labels was accomplished, and the coverslips were sealed and ready for further number counting.

Single-cell fluorescence imaging and data analysis

The bright field and dark field fluorescent images were taken on the Leica DMI3000b microscope by the iXon Ultra 897 Electron-Multiplying Charge-Coupled Device (EMCCD) camera at room temperature (25 ℃). The image processing procedure was performed with the Fiji program as reported [41, 42].

The synthesis of FeNPs

As previously reported, with a slight modification, 1.35 g of FeCl3·6H2O was dissolved in 75 mL of ethylene glycol. Following this, 3.60 g of sodium acetate was added, and the mixture was stirred for 30 min and sonicated for 5 min. The reaction was then conducted for 16 h at 200 ℃ in the reactor to produce magnetic core nanoparticles.

FeNPs assisted LDI-MS analysis

Immediately after sampling, the microchip was submerged in liquid nitrogen for 10 min to quench metabolic activity, followed by thawing at room temperature to facilitate cell lysis. Once returned to room temperature, the microchip was loaded with the FeNPs to form a co-crystallization condition. For the LDI-MS process. The samples were analyzed on a MALDI-TOF MS (UlrafleXtreme MALDI-TOF/TOF MS) in a positive mode. The intensity of the laser was set to 75% and the molecular weight from 80 to 500 Da was recorded.

Statistical analysis

Raw data analysis was conducted using Flexanalysis 3.4 software. Subsequently, data preprocessing—including data import, background removal, signal smoothing, feature peak alignment, peak extraction, and normalization—was performed using the MALDIquant package in R. Results are expressed as [mean ± SD]. t-SNE and training of machine learning algorithms were performed with Orange Data Mining software. The analysis for Fig. 3D used a perplexity of 50, a Euclidean distance metric, and was initialized with 30 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) components to ensure a stable and reproducible result. The analysis for Fig. 5B used a perplexity of 40, a Euclidean distance metric, and was initialized with 40 Principal Component Analysis (PCA) components to ensure a stable and reproducible result. The t-tests and Chi-square tests were carried out using SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). Additionally, Pearson’s correlation analysis and metabolic pathway analysis were performed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0 (https://www.metaboanalyst.ca/). The neural network is a Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP) model with backpropagation, implemented using scikit-learn. The network had a single hidden layer with 100 neurons, ReLU activation, and L2 regularization (Alpha = 0.0001). The model was trained with the Adam optimizer for 200 iterations.
