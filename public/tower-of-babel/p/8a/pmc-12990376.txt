Promotion of breast cancer by the DNPH1 enzyme
Authors: Sangphil Oh, Yuan Sui, Sook Shin, Ralf Janknecht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTo broaden understanding of breast cancer pathophysiology, we investigated the highly understudied DNPH1 enzyme in this disease. We found that DNPH1 is overexpressed in breast and also in prostate and lung tumors, correlating with disease aggressiveness. DNPH1 downregulation diminished the oncogenic potential of breast cancer cells and compromised respective stem cells. Likewise, DNPH1 ablation decreased the oncogenicity of lung cancer cells. Knockout of Dnph1 in mice revealed that DNPH1 is not essential for development, yet crucial for HER2-mediated mammary tumor formation. Notably, Dnph1 knockout reduced angiogenesis in HER2-induced mammary tumors and elevated AMP levels. This was accompanied by increased AMP-activated protein kinase activity and phosphorylation of YAP1, implying tapered activity of the YAP1 oncoprotein. These data have extensively shed light onto the cellular and physiological functions of the enigmatic DNPH1 protein in cancer. Overall, this highlights that DNPH1 is a promising target for cancer therapy.Subject areas: Cell biology, Cancer

Introduction

Breast cancer is the second most frequently diagnosed cancer in the world. About 2.3 million persons are expected to be diagnosed with this malady in 2026, and approximately 0.67 million women will die of this disease.1,2 In the United States of America alone, the annual incidence of breast cancer is 0.32 million cases, and more than forty thousand patients will succumb to this malignancy.3 The numbers of diagnosed women and those perishing from breast cancer are projected to rise by 40%–50% over the next 15 years, and particularly countries with a low human development index will bear the major brunt of those increases.4 These facts underline the urgent need to improve early detection as well as to uncover new avenues of therapeutic intervention for breast cancer, which may arise through a better molecular understanding of this debilitating and often fatal disease.

2′-Deoxynucleoside 5′-phosphate N-hydrolase 1 (DNPH1, previously also called RCL) is a predominantly cytoplasmic enzyme that cleaves the N-glycosidic bond of selected 2′-deoxyribonucleoside 5′-monophosphates (dNMPs) and may be upregulated in human breast tumors.5,6,7 Genetic evidence suggested that high DNPH1 expression might be associated with increased breast cancer risk, yet no causal relationship has been established.8,9,10 Moreover, ectopic DNPH1 expression induced, albeit very inefficiently, anchorage-independent growth of Rat1A fibroblasts,11 but failed to elicit tumor formation in corresponding mouse xenograft experiments.12 These data imply that DNPH1 is not a bona fide oncoprotein, yet could still be a promoter of breast tumorigenesis.

To resolve the role of the vastly understudied DNPH1 enzyme in breast cancer, we analyzed its expression in human breast tumors, studied its role in human and mouse mammary tumor cell lines, created a Dnph1 knockout mouse model to test its tumorigenic function in an autochthonous way, and uncovered molecular changes upon DNPH1 ablation. Collectively, our results highlight that DNPH1 is a promoter of breast cancer, thereby nominating DNPH1 as a potential target for tumor therapy.

Results

DNPH1 overexpression in breast cancer

Previously, a low number of human tissue samples of undefined breast tumor subtype were examined for DNPH1 mRNA levels, and the results suggested that DNPH1 transcription might be upregulated in breast cancer.7 To substantiate this, we bioinformatically analyzed publicly available databases13,14 and found that DNPH1 mRNA was significantly upregulated in breast tumors independent of HER2 and ER status, with triple-negative (HER2-, ER−, PR−) tumors displaying the highest average DNPH1 levels (Figures 1A, S1A, and S1B). Notably, high DNPH1 mRNA levels were associated with decreased survival of patients with breast cancer (Figures 1B, S1C, and S1D). Consistently, DNPH1 mRNA levels positively correlated with higher disease grade (Figure S1E) and increased incidence of metastasis (Figures S1F and S1G).

To complement the above results about DNPH1 mRNA expression in breast cancer, we probed for the presence of the human DNPH1 protein. Compared to non-cancer, immortalized MCF10A breast cells, DNPH1 overexpression was noted in many human breast cancer cell lines (Figure 1C); however, in this selection of 11 different breast cancer cell lines, DNPH1 protein was not most highly expressed in the triple-negative ones. Staining of matching normal and cancerous breast tissue substantiated that the DNPH1 protein was also overexpressed in the vast majority of tumors (Figures 1D and 1E) and even more so in metastases (Figure S1H). We conclude that DNPH1 is robustly overexpressed at the mRNA and protein level in breast tumors and correlates with an adverse prognosis, which is consistent with a potential role of DNPH1 as a tumor promoter.

Role of DNPH1 in breast cancer cell lines

Next, we stably downregulated DNPH1 with shRNA in 4T1 mouse mammary carcinoma cells, an often-used model for triple-negative breast cancer that also allows allografting into immunocompetent, syngeneic BALB/c mice. There was no change in in vitro cell growth or clonogenic activity, but the ability to invade through Matrigel became significantly reduced upon DNPH1 downregulation (Figures 2A–2D). In contrast to in vitro cell growth, DNPH1-downregulated 4T1 cells were compromised in growing tumors when injected into the mammary fat pad of BALB/c mice (Figures 2E and 2F). Similar to 4T1 cells, DNPH1 knockdown did not affect the in vitro growth and clonogenic activity of human SKBR3 breast carcinoma cells, but impaired their ability to invade in vitro as well as grow tumors in immunocompromised mice (Figure S2). Collectively, these data indicate that DNPH1 can promote tumor growth in vivo, while its ability to enhance cell invasion in vitro may additionally point to a pro-metastasis function.

We also expressed ectopic DNPH1 in 4T1 cells, thus allowing us to evaluate the impact of DNPH1 overexpression in the same cell line as utilized for DNPH1 downregulation in Figure 2. For this, we ectopically expressed either wild-type human DNPH1 or its S128A mutant that displays impaired catalytic activity15,16; please note that we did not overexpress mouse DNPH1, since we were interested in human breast cancer and the role of human DNPH1 therein, yet mouse and human DNPH1 are highly homologous with over 72% identity at the amino acid level and thus likely equivalent. We observed that neither wild-type nor mutated DNPH1 affected in vitro cell growth, clonogenic activity, or cell invasion (Figures 3A–3D), but the S128A mutant acted in a dominant-negative manner by compromising tumor growth in BALB/c mice (Figure 3E). Likewise, wild-type or S128A DNPH1 overexpression did not affect the in vitro growth of human MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (Figures S3A and S3B). However, wild-type DNPH1, but not the S128A mutant, was capable of enhancing in vitro MDA-MB-231 cell invasion (Figure S3C), which is different from our results with 4T1 cells, possibly because 4T1 cells may already express sufficiently high levels of endogenous DNPH1. Regardless, these data further support the notion that DNPH1 stimulates cellular invasion and additionally suggest that DNPH1 catalytic activity plays an important role in its ability to further tumorigenesis.

Breast cancer cell lines display heterogeneity that can manifest in the variable expression of surface markers, as detectable through flow cytometry. We noted that the proportion of 4T1 mouse mammary tumor cells displaying markers indicative of stem cell-like/tumor-initiating cells, CD44high/CD24high or EpCAM+/CD44high/CD24high, became reduced upon DNPH1 ablation (Figures 4A–4C); please note that for human stem cells, CD44high/CD24low is an accepted marker, while both CD44high/CD24high and CD44high/CD24low seem to mark mouse mammary tumor stem cells17,18 and consequently, we also found the reduction of respective CD24low cells upon DNPH1 ablation in 4T1 cells (Figures S4A and S4B). Stemness of cancer cells is often associated with epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, which is reflected by the enhanced expression of mesenchymal markers such as the transcription factors TWIST and ZEB2.19,20 Accordingly, we observed that reduced stemness upon DNPH1 ablation was accompanied by lower TWIST and ZEB2 levels (Figure 4D).

Furthermore, the ability to form non-adherent mammospheres, which is considered another litmus test for stem cell-like/tumor-initiating cells,21 was compromised upon DNPH1 knockout (Figures 4E and 4F). Accordingly, DNPH1 downregulation reduced the ability of 4T1 cells to initiate the formation of mammary tumors when fewer than 104 cells were implanted into the mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice (Figure 4G). Lastly, we found that DNPH1 overexpression, but not of its S128A mutant, stimulated mammosphere formation in human MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells (Figure S4C), while knockout of DNPH1 in human MCF7 breast cancer cells had the opposite effect (Figures S4D and S4E). Altogether, these data imply that DNPH1 may drive the genesis of breast tumors through the stimulation of stem cell-like/tumor-initiating cells.

Analysis of Dnph1 knockout mice

To examine the function of Dnph1 at the organismal level, we generated respective knockout mice. Dnph1 exons 2 and 3, as well as part of exon 4, which together encode for the majority of the Dnph1 open reading frame, were deleted, and accordingly, no more DNPH1 protein was observable in homozygous knockout cells (Figures S5A and S5B). We did not observe any gross changes in Dnph1−/− mice, and they were born at expected Mendelian frequency when mating heterozygous knockouts (Figure S5C), indicating that Dnph1 is neither essential during embryonic development nor postnatally.

The DNPH1 gene has been found to be a target of the ETV1 transcription factor,7 which can be activated by HER2-triggered posttranslational modifications22,23,24,25,26 and whose mRNA levels become reportedly elevated in mouse mammary tumors induced by HER2 overexpression.27 This may explain why DNPH1 mRNA is upregulated in human HER2+ breast tumors (see Figures 1A, S1A, and S1B) and instigated our analysis of Dnph1 function in MMTV-Her2 transgenic mice, a model for metastatic HER2+ breast cancer.28 Thus, we crossed Dnph1 knockout mice with MMTV-Her2 transgenic mice and established MMTV-Her2/Dnph1 compound mice (Figure S5D). As expected from a previous study,27 we observed Etv1 upregulation in Her2+ mammary tumors and, additionally, we found Dnph1 upregulation in respective HER2-triggered mammary tumors (Figure S5E), strengthening the notion that DNPH1 could be involved in their genesis.

To examine this, we compared tumor formation in MMTV-Her2/Dnph1+/+ mice to MMTV-Her2/Dnph1−/− mice. Dnph1 ablation delayed tumor onset, albeit this was not statistically significant (Figure 5A). But the average number of mammary tumors became significantly decreased by ∼2-fold at 10–12 months of age (Figure 5B). Importantly, the number of lung metastases per mouse was also significantly reduced upon Dnph1 knockout (Figure 5C), with an overall drop in lung metastasis incidence from 47% down to 7.7% at 10–12 months of age (Figure 5D). Staining for the proliferation marker Ki-67 was also reduced upon Dnph1 knockout (Figures 5E and 5F). Together with our above presented data on HER2+ SKBR3 human breast cancer cells (see Figure S2), these results strongly support that DNPH1 is capable of promoting HER2-mediated breast cancer formation.

DNPH1 was originally identified as a target gene of the MYC oncoprotein.11,29 Consistently, we found that MYC and DNPH1 transcript levels were positively correlated in human invasive breast carcinomas (Figure S6A), suggesting that DNPH1 may also be a promoter of MYC-mediated breast tumorigenesis. To test this, we utilized MMTV-Myc transgenic mice that develop metastatic breast cancer.30 However, we did not find any significant difference in tumor-free survival, number of mammary tumors or lung metastases per mouse, or incidence of lung metastasis when comparing MMTV-Myc/Dnph1+/+ to MMTV-Myc/Dnph1−/− mice (Figures S6B–S6E). These data suggest that DNPH1 is likely not relevant for MYC-triggered breast tumorigenesis.

Altered angiogenesis and AMP levels upon Dnph1 knockout

The cleavage of dNMPs by DNPH1 produces 2-deoxyribose 5-phosphate,5 which is readily dephosphorylated within cells to 2-deoxyribose that may be secreted and then act as an angiogenic factor.31,32,33,34,35,36,37 Hence, it was suggested that DNPH1 might stimulate angiogenesis.5 And indeed, immunohistochemical staining for CD31, a marker of blood vessels, revealed that vessel density became significantly reduced in age- and weight-matched mammary tumors isolated from MMTV-Her2/Dnph1−/− compared to MMTV-Her2/Dnph1+/+ mice (Figures 6A and 6B). Similarly, analysis of tumors not matched for age or weight showed a reduction of vessel density upon Dnph1 knockout regardless of tumor weight (Figure 6C). As such, the reduction of angiogenesis may be one way in which DNPH1 deficiency curtails tumor development.

To glean more insights into the action of DNPH1, we performed a mass spectrometric metabolite screen on mammary tumors isolated from MMTV-Her2/Dnph1 compound mice. 50 out of 596 recognized biochemicals were significantly different upon Dnph1 ablation (Figure S7A). Strikingly, AMP, GMP, and UMP levels (CMP was not detected in our analysis) became increased upon Dnph1 ablation in these mammary tumors (Figure 6D), while no change in dAMP or dCMP was detectable (Figure S7B). Moreover, there was no change in glucose, pyruvate, lactate, or glutamine (Figure S7C), suggesting that Dnph1 ablation does not deprive cells of those central metabolites. In parallel with the observed changes in NMPs, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis revealed that several pathways related to nucleotides became significantly altered upon Dnph1 ablation (Figure S7D). We also performed bulk RNA sequencing on mammary tumors isolated from MMTV-Her2/Dnph1 compound mice, but did not find changes in nucleotide pathways upon Dnph1 knockout (Figure S8).

The observed 4-fold increase of AMP in Dnph1 knockout tumors predicted that the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a master metabolic sensor,38 would become stimulated. And indeed, we observed by immunohistochemical staining that AMPKα phosphorylation on T172 (an indicator for AMPK activation) became enhanced upon Dnph1 ablation (Figure 6E). Furthermore, AMPK is known to directly or indirectly affect the phosphorylation of the YAP1 oncoprotein, thereby diminishing its oncogenic potential.39,40,41 Accordingly, we found by immunohistochemical staining that YAP1 phosphorylation was also increased upon Dnph1 ablation (Figure 6F). This suggests that DNPH1 can normally restrain AMPK activity and thereby enhance YAP1 activity, which would promote tumorigenesis.

Discussion

In this report, we have firmly established that DNPH1 is overexpressed in breast tumors and potentially predicts aggressive disease. This is consistent with three recent reports that genetically associated high DNPH1 expression with increased breast cancer risk.8,9,10 Arguably more important, our data obtained with DNPH1-modulated breast cancer cells and Dnph1 knockout mice provided compelling experimental evidence that DNPH1 is required for efficient breast tumorigenesis. As such, a pathophysiological function of DNPH1 has been proven at both the cellular and organismal levels. In addition, our creation of Dnph1 knockout mice facilitated the revelation that DNPH1 is – at least in mice – not an essential protein, which implies DNPH1 inhibitors to be potential chemodrugs with minimal side effects.

DNPH1 appeared to be unnecessary for the growth of breast cancer cells in vitro, yet growth in mammary fat pads was dependent on the presence of DNPH1. No less than in part, this discrepancy may be explained by the fact that DNPH1 was required for efficient angiogenesis in the MMTV-Her2 mammary tumor model, whereas angiogenesis is obviously irrelevant for in vitro cell growth. Also, we observed that metastasis in MMTV-Her2 mice became reduced upon Dnph1 knockout, and consistently we observed that DNPH1 was a promoter of in vitro cell invasion that is involved in the spread of tumor cells away from the primary site, suggesting another way how DNPH1 fosters breast cancer (Figure 7). Since metastasis is the predominant cause of death for breast cancer, this might explain why DNPH1 overexpression correlated with lower patient survival. Further, the number of mammary tumors arising in MMTV-Her2 mice was decreased by half upon Dnph1 knockout, suggesting that DNPH1 plays an important role in tumor initiation; this would be consistent with DNPH1 stimulating stem cell-like cells that are crucial for tumor initiation (Figure 7). In line with this notion, we found that DNPH1 ablation in breast cancer cells reduced the abundance of stem cell-like/tumor-initiating cells and also compromised the ability to form non-adherent mammospheres, which are considered to be enriched in stem cells.21 Notably, Dnph1 transcripts were also enriched in murine mammospheres and embryonic stem cells according to reported datasets.42 And importantly, DNPH1 ablation also reduced tumor initiation when fewer than 104 4T1 cells were allografted in a limiting dilution assay, one of the gold standards for the detection of stem cell-like/tumor-initiating cells.

Model how DNPH1 promotes breast tumor development and progression

This model is centered around a DNPH1-YAP1 axis, yet DNPH1 may utilize to-be-identified effectors other than YAP1 to stimulate, e.g., stem cells or angiogenesis.

Although DNPH1 was overexpressed in various subtypes of breast cancer, this does not necessarily mean that DNPH1 is a promoter of breast cancer in all of these subtypes. Clearly, our data with MMTV-Her2 mice and the HER2+ SKBR3 human breast cancer cells strongly suggest a tumor-promoting role for DNPH1 in HER2+ breast cancer; and our data with 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 cells imply that DNPH1 is also important in triple-negative breast cancer. However, the fact that Dnph1 knockout did not affect mammary tumorigenesis in MMTV-Myc transgenic mice implies that DNPH1 is not universally required for efficient breast tumorigenesis. Future studies should comprehensively delineate which breast cancer subtypes are susceptible to DNPH1 levels for their development.

The production of 2-deoxyribose 5-phosphate upon DNPH1-mediated cleavage of dNMPs was suggested to be a mechanism by which DNPH1 may enhance angiogenesis through increasing levels of its dephosphorylation product, 2-deoxyribose.5 However, DNPH1 does not efficiently cleave canonical dNMPs (dAMP, dGMP, dCMP, dTMP), but rather the rare 5-hydroxymethyl-2′-deoxyuridine 5′-monophosphate (hmdUMP) and, with a lesser efficiency, also 5-hydroxymethyl-2′-deoxycytidine 5′-monophosphate (hmdCMP).6,43 Most of the cellular hmdUMP seems to arise through the deamination of hmdCMP,6 which is generated as a degradation product of DNA being hydroxymethylated on cytosine residues that typically make up <0.1% of nucleotides within DNA.44 As such, hmdCMP (and hmdUMP) levels are expected to be at least 1,000-fold lower than the concentrations of canonical dNMPs (and that is likely a great underestimation, since canonical dNMPs arise primarily through anabolic pathways and not through DNA degradation) that are on average < 20 μM within cells.45 Even when contemplating, in extremis, that DNPH1 would cleave all hmdCMP/hmdUMP, this suggests that 2-deoxyribose levels would not be enhanced by more than 20 nM within cells, and even less so in the tumor microenvironment or circulation after the secretion of a fraction of this intracellular 2-deoxyribose. Such small changes in extracellular 2-deoxyribose levels will not result into more angiogenesis, since reported effective pro-angiogenic concentrations of extracellular 2-deoxyribose lie within 1–100 μM.33,37,46,47 Accordingly, it is not plausible that the generation of 2-deoxyribose is relevant for DNPH1’s pro-angiogenic function.

Interestingly, we observed that AMP levels were elevated by 4-fold in MMTV-Her2/Dnph1−/− mice compared to MMTV-Her2/Dnph1+/+ mice. One could reason that DNPH1 cleaves dAMP and thus impedes the reversible conversion of dAMP into AMP through the action of adenylate kinases48; following this logic, Dnph1 knockout would lead to elevated dAMP levels and thus also cause an increase in the AMP concentration. But again, this contradicts the recent finding that dAMP is not a relevant substrate for DNPH1 in vivo.6,43 It also does not chime with the fact that dNMP levels are in general about 10-fold less than NMP levels in cells,45 indicating that even if all available dAMP were converted into AMP, this would result at most in an only 10% increase in AMP levels. Regardless, we observed upon Dnph1 knockout a 4-fold increase of AMP levels that can greatly stimulate AMPK activity.38 Accordingly, autophosphorylation of AMPKα on T172 became enhanced upon Dnph1 ablation. Although AMPK can both suppress and promote tumorigenesis depending on the cancer being studied,49 the prevailing evidence supports the notion that AMPK opposes breast cancer development.50 Hence, DNPH1 may normally suppress AMPK activity by keeping AMP levels low and thereby stimulating breast tumorigenesis.

AMPK can directly phosphorylate YAP1 or induce LATS1/LATS2-mediated phosphorylation of YAP1.39,40,41 YAP1 phosphorylation inhibits its activity51 and we observed increased YAP1 phosphorylation in MMTV-Her2/Dnph1−/− mammary tumors. Given that YAP1 is oncogenic in the breast,52,53 this indicates that DNPH1 may promote breast cancer through suppressing the AMPK-dependent phosphorylation of YAP1. In fact, YAP1, similar to our results for DNPH1, can stimulate various aspects of breast cancer development, including tumor initiation, growth, vascularization, and metastasis (Figure 7).54,55,56 However, YAP1 is not the only client protein of AMPK, and hence the phosphorylation of other substrates may also contribute to AMPK’s ability to suppress breast cancer. This could include the phosphorylation of HIF1A, which may inhibit angiogenesis,57 or phosphorylation of SNAI1 that can compromise metastasis and stem cell characteristics.58 Similarly, the phosphorylation of TSC2 and RAPTOR by AMPK leads to the inhibition of mTOR, which can suppress metabolic pathways important for tumorigenesis.59,60

The levels of 50 metabolites were significantly changed upon Dnph1 ablation in MMTV-Her2-dependent mouse mammary tumors. Thus, DNPH1 may not only act through affecting the concentration of AMP, but possibly also through other metabolites. One such metabolite could be NAD+, whose concentration was increased in Dnph1 knockout tumors (see Figure S7E). The ratio of NAD+ to NADH is key to the redox regulation of cells, and often this ratio becomes enhanced in cancer cells.61 Unfortunately, our metabolomic analysis did not detect NADH, so it remains unclear if the NAD+ to NADH ratio would have changed. Activation of AMPK has been shown to lead to an increase in the NAD+/NADH ratio,62 which may explain why NAD+ levels became elevated in Dnph1−/− mammary tumors. NAD+ also has redox-independent functions by being a substrate for enzymes, including the sirtuins and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases.63 Whether the observed 1.7-fold increase in NAD+ levels would alter the activity of those enzymes in a meaningful way and how that might affect tumorigenesis should be studied in the future.

Our RNA sequencing data imply that Dnph1 knockout in MMTV-Her2 mice could also affect mammary tumorigenesis through other mechanisms. For instance, Dnph1 ablation seemingly compromised DAP12 signaling and DAP12 interactions (see Figure S8B). DAP12 is an adaptor protein that influences cytokine production and macrophage activation, but has also been observed to be overexpressed in breast cancer cells, positively correlates with metastasis, and predicts poor survival.64 How DNPH1 ablation would lead to reduced DAP12 signaling and thereby compromise tumorigenesis is another interesting future avenue of research. In addition, knocking out Dnph1 led especially to the activation of pathways related to collagen and the extracellular matrix (see Figure S8B). Although increased collagen formation may suppress metastasis, as observed upon Dnph1 knockout in MMTV-Her2 mice, collagens can also have pro-tumorigenic functions65,66 and thus the biological significance of a DNPH1-collagen relation remains tenuous without further studies.

While we have focused here on the role of DNPH1 in breast cancer, DNPH1 may also impact other cancers. For instance, bioinformatic analyses revealed that DNPH1 mRNA was upregulated in prostate cancer, with higher DNPH1 levels being associated with more metastasis and less survival, and DNPH1 protein was also overexpressed in prostate cancer cell lines and tissues (Figures S9A–S9E). Similarly, DNPH1 mRNA was upregulated in lung cancer and higher levels were associated with reduced survival of patients with lung cancer (Figures S9F–S9J). And we confirmed that DNPH1 was also overexpressed at the protein level in both small and non-small cell lung cancer (Figures S9K–S9L). In addition, downregulation of DNPH1 in murine LLC1 Lewis lung carcinoma cells had the same consequences as in 4T1 mammary carcinoma cells: no change in in vitro cell growth, but the reduction of tumor growth after implantation into respective syngeneic mice (Figure S10). Hence, DNPH1’s role as a tumor promoter is likely not limited to breast cancer.

In conclusion, this report has substantially enhanced our knowledge about the in vivo functions of DNPH1, whose physiological role has so far remained a mystery, and firmly established DNPH1 as a promoter of breast tumorigenesis. A corollary of our studies is that DNPH1 presents itself as a potential target for therapy of breast and other, including prostate and lung, cancers. And the fact that Dnph1 knockout mice show no obvious phenotype suggests that DNPH1 inhibitors would have little to no side effects, a highly desirable trait for any anti-cancer drug.

Limitations of the study

One limitation of our study is the use of whole-body Dnph1 knockout mice, which confounds the determination in how far the effects observed upon Dnph1 ablation were attributable to the cancer cells and/or to the tumor microenvironment. Although we found that Dnph1 knockout results in reduced mammary tumor angiogenesis and enhanced AMP levels, we did not provide a complete molecular mechanism of how DNPH1 impacts endothelial cells and intracellular AMP levels. In addition, we did not clarify if the modulation of angiogenesis or AMP levels would be seminal for the oncogenic function of DNPH1. Also, while some of our results indicate that catalytic activity of DNPH1 is required for its function, we did not comprehensively determine the degree to which catalytic activity of DNPH1 is necessary for its oncogenic function. In this regard, the creation of a mouse model allowing for the conditional knock-in of catalytically impaired Dnph1 would tremendously help to illuminate this in autochthonous models of breast cancer. Lastly, while our study showed that DNPH1 is important for HER2-driven mammary tumorigenesis in mice, there has been no analysis of autochthonous mouse models for ER+ or triple-negative breast cancer, limiting the significance of our data obtained with MCF7 (ER+) and 4T1 or MDA-MB-231 (triple-negative) cells.

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

Animal experiments conducted at the University of Oklahoma Health Campus were approved by the respective Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval numbers 16-006, 18-089, 21-055). Further, work with mice and their care were in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. Mouse genotypes were confirmed by PCR analysis from DNA extracted from tails as detailed below.

Method details

Immunohistochemistry

Human breast tissue microarray A312 encompassed 20 breast tumor cores (in duplicate) and 20 matching normal tissue cores, while human prostate cancer tissue microarray A302 encompassed 31 prostate tumor cores (in duplicate) and 31 matching normal tissue cores. Staining was performed with a Leica BOND-III machine employing a 20 min treatment with Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution 1 and rabbit polyclonal DNPH1 antibodies (Sigma-Aldrich) at a 1:200 dilution, followed by counterstaining with hematoxylin.67 DNPH1 staining intensity was electronically measured as described.68 If there were duplicate tissue cores, the respective staining intensity was specified as the average of the two individual staining intensities. For LC10010d small cell cancer and LC10011b non-small cell carcinoma lung tissue microarrays, slides were stained with DNPH1 antibodies as above. Likewise, paraffin-embedded sections of mouse tumors were stained with antibodies for CD31 (1:50 dilution; 20 min antigen retrieval), Ki-67 (1:100 dilution; 20 min antigen retrieval), AMPKα phosphorylated on threonine 172 (1:100 dilution; 40 min antigen retrieval), or YAP1 phosphorylated on serine 127 (1:100 dilution; 40 min antigen retrieval).

Production and purification of DNPH1 antibodies

The peptide CLFRPQSGRVLSAMIRGAADGSRFQ, which corresponds to human DNPH1 amino acids 117-141, was coupled to keyhole limpet hemocyanin (KLH) and utilized to immunize rabbits at Cocalico Biologicals (Reamstown, Pennsylvania, USA). Resulting antibodies were affinity purified according to the recommendations of the manufacturer (BioRad) on Affi-Gel 10 resin, to which the above peptide was chemically coupled. One of these DNPH1 antibodies (RCL#1471) was used at a 1:500 - 1:1,000 dilution for Western blotting.

Western blotting

Proteins were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate gel electrophoresis69 and then subjected to blotting onto PVDF membranes.70 These were incubated with indicated rabbit antibodies,71 while horseradish peroxidase-coupled anti-rabbit antibodies were employed as secondary antibodies.72 This was followed by enhanced chemiluminescence73 and visualization through either exposure to film or the LI-COR Odyssey XF imaging system.74

Cell culture and virus production

Cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). They were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.75 For retro-/lentivirus production, respective plasmids were transiently transfected by either the calcium phosphate coprecipitation method76 or with the help of polyethylenimine77 into 293T cells that were seeded onto poly-L-lysine-coated plates.78 Virus was purified and then utilized for infection as described.79,80 For RNA interference, 21-nucleotide-long shRNAs were embedded into the miR30 gene81 and cloned into a lentiviral vector. For CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockout, 20-nucleotide-long sgRNAs were cloned into lentiCRISPRv2. Sequences being targeted by the utilized shRNAs and sgRNAs are listed in Table S1.

Cell growth, clonogenic and invasion assays

Cells were seeded into 24- or 96-wells and then grown for indicated times,82 after which the PrestoBlue cell viability kit was employed to measure cell growth.83 The clonogenic assay was performed by seeding cells into 6-wells and staining with crystal violet once visible colonies appeared.84 For in vitro invasion, cells were seeded on top of 8 μm chambers with Matrigel coating85 and cells traversing to the bottom of the filter chambers were stained and counted.86

Cell grafting into mice

These mouse experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval numbers 16-006, 18-089, 21-055) at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center and respective mouse work and care were in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. Cells were injected into female mice under isoflurane anesthesia.87 Specifically, 5x105 4T1 cells in 100 μl HBSS were injected into the 4th mammary fat pad of ∼10 weeks-old female BALB/cJ mice; alternatively, to determine the tumor-initiating frequency, 102-104 4T1 cells in 100 μl of 1:1 PBS/Matrigel were injected and mammary tumor formation assayed 15 days later; for SKBR3 cells, 5x105 cells in 100 μl of a 1:1 PBS/Matrigel mixture were injected into the 4th mammary fat pad of ∼9 weeks-old female Foxn1nu/Foxn1nu outbred athymic mice; and for LLC1 Lewis lung carcinoma cells, 5x105 cells in 100 μl HBSS were injected subcutaneously into ∼8 weeks-old female C57BL/6J mice. Tumor length and width were measured with calipers and tumor volume calculated as 0.5 x length x width x width.

Flow cytometry and mammosphere assay

4T1 cells were seeded onto 6-well plates at a density of 105 cells/well. Two days afterwards, cells were trypsinized, pushed 3-times through a 25G needle, resuspended in 100 μl PBS/2% fetal bovine serum and then stained for 30 min on ice with CD44-PE (1:100 dilution), CD24-FITC (1:100 dilution) and EpCAM-APC (1:200 dilution) antibodies. Stained cells were then analyzed with a FACSCalibur flow cytometry system (BD Biosciences). FlowJo software was utilized for analysis. For the mammosphere assay, 104 cells in 2 ml of DMEM/F-12 media supplemented with 1x B-27 supplement, 20 ng/ml human epidermal growth factor, 20 ng/ml basic fibroblast growth factor, 4 μg/ml heparin and 0.5% methylcellulose were seeded into ultra-low attachment 6-well plates. Seven days later, mammospheres were counted.

Generation of Dnph1 knockout mice

Linearized Dnph1 knockout vector was electroporated into 129/SvJ embryonic stem (ES) cells that were derived from male embryos. ES cells with a homologous recombination event were selected for by the presence of neomycin resistance (positive selection) and absence of thymidine kinase (negative selection). Genomic DNA was isolated and analyzed by Southern blotting for correct integration of the targeting cassette. Confirmed recombined ES cells were injected into C57BL/6 blastocysts and transferred into foster mothers. This work was done with the generous and highly appreciated help of the Mayo Clinic Rochester Knockout Core Facility, which had approval for the corresponding mouse work from the local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and followed institutional and national guidelines for the work with and care of mice.

Resultant male chimera, recognizable by patches of Agouti fur color, were then crossed to C57BL/6 female mice. Agouti-colored offspring from this cross were tested by PCR as described68,88 with primers neo-r2 (5′-CGAGATCAGCAGCCTCTGTTCCAC-3′; located at the end of the neo-cassette) and Rcl-9740-rev (5′-CTACACTGTAAGTACCATGATGGC-3′; located in intron 1 downstream of the neo-cassette), yielding a 300 bp product and indicating the presence of a hypomorphic Dnph1 allele. Positively testing offspring were further crossed to MMTV-Cre mice. Resulting female offspring from this cross, which would express Cre recombinase at high levels in their ovaries, were bred to C57BL/6 male mice. Resulting pups were tested by PCR using primers neo-r2 and Rcl-test-3-rev (5′-TAGTTCCCAGCACCCACATGGAGG-3′; located downstream of the loxP site in exon 4 of the targeting construct), which yielded a 253 bp product in the event of correct Cre-mediated excision. Confirmed Dnph1 knockout mice were then backcrossed for at least five generations with FVB/NJ mice.

Generation of MMTV-Her2/Dnph1 and MMTV-Myc/Dnph1 compound mice

Animal experiments described in this chapter were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval numbers 16-006, 18-089, 21-055) at the University of Oklahoma Health Sciences Center and respective mouse work and their care were in accordance with institutional and national guidelines. FVB mice containing the non-activated rat Her2/neu proto-oncogene under the control of the MMTV (mouse mammary tumor virus) 3′-LTR promoter (MMTV-Her2 mice28; a kind gift from Dr. Sandra Gendler, Mayo Clinic Scottsdale) were crossed with Dnph1 knockout mice (which had been backcrossed for at least five generations onto an FVB/NJ background; see above). MMTV-Her2/Dnph1+/- mice were then intercrossed to set up littermate cohorts for analysis. Mice were genotyped for Dnph1 status by PCR with primers Rcl-9547-for (5′-GGAAACAGGCTGTCATAAACGCAC-3′; located in intron 1 upstream of the neo-cassette), Rcl-9824-rev (5′-AGCCTGAGGATCATGGCTCATAGC-3′; located in intron 1 downstream of the neo-cassette, a region that got deleted in the knockout Dnph1 allele) and neo-f1 (5′-GCTACTTCCATTTGTCACGTCCTGC-3′; located at the beginning of the neo-cassette); a 278 bp product was indicative of the wild-type Dnph1 allele, and a 406 bp product for the knockout allele. Concurrently, PCR genotyping for Her2 was done with primers 5′-CAGGTGCAAGCACTATTGACC-3′ and 5′-CTCAGAGCTCAGATCAGAACC-3′, yielding a 559 bp fragment only in Her2-positive animals.89

FVB mice containing the mouse Myc proto-oncogene under the control of the MMTV promoter (MMTV-Myc mice30) were obtained from the NCI Mouse Repository. They were crossed with Dnph1 knockout mice (which had been backcrossed for at least five generations onto an FVB/NJ background; see above) to eventually obtain experimental animals. PCR genotyping for the Myc transgene was done with primers 5′-CCCAAGGCTTAAGTAAGTTTTTGG-3′ and 5′-GGGCATAAGCACAGATAAAACACT-3’ (825 bp amplified fragment in Myc-positive animals).

Determination of metabolite levels

Mammary tumors were dissected from 7-12 months old MMTV-Her2/Dnph1 compound mice (similar average tumor weights for wild-type and Dnph1 knockout groups) and frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were then sent on dry ice to Metabolon (Durham, North Carolina, USA), where they were analyzed using ultrahigh performance liquid chromatography – tandem mass spectrometry.

RNA sequencing

RNA was extracted from mouse mammary tumors (similar average tumor weights for wild-type and Dnph1 knockout groups; age range of 7-12 months) with Trizol90 and further purified with the RNeasy Mini kit.91 RNA sequencing was performed by Novogene (Sacramento, California, USA) and raw data deposited with the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) as BioProject PRJNA753535. Analysis of data was done as described.92,93

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

If not otherwise indicated in the figure legends, means with standard deviation have been depicted in the figure panels. The statistical tests used, n numbers and P values can be found in the figure panels or in the figure legends. Statistical significance was assessed with GraphPad Prism Version 6.0h with the exception for the overall test for differences in stem cell frequencies, which was executed through a website (https://bioinf.wehi.edu.au/software/elda/). A P value of less than 0.05 was deemed to be statistically significant. Abbreviations for statistical significance are: ns, not significant; ∗, P < 0.05; ∗∗, P < 0.01; ∗∗∗, P < 0.001; ∗∗∗∗, P < 0.0001.
