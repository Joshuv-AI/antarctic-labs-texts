Oral paraneoplastic manifestations and findings associated with extra-oral surgically treated malignancies: a prospective cross-sectional study of 300 cases
Authors: Veronika Liskova, Jan Liska, Nikoleta Molnarova, Lucie Nechutna, Ondrej Topolcan, Ladislav Pecen, Ilona Zednikova, Tomas Skalicky, Jiri Molacek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the prevalence of oral paraneoplastic manifestations in patients with biopsy-confirmed extra-oral malignancies and to identify oral findings with potential paraneoplastic significance that could serve as new targets for dental screening.MethodsA single-center prospective cross-sectional study was performed. A total of 300 consecutive surgical oncology patients (April 2024–September 2025) underwent standardized oral examinations one day prior to planned oncologic surgery. Extraoral and intraoral findings—including mucosal changes, perfusion abnormalities, tongue morphology, and candidal colonization—were evaluated by oral medicine specialists. Only primary extra-oral malignancies were included and categorized into upper gastrointestinal, colorectal, hepatopancreatic, lung, and breast cancer. Mycological cultures were obtained in all patients. Statistical assessment used Chi-square test, multivariate stepwise selection logistic regression models and univariate models of selected confounders (SAS software).ResultsClassical paraneoplastic oral syndromes (e.g., paraneoplastic pemphigus, acanthosis nigricans) were not detected. Oral candidiasis was uncommon and appeared mainly in patients with advanced disease, suggesting limited usefulness as an early indicator. In contrast, labial and lingual hypoperfusion occurred significantly more often in gastrointestinal cancers (p ≤ 0.0001, labial hypoperfusion was differentiated in 25.5% (24/94) of gastrointestinal malignancies). Patients with breast cancer showed a markedly higher prevalence of tongue enlargement and dental impressions (both p < 0.0001, with tongue enlargement detected in 51,3% (80/159) of cases), as well as lateral deviation during protrusion (p = 0.0152).ConclusionTraditional oral paraneoplastic manifestations were virtually absent in this cohort. However, simple clinical findings—particularly perfusion changes and tongue morphology—showed frequent tumor-specific associations and may merit further investigation for the verification as potential early paraneoplastic indicators. As a pilot study, the results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Following validation in large multicenter studies, these findings may bring a promising opportunity to strengthen the role of dentists in multidisciplinary prevention programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04317-3.Keywords: Oral cavity, Paraneoplastic process, Breast cancer, Gastrointestinal cancer, Tongue enlargement, Hypoperfusion, Dental screening

Background

According to recent global studies, there were nearly 20 million new cases of cancer in 2022 [1]. In the same year, 9.7 million patients died of cancer [1]. Globally, one in five people will be diagnosed with cancer during their lifetime, and one in nine men and one in twelve women will die from it [1]. Despite advances in medicine, cancer incidence projections indicate continued growth in the coming decade [2]. Prevention and early detection are essential to achieving a good prognosis in cancer [3].

Many preventive programs are active around the world [2, 4–6]. These initiatives rely heavily on existing clinical and epidemiological information. Progress has been made in many diagnostic fields [5]. Digitization, new examination methods, and the use of artificial intelligence technology have contributed to earlier and more accurate detection [4]. However, diagnostic use of systemic manifestations associated with malignancy has declined. These symptoms may have the characteristics of paraneoplastic manifestations [7]. A major barrier to their use is low clinical awareness of their signs among the professional public. There are many case reports of uncertain value and only a few comprehensive studies available on paraneoplastic processes. Approximately 8% of cancers present with paraneoplastic manifestations described in the literature [7, 8].

In 1976, dermatologist H.O. Curth established criteria for cutaneous paraneoplastic syndromes associated with underlying cancer [9]. The criteria are: (1) concurrent onset of both the neoplasm and the paraneoplastic manifestation; (2) Parallel clinical course, progressing and resolving together; (3) Specific type of skin disease in association with a particular type of cancer; (4) The skin conditions are not a part of genetic syndrome; (5) Strong and consistent association between the two processes [9]. Curth’s dermatological criteria are also applicable in other fields of medicine [9, 10]. Correct identification of paraneoplastic syndrome can reveal malignancy before it reaches an advanced stage [10]. Furthermore, this phenomenon can be used to monitor the success of therapy. Resolution of the tumor eliminates the associated process, and if the paraneoplastic syndrome reappears, it may indicate recurrence of malignancy [9, 10].

The most common paraneoplastic manifestations are endocrine syndromes, neurological syndromes, and skin, rheumatological, and hematological manifestations [8, 9]. Manifestations in the oral cavity are reported much less frequently [8]. However, dental manifestations may be the initial indication of ongoing malignancy, which can be diagnosed during regular dental examinations [10]. Some symptoms are not specific, and their cause is not usually attributed to the tumor. For example, activation of the herpes zoster virus is often thought to be caused by immunosuppression or stress rather than oncologic disease. Manifestations of peripheral neuromas (associated with multiple endocrine neoplasia, MEN) or paraneoplastic pemphigus (PNP, usually associated with hematological malignancies) are specific but rare [10–15]. In the past, PNP was recognized in our hospital as an early marker for the detection of tonsil carcinoma [10], an example of which is shown in Fig. 1A. Other symptoms often go unnoticed, or their cause is not fully resolved. Examination of the tongue coating, along with assessment of the structure, elasticity, or hypoperfusion of the tongue and lips is a key component of routine oral examination. Pathological findings in this region raise suspicion of avitaminosis, fungal infections, or anemia. These include, for example, atrophy of the posterior tongue, where oral candidiasis is usually suspected instead of anemia, which may be related to a tumor in the digestive tract [16, 17]. If there is no local cause of hypoperfusion (tissue scarring) and fungal culture results are negative, blood tests should be considered. A decrease in red blood cell counts or hemoglobin levels, along with a change in red blood cell volume, may indicate the cause of anemia. Figure 1B shows tongue atrophy in colorectal cancer.

There are significant gaps in the existing literature regarding the association between diagnosed cancer and the occurrence of paraneoplasia, and conditions in the oral cavity associated with malignant tumors that can be treated surgically are rarely described.

This study aims to evaluate the true prevalence of oral paraneoplastic symptoms and potentially tumor-associated manifestations in patients undergoing surgical oncology treatment. Our goal is to investigate associations between confirmed malignancies and changes in the oral mucosal and tissues, with the aim of identifying new targets for cancer screening in dental practice. This might increase the chance of early diagnosis and improve the prognosis of patients.

Methods

Between April 2024 and September 2025, 300 patients with extraoral malignancies hospitalized at the Surgical Clinic of University Hospital Pilsen were examined by dentists from the Department of Oral Medicine, Dentistry Clinic. These examinations were conducted as part of a standardized, protocol-based, single-center prospective cross-sectional study.

Race/ethnicity data

All patients examined in this study were of Caucasian ethnicity and recruited locally from Central and Eastern Europe. The ethnic homogeneity of the study cohort represents an inherent limitation of this single-center prospective study and may reduce the external validity of the findings.

Inclusion criteria

Cases with extra-oral malignancy confirmed by biopsy. Only primary malignant tumors. Only patients who signed the informed consent and were able to cooperate during the clinical examination of the oral cavity. Only cases with complete anamnestic data and available mycological culture results were included. Only malignant diagnoses represented by at least 5 cases were included to enable meaningful statistical evaluation.

Exclusion criteria

Cases without confirmation of malignancy. Metastases or recurrences of tumors. Patients without signed consent or unable to cooperate during clinical examination. Cases with incomplete anamnestic data or the result of mycological cultivation. Rare diagnoses with insufficient numbers for meaningful analysis.

Fifteen cases were excluded from this study because of a rare primary tumor combined with non-specific oral mucosal manifestations. The inclusion and exclusion of cases in the study are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Flowchart of inclusion and exclusion of cases in the study

Cases for the study were chosen from the surgical operating schedule of the Surgical Clinic, University Hospital Pilsen. Patients were always examined by one of four doctors from the Oral medicine department one day prior to the planned surgery. The reason for this was to perform the oral examination before preoperative procedures and the administration of indicated medications, or antibiotics. Two of the examiners were experienced certified doctors, and two were in specialist training. This study design reflects the feasibility of screening in real settings for dentists.

Clinical examinations were always performed after the patient had been informed and had signed an informed consent form. After reviewing the patient´s history and chronic medication, participants were asked about previous treatment, oncological duplicities, and performed hormonal, radio-, or chemotherapy. Given the pilot nature of this study, particular attention was paid to patient age, especially among those with breast cancer, in light of ongoing discussions regarding the optimal design of preventive programs for this malignancy. As patients undergoing oncological surgery were generally in good clinical condition, information on all current medications was recorded, with particular emphasis on drugs associated with hyposalivation or those with the potential to induce oral candidiasis through immunosuppression or microbial imbalance. The patients’ history of antibiotics, antimycotics and corticosteroid use was assessed. Smoking was evaluated based on current use and pack-years.

The clinical examination was performed and its results recorded using the schematics shown in Fig. 3.

Schematics for clinical assessment of oral mucosal manifestation

The extra-oral examination was focused on cutaneous acanthosis nigricans (thickened hyperpigmented skin in the axilla or neck region), Muir-Torre syndrome (sebaceous gland carcinoma), and Peutz-Jeghers syndrome (perioral and mucosal hyperpigmentation). Intraoral aspection and manual palpation are standard procedures for oral medicine specialists. Oral mucosa was examined for Cowden syndrome papillomatosis and the jaws were palpated for the presence of Gardner syndrome osteomas. All paraneoplastic syndromes with manifestation in the oral region were assessed. Each region of the oral mucosa and underlying tissue was examined by visual inspection for the presence of dystrophy, hyper-keratinization, or oral mucosal tumors. The tongue was assessed for its coating, and the palate for atrophy. Perfusion and hypertrophy or hypotrophy of the lips and tongue were assessed by bimanual palpation in order to evaluate tissue pallor and resistance. The intensity of palpation was standardized among individual examiners prior to the start of the study. The standard method for assessing hypoperfusion of the tongue and lip, which minimized inter-examiner bias, involved compressing the relaxed tissue to half its thickness. Tongue enlargement (macroglossia) was assessed relative to the inner border of the mandibular dentition when the relaxed tongue contacted the lower incisors. A schematic diagram of this examination is shown in Fig. 4. For this part of the clinical examination, edentulous patients were asked to wear their dentures. The tongue was tested for the presence of dental impressions (clinical indicator of tongue enlargement in dentate patients) and the ability to stick out directly or with a deviation. Variants such as lingua plicata or black hairy tongue were recorded. Teeth were tested for mobility by palpation (graded 0-III. degrees). At the end of examination, a swab for mycological cultivation was performed (results for Candida albicans or. C. non-albicans graded 0, 5, 15, 30, 100 and 150 colony forming units).

Schematic diagram for the clinical assessment of tongue enlargement. The basic position is achieved by instructing the patient to touch the lower incisors with the tip of the tongue while the tongue muscles are relaxed. Line A represents the physiological position, with the relaxed tongue contained within the space defined by the inner surfaces of the mandibular teeth. Line B indicates macroglossia

For the evaluation of results, malignant diagnoses were divided into the following groups: upper digestive tract (C15-C17: esophagus, stomach, small intestine); colorectal carcinoma (C18-C21); hepatopancreatic region (C22-C25); lungs (C34) and breast (C50).

Statistical analyses were performed using the Chi-square test, SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, USA). Multivariate stepwise selection logistic regression models and univariate models with confounders: Age; Female gender; Malignant duplicity, Malignant triplicity; Smoker; Ex-smoker; PYs; Radiotherapy; Chemotherapy were calculated. Due to exploratory matter of presented study, no adjustment for multiple comparison was used and alpha level was 0.05 (5%) for each single hypothesis.

There were no positive associations between verified extra-oral malignancy in our pilot study cohort and the presence of acanthosis nigricans or previously documented oral paraneoplastic syndromes. However, several new associations were observed. During bimanual palpation of the lower lip, tissue pallor was detected using standardized palpation pressure applied for the assessment of pathological resistance. Signs of hypoperfusion of lips and tongue as a manifestation of possible anemia in gastrointestinal cancer and clinical changes of the tongue in breast cancer were found with a notably higher frequency. Statistically significant differences in labial hypoperfusion between gastrointestinal cancers and other types are shown in Fig. 6 (p < 0.0001; 94 gastrointestinal cancers and 206 other malignancies). A similar pattern was present for lingual hypoperfusion (p = 0.0001). As expected in the evaluation of the pilot study, the performance of both multivariate and univariate models was poor, indicating the need for improved data stratification in subsequent projects. The enlargement of the tongue was significantly associated with breast carcinoma as shown in Fig. 7 (p < 0.0001; 159 breast cancers compared to 141 other tumors). For this malignancy dental impressions on the lateral tongue borders (p < 0.0001) and lateral deviation during protrusion were also frequent (p = 0.0152). The multivariate logistic regression calculations didn´t revealed any significant confounder in case of the labial hypoperfusion. The multivariate model was equal to the best univariate model focused on actual smoking with p = 0.0726. For macroglossia, age was the only significant confounding factor (p = 0.0005; macroglossia was more frequent in younger patients), with no other apparent interactions.

Hypoperfusion of the lower lip was significantly more frequent in gastric and colorectal cancer. The incidence was 25.5% in gastrointestinal cancer compared with other malignancies (mean 1.96%). C15-C17: esophageal, gastric, and duodenal cancers: 25% (5/20); C18-C21: colorectal cancer: 25.7% (19/74); C22-C25: hepatopancreatic cancer: 7.7% (2/26); C34: lung cancer: 4.8% (1/21); C50: breast cancer: 0.6% (1/159)

The enlargement of the tongue was significantly more common in breast cancer with 50.3% compared with other malignancies (mean 10.6%). C15-C17: esophageal, gastric, and duodenal cancers: 10% (2/20); C18-C21: colorectal cancer: 16.2% (12/74); C22-C25: hepatopancreatic cancer: 7.7% (2/26); C34: lung cancer: 0.0%; C50: breast cancer: 50.3% (80/159)

No patient suffered from a developmental syndrome like Down, Beckwith-Wiedemann, or Pierre-Robin syndrome (which are among other symptoms characterized by macroglossia). No long-term surgical procedure affecting the findings on the tongue preceded the clinical examination. All patients were hospitalized for a short duration before surgery, usually on the day of the dental examination, one day prior to the planned oncologic surgery.

As a highly important result, within the cohort of patients with breast cancer, 20 patients were younger than 45 years of age, and 50 patients were below 50 years of age.

Discussion

In 2022, there were almost 20 million new cases of cancer worldwide (19.96 million) [1]. Altogether, 22.4% of these were diagnosed in Europe and 21.1% in the Americas, despite these regions being home to while having only 9.6% and 13.1% of the global population [1]. The most frequent cancer in both sexes is lung cancer (12.4%), followed by female breast cancer and colorectal cancer with 11.6% and 9.6%, respectively [1]. Many malignancies have an increasing incidence, and in some cases this global trend is alarming (e.g., pancreatic cancer) [18, 19]. The logical goal is therefore to diagnose these diseases as early as possible, when they are still radically curable. Some malignant diseases are diagnosed during the process of population screening (colorectal cancer, breast cancer, prostate cancer), while others are screened in high-risk groups (lung cancer).

Adenocarcinoma is the most common histological subtype in lung carcinoma, partly induced by air pollution [20–23]. The risk of lung cancer is increased by the volume and duration of smoking [3, 24, 25]. Our study had 42.9% active smokers in lung cancer cases (with 19% of non-smokers with C34), with the lowest rate of smoking in breast carcinoma patients (19.3%). The whole cohort had 23.67% (71/300) active smokers; lower than the national smoking rate in Czechia, which is 30% [26]. Preventive programs for lung carcinoma usually offer screening only to smokers and ex-smokers. Lung cancer that is associated with smoking, historically specific to males, is increasing in females in recent generations in Europe and North America [3, 27–31]. A similar pathologic trend was observed in our pilot study, where 52.4% of lung cancer patients were female.

In females, breast cancer accounts for almost one quarter of new cancer cases (23.8%) [1].

Breast cancer (dg. C50) has numerous lifestyle risk factors [32]. Breast cancer in males accounts for less than 1% of C50 cases [33]. In our study this rate was higher, with 2.5% of cases (4/159). Despite the increase in incidence, the mortality in the European Union has fallen by 3.9% between 2012 and 2017 [34]. In 2018, the global ratio of premenopausal to postmenopausal (< 50; ≥50 years of age) breast cancer was 1 to 2.17 [35]. The increase in the incidence in premenopausal cases has occurred in high-income countries [35]. In 2020, in Central-Eastern Europe, 5.1% of global annual female breast cancer incidence occurred in patients younger than 50 years (while 50 + patients accounted for 7.8%) [2]. In Czechia, there is an average annual increase of 1.9% in premenopausal cases and 1.2% in postmenopausal patients [35]. In patients younger than 50 years, this is the worst trend of incidence among European countries [35]. The observed incidence rates are frequently related to screening practices [35]. The WHO recommends screening for female breast cancer every 2 years between the ages of 50–69 years [36]. Breast cancer screening in Czechia begins at age 45, which still does not cover the entire age profile of current cases. In our cohort, 12.6% of patients with C50 were younger than 45 (20/159) and 31.5% were premenopausal (50/159).

Colorectal cancer incidence is influenced by dietary and behavioral changes (increased intake of animal-source foods and a sedentary lifestyle) leading to overweight [37, 38]. The screening program combines colonoscopy and immunochemical stool marker testing [6, 39, 40]. Iron deficiency preoperative anemia is the most common extraintestinal symptom of colorectal cancer [16, 17]. It is caused by tumor-associated inflammation and chronic blood loss [16, 17]. This anemia is detected in 30–75% of cases [16, 17, 41]. Anemia brings peripheral tissue hypoperfusion, which is measurable [42]. Stomach and liver cancer are associated with co-infection by Helicobacter pylori or hepatitis B and C viruses [43, 44]. Screening for pancreatic cancer is extremely challenging and currently nearly absent [18]. The aim is therefore to identify high-risk groups of patients for whom more extensive diagnostic assessment is justified.

The presence of paraneoplastic syndromes has been known for over 100 years, but a gradual understanding of their etiopathogenesis, whether based on the ability of tumors to secrete various substances or the presence of immune cross-reactivity between the tumor and healthy host tissue, only emerged much later [9]. Today, we know that paraneoplastic manifestations can precede the manifestation of the tumor itself, and this is a fact that can be used to detect malignant disease or at least raise suspicion of its presence [7, 10].

However, the problem is that there is very limited awareness of this issue among the general dental population. While some manifestations in the oral cavity are well described, others are still subject to debate [45]. The overgrowth of Candida albicans or non-albicans Candida species is frequently associated with advanced cancer cases [46]. In our pilot study, only patients with lung or gastrointestinal cancer showed notable colonization by C. albicans (> 10 CFU). Candida non-albicans species were detected exclusively in lung and hepato-pancreatic cancer cases. These findings suggest that superficial oral candidiasis is unlikely to serve as a useful indicator of early (surgically treatable) malignancy.

Acanthosis nigricans (malignant Type 1) is usually associated with gastric adenocarcinoma [7, 47, 48]. Muir-Torre syndrome with a combination of dermal sebaceous carcinoma and colorectal cancer can be detected through facial skin tumors [49]. Peutz–Jeghers syndrome has characteristic hyperpigmentation of the labial skin and oral mucosa and indicates an increased risk of gastrointestinal, breast, and pancreatic cancer [50, 51]. Cowden syndrome involves oral mucosal papillomatosis and is associated with cancer of the thyroid gland or the breast [52, 53]. Gardner syndrome is associated with colorectal cancer and is frequently diagnosed based on paraneoplastic manifestation of osteomas in mandible or skull [54]. The absence of paraneoplastic manifestations of acanthosis nigricans or the above-mentioned syndromes does not refute their links to malignancies. However, their low incidence compared to the number of cancer cases confirms their limited usefulness in dental preventive checkup.

Extensive research is underway worldwide to identify new serum tumor markers with high sensitivity and specificity in detecting malignant diseases, but it is often overlooked that clinically observable findings can function as natural markers. Our pilot study showed that lip hypoperfusion was clinically identifiable by bimanual palpation in up to 25.5% of patients with gastrointestinal malignancies one day before planned oncological surgery, suggesting a potential role for this finding as a clinical indicator warranting further investigation. The true outcome in primary dental prevention using this examination might be even greater. The observed detection rate may be partly influenced by the frequent need for preoperative treatment of anemia, which is present in up to 75–80% of patients with colorectal cancer. Variations in fluid balance may also affect the perfusion of soft tissues in the head and neck. However, in our study, patients received the same perioperative regimen across malignant diagnoses from the time of admission to dental examination. Comparison with a population without diagnosed malignancy is necessary to draw clinical conclusions in this regard.

Our study detected frequent clinical changes of the tongue in patients with the breast cancer. Enlargement of the tongue (macroglossia) can be present as congenital or acquired [55, 56]. True macroglossia is a very uncommon condition caused by muscular hypertrophy of the tongue, glandular hyperplasia, inflammation, or lesions such as lymphangioma [56, 57]. As an acquired condition, it is associated with amyloidosis, metabolic, endocrinologic and oncologic processes or circulatory congestion [56]. Macroglossia can also be a result of prolonged surgical procedures [58]. Patients with breast carcinoma in our cohort had significantly higher frequencies of tongue enlargement, dental impressions on the tongue, as well as lateral tongue thrusting in comparison to other malignancies. The incidence was very high with 50.3%, 28.9% and 20.1% compared to its rarity in the general population [56, 57, 59]. The exact etiology of this condition requires further investigation in a larger cohort, but its potential diagnostic value appears promising. Our conclusions are not definitive, but they could serve as hypotheses for future specified studies on this issue. Macroglossia, as identified in our study, may be influenced by several confounding factors, many of which are typically recognized during the preoperative assessment. Common conditions such as inflammation or allergic reactions may cause changes in tongue volume and perfusion, which should be distinguishable by dental examination. The aim of our study is not to position primary care dentists as screeners for extraoral malignancies, but to encourage more frequent examination of the soft tissues of the oral cavity and referral for specialized evaluation when suspicious findings are present.

The findings of this study may be clinically relevant, as dental examinations have established screening value for pathological processes. Preventive examinations of the general population are typically performed twice a year, and patients across all age groups are seen by dentists more frequently than by any other specialist. Increasing dentists’ awareness of paraneoplastic and other suspicious findings could help address gaps in preventive programs related to age, comorbidity, or non-smoking status.

Conclusion

In this prospective cross-sectional study of 300 surgically treated patients with extra-oral malignancies, classical oral paraneoplastic manifestations were virtually absent, confirming their rarity in everyday oncologic practice. Nevertheless, the study identified two clinically meaningful patterns: (1) signs of labial and lingual hypoperfusion occurring significantly more often in gastrointestinal cancers, and (2) distinct morphological and functional changes of the tongue—particularly enlargement, dental impressions, and lateral deviation—frequently present in patients with breast cancer.

These findings, although preliminary, suggest that subtle vascular and structural alterations in the oral cavity may reflect tumor-specific systemic effects. Given the accessibility of the oral cavity and the frequency of routine dental examinations across all age groups, these observations, following appropriate validation, may support greater integration of dental practitioners into interdisciplinary prevention programs. Systematic assessment of perfusion changes and tongue morphology may complement existing screening strategies, particularly in high-risk or under-screened populations. As a pilot study, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Larger, prospective, multicenter cohorts with objective diagnostic tools are required to validate these associations, clarify their biological basis, and determine their true diagnostic or prognostic value. If confirmed, the incorporation of these simple clinical markers into dental prevention programs could contribute to the early detection of pathological conditions, which, following proper verification, may also help identify undetected tumors and thereby improve patient treatment outcomes.
