Ethical thinking and practice in the healthcare professions
Authors: David T Ozar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEvery aspect of what healthcare professionals do for patients is ethically significant, so it might seem strange that, when healthcare professionals are asked if anything ethically significant has occurred, their answer frequently is: “No, there were no ethical issues here.” But this answer does not mean that nothing ethically significant occurred during their caregiving. For healthcare professionals attend to the ethical content of their caregiving so routinely, so habitually, that their doing this is rarely thought of as involving a method of ethical reflection. This essay will examine this kind of ethical reflection in detail: its main components, how it works in practice, and how to grow in doing it effectively.Keywords: Profession, Professional ethics, Ethical thinking, Aspirational ethics, Habit, Self-formation, Virtue-based ethical thinking

Introduction

Every aspect of what healthcare professionals do for patients is ethically significant, from the simplest actions to make a patient more comfortable to the most complex efforts to avoid drug interaction, from making the simplest of diagnoses to the most complex surgical procedures and the most sophisticated forms of psychotherapy. But when an ethicist like the present author asks a group of clinicians if anything ethically significant has occurred while they have been caring for their patients, the answer frequently is: “No, there were no ethical issues here.” It is true that, in the economic and social situations in which they must care for patients today, healthcare professionals are unavoidably very busy people who are focusing their attention on properly caring for their patients. But this answer does not mean they think nothing ethically significant occurred during their caregiving, nor that they do not care whether their caregiving actions have been ethically appropriate. Instead, what it typically means is that nothing happened in their caregiving that required them to take time away from their caregiving in order to spend that time specifically focused on an ethical question.

For the ethical thinking that is going on while healthcare professionals are caring for their patients is significantly different from the ethical thinking that takes place when directly caring for patients is set aside in order to focus specifically on ethical questions that have arisen. Most of the case studies and more theoretical examinations that make up the literature of bioethics have focused on how ethical thinking of the latter kind works. The former kind of ethical thinking has rarely been studied in detail, either in the bioethics literature or the professional ethics literature. The focus of this article is on the kind of ethical thinking that healthcare professionals are doing while they are focused on caring for their patients.

It will be useful to begin with a brief, introductory description of this kind of ethical thinking and then provide a ‘roadmap’ of the rest of the article. When healthcare professionals make healthcare decisions and carry them out in caring for and interacting with their patients, healthcare professionals typically take it for granted that their actions are ethically appropriate even though they are not focusing self-consciously on this aspect of what they do. It might seem that these actions are chosen without ethical thinking to support what they are doing. But an important kind of ethical thinking is going on here, and two of its components have often been pointed out. First, like the vast majority of professionals’ actions in every profession, most of healthcare professionals’ actions and interactions in the care of their patients are done from habits, habits learned in training and in years of practice. Second, much of the ethical content of healthcare professionals’ caregiving is grounded in their having observed and imitated the characteristics of ethically admirable members of their profession, which characteristics they have then worked to make habitual in their own practice [1]. In other words, if one imagines that the practical and ethical thinking here was self-conscious, its content would be, “I am going to conform my action in this situation to the characteristics I have observed in ethically admirable members of my profession in situations like this.” But for someone who has made such conforming habitual, this way of shaping one’s actions doesn’t involve self-conscious reflection at the time.1

It is these two components of healthcare professionals’ ethical thinking that have prompted bioethics scholars, and scholars of professional ethics generally, to note analogies between this kind of ethical thinking and virtue-based ethical thinking [2]. The ways in which a description of this kind of ethical thinking is like, but also different from, ethics scholars’ interpretations of virtue-based ethical thinking will be examined in detail below.

In order to explain what goes on and what goes into this kind of ethical thinking, it is crucial to stress that healthcare professionals are members of a profession and therefore to examine the ethical and practical implications of this fact. The following two sections will focus on these topics. With that as background, the cognitive components of this kind of ethical thinking will be described in detail in the fourth section and will then be compared in the fifth section to various philosophers’ descriptions of virtue-based ethical thinking. This will be followed by an overview of the central contents of professionals’ ethics, and the article will conclude with some suggestions about how one can become more effective in doing this kind of ethical thinking.2

Professions and professional ethics

The fact that healthcare professionals are members of a profession is crucial to understanding how they do their ethical thinking. A profession is a group of people who have developed sufficiently dependable expertise in helping other people in a matter that the relevant society values highly that the society therefore designates this group to be the society’s providers of that help, entrusting the group with social authority to determine what counts as a need for that help, what are appropriate efforts to provide it, and whether such efforts have succeeded or not. The relevant expertise of a profession consists of both a body of knowledge and the skills needed to dependably apply this knowledge to benefit those the profession serves.3 With regard to the healthcare professions, the relevant form of this two-fold expertise concerns what is needed to help people in matters of health, both in supporting its maintenance and in responding to illness and disease, accidents, and other ways in which health can be lessened or put at risk.

The twofold nature of professional expertise means that formal education is required. For healthcare professionals, this means formal education in the relevant biomedical sciences and then extensive learning-in-practice from persons already expert so that the learners can become skilled in applying their formal knowledge to unique individual patients.4 This requirement means in turn that, given the ordinary workings of a society’s division of labors, this kind of two-fold expertise is not available to most people in the society. That is, a profession’s expertise is unavoidably exclusive to a limited number of people in a given society, even though it is the only means by which the people of that society have dependable access to something they highly value. Because of this, no reasonable society would permit such important expertise to be exclusively controlled by a small group without some way of managing how the group possessing this expertise will use it.

The solution to this problem is, along with distinctive expertise, the other central component of the idea of a profession; namely, that the expert group, both collectively as a group and all of its members personally, deliberately commit themselves both to practice their expertise in ways that do not exploit the rest of the society and to use the authority that the society gives to them in matters of their expertise for the benefit of those who need their services. This is obviously an ethical commitment, a commitment about how each profession as a whole and each member of that profession will live their lives and practice their expertise. The contents of this commitment, which will be surveyed below, are often summarized by saying that the members of a profession commit themselves to making the wellbeing of the persons whom they help by means of their expertise a Top Priority in the value systems that shape how they live and act. Thus the content of healthcare professionals’ commitments is often summarized, oversimply as will be explained, by saying, “The patient always comes first.”

The general idea of a profession is widely understood, but what is less well understood is that this picture of a profession needs to be considered an ideal picture because so many aspects of it can be very difficult to actualize in practice. One point that needs particular emphasis is that the phrase ‘a Top Priority’ is the correct way to say this, even though many of the professions themselves and much of the literature about professional ethics may say ‘the Top Priority’ instead.

For no reasonable person actually expects the members of a profession to have only one Top Priority. The health of their patients will not be any healthcare professional’s only Top Priority. There will also be individuals, the members of the professional’s family, other persons in their lives, etc., whose wellbeing will have comparable Top Priority in the decisions they make. In addition, circumstances can arise in which a professional is providing services to two needy persons (or groups) at the same time and cannot maximize the wellbeing of both simultaneously. In both kinds of circumstances, a professional can have very complex ethical decisions to make because two or more of their Top Priorities are at significant risk and both cannot be fully responded to at the same time. In addition, specifically with regard to the healthcare professions, the health of a whole society may sometimes, as during the Covid-19 pandemic, be seen as competing with or in special circumstances even outweighing the health of an individual patient being cared for. But stressing these complexities does not change the fact that every professional has made a personal commitment to the larger society that the wellbeing of those whom he or she serves as a professional is a Top Priority in a way that other persons’ wellbeing is not necessarily a Top Priority for persons who are not members of a profession.5

Of course, even the most committed humans can make mistakes of judgment in complex ethical situations and can fail to set aside their self-interest or the interests of persons dear to them when doing so would be professionally appropriate.6 In addition, especially with regard to the healthcare professions, healthcare professionals’ patients and others who care about them may accuse the professionals involved of having failed ethically in situations in which these persons’ hopes for the patient cannot be fulfilled. Very few people in the larger community have any detailed understanding of the specific contents of each profession’s ethical norms or recognize in times of stress that every professional has more than one Top Priority and may therefore have very difficult ethical decisions to make. The real world in which professionals are trying to faithfully carry out their commitments to their profession’s ethics is considerably more complex than the general idea of a profession might seem to suggest. But even so, the vast majority of professionals take their obligations to the larger society and to the persons they serve very seriously. And this is particularly true of healthcare professionals, as the members of the healthcare professions admirably demonstrated, facing significant personal risk, working extraordinary numbers of hours, and in these and other ways making considerable sacrifices, in order to care for great numbers of very ill patients during the Covid-19 pandemic. It is also evident in the widespread experience of moral distress on the part of healthcare professionals when competing professional-ethical concerns cannot all be addressed [9, 10].

One particularly important aspect of the real world of healthcare professionals’ lives is the distance between the complexity of a healthcare profession’s expertise and the public’s understanding of it. The expert judgments of a single professional or a group of professionals are all too often presumed to be absolutely certain. But every healthcare professional knows that the expertise they can offer to the patients they serve is limited in important ways and cannot possibly be certain. It is limited first of all because the expertise of a single professional, or even of a large group of professionals practicing together, is not the expertise of the whole profession. But even more important, every profession’s – and therefore every professional’s – expertise is based either on the relevant sciences or on the accumulated insights of previous practice or both, and neither of these is able to predict what is needed in this unique particular concrete situation with absolute certainty. Thanks to advancements in biomedical science, many healthcare diagnoses, prognoses, and therapy recommendations are becoming statistically more dependable; but none of them are, or ever will be, absolutely certain about what will happen for a particular patient, even though patients and their families wish such certainty is possible.

Some other ways in which the real world of professional life can make practicing in accord with the idea of profession more challenging will become clear in the section below on The Contents of Professional Ethics. The idea of a profession is an ideal, but not in the sense of something that cannot possibly be achieved. It is rather an ideal in the sense of something that is in fact reachable by real people in varying degrees and that, because it is so valuable, continually calls for additional effort to achieve it more fully [1].

Learning one’s profession’s ethics

There are two sources from which the members of the professions learn the detailed contents of their profession’s ethics. One source is the codes of ethics and similar statements about ethics produced and published by the relevant professional associations and the professional literature that comments on these. The second source is learning how to live as a professional by observing practicing members of one’s profession.

Almost every profession has a published code of ethics or similar document.7 Some of these codes describe desirable characteristics of members of the profession, but usually with little information about how to achieve them. Others are more narrowly behavioral, indicating how to respond properly to certain kinds of situations that can arise or offering lists of behavioral prohibitions; and many of the codes are combinations of these. Such codes are typically formal products of a professional organization, established, amended, etc., through an internal political process within the organization.

In most professions, if there is formal training in ethical conduct in their professional schools, this training is built around one or more of the relevant codes, and the learning of the profession’s ethics can therefore be largely behavioral even if it is supplemented with realistic cases about applying the code. In the healthcare professions, the rise of bioethics as an academic discipline has enhanced the teaching of professional ethics by supplementing the professions’ codes with realistic cases and providing learners with examples of and ideally with practice in the various methods of ethical thinking that the discipline has developed.8

The second source from which the members of the professions typically learn the contents of their profession’s ethics is by observing practicing members of the profession. The focus of this kind of learning is not primarily behavioral. Instead, the focus of this kind of learning is on how that person’s understanding of their profession guides their choices about how to act in various situations. Many members of the healthcare professions have been motivated to join their profession through contact with members of the profession whose actions and whose reasons for acting in that way have been considered admirable by the observer. Obviously, the best situation for learning in this way is by being able to communicate with the person to learn their reasons for acting as they have. But short of this, the observer may nevertheless believe that the actor’s reasons for acting are admirable from what else the learner knows about the actor or from the respect the actor receives from others whose views the observer respects.

For at the same time that observing practicing professionals’ admirable conduct can inform the learner of relevant ethical content, it can also motivate the learner to imitate what has been observed. Assuming the learner wants to become a successful member of the relevant profession, the observed conduct is received as an example of how a member of that profession ought to act and of what kind of person a member of the relevant profession ought to be. Of course, if the learner does not actually want to become a professional, then something different will happen. But the present effort to describe the method of ethical thinking of practicing professionals presumes that they chose to become successful members of their professions and therefore are choosing to continually strive to grow in their habitual grasp of the content and practice of their profession’s commitments.

This learning is most effective when the learner not only observes and commits to imitate, but then also takes time to reflect on what is being learned in order test his or her own conduct accordingly. Such reflection will also lead the learner to become more detailed in his or her observations and more self-consciously selective about whom he or she will choose to imitate and in what ways. Continued observation and efforts to imitate together with reflection on his or her own ways of acting can then enable the learner to form habits of perceiving, evaluating alternatives, and choosing, along with many other supportive habits, by which the learner can increasingly become able to practice, that is, to act and to relate to others, as a member of that profession ought to do.9 Every professional whose practice is now admirable and worth imitating began his or her ethical learning in this way and has been adding to it in this same way again and again over the years.10

Of course, there are also situations in which the learner observes a practicing professional whose actions violate the profession’s ethical standards. Recognizing that this is the case requires the learner to already have some grasp of the relevant standards, and this is a reason for including didactic training in the profession’s codes. What is to be hoped for when what the learner observes is contrary to professional standards is that the observer will be motivated to determine other ways of handling similar situations rather than imitating what they have observed and be motivated to avoid becoming that kind of professional.

To a much greater extent than behaviorally focused learning alone, ethical learning by observation and imitation can lead a young professional not only to develop an understanding of what the profession’s ethical commitments require that is concrete rather than abstract, but also, if what is observed is also imitated, to initiate the forming of a relevant habit of dependable ethical practice as well. It also prompts young professionals to recognize that, like those they observe, they are not just passive recipients of a set of ethical standards, but choosers of a way of life, active moral agents who represent their profession to the world and who are therefore also responsible to carefully evaluate the profession’s accepted standards and practices if these are or have become ethically problematic and to work for appropriate changes. It is not irrelevant that this second kind of learning is also employed as an essential part of professionals’ learning how to apply their technical knowledge to help others, as the medical residents’ mantra, “See One, Do One, Teach One,” indicates. Of course, this mantra is seriously hyperbolic since one must observe an action with its variations in different circumstances many times to be able to dependably do it correctly, much less teach it.11

This kind of learning can be expected to correlate with codes listing traits that are desirable for members of the profession, provided these are well written; but it will often point to subtler characteristics of admired members of the profession. For in this second way of learning ethical content, these traits are not just named and recommended; they are learned by being demonstrated in practice by living exemplars, and something else will begin to be learned as well. By being motivated to mirror what has been observed in his or her own conduct in practice settings, the learner will begin to practice the distinctive kind of ethical thinking that is the focus of this article.12

Ethical thinking in the practice setting

When healthcare professionals are caring for their patients, their knowing how they ought act to benefit their patients’ health and how they ought to relate to patients and others and that they can dependably act and relate accordingly does not happen because they have memorized their profession’s code, but because they have established habits of acting as a member of their profession ought to act. This why, as noted above, if a healthcare professional’s reasons for acting in a certain way in their professional role could be ascertained without a formal question that appears to require a formal answer, the reason for acting in this way would be something like, “I am choosing this action in this situation because it conforms to the characteristics I have observed in expert and ethically admirable members of my profession in situations like this.” What is going on in this kind of habituated ethical thinking? There are three components to this kind of ethical thinking and each of these components is most easily described as a set of steps.

The first two components, both of which are historical in nature, have already been alluded to in previous sections. One of these is healthcare professional’s awareness of being a professional, which awareness implies the steps by which he or she became a member of that profession and has worked since then to shape themself accordingly. There was the initial choice or series of choices to become a member of this profession, then many choices, each ethically laden, to become a dependable practitioner of the profession’s healthcare expertise, the choice or series of choices to commit themselves to practicing in accord with the profession’s ethical commitments to the larger society and to understand the implications of those commitments for oneself, and then many, many choices to identify and then imitate the ethical characteristics of admirable members of the profession. Each of these historical choices contributed to the formation of the habitual patterns of practice that ethically shape a healthcare professional’s daily care of their patients.

The other historical component, far less explicit in the healthcare professional’s awareness, but necessarily embedded in their awareness of themselves as a professional, is the series of steps by which an expert group becomes a health profession. First, a group of persons comes to be acknowledged by the larger society as possessing sufficient healthcare expertise to dependably assist the society’s people when their health is compromised or at risk. Then the choices are made by which the society socially authorizes that group to be the principal provider of this kind of assistance and the determiner of what will be counted as healthcare expertise in that society. But this only happens if the society can also be confident that the expert group collectively and its members individually commit themselves, as noted above, to use this authority in socially agreed ways; that is, the social choices by which that group is declared a profession are conditional in this way. While the particular steps in this process are historically contingent, the result is that the ethical correctness of the habitual patterns of practice that affect the healthcare professional’s care for patients is secured by the larger society’s acceptance of that profession’s commitments as ethically adequate.

That is, the normative import of every profession’s ethics is derived first of all from the mutual acceptance of these norms by the professional group and the larger society. For the ethical norms of a profession are first of all socially accepted standards.13

The third component is the instant of ethical thinking that is implied in the healthcare professional’s act of choosing a particular caregiving action and the accompanying conviction that this action is ethically appropriate because “it conforms to the characteristics I have observed in expert and ethically admirable members of my profession in situations like this.” It is again thanks to the power of habit that the third component of this kind of ethical thinking can take place in an instant; but for clarity’s sake, the four kinds of thinking that constitute it will again be described as distinct steps.

The first step is getting an accurate picture of what is ethically significant in the concrete choice-situation at hand. The second step is to identify a picture of an admired professional who was responding ethically in the same kind of situation. For each of the learning experiences referred to in the previous section involves not only observation of how the observed professional acted but also observation of the kind of situation in which his or her action was appropriate. The third step is to compare these pictures, to see if they match, that is, if the situation in which the admired professional acted is the same kind of situation as the one in which a choice is now needed, or at least similar enough that the picture of that way of acting is appropriate to be imitated in the situation at hand. The fourth step is, if the match is close enough, to imitate the admired professional’s way of acting.

It is important to stress that neither the picture of the situation at hand nor the picture of the admired professional responding to this kind of situation should be understood as abstractions. Instead, in both cases what is observed and what is retained for possible imitation are whole sets of concrete realities, persons and their bodies and their feelings and their interacting with one another and in some physical location. In the same way, the comparing of these two pictures is not in terms of abstractions, but is a comparison of one whole picture with another, and the act of imitating is not an imitating of a narrowly defined act, but an imitating of the whole way in which the admired professional responded to the situation. This is why the word ‘picture’ is being used here. When one takes a picture, one unavoidably includes much that is also relevant to the situation.14

In the simplest examples of this kind of ethical thinking, the picture of the concrete situation at hand will correspond closely to the picture of an admired professional responding ethically in a particular situation. This is what the experience of this kind of ethical thinking is typically like for early learners, i.e. before relevant habits have become established and the steps of the process have become more deliberate and self-conscious.15 Imagine a young surgeon who is, for the first time, the person responsible for determining and for informing his or her patient that, because of the effects of her diabetes on the circulation in her feet, only the amputation of her left foot will permit her to avoid life-threatening sepsis. Fortunately, once in medical school and several times as a surgical resident, this young surgeon has observed experienced surgeons wrestling with the same kind of clinical decision and then speaking with their patients about it. Two of these surgeons, he thought, stressed the clinical necessity of the amputation without much awareness of the sense that the patient was losing part of her body and would experience mobility changes that would significantly affect how she lived. A third surgeon, who he had observed twice, once when the patient was a woman and once when the patient was a man, seemed much more attentive to how the patient viewed the amputation, not lessening the risk of life-threatening sepsis, but responding more to where the patient was. In comparing the picture of his patient with these three pictures of experienced surgeons at work, this surgeon concluded that the third picture, especially when the patient was a woman, was the best match with the picture of himself speaking to this patient.

Before very long, however, the young professional will have experienced hundreds of different kinds of clinical situations and hundreds of different kinds of patients, and if they have been lucky, they will have encountered healthcare professionals who live their ethical commitments in admirable ways. There will be cases like the one above in which affirming a match between the situation at hand and the learner’s observation of ethically appropriate conduct is easy. But there will also be situations that are more ethically complex, with different elements of it calling for separate ethical consideration and prompting multiple pictures of admired professionals responding, some one way, others another way, depending on which aspects of the situation at hand they considered most important. But if the learner is observant and reflective, the learner’s stock of pictures of more and more situations needing ethical clarity and the learner’s stock of observed admirable responses will be built up, and the need for more careful comparison to determine if pictures match will also grow.

Over time, if a person’s ability to do this kind of ethical thinking matures, they will also begin to take account, in their comparing of pictures, of relevant differences between the personalities of admired professionals and themselves and to modify their imitating accordingly. A process will also begin to take place in which the healthcare professional will begin to formulate a picture of how an ideal member of his or her profession would act in most clinical-decision situations, built up of the stored memories of admirable professionals encountered over years, which may then become the picture habitually referred to when choices need to be made. And when a situation arises where the professional’s established habits of ethical practice do not resolve what ought to be done, the first recourse of the mature practitioner of this way of ethical thinking may well be to do the three steps described above in a deliberate, self-conscious way before taking time out from caregiving to address the matter more formally.

The psychologist, Jerome Brunner [20], has proposed that, in addition to the logical/syllogistic work that the human mind engages in and that is employed in other kinds of ethical thinking, humans are also capable of a different kind of reasoning in which they judge whether a story—or a ‘picture’ as this term is used here – makes narrative sense. That is, humans judge whether the actions, intentions, etc., of those involved fit together narratively. The third step, comparing the pictures, would certainly involve determining both whether each of the pictures being compared makes “narrative sense” itself, and then whether the two pictures make narrative sense when combined, in order for the comparison to be ethically meaningful.

Many healthcare professionals learn to use this method and thus build the relevant habits of professional conduct without realizing it is a distinctive kind of ethical thinking, and they may even grow more proficient at doing it without having a plan for such progress. But there are three activities that, if deliberately undertaken, will enhance a professional’s initial learning and then their growing more proficient in this method of professional ethical thinking. These three activities are briefly described in the final section of this article.

Analogies with accounts of virtue-based ethical thinking

Many authors have proposed similarities between the kind of ethical thinking attributed here to healthcare professionals when caring for patients and virtue-based ethical thinking. Virtue-based thinking was the dominant focus of Western theorizing about what ought to be done from Plato and Aristotle into the Renaissance and after. But then, for many years, it received little emphasis in favor of theories of ethical thinking that were more action-focused, i.e. in contrast with a focus on virtues as characteristics of persons; namely, value-maximizing (consequentialist) ethical thinking; rule/principle-based (deontological) ethical thinking; and rights-based ethical thinking.

But scholarly theorizing about virtue-based ethical thinking has begun to receive renewed attention in recent years, especially since the publication of Alastair MacIntyre’s After Virtue [21]. However, like MacIntyre, contemporary philosophers theorizing about virtue-based ethical thinking have focused almost exclusively on questions about its ethical validity; that is, whether one version or another of virtue-based ethical thinking does correctly inform one about what ought to be done and why (or why not). MacIntyre [21] provides an example of such validity-focused theorizing that is relevant to healthcare professionals’ ethical thinking. After arguing that action-focused forms of ethical thinking are seriously flawed in ways that can only be corrected by turning to virtue-based ethical thinking, (i.e. MacIntyre’s own carefully elaborated version of Aristotle’s account of virtue), he proposes that the validity of ethical thinking depends on its conformity to the society’s systems of socially accepted standards (which he calls “practices”) when these in turn conform to widely accepted social values and ideals. On this account, it is precisely the importance to humans of risks and losses to health and function and therefore the widely accepted social value of access to professional healthcare that grounds the validity of healthcare professionals’ ethical thinking.

With regard to the proposal that the ethical thinking of healthcare professionals when caring for patients is similar to virtue-based ethical thinking, this proposal is correct in several ways. There are, however, many different ways of understanding virtue-based ethical thinking and some of these correspond to the kind of ethical thinking under consideration here more than others.16 Even so, both the role of habituated patterns of reflection and the role of the observation-and-imitation learning process associated with this kind of ethical thinking are widely taken for granted in the theoretical work about virtue-based ethical thinking.

Another and philosophically more important potential analogy is that, in every interpretation of virtue-based ethical thinking, “how it is best or right or proper to conduct oneself is explained in terms of how it is best for a human being to be” [23] and this is obviously also true of the kind of ethical thinking described in this article.17

A fourth characteristic of healthcare professionals’ ethical thinking when caring for patients concerns the motivation that prompts imitation, both at the start in the learner and also in the experienced professional who continues to learn by observation and imitation; namely, admiration. Several theorists see this as an important component of virtue-based ethical thinking, especially Zagzebski [14, pp. 30–59], who provides a detailed study of the role of this emotion in ethical thinking. However, like the other contemporary theorists about virtue-based ethical thinking, Zagzebski’s work is primarily focused on questions of validity. While potentially informative about healthcare professionals’ ethical thinking when practicing, these theorists typically take the cognitive structure of observation and imitation-based ethical thinking for granted rather than proposing or asking for a careful description of its details.18

The contents of professional ethics

The complete contents of any profession’s ethics cannot be written down. But there are nine general ethical themes that recur in the contents of every profession’s ethics which can be briefly stated as nine questions about how the members of a given profession ought to act.19

Many of the themes summarized here are rarely mentioned in the formal codes, but are nevertheless typically understood and appreciated, and observed and imitated, in the practice of admirable professionals. These nine themes, in the form of questions and examples of ‘sub-questions’ about the same theme, with selected examples from the healthcare professions are:

1. Who is (are) this profession’s Chief Client(s)? Every profession’s expertise is directed to the service of persons in need of help regarding the important human value that is the focus of that profession’s expertise. But there are differing degrees of need, and the proper priorities among these will ordinarily be part of each profession’s ethical guidelines. Healthcare professionals, for example, are to give priority to persons experiencing emergent life-threats over those experiencing other healthcare issues.

2. What are the Principal Outcomes of this profession’s practice? Most professions’ expertise can actually benefit people in a number of ways, i.e. serve a number of human values. Each profession’s ethics offers guidelines about how the valuable outcomes of professional practice are to be ranked. For Nursing, for example, patients’ safety and a caring relationship are often cited as that profession’s Principal Outcomes. Regarding Medicine’s Principal Outcomes, the development of life-extending technologies, their possible use for permanently unconscious patients, and other complex factors have rendered Medicine’s Principal Outcomes more difficult to rank than they once were. In addition, the fact that the different healthcare professions have differing Principal Outcomes, while rarely carefully explored, is one reason communication between members of different healthcare professions is sometimes very complicated.

3. What is the ideal decision-making relationship between members of this profession and the person or group being served? There was a time when a professional’s expertise was assumed to justify the professional making all the relevant decisions for the person served. But in many contemporary societies today, the ethical emphasis is much more on there being a process of shared decision-making between the professional and the person(s) being served, along with special guidelines when the person(s) being served is (are) unable to directly participate in the decision.

4. What are the standards of competent practice for this profession? Mastering a profession’s expertise is essential for entrance into the profession, and every healthcare profession has standards of competent practice and also requirements that additions to its expertise be incorporated into members’ practice in timely fashion, as well as guidelines to preserve the priority of expert practice over other motivations that might distract from it, for example, harmful conflicts of interest.20

5. What sacrifices are required of members of this profession and in what respects do the obligations of this profession to those it serves take priority over other morally relevant considerations affecting its members? As noted above, the wellbeing of the person (or group) being served is to be a Top Priority for every professional; but situations can arise in which other Top Priorities are ethically relevant. While some professions’ codes of ethics address some situations of this sort, for example, that physicians should not care for members of their own family, it is primarily from respected professionals’ ethical practice that most professionals receive ethical guidance when this happens.

6. What are the ideal kinds of relationships between the members of this profession and co-professionals and between the members of this profession and other parties who assist them in their professional practice? Here too, formal ethical standards may address how some of these relationships should be handled, for example, the ethics of referral to another practitioner. But most professionals learn the ethics of these relationships from the actual practice of experienced and admired members of the profession.

7. What is the ideal relationship between the members of this profession and the larger community? Professionals have two kinds of relationships with the larger community, their relationships with the individuals or groups they directly serve with their expertise, and the relationships they have via their participation in professional organizations’ interactions with the larger community. In both relationships, their words should affirm and their actions should strive to fulfill the ideals embodied in the idea of a profession even when there are social barriers that complicate these efforts.

8. What ought the members of this profession do to make access to the profession’s services available to everyone who needs them? The idea of a profession implies that the principal criterion that should guide every professional’s priorities regarding whom to serve is the person’s (or group’s) need for their expert assistance. In societies where people’s access to professional healthcare services is heavily embedded in the workings of the free enterprise marketplace, people’s healthcare needs are not the primary determinant of their access to professional services because the Top Priority of the free enterprise marketplace is freedom of contract. This means, for example, that healthcare professionals who directly serve their patients will have ethical decisions to make in order to assure that economic forces are not systematically excluding persons who need their services from their practices, even though their resources are limited and they have other Top Priorities that deserve consideration. In a similar way, healthcare provider institutions have ethical decisions to make in order to assure that economic forces are not systematically excluding persons who need the services of their professional staff [29].

9. What are the members of this profession obligated to do to preserve the integrity of their commitment to its values and to educate others about them? Because the ideals of professional life are aspirational ideals [1], every professional is committed to making the effort necessary to keep growing in the aspirational ideals of his or her profession, both in enhancing their understanding of the content of their profession’s ethics and in becoming more and more skilled at the method of professionals’ ethical thinking that is the focus of this essay. In addition, professionals may have some obligation to model the values to which they are professionally committed in how they live the other aspects of their lives. Healthcare professionals, for example, may have some obligation to model health-supporting behaviors in their personal lives.

Growing more skilled in this kind of ethical thinking

Proficiency in this kind of ethical thinking is only achieved gradually over time, so achieving it is necessarily a matter of self-formation over years. Fortunately, there are three specific skills that, once mastered, will make such growth not only more effective, but also a more self-conscious process. That is, they will enable the healthcare professional to see growth happening and to know that the person he or she is striving to become and is actually becoming is a person he or she is actively choosing to be.

These three skills are honest self-assessment, honest self-commendation with repetition when appropriate, and honest self-correction when needed. The ideal would be that these three skills are focused on and practiced every day. For many people, one effective way of doing this involves making time at the end of each day when that day’s actions and relationships can be reflected on and assessed in light of that person’s ethical commitments as a professional. Incidents deserving commendation should be noted first because they support the professional’s aspirations and encourage him or her to continue to improve. Then incidents involving a shortfall from ethical excellence should be noted and time taken to ask what would have been a better way to act in that situation, together with a commitment to change accordingly. In relation to shortfalls, it is important to stress the difference between being critical of ethical shortfalls in ways that undermine confidence and a more affirmative response that names the shortfall and identifies appropriate conduct, but that also supports the professional’s aspirational efforts to grow in self-formation.21

The more fully and the more self-consciously such self-formation is achieved, the richer and the more ethically perceptive will a practicing professional’s day-in day-out ethical thinking be. For this is how conforming one’s actions and relationships to “what a member of my profession ought to do,” that is, the distinctive method of professional-ethical thinking, will be more and more fully actualized.
