Omics integration identified CRIP2 as a key mediator of olaparib resistance in prostate cancer
Authors: Weicheng Tian, Yize Li, Haiyin Xiao, Shanghua Cai, Jinchuang Li, Biyan Wen, Zeheng Tan, Zhenjie Wu, Zhaodong Han, Yulin Deng, Yongding Wu, Huichan He, Jianheng Ye, Weide Zhong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mechanisms driving resistance to poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis), such as Olaparib, in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) that emerge after androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) remain largely undefined. This study aimed to systematically identify tumor epithelial cell subpopulations associated with Olaparib resistance by integrating single-cell transcriptomics, spatial transcriptomics (ST), and bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data, and to construct a risk prognostic model.MethodsSingle-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from six CRPC patients were subjected to quality control, clustering, annotation, and dimensionality reduction to identify distinct epithelial cell subpopulations. The Scissor algorithm, combined with Olaparib half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) phenotype data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database, was employed to select the Scissor+ cell subpopulations significantly associated with drug resistance. The BayesPrism algorithm was then used to estimate the proportion of Scissor+ cells in the bulk RNA-seq samples, to explore their relationship with prognosis. ST data from the 10x Genomics platform were integrated to validate the spatial distribution of the Scissor+ cells in tissues. Additionally, bulk RNA-seq data from multiple cohorts [including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO)] were integrated to construct an olaparib resistance-associated gene (ORAG) risk model, which was optimized through various machine-learning algorithms. Finally, the core gene CRIP2 was validated through in vitro drug sensitivity assays to investigate its role in olaparib resistance.ResultsThe Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) analysis of the single-cell data revealed significant cellular heterogeneity in the CRPC tissues, and the Scissor algorithm identified the epithelial cell subpopulations (Scissor+) strongly linked to Olaparib resistance. The BayesPrism analysis showed that the increased proportion of Scissor+ cells in the bulk samples was significantly associated with a poor prognosis (P=0.04). ST further validated that this subpopulation was predominantly enriched in tumor regions, displaying a clear spatially specific expression pattern. In seven independent bulk RNA-seq cohorts, the ORAG model, which was built using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and random survival forest (RSF) algorithms, showed excellent prognostic predictive performance [with the highest concordance index (C-index) of 0.722]. CRIP2 was identified as a key risk gene, and found to be correlated with a worse survival prognosis, higher tumor stage (T stage), Gleason score, and prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels. In vitro experiments confirmed that CRIP2 knockdown significantly increased the sensitivity of LNCaP and C4-2B cells to olaparib.ConclusionsThis study was the first to systematically identify olaparib resistance-associated tumor epithelial cell subpopulations in CRPC by integrating single-cell, spatial, and bulk multi-omics data. The constructed ORAG risk-prediction model has great potential for clinical application. CRIP2, which was identified as a key regulatory factor of resistance, offers a novel strategy for personalized treatment and the study of resistance mechanisms in CRPC.Keywords: Prostate cancer (PCa), olaparib resistance, olaparib resistance-associated gene (ORAG), CRIP2

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study systematically identified olaparib resistance-associated epithelial subpopulations (Scissor+) in castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) using multi-omics integration. A robust olaparib resistance-associated gene (ORAG) risk-prediction model was developed, and CRIP2 was identified as a key gene driving resistance. The knockdown of CRIP2 significantly enhanced olaparib sensitivity in vitro.

What is known, and what is new?

• Olaparib is clinically applied in CRPC, but mechanisms of resistance remain poorly understood and predictive biomarkers are lacking.

• This study integrated single-cell, spatial, and bulk transcriptomic data to identify resistance-related subpopulations and construct a novel ORAG-based prognostic model. CRIP2 was identified and experimentally validated as a potential therapeutic target regulating olaparib resistance.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The findings offer a valuable framework for predicting olaparib response and guiding personalized therapy in CRPC. The ORAG model and CRIP2 may serve as clinical tools, and future studies should focus on elucidating the mechanistic role of CRIP2 in drug resistance.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is one of the most common malignancies of the male genitourinary system, with both its incidence and mortality rates consistently ranking among the highest worldwide (1,2). The androgen receptor signaling axis plays a central regulatory role in maintaining PCa cell growth and survival. Consequently, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT) has long been considered the standard treatment for locally advanced and metastatic PCa (3-5). However, after 18–24 months of ADT (6,7), the majority of patients inevitably progress to castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC), developing drug resistance and heightened invasiveness, which ultimately lead to patient mortality.

Poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPis), such as olaparib, have been approved for the treatment of CRPC patients with DNA damage repair (DDR) deficiencies, such as BRCA mutations (8,9). DNA single-strand breaks are typically repaired by poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), which binds to the damage sites and initiates repair. PARPis block the dissociation of PARP from DNA, leading to the accumulation of PARP at the damage sites, interrupting the repair process, and inducing more severe DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs) (10-12). When DSBs cannot be effectively repaired by homologous recombination repair (HRR), cells inevitably experience genomic instability and are driven to programmed cell death. Consequently, PARPis, such as olaparib, have been widely applied in the treatment of various solid tumors, including PCa, under the synthetic lethality paradigm, specifically targeting HRR-deficient tumors such as those with BRCA1/2 mutations (9,13). However, clinical resistance to olaparib is common, and its molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood (14-16).

Current research primarily focuses on the functional loss and compensatory mechanisms of DNA repair pathways but often overlooks the profound effect of cellular heterogeneity in the tumor microenvironment on the resistance process. With the rapid advancement of single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) technologies, it is now possible to characterize the dynamic alterations in cellular composition and functional states in the tumor microenvironment at near-single-cell resolution, offering novel insights into the mechanisms underlying therapeutic resistance (17-20).

Therefore, in this study, we integrated scRNA-seq, ST, and multi-cohort bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) data to systematically identify and validate a key epithelial cell subpopulation (Scissor+) in CRPC that is closely associated with Olaparib resistance. We further constructed an olaparib resistance-associated gene (ORAG) risk model and use machine-learning algorithms to identify the key resistance driver CRIP2. Finally, we experimentally validated the functional role of CRIP2 in regulating drug sensitivity. This study provides new theoretical insights into the cellular basis of PARPi resistance and offers potential strategies for precise stratification and targeted therapy for CRPC patients. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD and MDAR reporting checklists (available at https://tau.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tau-2025-543/rc).

Methods

Data collection

The scRNA-seq dataset (GSE137829) was obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. The GSE137829 dataset comprised six CRPC patient samples (see Table S1 for details of the clinical information). The bulk RNA-seq datasets were collected from multiple public sources, including The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), GSE21034 (Taylor), GSE54460, GSE107299 (CPC), E-MTAB-6128 (CIT), DKFZ2018 (DKFZ), GSE70769 (Stockholm), and GSE189186. The gene expression and clinical data were retrieved from TCGA (http://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/), ArrayExpress (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/arrayexpress/), and GEO (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The ST data were downloaded from the 10x Genomics platform (https://www.10xgenomics.com/). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

ScRNA-seq analysis

The scRNA-seq data were preprocessed using the Seurat R package (version 5.1.0). To ensure quality control, only cells with 500–7,000 detected genes and <10% mitochondrial content were retained. Highly variable genes (n=3,000) were identified using the “FindVariableFeatures” function. A principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted for dimensionality reduction, followed by batch-effect correction using the Harmony algorithm. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was employed for nonlinear embedding and visualization. Cell type annotation was conducted based on canonical markers curated from literature (21,22) (Table S2).

Scissor-based subpopulation identification

The Scissor algorithm was used to integrate phenotypic information [olaparib half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50)] from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database with CRPC single-cell expression data and bulk transcriptomes (23). Scissor was used to identify the epithelial subpopulations (Scissor+) that were highly correlated with olaparib resistance.

BayesPrism analysis

BayesPrism (version 2.0) was used to estimate the deconvoluted proportion of Scissor+ cells in the bulk RNA-seq datasets, and assess their association with patient prognosis. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and log-rank tests were used to evaluate prognostic significance of CRIP2 expression in prostate cancer patients.

ST analysis

Spatial data were preprocessed using Seurat (version 5.1.0). Low-quality spots with <300 detected genes or >30% mitochondrial content were removed. SCTransform normalization was applied, followed by PCA and clustering using FindClusters. UMAP and SpatialDimPlot were used for dimensionality reduction and spatial visualization. The “AddModuleScore” function was used to calculate the Scissor+ enrichment scores across spatial regions based on differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Machine learning-based modeling

Using TCGA-Prostate Adenocarcinoma (PRAD) dataset as the training set, we applied 98 algorithmic combinations to select the optimal predictors of prognosis. Model performance was evaluated across the internal and external validation cohorts (Taylor, CPC, GSE54460, CIT, DKFZ, and Stockholm) based on the concordance index (C-index). The final ORAG risk model was developed using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) + random survival forest (RSF) combination with the best predictive performance.

Nomogram construction

A nomogram model integrating risk scores and clinicopathologic features [the Gleason score and American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) stage] was developed to predict biochemical recurrence (BCR). Model calibration and time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to evaluate predictive accuracy for BCR.

Functional enrichment analysis

The DEGs in the Scissor+ cells were functionally annotated using the DisGeNET module on the Metascape platform (24) (http://metascape.org), providing disease-relevant biological insights.

Western blot analysis

To evaluate the knockdown efficiency of CRIP2, three siRNA fragments (si-1, si-2, si-3) were transfected into C4-2B and LNCaP cells for 48 hours. Total protein was extracted and quantified using a BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated with primary antibodies: anti-CRIP2 (Abclonal, A9038, Rabbit pAb, 1:1,000) and anti-GAPDH (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, Mouse mAb, 1:25,000). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies: HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Boster, BA1055, 1:10,000) and HRP-conjugated AffiniPure Goat Anti-Mouse IgG (H + L) (Boster, BA1050, 1:10,000). Bands were visualized using an ECL detection system, and ImageJ software was used for densitometric quantification of protein expression. GAPDH served as a loading control.

Drug sensitivity assays

To assess the effect of CRIP2 silencing on olaparib sensitivity, LNCaP and C4-2B cells were treated under the following three conditions: control [negative control (NC)], olaparib, and olaparib + siCRIP2. The cells were seeded into 96-well plates, and cell viability was measured at 0, 24, 48, 96, and 120 hours using the Cell Counting Kit 8 (CCK-8) assay. The optical density at 450 nm (OD450) values were used to quantify proliferation and assess treatment response.

Statistical analysis

All the statistical analyses and data visualizations were conducted using R software (version 4.5.1), GraphPad Prism (version 9.0), and the CNSknowall platform (https://cnsknowall.com). Group comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were plotted and compared using the log-rank test. A two-sided P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The design of this study is outlined in Figure 1A. By integrating the scRNA-seq data from six CRPC patients, we systematically analyzed the cellular heterogeneity in PCa tissues, identifying multiple cell subpopulations, including epithelial cells, immune cells, and stromal cells. UMAP visualizations demonstrated excellent consistency across the patient samples (Figure 1B). A total of 14 cell clusters were identified (Figure 1C), which were further annotated into nine major cell types, including luminal epithelial cells, basal cells, T cells, B cells, Mast cells, myeloid cells, endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and myofibroblasts (Figure 1D). Cell type identification was based on the expression of classical marker genes, and the results were visually represented with bubble plots, which highlighted the distinct expression profiles of different cell populations (Figure 1E). Focusing on the tumor epithelial cell population, UMAP dimensionality reduction further subdivided these cells into five functionally heterogeneous subpopulations (Figure 1F), suggesting that tumor epithelial cells may play distinct biological roles in the progression of CRPC.

Identification and spatial distribution of olaparib-resistant Scissor+ cells in tumor epithelial cells of CRPC

To explore the cellular mechanisms underlying olaparib resistance, we applied the Scissor algorithm to integrate CRPC single-cell transcriptomics with bulk RNA-seq samples and olaparib IC50 phenotype data. This analysis successfully identified a Scissor+ cell population in tumor epithelial cells that was significantly associated with the resistant phenotype (Figure 2A). Functional annotation using the DisGeNET database revealed that the Scissor+ cells were significantly enriched in disease pathways closely linked to PCa progression, suggesting that these cells may play a pivotal role in tumor resistance and malignant progression (Figure 2B).

Next, we used the BayesPrism method to quantify the proportion of Scissor+ cells in bulk transcriptome samples. Quality control plots and gene expression consistency assessments (Figure 2C-2E) showed that BayesPrism had high deconvolution accuracy across various gene types, including protein-coding genes and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs). The Kaplan-Meier survival analysis revealed that patients with a higher proportion of Scissor+ cells had a significantly worse prognosis (Figure 2F).

Further ST analysis was performed to investigate the spatial localization of Scissor+ cells. UMAP dimensionality reduction displayed the distribution patterns of different cell subpopulations in spatial tissue sections (Figure 2G,2H), revealing clear segregation between tumor regions, mixed regions, and surrounding tissue areas (Figure 2I). The spatial mapping of the Scissor+ scores (Figure 2J) further confirmed the high local expression of the Scissor+ cells in the tumor tissue, suggesting that this subpopulation may play a significant pathological role in the resistant microenvironment.

Development and multi-cohort validation of an ORAG model based on integrated machine-learning approaches

To systematically identify the key gene combinations associated with Olaparib resistance and those with prognostic predictive value, we conducted an extensive screening of 98 machine-learning algorithm combinations in TCGA-PRAD cohort. Among these, the integration of the LASSO and RSF algorithms achieved the best performance based on the C-index, balancing variable selection capability and model stability (Figure 3A). In the optimized RSF model, the error rate markedly decreased and plateaued with an increasing number of decision trees, indicating strong generalizability (Figure 3B, left panel). The variable importance analysis ranked CRIP2 as the top predictor, suggesting it is the most critical risk-associated gene in the ORAG model (Figure 3B, right panel).

To assess the robustness of the ORAG model, we performed external validation in six independent bulk RNA-seq cohorts (Taylor, GSE54460, CPC, CIT, DKFZ, and Stockholm). The Kaplan-Meier survival curves showed that the model could effectively stratify patients into high- and low-risk groups, with higher risk scores significantly associated with a poorer prognosis (Figure 3C-3I, survival curves). The time-dependent ROC curve analysis further showed that the ORAG model achieved consistently high area under the curve (AUC) values across the cohorts, showing its strong predictive performance and potential for clinical translation (Figure 3C-3I, ROC curves). Collectively, these results highlight the cross-cohort robustness and high generalizability of the ORAG model, providing a promising tool for risk stratification and personalized management in CRPC patients.

Construction of a nomogram integrating clinical features and the ORAG score to predict BCR in PCa

To enhance the clinical applicability of the ORAG model, we developed a personalized nomogram for predicting BCR by integrating the ORAG risk score with key clinicopathological variables, including the Gleason score and AJCC stage, based on the multivariate Cox regression analysis (Figure 4A). The nomogram generated a total score for each patient to estimate the probability of being BCR-free at 1, 3, and 5 years, offering strong clinical interpretability and practicality. The calibration curve analysis demonstrated excellent agreement between the predicted and observed probabilities of BCR-free survival at all the evaluated time points, confirming the predictive accuracy of the model across different temporal scales (Figure 4B). Further, the time-dependent ROC curve analysis revealed that the integrated nomogram model outperformed individual clinical indicators such as the Gleason score and AJCC stage at 1-, 3-, and 5-year time points (Figure 4C), with significantly higher AUC values, underscoring its potential clinical utility in the recurrence risk stratification of PCa patients.

CRIP2 functions as a core ORAG driving olaparib resistance and tumor progression in PCa

To elucidate the functional relevance of the core genes in the ORAG model in the development of olaparib resistance, we performed a differential expression analysis using the GSE189186 dataset, comparing olaparib-resistant and wild-type LNCaP and C4-2B PCa cell lines. A total of 202 genes were upregulated in the resistant LNCaP cells, while 145 genes were upregulated in the resistant C4-2B cells. Notably, CRIP2 was consistently and significantly upregulated in both cell lines under the resistant condition, which suggests that it has a potential role in mediating olaparib resistance (Figure 5A).

Next, we integrated the resistance-associated DEGs with key predictive genes from the ORAG model via a Venn diagram, identifying CRIP2 as a shared core gene at the intersection (Figure 5B). The univariate Cox regression analysis showed that CRIP2 had the highest hazard ratio (HR) among all the candidate genes, establishing it as a strong adverse prognostic factor (Figure 5C). Further, in the RSF model variable importance ranking (Figure 3B, right panel), CRIP2 was ranked as the top predictor, reinforcing its central role in the ORAG model. The Kaplan-Meier survival analyses across multiple public datasets, including TCGA, confirmed that high CRIP2 expression was significantly associated with a shorter progression-free interval (PFI), supporting its value as an independent prognostic marker (Figure 5D and Figure S1). Additionally, in TCGA clinical samples, CRIP2 expression was positively correlated with multiple adverse pathological features, including a higher T stage (Figure 5E), N stage (Figure 5F), clinical stage (Figure 5G), Gleason score (Figure 5H), and elevated PSA levels (Figure 5I), further underscoring its critical role in PCa progression and the development of resistance to olaparib.

As Figure 6A,6B show, all three siRNAs significantly reduced CRIP2 expression compared to the NC group (P<0.001), with consistent knockdown efficiency observed in both cell lines. To investigate the functional effect of CRIP2 depletion on cell proliferation, two siRNAs with the most effective knockdown efficiency (si1 and si3) were selected for CCK-8 assays. As Figure 6C shows, treatment with olaparib alone significantly inhibited cell proliferation in both the C4-2B and LNCaP cells. Notably, CRIP2 knockdown further enhanced the olaparib-induced suppression of cell proliferation, suggesting that CRIP2 contributes to resistance mechanisms and its silencing sensitizes PCa cells to PARP inhibition.

CRIP2 expression is associated with DNA repair activity and uncovers a CRIP2-GPX4 axis driving olaparib resistance

To assess the relationship between CRIP2 and DNA repair, we first interrogated the CancerSEA database (25). DNA repair activity was quantified using the z-score algorithm proposed by Lee et al. (26), implemented through the GSVA R package (27). After normalization with the scale function, gene set scores were derived and correlated with gene expression using Pearson analysis. Across TCGA data, CRIP2 showed a strong positive correlation with DNA repair scores, a finding that was consistently validated in external cohorts including GSE54460, GSE111177, and ICGA (Figure 7A-7D). These results implicate CRIP2 as a potential enhancer of DNA repair activity and a candidate driver of resistance to the PARP inhibitor olaparib in prostate cancer.

Gene set intersection analysis identified 35 overlapping candidates between CRIP2-associated and DNA repair–related genes (Figure 7E). PPI network construction revealed that CRIP2 occupied a central node within the DNA repair interactome, underscoring its likely regulatory significance (Figure 7F). Heatmap visualization further confirmed distinct expression patterns of these candidate genes between CRIP2-high and CRIP2-low tumors (Figure 7G). Unexpectedly, CRIP2 expression was also strongly correlated with GPX4 (R=0.411, P<0.001) (Figure 7H), a master regulator of ferroptosis and redox balance. GPX4 safeguards membrane integrity by detoxifying lipid peroxides and is indispensable for oxidative stress tolerance and DNA damage protection (28,29). Notably, GPX4 inhibition has been shown to dramatically potentiate the efficacy of PARP inhibitors in several malignancies (28,30,31). Taken together, these findings suggest that CRIP2 may drive olaparib resistance by upregulating GPX4, thereby strengthening antioxidant defenses and DNA repair pathways while blunting PARP inhibitor-induced DNA damage. The identification of a CRIP2-GPX4 axis highlights a previously unrecognized mechanism of therapeutic resistance and offers a rationale for targeting ferroptosis resistance to improve PARP inhibitor efficacy.

Discussion

The application of PARPis in PCa has garnered increasing attention in recent years, particularly in the context of CRPC. Molecular studies have revealed that approximately 10% of localized PCa patients and up to 20–30% of metastatic patients harbor deficiencies in DDR, commonly involving mutations in HRR genes such as BRCA2, BRCA1, ATM, and CDK12 (8,32). These findings provide a strong rationale for introducing PARPis into CRPC treatment, as tumors with HRR defects are more susceptible to DNA damage induced by PARP inhibition, resulting in synthetic lethality and selective tumor cell death (33,34).

Initial translational studies, including phase II trials such as TOPARP, TRITON2, TALAPRO-1, and GALAHAD, have shown the anti-tumor activity of PARPi monotherapy in metastatic CRPC (35-39). However, despite the initial efficacy of PARPis in HRD-positive tumors, resistance to PARPis is almost inevitable. A substantial proportion of HRD-positive tumors exhibit innate resistance, and even initially responsive cases frequently acquire resistance after several months of treatment (40). Deciphering the mechanisms underlying PARPi resistance remains challenging due to tumor genomic heterogeneity and the multifactorial nature of resistance pathways. Moreover, obtaining post-resistance tumor samples for molecular analysis is often impractical.

The advent of scRNA-seq and ST has enabled the high-resolution dissection of the tumor microenvironment, offering new insights into cell-type-specific dynamics, functional states, and spatial architecture. Scissor, an algorithm that integrates scRNA-seq, bulk transcriptomic data, and clinical phenotypes, can accurately identify subpopulations associated with specific clinical outcomes (23). Meanwhile, BayesPrism employs a Bayesian framework to deconvolute bulk RNA-seq data, facilitating the inference of cell-type-specific expression profiles and uncovering functionally relevant resistant cell populations (41).

In this study, we conducted an integrative multi-omics analysis to systematically identify the CRPC epithelial subpopulations associated with olaparib resistance at single-cell resolution. Using the Scissor algorithm, we precisely delineated a Scissor+ subpopulation highly correlated with Olaparib resistance. Coupled with BayesPrism deconvolution and ST, we examined the spatial enrichment and adverse prognostic implications of these cells. Subsequently, we constructed a robust ORAG signature and identified CRIP2 as a core component.

CRIP2, a LIM domain protein with regulatory functions in cytoskeletal dynamics, transcription, and signaling (42), is generally regarded as a tumor suppressor. The downregulation of CRIP2 has been reported in several malignancies, including nasopharyngeal carcinoma and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (43,44), and low CRIP2 expression is associated with increased proliferation, migration, invasion, and a poor prognosis. The re-expression of CRIP2 has been shown to reverse these phenotypes and promote apoptosis. Similar anti-tumor effects have been observed in breast cancer, where miR-449a-mediated suppression of CRIP2 was shown to facilitate recurrence and metastasis (45).

Notably, CRIP2 has context-dependent oncogenic potential. For instance, in non-small cell lung cancer, CRIP2 is upregulated in radiotherapy-resistant cell lines and is implicated in radio-resistance (46). In hematologic malignancies, the epigenetic inactivation of PRC2 leads to CRIP2 overexpression, which in turn induces TRAP1 and suppresses mitochondrial apoptosis, contributing to chemotherapy resistance (47). These findings suggest that CRIP2 may enhance tumor cell survival under stress by regulating mitochondrial pathways, stress responses, or metabolic and inflammatory signals.

Thus, CRIP2 functions appear to be highly context-specific, shaped by the surrounding signaling networks. Its ability to modulate key pathways such as NF-κB inflammation, HIF-1α-driven metabolic adaptation, and mitochondrial-dependent apoptosis may determine its dual roles as a tumor suppressor or oncogene (42,46). Clarifying these mechanisms in different tumor ecosystems will be pivotal for the rational development of CRIP2-targeted therapies.

Our multi-omics analysis revealed that CRIP2 was significantly upregulated in malignant tissues compared to benign prostatic hyperplasia tissues in PCa. The multivariate Cox regression analysis showed that high CRIP2 expression serves as an independent adverse prognostic factor even after adjusting for age, the Gleason score, and TMPRSS2-ERG fusion status (HR >1.3). Further, high CRIP2 expression was found to be correlated with shortened progression-free survival and unfavorable clinicopathological features (e.g., a high Gleason grade, an advanced T/N stage, and elevated PSA levels) (48), supporting its potential as both a resistance-associated target and a prognostic biomarker.

At the functional level, CRIP2 was markedly upregulated in CRPC cell lines with acquired resistance to olaparib. In vitro experiments demonstrated that CRIP2 knockdown significantly sensitized resistant cells to olaparib, underscoring its pivotal role in modulating drug resistance. By integrating single-cell and bulk transcriptomic data, we delineated the cellular states associated with olaparib resistance and identified CRIP2 as a key driver of this phenotype. Based on these findings, we developed the ORAG model, which proved to be an effective tool for prognosis prediction and for guiding precision therapy with PARP inhibitors. Moreover, CRIP2 may act as a promising therapeutic target to enhance treatment efficacy and overcome resistance in CRPC. Mechanistically, we further found that CRIP2 upregulates GPX4 expression, thereby strengthening cellular antioxidant defenses and DNA repair capacity, which in turn attenuates olaparib-induced DNA damage and promotes resistance. Collectively, these results highlight the CRIP2-GPX4 axis as a crucial determinant of PARP inhibitor efficacy and provide a rationale for novel combination strategies aimed at suppressing ferroptosis resistance to improve therapeutic outcomes.

At the same time, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the single-cell transcriptomic analysis was based on only six CRPC patient samples, which may restrict the generalizability of the identified olaparib resistance-associated epithelial subpopulation (Scissor+). Validation in larger and more diverse patient cohorts will therefore be needed to confirm the robustness of these findings. Second, the functional validation of CRIP2 in mediating olaparib resistance was performed exclusively in vitro using prostate cancer cell lines. Although these results strongly support the role of CRIP2, the absence of in vivo validation means that the influence of the tumor microenvironment and other resistance mechanisms may not have been fully captured. Future work should thus incorporate in vivo experiments, such as mouse xenografts or patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, to further assess the impact of CRIP2 modulation on olaparib sensitivity under physiologically relevant conditions. Such efforts will be essential for clarifying the translational potential of CRIP2 and for guiding the rational design of therapeutic strategies to overcome PARPi resistance in CRPC.

Conclusions

This study represents the first comprehensive multi-omics investigation to elucidate the cellular and molecular basis of olaparib resistance in CRPC. By integrating single-cell transcriptomics, ST, and bulk RNA-seq data, we successfully identified a distinct drug-resistant epithelial subpopulation (Scissor+ cells) enriched in tumor regions and associated with a poor prognosis. The ORAG model, which was developed using LASSO and RSF algorithms, showed robust prognostic performance across multiple independent cohorts, offering a reliable tool for patient risk stratification.

Notably, we identified CRIP2 as a core resistance-associated gene that is significantly upregulated in olaparib-resistant PCa cells. Functional assays confirmed that CRIP2 knockdown markedly increased cellular sensitivity to olaparib, underscoring its potential as both a prognostic biomarker and a therapeutic target. These findings not only extend our understanding of drug resistance mechanisms in CRPC but also pave the way for CRIP2-targeted strategies to enhance the efficacy of PARPi-based therapies. Future work should focus on elucidating the molecular pathways regulated by CRIP2 and validating its clinical utility in prospective studies.
