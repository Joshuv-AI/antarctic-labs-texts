Image optimization for low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using deep learning: a pilot study
Authors: Quinton J. Keigley, Leah C. Henze Bancroft, Kelley Salem, Kuan-Hao Su, Abolfazl Mehranian, Matthew G. Spangler-Bickell, Alan B. McMillan, Scott B. Perlman, Roberta M. Strigel, Tyler J. Bradshaw, Samuel A. Hurley, Lu Mao, Amy M. Fowler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPositron emission tomography with magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI) provides noninvasive molecular characterization of breast cancer and has the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy, staging, treatment response assessment, and guide personalized care. Reducing the radiation dose from 2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose (18F-FDG) to a level similar to digital mammography while maintaining image quality may facilitate clinical utilization. This study was performed to evaluate diagnostic image quality and lesion conspicuity of low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using denoising and to evaluate the effect of denoising on radiotracer uptake semi-quantification.MethodsThis pilot study was a secondary analysis of a single-institution prospective study of 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI for 23 women with primary invasive breast cancer. Random undersampling of the PET data from a 30-min simultaneous prone 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI was used to produce simulated low-dose (SLD) images approximating 90% reduced injected activity (37 MBq). A deep learning-based, denoising (DN) algorithm was then applied. SLD and DN datasets were evaluated by three readers for image quality and lesion conspicuity and were compared to full-dose images to assess clinical acceptability. Image noise was quantified by liver SUVstandard deviation. 18F-FDG uptake (SUVmax and SUVmean) was measured in tumor and normal breast tissue and compared between datasets. Wilcoxon signed rank test, repeated measures analysis of variance, and Bland–Altman analyses were performed.ResultsCompared to SLD, DN datasets scored better for artifacts, perceived signal-to-noise ratio, image sharpness/resolution, and overall image quality (p < 0.001). Compared to full-dose images, DN scored better than SLD for diagnostic image quality (p < 0.001) and lesion conspicuity (p = 0.002). For diagnostic image quality, DN was equivalent or slightly inferior to full-dose images in 89.9% (62/69) of reads. Readers preferred DN (76.8%; 53/69) to SLD (4.3%; 3/69), and both were preferred equally in 10.1% (7/69) of reads. Compared to SLD, quantitative image noise was less in DN images (p < 0.0001). Agreement was excellent between all datasets for tumor SUVmax and SUVmean.ConclusionsAcceptable image quality may be achieved with low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using denoising, which warrants further prospective validation.

Background

Breast cancer is the most common cancer type in females (excluding non-melanoma skin cancer) and is the highest cause of cancer death for women younger than 50 years old [1]. Advancements in screening mammography have allowed for earlier detection and treatment of breast cancer. Locoregional evaluation for detection, diagnosis, staging, and treatment monitoring are achieved with mammography, digital breast tomosynthesis, ultrasound, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Additional vascular-based and functional breast imaging techniques, such as contrast-enhanced mammography, molecular breast imaging, positron emission mammography (PEM), and dedicated breast positron emission tomography (PET), have been developed to supplement traditional anatomic imaging [2–4].

2-deoxy-2-[18F]fluoro-D-glucose (18F-FDG) PET with computed tomography (PET/CT) is used for staging of newly diagnosed locally advanced (clinical stage IIB to III) and inflammatory breast cancer, therapy monitoring, and detection of recurrence [5]. Combining PET with MRI provides noninvasive molecular characterization of breast cancer and has the potential to improve diagnostic accuracy for staging and treatment response assessment, and to guide personalized care [6–10]. Furthermore, whole-body 18F-FDG PET/MRI has approximately half the radiation dose of whole-body PET/CT (mean effective dose 9.3 mSv versus 17.6 mSv, respectively) [11]. However, the radiation dose from PET/MRI still exceeds other breast imaging modalities with limited clinical utilization in part due to current radiation exposure [8, 12]. For example, effective dose estimates are 0.56 mSv for screen-film mammography [12], 0.44–0.49 mSv for full-field digital mammography [12, 13], 0.60 mSv for digital breast tomosynthesis alone [13], 1.13 mSv for combined full-field digital mammography plus digital breast tomosynthesis [13], 0.70 mSv for contrast-enhanced mammography [13], and 2.0–2.5 mSv for molecular breast imaging with 99mTc-sestamibi [3]. In comparison, effective dose estimates are 8.2–9.9 mSv for whole-body 18F-FDG PET/MRI [11] and 3.6–7.3 mSv for prone 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI [14]. Thus, there is a need for methods to reduce overall radiation dose associated with PET/MRI without losing image quality.

Deep learning techniques have the potential to improve PET image acquisition and processing through multilevel implementation [15–17]. Using deep learning algorithms to mitigate image noise may facilitate lower administered radiopharmaceutical activity without a negative impact on reader interpretation [18]. Coupling PET with MRI in place of CT can further reduce radiation exposure to patients, which is particularly relevant for pediatric populations and for radiosensitive organs such as the breast. While most deep learning models have been trained with and utilized for PET/CT, there are relatively few investigations into the performance of deep learning models applied to PET/MRI [19–21]. Furthermore, the feasibility of deep learning-based techniques for enhancing image quality at lower radiation doses has not been specifically applied to breast PET/MRI. Potential clinical relevance of low-dose breast PET/MRI includes preoperative staging and treatment planning, therapy response assessment, and high-risk screening, for which radiation dose concerns has been one barrier to clinical adoption [6, 8]. The overall goal of this research was to determine if a deep learning approach can effectively reduce the radiation dose of 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI to a level similar to mammography, while preserving image quality. The purpose of this pilot study was to evaluate diagnostic image quality and lesion conspicuity of low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using a deep learning-based image enhancement model to reduce noise and to evaluate the effect of denoising on radiotracer uptake semi-quantification.

Methods

Study design

This study was performed as a retrospective secondary analysis of data from a completed single-institution, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant, institutional review board–approved, prospective study conducted between January 2016 and August 2018 with detailed methods previously published [22]. Briefly, women with biopsy-proven invasive breast cancer measuring at least 10 mm in size by imaging were included. This size cut-off was chosen to avoid partial volume effects on SUV semi-quantification. Fasting women underwent prone simultaneous breast PET/MRI using an eight-channel breast coil (SIGNA PET/MR, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA). Uptake time was 83 ± 8 min (range, 75–113 min) after injection of 380.4 ± 26.4 MBq (range 320.7–427.2 MBq) of 18F-FDG (SOFIE, Romeoville, IL, USA). A single PET bed was acquired for 30 min simultaneously during MRI acquisition. MRI sequences consisted of Dixon-based fat and water images for attenuation correction and a standard clinical protocol for dynamic contrast enhanced breast MRI with gadobenate dimeglumine 0.1 mmol/kg (MultiHance; Bracco Diagnostics, Milan, Italy).

For this pilot study, PET/MRI data from all 23 women (median age 49 years; IQR, 41–59) with 24 biopsy-proven breast malignancies were included from the primary study, which has been previously reported [22]. The primary study publication focused on the validation of simultaneous time-of-flight breast PET/MRI for quantitative evaluation of glucose uptake in primary breast cancer using 18F-FDG at the current clinical standard level of injected activity, whereas this manuscript focuses on radiation dose reduction and evaluation of image quality. As previously published (Table 1), the majority of tumors were invasive ductal carcinoma (16/24; 67%), were grades 1 or 2 (18/24; 75%), were estrogen and progesterone receptor positive, and were human epidermal growth factor 2 negative (19/24; 79%). Image processing

Retrospective random undersampling of the list-mode PET data was used to produce simulated low-dose PET images [23]. PET data were segmented into very short time intervals and randomly selected to be included into the final sinogram to achieve a target fraction of approximately 1/10th the events acquired in the fully sampled study. This approach for undersampling has an advantage over simply shortening the duration by 90%, which will capture motion and other artifacts inherent with a longer PET acquisition bed duration.

PET images were reconstructed from list-mode data using: 600 mm field-of-view, 256 × 256 matrix, 28 subsets, 2 iterations, time-of-flight ordered-subset expectation maximization with point spread function correction (TOFOSEM). Additionally, the full-dose dataset was reconstructed using time-of-flight block sequential regularized expectation maximization with point spread function correction (TOFBSREM; Q.Clear) and β = 350.

A vendor-provided, deep learning-based, denoising algorithm (GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL, USA) was applied to the simulated low-dose images [24]. This investigational (not commercially available) deep learning enhancement model (DLE-standard version) was trained using multi-center, multi-scanner whole-body oncology 18F-FDG PET/CT exams to produce BSREM-like output images from conventional OSEM input images, as previously described [24]. Briefly, the DLE model is a three-dimensional residual convolutional encoder-decoder (U-Net) network trained through supervised learning cycles with partial-duration (high noise) input images reconstructed with OSEM and full-duration (low noise) reference images reconstructed with BRSEM, to produce a model which outputs BSREM-like denoised images (OSEM + DLE) [24]. Given similar injected activity levels (380.4 ± 26.4 MBq for this study, 391 ± 136 MBq for the DLE model training data) and longer scan duration for the breast PET/MRI data (mean 30 min fully sampled, 3 min at 1/10 undersampling) compared to the whole-body PET/CT data used to train the DLE model, the simulated low-dose level in this study is above the minimum range of the training distribution.

The current study focused on evaluating the DLE-standard version model for breast PET/MRI data. Three PET datasets were generated for each patient participant: 1) simulated low-dose images without denoising (SLD), 2) simulated low-dose images with denoising (DN), and 3) full-dose images.

Radiation dose

Effective dose (mSv) was calculated as the administered activity (MBq) multiplied by 0.019 mSv/MBq for 18F-FDG (based on the International Commission on Radiological Protection publication 128) [25].

Image assessment

A standardized display protocol was used with Mirada XD software version 3.6.11 (Mirada Medical, Oxford, United Kingdom) (Fig. 1). The SLD and DN datasets were displayed in a blinded, randomized order and independently reviewed by three board-certified radiologists and/or nuclear medicine physicians with 3 to 41 years’ experience (QJK, AMF, SBP). In a single reading session, the readers rated the SLD and DN datasets using a 5-point scale, with 1 being the worst and 5 the best. Images were evaluated for artifacts, perceived signal-to-noise ratio, image sharpness/resolution, and overall image quality. The readers also rated the diagnostic image quality and lesion conspicuity in SLD and DN compared to the full-dose acquisition (TOFBSREM reconstruction) as a measure of clinical acceptability. Image noise quantification

Five non-overlapping 2-cm diameter spherical volumes-of-interest were placed in the right hepatic lobe of the liver across multiple axial slices for each participant to obtain SUVstandard deviation. Volumes-of interest avoided edges, large veins and bile ducts. SUVstandard deviation were averaged for each subject.

Tumor and normal breast standardized uptake value (SUV) semi-quantification

Volumes-of-interest for tumor and normal tissue were created using the full-dose acquisition dataset (TOFOSEM reconstruction) to obtain maximum standardized uptake values (SUVmax) and mean standardized uptake values (SUVmean). For the biopsy-proven malignant breast lesions, volumes-of-interest were drawn with an isocontour threshold of 30% of the lesion’s SUVmax. For normal contralateral breast fibroglandular tissue (FGT), a 1-cm diameter spherical volume-of-interest was used. These measurements were performed by two board-certified radiologists and/or nuclear medicine physicians with 3 to 11 years’ experience (QJK, AMF). The volumes-of-interest were automatically propagated to the SLD and DN datasets.

Statistical analysis

For this retrospective secondary analysis, the sample size was fixed by the primary published study [22]. Ordinal scores were summarized by mean and standard deviation. Reader-averaged scores were compared between datasets using the Wilcoxon signed rank test. The win ratio for DN or SLD dataset preference, treating equivalent and neither as ties was calculated [26]. The combined agreement between all individual readers was assessed with Fleiss’ kappa [27] and percentage of reader consensus. Bland–Altman analysis with intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) was performed to compare SUV measurements in the malignant breast lesions and normal breast tissue between image datasets [28]. Bland–Altman analysis with ICC was also performed using log-transformed data. Between-dataset agreement was considered “excellent” for ICC ≥ 0.90, “good” for 0.75 ≤ ICC < 0.90, “moderate” for 0.50 ≤ ICC < 0.75, and “poor” for ICC < 0.50 [29]. Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was used to test the differences of SUVstandard deviation on SLD, DN, and full-dose datasets. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used to test the differences of SUVmean and SUVmax on SLD, DN, and full-dose datasets, followed by pairwise (paired) t-tests with Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Radiation dose

For the full-dose dataset, the effective dose (mean ± standard deviation) was 7.23 ± 0.50 mSv (range 6.09–8.1 mSv) for 380.4 ± 26.4 MBq (range 320.7–427.2 MBq) of actual injected 18F-FDG activity. The effective dose for the SLD dataset was 0.72 ± 0.05 mSv (range 0.61–0.81 mSv) for 38.0 ± 2.6 MBq simulated injected 18F-FDG activity (range 32.1–42.7 MBq).

Image assessment

There were 23 breast PET/MRI exams and three readers for a total of 69 reads. Compared to SLD, the DN datasets had less artifacts (p < 0.001), improved perceived signal-to-noise ratio (p < 0.001), better image sharpness/resolution (p < 0.001), and overall better image quality (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Compared to the full-dose acquisition, DN scored better than SLD for diagnostic image quality (p < 0.001) and lesion conspicuity (p = 0.002) (Table 2). For diagnostic image quality, DN was rated as equivalent or slightly inferior to full-dose images in 89.9% (62/69) of the reads. Overall, the readers preferred DN (76.8%; 53/69) to SLD (4.3%; 3/69) in the majority of the reads, and both datasets were preferred equally in 10.1% (7/69) of the reads. The win ratio for preference of DN over SLD was 17.67 (95% CI: 5.52–56.53). Two readers recorded that neither SLD nor DN were preferred in the remaining 8.7% (6/69) of the reads. Per the reader questionnaires, images were given unfavorable scores due to artifacts (e.g., image streaking, haze around breast; Fig. 2) and difficulty detecting smaller lesions due to image smoothing. The between-reader Fleiss kappa was 0.034 (p = 0.676) with low subject-to-subject variation due to predominance of DN preference. Discussion

This pilot study aimed to compare image quality and radiotracer uptake semi-quantification of simulated low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI with and without a denoising algorithm in women with biopsy-proven primary invasive breast cancer. A simulated 90% reduced injected radiopharmaceutical activity, used as an approximation of the effective dose of mammography-based techniques, resulted in an effective dose of 0.72 ± 0.05 mSv. Compared to simulated low-dose images without denoising (SLD), the DN datasets had less artifacts, improved perceived signal-to-noise ratio, better image sharpness/resolution, and overall better image quality. Regarding image quality, the readers preferred DN over SLD (76.8% versus 4.3%, respectively). Overall, DN was equivalent or slightly inferior to full-dose images in 89.9% (62/69) of the reads. Objective noise measurements confirmed the denoising capability of the deep learning method and were concordant with the subjective reader study results of better perceived signal-to-noise ratio of the DN dataset compared to SLD. Compared to DN, SLD and full-dose images had higher SUVmax for tumors. Compared to SLD, DN and full-dose images had lower SUVmax for normal breast. These results for SLD images are expected since SUVmax is determined by a single voxel, affected by statistical fluctuation, and thus sensitive to image noise. There was no significant difference in SUVmean for tumors and normal breast between all three datasets. For tumor SUVmax and SUVmean, agreement was excellent between all three datasets.

Concerns regarding radiation dose exposure may be impeding clinical implementation of radionuclide-based breast imaging modalities such as molecular breast imaging, PEM, high-resolution dedicated breast PET, and breast PET/MRI [8, 12]. This issue has motivated research into strategies for lower radiotracer doses. In the study by Satoh et al., the authors demonstrated that 25% of the standard injected activity of 18F-FDG, which has an estimated effective dose of 0.9 mSv for a 60 kg person, yielded clinically acceptable image quality for dedicated breast PET [30]. Furthermore, Freitas and colleagues investigated the feasibility of reduced-dose 18F-FDG PEM for detecting newly-diagnosed breast cancer [31]. They reduced the injected activity from 370 MBq, with an approximate effective dose of 6.2–7.1 mSv, down to 37–185 MBq, with an effective dose of 0.62–0.71 mSv to 1.24–1.42 mSv. Compared to the detection rate of concurrently performed breast MRI (25/25; 100%), known malignant breast lesions were identified in 24 of the 25 patients (96%) undergoing reduced-dose PEM.

There has been one previously published investigation into dose reduction for breast PET/MRI. Sah et al. investigated the feasibility of lowering18F-FDG injected activity in time-of-flight breast PET/MRI by randomly removing counts from the list mode PET data to produce simulated dose-reduced PET images [14]. This retrospective study of 26 women with breast cancer showed that simulated lower dose PET images were clinically acceptable for interpretation [14]. By reducing the injected radiopharmaceutical down to 10% of the standard activity, the effective dose is lowered to about 0.5 mSv (range 0.4–0.7 mSv), which is comparable to mammography (0.44–0.56 mSv) [14]. However, a drawback of dose reduction strategies is increased image noise [32].

The present study lends additional evidence supporting the acceptability of low-dose 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI and expands the current literature by incorporating deep learning to improve low-dose image quality. Deep learning enhancement techniques have been growing in PET technology because of their positive impact on image acquisition and processing with an added advantage of noise reduction [32]. The focus of this pilot study was on the application of a deep learning enhancement model trained using PET/CT data for breast PET/MRI. Our results demonstrated that DN was superior to SLD in all individually assessed categories including artifacts, perceived signal-to-noise ratio, image sharpness/resolution, and lesion conspicuity. These results support that deep learning can mitigate the expected tradeoff of lower injected radioactivity and increased image noise. Potential benefits of noise reduction in PET are lower injected radiopharmaceutical activity (i.e., reduced radiation exposure to the patient and technologist) or shorter image acquisition time (i.e., less scanner time for improved patient comfort, less motion artifacts, enhanced patient access for scheduling) [33, 34].

This study has some limitations. The sample size is relatively small and constrained by the number of participants imaged with 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI in the original study [22]. However, the sample size is comparable to other feasibility studies addressing dose reduction for PET/MRI which range from 20 to 30 patients [14, 19–21]. As with other proof-of-concept studies, the retrospective approach of using undersampled PET data represents a best approximation of expected results obtained using lower injected FDG activity but requires further prospective validation. Also, the effect of denoising on small lesions was not evaluated since an inclusion criterion of the original prospective study was a tumor size of at least 1 cm to avoid partial volume effects on SUV semi-quantification [22]. This research reports the performance of one vendor’s denoising algorithm using a single PET/MRI system with time-of-flight capability which may limit generalizability to other PET/MRI systems. Although different reconstruction algorithms were used in the reader study for the low-dose images (OSEM) and the full-dose images (BSREM), this approach is consistent with how the DLE model was trained [24] and thus is not expected to introduce significant bias. Lastly, the reported results primarily reflect image quality preference rather than diagnostic accuracy for cancer detection.

Based on the results of this pilot study, acceptable image quality may be attained for 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using lower doses with denoising. While this study focused on one technique (denoising) for preserving image quality at the effective dose of mammography, other technical approaches may also be feasible for reducing the effective dose of 18F-FDG PET/MRI. Additional next steps could include comparisons of the DLE model with other established reconstruction or smoothing techniques such as reconstructing the SLD data using TOFBSREM and using post-reconstruction Gaussian filtering. The prospect of low-dose PET images in 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI could help extend its current indications and clinical applicability. Referring clinicians and patients would have the reassurance of a radiation exposure on par with mammography-based techniques without sacrificing image integrity that is crucial for lesion detection, lesion assessment, and appropriate management recommendations. The next step to validate these promising initial findings and further assess clinical value would require a larger, prospective study with an actual 90% reduction in 18F-FDG injected activity or a second scan 6 h post-injection when the full dose has decayed to 10%. Future trial designs should also include a real-world clinical setting involving patients with no restrictions on lesion size and a broadened scope to include lesion detection, false-negative, and false-positive assessments. Further dose reduction, such as ultra-low dose PET/MRI with 1% standard radiotracer injected activity, may be possible using artificial intelligence transformers [20] and would enable broader applications to include screening patients at elevated lifetime risk of breast cancer. Lastly, this concept could apply to other radiopharmaceuticals used in breast PET imaging, such as 16α-[18F]fluoro-17β-estradiol.

Conclusions

Acceptable imaging quality may be achieved in 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI using lower doses with denoising. By reducing the injected radiopharmaceutical to 10% of the full-dose activity in 18F-FDG breast PET/MRI, the effective dose is lowered to a level comparable to digital mammography which supports further investigation of the clinical utility of this hybrid technique for patients with dual indications for breast MRI and PET/CT. Larger trials are needed to prospectively evaluate using lower injected activity with denoising algorithms to validate these findings.
