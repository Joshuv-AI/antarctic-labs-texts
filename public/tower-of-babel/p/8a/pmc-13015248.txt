Clinical characteristics and molecular epidemiology of KPC-NDM co-producing carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae in China: a multicentre retrospective case-control study
Authors: Xi Li, Xinhong Han, Xinyan Tang, Zhaokun Fan, Li Ding, Ronghua Geng, Qiong Chen, Junwei Huang, Jiayao Yao, Longjie Zhou, Xiaoxing Du, Jun Liu, Yanghu Xie, Shan Sun, Haitao Yu, Ruirui Ma, Jintao He, Ying Fu, Hua Zhou, Yunsong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundKPC and NDM co-producing carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (KN-CRKP) is an escalating global health threat with limited treatment options.MethodsHere, we conducted a multicentre retrospective case-control study on KN-CRKP in China (2020–2025), collecting 3012 non-duplicated CRKP isolates, of which 71 (2.4%) were KN-CRKP. For clinical analysis, 39 patients with KN-CRKP infections were identified from 71 isolates and matched in a 1:2 ratio with 78 patients infected by KPC-2-producing CRKP. For global analysis, we retrieved 100,141 genomes from GenBank, of which 662 non-redundant KN-CRKP sequences were combined with our 71 KN-CRKP isolates. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole-genome sequencing (WGS), plasmid transfer and stability, fitness cost, transcriptomics and Bayesian phylogeography were used to investigate evolution mechanisms and molecular epidemiology of KN-CRKP strains.FindingsAmong the infected patients, hospital-acquired or ventilator-associated pneumonia was the most common (41.0%, 16/39). Patients with KN-CRKP infections had a non-significantly higher 28-day mortality (38.5%, 15/39) than the KPC-CRKP control group (24.4%, 19/78; p = 0.133), but a significantly higher in-hospital mortality (46.2%, 18/39 vs. 25.6%, 20/78; p = 0.036). Independent risk factors included diabetes, a history of CRO (carbapenem-resistant gram-negative organism) infection treated with ceftazidime-avibactam (CZA) treatment, and recent use of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations. All KN-CRKP isolates were resistant to carbapenems and CZA, but exhibited highly susceptible to colistin (98.6%), cefiderocol (94.4%), and aztreonam-avibactam (100%). The predominant KN-CRKP clones were ST11 (73.2%) and ST307 (15.5%). Global surveillance of 733 global KN-CRKP identified ST11 as a pandemic lineage diverging into China/Brazil subclusters, with emerging clones ST147/ST307 showing post-pandemic increases. Plasmid profiling revealed blaNDM mainly on IncX3/IncN plasmids and blaKPC on IncR/IncFII plasmids, with novel hybrid plasmids carrying both carbapenemases identified. blaNDM-carrying plasmids had higher transferability/stability than blaKPC-carrying ones. Research in context

Evidence before this study

A search of NCBI PubMed with the terms “carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae” and “KPC and NDM” returned 15 results (search conducted on July 31st, 2025). Most reports have involved small, localised outbreaks and have described the molecular characteristics of clinical isolates only. Among these papers, Zhang et al. recently conducted a systematic and comprehensive analysis of 413 global KN-CRKP genomes to understand the epidemiology, resistance and virulence profiles, and dissemination mechanisms. However, their study did not incorporate detailed clinical information on patients infected with KN-CRKP strains.

Added value of this study

This study integrated detailed patient clinical data with genomic data of KN-CRKP isolates. We conducted a multicentre case-control study, demonstrating a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate for KN-CRKP infections (46%) than for single-KPC-2-producing CRKP infections (26%). We also identified key independent risk factors for these infections. From a global perspective, our study analysed 733 KN-CRKP strains by combining our 71 isolates with 662 non-redundant genomes retrieved from a pool of 100,141 genomes from GenBank. This extensive dataset allowed us to reveal new and dynamic trends in the global epidemiology of KN-CRKP, particularly highlighting the escalating local and global prevalence of ST307. Moreover, we provide direct evidence for the de novo emergence of KN-CRKP through in vivo interspecies plasmid transfer, highlighting the pivotal role of mobile genetic elements in the dissemination of resistance. More importantly, the global transmission dynamics of KN-CRKP strains were further explored using Bayesian evolutionary analysis.

Implications of all the available evidence

The high prevalence of KN-CRKP isolates in recent years highlights the importance of a comprehensive analysis that combines clonal expansion, promiscuous plasmid mobility, and host adaptation. This study provides a comprehensive understanding of the emergence and dynamic spread of KN-CRKP from a global perspective by linking genomic data to clinical outcomes and risk factors. The emergence of KN-CRKP strains poses significant clinical challenges, with a significantly higher mortality rate. Effective surveillance and stringent infection control are urgently needed to prevent further spread.

Introduction

Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) has emerged as a formidable global health threat, posing a significant challenge to clinical management due to multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype and its ability to cause severe infections.1 Currently, the primary mechanism underlying carbapenem resistance in K. pneumoniae is the production of carbapenemases, a diverse group of enzymes, including KPC-2, NDM-1/5, and OXA-48, which hydrolyse carbapenem antibiotics and severely restrict treatment choices.2,3 Alarmingly, KPC and NDM co-producing carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (KN-CRKP) strains, have escalated from sporadic cases to sustained transmission in recent years. In China, the prevalence of these strains raised from 0.28% in 2016 to 0.58% in 2017,4 while globally, the ratio of KN-CRKP in K. pneumoniae significantly increased from 0.03% in 2014 to 3.10% in 2023, with a particularly rapid rise observed from 2017 to 2023.5 Importantly, KN-CRKP strains exhibit extensive resistance phenotypes, including ceftazidime-avibactam, imipenem-cilastatin-relebactam and meropenem-vaborbactam, which severely limits the available antimicrobial treatments,2,6 and underscores the urgent need to understand their dissemination dynamics and to develop effective countermeasures.

Sporadic reports have indicated that KN-CRKP strains circulate within high-risk clones. Dissemination is often facilitated by promiscuous plasmids, with KN-CRKP isolates reported across China in high-risk sequence types (STs), such as ST11, ST15, and ST1049.7, 8, 9 Recently, Zhang et al. conducted a systematic and comprehensive analysis of global KN-CRKP (413 genomes) to understand its epidemiology, resistance and virulence profiles, and dissemination mechanisms.5 However, the research of the clinical outcomes, molecular characteristics, and underlying mechanisms driving the co-production and transmission of KN-CRKP strains remain critically limited.

Notably, infections caused by KPC-producing CRKP (KPC-CRKP) or NDM-producing CRKP (NDM-CRKP) impose a substantial clinical burden, leading to significantly increased morbidity and mortality, particularly among critically ill patients, those with underlying comorbidities, or individuals undergoing invasive procedures.10,11 In addition, the multinational CRACKLE-2 cohort study reported an alarming 19% all-cause 30-day mortality rate for CRKP (dominant by KPC-CRKP) infections.10 Furthermore, outbreaks associated with clonal transmission of CRKP strains could lead to patient fatalities, as observed in a fatal outbreak of ST11 hypervirulent CRKP in a Chinese hospital.12 In contrast, the specific clinical impact of KN-CRKP strains remains uncleared, and no studies have compared clinical characteristics, risk profiles, or outcomes between KPC-CRKP and KN-CRKP infections. Consequently, as the isolation of KN-CRKP strains continues to increase, there is a pressing need to understand the associated clinical risk factors.

In this study, we performed a multicentre retrospective study encompassing a substantial cohort of KN-CRKP strains from geographically diverse regions in China, aiming to bridge these critical knowledge gaps by elucidating the alarming expansion of these threatening pathogens. Our findings provide the insights into the clinical impact, evolutionary dynamics, and therapeutic vulnerabilities of these extensively drug-resistant strains, emphasing the need for coordinated surveillance programmes and innovative infection control strategies to mitigate their global spread.

Methods

Bacterial isolation and clinical data collection

Seventy-one KN-CRKP isolates were collected from 3012 CRKP clinical strains across 11 hospitals in different regions in China between November 2020 and June 2025. Only the first KN-CRKP isolate per patient was included. Among these, 39 patients were identified with KN-CRKP infections and included in the clinical analysis. A 1:2 matched case-control study design was employed. Potential controls (373) were first identified from patients with KPC-CRKP strains isolated, matched strictly by hospital source and time of bacterial isolation (±4 months). Among these, 189 colonised and data incompleted cases were excluded. From the remaining patients with confirmed KPC-CRKP infections, 78 were randomly selected as the control group (Fig. 1A). Demographic variables, comorbidities, antibiotic exposure, and clinical outcomes (28-day mortality and in-hospital mortality) were extracted from electronic medical records.

Infection was defined as the presence of relevant clinical signs and/or symptoms (e.g., fever, hypothermia, leucocytosis, localised inflammatory signs) plus microbiological confirmation of a K. pneumoniae from a normally sterile site (e.g., blood, puncture fluid) or, in the case of non-sterile sites (e.g., sputum, urine), evidence that the isolate is causally related to the clinical syndrome rather than representing colonisation or contamination.13

One patient (MAZ) with abdominal infection yielded three distinct isolates: a KN-CRKP strain KPN8363, a KPC-2-producing CRKP strain KP3520, and an NDM-1-producing Citrobacter freundii strain CF8363. These three isolates underwent comparative genomic and phenotypic analyses to investigate the mechanism of KN-CRKP formation. The patient's antibiotic treatment history prior to isolation is shown in Figure S1.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Provincial People's Hospital (QT2025121).

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

The Minimum Inhibitory Concentrations (MICs) of the tested 12 different antimicrobial agents were determined using the microbroth dilution method according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines14 (https://clsi.org/). MIC breakpoints from the CLSI guideline M100 35th edition was used for the interpretation of susceptibility, except for colistin (CST) and aztreonam-avibactam (AZA), for which the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) clinical breakpoints were applied (https://www.eucast.org/), and the FDA breakpoint was used (https://www.fda.gov/) for tigecycline (TGC) (S ≤ 2) and eravacycline (ERV) (S ≤ 0.5). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain.

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of cefiderocol (CFDC) was determined in iron-depleted cation-adjusted Mueller-Hinton broth (ID-CAMHB). Briefly, CAMHB was processed with Chelex 100 resin to deplete cations, filtered through a 0.2 μm membrane, adjusted to pH 7.2–7.4, and supplemented with calcium, magnesium, and zinc to achieve final concentrations of 22.5 mg/L, 11.25 mg/L, and 0.56 mg/L, respectively.

Whole-genome sequencing analysis and Bayesian skyline analyses

Genomic DNA of all isolated KN-CRKP strains was extracted using a QIAamp DNA minikit (Qiagen) and subjected to Illumina sequencing (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA) with 2 × 150-bp paired-end libraries. Sequence reads were assembled using Shovill v0.9.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/shovill). Seventeen representative strains, including KPN8363, KPN0050, KPN0033, KPN6137, KPN6862, KPN1214, KPN2356, KPN40SH, KPN0208, KPN1034, KPN0853, KPN2992, KPN3731, KPN2221, KPN2178, KPN44SH and KPN3106, were selected for long-read genome sequencing using the Nanopore platform, and sequence reads were assembled by de novo hybrid assembly using Unicycler v 0.4.8.15 The selection of isolates for long-read sequencing followed an iterative strategy. We first sequenced representative strains of different sequence types (STs). The complete plasmid structures obtained served as references to map short-read data from the remaining isolates. Those whose plasmids remained unresolved then underwent a second round of long-read sequencing.

The phylogenetic tree of KN-CRKP strains were constructed using a maximum-likelihood approach based on core-genome SNPs. Core genome alignment and SNP calling were performed with Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), and the resulting filtered SNP alignment was used to infer the phylogeny with FastTree v2.1 under the General Time Reversible (GTR) nucleotide substitution model,16 and then visualised using tvBOT.17 Multilocus sequence type (MLST) was performed using mlst v2.1118 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst). Antimicrobial resistance genes, and plasmid replicon typing were obtained using ABRicate v1.0.1 with the NCBI databases.19,20 The Kleborate databases were used for the detection of virulence factors and serotype.21 Conjugation transfer elements were predicted using oriTfinder2.22 Proksee v2.023 was used to compare and visualise the structural variations between the plasmid sequences. Linear comparisons of plasmid structures were performed using EasyFig v2.2.3.24

Public KN-CRKP genomes (n = 662) were downloaded from NCBI on July 7, 2025 (Table S1). After excluding genomes with incomplete metadata, poor quality, or duplicates, 235 ST11 and 36 ST307 isolates were retained for Bayesian analyses. Based on the core-genome SNP phylogeny, ST11 isolates were partitioned into two clades, designated ST11-Cluster I (n = 203) and ST11-Cluster II (n = 32). Core-genome SNP alignments were generated using Snippy, recombination was masked using Gubbins v3.3.4, and temporal signal was assessed in TempEst v1.5.3. Time-scaled phylogenies were inferred in BEAST v1.10.4 (GTR + I+Γ, uncorrelated lognormal relaxed clock, Bayesian Skyline),25 with geographic location modelled as a symmetric discrete CTMC trait. MCMC was run for 500 million generations (sampling every 50,000 steps) for ST11 clusters and 200 million generations (every 20,000 steps) for ST307, with 10% burn-in; convergence was confirmed in Tracer v1.7.2 (https://github.com/beast-dev/tracer/releases/tag/v1.7.2) (ESS > 200). Maximum clade credibility (MCC) trees were summarised in TreeAnnotator (mean node heights) and visualised in FigTree v1.4.4 (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/), with phylogeographic outputs mapped in SpreaD3 v0.9.6 (https://github.com/phylogeography/SpreaD3).

Plasmid transfer assays

To evaluate the transferability of carbapenemase-encoding plasmids, conjugation experiments26,27 were performed in filter-based mating using E. coli J53 as the recipient strain and 17 representative KN-CRKP strains (Table S2, selected based on ST and plasmid replicon type) as donor strains. Transconjugants carrying blaNDM and blaKPC genes were selected on Mueller-Hinton agar supplemented with sodium azide (100 mg/L) plus either ampicillin (100 mg/L) or aztreonam (100 mg/L). Successful plasmid transfer was confirmed using MALDI-TOF MS, AST, and PCR sequencing (for plasmid replicons and blaNDM and blaKPC genes).

To investigate interspecies plasmid transfer, conjugation was performed using C. freundii (CF8363) harbouring blaNDM-1 as the donor and a K. pneumoniae strain (KP3520) harbouring blaKPC-2 as the recipient. Transconjugants were selected on Mueller-Hinton agar plates containing 100 mg/L amikacin and 100 mg/L ceftazidime/avibactam. Conjugation transfer efficiency was calculated as the ratio of transconjugant CFUs to donor CFUs, averaged over three independent experiments.

Plasmid stability testing

Plasmid stability was evaluated for selected transconjugants and the clinical KN-CRKP strain KPN8363 over 10 days of serial passage in LB broth (1:100 dilution ratio) under non-selective conditions.27,28 Plasmid retention was quantified by screening fifty randomly selected colonies per biological replicate via PCR amplification of the resistance markers (blaKPC and blaNDM) and plasmid-specific sequences. Plasmid loss rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of PCR-negative colonies among the total tested.

Fitness cost

Growth rate measurements were performed using a Bioscreen C MBR instrument (Oy Growth Curves Ab Ltd., Finland)29 for J53, J5195_NDM, J3731_KPC, J1794_D, J6137_D, KP3520, KP3520_NDM, KPN8363, KP2281, KP2281_NDM. Triple independent overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in MH broth and incubated at 37 °C. OD600 values were recorded every 10 min for 20 h. Growth rates were calculated from OD600 data using an R script.

RNA-sequencing and differential expression analysis

For comparative transcriptomic analysis, three biological replicates of each isolate (KPN8363 and KP3520 from patient MAZ) were cultured overnight in LB broth, diluted 1:100 in fresh LB broth, and harvested at the mid-log growth phase. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was performed using the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome of KPN8363 using Bowtie2 v2.4.2.30 Read distribution and coverage were assessed with SAMtools v1.9. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edgeR v3.20.2,31 applying thresholds of |log2 fold change| >1.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction). Functional enrichment analysis of DEGs was performed via the KEGG and GO databases with clusterProfiler v4.0.32

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software v4.4.2 with packages including gtsummary, dplyr, tidyverse, janitor, and flextable, and GraphPad Prism v7. For the clinical analysis, continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages (n, %). Baseline comparisons between groups were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and Fisher's exact test (with simulated p-values when appropriate) for categorical variables. Univariable logistic regression was used to identify potential risk factors, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported. Variables with a univariable p < 0.20 were included in a multivariable logistic regression model to determine independent predictors, reporting adjusted odds ratios (aORs), and 95% CIs. For clinical outcomes, comparisons between groups were conducted using Fisher's exact test or chi-square test. For fitness cost assays, statistical significance in growth rate differences was determined using Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA test, with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All p-values were two-tailed, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics and risk factors of the KN-CRKP infections

A total of 39 patients with KN-CRKP infections (cases) were included in the clinical analysis and matched with 78 patients with KPC-CRKP infections (controls) (Table 1). Among the KN-CRKP group, the distribution of primary infection sites was as follows: 41.0% (16/39) had HAP-VAP, 35.9% (14/39) had abdominal infections, 10.3% (4/39) had urinary tract infections, and 12.8% (5/39) had other infections. Of these 39 patients, 23.1% (9/39) developed secondary bloodstream infections, originating from pulmonary sources in 4 cases and abdominal sources in 5. Among the KPC-CRKP control group, the distribution of primary infection sites was as follows: 50.0% (39/78) HAP-VAP, 43.6% (34/78) abdominal infections, 3.8% (3/78) urinary tract infections, and 2.6% (2/78) had other infections. Notably, 26.9% (21/78) patients developed secondary bloodstream infections.

Regarding clinical outcomes, the 28-day mortality rate for patients with KN-CRKP infections was 38.5% (15/39), numerically higher than the 24.4% (19/78) observed in the KPC-CRKP control group (p = 0.133, Fisher's exact test). The in-hospital mortality rate was significantly higher in patients with KN-CRKP infections (46.2%, 18/39) than in those to 25.6% (20/78) in the KPC-CRKP control group (p = 0.036, Fisher's exact test) (Table 1). Furthermore, the in-hospital mortality rates in the patients with secondary bloodstream infections (77.8% vs. 38.1%, p = 0.051, Fisher's exact test) and abdominal infections (57.1% vs. 17.6%, p = 0.013, Fisher's exact test) were significantly higher in patients with KN-CRKP infections compared to the KPC-CRKP control group.

In univariate logistic analysis, several factors were significantly associated with KN-CRKP infection, including diabetes (p = 0.019) and a history of CRO infection treated with CZA (p = 0.025). Other factors such as COPD (p = 0.145), hospitalisation in the past 3 months (p = 0.233), admission to the intensive care unit in the past 3 months (p = 0.511), and the use of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations in the previous 3 months (p = 0.066) also showed similar trends. Multivariate logistic regression analysis, adjusting for potential confounders, identified independent risk factors for acquiring KN-CRKP infection, including diabetes (OR, 2.99; 95% CI, 1.24–7.35; p = 0.01), history of CRO infection treated with CZA (OR, 4.79; 95% CI, 1.47–16.9; p = 0.01), and usage of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (no including CZA) in the previous 3 months (OR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.04–8.12; p = 0.04) (Table S3).

The characteristics of KN-CRKP strains

All 71 KN-CRKP isolates (Table S4) exhibited resistance to carbapenems and CZA. Elevated resistance rates were observed for amikacin (AMK, 84.5%, 60/71), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 93.0%, 66/71), and susceptibility rates for eravacycline (ERV) was only 2.8% (2/71). Notably, aztreonam-avibactam (AZA) exhibited complete susceptibility (100%), with susceptibility rates of 98.6% (70/71) for colistin (CST) and 94.4% (67/71) for cefiderocol (CFDC) (Fig. 1B, Tables S5–S6).

WGS revealed that seven distinct STs among these KN-CRKP isolates, with ST11 predominating (73.2%, 52/71), followed by ST307 (15.5%, 11/71), ST727 (4.2%, 3/71), ST15 (2.9%, 2/71), ST111 (1.4%, 1/71), ST37 (1.4%, 1/71), and ST1545 (1.4%, 1/71) (Fig. 1C, Table S7). The ST11 lineage demonstrated pan-hospital distribution, being identified in all participating medical institutions. Notably, 90.4% (47/52) of ST11 isolates predominantly exhibited KL64 serotype, whereas all ST307 isolates uniformly displayed KL102 serotype. Carbapenemase profiling revealed distinct resistance gene combinations among ST11 isolates: blaKPC-2/blaNDM-1 (50.0%, 26/52), blaKPC-2/blaNDM-5 (44.2%, 23/52), and blaKPC-2/blaNDM-13 (3.8%, 2/52). Only one ST11 isolate exhibited a blaKPC-25/blaNDM-1. The genes associated with the yersiniabactin siderophore system (including fyuA, irp1, irp2, ybtA/E/P/Q/S/T/U/X) were present in 81.7% (58/71) of isolates (Table S8). Furthermore, the regulator genes rmpA/rmpA2, which are associated with the hypermucoid phenotype, were detected in 69.0% (49/71) of the strains. The aerobactin siderophore system genes (iucA/B/C/D, iutA) were carried by 66.2% (47/71) of isolates. In contrast, the salmochelin system genes (iroB/C/D/N) were less common, with a carriage rate of 4.2% (3/71).

Global epidemiological characteristics of KN-CRKP strains

A total of 100,141 Klebsiella genomes were retrieved from GenBank (as of July 7, 2025). Through genetic screening, sequence redundancy checks, and quality control, 662 non-redundant KN-CRKP sequences were ultimately selected and combined with 71 KN-CRKP sequences from this study for subsequent global analyses (Table S1, Table S9, Figure S2). The 733 KN-CRKP strains were distributed across 33 countries spanning six continents, with China (38.0%, 279/733) and the USA (30.4%, 233/733) being the predominant sources (Fig. 2A and B). 79 STs were identified, with ST11 predominantly (34.6%, 254/733), followed by ST147 (9.4%, 69/733), ST258 (7.3%, 54/733), and ST307 (5.0%, 37/733) (Fig. 2C). Notably, the prevalence of these STs showed distinct temporal trends; ST11 peaked in 2020 (54.1%) and 2023 (38.8%), whereas ST307 and ST147 experienced a significant surge post-COVID-19 pandemic, increasing approximately 3-fold (from 4.90% to 12.69%) and 4-fold (from 6.67% to 23.7%), respectively. Conversely, ST258 showed a seven-fold increase (1.64%–12.2%) in prevalence during the pandemic years (2020–2022) (Fig. 2B). The most common carbapenemases combination were KPC-2 and NDM-1 (47.6%, 349/733), KPC-2 and NDM-5 (16.6%, 122/733) (Fig. 2C).

Global transmission dynamics of KN-CRKP strains using Bayesian evolutionary analysis

Phylogenetic analysis of ST11 KN-CRKP revealed two distinct clusters: one (ST11-Cluster I) predominantly found in China (99.0%, 201/203) and another (ST11-Cluster II) primarily circulating in non-Chinese regions (96.9%, 31/32) (Fig. 2D). The origin and transmission dynamics were further analysed using Bayesian phylogeographic analysis (Fig. 2E), revealing ST11-Cluster I (n = 203) estimated a tMRCA of around 2007 (95% HPD: 2004–2011) from China, with an evolutionary rate of 2.137E-3 (95% HPD: 1.64E-3–2.70E-3), and regional migration rates of 2.46E-3 (95% HPD: 6.68E-5–6.28E-3). The ST11-Cluster II (n = 32) estimated an earlier tMRCA of around 1998 (95% HPD: 1978–2012) from Brazil with 45.8% posterior probability for the root, an evolutionary rate of 7.30E-3 (95% HPD: 1.50E-3–1.46E-2), and regional migration rates of 3.48E-2 (95% HPD: 7.70E-3–6.96E-2) (Figure S3 and Table S10). Subsequently, the ST11-Cluster II clone spread from Brazil to seven other countries, demonstrating extensive intercontinental transmission across South America, Asia, and Europe.

The Bayesian Skyline analysis of 36 ST307 isolates estimated a tMRCA of around 2003 (95% HPD: 1992–2012) from the USA with 95.0% posterior probability for the root, an evolutionary rate of 4.75E-3 (95% HPD: 2.25E-3–7.45E-3), and regional migration rates of 2.36E-2 (95% HPD: 6.69E-3–4.41E-2) (Figure S3 and Table S11). Subsequently, The ST307 clone spread from the United States to countries including Russia, Argentina, India, China, and South Africa (Fig. 2F). Interestingly, temporal analysis of the ST307 population size showed a stable period before the COVID-19 pandemic (prior to 2020), followed by a significant 80% decline during the pandemic years (2020–2022), and a return to stabilisation from 2023 onwards.

Plasmid characteristics of KN-CRKP strains

A systematic analysis of the plasmid distribution patterns in 71 KN-CRKP strains revealed distinct genetic architectures. The majority of isolates (64/71, 90.14%) harboured blaKPC-2 and blaNDM on separate plasmids, whereas 6 strains harboured both genes on a single plasmid. Notably, one strain exhibited a unique chromosomal-plasmid partitioning, with blaNDM integrated into the chromosome and blaKPC-2 plasmid-borne.

The blaKPC-2 gene spread primarily through five plasmid types with distinct conjugation profiles (Fig. 3A–F): IncR and RepB(R1701)-type plasmids both contained complete conjugation systems (oriT-relaxase-T4SS-T4CP). In contrast, IncFIA (HI1)/IncFII(K) plasmids carried partial conjugation machinery (relaxase-T4SS-T4CP). IncFII(pHN7A8)-type plasmids (24/71, 33.8%) retained only the oriT element; while IncR/IncFII(pHN7A8) hybrid plasmids (27/71, 38.03%) completely lacked conjugation elements. Among the 17 representative donor strains used in the conjugation assays, transfer was successful for a subset of the tested blaKPC-carrying plasmids. Specifically, conjugation occurred for 2 out of 4 IncR/FII(pHN7A8)-type plasmids, 4 out of 6 IncFII(pHN7A8)-type plasmids, 2 out of 3 repB (R1701)-type plasmids, and 1 out of 2 IncR-type plasmids. The single tested IncN/IncR/IncFII (pHN7A8) hybrid plasmid was successfully transferred, while the single IncC/IncFII (Yp) hybrid plasmid failed to transfer (Table S2).

The blaNDM genes were predominantly plasmid-associated (70/71, 98.59%) with 5 distinct conjugation profiles. The most prevalent plasmid type was IncX3 (28.57%, 20/70) (Fig. 3G–N), distributed among ST11 (n = 8), ST307 (n = 11; blaNDM-5 = 6, blaNDM-1 = 5), and ST15 (n = 1) strains. All IncX3 plasmids contained essential conjugation elements. Other plasmid types carrying blaNDM included IncB/O/K/Z (n = 11), IncI1-I (Alpha) (n = 4), IncN (n = 12), and IncFII (Yp) (n = 6), all of which possessed complete conjugation systems (oriT-relaxase-T4SS-T4CP). In contrast, two blaNDM-5-carrying IncFII plasmids retained only the oriT element, indicating incomplete conjugation machinery. During conjugation experiments, all types of blaNDM-carrying plasmids were consistently conjugated successfully.

Plasmid stability was evaluated over 10 days of serial passages (Figure S4 and Table S12). The clinical KN-CRKP strain KPN8363 demonstrated remarkable stability, maintaining 100% retention of both blaNDM- and blaKPC-carrying plasmids throughout the passage experiments. In contrast, while the blaNDM-carrying plasmids in in vitro transconjugants (J1794_NDM, J1794_D_NDM) exhibited high stability (96%–98% retention at Day 10), the stability of blaKPC-carrying plasmids in transconjugants (J3731_KPC, J1794_D_KPC) was notably lower and more variable (6%–78% retention at Day 10). Overall, these results indicate that blaNDM-carrying plasmids possess superior conjugation efficiency and stability, suggesting that the acquisition of blaNDM-carrying plasmid by a KPC-CRKP strain is a highly probable pathway for the formation of KN-CRKP.

The mechanism of formation of KN-CRKP strains

The formation of KN-CRKP in clinical settings involves multiple synergistic mechanisms (Fig. 4A). First, interspecies plasmid transmission was demonstrated in patient MAZ, where a C. freundii strain carrying blaNDM-1 coexisted with two sub-populations of the same CRKP (2 SNP differences), one harbouring blaKPC-2 and the other co-producing both enzymes. Identical blaNDM-1 plasmids were found in C. freundii and KN-CRKP. Crucially, in vitro conjugation assays revealed efficient plasmid transfer from C. freundii to CRKP under diverse conditions (no antibiotic: 1.3 ± 0.7 × 10−4; cefoperazone/sulbactam: 1.3 ± 0.4 × 10−4; CZA: 1.8 ± 0.5 × 10−4) (Table S13). Second, interregional spread and interclonal dissemination were evidenced by an ST307 KN-CRKP strain in Jinhua (Zhejiang Province, China), phylogenetically clustered with Hangzhou isolates (SNP difference <35), correlating with the patient's hospitalisation history. Conserved blaNDM plasmid architectures (e.g., IncX3, IncN and IncFII) across ST11 and ST307 lineages further supported interclonal plasmid dissemination. Third, IS26 mediated genomic remodelling was critical for KN-CRKP stabilisation. Analysis revealed that blaKPC-2 plasmid diversification primarily arose from localised IS26-mediated recombination rather than horizontal transfer, whereas blaNDM-1 plasmids exhibited discordant evolutionary trajectories consistent with frequent cross-strain transmissions. Notably, blaNDM-1 chromosomal integration via IS26 transposition occurred in ST307 strain KPN3731, forming a canonical IS26-blaNDM--IS26 composite transposon (Figure S5). Furthermore, IS26-related plasmid fusion generated four strains with hybrid blaKPC-blaNDM carrying plasmids (Fig. 3O–Q and Figure S5), all of which retained complete transfer systems. In ST11 strains, two IncN/IncR/IncFII(pHN7A8) hybrid plasmids were identified: KPN0853 was formed through insertion of an IncR/IncFII(pHN7A8)-type blaKPC plasmid into an IncN-type blaNDM plasmid, while KPN3106 resulted from the integration of an NDM-containing fragment into a blaKPC plasmid. Additionally, one ST727 strain carried an IncC/IncFII hybrid plasmid, which was formed through recombination between an IncC-type blaKPC plasmid and an IncFII-type blaNDM plasmid.

These findings collectively establish that KN-CRKP emergence is due to the convergence of (1) high mobility of blaNDM plasmids across species and clonal boundaries and (2) genomic remodelling of plasmids via IS26-mediated recombination, plasmid fusion, and chromosomal integration, leading to the co-localisation and stabilisation of both resistance determinants.

Fitness cost

To assess the fitness costs associated with plasmid acquisition, we firstly compared the maximum growth rates and area under the growth curve (AUC) of E. coli J53, its transconjugants, and the clinical K. pneumoniae isolates (Fig. 4B–D). The single blaNDM-plasmid and single blaKPC-plasmid carrying transconjugants (J5195_NDM strain and J3731_KPC strain) showed no significant fitness cost compared to the E. coli J53 recipient strain. In contrast, strains harbouring both blaKPC and blaNDM plasmids exhibited a statistically significant reduction (3%–6%) in relative growth rate (J1794_D strain and J6137_D strain, 0.97 ± 0.03 vs. 1.00 ± 0.03, p = 0.04; 0.94 ± 0.03 vs. 1.00 ± 0.03, p = 0.0001) compared to E. coli J53. In addition, the area under the growth curve of J6137_D was reduced by 5.27% compared with that of E. coli J53 (1234 ± 10.97 vs. 1299 ± 35.92, p = 0.04). Notably, no statistically significant differences in maximum relative growth rates or AUC were observed between the KPC-CRKP strains (KP3520 and KP2281) and their transconjugants harbouring an additional blaNDM-carrying plasmid (KP3520_NDM and KP2281_NDM). Similarly, the clinical KN-CRKP isolate KPN8363 did not show a significant fitness cost compared to KP3520 and KP2281. These results suggest minimal fitness costs associated with the acquisition of the blaNDM plasmid in these clinical K. pneumoniae backgrounds.

Differentially expressed genes and pathways associated with KN-CRKP formation

To elucidate the transcriptomic changes underlying KN-CRKP formation, RNA-seq comparison between a KN-CRKP strain (KPN8363) and its isogenic KPC-CRKP strain (KP3520) identified 92 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Fig. 4E). Among these, 57 chromosomal genes showed differential expression (Table S14): upregulated genes included those involved in glycerol/propanediol metabolism (glpA/D/Q, dhaB/T, glpF), carbon metabolism (fruA, alsE, mglB), specific amino acid metabolism (argO, hrsA, mepS), cell membrane/wall biosynthesis and modification (dgkA, yvqK, acoR, iraM), and the Na+/H+ antiporter (nhaA); whereas paaZ and aceA were downregulated. On the blaNDM plasmid, 34 genes were upregulated, including transposase-related genes (Tn3), conjugation and type IV secretion system components (virB4, ptlH, traG), DNA replication regulators (stpA, pir), and stress-responsive metabolic genes. The blaKPC plasmid exhibited upregulation of hin, encoding a DNA invertase (Fig. 4F). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated marked upregulation of glycerolipid and glycerophospholipid metabolism (adjusted p = 0.002, Benjamini-Hochberg correction), suggesting membrane remodelling for dual plasmid carriage, alongside enhanced bacterial secretion system (T4SS, adjusted p = 4.16E−04, Benjamini-Hochberg correction) activity linked to plasmid conjugation (Fig. 4G). A trend of upregulation in the phosphotransferase system (PTS) (adjusted p = 0.06, Benjamini-Hochberg correction) suggested compensatory carbon uptake mechanisms. These coordinated expression changes-facilitating metabolic adaptation, plasmid transfer, and stability-likely contribute to the persistence of KN-CRKP despite fitness costs.

Discussion

The escalating clinical burden of KN-CRKP infections necessitates urgent attention owing to severely limited therapeutic options.4,33 We observed a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate for patients with KN-CRKP infections compared to those infected with KPC-CRKP. The near-pan-resistance phenotype of KN-CRKP strains, which severely limits therapeutic options, is a key factor contributing to this exacerbated mortality. Critically, while KPC-CRKP can often be treated with CZA, the additional production of NDM in KN-CRKP confers resistance to this last-line agent. Despite this extensive resistance, our in vitro susceptibility testing revealed promising activity for CST, CFDC, and AZA, representing vital last-line therapeutic options.

Beyond the general clinical impact, our analysis of bloodstream infection cases within the cohort highlighted the significant clinical challenge posed by KN-CRKP strains. Among the nine patients who developed KN-CRKP bloodstream infections, seven died, underscoring the high mortality associated with these extensively drug-resistant pathogens. For the two surviving patients with bloodstream infections, definitive therapy involved combination regimens of CST with TGC or CZA. In addition to antimicrobial treatment strategies, the patient's underlying health status (e.g., Charlson Comorbidity Index, as shown in Table S15) plays a crucial role in treatment outcomes. While colistin demonstrated favourable clinical outcomes in some cases, its significant toxic side effects remain a major concern.33, 34, 35 Furthermore, while AZA has recently been approved in China, robust clinical data regarding its efficacy against KN-CRKP remain limited, and CFDC is still awaiting approval. Therefore, in addition to exploring and evaluating the clinical efficacy of AZA, preventive measures against KN-CRKP infections are of paramount importance.

Understanding the risk factors for acquiring KN-CRKP is crucial for developing targeted prevention strategies. Our multivariate logistic regression analysis identified risk factors that can be broadly categorised into patient comorbidities and extrinsic antibiotic pressures. From host perspective, diabetes emerged as a significant independent risk factor. This aligns with the well-established understanding that diabetes could compromise immune function and increases susceptibility to severe infections. Extrinsic factors, especially antibiotic selective pressure, also played a crucial role. Our findings corroborate existing evidence that prior antibiotic exposure, especially to broad-spectrum agents, is associated with the selection and evolution of CRO.36,37 Specifically, prior use of β-lactam/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations underscored the significant impact of broad-spectrum antibiotic pressure in facilitating the emergence of highly resistant strains. More importantly, a strong association was observed with a history of CRO strains infection treated with CZA. This suggests that CZA exposure, while targeting KPC, may inadvertently select for NDM acquisition by existing KPC-CRKP strains, leading to the de novo emergence of the dual-carbapenemase phenotype. This finding suggests that prior exposure to CZA, a crucial therapeutic agent for KPC-producing strains, might paradoxically create a selective pressure favouring the emergence of KN-CRKP, thereby may contributing to the worse outcomes. Such a scenario could arise where KPC-CRKP strains, under CZA pressure, acquire NDM-encoding plasmids from other co-colonising or co-infecting resistant organisms, as demonstrated by our in vitro interspecies plasmid transfer experiments. These insights are vital for improved risk stratification and the implementation of targeted prophylactic and infection control measures in high-risk patient populations.

Notably, our study provides compelling real-world evidence for the de novo formation of KN-CRKP strains through multiple synergistic mechanisms in clinical settings. A particularly illustrative case is that of patient MAZ with abdominal infection, where combined genomic and clinical analyses strongly indicated in vivo interspecies transfer of a blaNDM-1 plasmid from C. freundii to a KPC-2-producing K. pneumoniae strain. This crucial observation of interspecies plasmid transmission is a significant pathway for the de novo emergence of KN-CRKP strains. This finding is consistent with a previous genomic epidemiological study by León-Sampedro et al., which used sequence analysis to demonstrate that carbapenem resistance plasmids pOXA-48 can transfer among various enterobacteria within the gut microbiota of colonised patients.38 Our in vitro conjugation experiments further corroborated this potential pathway, showing efficient plasmid transfer from C. freundii to K. pneumoniae. Although not statistically significant, the numerically higher conjugation efficiency observed under CZA pressure than under antibiotic-free conditions suggests that antibiotic exposure may inadvertently facilitate the de novo formation of KN-CRKP through interspecies horizontal gene transfer, aligning with concerns about antibiotic-enhanced plasmid conjugation.39 Furthermore, our findings highlight the critical role of plasmid fusion events and IS26-mediated recombination in generating hybrid plasmids that co-harbour both blaKPC-2 and blaNDM. The identification of novel IncN/IncR/IncFII hybrid plasmids, for instance, represents the formation of formidable “super-resistance vehicles” capable of overcoming incompatibility barriers and facilitating rapid resistance accumulation. These findings underscore that the emergence of KN-CRKP is not merely a consequence of co-occurrence but an active process involving dynamic genetic exchange and recombination.

The widespread dissemination of KN-CRKP is associated with the complex interplay between clonal expansion, promiscuous plasmid mobility, and host adaptation. Our molecular epidemiological survey revealed and extended the pivotal role of high-risk clones, suggesting that ST11-KL64 remains a dominant endemic threat, particularly in China, which aligns with prevalent single-carbapenemase CRKP clones.40 This clone presents a significant clinical threat because of its combination of a highly resistant phenotype and sustained virulence.41 These findings are consistent with comprehensive global analyses identifying high-risk clones, such as ST11-KL64 and ST258-KL107, as key drivers of KN-CRKP dissemination worldwide.5 Crucially, our expanded dataset reveals significant diversification within ST11, separating into geographically distinct subclusters, including ST11-Cluster I, predominantly China-centric, and ST11-Cluster II, originating earlier in Brazil and disseminating extensively across continents). This genetic plasticity of ST11 facilitates the acquisition of diverse resistance plasmids while maintaining fitness and pan-hospital endemicity.4,7 Simultaneously, our global analysis highlights the new and escalating threat posed by ST307-KL102. ST307 is a globally disseminated high-risk clone frequently associated with diverse carbapenemases (e.g., OXA-181 in Africa and OXA-48/KPC-2 in Europe).42, 43, 44, 45 While previously less associated with KN-CRKP in China (primarily linked to IMP or NDM alone),46,47 ST307 has rapidly emerged post-COVID-19, demonstrating a significant ∼3-fold increase, likely fuelled by interregional and international transmission of blaNDM-5-IncX3 plasmids and strains clustering closely with US isolates. Notably, this lineage experienced a dramatic population contraction during the COVID-19 pandemic (2020–2022) before stabilisation, underscoring the potential impact of large-scale healthcare disruptions on transmission dynamics. ST307 strains were primarily isolated from China and the United States, with China being the dominant source. During 2020–2022, strict public health measures in China likely limited the transmission of ST307. Additionally, changes in antimicrobial resistance during the COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed; for instance, a study in China reported a decline in CRKP isolation rates in paediatric hospitals, with shifts toward other carbapenem-resistant organisms.38 Another study in northern Brazil indicated a transition from KPC-to NDM-producing CRKP during the pandemic.48 These findings collectively demonstrate that KN-CRKP dissemination is propelled by both the expansion of established, adaptable clones like ST11 and the alarming rise of highly mobile lineages like ST307, facilitated by promiscuous plasmid transfer and recombination events.

While the acquisition of multiple resistance plasmids often incurs a fitness cost, our study sheds light on how clinical KN-CRKP strains achieve stable coexistence and successful dissemination. Parental strains maintained high stability for both blaNDM- and blaKPC-bearing plasmids throughout 10 days of serial passage. This striking disparity in plasmid persistence between parental strains and horizontally acquired transconjugants, where blaNDM-carrying plasmids showed high stability (96%–98% retention by day 10) while blaKPC-carrying plasmid stability was more variable and dramatically declined in some transconjugants (as low as 6% retention by day 10), suggests that KN-CRKP resulted from the transfer of blaNDM genes into KPC-producing strains. Besides, the lower conjugation frequency observed for blaKPC-2-carrying plasmids may be attributed to the presence of a functional conjugation inhibitor gene, finO, which is commonly found on IncFII plasmids and acts to repress their self-transfer.49 The observed stable coexistence in clinical strains, despite the initial fitness cost in laboratory E. coli models, underscores the complexity of host-plasmid co-evolution. The intrinsic properties of blaNDM-carrying plasmids, often IncX3, are pivotal, characterised by promiscuous interclonal dissemination, superior transmission efficiency, and high stability compared to blaKPC-carrying plasmids, contributing to the wider spread of blaNDM. Previous studies, through plasmid analysis, have suggested that the emergence of KN-CRKP is primarily related to the transfer of NDM-encoding plasmids.50,51 Our results, particularly the higher stability of blaNDM-carrying plasmids in both clinical strains and in vitro transconjugants, support this perspective. Furthermore, our observation of the reduced stability of KPC-type plasmids within dual-carbapenemase strains provides additional evidence supporting the pivotal role of blaNDM-carrying plasmid acquisition in the formation of these extensively drug-resistant isolates. To elucidate the molecular basis of plasmid stability and transfer capabilities, we investigated key maintenance systems and OmpK36 (Tables S16–S18). Analysis of 17 complete plasmid sequences revealed distinct maintenance systems: complete Type II restriction-modification (RM) systems were identified in two hybrid plasmids (co-harbouring blaKPC-2 and blaNDM) and one standalone blaNDM plasmid, but were absent from other blaKPC-2 and blaNDM plasmids. Conversely, toxin-antitoxin (T/AT) systems were more prevalent on blaKPC-2 plasmids (85.7%, 12/14) than on blaNDM plasmids (46.2%, 6/13), with all three hybrid plasmids carrying T/AT systems. The absence of both systems in strain KPN8363 implies alternative stability mechanisms. These findings suggest that the co-occurrence of RM and T/AT systems may contribute to the evolutionary success and persistence of the hybrid strains in this study, while their absence in certain clinical isolates points to alternative adaptive pathways for plasmid maintenance. Besides, the fact that plasmid transfer was successful across diverse OmpK36 genotypes, including a wild-type strain, indicates that while these specific mutations may adjust the process, they are not an essential requirement for conjugation. Furthermore, these results also reveal that blaKPC-encoding plasmids in KN-CRKP strains are inherently stably maintained within their bacterial hosts, while blaNDM-encoding plasmids are likely acquired through subsequent horizontal gene transfer events.

Crucially, these plasmid-mediated resistance strategies are significantly supported by host bacterial adaptations, as revealed by our transcriptomic analysis. Comparing a clinical KN-CRKP strain with its KPC-2-only progenitor, we revealed significant upregulation of chromosomally encoded genes involved in glycerol/propanediol metabolism, carbon metabolism, and cell membrane/wall biosynthesis. Chromosomal mutations in the host are one of the most common compensatory evolution mechanisms after plasmid acquisition.52,53 Such metabolic reprogramming likely serves as a compensatory strategy to mitigate the fitness burden imposed by carrying multiple large plasmids. Concurrently, the upregulation of NDM plasmid-encoded transposases and Type IV Secretion System (T4SS) components directly enhances plasmid mobility and conjugation potential.54 This partnership between the host and its plasmids-where the host supports the plasmids' presence and the plasmids help with spreading-is a key factor for the stability and epidemiological success of these extensively drug-resistant pathogens.

In addition, our study has a few limitations. First, while our current analysis provides valuable insights, future investigations should aim to expand the cohort size, particularly increasing the number of patients with bloodstream infections. This expansion will allow for a more robust statistical analysis of the clinical outcomes and risk factors associated with these severe infections caused by KN-CRKP, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness of our clinical findings. Besides, while our isolates were predominantly collected from a specific region (Zhejiang), global genomic data and the multicentre case-control design allow for a comprehensive understanding of KN-CRKP. However, the uneven geographic distribution remains a limitation, and future surveillance should prioritise underrepresented regions to enhance the generalisability of the conclusion. Second, we also need to study how these bacteria spread in the environment. Using tools like metagenomics could provide better understanding towards the transmission dynamics and inform targeted interventions. Third, our study only provides real-world data for the de novo formation of ST11 dual-carbapenemase strains; further studies are needed to observe ST307 strains.

In conclusion, our study revealed that KN-CRKP infections are associated with higher mortality than KPC-CRKP infections, linked to near-pan-resistance that severely limits therapeutic options. The formation and rapid dissemination of these KN-CRKP strains occur through multiple synergistic factors: (1) clonal expansion of high-risk lineages, (2) efficient plasmid-mediated dissemination, (3) interspecies transfer of resistance determinants, and (4) host-pathogen co-adaptation. In addition to the prevalent ST11 KN-CRKP clone, our study highlights the emerging threat and global expansion of the ST307 clone. These findings mandate enhanced surveillance for KN-CRKP strains and stringent infection control measures in healthcare settings, particularly by targeting promiscuous plasmid mobility and interspecies transfer events that are associated with their formation.
