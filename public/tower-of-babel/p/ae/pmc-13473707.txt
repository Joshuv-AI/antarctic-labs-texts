Point-of-Care Ultrasound for Suspected Anorectal Infections in the Emergency Department
Authors: David Wasiak, Neil Wallace, James Patterson, Omar Rizvi, Srikar Adhikari, Josie Acuña
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to evaluate the use of point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) in the initial screening assessment and diagnosis of perirectal abscess and its impact on patient management in the emergency department (ED).MethodsThis is a retrospective review of patients who presented to two urban academic medical centers at an university in Arizona, United States, with clinical presentations concerning for anorectal infections over a three-year period. The institution’s POCUS database was reviewed for examinations of the anorectal region. Electronic health records were reviewed for demographic characteristics, history, physical examination findings, ED course, additional imaging studies, consultations, impact of POCUS on patient care, and final disposition.ResultsA total of 27 cases were included in the final analysis. The most common presenting symptoms were pain (n=20, 74.1%), followed by swelling (n=9, 33.3%) and fever (n=6, 22.2%). The most common physical examination findings were tenderness to palpation (n=15, 55.6%), fluctuance (n=9, 33.3%), and presence of a palpable mass (n=8, 29.6%). The most common sonographic findings were fluid collection (mean=15.5, 57.4%) and cellulitis (mean=17, 63%). Fluid collections were further classified by location: Perirectal (n=5, 18.5%), perianal (n=10, 37%), and gluteal (n=6, 22.2%). The findings of the POCUS examinations were used in medical decision-making in 25 (92.6%) cases. Of the five patients with a final diagnosis of perirectal abscess, four (80%) were identified in the ED using POCUS. Of the 12 perianal abscesses diagnosed in the ED based on POCUS findings, 10 (83.3%) agreed with the final discharge diagnosis.ConclusionPoint-of-care ultrasound has the potential to be a useful, noninvasive tool in the evaluation of anorectal complaints in the ED. In this retrospective review, POCUS findings were frequently documented in clinical decision-making and showed apparent concordance with final diagnoses in selected cases. While not a replacement for advanced imaging in all cases, it may serve as a valuable first-line modality to guide early management. Additional training and standardized imaging protocols may improve diagnostic performance. Larger prospective studies are needed to further define the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS and establish its role in the evaluation and management of anorectal complaints in the emergency setting.

Introduction

Patients with anorectal symptoms often seek care in the emergency department (ED). The differential can be wide, including infectious sources such as perianal abscess, perirectal abscess, cellulitis, and noninfectious sources such as hemorrhoids and fistula. Physical examination alone may be insufficient to distinguish superficial perianal disease from deeper perirectal or supralevator infection, particularly when external signs are subtle or absent. Symptoms of pain often precede visible signs of infection on physical exam. Early diagnosis is important as delayed care can lead to worsening outcomes, including enlargement of the abscess, fistula, and sepsis.

When imaging is required, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is considered the gold standard imaging modality, while computed tomography (CT) is commonly used because of its availability [1-3]. There is an appreciable misdiagnosis rate by CT that is later diagnosed surgically, especially in those who are immunocompromised [4]. Needle aspiration at the point of maximal tenderness has been recommended in the literature to diagnose this condition, but it is invasive [5]. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) offers a viable option in the initial screening for anorectal pathology.

A transcutaneous ultrasound examination offers a portable, quick, and noninvasive tool for bedside screening of anorectal pathology by trained emergency physicians to determine if patients require further imaging, such as CT or MRI, or alternatively, urgent consult or procedure. Prior studies have shown that emergency physicians are accurate in the diagnosis of cellulitis versus abscess using POCUS [6]. Case reports have shown that transperineal ultrasound can be used at the bedside to diagnose an intersphincteric perirectal abscess [5,7]. Beyond case reports, there is limited data that explores the utility of POCUS in the evaluation of anorectal symptoms in the acute setting. We aimed to describe the use of physician-performed POCUS in adults evaluated for suspected anorectal or gluteal infections and to assess its documented association with clinical decision making in the ED.

Materials and methods

This is a retrospective review of ED patients who presented at two urban academic centers at the University of Arizona, Tucson, Arizona, United States, and underwent a documented POCUS evaluation of the anorectal, perianal, perirectal, or gluteal region for suspected infection. Both study sites are home to an Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education-accredited Emergency Medicine residency program. The sites are staffed by Emergency Medicine residents, fellows, and attending physicians. Data were collected over three years from August 1, 2022, to July 1, 2025. Institutional Review Board of the University of Arizona approved the study (protocol number: 1806663505).

The decision to perform a POCUS examination on a patient to further evaluate for an anorectal complaint was ultimately at the discretion of the treating EM attending physician. POCUS studies were performed by ED residents, fellows, and attending physicians, and interpretations were documented in real time. All POCUS studies were archived in a web-based workflow solution database, QPathE (Qpath; Telexy Healthcare, British Columbia, Canada). This database included all POCUS exams performed at both sites and included reports detailing indications, findings, and diagnoses. All images were reviewed for quality assurance by an emergency ultrasound fellow or emergency ultrasound fellowship-trained physician.

The QPathE database was queried for eligible subjects who received a POCUS examination of the rectal or gluteal region for suspected infection, followed by a review of patients’ electronic health records (EHRs). Inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 years and chart documentation available in the EHR. Patients for whom POCUS was performed for non-infectious indications were excluded. Pediatric patients, patients with incomplete information, and patients for whom the ultrasound report was not completed were excluded from the study.

Study protocol

All exams were performed using the linear or curvilinear probe. Examinations were performed in orthogonal planes over regions of maximal tenderness or superficial skin findings, sweeping through perianal/perirectal and gluteal regions. Color or power Doppler was utilized when a region suspicious for a mass or abscess was identified. Perianal abscesses were defined as collections within 3 cm of the anal verge and superficial to the external sphincter. Perirectal abscesses were defined as collections involving deeper anorectal spaces, including the ischioanal or ischiorectal, intersphincteric, supralevator, or deep postanal spaces. Gluteal abscesses were defined as collections in gluteal soft tissues, not contiguous with the anal canal.

Two ultrasound experts reviewed images saved in QPathE, noting specific sonographic findings including the presence of abscess, cellulitis, myonecrosis, subcutaneous air, fasciitis, and pyomyositis. Experts reviewed the stored images using a standardized abstraction form. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. The ultrasound experts were emergency medicine-trained physicians who completed a fellowship in emergency ultrasound. Experts were blinded to the final diagnosis of the studies. The patient's discharge diagnosis by the treating team was considered the final diagnosis.

Patient charts were reviewed by a data abstractor using a standardized data extraction form with the online data management and survey tool, REDCap [8]. The standardized form included information about patient demographic characteristics, medical history, physical examination findings, ED course, additional imaging studies ordered, consultations called, procedures performed, and impact of the POCUS study on patient management. The documented clinical use of POCUS was defined as explicit mention of POCUS findings in the medical decision-making section. Potential management associations, including consultation, antibiotics, additional imaging, procedures, and disposition, were abstracted when the treating team explicitly linked these decisions to POCUS findings.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Excel 365 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, Washington, United States) to generate descriptive statistics and analysis. Continuous data were presented as means with standard deviations (SDs). Dichotomous and nominal data were presented as percentages and frequencies. For reporting concordance with discharge diagnosis, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Interrater agreement was reported using percent agreement and Cohen’s kappa.

Discussion

In this selected retrospective cohort of ED patients who underwent POCUS for suspected anorectal or gluteal infection, POCUS was frequently incorporated into clinical decision-making and showed apparent concordance with final diagnoses for several conditions. Anorectal complaints present challenges in diagnosis, which can be owed to the sensitive area and overlapping symptoms among various conditions. These challenges are only heightened in the acute care setting. Additionally, with anorectal pathology, there is a need to distinguish superficial from deep pathology as it highly impacts management. Some conditions, especially perirectal abscesses, may not be externally visible and require imaging. The standard modalities for diagnosing a perirectal abscess are CT or MRI [8,4]. Cross-sectional imaging is typically used to differentiate between a perianal abscess, which is located beneath the skin of the anal canal and does not transverse the external sphincter, and a perirectal abscess. A perirectal abscess can be identified by its location beyond the external anal sphincter [9]. Ultrasound is emerging as a possible alternative imaging modality, but little on POCUS, in particular, has been explored.

In this study, POCUS findings were incorporated into medical decision-making in most cases (92.6%) and frequently influenced key aspects of care, including early consultation, antibiotic initiation, procedural decisions, and patient disposition. Our results demonstrate that POCUS showed positive agreement with final discharge diagnoses for conditions such as cellulitis (90.9%), gluteal abscess (85.7%), perianal abscess (83.3%), and perirectal abscess (80%). These findings suggest that POCUS may have a role in risk stratification by helping physicians identify patients who may require additional imaging, consultation, or procedural intervention. However, this hypothesis requires prospective validation.

The majority of perirectal abscesses, traditionally considered more difficult to diagnose clinically, were correctly identified in the ED. The single missed perirectal abscess was misclassified as a perianal abscess, highlighting that while ultrasound may reliably detect the presence of a fluid collection, differentiating the exact anatomic location can remain challenging. This could potentially be improved by targeted education. The misclassification of one perirectal abscess as perianal suggests that targeted education on anorectal anatomy, standardized scanning planes, and sonographic criteria for deep space involvement may be necessary before broader implementation. While many Emergency Physicians, particularly graduates over the past 10 years, have the knowledge and training to perform POCUS in various applications, they are likely not well-versed in anorectal ultrasound. Anorectal ultrasound is likely not performed as often, given the sensitive location and lack of supportive literature. However, the identification of a drainable abscess using POCUS is standard for Emergency Physicians [10,11]. It is conceivable that minimal education or the creation of a standardized protocol could be created to enhance diagnostic capabilities. For example, reviewing the definition between perianal and perirectal might require education. An additional area of education could be in the identification of more complicated soft tissue and muscle infections beyond cellulitis and fluid collections. Findings of myonecrosis were never documented in the ED as a finding in the three cases of confirmed myositis and two cases of pyomyositis (Figure 2). Education strategies could focus on emphasizing the importance of scanning through all fascial planes and noting when there is muscle involvement that could lend to the diagnosis of myositis or pyomyositis.

The findings of this study are consistent with prior literature demonstrating the utility of ultrasound in distinguishing cellulitis from abscess in soft tissue infections [12,13]. However, evidence specifically addressing anorectal pathology has been limited largely to case reports. Our study expands on this by providing a broader clinical sample and suggesting that this modality may have practical value in the acute care setting beyond isolated cases. It has been well established that POCUS offers several advantages over CT. Ultrasound avoids radiation exposure, can be performed rapidly at the bedside, and may expedite clinical decision-making. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, the clinical impact observed in this study is notable. POCUS contributed to earlier consultations and interventions and, in some cases, helped avoid unnecessary procedures. This suggests that its role may extend beyond diagnosis to improving workflow efficiency and patient outcomes in the emergency setting.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. This was a retrospective chart review with a convenience sample across two sites. There is a risk of selection bias as not every patient with anorectal complaints underwent a POCUS evaluation. Because POCUS was performed at the physician's discretion rather than according to a standardized enrollment protocol, the study population represents a selected subset of patients and should not be interpreted as representative of all ED patients with anorectal complaints.

The small sample size of this study limits generalizability, reducing the probability that the sample accurately represents the broader population. The generalizability of the study is also limited, as it was performed at academic medical centers with multiple ultrasound-trained fellowship faculty and fellows, which would increase comfort and use of POCUS in the evaluation of patient complaints. There was no standardized protocol for POCUS studies performed for anorectal complaints, which could lead to differences in image quality and views, and could benefit from a standardized protocol. Additionally, the lack of a uniform gold standard comparison limits the ability to definitively assess diagnostic accuracy. For example, some patients received CT, while others had findings confirmed with MRI. Several patients received no additional imaging but instead underwent a procedure in the ED or operating room to confirm findings.

Future research should focus on larger, multicenter, prospective studies with standardized imaging protocols and training to better define the sensitivity and specificity of POCUS in anorectal pathology.

Conclusions

POCUS has the potential to be a useful, noninvasive tool in the evaluation of anorectal complaints in the ED. In this retrospective review, POCUS findings were frequently documented in clinical decision-making and showed apparent concordance with final diagnoses in selected cases. While not a replacement for advanced imaging in all cases, it may serve as a valuable first-line modality to guide early management. Additional training and standardized imaging protocols may improve diagnostic performance. Larger prospective studies are needed to further define the diagnostic accuracy of POCUS and establish its role in the evaluation and management of anorectal complaints in the emergency setting.
