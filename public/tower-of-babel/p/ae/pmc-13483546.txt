Effect of oral health education by audio aids, braille, tactile models on the oral health status of visually impaired adults: a 6-month follow-up study
Authors: Derya Yıldırım, Seraceddin Levent Zorluoğlu, Esra Seden Navruz, Hasibe Taşkın, Mehmet Egemen Aydemir, Sevgi Kirboyun Tipi, Tarık Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral and dental health education is typically delivered using visual methods on jaw models. However, these methods are insufficient for individuals with visual impairments. This study aims to contribute to the oral and dental health of visually impaired adult individuals through specially designed educational methods incorporating audio guidance, Braille, and tactile models. The objectives of the study are to assess the oral health profile of visually impaired individuals, to develop an oral and dental health education model using tactile and auditory materials, to improve oral health through this training, and to evaluate whether visually impaired individuals benefit from the education provided.MethodsA questionnaire on oral hygiene habits was administered to 37 visually impaired adult individuals in a clinical setting. Following clinical examination, their oral health profile was assessed using the plaque index, gingival index, and OHIP-14. Subsequently, oral health education was provided through a combination of audio guidance, Braille, and tactile model-based methods. The training and clinical evaluations were repeated at the 1st, 3rd, and 6th months to monitor changes over time. Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 27. Normality was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Levene’s test. Depending on the distribution, Independent Samples t-test or Mann-Whitney U test was used for comparisons between two groups, and Paired Samples t-test or Friedman test for related samples. For comparisons among three related groups, Repeated Measures ANOVA or Friedman test was applied. Post hoc analyses were performed using Bonferroni correction, and the Marginal Homogeneity test was used for dependent categorical variables.ResultsWithin-group comparisons across time points revealed statistically significant differences in gingival index (GI), plaque index (PI), and OHIP-14 scores (p < 0.05). According to the Bonferroni-adjusted analyses for GI and PI, statistically significant differences were observed between the 6th-month measurements and those at baseline, the 1st month, and the 3rd month; between the 3rd-month measurements and baseline and the 1st-month measurements; and between baseline and the 1st-month measurements (p < 0.05). Overall, the scores decreased progressively over time. At each follow-up session, both the gingival and plaque indices declined, indicating a statistically significant improvement in oral health status.ConclusionsIdentifying the oral health needs of visually impaired individuals, developing specialized education strategies, and making service delivery accessible are of great importance both for improving individuals’ quality of life and for ensuring equity in healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08692-x.

Background

Visual impairment is a term that encompasses low-vision conditions such as visual disturbances, partial vision loss, blurred vision not corrected with refractive correction, total blindness, reduced contrast sensitivity, and visual field defects [1]. Worldwide, at least 2.2 billion people have near or distance vision impairment. Vision loss can affect individuals of all ages [2]. According to the November 2023 Disability Statistics Bulletin of the Turkish Ministry of Family and Social Services, the number of visually impaired individuals who are registered and alive in the National Disability Data System is 215,076 [3]. Oral health is a factor that affects an individual’s general health, quality of life, work performance, social interactions, and self-confidence [4, 5]. Visually impaired individuals rely predominantly on their hearing, touch, and smell senses to perceive their environment and maintain daily activities [6]. It has been shown that visually impaired individuals may have lower levels of oral hygiene compared to their sighted peers [7–9].Studies have reported that poor oral hygiene is common among visually impaired individuals, and that dental caries and various degrees of periodontal diseases are frequently observed. Due to numerous environmental and societal challenges, visually impaired individuals often experience difficulties in accessing healthcare services [10–13]. For individuals without visual impairment, oral health education is provided visually through models, videos, and hands-on demonstrations. Oral and dental health education and motivation can be delivered using visual, auditory, and tactile learning materials such as videos, songs, jaw models, and brochures [14, 15]. However, these methods cannot be used in oral and dental health education for visually impaired individuals [5]. In oral health education for visually impaired individuals, the use of tactile and auditory materials is required [5, 16]. Three-dimensional tooth and jaw models, auditory educational materials, and informative brochures prepared in the Braille alphabet enable these individuals to gain awareness about oral health and acquire practical skills [5, 16, 17]. Oral health education provided visually through dental and jaw models is ineffective in visually impaired individuals, and consequently, their oral health care remains insufficient [6, 18, 19]. It has been reported that providing oral hygiene education to visually impaired individuals through multi-sensory methods that include tactile and auditory approaches may be beneficial [6, 19, 20]. There are studies demonstrating that this education can be supported with documents prepared in the Braille alphabet, a tactile writing system that facilitates the learning and comprehension of visually impaired individuals [21, 22]. However, the existing studies have been designed for visually impaired children and conducted in school settings rather than clinical environments [5, 16, 23–25]. To the best of our knowledge, there is a paucity of research involving the clinical follow-up and oral health examination of visually impaired individuals. Furthermore, studies evaluating oral health education models specifically designed for visually impaired adults remain limited.

The aim of this study was to develop a multimodal oral health education model for visually impaired adults and to evaluate the effects of this educational method on oral health outcomes.

Methods

The research protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Süleyman Demirel University (decision no: 286/2023). Power analysis was performed using the G*Power 3.1 software. Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.25), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and nonsphericity correction coefficient ε = 0.75, and considering that four repeated measurements would be performed, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 29 participants [25]. The assumed effect size was based on Cohen’s standard classification for repeated-measures designs and was supported by a previous study reporting improvements in oral health outcomes following educational interventions in visually impaired individuals [25].

Participants were recruited through the Isparta Branch of The Turkish Association Of The Visually Impaired and consisted of individuals registered as totally blind according to association records. A total of 37 visually impaired individuals residing in Isparta, Türkiye, were included in the study. These participants were individuals registered as totally blind according to association records and self-reports. Individuals with disabilities other than visual impairment and those without access to a smartphone for QR code use were excluded from the study.Informed consent prepared in the Braille alphabet was obtained from the visually impaired participants. This study was designed as a single-group, quasi-experimental pretest–posttest study with repeated measures, conducted in a clinical setting over a 6-month follow-up period.

An educational model consisting of three components was developed to provide oral health education to visually impaired individuals.

In this educational model, materials created using tactile, auditory, and Braille alphabet-based methods were utilized. For tactile education, healthy tooth models that allowed individuals to perceive tooth morphology through touch and jaw models produced using a three-dimensional printer (Ultimaker2+, 2016) were designed, and caries-simulated tooth models were created by abrading the surfaces of the healthy tooth models. After lightly abrading the surface of the healthy tooth model, a cyanoacrylate material was adhered to simulate a tooth with calculus deposits (Fig. 1). For tactile education, photographs demonstrating toothbrushing movements on a jaw model were taken, converted into vector drawings, embossed, and used to create an oral health brochure (Fig. 2).

For the auditory component, a two-minute audio recording was developed to describe, in simple terms, which regions should be brushed and how during toothbrushing, ensuring comprehensibility for the participants. This audio recording was both delivered to participants’ smartphones and integrated into the oral health brochure containing vector drawings (Fig. 2). Throughout the study period, this QR-coded auditory material was provided to the patients, and they were instructed to follow the verbal guidance and brush their teeth throughout the duration of the audio recording. Thus, the required brushing duration was standardized through the audio recording. A Braille-based brochure explaining toothbrushing techniques and oral health recommendations was developed. In addition, an informed consent form was prepared in Braille (Fig. 3).

After the educational materials were designed and prepared, training on attitudes and communication toward visually impaired individuals was provided by an instructor specialized in visual impairment to the dentists who conduct the examinations, to auxiliary healthcare personnel, and to fifth-year dental students completing their clinical rotations. A familiarization meeting event was conducted prior to the onset of examinations and education to improve motivation and adaptation of visually impaired individuals to the process. During the meeting, participants were informed about the research process, and the dental hospital where the study was conducted was introduced. Interaction and communication between visually impaired individuals and healthcare staff were facilitated. Following the introductory meeting, visually impaired individuals who agreed to participate in the study were scheduled for examinations at baseline and at the 1-, 3-, and 6-month follow-up visits.

All examinations and educational interventions were conducted by four dentists, who were calibrated by a specialist with 25 years of experience at Süleyman Demirel University, Faculty of Dentistry, Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology. Written informed consent prepared in Braille was obtained from all participants. Each training session lasted approximately 15 min and was delivered individually. Oral hygiene practices were explained and demonstrated using three-dimensional tactile models tailored to visually impaired individuals, followed by supervised toothbrushing practice to provide individualized feedback and ensure correct technique. The training was initially delivered at baseline and reinforced at each follow-up visit (1st, 3rd, and 6th months). To ensure consistency across participants, all procedures were conducted according to a standardized protocol, and all clinicians underwent a calibration process prior to the study. The intervention was delivered using identical materials for all participants, including tactile models, Braille-based educational brochures, and audio recordings accessible via QR code. Participant adherence was assessed verbally at each follow-up visit by asking about toothbrushing frequency, brushing technique, and use of the audio materials. In addition, improvements in plaque index and gingival index scores over time were considered indirect indicators of adherence to the intervention.

Questions prepared to determine oral hygiene habits were asked. A structured questionnaire evaluating oral hygiene habits (toothbrushing frequency and technique) was specifically developed for this study by the authors, based on clinical experience. The questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1. To evaluate the participants’ oral hygiene habits, different brushing techniques were explained to the participants, and they were asked which technique they used and about their oral hygiene habits. The horizontal brushing method (placing the toothbrush horizontally on the teeth and brushing with back-and-forth movements), the vertical method (placing the toothbrush vertically on the teeth and brushing with up-and-down movements), and the Modified Bass Technique (placing the bristles at a 45-degree angle toward the gingiva and brushing with a sweeping motion from the gingiva toward the tooth) were explained, and the brushing method used by the participants was recorded (Table 1).

The Modified Bass technique is a toothbrushing method in which the toothbrush bristles are placed at a 45° angle to the gingival margin, allowing slight penetration into the gingival sulcus and facilitating plaque removal through small vibratory movements. The Modified Bass technique was explained to participants using QR code–based audio materials and the educational brochure.

OHIP-14 questionnaire was administered to visually impaired individuals. The OHIP-14 is a comprehensive and subjective instrument used to assess the impact of oral health on quality of life. Responses were recorded using a 5-point Likert scale (0: never, 1: rarely, 2: sometimes, 3: often, 4: very often). OHIP-14 scores were interpreted as follows: 0–7: minimal impact (good quality of life); 8–14: moderate impact; ≥15: severe impact (oral health significantly affects quality of life) [26].

At baseline, an oral examination was performed. Plaque index (PI) scores were recorded for all participants. The plaque index (PI), developed by Löe and Silness, was used to assess the amount of plaque at the gingival margin of each tooth using visual inspection and a periodontal probe. The scoring system was as follows: 0: no plaque; 1: a thin plaque layer detectable with a probe; 2: a moderate amount of plaque visible to the naked eye; 3: abundant plaque accumulation at the gingival margin with interdental spaces filled with plaque.

Plaque index scores were interpreted as follows: 0–0.9: low plaque accumulation (good oral hygiene); 1–1.9: moderate plaque accumulation; 2–3: high plaque accumulation (poor oral hygiene) [27]. At baseline, the gingival index (GI) was measured. Gingival Index (GI) is used to determine the degree of inflammation and bleeding at four sites: mesial, middle, and distal points on the vestibular surface, and one point on the lingual/palatal surface of each tooth.

The scoring system was as follows: 0: no visible signs of inflammation; 1: mild inflammation with changes in gingival color and consistency; 2: moderate inflammation with bleeding on gentle probing; 3: severe inflammation with spontaneous bleeding. Gingival index scores are interpreted as: “0–1: healthy or mild gingivitis; 1.1–2: moderate gingivitis; 2.1–3: severe gingivitis.” [28].

After measuring the gingival and plaque indices in the initial examination, oral and dental health education using all three methods were performed. Tactile education was provided using three-dimensional printed tooth and jaw models; Braille-based education was delivered through an oral health brochure with vector illustrations; and auditory education was provided via QR code–based materials. Following the baseline examination, toothbrushes and toothpaste were provided to the participants. Participants were instructed to brush their teeth twice daily for two minutes using audio guidance accessed via their mobile devices or by scanning the QR code on the oral health brochure. Following the baseline examination, participants underwent periodontal treatment. Follow-up examinations were conducted at the 1-, 3-, and 6-month time points. At each follow-up visit, an oral examination was conducted, plaque and gingival index data were recorded, and oral health education was reinforced. New toothbrushes and toothpaste were provided at the 3-month follow-up visit. The OHIP-14 questionnaire was administered to the participants at the 6-month follow-up. The participants were asked to indicate which educational model they considered most beneficial (tactile, auditory, or Braille), and this question was administered in an open-ended format.

Descriptive statistics (number, percentage, mean, standard deviation, median) were used for data analysis in this study. Changes in GI and PI scores across time were analyzed using the Friedman test, while OHIP-14 scores were analyzed using the paired samples t-test. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Normality of the OHIP-14 difference scores was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the assumption of normality was met. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS version 27.

Results

All participants completed the study, and no dropouts occurred during the follow-up period.

A total of 37 visually impaired individuals aged 30–49 years (mean age: 38.32 ± 5.37 years), including 17 females (45.9%) and 20 males (54.1%), were included in the study. The socio-economic distribution of the 37 participants showed that 5.4% were in the high-income group, 56.8% in the middle-income group, and 37.8% in the low-income group. Regardless of income level, all participants received the same educational intervention. In addition, toothbrushes and toothpaste were provided to all participants at the 1st and 3rd follow-up visits for their use. Participants were also asked about the frequency of their dental visits. All participants had social security coverage and were able to access dental care in public hospitals. Among the participants, 45.9% reported visiting a dentist when symptoms occurred, 37.8% every six months, and 16.3% once a year. These findings suggest that limited access to dental care due to low income was not a concern in this population. The majority of the participants were found to have a secondary (high school) level of education (67.6%), followed by primary or lower education (18.9%) and higher education (13.5%).

Discussion

Communication in visually impaired individuals can be achieved through tactile, auditory, and Braille methods. There are studies in which oral hygiene education in visually impaired children has been delivered using Braille, auditory materials, and three-dimensional tooth and jaw models, and their outcomes evaluated [29–31]. However, no study has been conducted evaluating oral health in adult visually impaired individuals by implementing educational models. As communication methods differ between children and adults, oral hygiene practices may also vary. Therefore, it is important to produce educational materials for oral hygiene training in adult visually impaired individuals and to investigate their effectiveness. This study is the first to evaluate oral hygiene status and the impact of oral health on quality of life in adult visually impaired individuals by simultaneously applying Braille, tactile, and auditory educational models.

In contrast to previous studies that have predominantly focused on pediatric populations, our findings extend the evidence base to adults, suggesting that multisensory educational approaches remain effective across different age groups. In this context, it is also necessary to target visually impaired adults who did not receive such education during childhood. This highlights the importance of adapting, rather than directly transferring, pediatric educational strategies to adult populations.

Plaque and gingival index measurements were performed to assess the effectiveness of the oral hygiene education models. Evaluating plaque and gingival indices in non-clinical settings presents challenges in terms of study standardization and implementation. However, in our study, a follow-up design was implemented while maintaining clinical standards. Previous studies have been reported to be conducted in school environments [16, 29, 30, 32]. Assessment of plaque and gingival indices is significantly affected by the environment in which the measurements are performed. Field studies conducted in schools offer advantages in reaching large sample groups and evaluating the effects of community-based oral health education; however, the absence of standardized lighting and the lack of a dental unit limit measurement accuracy. This may lead to errors particularly in detecting plaque accumulation in interdental areas and gingival inflammation. In contrast, measurements performed in a clinical environment using a dental light, periodontal probe, and appropriate patient positioning provide higher precision and reproducibility [22, 33, 34]. In our study, examinations were carried out in a clinical environment to ensure standardization. This methodological difference may partly explain why the magnitude of improvement observed in our study appears more pronounced compared to some school-based studies, where environmental limitations could have affected measurement sensitivity.

In the literature, tactile, auditory, and Braille methods have been used either individually or in combination for oral and dental health education in visually impaired children [29, 31, 32]. Suharsini et al. [5] used songs that taught toothbrushing and models simulating dental calculus. They delivered oral hygiene education through such materials. In that study conducted in children, songs were used as auditory materials to facilitate communication with the children. Since we worked with adults in our study an audio-recorded training material accessible via QR code was created. Thus, an individualized educational material that patients could access at any time was developed. Additionally, generating this material as a QR code enabled the creation of an up-to-date educational resource that can be easily integrated with modern technology. In our study, it was considered important that visually impaired individuals perceive healthy tooth anatomy, pathological conditions such as caries, and deposits such as calculus, in order to ensure oral health awareness and effective toothbrushing. Before oral hygiene education, healthy tooth models and caries and calculus formations created on these models were used to explain these concepts. Participants were taught, using the models, which pathological conditions might arise as a result of inadequate oral hygiene. The preference for tactile models may be explained by their ability to support experiential learning. Through direct touch, visually impaired individuals can actively explore oral structures, which facilitates motor skill acquisition and enhances understanding. This hands-on approach may be more effective than passive learning methods [29, 31]. In the study by Muraleedharan et al. [30], it was concluded that a multisensory approach (combination of auditory, tactile, and performance methods) improved oral hygiene when applied with specialized instruction for visually impaired individuals. Parallel to this, in our study, the educational model created using auditory, tactile, and Braille methods was applied simultaneously to visually impaired individuals, and an improvement in their oral health was observed at the end of the study. Consistent with Muraleedharan et al., our findings support the effectiveness of multisensory educational approaches; however, our study further demonstrates that integrating modern digital tools, such as QR code-based audio materials, may enhance accessibility and reinforce learning in adult populations. Different follow-up durations have been applied in previous studies [16, 29–32]. Das et al. [29] evaluated results with a three-month follow-up, Muraleedharan et al. with a nine-month follow-up, and Sardana et al. [16] with a six-month follow-up. In our study, participants were followed for six months, with control examinations conducted at the first, third, and sixth months after the baseline examination, and education was repeated at each visit.

Reinforcing the training at every follow-up strengthened oral hygiene habits, and through clinical examinations, the effect of the education on oral hygiene behavior was evaluated. The repeated reinforcement applied in our study may explain the sustained improvements observed over time, aligning with behavioral learning theories that emphasize repetition for habit formation.

In this study, improvements in oral hygiene were observed in adults using these educational models, and reductions were noted in plaque and gingival index values. No previous study evaluating oral health-related quality of life with OHIP-14 in visually impaired individuals was identified, and in our study, OHIP-14 scores decreased while quality of life increased. The observed reductions in GI and PI scores were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, indicating a substantial improvement in gingival health and oral hygiene. Similarly, the decrease in OHIP-14 scores reflects a meaningful enhancement in oral health-related quality of life. These findings are in line with previous studies reporting improvements in clinical parameters following oral health education in visually impaired children; however, our study additionally demonstrates that such improvements can translate into enhanced quality of life in adults, an aspect that has been insufficiently explored in the literature.

In our study, GI scores decreased from 1.96 at baseline to 0.79 at the 6-month follow-up, while PI scores decreased from 2.24 to 1.20. Similarly, Indurkar et al. [31] reported reductions in GI and PI scores following oral health education interventions in visually impaired children, with GI scores decreasing from 1.23 to 1.00 and PI scores from 2.17 to 1.80. Das et al. [29] also reported significant reductions in plaque and gingival scores following oral health education interventions in visually impaired children during a 90-day follow-up period. The greater reductions observed in our study may be associated with the repeated reinforcement sessions and the multimodal educational approach incorporating tactile, auditory, and Braille-based materials. Differences in study populations may also have contributed to the observed findings. Unlike pediatric populations, visually impaired adults are generally responsible for maintaining their own oral hygiene and may demonstrate greater self-directed adherence to educational interventions. This may have contributed to the more pronounced reductions observed in our study.

The Hawthorne effect, defined as behavioral changes due to participants’ awareness of being observed, may have influenced the outcomes of this study and cannot be completely excluded. However, the sustained and progressively increasing improvements observed over the 6-month follow-up period suggest that the findings are unlikely to be solely explained by this effect. In addition, the repeated reinforcement of the educational intervention supports that the observed improvements are attributable to the structured educational program.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the research was conducted on a relatively small sample within a specific geographic region, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of visually impaired individuals. As such, the results may differ across populations with varying demographic and socio-cultural characteristics. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples are needed to improve the external validity of the findings. Second, the duration of the study was limited to six months. Longer follow-up periods are necessary to evaluate the sustainability of behavioral changes in adults, the evolution of individuals’ attitudes toward oral hygiene over time, and the long-term maintenance of acquired habits.

Another limitation of the study is the absence of a control group. However, withholding oral health education from visually impaired individuals was considered ethically inappropriate. Therefore, all participants received the intervention, and a repeated-measures design was employed to enable within-subject comparisons over time. Additionally, periodontal treatment was provided to all participants, which may have influenced the clinical outcomes. Consequently, the independent effects of the educational intervention could not be fully distinguished from those of periodontal treatment. Another limitation of this study is that, although socioeconomic status and educational level data were collected, no subgroup analyses were performed to assess their potential impact on oral health outcomes because the distribution of participants across subgroups was not homogeneous and some categories included a limited number of participants, which may have reduced the statistical reliability and interpretability of subgroup comparison.

One of the important aspects of this study is its focus on the oral health of visually impaired individuals, who represent special-needs of population. Due to the limited number of studies on oral and dental health education of visually impaired adult individulas in the literature, this results of this study and developed educational materials are tought to be helpful for the dental health professionals and visually disabled patients. The use of multisensory educational methods—such as auditory guidance, Braille materials, and tactile models—during this process increased accessibility, supported active participant engagement, and serves as a strong example of inclusive educational strategies for individuals with disabilities. Additionally, the combined use of both clinical indicators (e.g., plaque index, gingival index) and assessing participant attitudes enabled comprehensive interpretation of the findings.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of oral health in visually impaired adults under standardized clinical conditions, filling a significant gap in the literature regarding clinical follow-up in this population. Our findings demonstrate that an educational intervention incorporating Braille, auditory guidance, and tactile models leads to a statistically significant improvement in gingival and plaque indices. These results indicate that tailored multisensory educational strategies are highly effective in enhancing oral hygiene among visually impaired individuals.

Furthermore, integrating the OHIP-14 scale enabled a nuanced assessment of how oral health directly affects quality of life in this cohort. The development and implementation of accessibility tools—specifically, Braille-based informed consent forms and instructional brochures alongside original vector-based tactile illustrations—represent a scalable model for inclusive healthcare. In conclusion, providing accessible, specialized educational materials is essential for improving clinical outcomes and ensuring health equity for visually impaired adults Multimodal oral hygiene education and systematic follow-up are necessary for the sustainable improvement of oral health in visually impaired adults.
