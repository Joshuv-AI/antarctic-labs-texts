The impact of orthogonal image-guided frequency on positioning error correction and dose in postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer
Authors: Fangfen Dong, Shanting He, Bing Wu, Jiaming Li, Zhixin Wang, Yong Yang, Benhua Xu, Yifa Zhao, Xiaobo Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeThe incidence and mortality of breast cancer are increasing year by year. Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) after breast cancer surgery is one of the important ways of comprehensive treatment of breast cancer, but the frequency of IGRT and additional radiation damage are one of the key problems of clinical attention. To investigate the impact of different orthogonal image-guided frequencies on positioning error correction, plan target volume (PTV) margin expansion, and dose distribution in postoperative radiotherapy for breast cancer patients, providing a reference for the optimal orthogonal image-guided frequency in clinical practice for post-surgical breast cancer radiotherapy patients.Materials and methodsFrom January 2021 to January 2022, 61 patients undergoing postoperative intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) for breast cancer at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital were selected. Prior to each radiation session, initial setup errors were measured as the control group (Group A) using real-time orthogonal images aligned with digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRR). Automatic couch adjustments were then performed based on alignment results within specified error tolerances to determine residual setup errors. Data collected were categorized into experimental Groups B1 to B5 based on different verification frequencies (every 1 to 5 days). Statistical analysis was conducted on the grouped data to calculate PTV expansion margins under different error conditions and assess their impact on dose.ResultsIn the study involving 61 patients, a total of 915 datasets were collected. The initial setup errors in three translational directions were (3.57 ± 3.48) mm, (2.76 ± 2.60) mm, and (2.65 ± 2.28) mm, respectively. With increasing verification frequency, residual setup errors gradually decreased. Under daily verification frequency, residual errors were (1.65 ± 0.90) mm, (1.60 ± 0.94) mm, and (1.56 ± 0.90) mm in the three translational directions. Pairwise comparisons between control and experimental groups indicated that differences were statistically significant except between B3 Vs B4, and B4 Vs B5. Based on setup error calculations under different verification frequencies, the reference PTV expansion margins decreased with increasing frequency. Specifically, under daily verification, the calculated PTV expansion reference values reduced from initial uncorrected setup errors of 6.53 mm, 5.07 mm, and 4.01 mm to 1.26 mm, 1.41 mm, and 1.29 mm, respectively. The differences between verification frequencies of every 3 days, 4 days, and 5 days were minimal. Finally, virtual error plans were designed in the treatment planning system based on average errors for each group of patients. Deviations in target area doses were statistically significant compared to the original plan across different imaging frequencies.ConclusionDaily image-guided setup verification (including correction) effectively reduces setup errors in postoperative breast cancer IGRT. Variations exist in the efficacy of error correction and its impact on PTV expansion and dose among different verification frequencies. Higher frequencies exhibit more significant error correction effects, necessitating smaller PTV expansions. At the same time, daily image guidance can reduce the dose of the heart and left lung. Further follow-up studies are required to track the impact of these deviations on dose and clinical efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16443-y.Keywords: Breast cancer, Image-guided frequency, Intensity-modulated radiotherapy, Setup error

Introduction

According to the 2022 GLOBOCAN global cancer statistics, there were 2,308,897 new cases of female breast cancer diagnosed, accounting for 11.6% of all cancer cases. This makes female breast cancer the most common type of cancer among women worldwide and a leading cause of cancer-related deaths in females. It’s notable that among transitional female populations, breast cancer, cervical cancer, and ovarian cancer mortality rates have surpassed those of lung cancer [1]. Postoperative radiation therapy (RT) is a crucial component of treatment plans for breast cancer patients. It is typically used as adjuvant therapy following breast-conserving surgery to reduce the risk of local recurrence. Additionally, in specific patient groups, RT may also be employed after mastectomy to further enhance treatment outcomes and survival rates [2]. Positioning errors in radiation therapy can lead to inadequate target area dosing, thereby reducing efficacy and increasing radiation exposure to critical organs, potentially causing side effects. Therefore, minimizing positioning errors is crucial for improving treatment efficacy and patient survival [3, 4]. IGRT effectively reduces setup errors, enhances treatment accuracy, improves clinical outcomes, and reduces side effects [5].

Commonly used IGRT devices include two-dimensional or three-dimensional modes, with and without radiation, each having its own advantages and disadvantages [6–8]. Single kV-level two-dimensional orthogonal image-guided technology is widely used due to its speed and lower radiation dose. However, excessive scanning frequencies can result in additional absorbed doses for patients, potentially causing secondary harm to target areas or critical organs [9–12]. Therefore, to provide recommendations for the frequency of orthogonal image-guided verification in postoperative breast cancer radiotherapy, and to apply IGRT rationally to ensure maximum treatment efficacy while limiting potential side effects, this study aims to:

Compare and analyze differences in positioning errors under different orthogonal image-guided frequencies.

Calculate reference values for PTV expansion margins under different orthogonal image-guided frequencies.

Incorporate positioning errors under different orthogonal image-guided frequencies into virtual treatment planning, and analyze dose differences to target areas and critical organs.

Patients and methods

Patient data

A total of 61 breast cancer patient cases from the Department of Radiation Oncology, Union Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, were selected from January 2021 to January 2022. These patients underwent radiotherapy using the Varian 23E linear accelerator with a dose of 40 Gy/15 fractions, and orthogonal image-guided systems were used for image guidance during each fraction of the treatment course. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Union Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, with the ethical approval number 2022QH018.

The inclusion criteria for patients were: (1) pathologically confirmed breast cancer patients; (2) patients requiring IMRT after radical mastectomy for breast cancer; (3) patients with arms that could be raised and able to fully expose the affected breast; (4) patients with a KPS score greater than 80. The exclusion criteria for patients were: (1) patients with difficulty supporting their arms and unable to meet the positioning requirements for thermoplastic membranes; (2) patients with other discomforts or unable to complete the entire research process.

Positioning fixation and CT simulation localization

The patient was fixed using a single frame (Klarity R612, Guangzhou Clarity) with or without thermoplastic film (Klarity R322, Guangzhou Clarity). The principles of positioning fixation include: (1) meeting the treatment requirements; (2) ensuring the patient has a comfortable position that can be maintained for a long time, avoiding positioning errors caused by forced postures; (3) ensuring reproducibility, so that the patient can maintain the same fixation effect throughout the entire treatment process, reducing positioning errors between fractions and within fractions.

A Philips 16-slice large-bore spiral CT scanner was used, with the “Chest, C, Helical” scan sequence selected. The scan range was from the neck to the lumbar spine, with a slice thickness of 2.5 mm, tube voltage of 120 kV, tube current of 325 mA, and pitch of 0.813.

Target area and critical organ delineation

The patient’s positioning CT images were imported into the Varian Eclipse V.15.6 treatment planning system, where the target area and organs at risk (OAR) were delineated by chief physicians or senior physicians. The clinical target volume (CTV) and PTV were delineated according to the definitions in the International Commission on Radiation Units (ICRU) Report 83. At the same time, healthy-side breast, heart, lungs, ipsilateral humeral head, and spinal cord were also delineated as OAR.

Prescription dose and plan design

The prescribed dose is: total dose of 40 Gy/15F for PTV; OAR dose limits are: Dmean < 6 Gy for the heart (left-sided breast cancer), Dmean < 2 Gy for the heart (right-sided breast cancer), V5Gy < 50% and V20Gy < 25% for the affected lung, Dmax < 20 Gy for the spinal cord, and Dmean < 25 Gy for the affected thyroid. It is stipulated that 95% of the PTV volume should receive 95% of the prescribed dose.

The intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) plan was designed using the Varian Eclipse V.15.6 treatment planning system (USA), with 6 MV X-ray energy and a calculation grid of 2.5 mm. The plan consists of 7 fields of irradiation, with two opposing fields designed based on the different patient positions, and another two chest wall irradiation fields formed by converging 10°. Three irradiation fields are used for the neck lymphatic drainage area.

Treatment implementation and data acquisition

During the treatment of the patient, two therapists were responsible for positioning together. Before each treatment, 45° and 135° real-time positioning images (also known as A and B images) were acquired using an orthogonal image-guided positioning system for position verification. This image guide method has the advantages of fast shooting speed and low imaging dose. The imaging conditions were automatically determined and set based on whether the tumor target was located on the left or right side and the amount of patient tissue along the penetration path. The conventional settings for A and B images were 110 kV, 100 mA, 100ms and 120 kV, 200 mA, 200ms, respectively, as shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic diagram of the registration interface for guided photography of the left breast tumor image: Positional CT is the three-dimensional diagram of the CT image scanned during the positioning of the patient, DRR is the two-dimensional image reconstructed according to the localization image, and live image is the image obtained during the image guidance process

Highly experienced therapists utilize the orthogonal image-guided system software to automatically register the acquired images with the digitally reconstructed radiographs (DRR) generated by the treatment plan. It is worth noting that we defined experienced therapists as those with intermediate or higher titles and more than 3 years of clinical experience. If automatic registration fails to yield results, manual registration is adopted. The registration process involves setting a registration box centered on the treatment target area, while referencing anatomical landmarks such as the chest wall, ribs, thoracic vertebrae, and clavicle as regions of interest. The resulting positioning errors in the x (left-right), y (superior-inferior), and z (anterior-posterior) directions are recorded.

The initial positioning error obtained during the first image-guided verification is defined as the initial positioning error. According to institutional and reference guideline standards, the error tolerance range is set at 3 mm [13, 14]. If the error exceeds 3 mm, the image-guided system’s correction software is used to automatically move the treatment table for correction. After correction, real-time images are reacquired and verified until all errors fall within the acceptable range before treatment can be administered. Any remaining errors before treatment are defined as residual errors. If the initial error already meets the treatment requirements, no correction is necessary, and the initial error is equal to the residual error.

Data grouping

There are 61 patients, with each patient undergoing 15 image-guided sessions, resulting in 15 sets of initial errors and residual errors, totaling 915 sets. The initial positioning error data is defined as the control group (Group A). The combination of initial positioning error and residual positioning error data is extracted at different frequencies (every 1 to 5 days) to form the experimental groups (Groups B1 to B5). Group B1 represents the positioning error after image guidance correction every day, and group B2 represents the positioning error of image guidance correction every other day. And so on, group B5 represents the daily positioning error in the case of an image guide correction every 5 days. The grouping method is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Schematic diagram of grouping positioning error data based on different orthogonal image-guided frequencies: In the grouping, the black box represents no image guide correction, and the green box represents image guide correction. For example, group A represents the initial positioning error in patient positioning if image guidance correction is not performed daily. Group B1 represents the residual positioning error if image guidance correction is performed daily. Group B2 represents the case where image guide correction is performed one day apart. And so on, group B5 represents the case of image booting 4 days apart

Observation indicators

Firstly, compare the difference and distribution of errors in three translation directions between Group A and Group B. Secondly, calculate the margin-to-PTV (planning target volume) expansion boundary for each group based on the PTV margin expansion formula M = 2.5Σ + 0.7δ recommended by Van Herk [15], where Σ represents the standard deviation of the mean fractional error for each patient, and δ represents the root mean square of the standard deviation of the fractional error for each patient. Finally, incorporate the actual positioning errors of each patient in different groups into the treatment planning system for dose calculation, and compare the correlations and differences between groups. The specific treatment plan design method is as follows: assuming that the error in the three-dimensional direction of each group is (x, y, z), and the original plan origin is (x0, y0, z0), then the original plan is copied into a new plan, and the new plan origin is set as (x0 + x, y0 + y, z0 + z), and then the new dose distribution results are calculated without changing other optimization conditions. Then, the dose of target and normal tissue under the treatment plan with different grouping errors for each patient was recorded.

Data processing and statistical charting methods

Use Excel for data collection and organization. Apply SPSS 22.0 to conduct paired-sample t-tests on the mean values of the two groups of measurement data. Use Pearson’s test to assess the linear correlation between two continuous variables. Utilize R language for data visualization and charting.

Discussion

Image-guided radiotherapy (IGRT) aims to ensure precise alignment of the actual position of tumor targets during treatment with the planned position in the treatment plan. There are various types of IGRT technologies available [16], each with its advantages and limitations [17]. For breast cancer patients, clinically available image guidance modalities include Electronic Portal Imaging Device (EPID), Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT), Fan-beam Computed Tomography (FBCT), kV orthogonal imaging systems, optical surface monitoring systems, etc [18–20]. According to the basic guidelines for radiotherapy quality control proposed by the Radiotherapy Quality Control Expert Committee of China National Cancer Diagnosis and Treatment Quality Control Center [21]: Location verification must be performed for the first treatment; Position verification at least once a week to ensure treatment accuracy. The NCCN [22] guidelines recommend weekly imaging tests, but suggest that in “certain circumstances” more frequent imaging may be appropriate. Yet, the benefits and potential radiation safety implications of different verification frequencies are poorly reported.

Orthogonal image-guided technology is widely used in breast cancer radiotherapy due to its low dose and high efficiency. Studies have shown that daily orthogonal imaging guidance can reduce setup errors to (3.58 ± 2.35) mm, (3.51 ± 2.08) mm, and (4.44 ± 3.62) mm [23]. Increasing the frequency of orthogonal imaging during treatment further reduces residual setup errors to (1.78 ± 1.26) mm, (1.98 ± 1.49) mm, and (1.67 ± 1.27) mm. This study collected initial errors and residual errors data from daily image guidance sessions, grouped and compared them based on different verification frequencies. Results indicated initial setup errors for breast cancer radiotherapy were (3.57 ± 3.48) mm, (2.76 ± 2.60) mm, and (2.65 ± 2.28) mm in three dimensions. As verification frequency increased, residual errors decreased gradually, with residual errors in three translation directions reduced to (1.65 ± 0.9) mm, (1.60 ± 0.94) mm, and (1.56 ± 0.9) mm under daily verification. Statistical comparisons between control and experimental groups showed significant differences in most pairs, except between B3 and B4, and B4 and B5. Further analysis of setup error distributions among six groups demonstrated a decreasing proportion of setup errors ≤ 3 mm from B1 to B5.

To ensure adequate coverage of the tumor target volume with prescribed doses, appropriate margins around the CTV are essential to form the PTV [24]. Studies [25, 26] suggest PTV margins for breast cancer CTV depend on patient characteristics, radiotherapy techniques, and verification frequency, typically requiring 5–10 mm PTV margins. Calculations based on Van Herk’s formula M = 2.5Σ + 0.7δ for PTV margins under different setup errors showed reductions with increasing verification frequency. For instance, daily verification reduced PTV margins from initial setup errors (uncorrected) of 6.53 mm, 5.07 mm, 4.01 mm to 1.26 mm, 1.41 mm, 1.29 mm. Similar margins were calculated between every 3 days, 4 days, and 5 days verifications, ranging from 3 to 6 mm. Another interesting feature of the results is that the systematic and random errors of group B5 are smaller than those of group B4 in the x direction, resulting in a smaller calculated PTV. The reason may be that in the x direction, the error correction effect of group B5 on day 6 and day 11 is better than that of group B4 on day 5, day 9 and day 13. Therefore, it is also suggested that the patient’s positioning error may be larger on the 6th and 11th days, and image guidance correction is more necessary. Notably, studies like Nienke et al. [27] using orthogonal imaging for real-time tracking suggested even smaller PTV margins of 1–3 mm depending on treatment duration, indicating higher verification frequencies and shorter imaging intervals can further reduce PTV margins. In combination with the results of this study and existing studies, for patients who increase the frequency of image guided validation once or more per day and in divided times, the external boundary of PTV can be appropriately reduced to 3–5 mm, and clinical adjustment can be combined with specific application.

Regarding dose accuracy, which is crucial for effective radiotherapy. At present, most studies [28, 29]show that the effect of CBCT frequency on target dose coverage and organ-at-risk (OAR) dose in breast cancer patients is still largely uncertain. A study [28] found no significant difference in V95% of breast CTV and simultaneous integrated boost CTV doses between daily and alternate day CBCT. The dose distribution in OAR was similar between the two imaging protocols. However, weekly CBCT though led to a significant decrease in dose coverage compared to daily CBCT and a small but significant dose increase in most OAR. To evaluate the impact of different setup error groups on target and normal tissue doses, various setup errors were incorporated into each patient’s treatment plan for recalculating PTV, thyroid, heart, esophagus, spinal cord, and left/right lung radiation doses. Statistical analysis revealed minor deviations (within 3%) in PTV D98, D95, and D90 compared to the original plan, but larger deviations in D2 and Dmax under initial setup errors, with Dmax deviations reaching up to 9.84%.

Overall trends indicate that for normal tissue doses, the lower the image guidance frequency, the larger the deviations. Among these, the heart, left lung, and esophagus are most affected by setup errors caused by different frequencies. Under initial setup errors, the average dose deviation for the heart can reach 19.25%, while the V20Gy deviation for the left lung can reach 18.03%. This is mainly because the lower the frequency of image guidance, the less correction for the positioning error, and the greater the positioning error beyond the 3 mm limit, which may bring greater dose deviation. This is similar to the results of studies using CBCT, Mankinen M et al. [30] suggested that daily CBCT image guidance is necessary from the perspective of dose deviation, and it is also recommended to combine CBCT and SGRT to improve the coverage of the target area and the irradiation dose of normal tissue. Meanwhile, Peng et al. [31] proposed a study on Optimal fractionation and timing of weekly cone-beam CT in daily surface-guided breast cancer radiotherapy for breast cancer suggest that the combination of daily SGRT and CBCT for more than 3 weeks is also recommended. In conclusion, while normal tissue doses remained within acceptable limits across all groups in this study, considerations for larger tumors in left breast cancer patients emphasize stricter limits for heart and left lung doses. Comparative analysis of target and normal organ doses among groups highlighted significant differences with strong correlations, underscoring the differential impact of setup errors on PTV, where daily corrections showed minimal variability.

Furthermore, when increasing the frequency of image-guided verification, consideration must be given to the additional radiation dose associated with this technology, alongside assessing the benefits of daily or more frequent image guidance. According to Quinn et al. [32], adding daily KV-CBCT verification to treatment increases doses to the contralateral breast, ipsilateral breast, contralateral lung, and heart by approximately 12%, 0.3%, 24%, and 13%, respectively, posing an additional risk of secondary malignancies. In this study, each imaging guidance session entails a dose of 0.85 mGy, resulting in a total dose of only 12.75 to 25.5 mGy (0.85 * 15 to 30 sessions, with potential re-imaging required after each guidance session). This dose represents an extremely small proportion, ranging from 0.0003% to 0.0006% of the total prescribed dose, nearly negligible. This indicates that even with increased image guidance frequency, the additional radiation dose received by patients remains very low. In addition, in terms of accuracy and accuracy, studies have confirmed that for breast cancer cases, the effects of orthogonal KV 2D imaging and CBCT position verification are similar, and whether the positioning error calculated by orthogonal KV image guided positioning technology is close to CBCT online correction technology, and has the advantages of lower acquisition time and imaging dose [33–35]. Therefore, using orthogonal image guidance to correct setup errors and increasing image guidance frequency to enhance radiotherapy precision does not impose a significant radiation burden on patients, thereby maximizing treatment effectiveness while minimizing potential side effects.

While this study comprehensively explores differences in setup errors, PTV margins, and doses under various image guidance frequencies, there are also some shortcomings and limitations in this study. For example, the number of subjects needs to be increased to improve the accuracy of the results. Secondly, only one image guidance method is used for data analysis, which limits the reference range of research results. Finally, this study also needs to increase the follow-up study on the clinical prognosis of patients to improve the therapeutic effect of patients.

Future research should focus on advancing image-guided techniques with developments in large-field radiotherapy and adaptive technologies, emphasizing radiation-free, real-time tracking, and the effective combination of multiple image guidance methods [36]. Future considerations may include ultrasound-guided, electromagnetic-guided, or MRI-guided techniques, applying these methods alone or in combination at appropriate frequencies in clinical practice [37, 38], further optimizing breast cancer image guidance strategies. Additionally, the integration of deep learning and artificial intelligence technologies in this field shows promise, potentially predicting patient setup errors during treatment through deep learning models and enabling early correction or plan optimization [39, 40]. This approach not only enhances treatment precision but also reduces patient radiation exposure, thereby improving overall treatment efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

This study conducted a comparative analysis of the impact of different frequencies of orthogonal image guidance for breast cancer on positioning errors, PTV margins, and dose deviations. The preliminary results show that daily image guidance correction has significant benefits and can effectively reduce the positioning error. In terms of dosimetry, the exposure dose to the heart and left lung can be reduced, while the additional radiation dose guided by daily images remains low. Further exploration can be conducted in the future to investigate clinical benefits, integrate new image guidance techniques, and introduce artificial intelligence technologies to further improve treatment accuracy.
