Safety and feasibility of the bowel-first versus conventional approach in intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease: a propensity score-matched study
Authors: Weilin Qi, Wei Liu, Xiaolong Ge, Wei Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPenetrating Crohn’s disease (CD) is a severe phenotype marked by fistulae, abscesses, and complex intra-abdominal inflammation, often necessitating surgical intervention. Surgical management is particularly challenging in the presence of dense adhesions and severe mesenteric thickening. The bowel-first approach—where the diseased bowel is transected prior to mesenteric dissection—has been proposed as an alternative to conventional techniques, yet comparative evidence remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of adult patients with penetrating CD who underwent elective intestinal resection at our center between January 2019 and December 2022. Patients were categorized into bowel-first or conventional groups according to the surgical technique used. Propensity score matching (1:1) was performed based on demographic, laboratory, and disease-related variables to balance baseline characteristics. The primary outcomes were overall and major postoperative complication rates. Secondary outcomes included total operative time, bowel resection and anastomosis time, intraoperative blood loss, and length of postoperative hospital stay.ResultsA total of 122 patients were included (91 conventional, 31 bowel-first). After matching, 31 patients remained in each group with well-balanced baseline characteristics. Total operative times were comparable between the bowel-first and conventional groups (211.13 (55.84) min vs. 239.39 (67.20) min; P = 0.077). However, bowel resection and anastomosis time was significantly shorter in the bowel-first group (48.71 (9.83) min) compared to the conventional group (65.81 (17.56) min; P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in intraoperative blood loss (80 [50, 150] mL vs. 100 [50, 200] mL; P = 0.492) or postoperative hospital stay (10 [8, 20] days vs. 9 [8, 15] days; P = 0.676). Rates of overall postoperative complications (35.48% vs. 32.26%; P = 0.788) and major complications (6.45% in both groups; P = 1.000) were also similar.ConclusionsThe bowel-first approach is a safe and feasible alternative to conventional surgery for intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease. This technique significantly reduces bowel resection and anastomosis time without increasing operative risk or length of hospital stay and may be especially advantageous for patients with severe mesenteric thickening or complex adhesions. Further prospective studies are warranted.Keywords: Crohn’s disease, Penetrating phenotype, Bowel-first approach, Surgical outcomes, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by transmural inflammation that can affect any region of the gastrointestinal tract [1, 2]. Among its clinical manifestations, penetrating CD represents a particularly severe phenotype, characterized by transmural disease progression leading to complications such as fistulae, abscesses, and perforations. Approximately 50% of patients develop penetrating complications over the course of their illness. The management of penetrating CD requires an individualized, multidisciplinary approach that combines optimal medical therapy with surgical intervention when indicated [3, 4]. Although advances in biologic therapies have delayed the need for surgery in many cases, operative management remains the cornerstone for patients with established penetrating complications following appropriate preoperative optimization [3].

Effective surgical management of intra-abdominal penetrating CD relies on meticulous preoperative planning, including detailed imaging, clinical assessment, nutritional optimization, and infection control. Infection control often necessitates percutaneous drainage and antibiotic treatment when sepsis is present [5]. The primary goal of surgery is complete resection of the macroscopically diseased bowel. However, surgical intervention is frequently complicated by factors such as extensive intra-abdominal inflammation, fragile tissues, thickened mesentery, and unpredictable intraoperative findings.

The heterogeneity of Crohn’s disease precludes the adoption of a universally standardized surgical technique. Traditionally, the operative approach begins with adhesiolysis and mobilization of the affected intestinal segment and its associated mesentery, followed by mesenteric dissection and subsequent bowel resection. However, dense adhesions or complex fistulas can render the conventional approach technically challenging, resulting in prolonged operative time, increased blood loss, and an elevated risk of injury to adjacent organs. As an alternative, the bowel-first approach begins with transection of the diseased bowel segment, followed by mesenteric mobilization and dissection. This technique enhances visualization of anatomical planes and minimizes manipulation of inflamed tissue, thereby reducing the risk of inadvertent injury.

Despite these potential advantages, the impact of the bowel-first approach on key surgical outcomes—including postoperative complication rates, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, and hospital stay—remains poorly defined. Therefore, we conducted a direct comparison with conventional techniques to evaluate the safety and feasibility of the bowel-first approach in patients with intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease, with the goal of informing surgical decision-making and improving management strategies for this complex patient population.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with penetrating Crohn's disease who underwent surgical resection at our center between January 2019 and December 2022. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Sir Run Run Shaw Hospital (approval number: [2023–630-01]). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age 18 years or older; (2) a confirmed diagnosis of penetrating Crohn’s disease based on clinical, endoscopic, radiological, and histological findings; (3) elective intestinal resection; (4) surgery performed by the same dedicated surgical team; and (5) availability of complete medical records for analysis. Exclusion criteria were: (1) patients with stricturing or inflammatory phenotypes without penetrating complications; (2) emergency surgery for perforation or massive bleeding; and (3) concurrent malignancy.

Preoperative management

Optimal management of intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease required multidisciplinary team discussions, particularly in the perioperative period [6, 7]. Comprehensive preoperative assessment included computed tomography enterography (CTE) or magnetic resonance enterography (MRE) to identify both luminal and extraluminal disease manifestations. In the acute phase, antibiotics were administered as indicated, with percutaneous drainage performed to control abscesses when necessary [8]. Patients presenting with hypoalbuminemia (serum albumin < 30 g/L) or marked intra-abdominal inflammation received individualized nutritional support, either enteral or parenteral, for a minimum of two weeks prior to surgery. Additionally, corticosteroid therapy was tapered to less than 20 mg/day of prednisolone or equivalent before the surgical intervention.

Surgical procedure

The laparoscopic procedure involved meticulous dissection of the intestinal tract, followed by resection and anastomosis through an umbilical incision. Conversion to open surgery was considered when laparoscopy was not feasible, particularly in cases of dense adhesions or significant intraoperative bleeding obscuring anatomical structures [9]. Direct open surgery was selected for patients with multiple prior abdominal operations, suspected extensive adhesions, or when preoperative imaging suggested complex fistulizing disease precluding safe laparoscopic access.

For internal fistulas, the surgical strategy comprised resection of the affected bowel segment and repair of involved organs when necessary [10]. In cases where both intestinal loops were extensively diseased, double resection was performed. During fistula division, dissection followed the wall of the diseased bowel to avoid injury to adjacent healthy tissue.

Intraoperative assessment of mesenteric thickening severity utilized a standardized scale referencing the work of Coffey et al. [11], based on surgical palpation and visual inspection: mild (< 10 mm thickness, pliable tissue), moderate (10–15 mm, partial rigidity), and severe (> 15 mm, woody consistency with loss of vascular planes). The decision to employ the bowel-first approach intraoperatively adhered to a predefined protocol initiated in June 2021. This approach was considered if any of the following criteria were met: (1) severe mesenteric thickening precluding safe mobilization; (2) complex fistulas involving multiple organs; or (3) dense mesenteric adhesions obscuring anatomical planes. The attending surgeon (W.Z.) made the final decision based on these criteria following initial exploration.

The conventional approach consisted of adhesiolysis, mesenteric dissection, and subsequent bowel resection. In contrast, the bowel-first approach involved initial transection of one or both ends of the diseased bowel, followed by mobilization and dissection of the mesentery, as depicted in Fig. 1. In both the conventional and bowel-first procedures, a stapled wide, side-to-side (functional end-to-end) anastomosis was created [12]. Temporary stoma formation was considered in cases with significant abdominal contamination.

Data collection

Patient demographics, clinical characteristics, laboratory parameters, and operative details were extracted from electronic medical records. Baseline covariates included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, serum albumin, hemoglobin (HB), C-reactive protein (CRP), disease duration, disease location (as per the Montreal classification [13]), mesenteric thickening, presence of anal fistula, history of prior abdominal surgery, and surgical approach (laparoscopic versus open).

Outcome measures

Primary outcomes comprised postoperative complications, graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system [14]). Major postoperative complications (Clavien-Dindo grades III–IV) were defined as anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal abscess requiring drainage, or any complication requiring reoperation within 30 days of surgery. Minor complications (Clavien-Dindo grades I–II) included wound infection, ileus, transfusion requirement, dehydration/acute kidney injury, deep vein thrombosis, or any other complication not meeting the criteria for major complications. Secondary outcomes included perioperative measures: total operative time, bowel resection and anastomosis time, estimated blood loss, and length of postoperative hospital stay. Bowel resection and anastomosis time was defined as the period from the first surgical maneuver to divide the bowel or its mesentery for resection, to the completion of the final suture or staple for the anastomosis.

Statistical analysis

Patients were classified into bowel-first or conventional surgery groups according to the surgical technique used. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation) or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables.

To minimize selection bias and balance baseline characteristics, 1:1 propensity score matching was performed using the nearest neighbor method without replacement. Propensity scores were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, incorporating the following covariates: age, gender, BMI, smoking status, serum albumin, hemoglobin, CRP, disease duration, disease location, mesenteric thickening, anal fistula, prior abdominal surgery, and surgical approach (laparoscopic versus open).

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 122 patients were included in the study, with 91 undergoing the conventional surgical approach and 31 managed with the bowel-first technique. To minimize baseline differences and potential confounding, propensity score matching was performed using a 1:1 nearest-neighbor algorithm without replacement. This process yielded 31 matched pairs for subsequent analysis.

Before matching, most baseline characteristics were comparable between groups (Table 1). However, a significant imbalance was observed in the severity of mesenteric thickening, with a higher proportion of patients in the bowel-first group exhibiting severe mesenteric thickening (58.1% vs. 24.2%, P < 0.001). In addition, the proportion of non-laparoscopic procedures (45.2% vs. 25.3%, P = 0.064) demonstrated borderline differences. After matching, all baseline variables were well balanced between the bowel-first and conventional groups. There were no statistically significant differences in demographic or clinical characteristics, including age, gender, body mass index, smoking status, serum albumin, hemoglobin, C-reactive protein, disease duration, disease location, mesenteric thickness, presence of anal fistula, prior abdominal surgery, or surgical approach (all P > 0.05). The propensity score matching process thus effectively minimized baseline imbalances. Detailed post-matching baseline characteristics are presented in Table 2.

Following propensity score matching, perioperative and postoperative outcomes were compared between the two groups (Table 3). The total operative time was similar between the bowel-first group (211.13 (55.84) min) and the conventional group (239.39 (67.20) min; P = 0.077). However, bowel resection and anastomosis time was significantly shorter in the bowel-first group (48.71 (9.83) min) compared to the conventional group (65.81 (17.56) min; P < 0.001). There were no significant differences in intraoperative blood loss (80 [50, 150] mL vs. 100 [50, 200] mL, respectively; P = 0.492) or postoperative length of hospital stay (10 [8, 20] days vs. 9 [8, 15] days, respectively; P = 0.676) between the two groups. Overall postoperative complication rates were comparable (35.48% vs. 32.26%; P = 0.788). In both groups, the most common major complication was abdominal abscess requiring percutaneous drainage, occurring in 2 cases (6.45%) in each group (P = 1.000).

Discussion

This study represents the first propensity score-matched analysis to directly compare the bowel-first approach with the conventional surgical technique in patients undergoing resection for intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease. Our findings demonstrate that the bowel-first approach is not associated with higher rates of postoperative complications, increased intraoperative blood loss, or longer postoperative hospital stays compared to the conventional technique. Although total operative time was similar between groups, the bowel-first approach was associated with significantly shorter bowel resection and anastomosis times. These results suggest that the bowel-first approach is a safe and feasible surgical option and may offer greater procedural efficiency for selected patients with complex intra-abdominal Crohn’s disease.

The surgical management of penetrating Crohn’s disease remains challenging due to the presence of dense adhesions, fistulous tracts, and severe mesenteric thickening, which can obscure anatomical planes and increase the risk of inadvertent injury during dissection. Traditional techniques, which prioritize formal mesenteric dissection prior to bowel resection, are often technically demanding or potentially hazardous in these situations. Our findings support the evolving paradigm that initial bowel transection—prior to complete mesenteric mobilization—facilitates safer and more effective dissection by improving intraoperative visualization, reducing tissue traction, and potentially decreasing procedural complexity.

It is noteworthy that the favorable surgical outcomes observed in both study groups may be attributable, in part, to our rigorous preoperative optimization protocol. This included multidisciplinary team planning, nutritional support, and aggressive infection control measures. Adherence to these perioperative management principles is essential, regardless of surgical technique, for optimizing outcomes in high-risk patients with Crohn’s disease and should be considered the standard of care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center study design introduces the potential for selection and information bias, despite the use of comprehensive propensity score matching to adjust for known confounders. Second, the relatively limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect small differences in infrequent outcomes, and may partially explain non-significant trends observed in operative times. Third, long-term follow-up data regarding disease recurrence and patient-reported quality of life were not available and remain important directions for future investigation. Lastly, although the choice of surgical approach followed predefined intraoperative criteria, some degree of residual confounding related to individual surgeon judgment and intraoperative decision-making cannot be excluded.

Future studies should focus on multicenter, prospective designs with larger sample sizes to confirm these findings and improve generalizability. Additional research with extended follow-up is also needed to assess long-term recurrence and reoperation rates associated with each approach. Notably, the bowel-first technique is often selected in cases featuring severe mesenteric thickening, where mesenteric transection is typically performed closer to the bowel to facilitate hemostasis, resulting in greater retention of mesenteric tissue. Whether this mesenteric retention influences the risk of disease recurrence is unclear and warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

Our findings demonstrate that the bowel-first approach is a safe and feasible alternative to conventional surgery for intra-abdominal penetrating Crohn’s disease. This technique is not associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications, intraoperative blood loss, or prolonged hospital stay. Importantly, the bowel-first approach significantly reduces the time required for bowel resection and anastomosis, thereby enhancing intraoperative efficiency. This approach may be particularly beneficial for patients with severe mesenteric thickening or complex adhesions. Nevertheless, further prospective studies are warranted to evaluate its long-term efficacy and broader clinical applicability.
