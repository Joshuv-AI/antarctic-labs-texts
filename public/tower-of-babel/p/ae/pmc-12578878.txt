Activation of enzymatic catalysis via nucleic acid hybridization affords synergistic coupling of specificity, potency, and signal amplification
Authors: Mireia Casanovas Montasell, Lou M. V. Raeven, Mathilde Ravn Malm, Jan C. M. van Hest, Alexander N. Zelikin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this work, we establish the use of nucleic acid hybridization to program the activation of catalysis in enzymes. We do this via “thiol switching”, using thiolated oligonucleotides: a protein is inactivated by conjugation to an oligonucleotide via a disulfide linkage; hybridization of the thiolated complementary oligonucleotide ensues disulfide exchange, the liberation of the enzyme, and the activation of enzymatic catalysis. In doing so, we couple the most specific recognition event (hybridization) to the most effective tool of signal amplification (catalysis). As potential applications, we illustrate the use of hybridization-activated catalysis for programming the specificity of activation for one specific enzyme and engineering of Boolean logic elements. For biosensing, we illustrate the sequence-specific detection of a target oligonucleotide at a nanomolar level, in the presence of gram quantities of genomic nucleic acids, using simple mobile phone camera. We anticipate that this technology will find use in diverse areas of biotechnology and biomedicine.

Introduction

Nucleic acid hybridization is arguably the most specific interaction in biotechnology1–3. In turn, enzymatic catalysis is among the most reliable tools for signal amplification in biosensing applications4. The combination of these two platforms is highly sought after and is expected to deliver a synergistic boost to diverse applications across the range of biotechnology and biomedicine5–8. Current successes on this avenue are already sound and impactful, but are limited to the use of active enzymes and are largely developed for enzyme patterning8,9. Significantly broader applications could be envisioned for the coupling between hybridization and conditional enzymatic activity. For example, enzyme activity turn-on as a read-out could replace or complement the existing techniques that report on hybridization, specifically the relatively low signal-to-noise fluorescence resonance energy transfer10 or the equipment-heavy fluorescence quantification methods11. From a different angle, the use of hybridization to switch enzymatic activity would enable the applications in which on-demand catalysis is warranted12–14, such as imaging and biosensing15, biochemistry16,17, bio-production schemes18, synthetic biology19, and biomedicine20–22, specifically through establishing sequence-encoded activation of individual enzymes in biochemical mixtures. However, these prospects remain largely unfulfilled, because successes in coupling hybridization and enzyme activity are scant. In one study, a single chain DNA-based aptamer was designed to bind and thereby inhibit activity of thrombin23. In another approach, nucleic acid conjugates with a protein could control enzymatic activity when the complementary strand added or removed an inhibitor from the protein active or allosteric site24–27. An enzyme could also be encapsulated into a supramolecular nucleic acid cage assembled via the origami technology28. In arguably the most elegant approach, hybridization was used to achieve the DNA-directed re-assembly of split proteins29–33. These and other examples2,19 are inspirational but may be limited by scope and scalability.

In pursuit of a broadly applicable chemical platform to control activity of enzymes in biotechnology, we focus on the chemistry of thiols. Protein cysteinome includes an incredibly diverse range of enzymes34–36. Reversible thiol oxidation and reduction, termed “thiol switching”, is indeed a natural mechanism to control enzymatic activity34,36,37. However, the examples of its use in biotechnology are rather rare19,38–41. In large part, this is because thiol-disulfide exchange is an indiscriminate process that considers all thiols and disulfides in the pool, which makes it hard, if possible, to engineer specificity of disulfide exchange. Results presented in this work illustrate that nucleic acid hybridization resolves this limitation. We illustrate that hybridization affords potent, sequence-specific enzyme activity switching, which we use to encode enzymatic activity and to design Boolean logic gates. In turn, enzymatic catalysis is a powerful readout to report on hybridization, which we use to detect target DNA sequences using mobile phone camera. We believe our results deliver the sought-after coupling of the most specific biomolecular interaction and the most reliable tool of signal amplification, with illustrated applications in biotechnology.

Results and discussion

The highest specificity of nucleic acid hybridization makes it a unique tool that enables proximity-mediated chemical transformations42–44. With this in mind, we hypothesized that a strand of nucleic acid can be conjugated to a thiol within or in the vicinity of the active site of a protein via a disulfide linkage, to produce a catalytically-inactive conjugate, termed zymogen. Hybridization of the thiol-containing complementary strand should enable the thiol-disulfide exchange, the liberation of the protein thiol, and thereby the activation of catalysis, in a highly specific, sequence-defined manner (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the proposed DNA-encoding of enzymatic activity, achieved through reactivation of the enzyme from the protein-oligonucleotide mixed disulfide using a thiolated complementary strand.

To investigate this, we used a 20 nucleotide-long DNA sequence that contained a 5’- terminal thiol group (Fig. 2A). The thiol was first exposed from the protecting groups using common reducing agents, dithiothreitol (DTT) or dithiolbutylamine (DTBA). The oligonucleotide was immediately thereafter reacted with Ellman’s reagent, to produce a chain with a terminal thiol that is activated towards thiol-disulfide exchange (DNA-ER, Fig. 2A). This reaction was followed using HPLC, which revealed the consumption of the starting oligonucleotide and the production of a new product with distinctly different retention time and a characteristic absorbance at a wavelength corresponding to the conjugated Ellmans’s reagent (325 nm; Fig. 2B). The protein used in most experiments in this study was creatine kinase (CK), which uses creatine phosphate to convert ADP to ATP. The catalytically active form of this protein is an 82 kDa homodimer; each monomer unit has four thiols that can form mixed disulfides38, of which Cys-254 and Cys-283 are the two most sterically accessible19. Cys-283 is in the protein active site, and the protein with covalently blocked Cys-283 is devoid of catalytic activity19, as is the protein split into monomer units. Here and throughout the manuscript, we refer to the disulfide-containing protein conjugates with reversibly deactivated activity as chemical zymogens19,38.

For conjugation with DNA, the protein was mixed with DNA-ER (Fig. 2D). Following a 3 h incubation, the reaction mixture was supplemented with iodoacetamide, a thiol-reactive molecule that covalently, irreversibly inactivates CK that is not conjugated to the nucleic acid. The reaction products were separated from excess DNA strands and iodoacetamide via spin filtration, and thereafter from the non-conjugated protein using a nucleic acid purification kit. The denaturing, non-reducing gel electrophoresis assay confirmed successful conjugation and indicated that each protein subunit contained multiple DNA chains, most prominently two (Fig. 2E). In turn, the non-denaturing analysis of the conjugates using protein mass photometry indicated that conjugation to DNA resulted in splitting of the CK homodimer into monomer units (Fig. 2F). The conjugates are based on disulfide chemistry and as expected, the addition of DTT afforded the degradation of the conjugate (Fig. 2E) and the re-assembly of the protein homodimer (Fig. 2F).

Catalytic activity of CK was quantified using a coupled enzymatic reaction: ATP produced by the kinase is used by luciferase for an enzymatic reaction that is accompanied by the emission of light, directly proportional to the concentration of ATP in the solution19. Solutions of the CK-DNA conjugate were supplemented with luciferase and luciferin as well as ADP and creatine phosphate, but no light emission was registered (Fig. 2G). This illustrates that the CK-DNA conjugate was devoid of catalytic activity. The addition of DTT was followed by the evolution of luminescence, that is, reductive reactivation of the kinase from its DNA-containing zymogen. Reactivation of CK using DTT illustrates that conjugation of DNA to CK is reversible. Moreover, it also implies that the Cys-283 in the protein active site was protected from the irreversible deactivation by iodoacetamide by the conjugated DNA39. Thus, while multiple thiols in CK are available for conjugation, it is the DNA conjugation to Cys-283 that affords the chemical zymogen with reversible switch of activity.

For discussion of the developed bioconjugation method, we acknowledge that conjugation via protein cysteine thiols is regioselective, but is not perfectly regiospecific, not if compared to the activity-base conjugation in the context of cysteinome45 or precision-guided covalent inhibitors46. When multiple thiols are available for bioconjugation, the most sterically accessible Cys are involved in bioconjugation, and for creatine kinase this naturally guides the conjugation to Cys-254 and Cys-283, of which the latter is important for catalysis19. The implemented covalent deactivation (using iodoacetamide) at the end of synthesis permanently deactivates all protein molecules that are not protected by the DNA strands at Cys-283. Thus, while the method of synthesis is only regioselective, the isolated creatine kinase with DNA encoded reversible activity is modified at Cys-283 regiospecifically.

Over the course of project development, we found that of the two product purification steps, the removal of the non-conjugated DNA is highly important, because the non-reacted DNA can interfere with the enzyme reactivation via competitive hybridization. In contrast, the removal of the non-reacted protein is not critical, because the addition of iodoacetamide at the end of synthesis deactivates the non-conjugated CK, thereby minimizing or altogether eliminating potential background signal.

Coupled enzymatic read-out was used to investigate the sequence specificity of reactivation of the synthesized zymogens (Fig. 3A). The use of a thiolated non-complement sequence (marked as “NC”) ensued no production of light in the reaction mixture, indicating minimal if any enzyme reactivation. In stark contrast, the addition of the thiolated complementary sequence (marked as “C”) was accompanied by the strong emission of light. This indicates hybridization of DNA, the ensuing disulfide exchange, the liberation of CK monomer units and thereafter their dimerization into the native homodimeric, catalytically active CK. Results in Fig. 3A illustrate the sequence-specific reactivation of enzymatic activity via nucleic acid hybridization. We note that thiols are prone to oxidation with air oxygen, but in practice, we found that even the two weeks-old stocks of the thiolated complementary oligonucleotide were competent in reactivating the encoded zymogen (Supplementary Fig. 1). Next, we investigated the tolerance of the DNA encoded zymogen reactivation to shortening of the activation strand. The 3’-thiolated activator strands were synthesized with a total length between 20 and 12 nucleotides (Fig. 3B). We found that all thiolated activator strands that are truncated from the 5’end (thus have the preserved sequence at the end that is proximal to the protein thiol) were equally effective at enzyme reactivation, at least at the chosen concentration. Somewhat unexpectedly, sequence shortening from the 3’- end (positioning the thiol group gradually further away from the enzyme cysteine thiol), was also largely non-detrimental, and complementary sequences as short as 14-nt afforded zymogen reactivation that was statistically superior to the non-specific reactivation using the full-length non-complement sequence (Fig. 3B). It is likely that the flexibility of both, the single chain DNA strand and the protein aid in the conformational adaptation that allows for the thiol and the disulfide to come into proximity for the chemical reaction to occur.

DNA hybridization is characterized by a high affinity of interaction, and in the proximity-mediated chemical transformations, it enables chemical reactions to proceed at low molarity of the interacting partners42,44. To investigate this for enzyme activation, we visualized CK zymogen reactivation using matched concentrations of DNA (complementary and non-complementary) and DTT, all taken at 1 µM (Fig. 3C). Under the same conditions, we also tested DTBA, which is an order of magnitude stronger reducing agent than DTT47. At this low concentration, only the DNA-mediated disulfide exchange afforded zymogen reactivation, and only when using the correct, complementary sequence of DNA, whereas when using DTT or even DTBA, enzyme reactivation was not observed. This demonstrates that DNA-encoded zymogen reactivation is significantly superior to the indiscriminate, non-specific thiol chemistry found in most biochemical mixtures. Enzyme reactivation was readily detected at concentration of the thiolated complementary oligonucleotide strand as low as 25 nM, either via quantitative luminescence measurements or via well imaging and quantification of pixel intensity (Fig. 3D and E, respectively). Over several rounds of experiments, this finding had statistical significance even relative to the enzyme reactivation using a 40-fold higher (1 µM) concentration of the non-complement sequence (Fig. 3E). Taken together, results in Fig. 3 illustrate the direct coupling of nucleic acid hybridization and enzymatic read-outs whereby hybridization is a highly potent and at the same time highly specific activator of enzymatic catalysis.

Stimuli-responsive, on-demand activation of enzymatic activity is used in diverse applications in biotechnology and in biomedicine12–22. Both the tools of physics15,17,18,21,48 and of chemistry7,24,26,38 are important in this context. The grand challenge in the field is to control activity of multiple enzymes in their mixture, an undertaking that requires the use of mutually orthogonal mechanisms of enzyme activation. The sequence specificity of enzyme activation that is inherent with DNA encoding of enzymes lends itself for these applications. As a step towards this goal, we performed DNA encoding of activity for two enzymes, namely CK and the T7 polymerase, and used the production of RNA as an illustrative read-out. ATP is one of the monomers for the de novo production of nucleic acids. DNA-encoded activation of CK and the ensuing production of ATP can therefore control the production of RNA in a nucleic acid sequence-specific manner (Fig. 4A, B). In turn, T7 polymerase can also undergo thiol-to-disulfide interconversion with an associated on/off switching of activity19, subject to nucleic acid sequence-specific reactivation (Fig. 4C, D). For the CK encoded zymogen, there was also no transcription observed when no activator strand was present (Fig. 4B, lane 1) or when zymogen reactivation was attempted using the non-complementary antisense sequence (lane 3). In contrast, the use of complementary DNA strand afforded production of the ATP and thus enabled the formation of the RNA transcript by the T7 polymerase (lane 5). In much the same way, the disulfide containing T7-DNA conjugate showed no production of RNA when no activator strand was added or in the presence of the non-complementary strand (Fig. 4D, lanes 2 and 3), whereas the addition of the complementary strand afforded successful transcription (lane 4). In these experiments, the thiolated DNA sequences were designed such that the complementary strand for the activation of creatine kinase (Fig. 4A, B) was the non-complementary strand in the T7 reactivation experiments (Fig. 4C, D), and vice versa. To further illustrate the use of orthogonal activation of individual enzymes, we employed DNA-mediated activation of enzymatic activity in the assembly of biomolecular logic gates, for potential applications in synthetic biology48–51. To realize the Boolean logic gate OR, creatine kinase was used to engineer two conjugates, both being DNA-encoded, and responding to two different sequences (Fig. 4E). The activation of the kinase zymogen could be performed using one complementary strand OR the other complementary strand. In both cases, this afforded catalytically active kinase that converted ADP into ATP, which was thereafter readily detected via the coupled luciferase assay (Fig. 4F). To engineer the Boolean element AND, we used creatine kinase AND the T7 polymerase, individually encoded with a dedicated nucleic acid sequence (Fig. 4G, H). In such a case, activation of just one of the DNA-encoded zymogens proved to be insufficient for transcription (Fig. 2H, lanes C1 and C2). Both enzymes had to be activated, to perform the synthesis of ATP by the kinase and to perform the synthesis of RNA by the polymerase (Fig. 4H, lane 5). Primitive as these are, these two Boolean elements together can be used to solve rather complex mathematical equations, and are foundational for biomolecular computing11. More importantly, these data illustrate the possibility to activate individual enzymes in complex biochemical mixtures using mutually orthogonal triggers.

Lastly, to illustrate the synergistic coupling of the highly potent hybridization that characterizes DNA hybridization and the highly favorable signal amplification associated with enzymatic catalysis, we developed an approach to detect DNA hybridization using minimal equipment, namely a mobile phone device. The key to this platform is our realization that sufficiently strong luminescence can be readily imaged using standard mobile phone camera. Enzymatic generation of luminescence is the main enabling factor for this application. The overall approach therefore relies on the encoding of enzymatic activity of CK using the specific nucleic acid sequence, which is complementary to the thiolated activator sequence (marked in Fig. 5A as A, 16 nt) and to the target analyte (T, non-thiolated, 30 nt). We also use a blocking sequence B (30 nt, complementary to strands T and the reporter conjugate C). During the developed workflow, the solution containing the blocking sequence is exposed to the test solution, which optionally contains the target analyte T. Thereafter, this solution receives the activator strand A and thereafter the reporter conjugate C. In the absence of the target sequence T, the blocking sequence B remains free and hybridizes with A, which prevents A from activating the conjugate C. The production of ATP by the reactivated kinase was first registered via an imaging plate reader for benchmarking (Fig. 5C), which illustrates the success of this biosensing approach. Specifically, the presence of the target analyte sequence was readily detected via luminescence imaging. Notably, the same outcome was obtained when biosensing was tested using a one week-old complementary sequence (A), which demonstrates that the system is stable against thiol oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 3). The maximum possible silencing of the kinase activity during the synthesis of the conjugate was essential and provided the optimized signal-to-noise contrast of imaging.

Enzymatic signal amplification that ensues the protein activation event was so pronounced that the analysis of DNA hybridization was possible using a mobile phone camera within a light protected box (Fig. 5D). In fact, visualization of luminescence using a high-end imaging plate reader and a mobile phone afforded the same results (c.f. Fig. 5C, D). Using mobile phone imaging, we were able to detect as low as 25 nM of the target, non-thiolated oligonucleotide (Fig. 5E). It should be highlighted that the detection method illustrated herein reports on the presence of DNA of the specific sequence. To illustrate this, we used a mobile phone camera and detected the target sequence T in the presence of 10-fold higher concentration of scrambled sequence oligonucleotide (Fig. 5F) or in the presence of gram quantities of genomic DNA (Fig. 5G).

The drive to use mobile phone in biosensing is incredibly strong; numerous reports on the subject have appeared recently, and the detection of nucleic acids has been of particular interest52,53. Coupling the recognition event to a catalytic signal amplification has proven to be key to the high sensitivity read-outs. It complements, and in some instances even altogether replaces the amplification of nucleic acid copies (e.g., via polymerase chain reaction)54–56. Colorimetric and particularly fluorimetry-based read-outs are of the highest frequency of use52,53. Nevertheless, luminescence is the most favourable read-out (compared to colorimetry and fluorescence) in terms of signal-to-noise. Compared to fluorescence-based readouts it is further advantaged in that it requires no external light source for excitation. Thus, biosensing imaging shown in Fig. 5 was performed by a mobile phone camera and no other equipment whatsoever. We strongly believe that the enzymatic read-out and the imaging using a mobile phone provide a time- and cost-effective alternative and can be applied in settings where advanced instrumentation is not-available (point of care).

Taken together, results of this study establish specific activation of enzymatic catalysis through DNA hybridization. The latter is a recognition event with the highest selectivity and potency. We use thiolated DNA sequences and perform “thiol switching” on enzymes that can form disulfide conjugates with thiolated, single stranded oligonucleotides. Disulfide exchange is largely an indiscriminate, non-specific process, but under the control of DNA hybridization, through the proximity mediated transformations, it afforded potent, sequence-specific enzyme reactivation. We believe that the results of this study are highly important in establishing a superior coupling between the most specific recognition event (hybridization) and the most powerful tool of signal amplification (catalysis). Unlike DNA-induced re-assembly of split proteins29–32,57, this method does not require protein engineering or the design of protein splitting. Compared to the origami techniques28, this method can be readily scaled to milligram or even higher protein quantities. The scope of this technology is naturally limited to the thiol-containing proteins in which the thiol group is within or near an active site, but this range of proteins is rather broad and includes cysteine proteases38, diverse kinases35, polymerases19, and other proteins41,58,59. We believe that the potential applications of DNA encoded enzyme activation are broad, including but not limited to synthetic biology (engineering of synthetic cells), bio-computing, and point-of-care biosensing.

The following buffers were used throughout the experiments: Tris buffer: 25 mM Tris HCl, 8 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, pH 8, autoclaved; phosphate buffer: 50 mM Sodium Phosphate, pH 7.8, autoclaved; borate buffer: 10 mM Sodium Borate, pH 8, autoclaved; RNA transcription buffer: 40 mM Tris HCl, 10 mM NaCl, 8 mM MgCl2, 2 mM Spermidine, pH 8, autoclaved.

HPLC characterization of DNA conjugate to Ellman’s reagent

For this analysis, DNA-ER was prepared by resuspending a vial of dried thiolated DNA sense 1 in 100 µL of 5 mM DTBA (in 10 mM Borate Buffer, pH 8). After 2 h incubation shaking at RT, 150 µL of 20 mM Ellman’s reagent were added to the DNA solution and incubated ON. The sample was purified in NAP5 column and concentrated in spin filter of (3000 Da cut-off). The DNA conjugate was analysed in HPLC together with a sample of non-modified DNA with matched concentrations, using EC-C18 Poroshell column with particle size of 2.7 μm, a length of 100 mm and an internal diameter of 4.6 mm (flow rate: 0.4 mL/min). The mobile phase was established by using a solvent system consisting of A: Milli-Q with 8 mM ammonium acetate pH adjusted to 5.4 and B: acetonitrile with 10 % Milli-Q and 8 mM ammonium acetate.

Elution was performed starting with solvent B 5% to B 100% over 23 min, and kept at 100% for 5 min at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. Detection was performed by UV detector (260 nm and 325 nm).

LC-TOF characterization of DNA conjugate to Ellman’s reagent

For this analysis, the DNA-ER conjugate described in the previous section for HPLC characterisation was used. The modified DNA was analysed together with unmodified DNA by LC/MS on Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system coupled to Agilent 6230 ESI LC/TOF, equipped with Phenomenex Clarity 3 μm oligo-RP 50×4.6 mm column. Sample elution was performed using a gradient from 100% 15 mM hexylamine with 25 mM HFIP to 90% MeCN over 9 min at 40 °C with 0.25 mL/min flow rate. Deconvolution was performed using the oligonucleotide algorithm in the Agilent Masshunter BioConfirm software v12.

Synthesis of DNA-based creatine kinase zymogens

First, the dried thiolated DNA sense oligonucleotides (from vendor) were dissolved in 100 µL of 5 mM DTT or DTBA in phosphate buffer by shaking for 1 h at room temperature. Then 150 µL of Ellman’s reagent 20 mM were added, and the mixture was incubated for 30 more minutes. After this time the sample was purified by gel filtration (NAP-5) in the same buffer and concentrated using spin filters (VWR 3 kDa cut-off). The obtained activated oligonucleotides were mixed with 0.5 equivalents (for catalytic experiments) or 0.1 equivalents of CK (for experiments linked to transcription) and incubated for 3 h at 37 oC with mild shaking. Finally, the sample was mixed with 2000 eq. of iodoacetamide and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The product was purified by NAP-5 columns, and thereafter by spin-filtration in Amicon filters 30 kDa cut-off.

For the zymogen samples analysed in gel electrophoresis, the final product was obtained after one more round of purification using the mirVana™ PARIS™ RNA purification kit. In this case, the steps described in the protocol section “Final RNA isolation” from the vendor were followed.

Gel electrophoresis of CK zymogens

Samples with ~2 µg of CK or CK-DNA zymogens were prepared in Milli Q water and mixed with LDS buffer (1:4 final dilution) and NuPAGETM sample reducing agent (in a final dilution of 1:8). For each, a sample without reducing agent was also prepared. Samples were incubated 20 min at 45 oC and then loaded into a NuPAGETM gel (4 to 12% bis-tris, 1.0 mm) and run for 60 min in MOPS buffer at 150 V. Finally, the gel was stained with Coomassie blue and imaged inside an ImageQuant LAS 4000 camera system (GE Healthcare).

Mass photometry characterization of CK zymogens

To determine molecular masses, pristine CK and CK-DNA conjugates were analysed on a TwoMP mass photometer (Refeyn Ltd., Oxford, England) with and without DTT. From a CultureWellTM 50-well reusable silicon gasket (3 mm diameter x 1 mm thickness, well volume 3-10 µL, Grace Bio-Labs, Oregon, USA), 8 wells were cut out and positioned at the center of a clean microscope slide (24 ×50 mm, 170 ± 5 µm, No. 1.5H, Paul Marienfield, Germany). Microscope slides were pre-cleaned by sequential sonication in isopropanol and dried under a clean nitrogen stream. Calibration of the system was performed by measuring the ratiometric contrast of thyroglobulin (670 kDa), apoferritin (480 kDa), and BSA (66 kDa monomer and 132 kDa dimer) at final concentrations of 10 nM, 5 nM, and 5 nM respectively. A 19 µL droplet of phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8, autoclaved, filtered through a 0.22 µm filter) was added to a well to permit lens focusing. 1 µL of a mixture of 200 nM BSA, 100 nM apoferritin, and 100 nM thioglobulin was added to the droplet to obtain a final volume of 20 µL. Final droplet concentrations of 20 nM for pristine CK and CK-DNA conjugates were applied for the sample measurements. Dilution of the original samples of 8.1 µM CK or CK-DNA incubated with or without 83 mM DTT for 2 h were performed immediately before measuring. Each mass photometry measurement was recorded for 60 sec. Distribution histograms and gaussian fits were generated using DiscoverMP software (Refeyn Ltd., Oxford, England).

Reactivation of CK zymogens with DTT

Reactivation of the CK-DNA zymogens was quantified via a coupled bi-enzymatic assay. A white 96-well plate was loaded with the following samples: 1 µM of CK-DNA, +/− 100 µM DTT and 5 µM Luciferin, ADP and creatine phosphate (CP) and 1:50 dilution of CellTiter-Glo ® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay luciferase (Promega). Wells were completed to 100 µL final volume with Tris Buffer. Luminescence was monitored at 37 °C in regular time intervals over 1 h (RLU integrated every 2 s) in a plate reader.

General protocol for reducing thiolated DNA strands as activators

Complementary or non-complementary DNA strands used as activators for each experiment were resuspended in 150 µL of 5 mM DTT or DTBA in phosphate buffer, shaking for 1 h at room temperature. After this time the samples were purified by gel filtration (NAP-5) and the concentration of active thiols was quantified using Ellman’s reagent test and a calibration curve at absorbance 412.

Reactivation studies of CK zymogens via DNA hybridization

For each of these experiments, a white 96-well plate was loaded with the following samples: 1 µM of CK-DNA, +/− 1 µM DNA complementary 1 (20, 18, 16, 14 or 12 nucleotides, shortened on 3’ or 5’ ends) or non-complementary 1 (according to active thiols) and 5 µM Luciferin, ADP and CP and 1:50 dilution of CellTiter-Glo ® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay luciferase (Promega). Luminescence was monitored at 37 o C in regular time intervals over 1–3 h (RLU integrated every 2 s) in a plate reader. These experiments were performed in 3 independent replicates.

Reactivation of CK zymogens with DNA strands vs. DTBA or DTT in equimolar concentrations

A white 96-well plate was loaded with the following samples: 1 µM of CK-DNA, +/− 1 µM DNA complementary 1 or non-complementary 1 (according to active thiols), +/− 1 µM DTBA or DTT and 5 µM Luciferin, ADP and CP and 1:50 dilution of CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay luciferase (Promega). Influence of the DNA strands concentrations in reactivation of the CK zymogens

A white 96-well plate was loaded with the following samples: 100 nM of CK-DNA, +/− a range of 1000-25 nM DNA complementary 1 or non-complementary 1 (according to active thiols) and 5 µM Luciferin, ADP and CP and 1:50 dilution of CellTiter-Glo ® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay luciferase (Promega). Reactivation of CK zymogens via DNA hybridization to power transcription

In small Eppendorf® tubes, solutions with 5 mM CP, 5 mM ADP, 2 µM CK-DNA and +/− 3 µM DNA complementary 1 or non-complementary 1 were prepared, and volume was completed to 100 µL with Tris buffer. As positive and negative controls, a sample with 5 mM ATP and a sample without zymogen were prepared, respectively. The samples were incubated for 2.5 h at 37 °C and then transferred to Amicon filters with a 3 kDa cut-off and centrifuged for a total of 20 min at 10,000g, with the addition of 50 µL Tris buffer after 10minutes. The eluted fractions for each sample were collected and stored in −20 °C.

For transcription, 10.5 µL of each collected sample was mixed with 4 μL transcription buffer, 3 μL of NTPs (from 100 mM stocks GTP, UTP, CTP), 0.5 μL DNA template and 1 μL T7 polymerase. After 3 h incubation at 37 °C, 1 μL of DNAse was added to stop each reaction and incubated for 20 more min. To analyse the samples were mixed 1:1 with RNA loading dye and SYBR Green II and loaded in 1% agarose gels. The gel was run for 1.5 h at 50 V and imaged inside an ImageQuant LAS 4000 camera system (GE Healthcare) with 2 sec exposure.

Synthesis of DNA-based T7 RNA polymerase zymogens

First, the dried thiolated DNA sense 2 oligonucleotides were resuspended in 100 µL of 5 mM DTT in phosphate buffer, shaking for 1 h at room temperature. Then, 10 equivalents of the activated oligonucleotides were added to the solution of T7 polymerase, which added to a total volume around 50 µL, and incubated for 3 h at 37 °C with mild shaking. Finally, the sample was purified in 30 kDa Amicon spin filters.

Reactivation of T7 RNA polymerase zymogens via DNA hybridization

Biosensing of target DNA coupled to enzymatic reactivation

First, 1 µM of blocking DNA was incubated with 1 µM of target DNA (or scrambled DNA 1 or 2) for 1 h at 37 °C (In Tris buffer pH 8, final volume 100 µL), in Tris buffer (final volume of 100 µL). Then, 1 µM of reduced thiolated DNA activator (16nt length, shortened on the 5’) was added to the mixture and incubated for 45’ more. After that time, 1 µM of zymogen CK-DNA was added together with 5 µM of substrate mixture (luciferin, ADP, CP and 1:50 dilution luciferase). Luminescence was recorded in a plate reader after 1 h incubation at 37o. At the end point, luminescence was imaged inside an ImageQuant LAS 4000 camera system (GE Healthcare) with 20 sec exposure. An image was also taken using a smartphone (iPhone 15 Pro) camera with 30 sec exposure, inside a paper box to avoid any light contamination. These experiments were performed in 3 independent replicates.

A similar experiment was performed using a DNA activator that had been reduced a week previous to the biosensing experiment, and kept in the freezer until then.

Dose-response analysis of target DNA for biosensing coupled to enzymatic reactivation

In this experiment, 1 µM of blocking DNA was incubated with a range of target DNA concentrations (from 2 µM to 25 nM), or 2 µM scrambled DNA 1, or a combination of both together (1 µM scrambled DNA with 100 nM target DNA) for 1 h at 37 °C (In Tris buffer pH 8, final volume 100 µL). Then, 1 µM of reduced thiolated DNA activator (16nt length, shortened on the 5’) was added to the mixture and incubated for 45’ more. After that time, 1.5 µM of zymogen CK-DNA was added together with 5 µM of substrate mixture (luciferin, ADP, CP and 1:50 dilution luciferase). Luminescence was recorded in a plate reader after 1 h incubation at 37o. At the end point, luminescence was imaged using an iPhone 16 camera with 30 sec exposure.

Biosensing of target DNA in the presence of genomic DNA

In this case, 1 µM of blocking DNA was incubated with 2 g/L of genomic DNA from herring sperm, or 500 nM target DNA, or a combination of both together for 1 h at 37 °C (In Tris buffer pH 8, final volume 100 µL). Then, 1 µM of reduced thiolated DNA activator (16nt length, shortened on the 5’) was added to the mixture and incubated for 45’ more. After that time, 1 µM of zymogen CK-DNA was added together with 5 µM of substrate mixture (luciferin, ADP, CP and 1:50 dilution luciferase). Luminescence was recorded in a plate reader after 1 h incubation at 37o. At the end point, luminescence was imaged using an Iphone 16 camera with 30 sec exposure.

Statistical analysis and reproducibility

Unless stated otherwise, presented are based on three independent experiments and shown as mean±st.dev. Where reported, statistical significance was evaluated with a one- or two-way ANOVA with the Sidak’s multiple comparisons test performed in Graphpad Prism®. Where shown, gel electrophoresis images and luminescence photography images show representative results over multiple rounds of experiments.
