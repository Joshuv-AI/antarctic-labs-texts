Implementing a telephone‐based support program to address loneliness in older adults
Authors: Carla M. Perissinotto, Katrina Hough, Soe Han Tha, Bri Matusovsky, Danielle Escueta, Preston Burnes, Ashwin A. Kotwal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSocial isolation and loneliness are key social determinants of health linked to poor outcomes. While telephone‐based support programs have some evidence, their implementation remains understudied. We evaluated the Friendship Line, a 24‐h telephone‐based support program for older adults, using an implementation science lens. This included structured questionnaires and semi‐structured interviews with staff (N = 13), volunteers (N = 17), and older adult callers (N = 27), totaling 57 participants. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically and mapped onto the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. Six key features were identified for the success of telephone‐based loneliness support programs: (1) fostering trust and reliability through 24‐h access; (2) creating a safe and nonjudgmental space; (3) ensuring consistency and relationship building; (4) balancing stability and adaptability during changes; (5) securing ongoing funding; and (6) providing specialized training focused on loneliness and aging. These programmatic features contributed to caller satisfaction, reduced loneliness, and improved connectedness. Effective and sustainable telephone‐support lines addressing social isolation and loneliness require multifactorial strategies, including skill development, robust organizational infrastructure, and tailored training to meet the diverse needs of callers.

INTRODUCTION

In May of 2023, Surgeon General Dr. Vivek Murthy published an advisory citing evidence that loneliness and isolation are an epidemic and a call to action for a national strategy to advance social connection. 1 Social isolation and loneliness, key components of social connection, are prevalent among older adults, with some studies citing nearly a quarter of older Americans as socially isolated and over 40% lonely. 2 , 3 , 4 Loneliness is the subjective feeling of being alone, and social isolation refers to a deficit in the number of relationships or contact with others. 5 The health effects from both include increased risk of death, increased functional decline and frailty, worse cardiovascular disease outcomes, polypharmacy, and higher health care costs. 2 , 4 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Groups disproportionately impacted by the effects of loneliness and social isolation include older adults, racially and ethnically minoritized communities, and lower‐income communities. 1 , 4 Although they are related, loneliness and social isolation are typically distinct experiences with separate pathways impacting downstream health outcomes. Consequently, each may have different approaches for intervention.

Prior to the COVID‐19 pandemic, community‐based organizations had long been taking a leading role in efforts to improve social connection through a variety of in‐person, telephone‐based, and group‐based interventions. During the pandemic, however, interventions experienced a rapid shift from in‐person to video‐ or telephone‐only options. 10 Telephone‐based lines further grew in popularity due to perceptions of their low cost, ease of implementation, and prior literature demonstrating effectiveness for participants 11 and staff or volunteers. 12 , 13 , 14 Phone programs are now common across the country, and previously established phone‐based interventions have greatly increased their capacity. However, there is substantial variation in programs; some are ad‐hoc versus structured and far‐reaching, and they range from free, to non‐profit, to for‐profit interventions. 15 Importantly, many phone‐support lines are either crisis lines geared toward suicide prevention (i.e., hotlines) or can be emotional support lines (i.e., warm‐lines) centered on preventing crises and addressing the needs of specific groups and communities.

There is evidence for the use of phone support for social connection, but little is known about the best ways to implement, scale, adapt, and sustain phone‐based programs in real‐world settings. Moreover, knowledge gaps remain in describing the training, recruitment, and experiences of staff and volunteers. For example, one study investigated the process of initiating a telephone‐outreach program, but lacked a formal implementation science framework, limiting its ability to inform program development in other settings. 16 The frequent assumption that something is better than nothing can lead to a failure to recognize that specific training and expertise in topics, such as aging and loneliness, may be critical to success. Addressing these knowledge gaps could inform the sustainability and scaling of many telephone‐based interventions that began during the pandemic and continue to meet a pressing public health challenge.

We, therefore, sought to evaluate the implementation of a long‐established telephone‐based intervention, the Friendship Line (FL), for loneliness in older adults using the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR). 17 We used primarily qualitative methods to understand different aspects of implementation from the perspective of diverse stakeholders, including program leadership, paid and volunteer staff, and diverse older adult callers. We focus on implementation outcomes rather than clinical outcomes because these serve as “necessary preconditions for attaining subsequent desired changes in clinical or service outcomes.” 18

METHODS

The intervention

The FL, at the Institute on Aging, was established in 1973 and receives more than 300,000 calls annually from across the United States. Founded by the late Patrick Arbore, the FL is a telephone‐based support call line specifically designed to meet the needs of lonely older adults whose needs were not (and are not currently) being met by other crisis support lines. Uniquely, the FL functions as both a crisis phone line (accredited by the American Association of Suicidology [AAS]) and an emotional support line where callers may utilize the service in nonemergency situations. The FL is staffed by a combination of volunteer and paid staff members who undergo specific training designed by the FL to account for the target population and need to meet suicide accreditation guidelines. While they do not have a counseling certification, these volunteers and staff members are termed counselors by the FL. When the COVID‐19 pandemic started and call volume increased, an upgrade to the call center software enabled all training and operations to become remote; consequently, counselors are now dispersed broadly and able to work from home.

The FL offers 24/7 service and has two major components: inbound and outbound calls (Figure 1). Outbound calls are outreach calls to individual callers with a purpose of connecting with people on a regular basis to help monitor physical and mental health concerns and provide connection, empathy, and emotional support. Callers can self‐refer or are referred by others, including family, friends, social workers, health care providers, or health plans. Callers agree to receive calls from FL counselors ranging from daily to monthly, based on assessments and caller requests. Inbound calls, sometimes referred to as a warm line, are offered 24 h a day and, at the time of data collection, were available nationally. Due to funding shifts, inbound calls are now limited to California residents due to the state's ongoing support for the intervention. The FL is uniquely designed for older adults, and thus different from other warm lines (including the newly established national suicide prevention line). All calls (inbound and outbound) average 10–15 min, with a small minority of callers who call daily. In California, the FL further supports adults over 18 who identify as having a disability. There can be a wait time depending on call volume and limited staff overnight. The FL is intended as both an intervention to prevent suicide and provide social support and companionship for lonely callers.

Holistic view of Friendship Line services. Established in 1973, the Friendship Line is the only accredited 24‐hour support line for adults over age 60 and adults with disabilities at risk for, or experiencing, loneliness.

The FL follows the AAS guidelines on screening, recruiting, and training of all new staff and volunteers. All counselors undergo a series of interviews and screenings, including discussions on their comfort with addressing suicide and understanding the symptoms and presentation of suicidality. Counselors receive at least 40 h of didactics and hands‐on training on topics such as suicide risk assessment, active listening, ageism, grief, loss, and loneliness before working independently on answering or making phone calls. Foundational training also includes information about harm‐reduction, stages of change, and the interaction of multiple mental health symptoms with personality traits. After completing initial training, FL counselors have supervisor oversight throughout their shifts, including supervisors auditing notes, randomly listening in on calls, and check‐ins every 3 months.

The evaluation

An implementation science centered evaluation occurred between 2020–2022 and included structured electronically administered surveys (Table S1) for paid staff and volunteers (N = 59), semi‐structured phone‐ or video‐based interviews of paid staff (N = 13) and volunteers (N = 17), and semi‐structured phone‐based interviews of older adult callers (N = 27). The study was reviewed and approved by the UCSF Institutional Review Board.

Recruitment and data management

Our sample for analysis included individuals throughout the organizational structure of the FL, including executive leadership staff (n = 7), full‐time employees (n = 6), volunteers (n = 17), and persons receiving services from the FL (n = 27). For FL staff and volunteers, we first sent an email requesting survey participation, and those interested in an additional interview were able to leave contact information for further outreach. For FL callers, we purposively sampled based on age, gender, race/ethnicity, and non‐English speaking callers to ensure a diverse sample (Table S2). As the focus of this study was on the implementation of the FL, we wanted to avoid interference with the intervention itself. Thus, callers were asked via an automated voice system after their phone calls with counselors if they were interested in working with UCSF on an evaluation of the FL and would agree to be interviewed. Callers who responded yes on the automated system were then contacted by UCSF researchers and given further information on the interview process and offered compensation with a $20 gift card. All interviews were 30–60 min and conducted in English, except for two interviews in Spanish that were conducted and translated by one author with native fluency in Spanish (C.M.P.). Interviews were audio recorded, professionally transcribed, and uploaded into NVivo (lumivero.com) qualitative data analysis and research software.

Implementation science framework and analysis

We adapted the CFIR, a well‐described multi‐level implementation determinant framework, to guide our qualitative analysis. 17 CFIR provides a comprehensive taxonomy of operationally defined constructs that are likely to influence the implementation of complex programs. CFIR constructs are organized into five major domains: the outer setting, the inner setting, the process used to implement the program, intervention characteristics, and the characteristics of individuals involved in the intervention. Each domain contains further additional subcodes describing more specific factors impacting implementation. Interview guides focused on understanding relationships between counselors and callers, FL operations, and organizational characteristics, including training, evaluation of programs, and funding. To elicit organic conversations with participants, we did not construct interview questions to align only with CFIR domains. Instead, researchers used CFIR as a guiding framework to draft questions on how the FL operates and sustains operations amidst changing circumstances. Interview guides placed additional emphasis on understanding the CFIR outer setting and distinguishing features of the FL, and characteristics of the inner setting that could contribute to scaling of the FL. This adaptation of CFIR enhanced researchers’ understanding of the data by allowing greater freedom to include themes under multiple domains and include data from FL call participants, which CFIR generally does not include. The necessary changes improved data analysis procedures, but still remained aligned with CFIR's model.

Under CFIR, the inner setting refers to the setting where the intervention exists and is being implemented, such as a hospital. In this case, the inner setting refers to the community‐based organization, and this domain includes information on culture, communication, and structure. Outer setting refers to the larger system in which the inner setting exists. For the FL, this refers to their parent organization, the Institute on Aging, and the greater funding and social landscape in which nonprofits operate. The process domain includes strategies and activities involved in implementing the intervention. It includes collaboration, assessing needs, planning, adapting to changes, engaging populations, and reflecting on implementation. This is the broadest of all domains. Intervention characteristics is the domain that describes the innovation itself, including its purpose, its source, its evidence base, cost, design, and so on. Lastly, individual characteristics describe the interested parties involved in the intervention, including funders, designers, recipients, staff members, volunteers, policy leaders, and so on.

We used a combined inductive and deductive approach where thematic constructs that arose inductively from the data were systematically mapped onto CFIR's major domains (Figure 2). To maintain the flexibility of inductive analysis, we only used CFIR's five main domains and avoided using CFIR's smaller domain‐specific subcodes. Analysts found the subcodes to be restrictive in capturing the complexity of the detail in the data, while also missing key elements of what was addressed through probing in interviews. Three research staff (C.M.P., K.H., B.M.) conducted the qualitative analysis using NVivo software. Analysts met three times throughout coding (beginning, half‐way through, and ending) to confirm results and discuss discrepancies in the creation of codes or overlapping codes. Analysts were able to consolidate redundant codes into one group and move forward in analysis using a single codebook (Table S3). At the end, researchers met to validate data and ensure rigorous qualitative coding. In the event of any disagreements over analysis, a larger discussion was held among the entire research team to resolve. This approach allowed us to avoid preconceived notions or limitations that may have occurred if analysts considered only CFIR constructs in initial reviews. Adapting CFIR enabled a greater understanding of key implementation aspects from the perspective of staff, volunteers, and callers (as participants and outcomes are not a usual part of CFIR methodology) to guide major implementation recommendations. We did, however, include participant perspectives to demonstrate areas where there may have been divergences of experiences between callers and staff of the FL.

RESULTS

Our implementation science evaluation highlighted several aspects relevant to the success of the FL, organized within the five CFIR domains (Figure 2): Intervention Characteristics, Outer Setting, Inner Setting, Characteristics of Individuals, and Process. These aspects were further clarified and summarized into six key features: (1) fostering trust and reliability through 24‐h telephone access; (2) creating a safe and nonjudgmental space for callers; (3) ensuring consistency and relationship building for those receiving calls; (4) maintaining stability and adaptability during organizational changes, such as the COVID‐19 pandemic; (5) securing necessary funding to support a growing population of lonely adults; and (6) a unique focus on loneliness and aging. Example quotes from staff and volunteers supporting our findings are summarized in Table 1, and participant themes are presented in Table 2. While implementation studies do not tend to focus on participant outcomes, we present tables showing similarities and divergences in findings between FL staff and participants (Figure 3 and Table 3). As noted in Table 3, some of the biggest differences were in the call time limits and the wait times. While some callers wanted longer calls, this ultimately can affect wait times and access to the service—a tension that both staff and callers noted. Intervention characteristics (characteristics of the FL)

Overall, FL counselors had a clear understanding of the purpose of both inbound and outbound calls and the unique population they serve (Table 1). This helped with key feature 1: fostering trust and reliability. They often felt the tension of funding and time constraints, and how this impacted their ability to grow and adapt. A frequently discussed issue was the time limit on calls; while some found it sufficient, a larger majority desired more flexibility, recognizing the limits were to accommodate increasing call volumes. Callers had differing perspectives on the time limit. There were some callers who did not realize there would be call limits or different staff who attended to them. Other prominent themes included a strong awareness of the FL's philosophical framework that aims to meet people where they are, at any time, without judgment and with the hope of reducing feelings of loneliness (key feature 2: creating a safe and nonjudgmental space). The values of the FL were evident in the intervention and felt by counselors as well as frequent and new callers who recognized FL's unique service to older adults—a need not being met by other support services.

Outer setting (external policies, incentives, and pressures)

The FL staff and volunteers noted aging‐related barriers such as age‐related disabilities, sensory impairments, cognitive impairments, ageism, and at times, suicidality. Ageism was reflected in the challenge of sustained funding, given that many funding opportunities are focused on younger adults and children. Aging‐related barriers, such as limited technological literacy and cognitive and sensory impairments, meant the FL needed to be cautious in integrating technological advances so that both clients and counselors could use their systems and ensure that a new technological advance did not result in a barrier to its use. Training content for counselors reflected education on the needs and were inclusive of older adults and adults living with disabilities. Ultimately, callers appreciated the tailored focus on age, which fostered trust and a nonjudgmental space (key feature 6: a focus on loneliness and aging).

Additional external factors included the uniqueness of the FL in serving a diverse group tied together by a unifying theme of loneliness in the context of other support services. There was a general notion that this service fills a unique need, given the history and stability of the FL, as well as its growth over time. Several staff members also commented on challenges relating to the need for change and expansion over time. This was most evident during the COVID‐19 pandemic when counselors needed to meet higher call volumes, while ensuring both staff and callers felt connected and supported. As an accredited suicide crisis line, the FL was used by callers experiencing suicidality, a theme raised by callers, staff, and volunteers. Lastly, the leadership staff appreciated the opportunity for growth and partnership with larger organizations and payors, with the hope that steady funding would allow the FL to continue to serve at‐risk populations and align on health system incentives (key feature 5: sustaining funding).

Inner setting (FL program characteristics, culture, and implementation readiness)

Paid and volunteer staff noted a strong internal community of support, although this was partly disrupted by the pandemic, creating additional stress and barriers to maintain connection. Additionally, specialized training is a core component of the FL. While experiences varied, most (78%) were satisfied with the training and desired additional and ongoing training to meet the needs of callers. This was particularly relevant as staff and volunteers noted the difficulty in taking emotionally intense calls and appreciated existing training and the need for ongoing support. Training in active listening was essential, as was leaving the calls open‐ended without specific goals in order to facilitate comfort and trust among callers. Of note, these views from counselors were consistent with beliefs from leadership staff who believed ongoing training and adaptation are key for ongoing success (key feature 4: stability and adaptability). Lastly, the FL's unique role was clear throughout the organization as staff and volunteers heard from callers that the FL provided a unique service compared to other call lines.

Characteristics of individuals (individual characteristic, behaviors, and knowledge)

Themes in the individual domain included the skills, comfort level, and the challenges of taking difficult calls, such as those focused on suicidality. Staff described a need to establish trust with callers through their own personal attributes, FL training, and colleague support. Staff also reflected on the strength of having a diversity of callers, meaning that they appreciated the wide range of callers with different needs and experiences. Accordingly, staff were grateful for and aware of the need to staff the FL with people of diverse backgrounds and language abilities (key feature 3: consistency and relationship building). FL staff had a great deal of awareness of the prevalence of loneliness and the importance of the FL in addressing this need. Lastly, staff and volunteers described satisfaction and professional growth from their involvement with the FL. Together, these topics contributed to staff identifying with the values of the FL and overall feelings of commitment to the work.

Implementation process (executing, planning, and evaluating)

Establishing and maintaining a growing support line like the FL requires reflection and evaluation at many levels. The focus on persons with disabilities and older adults necessitated frequent reassessments, as FL leaders and staff had to frequently ensure the needs of the callers were being met and that any technological or process changes remained inclusive. New contracts with payors, which increased call volume, led to changes in baseline training, intermittent training for current counselors, and technology reviews to efficiently staff the call lines (key feature 4: sustainability and adaptability). Staff reflected on the impact of challenging calls and repeat callers, and how both affect meeting the needs of individual callers and scaling a program with limited resources. In addition, there were some challenges in supervision once the FL phone system (iCarol) went remote. Counselors reflected that when they were all in person, it was easy to pull a supervisor to listen in on the call by motioning them over. This was harder when you had to message them through the system to hop‐on, but still doable.

A detractor to successful implementation discussed by some staff was a sense of disconnect at times from the parent organization, the Institute on Aging, as the FL is a distinct service line that has consistently been challenged in generating sufficient revenue to cover expenses. This created some mistrust and questioning if funding might be distributed more equally throughout the Institute on Aging to ensure ongoing FL service availability. This concern was ultimately driven by staff and volunteers’ awareness of the need for the FL services and the desire to see the program continue.

DISCUSSION

Our evaluation of the FL highlights the complexities involved in effectively implementing a telephone support line. It underscores the importance of a specific focus for the telephone line and, accordingly, specific training and commitment to addressing aging and loneliness. Successful implementation requires a comprehensive understanding of all influencing factors as outlined in the CFIR domains (Figure 2). Interviews across stakeholders from the Institute on Aging's FL highlight that a clear vision and set of values are crucial for both staff and the people they serve. For example, if serving a unique population such as older adults, staff training and interests must reflect this focus. Structured training procedures are essential not only for the sustainability and satisfaction of callers and staff, but also to ensure that no harm is inadvertently caused to potentially vulnerable adults who are already suffering. Key elements for building dependability and trust, which are central to successful implementation, include meeting callers where they are, being nonjudgmental and not solution‐focused, and being available at any time—all of which require funding commitment to have stable staff and not solely volunteers (key takeaways in Table 4).

Staffing of telephone lines, like the FL, is critical with a combination of both paid and volunteer staff to ensure a large enough and diverse workforce who also have passion and commitment to their work in order to not leave the phones unanswered. This staffing need is reflected in the results discussing wait times and call length (Tables 1 and 2). The FL continuously evaluates how to staff to maintain the existing service and how to expand and scale in different ways to meet growing demands. Staff and clients frequently questioned call length, recognizing that the current time limits might not be sufficient (Table 3). Some staff and clients noted the possibility of increasing call frequency—up to three times a day—instead of extending call length. This approach allows for ongoing support while managing call volume.

Our findings indicate that clients experiencing the highest levels of loneliness still value and love the FL, suggesting the FL provides an essential outlet for companionship. Nevertheless, addressing chronic loneliness remains a significant challenge and can be emotionally taxing to staff. For those experiencing chronic loneliness and calling the FL daily, the service still offers critical support and connection directly by consistently being there for callers, and by offering referrals for those experiencing chronic and severe loneliness. Understanding the ideal call length and how to support the chronically lonely could be a valuable area for future research since longer calls may not necessarily increase efficacy.

The FL has endured for 50 years, suggesting that callers and the community value its existence. FL staff and volunteers also see the need and benefit of the FL, yet sustaining funding is one of the largest barriers to implementation and scaling. The FL continuously looks for ways to maintain and expand funding while ensuring the service remains free. This has meant adapting, growing, and securing larger contracts at the county and state level. Adapting has meant limiting calls per day to ensure access to a greater number of people, and adapting or improving training and the technology over time. The FL operates on a limited budget and, accordingly, is often unable to make investments necessary to scale. FL leaders note that with scale, the marginal cost of each call could be reduced, wait times could be shorter, services could be offered in additional languages, and promotion could be enhanced across the state as well as nationally. Without scale, there are limitations in each of those areas. Yet, ageism continues to be a prominent theme that many believed was a barrier to sustained funding and growth.

As demand surges, and without sufficient funding, telephone‐based lines run the risk of not being able to meet the needs of all. Loneliness is not a 9 a.m.‐to‐5 p.m. experience and, accordingly, other lines such as the United Kingdom's silver line have purposefully created a 24‐h service; therefore, telephone lines must be prepared to meet callers needs at best fully, and at worst, avoid causing harm by having calls answered by inexperienced, ill‐prepared, or unempathetic staff or volunteers. If trust or the needs of callers are not met, the intervention will fail and could worsen loneliness. Ultimately, the successful implementation of phone‐support lines requires an understanding of the values and culture of the organizations creating programs. Training in loneliness, suicide, and aging topics, as demonstrated by the FL, can also pave the way for readiness for future public health emergencies and meet the needs of aging callers.

This paper has strengths and limitations. While there are a few studies examining the role of telephone‐based programs to address loneliness and isolation—to our knowledge—ours is the first to use an implementation science framework to guide the evaluation of their success. This study's strength is also in studying one of the longest standing telephone support lines in the country, and the only one solely focused on older adults and with suicide accreditation. COVID‐19 proposed an opportunity and a challenge, as we evaluated the FL in the midst of large programmatic changes that were occurring to accommodate larger call numbers during the pandemic. This may have introduced some dissatisfaction among staff who wanted more training, as in‐person and in‐depth trainings had to be made shorter and remote and thus this may have skewed results. COVID‐19 also led to higher numbers of lonely adults, increasing the complexity and need for calls during key months of the evaluation and may have also been reflected in staff feeling the high intensity of calls. In addition, for those callers experiencing chronic loneliness and calling the FL daily, the service offers critical support and connection. By being a part of a larger organization serving older adults, the FL is also well‐positioned to connect the daily callers with other opportunities for social connection. Understanding the ideal call length and how to support the chronically lonely could be a valuable area for future research, as longer calls may not necessarily increase efficacy. Lastly, while we used purposeful sampling in our methods, our Spanish‐speaking callers interviewed were just short of 10% of the overall interviewers, and we would have preferred to have an over‐representation of non‐English speakers. As a result, our sample of older adult callers may not generalize to persons with more significant cognitive impairments or non‐English speakers, since the majority of services continue to be primarily in English.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, as health systems and policy makers at the local, state, and federal level look for population‐based solutions to a growing number of people with loneliness, and as individuals look for ways to address their own loneliness, well‐executed and well‐defined telephone support programs are a promising intervention. It would be naïve and incorrect to assume that this would solve loneliness for all, given the pathways to loneliness and the varying needs of diverse people. Surgeon General Vivek Murthy's May 2023 advisory confirms the multifaceted nature of loneliness, and more specifically social connection, and outlines the foundational pillars needed to address loneliness pathways. Yet, the FL remains a promising tool when implemented correctly and with attention to core domains. The FL is intentional in its approach to training and delivery of services, something felt by staff and callers alike. Developing and maintaining a telephone support line takes skill, knowledge, intent, and commitment. Lastly, while seemingly low‐cost, effective implementation of telephone programs cannot be successful solely with volunteers, and a stable and knowledgeable workforce is needed. Policy leaders should focus on the challenges and opportunities faced by the FL, including the need for consistent funding, appropriate call length, and expansion beyond California. The question of ideal call length warrants further research, as does how we carve out services for those with chronic and severe loneliness.

The bar for a successful telephone support line is higher than often assumed, and intentionality in program design and execution is crucial. To succeed, we must reconsider how we value human connection, especially with older adults. Acknowledging these strength and limitations could inform the sustainability and scaling of many telephone‐based interventions that began during the pandemic and continue to meet a pressing public health challenge.
