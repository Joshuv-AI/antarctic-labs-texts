Evaluation and comparison of marginal adaptation, colour stability and fracture resistance of provisional resin crowns fabricated with conventional, CAD-milled and 3D printed techniques- a prospective within-subject clinical study
Authors: Hasan Sarfaraz, Mallika Shetty, Sania Hussain, Rajesh Shetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProvisional restorations are critical in dental practice, serving multiple functions including preserving oral function and aesthetics, maintaining tooth position, and protecting dentin and pulp. Traditional materials like polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) have limitations such as heat generation, shrinkage, and colour instability. Advances in computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) and 3D printing offer potential improvements. Background

Traditional provisional materials, such as polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA), have long been used for their satisfactory aesthetics and function. However, PMMA has notable drawbacks, including heat generation during polymerisation, shrinkage, monomer residue, colour instability, and inferior marginal adaptations when fabricated through conventional methods [1, 2]. To overcome these limitations, newer materials like bis-acrylates have been introduced, offering alternatives that can be used via direct, indirect, or combined conventional methods [2]. Further advancements came with the advent of computer-aided designing and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technology in the 1980s, which revolutionised the fabrication process by enhancing efficiency and reducing chairside production time [3]. The CAD/CAM milling method, a subtractive process involving the carving of pre-polymerised resin blocks, has been widely adopted. However, this method may result in material waste, requires rigorous maintenance, and can have limitations in accurately reproducing curved surfaces due to cutter motion constraints [4]. To address these challenges, three-dimensional (3D) printing, an additive manufacturing process, has recently been introduced. This technology constructs restorations layer by layer, with each layer polymerised simultaneously and bonded to the previous one, offering new possibilities for the fabrication of provisional restorations.

More recently, three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has emerged as an additive manufacturing alternative for provisional crown fabrication. In this technique, restorations are fabricated layer by layer through photopolymerisation, potentially reducing material waste and enabling fabrication of complex geometries with greater efficiency.

Several in vitro studies have compared conventional, CAD/CAM-milled, and 3D-printed provisional restorations and reported differences in marginal adaptation, fracture resistance, and colour stability [5–11]. CAD/CAM-milled PMMA restorations have generally demonstrated superior marginal accuracy, fracture resistance, and colour stability compared with conventionally fabricated and 3D-printed restorations [9–11]. However, the majority of available evidence is derived from laboratory-based investigations conducted under controlled conditions that do not adequately reproduce the oral environment. Clinical factors such as saliva exposure, temperature fluctuations, occlusal loading, and intraoral ageing may significantly influence the performance of provisional materials.

Furthermore, there is a paucity of prospective clinical studies directly comparing self-cure PMMA, CAD/CAM-milled PMMA, and 3D-printed provisional crowns within the same participants under standardized clinical conditions [12–14]. In particular, limited evidence exists evaluating these fabrication techniques simultaneously for marginal adaptation, colour stability, and fracture resistance using a within-subject clinical design. This lack of clinical comparative data creates uncertainty regarding the most suitable provisional restoration material and fabrication method for routine clinical practice.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to compare and evaluate the marginal adaptation, colour stability, and fracture resistance of provisional crowns fabricated using self-cure PMMA, CAD/CAM-milled PMMA, and 3D-printed resin in endodontically treated posterior teeth using a prospective within-subject clinical design.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a prospective repeated-measures within-subject clinical study, in which each participant received provisional crowns fabricated using three different techniques: self-cure PMMA (DPI Self-Cure), CAD/CAM-milled PMMA (Ceramill PMMA, Amann-Girrbach, Austria), and 3D-printed resin (3D Accuprint Crown and Bridge, D-Tech, India). A within-subject design was selected to minimize inter-individual variability related to occlusion, oral environment, tooth morphology, and masticatory forces. All participants received the crowns in a fixed sequence (Self-cure PMMA → CAD/CAM-milled PMMA → 3D-printed resin). Randomization and allocation concealment and washout periods were not performed because repeated removal and replacement of provisional restorations on freshly prepared teeth could compromise pulpal and periodontal health and affect restoration retention. However, the absence of randomization may have introduced sequence effects, carryover effects, and time-dependent confounding, which are acknowledged as limitations of the study.

The 7-day clinical wear period for each provisional crown was selected to simulate the short-term duration commonly encountered in routine fixed prosthodontic practice between tooth preparation and definitive crown placement. This duration was considered adequate for evaluating early clinical performance parameters such as marginal adaptation, colour stability, and resistance to functional loading.

Ethical approval was secured from the Ethics Committee of the institution, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to ethical guidelines, prioritising participant safety and data confidentiality.

Study population and sampling

The target population comprised patients aged 18 years and above requiring provisional crowns for endodontically treated first or second premolars and first molars, with adjacent and opposing teeth present in either arch. The sample size was determined using G*Power software for repeated-measures ANOVA, considering an effect size of 0.5, a 5% level of significance, and 95% power, resulting in 22 participants per group (total n = 66). Participants were recruited using convenience sampling from a single clinical centre, which may introduce selection bias. Inclusion criteria specified endodontically treated premolars or molars, while exclusion criteria encompassed grossly decayed teeth requiring posts, fractured or mobile teeth, and medical conditions such as rheumatic heart diseases and neuromuscular or psychological disorders, along with non-cooperative patients. To minimize procedural variability, all tooth preparations were performed by a single operator following standardized preparation guidelines.

Data collection

Data collection employed multiple instruments and procedures. Intraoral scans were captured using a Trios 3Shape scanner (3Shape Trios 3, Denmark), with STL files utilised for digital crown design. Marginal adaptation was assessed via a Labomed Magna R600 digital microscope (Labomed Magna R600, USA) equipped with camera, while colour stability was measured using a clinical spectrophotometer (Vita easy shade, Germany) on the day of placement and seven days later. Fracture resistance was evaluated using a UTM (Universal testing machine) (TSI-BSD-20kN), India) after crown removal. Quality control was maintained through standardised protocols for tooth preparation, crown fabrication, and calibrated measurement tools. Standardization of seating pressure and luting procedure was maintained during crown cementation to minimize variability in cement thickness and seating discrepancies.The study spanned six months, with participants attending three appointments over three weeks for crown placement and evaluation.

Variables and measurements

Work flow

On the 1st appointment impression using elastomeric material was made for making index and tooth preparation was done (Fig. 1) and intraoral scanning was done followed by fabrication of cold cure provisional crown was made with self-cure PMMA using direct method and cemented using 3 M ESPE provisional cement. The STL files were used for designing crowns using Composer Software and printing resin model of prepared tooth. (Figures 2 and 3). Crowns were provided in the following order: Self-cure PMMA (Week 1), CAD/CAM-milled PMMA (Week 2), and 3D-printed resin (Week 3).

Tooth preparation

Occlusal reduction was at approx. 1.5 mm. Axial reduction approx. 1 mm. Supragingival margin with shoulder finish line on buccal side and chamfer finish line on lingual side was made.

The choice of different finish lines was intended to simulate clinical variability.

After luting, buccal and lingual margins were photographed at 2.5× magnification using a Dental Operating Microscope. Excess cement was carefully removed prior to imaging to ensure accurate marginal visualization. Images were analysed using Adobe Illustrator with standardized scaling using a ruler reference, and multiple measurements were recorded for each crown to minimize measurement error. Examiner calibration was performed prior to data collection. (Fig. 4) Although only buccal and lingual surfaces were assessed, these sites were selected because they provided the most reproducible visualization and accessibility for standardized imaging under clinical conditions. However, the limited number of measurement sites may not fully represent circumferential marginal integrity and is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Colour stability

The colour (ΔE values) of all three types of provisional crowns was measured using a clinical spectrophotometer on the day of placement and again on the seventh day. (Fig. 5) Lower ΔE values indicated superior colour stability and reduced perceptible colour change. The obtained ΔE values were interpreted according to clinically accepted perceptibility and acceptability thresholds.

Colour analysis using Spectrophotometer

Fracture resistance

On day 7, provisional crowns were removed, cleaned, and re-cemented onto standardized 3D-printed resin models using provisional cement prior to mechanical testing. Fracture resistance was tested using a UTM, applying a compressive load to occlusal surface via a 5 mm metallic rod at 1 mm/min cross-head speed. (Fig. 6)

Fracture resistance measurement using UTM

The use of standardized 3D-printed resin dies allowed uniformity during testing; however, the absence of periodontal ligament simulation, fatigue loading, and thermocycling limits direct extrapolation of the findings to intraoral clinical conditions. These limitations are acknowledged when interpreting the biomechanical relevance of the results.

Statistical analysis

Data were managed systematically, recorded in standardised forms, and entered into a spreadsheet. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Repeated-measures ANOVA was used to compare marginal adaptation, fracture resistance, and colour stability across the three fabrication techniques, as each participant received all three crowns. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons (Tukey test) were performed where significant differences were observed. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, applying a 5% level of significance (p < 0.05) to determine significant differences.

Results

Descriptive statistics for the three fabrication methods—3D printed, CAD milled, and Self cure—across four critical parameters are presented.

As shown in Table 1, for buccal marginal adaptation, CAD milled specimens demonstrated the lowest mean value (30.205 μm) with minimal variability (coefficient of variation [CV] = 0.147), indicating superior consistency. In contrast, Self Cure exhibited the highest mean buccal marginal gap (60.291 μm) and moderate variability (CV = 0.378), while 3D-printed specimens showed intermediate values (37.114 μm, CV = 0.278). For lingual marginal adaptation, CAD-milled again performed best (mean = 27.218 μm, CV = 0.554), though with higher variability compared to buccal adaptation. Self Cure displayed moderate lingual gaps (37.273 μm, CV = 0.539), while 3D Printed specimens showed slightly larger gaps (32.150 μm, CV = 0.345). In terms of fracture resistance, CAD milled materials significantly outperformed others, achieving the highest mean force (1059.605 N) with low variability (CV = 0.288). 3D printed specimens exhibited moderate fracture resistance (818.612 N, CV = 0.415), whereas Self Cure had the lowest resistance (737.645 N) coupled with high variability (CV = 0.548). For colour stability, Self Cure demonstrated the highest mean colour change (10.591 ΔE, CV = 0.397), followed by CAD Milled (9.721 ΔE, CV = 0.335) and 3D Printed (7.339 ΔE, CV = 0.358). Overall, CAD-milled materials emerged as the most reliable option, offering superior marginal adaptation and fracture resistance with consistent performance. Self-cure PMMA demonstrated the greatest colour change (ΔE = 10.591), indicating the lowest colour stability among the tested materials. In contrast, 3D-printed crowns showed the lowest ΔE values and therefore the best colour stability.

Table 2 shows the analysis of variance (ANOVA) conducted to evaluate the differences in performance metrics among the three fabrication methods. The analysis revealed significant differences across the methods for three of the four assessed parameters. For buccal marginal adaptation, substantial differences were observed between the fabrication techniques (F = 25.423, p < 0.001), indicating that the fabrication method significantly influences the precision of the buccal marginal fit. Similarly, fracture resistance exhibited significant variations among the methods (F = 4.975, p = 0.010), suggesting that the choice of fabrication technique impacts the mechanical durability of the provisional crowns. Colour stability was also found to be significantly affected by the fabrication method (F = 5.319, p = 0.007), with discernible differences in colour maintenance across the techniques. In contrast, lingual marginal adaptation did not demonstrate statistically significant differences between the methods (F = 2.212, p = 0.118), implying that the fabrication technique may have a less pronounced effect on the lingual marginal fit.

Discussion

This study set out to compare three provisional crown fabrication techniques, 3D printed, CAD-milled, and Self cure across four critical parameters: buccal and lingual marginal adaptation, fracture resistance, and colour stability. The findings revealed distinct performance profiles for each method, with CAD milled crowns demonstrating superior marginal adaptation on both buccal and lingual surfaces, coupled with the highest fracture resistance. Specifically, CAD milled specimens exhibited the lowest mean buccal marginal gap (30.205 μm) and lingual gap (27.218 μm), alongside the greatest mean fracture resistance (1059.605 N). Self-cure PMMA demonstrated the highest colour change (mean colour change = 10.591 ΔE), ), indicating lower colour stability compared with CAD-milled and 3D-printed crowns, they compromised on mechanical properties, displaying the largest buccal (60.291 μm) and lingual (37.273 μm) marginal gaps and the lowest fracture resistance (737.645 N). 3D-printed crowns offered a balanced performance, with intermediate values across most parameters but lagged in fracture resistance compared to CAD milled. These results underscore a trade-off between aesthetics and mechanical performance, suggesting that clinicians must weigh the priorities of marginal precision, durability, and colour maintenance when selecting a fabrication technique. The clinical relevance of these findings lies in guiding practitioners toward evidence-based decisions, particularly in scenarios where long-term provisional restorations are required or when treatment plans demand high precision and reliability.

The findings of this study regarding marginal adaptation align with and expand upon existing research in the field. Yılmaz et al. similarly found that subtractive (CAD/CAM) and additive (3D printing) techniques resulted in significantly smaller marginal gaps compared to conventional methods, highlighting the superiority of digital fabrication techniques in achieving precise marginal adaptation [15]. This consistency across studies underscores the reliability of digital methods in enhancing the marginal fit of provisional restorations. However, the results further specify that within digital techniques, CAD-milled specimens demonstrated superior marginal adaptation with minimal variability on both buccal and lingual surfaces, outperforming 3D-printed specimens. Such evidence adds a layer of nuance to the comparison, suggesting that while both digital methods surpass conventional techniques, CAD-milled may offer an edge in marginal precision.

Haggag et al.‘s evaluated vertical marginal gap (VMG) using a digital microscope, and found that 3D-printed crowns exhibited better VMG than milled crowns, which contrasts with the findings, of this study, where CAD-milled showed the lowest mean values [14]. This discrepancy might be attributed to differences in study methodologies, such as the use of typodont models versus clinical application in the present study. The systematic review by Al Wadei et al. concluded that 3D-printed provisional crowns have superior marginal fit compared to CAD/CAM-milled and conventional resins, which partially aligns with the present results where 3D-printed specimens showed intermediate values between CAD-milled and self cure [16]. However, he present study introduces the comparative aspect of fracture resistance alongside marginal adaptation, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of fabrication techniques. Overall, the body of evidence, in the study, collectively emphasises the significance of digital fabrication techniques in improving marginal adaptation. However, it also reveals that the choice between CAD-milled and 3D-printed may depend on specific clinical requirements and priorities, as trade-offs between marginal precision, mechanical properties, and other factors like colour stability must be considered.

The results of this study regarding fracture resistance align with previous research in the field by Bhambu et al. and Al-Wahadni et al. Both found that CAD/CAM milled provisional FDPs exhibited significantly better fracture resistance compared to 3D printed and conventional methods, which is consistent with the findings of the present study, where CAD milled materials demonstrated the highest fracture resistance [17, 18]. However, the study introduces a more detailed comparison by highlighting the superior performance of CAD-milled over Self -cure materials, with significant differences between these two groups. The results of Bhambu et al. and Al Fodah et al. showed no significant difference between CAD/CAM and 3D-printed groups, which contrasts with our findings where CAD-milled specimens had significantly higher fracture resistance than self-cure but not significantly different from 3D-printed. This discrepancy might be due to differences in sample size, testing methods, or materials used.

AhmadAbadi et al. emphasised that material properties might be more significant than manufacturing methods in determining fracture strength [19]. While the results of the present study show CAD milled materials performing best, we also note that the difference between CAD milled and 3D printed was not statistically significant. This supports the idea that material composition plays a crucial role alongside fabrication techniques. Our findings, along with those of Bhambu et al. and Al Fodah et al., collectively underscore the superior mechanical durability of CAD milled provisional restorations [17, 18]. However, the clinical relevance lies in recognising that while digital techniques offer significant advantages, material selection and specific clinical requirements must guide the choice between CAD milled and 3D printed options. Othman et al. found that milled temporary crowns exhibited higher fracture resistance than 3D printed ones for molars, but not for incisors [11]. This suggests that the choice between milled and printed restorations might also depend on the type of restoration and its functional demands. Overall, the evidence indicates that CAD milled restorations provide reliable fracture resistance, making them suitable for provisional use in situations requiring higher mechanical demands.

The findings of the present study regarding colour stability concur with and extend the existing body of research. Coutinho et al. found that Heat Cure PMMA exhibited the least colour change, followed by Protemp4, with LuxaCrown showing the highest discolouration [20]. Similarly, in the present study, CAD-milled and 3D-printed specimens demonstrated better colour stability than cold-cured ones, with 3D-printed specimens showing the lowest mean colour change. This evidence reinforces the notion that digital fabrication techniques can offer improved aesthetics over conventional methods. Sayed et al. highlighted that 3D-printed provisional restorations cemented with clear cement had better colour stability, particularly in the aesthetic zone [21]. While the present study did not evaluate cement types, the superior colour stability of 3D printed specimens aligns with the potential benefits of digital techniques in aesthetic outcomes.

Galbraith et al. reported that 3D-printed provisional crowns demonstrated the best colour stability, which is consistent with our results where 3D-printed specimens outperformed self cure. However, in contrast to Galbraith et al., the present study found that CAD milled specimens also performed well, with colour stability only slightly inferior to 3D printed (8). Meena et al. concluded that CAD-CAM milled materials are the best for longer-duration provisional restorations in aesthetic regions which aligns with the finding of the present [22]. Although CAD/CAM-milled materials showed favourable colour stability within the short observation period of this study, longer-term clinical investigations are necessary before confirming their suitability for extended provisionalisation in aesthetic regions. The variability in results across studies may be attributed to differences in materials, staining solutions, and experimental setups. Overall, the evidence suggests that digital fabrication methods, particularly 3D printing, offer advantages in colour stability, which is crucial for provisional restorations in the aesthetic zone. However, material-specific properties and clinical protocols, such as cement selection and staining resistance, must also be considered to optimise aesthetic outcomes.

The study offers valuable clinical insights into provisional crown performance, showing that CAD/CAM-milled and 3D-printed crowns provide better fit, durability, and colour stability than conventional Self-cure PMMA, making them reliable options for extended provisionalisation.

Few limitations of this study should be acknowledged:

Short intraoral observation period (7 days) for colour stability evaluation.

Fracture resistance testing conducted under in-vitro laboratory conditions.

Absence of thermocycling or fatigue loading simulation.

Convenience sampling from a single clinical centre.

Fixed crown placement sequence without randomization.

Limited marginal measurement sites.

Formal intra-examiner reliability testing was not performed.

Future studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up periods, and fatigue loading simulation are recommended to further evaluate the long-term clinical performance of these materials.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the clinical performance of provisional crowns fabricated using self-cure PMMA, CAD/CAM-milled PMMA, and 3D-printed resin.

CAD/CAM-milled crowns demonstrated favourable marginal adaptation and fracture resistance, while 3D-printed crowns showed comparatively better colour stability. Self-cure PMMA exhibited greater colour change and lower mechanical strength. However, within the limitations of this short-term study, no single material can be categorically recommended for all clinical situations. The selection of provisional crown fabrication technique should therefore be based on specific clinical requirements, including mechanical load, duration of provisionalisation, and aesthetic demands.
