Fatal and non-fatal health outcomes of malaria among reproductive age women in Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa, from 1990 to 2023

Background

Malaria is a life-threatening infection disease caused by Plasmodium protozoan parasites, which are transmitted to humans by the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes [1]. An estimated 263 million cases of malaria were reported worldwide in 2023, with an incidence of 60.4 cases per 1000 at-risk people. Approximately 597,000 people died from the disease in the same year [2]. The region of Africa continues to bear the largest burden of mortality, with 95% of the estimated fatalities and 94% of malaria cases [1, 3]. In regions where malaria transmission is widespread, pregnant women and young children are at increased risk of contracting the disease and dying from it [4]. Women of Reproductive Age (WRA), particularly during pregnancy, are more vulnerable to malaria, which increases the risk of severe anemia, maternal death, and unfavorable birth outcomes [5–7].

The global burden of malaria has significantly decreased since 2000 as a result of increased access to malaria prevention strategies, such as the use of preventative chemotherapy and efficient vector control [4]. Several strategies have been used to prevent and eradicate malaria throughout Africa, such as the distribution of insecticide-treated bed nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying, and the use of artemisinin-based combination therapy (ACT) [8–10]. Despite these measures, the malaria burden is still high [1]. Moreover, malaria prevalence varies among African nations. This may be influenced by local differences in the distribution of determinants of malaria like climate change, non-adherence to medical treatment, resistance to anti-malarial drugs, and socioeconomic challenges [11, 12]. According to a study done in Eastern Africa, floods and climate change are additional challenges that contribute to the region's higher malaria burden [13]. Furthermore, adherence to treatments is hindered by population expansion, socio-economic challenges, and political instabilities in the region [14].

The data on the prevalence and burden of malaria among WRA in the Eastern Sub-Saharan African (ESSA) region are limited. Efforts to control the disease are complicated by factors such as flooding, climate change, underreporting, unclear epidemiological patterns, inadequate data from remote locations, ineffective surveillance, and problems with treatment accessibility and price [15, 16]. The Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) study is a comprehensive research initiative that measures global health loss caused by various diseases, including malaria [17, 18]. The findings of this study would inform policymakers, public health professionals, and international health organizations about effective malaria control and elimination activities, resource allocation, and healthcare planning to improve health outcomes of malaria in the region.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the ESSA region, which refers to the GBD regional classification. This region includes Burundi, Comoros, Djibouti, Eritrea, Ethiopia, Kenya, Madagascar, Malawi, Mozambique, Rwanda, Somalia, South Sudan, Uganda, Tanzania, and Zambia [19].

Data sources

This study utilized secondary data from GBD 2023 study, which is a comprehensive and systematic analysis of malaria disease burden among WRA (15–49 years) in the ESSA nations from 1990 to 2023. The GBD 2023 study used different data sources like open-source databases, surveys, surveillance, vital registration, verbal autopsy, individual studies, censuses, reports, and others to capture the widest range of information. The study was fully compatible with the Guidelines for Accurate and Transparent Health Estimates Reporting (GATHER), ensuring the replication of its methods. The entire GBD time series is updated annually with new data sources and improved methods, and thus GBD 2023 findings replace all previous GBD findings. We extracted GBD 2023 estimates for malaria from the GBD results tool. A comprehensive description of the data sources, quality, and modeling for the GBD 2023 estimates was found at: https://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-2023/sources.

Data analysis

The Cause of Death Ensemble model (CODEm) was used to estimate the fatal health outcomes of malaria disease (i.e., deaths, and Years of Life Lost (YLLs)). To ensure internal consistency between all causes of death and cause-specific deaths, the causes of death corrected analyses were used. The non-fatal health outcomes of malaria disease (i.e., Years Lived with Disability (YLDs), prevalence, and incidence) were estimated by using Bayesian meta-regression for Disease modeling. The overall burden of malaria disease was measured by Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALYs), which was the sum of the YLDs and the YLLs. The 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UIs) were calculated using the lower 2.5th and upper 97.5th percentiles of the 1000 ordered values based on 1000 draws from the posterior distribution of each quantity of interest.

The overall increase or reduction in age-standardized rates over the entire study duration, 1990–2023, was calculated by comparing the values at the start (1990) and end (2023) of the period, using the following formula: Percentagechange=Value2023-Value1990Value1990x100.

Ethics

This study was carried out as part of the GBD Collaborator Network. It used secondary data from GBD 2023 study. Moreover, the estimates were aggregated at the population level so that individual level data were adequately anonymized to ensure confidentiality of information.

Results

Prevalence of malaria

In the ESSA region, the estimated prevalence rate of malaria was 7296 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 5691, 9079) among WRA in 2023. The highest prevalence rate was observed in Mozambique [19627 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 12,347, 27,601)], whereas the lowest prevalence rates were observed in Eritrea [842 case per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 450, 1430)], Rwanda [1603 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 587, 3180)], Ethiopia [2147 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 551, 4089)], Somalia [2484 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 1404, 4372)] and Kenya [2710 cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 1389, 5682)]. Between 1990 and 2023, the prevalence rate of malaria in the ESSA region decreased by 58%, with substantial reduction observed in Rwanda (−87%) and Kenya (−82%). On the other hand, there was a remarkable increase in the rate in Djibouti (211%) and Ethiopia (106%) (Table 1).

Incidence of malaria

In 2023, the estimated incidence rate of malaria was 9625 new cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 7198, 12,279) in the ESSA region. The estimated incidence rate in Mozambique [16076 new cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 11,890, 21,714)] was the highest in the region, whereas the incidence rate in Eritrea [2267 new cases per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 962, 4539)] was the lowest. Compared to the rate in 1990, the incidence rate was decreased by 23% in the region in 2023. A significant decline was observed in Rwanda (−68%) and Kenya (−58%). In contrast, the incidence rate increased in Djibouti (176%) and Ethiopia (130%) (Table 1, Fig. 1).

Trends of malaria incidence rates among reproductive-age women in the ESSA region, 1990 to 2023

Mortality due to malaria disease

In the ESSA region, the mortality rate due to malaria among WRA was 5 deaths per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 1, 12) in 2023. The highest rate was recorded in Mozambique [15 deaths per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 5, 35)]. In contrast, the lowest rate was estimated in Ethiopia [1 deaths per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 0, 3)] and Kenya [1 deaths per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 0, 2)]. Compared to the rate in 1990, there was about 72% decline in the rate in the region in 2023. Rwanda (−91%) had the highest reduction. However, there was an increasing net change in Ethiopia (21%) (Table 1, Fig. 2).

Trends of malaria mortality rates among reproductive-age women in the ESSA region, 1990 to 2023

Years of life lost from malaria

In 2023, the estimated YLLs rate of malaria was 303 YLLs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 84, 716) in the ESSA region. The highest YLLs rate was recorded in Mozambique [905 YLLs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 279, 2059)], whereas the lowest YLLs rate was estimated in Ethiopia [48 YLLs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 7, 116)]. There was about 73% decline in the overall change of the YLLs rate in the ESSA region between 1990 and 2023. Except in Ethiopia, notable reduction in the rate of YLLs was underscored across the whole nations (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Years lived with disability from malaria

In 2023, the estimated YLDs rate from malaria was 101 YLDs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 70, 146) in the ESSA region. The highest YLDs rate was recorded in Mozambique [270 YLDs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 160, 400)], whereas the lowest rate was recorded in Eritrea [31 YLDs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 21, 45)]. Between 1990 and 2023, the net change of the YLDs rate was substantially reduced by 51%. The highest reduction was observed in Kenya (−75%) and Rwanda (−73%), whereas the highest increment was estimated in Djibouti (59%) and Ethiopia (11%) (Table 2, Fig. 4).

Trends of YLDs rates from malaria among reproductive-age women in the ESSA region, 1990 to 2023

Disability-adjusted life years from malaria disease

In 2023, the estimated rate of DALYs from malaria disease among WRA in the ESSA region was 403 DALYs per 100,000 WRA (187, 819). The highest DALYs rate was recorded in Mozambique [1175 DALYs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 530, 2305)], whereas the lowest rate was estimated in Ethiopia [86 DALYs per 100,000 WRA (95% UI: 34, 162)]. Between 1990 and 2023, the net change of the DALYs rate declined by 69% in the region. Similarly, there was considerable reduction of net change of the rate across the whole nations of the region. However, the rate remains unchanged in Ethiopia (Table 2, Fig. 5).

Discussion

This study presents the fatal and non-fatal health outcomes of malaria among WRA in the ESSA region from 1990 to 2023, using data from the GBD 2023 study. The study indicated the highest prevalence and YLDs rates of malaria among WRA in Mozambique, while the lowest prevalence rate in Eritrea, Rwanda, Ethiopia, Somalia, and Kenya. Low incidence and DALYs rates were observed in Eritrea and Ethiopia, respectively. Compared to the rates in 1990, there were substantial reductions in the non-fatal and fatal health outcomes of malaria in the region in 2023. However, the rates of non-fatal outcomes increased in Djibouti and Ethiopia.

Malaria incidence rate was estimated to be the highest in Mozambique among WRA. This may be associated with favorable environmental and climatic conditions like warm temperatures, vegetation coverage, and high humidity. All these conditions are conducive to mosquito breeding and parasite development [20]. Additionally, inadequate health care infrastructure, limited access to prevention measures, overburdened case management capacities, and the shortage of professionally trained health workers could also exacerbate malaria transmission in the country [21, 22]. On the other hand, the incidence rate in Eritrea was low. These could be explained by implementing integrated malaria control strategies like distributing ITNs, indoor residual sprays, and larval source management [10, 23, 24]. In addition to these interventions, research on insecticide and drug efficacy, regular surveillance, and strong health system linkage might result in low malaria incidence and put the country on a sustained path towards malaria pre-elimination [25].

Similarly, the lowest DALYs rate was recorded in Ethiopia, which may reflect the success of community-based health interventions that are based on increased utilization of the rapid diagnostic test, the health extension program, and extensive distribution of ITNs [4, 10, 26]. Although most individual countries have made substantial reduction, the overall change in the rates of prevalence, incidence, and YLDs from malaria among WRA increased in Djibouti and Ethiopia in 2023 compared to the rates in 1990. The cross-country variations observed may be linked with variations in ecological and climatic conditions, funding, insecticide resistance, and programmatic challenges [27–30].

The lowest prevalence rate of malaria among WRA was estimated in Eritrea, Rwanda, Somalia, and Kenya, which may reflect the effectiveness of current interventions. However, the highest burden of malaria in nations like Mozambique and the increased net change in Djibouti and Ethiopia need the implementation of targeted public health interventions and improved access to malaria control approaches. Based on the observed patterns, governments may consider strengthening malaria control efforts in high-risk areas and groups through ATC, improved access to ITNs, and integrated reproductive health services. Environmental and climatic factors, such as changing rainfall patterns and flooding, should also be incorporated into malaria control approaches through intersectoral collaboration and climate-resilient health planning. Additionally, community participation, strengthening surveillance, and sustainable financial resources are crucial to maintaining progress and achieving the target of ending malaria epidemics by 2030 [1, 14].

This study is subject to different limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The GBD study relies on secondary data for analyses, which may either underestimate or overestimate the actual disease burden across the ESSA countries. In some countries, specifically those affected by conflict, displacement, or weak health infrastructure, malaria incidence and mortality may be underestimated because of limited diagnostic capacity and incomplete surveillance. Moreover, variation in data sources and estimation models across the ESSA countries may introduce uncertainty into the regional comparison. Regardless of these limitations, GBD methodology provides a strong and standardized structure that allows significant assessment of long-term patterns in malaria burden among WRA throughout the ESSA region.

Conclusion

This study has revealed that there was substantial reduction in the rates of non-fatal and fatal health outcomes of malaria among WRA in the ESSA region in 2023 compared to 1990. However, the rates remain high and heterogeneous across the region. Therefore, strengthening the health service system and sustaining strong malaria control programs across the countries are crucial to end malaria epidemics by 2030.
