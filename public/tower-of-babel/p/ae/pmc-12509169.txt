Living bacteria-loaded antibacterial hydrogel-coated gauze: A revolutionary solution for multi-drug-resistant bacterial infections
Authors: Minghui Xie, Yang Xue, Wantong Lin, Zibing Jiang, Yichun Chen, Yue Pei, Xu Wu, Xiubin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic wounds infected by methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) pose critical therapeutic challenges due to the prevalence of multi-drug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. To address this issue, a biocompatible live bacteria-loaded hydrogel-coated gauze (APAG) was developed by integrating an engineering Pseudomonas sp. SC11pLAFR-GFP with an alginate/ε-polylysine-polyacrylamide (Alg/ε-PL-PAAm) double-network hydrogel. The hierarchical hydrogel architecture, achieved through physical entrapment in the Alg/ε-PL core, ensures bacterial viability, while the chemically crosslinked PAAm outer layer regulates metabolite diffusion kinetics, synergistically enhancing therapeutic efficacy. The engineered bacteria hydrogel gauze (SC11@APAG) demonstrated sustained antimicrobial production for more than 48 h through the metabolic activity of the loaded Pseudomonas sp. SC11. This biohybrid not only effectively combats MRSA infection in mice wounds but also enhances wound healing. This “bacterial antagonism” strategy combines the native antimicrobial properties of Pseudomonas sp. with advanced material engineering, establishing a new paradigm for intelligent biomedical textiles in combating antimicrobial resistance.

Introduction

Multi-drug-resistant (MDR) bacterial infections pose a grave threat to public health [1], as bacteria rapidly evolve to develop resistance to commonly prescribed medications [2]. Forecasts indicate that by 2050, MDR infections could lead to the loss of up to 10 million lives annually, outstripping the fatalities attributed to cancer [3]. Notably, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA), which emerged 60 years ago, has rapidly spread worldwide [4]. Such MDR infection complicates wound healing as the emergence of MDR bacteria reduces the effectiveness of antibiotics, preventing them from eliminating bacteria at the wound sites [5]. Consequently, patients suffer from prolonged wound infections and considerable pain [6]. Therefore, it is crucial to explore an effective and innovative strategy to combat MDR infections.

To effectively combat bacterial infections, various treatment methods have been developed, such as nano-therapy [7], photothermal therapy [8], ultrasound therapy [9], and bacteriophage therapy [10]. However, these methods are often complex, necessitating specialized equipment and incurring high costs, which pose significant barriers to the current therapeutic approaches. Therefore, there is a strong preference for simple and effective treatments in wound treatment. Recently, living bacteria-loaded delivery systems have garnered significant interest in the treatment of various diseases, including cancer, infectious diseases, inflammatory bowel disease, allergies, and respiratory infections [11]. These systems encapsulate living bacteria within hydrogels, allowing the bacteria to promote cell growth [12] and secrete beneficial active components [13]. Additionally, hydrogels utilized as wound dressings can shield the wound site from external contaminants, thereby preventing pathogen interactions and serving as a local reservoir for transdermal drug delivery [14] and infection treatment b), [15]. Among them, Pseudomonas sp. occupies a pivotal position in agricultural soil and the rhizosphere of plants [16], where it effectively competes with deleterious microorganisms such as fungi and bacteria [17]. This species possesses the ability to release a complex fermentation broth and exhibits many antibacterial mechanisms [18]. Especially, Pseudomonas sp. can rapidly produce a significant quantity of siderophores, fungal cell wall-degrading enzymes, and a variety of secondary metabolites (SM), including phenazine, lipopeptides, and koreenceine [19]. These SMs exhibit potent antibacterial activity against a wide range of bacteria and fungi. Furthermore, SMs can serve as novel antimicrobial agents, functioning similarly to antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents, and accelerate the healing process of wounds infected by bacteria [20]. Therefore, developing living hydrogels combined with SMs is a promising strategy for the treatment of MDR infections.

Inspired by the idea of “utilizing bacteria to combat bacteria”, and building upon the widely used traditional gauze, herein, a biocompatible gauze coated with engineered living hydrogels was constructed by integrating with Pseudomonas sp. SC11pLAFR-GFP (SC11). The hierarchical hydrogel coating could sustain the activity of SC11 while preventing the leakage of bacteria from the hydrogel. At the same time, the antibiotics produced by the loaded SC11 could be used for the treatment of chronic wounds infected with MRSA. Initially, the engineered bacterial strain Pseudomonas sp. SC11pLAFR-GFP (SC11) was constructed by incorporating a plasmid that expresses the green fluorescent protein (GFP) into the wild-type strain. This GFP served as a visible marker to facilitate the observation of SC11's status. Next, SC11 was dispersed within an alginate (Alg) solution and coated onto a gauze through a simple immersion strategy. Subsequently, the coated gauze was immersed in ε-poly(L-lysine) (ε-PL) solution to form physically crosslinked living coacervation hydrogels due to the coacervation of oppositely charged polyelectrolytes [21]. The Alg/ε-PL hydrogel (APG) layer could be formed within seconds by self-assembly without disturbing the bioactivity of encapsulated SC11. Thus, SC11 could grow and execute an antibacterial function in this protective hydrogel network. Moreover, the porous structure of the hydrogel network could facilitate the release of metabolites produced by SC11. However, SC11 could biodegrade the polysaccharides or break the electrostatic interaction between Alg and ε-PL and thus leak out from the gauze. Therefore, an outer layer of chemically crosslinked polyacrylamide (PAAm) hydrogel layer was further formed, serving as a stable protective barrier against the surroundings and the leakage of the SC11, while ensuring good permeability for small molecules via suitable crosslinking density [22]. As a result, a biocompatible engineered living gauze dressing, featuring SC11 encapsulated in Alg/PL-polyacrylamide hydrogel (SC11@APAG) was developed, as shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. S1. The results revealed that this gauze exhibited excellent antibacterial properties and good biocompatibility, making it highly effective for treating MDR bacteria-infected wounds with irregular shapes. It also possessed good air permeability and blood absorption capabilities. This work significantly broadens the potential applications of traditional gauze dressings, paving new avenues in the design of antibacterial materials, and enhancing the treatment options of MDR-infections.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the preparation of SC11@APAG gauze dressing and its application for the MRSA treatment.Fig. 1

Results and discussion

Firstly, the Alg/PL-polyacrylamide hydrogel-coated gauze encapsulating SC11 bacteria (SC11@APAG) was prepared and evaluated to confirm its suitability for wound application, such as its mechanical properties, flexibility, air permeability, etc. Fourier transform infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis of Alg/ε-PL and Alg/ε-PL-PAAm composites showed characteristic peak shifts: the broad O-H/N-H stretching peak at 3300 cm−1 widened upon PAAm addition, indicating enhanced hydrogen bonding, while the intensified amide I band at 1640 cm−1 confirmed successful composite formation through C=O interactions (Fig. S2). Thermal analysis (DSC-DTG) showed SC11@APAG had more complex, multi-stage decomposition than ordinary gauze (Fig. S3, Fig. S4), also confirming successful synthesis of the Alg/ε-PL-PAAm hydrogel composite. The mechanical properties of the gauze were evaluated via tensile measurement. As shown in Fig. 2a, the stress of SC11@APAG gauze increased with increasing strain and reached a peak of 100 MPa at ∼40 % strain, lower than that of the ordinary gauze with the highest stress of 120 MPa. The Young's modulus of SC11@APAG was 360 MPa, which was also lower than that of ordinary gauze with 534 MPa (Fig. 2b), which might be due to the hydrogel coating reducing the gauze's dense structure. Thus, this coated gauze maintained excellent flexibility, allowing it to be easily bent into a tubular shape (Fig. 2c) and withstand substantial deformation without fracturing. The rheological behaviors revealed that the storage modulus (G′) of the SC11@APAG gauze was lower than that of the ordinary gauze, confirming the good flexibility of the coated gauze (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the storage modulus (G′) exceeding the loss modulus (G″) across a frequency range of 0.1–100 rad s−1 indicated that the hydrogel could maintain its functionality over a broad frequency range when applied to the tissue surface. The strain sweep results (Fig. S5a) demonstrated that SC11@APAG retained a linear viscoelastic region far broader than conventional gauze, evidencing superior deformation resistance. Furthermore, structural recovery tests (Fig. S5b) revealed that SC11@APAG exhibited significant self-recovery capability compared to conventional gauze. The utilization of gauze is significantly advantageous due to its excellent air permeability, which promotes air circulation [23]. This circulation is crucial for maintaining an appropriate level of humidity in the wound area, thereby preventing excessive moisture accumulation [24]. Furthermore, the high air permeability helps inhibit the growth of bacteria and fungi, thus reducing the risk of wound infection [25]. As shown in Fig. 2e, the hydrogel coating did not impede the pore structure of the APAG gauze, thereby preserving its excellent air permeability, akin to that of the ordinary gauze. Thus, both gauzes exhibited similar water vapor permeability characteristics (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, the APAG gauze could exhibit commendable water absorption, achieving an equilibrium swelling ratio of 200 wt% to maintain an appropriate level of moisture for the wound (Fig. 2g), as well as 60 wt% in the simulated wound exudate (pH 5–6, proteases) (Fig. S6). The capacity for liquid absorption of the hydrogel dressing was also a crucial parameter in wound care to maintain optimal moisture levels for the wound while preventing excessive dampness [26]. The swelling ratio of SC11@APAG was lower than that of the ordinary gauze, owing to the modification of the gauze's surface structure by the hydrogel coating.

However, it is noted that the swelling ratio was suitable for the hydrogel coating to avoid the reduced robustness due to high swelling ratios [27], and also achieves optimal hemostatic performance by modulating the material's surface properties. Mechanistically, the lower swelling ratio creates microporous structures and surface charges that selectively retain coagulation factors while limiting blood absorption [28]. As shown in Fig. 2h, compared to conventional gauze with high absorption ratios and rapid blood uptake characteristics, the SC11@APAG gauze demonstrates controlled absorption kinetics. This is attributed to the hydrogel coating impeding the blood penetration into the gauze (Fig. 2i), resulting in a slower blood diffusion rate for SC11@APAG gauze compared to the ordinary gauze. From a hydrodynamic perspective, the microenvironment created by the coating reduces blood diffusion rates while maintaining local thrombin concentration, creating favorable conditions for platelet activation and fibrin formation [29]. These results indicated that the SC11@APAG gauze could effectively reduce the rapid blood loss of the patients. Thus, based on the advantages of the hydrogel coating gauze and its suitability for wound application.

The biological characteristics of strain SC11 presented in Fig. S7 demonstrate rapid proliferation capacity by completing both logarithmic growth and stationary phases within 24 h. Moreover, these SC11 could be effectively adsorbed onto the gauze fiber (Fig. 3c–d, Fig. S8), while retaining their regular shape and viability, as evidenced by the strong green fluorescence. Due to this effective attachment, the SC11 bacteria were successfully encapsulated within the hydrogel to construct the SC11@APAG gauze. Fig. 3e–f revealed that the bacteria were completely encapsulated in the hydrogel, and the resulting hydrogel gauze retained a well-defined porous structure to facilitate good air permeability for wound healing. The cultivation results after 72 h of incubation demonstrated that both the nutritional broth medium and agar plate containing SC11@APAG gauze maintained excellent cleanliness throughout the observation period, showing no discernible alterations in clarity or color (Fig. 3g–h). This prolonged stability confirmed the absence of bacterial leakage from the gauze. Conversely, within the same 72-h period, the medium and plate containing SC11@APG gauze without the PAAm hydrogel layer exhibited turbidity and were bordered by a yellow bacterial layer, respectively. These results confirmed that SC11 could degrade the polysaccharide hydrogel layer for escape, and the chemically crosslinked PAAm hydrogel effectively hindered this escape behavior. For 12 days, no bacterial leakage was detected at any time point (Fig. S9). Furthermore, as shown in Fig. S10, the SC11@APAG dressing retained potent antibacterial activity after 7 days and 14 days of refrigerated storage (4 °C), with no significant decrease in inhibition zone diameter. For biomedical applications, good hemocompatibility and cytocompatibility are of great importance [30]. Alg [31]and ε-PL [32] have been demonstrated to exhibit excellent biocompatibility, making them suitable candidates for wound dressing materials. Additionally, PAAm is widely recognized for its biosafety and is extensively used in wound dressings due to its non-toxicity, high toughness, and ability to maintain a moist wound environment [33]. As shown in Fig. 4a, no obvious hemolysis was observed for ordinary gauze, APAG, and SC11@APAG, respectively. The UV–Vis absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm further confirmed that the hemolysis ratios of ordinary gauze, APAG gauze, and SC11@APAG gauze were lower than 3 %, indicating the negligible hemolytic effect (Fig. 4b) [34]. Fig. 4d showed that the blood red cells treated with the PBS solution as a control, ordinary gauze, and the SC11@APAG gauze maintained their regular shape, confirming the excellent hemocompatibility of the SC11@APAG gauze. The cell compatibility of the ordinary gauze, APAG gauze, and SC11@APAG gauze was also evaluated through a direct contact experiment using L929 cells as a cell model, and the results showed that the L929 cell viability of all samples was similar and higher than 96 % after incubation for 24 h (Fig. 4c). Our genomic analysis revealed that strain SC11 harbors a triazine natural product toxoflavin biosynthetic gene cluster (BGC) (SC11_3560∼SC11_3569) (Fig. S11 and Table S1), which is homologous to the tox operon in Pseudomonas protegens Pf-5 [35]. The toxicity of toxoflavin is attributed to its redox properties: it acts as an electron carrier that bypasses the cytochrome system, resulting in hydrogen peroxide production. This peroxide-generating capacity explains its strong antibiotic activity [36]. Therefore, the antibacterial activity of the SC11 was evaluated via in vitro antimicrobial tests. As shown in Fig. 5a, an inhibition zone of SC11 against E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA was observed, with a diameter of 13.85 ± 0.66, 14.04 ± 1.04, and 12.01 ± 0.31, respectively (Fig. 5b). This result confirmed that SC11 has excellent antibacterial ability against Gram-positive bacteria and Gram-negative bacteria. Therefore, the SC11@APAG gauze also exhibited obvious inhibition zones on the agar plate after 24 h, while the ordinary gauze had no inhibition zone (Fig. 5a). The zone diameter for the SC11@APAG group was 25.11 ± 0.56, 24.49 ± 0.39 and 25.46 ± 0.36 mm, respectively (Fig. 5b). To validate the role of toxoflavin, we constructed a tox knockout mutant, which exhibited a complete loss of antibacterial activity against MRSA (Fig. S12). Consistently, the mutant strain-loaded gauze also failed to inhibit MRSA (Fig. S13), demonstrating that toxoflavin was mainly responsible for the antibacterial mechanism of strain SC11, as well as other possible synergistic effects of metabolites, by some literature reported that certain strains may produce antibacterial substances such as siderophores, cell wall-degrading enzymes, phenazines, and cyclic lipopeptides [19,37] and the genomic analysis results (antiSMASH bacteria version 7.1, Table S2).

The antibacterial activity against E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA of SC11@APAG was further confirmed and qualitatively evaluated through the colony-forming unit (CFU). As shown in Fig. 5c and e, the ordinary gauze in the control group exhibited obvious bacterial contamination on the agar plate after 24 h. In contrast, the number of bacterial colonies treated with SC11@APAG gauze decreased significantly with the increase in contact time, and the inhibition ratio reached 50 % in 2 h and ∼100 % in 24 h.

The antibacterial efficacy of the SC11@APAG gauze was further confirmed through a live/dead staining assay. Following contact with SC11@APAG, E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA bacteria were dyed using a mixed solution of SYTO-9 and propidium iodide (PI) [38]. As a control, bacteria treated with ordinary gauze were also included in the assay. As shown in Fig. 6a–f, the bacteria exposed to the ordinary gauze displayed intense green fluorescence with minimal red fluorescence, suggesting the high viability of the bacteria. Conversely, bacteria treated with SC11@APAG showed significant red fluorescence, indicating that the majority of the bacteria were effectively killed [38,39]. The killing efficacy was also confirmed by the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images (Fig. 6g–l). The E. coli, S. aureus, and MRSA treated with ordinary gauze exhibited typical morphological features with smooth surfaces, indicating their healthy state. However, the bacteria treated with SC11@APAG gauze exhibited irregular shapes, porous structures, and rough surfaces, accompanied by significant shrinkage and deformation of the cell membranes. SC11@APAG was evaluated alongside six commercial dressings (Aomi Medical Cellulose, MELOLIN, ConvaTec Silver, Bactroban Mupirocin, Elastoplast Expert, 3M Chlorhexidine) and ordinary cotton gauze (Fig. S14). In the inhibition-zone assay, the dressing produced a clearance zone of 25.5 ± 0.4 mm against MRSA, smaller than the zones generated by mupirocin (33.2 ± 0.6 mm) and chlorhexidine (34.0 ± 0.1 mm), but significantly larger than those of all non-medicated and silver-containing alternatives. During 24-h contact testing, SC11@APAG achieved complete bacterial eradication, matching the performance of mupirocin, silver, and chlorhexidine products and markedly surpassing every non-antimicrobial dressing (Fig. S15). This behavior reflects differing modes of action: mupirocin relies on rapid antibiotic diffusion to create wide inhibition rings, chlorhexidine delivers broad-spectrum activity through its cationic polymer structure, whereas SC11@APAG continuously produces antimicrobial agents in situ during bacterial contact, yielding a sustained and thorough sterilization effect under direct-touch conditions. To evaluate the allergenic potential of SC11@APAG, a sensitization test was performed on a mouse dorsal skin model, and the results indicated that no evidence of allergic reaction, such as erythema, edema, or itching, was observed over a 48-h period for SC11@APAG (Fig. 7b), demonstrating its excellent biocompatibility and low allergenic risk. This highlights its safety for biomedical applications requiring minimal allergic responses. Therefore, the antibacterial and wound-healing properties of SC11@APAG were further evaluated by establishing a severe MRSA-infected wound model, as shown in Fig. 7a. After distinct therapeutic interventions were implemented, the gauze was replaced multiple times on the first day, followed by a single replacement on the 3rd and 5th days, respectively. As shown in Fig. 7c, turbid pus in the wound was observed due to the mice's wounds developing severe infection. Thereafter, wounds treated with SC11@APAG exhibited reduced inflammation by day 2 and showed significant drying and scabbing by day 7. In contrast, the control group still displayed severe inflammation with white pus accumulation at the wound site by day 7. With the treatment of SC11@APAG, the wound healing process in mice progressed smoothly from day 5 to day 14, with a significant reduction in wound area. By day 14, the SC11@APAG-treated wounds were almost completely healed, with a wound healing rate of 94.3 %, while the control group still had a larger wound area with a healing rate of only 74.5 % and exhibited significant white inflammatory exudate (Fig. 7d). To validate the in vivo antibacterial efficacy, wound samples were collected on day 3, and lysogenic broth (LB) agar plate counting was performed to detect residual MRSA. Consistent with the in vitro results, SC11@APAG exhibited excellent antibacterial activity, as evidenced by the near absence of bacterial colonies in the SC11@APAG group, in contrast to the numerous colonies observed in the control group (Fig. 7f). Furthermore, a quantitative analysis of MRSA in wound exudates on day 3 demonstrated a substantial decrease in bacterial load in the SC11@APAG gauze group compared to the control group: control group (106 CFU) > SC11@APAG group (≈0 CFU) (n = 3, ***p < 0.001) (Fig. 7e). Histological analysis of wound tissues on day 14 using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining provided additional insights into the healing process. The control group exhibited blurred and fragmented collagen fibers in the dermis, along with a significant number of inflammatory cells. This indicates persistent inflammation in the subcutaneous tissue despite the reduction in wound size from day 7 to day 14. In contrast, the SC11@APAG group showed significantly fewer inflammatory cells, which had almost disappeared by day 14. Additionally, vascular hyperplasia and the presence of hair follicles and sebaceous glands were observed in the SC11@APAG group (Fig. 7g). These in vitro and in vivo findings highlight the potential of SC11@APAG hydrogel gauze in treating MRSA-infected wounds. The results demonstrate its superior antibacterial efficacy, reduced inflammatory response, and enhanced wound healing capabilities compared to conventional medical gauze, making it a promising candidate for managing drug-resistant bacterial infections in clinical settings.

Conclusion

In this study, we fabricated an advanced hydrogel consisting of biocompatible alginate (Alg) and ε-poly (L-lysine) (ε-PL) hydrogel loaded with SC11 and polyacrylamide hydrogel (PAAm) with excellent mechanical properties. This hydrogel encapsulation strategy (APAG) successfully maintained the activity of SC11 and prevented its escape to the wound environment. The air permeability of the gauze and the moisture retention and biocompatibility of the hydrogel make SC11@APAG hydrogel gauze a skin-friendly wound dress. In addition, the good absorption of the SC11@APAG gauze enables it to absorb wound exudates and keep the wound dry. The hydrogel gauze has been demonstrated to have excellent antibacterial properties on Gram-positive bacteria, Gram-negative bacteria, and especially drug-resistant bacteria, MRSA. Furthermore, the in vivo study demonstrated the effectiveness of SC11@APAG gauze in treating MRSA-infected wounds. Overall, the findings of this study provide a promising strategy for the treatment of drug-resistant bacterial infections and the development of new antimicrobial materials.

Experimental section

Materials

The following chemical reagents were commercially obtained: Sodium alginate (Alg, CAS No. 9005-38-3), ε-Poly-L-lysine (CAS No. 28211-04-3), acrylamide (CAS No. 79-06-1), tetramethylethylenediamine (TEMED, CAS No. 110-18-9), and trypsin (CAS No. 9002-07-7) were purchased from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ammonium persulfate (APS, CAS No. 7727-54-0) was acquired from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Conventional medical gauze bandages were procured from Winner Medical Co., Ltd. Sterilized deionized water was used in all experiments. Pseudomonas sp. SC11pLAFR-GFP (SC11) was isolated from the laboratory of a professor in South China University of Technology.

Preparation of Pseudomonas sp. SC11pLAFR-GFP (SC11)

The Pseudomonas fluorescens SC11pLAFR-GFP(SC11) strain was constructed by introducing a plasmid expressing the GFP gene into the wild-type strain. Before inoculation, SC11 was stored at −80 °C in solubility media (LB) containing 20 % (v/v) glycerol. To revive SC11, single colonies were streaked onto LB solid plates and incubated at 30 °C for 16 h. After that, single colonies were scraped into King's B liquid medium and cultured at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm until an optical density (OD600) of 1.2 was reached. The composition of the King's B medium (g/L) was: peptone, 20 g; glycerol, 15.15 g; KH2PO4, 0.514 g; MgSO4, 0.732 g. The bacterial solution was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cells were washed twice with sterile PBS buffer and resuspended in an appropriate amount of PBS, and the bacterial concentration was adjusted to 1 × 10 8 CFU/mL.

Preparation of SC11@APAG gauze

1 g sodium alginate was dissolved in 10 g sterile deionized water, added magnetic stirrer, and dissolved overnight under magnetic stirring. 1 g ε-poly(L-lysine) (ε-PL) was dissolved in 10 g sterile deionized water. 4 g acrylamide monomer, 0.032 g MBAA, and 0.016 g APS were dissolved in 6 g sterile deionized water. The cut gauze needs to be sterilized in advance using ultraviolet light. After preparing all solutions, SC11 was mixed with sodium alginate solution at a volume ratio of 1:10, and sterilized gauze was immersed in the mixture for 10 min. Excess solution was removed using sterile absorbent paper, and the gauze was then immersed in PL solution for 1 min to form the first hydrogel layer (SC11@APG) via ion crosslinking, immobilizing SC11 within the hydrogel network. Next, 10 μL of TEMED was added to the AAm precursor solution and waited for 30 s. Before gelation, SC11@APG was immersed in the solution for 10 s. After taking out, the excess solution, with absorbent paper, forming SC11@APAG. Finally, the product was washed multiple times with sterile deionized water to remove unreacted monomers, as detected by the UV spectroscopy (Fig. S16). The prepared SC11@APAG can be stored in a refrigerator at 4 °C.

Characterization

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, PerkinElmer, USA) was employed for chemical composition analysis. Tensile properties were evaluated using an HX-1007 universal material testing machine (LiXian Instruments Co., Ltd., Dongguan, China). Rheological properties of the gauze were measured using a rheometer (HAAKE MARS 40 and 60, Thermo Scientific, Germany). The morphology of SC11 was observed using an inverted fluorescence microscope (EVOS FL Auto, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS SUPRA 55, Carl Zeiss, Germany). Ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectroscopy was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (UV-2700, Shimadzu, Japan). All animal experiments were carried out in accordance with the regulations on animal experiments of the Shiyanjia Lab (Zhejiang, China) and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shiyanjia Lab (protocol no. YXSW2112095904 and YXSW2404120370) and Guangzhou University [protocol no. (2023)118].

Breathability test

The water vapor transmission ratio (WVTR) method was employed to evaluate the material's breathability. The specific procedure was as follows: the prepared hydrogel gauze (experimental group) and traditional medical gauze (control group) were separately sealed over the mouths of glass bottles (25 mm diameter) containing 50 mL of deionized water, which were then placed on a DF-101S constant-temperature heating magnetic stirrer (Shanghai Lichenbangxi) with the water temperature maintained at (100 ± 0.5) °C for continuous boiling. An electronic balance (JA1203, Shanghai Liangping) was used to weigh the remaining water mass in the bottles every 30 s, with continuous measurements taken for 12 min. The WVTR was calculated using the following formula:WVTR(g·m−2·h−1)=(m0−mt)/(A·t),where m0 is the initial mass of the water bottle, mt is the mass after time t, A is the moisture-permeable area of the material, and t is the testing time.WVTR(%)=(WVTRwithoutmaterial/WVTRwithoutmaterial)×100%

Swelling capacity test

The SC11@APC hydrogel gauze (n = 3) was dried and weighed (W0), then immersed in deionized water or simulated wound exudate (pH 5–6, 8.3 g/L NaCl and 0.37 g/L CaCl2, 1 mg/mL trypsin) for 1.0 h. After removal, surface liquid was blotted, and the sample was reweighed (Wt). Tests were triplicated to ensure reproducibility. The swelling ratio was calculated as:Swellingratio(%)=[(Wt−W0)/W0]×100%

Blood absorption capacity test

The blood absorption performance of the gauze was quantitatively characterized using the gravimetric method. An electronic analytical balance was employed to measure the mass change of samples immersed in fresh rabbit blood (EDTA-anticoagulated). For testing, gauze samples (n = 3) were evenly placed on the testing platform, and 200 μL of fresh rabbit blood pre-warmed to 37 °C was pipetted onto the samples. The wet weight change (Wg) of the gauze was recorded over 1 h, and the absorption ratio at each time point was calculated using the following formula:Absorptionratio(%)=[(Wg–W0)/W0]×100%

Encapsulation performance test

The encapsulation performance test was conducted by incubating 1 cm × 1 cm samples of SC11@APAG dressing (experimental group) and single-layer alginate hydrogel SC11@APG gauze without PAAm encapsulation (control group) in 10 mL LB liquid medium at 37 °C with 180 rpm shaking for 72 h while monitoring turbidity changes at 12-h intervals, with parallel experiments performed by placing gauze samples at the center of fresh LB agar plates for static 37 °C incubation over 72 h followed by bacterial growth observation, using triplicate samples for all experimental conditions.

The long-term encapsulation performance was evaluated by immersing SC11@APAG gauze (2 cm × 2 cm) in 5 mL of PBS buffer at 4 °C. Every 24 hours, a 50 μL of 100-fold diluted soaking solution was plated on agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours to monitor the bacterial leakage of SC11@APAG gauze. The experiment lasted for 12 days and five samples were used for parallel experiments for all experimental conditions.

Hemocompatibility test

The hemolysis test was conducted using deionized water as the positive control (PC) and normal saline as the negative control (NC). Anticoagulated rat blood was centrifuged (1000 rpm, 10 min), and 0.2 mL of the red blood cell (RBC) precipitate was mixed with 1.0 mL of normal saline. After centrifugation (1000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was removed, and 0.25 mL of normal saline was added to prepare an RBC suspension. Samples of G, APAG, and SC11@APAG (10 × 10 mm) were incubated in 1.0 mL normal saline at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by the addition of 20 μL RBC suspension and further incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. After incubation, 20 μL of the RBC solution was added to a cell counting plate for observation. The absorbance of the supernatant at 540 nm was measured, and the hemolysis rate was calculated as follows:Hemolysisrate(%)=(ODexp−ODneg)/(ODpos−ODneg)×100%where ODexp, ODneg and ODpos represent the OD values for the experimental, negative, and positive groups, respectively.

The RBCs were observed under an inverted microscope (60 × magnification), and five typical images were captured for each sample. A ruler was photographed at the same magnification for reference.

Cytocompatibility assessment

L929 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 10 4 cells well−1 and cultured for 24 h in a CO2 incubator. The cells were then co-cultured with the samples for 24 h, followed by the addition of 100 μL CCK-8 reagent (Macklin, Shanghai, China) to each well and incubation for an additional 2 h. Absorbance (OD) was measured using a microplate reader. Relative cell viability was calculated as:Relativecellviability(%)=((ODs−ODb)/(ODc−ODb))×100%where ODs, ODc, and ODb represent the average absorbance of the sample, control, and blank groups, respectively.

Antimicrobial studies—bacterial culture

Single colonies of Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC25922), Gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC6538), and Methicillin-Resistant.

Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC43300) were isolated from LB agar, inoculated into LB broth, and cultured at 37 °C for 12 h. Bacterial samples were adjusted to the desired concentration for subsequent experiments.

Inhibition zone test

The boiled agarose solution was cooled to ∼45 °C and mixed evenly with the bacterial suspension (109 CFU/mL) at a volume ratio of 1:10. The mixture was poured into agar plates to solidify. A circular well was created in the center, and 20 μL of SC11 bacterial solution (109 CFU/mL) or the prepared SC11@ APAG sample was placed in the middle. The plates were incubated at 37 °C overnight, and the inhibition zones were captured and measured.

Microbial growth profile and antibacterial ratio

A 10 μL bacterial suspension was spread on the surface of SC11@APAG gauze and covered with a sterilized polyethylene film. After a specific contact time, bacteria on the hydrogel and film were eluted with PBS buffer to an appropriate concentration. A 100 μL diluted bacterial suspension was transferred to LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 12–16 h. The antibacterial rate was calculated as:Antibacterialrate(%)=(CFUexp/CFUneg)×100%,where CFUexp and CFUneg represent the colony counts in the experimental and negative control groups, respectively.

Live/dead bacterial staining assay

The bacteria to be tested (108 CFU/mL) were centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 5 min, washed three times with PBS, and then mixed with ordinary gauze or SC11@APAG gauze for 24 h. After incubation, the experimental groups were centrifuged to remove the supernatant and washed with PBS. Following the manufacturer's instructions, bacterial viability after contact with the hydrogel gauze was assessed using the Live/Dead BacLight Bacterial Viability Kit (SYTO-9, Invitrogen, Cat. No. S34854; PI, Solarbio, Cat. No. C0080) and visualized via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, OLYMPUS FV1200, Japan).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS SUPRA 55, Carl Zeiss, Germany) was used for analysis. Samples were fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight, washed with PBS, and dehydrated using ethanol gradients (30 %, 50 %, 70 %, 90 %, and 100 % w/w). After drying, samples were adhered to conductive adhesive, sputter-coated with gold, and imaged in secondary electron mode.

Antibacterial effects in vivo and internal wound healing

The animal experiments were approved by the Hangzhou Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee. A Balb/c male mouse wound infection model was employed to evaluate the effects on wound healing. Prior to surgery, the experimental animals were acclimatized for one week. The animals were anesthetized with isoflurane, shaved, and depilated on the dorsal area using a depilatory cream, followed by disinfection with iodine. A full-thickness circular wound with a diameter of 5 mm was created on the dorsal skin of the mice. Subsequently, 20 μL of MRSA suspension (106 CFU/mL) was applied to the wound to establish the infection model. The wound was sealed with a 3M membrane, and successful infection modeling was confirmed by the appearance of turbid pus after 24 h. Commercially available ordinary gauze and SC11@APAG hydrogel gauze were applied to cover the infected wounds and secured with adhesive tape. Except for multiple changes on the first day, the gauze was replaced once on the 3rd and 5th days, respectively. The mice were randomly divided into two groups: (1) control group and (2) SC11@APAG group. Throughout the experiment, digital photographs of the wounds were taken at designated intervals, and the wound area was measured using ImageJ. The wound healing rate was calculated as follows:Woundhealingrate(%)=(S1−Sn)/S1×100%,Where S1 represents the wound area on day 1, and Sn represents the wound area on a specific day.

After the experiment, the mice were euthanized, and wound tissues were harvested for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Microscopic examination was performed to observe tissue regeneration, analyze inflammation, and assess collagen fiber distribution in the wound area. Additionally, on day 3, wound exudate was collected under sterile conditions using a cotton swab, placed in a 1.5 mL EP tube, and sonicated for 5 min. The samples were diluted at three different gradients, and 100 μL of each dilution was spread on agar plates. After 24 h of incubation, colony counting was performed.
