Epigenetic Alterations in Perioperative Anaphylaxis Among Chinese Patients: A Case‐Control Study Protocol
Authors: Huamei Cai, Zhe Li, Yun Yan, Ziqi An, Xiaowen Liu, Jing Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsPerioperative anaphylaxis (POA) is a rare but life‐threatening complication with significant risks to patient health. Our previous national epidemiologic survey indicates regional differences in the incidence of suspected POA in China, which may be related to environmental and genetic factors. Therefore, this study aims to explore epigenetic alterations in Chinese patients with POA.MethodsThis case‐control study will involve patients with POA reported on the National Perioperative Anaphylaxis Safety Initiative (NPASI) platform and patients visiting the anesthesia consultation clinic at the China‐Japan Friendship Hospital (CJFH). Forty patients and twenty healthy controls will be enrolled in this study, and the POA patients will undergo allergen tests, including skin tests (STs) and/or the basophil activation test (BAT). Demographic information and details of POA events will be collected, and their peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) will be isolated for DNA methylation detection. The fragmented DNA, after extraction and quality control, will be subjected to enzymatic conversion followed by whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) using high‐throughput sequencing. Data filtering and alignment will be performed using the BSMAP tool to map the reads to the reference genome. Differential methylation regions and functional gene pathways will be analyzed between POA patients and controls, aiming to identify epigenetically regulated genes linked to POA. The findings will be validated within the original data set to enhance result robustness.ConclusionsThis study aims to comprehensively investigate the epigenetic alterations associated with POA in Chinese patients. The results are expected to provide fundamental insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying POA, potentially guiding future research and informing preventive strategies in clinical practice.A schematic diagram for the recruitment and epigenetics study of POA patients can be seen in Figure 1.Figure 1Schematic diagram for recruitment and epigenetics study of POA patients.The research was registered on the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry and the registration number is ChiCTR2400089345.

Introduction

Perioperative anaphylaxis (POA) is an acute, life‐threatening hypersensitivity reaction that occurs during the perioperative period, often triggered by exposure to medications or substances used in anesthesia or surgery [1]. Clinically, it can present with skin symptoms, cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms. Severe POA can lead to respiratory and cardiac arrest, threatening the lives of patients [1, 2, 3]. The incidence of POA varies from one in 10,000 to 20,000 across different regions, including Europe and Asia [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. Our multicenter epidemiological study conducted in 2019 showed that the incidence of suspected life‐threatening POA in China is approximately 1/11,360 [9]. Allergen testing data from both a 12‐year single‐center study in mainland China and a 10‐year study in Hong Kong consistently identified neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) and antibiotics as the primary culprits, with chlorhexidine, sedatives, and opioids also contributing significantly [10, 11]. Notably, these studies revealed clear regional differences in POA incidence within China, with higher rates reported in the southern regions. Previous allergy‐related survey studies have also reported geographical and seasonal variations in the incidence of such reactions [12, 13]. For instance, US data from 1993 to 2005 revealed higher rates of anaphylaxis‐related emergency department visits in the northern states [14]. Similarly, a survey conducted in a Chilean children′s hospital from 2001 to 2010 revealed that the incidence of anaphylaxis correlated with latitude [15]. Such observations suggest that geographical and environmental factors, such as sunlight exposure, ultraviolet radiation, and individual vitamin D levels, may influence the susceptibility to allergic reactions [16, 17, 18]. However, the underlying biological mechanisms linking these environmental exposures to immune hypersensitivity remain unclear.

Recent advances in epigenetics offer a plausible mechanistic explanation [19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24]. Epigenetic modifications, particularly DNA methylation, are highly responsive to environmental cues and can regulate gene expression without altering the underlying DNA sequence [25]. It plays a significant role in the immune system's response to allergens and the development of allergic conditions [19, 21]. For example, one research found that asthma patients with high IgE levels had the lowest methylation levels, and epigenome‐wide association studies identified that polymorphisms in genes such as STAT6, FCER1A, and IL4 could serve as diagnostic tools for asthma patients [26]. A recent meta‐analysis focusing on the epigenetics of food allergy patients included 16 high‐quality studies, suggesting that epigenetic changes in genes related to food allergy are mainly concentrated on Th1/Th2 imbalance and regulatory T cell (Treg) function [20]. One research suggests that patients who died from anaphylactic shock induced by β‐lactam antibiotics, compared to control groups, exhibit differences in DNA methylation of genes regulating pathways such as MAPK, NOD‐like receptor signaling, and T‐cell differentiation in peripheral blood [27]. These pathways play crucial roles in modulating IgE‐mediated severe allergic reactions, indicating that methylation of specific genomic regions may regulate the occurrence of allergic reactions.

Therefore, epigenetic mechanisms may serve as a key link between environmental/geographical factors and individual susceptibility to POA. This study will firstly investigates the differences in PBMCs DNA methylation levels between POA patients and healthy individuals. This will help us to understand the genetic variations in POA patients and their potential connections to geographical differences.

Methods

Research Design

This study is a prospective case‐control study scheduled from December 2024 to July 2026. Ethical approval (NO. 2024‐KY‐168‐1) has been obtained from the Ethics Committee of CJFH. The research has been registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400089345).

Researchers will initially screen and recruit suspected POA patients visiting the anesthesia consultation clinic at CJFH or reported by anesthesiologists on the NPASI platform. Patients with suspected allergens, along with matched controls without allergies, will undergo allergen testing. Blood samples will then be collected at CJFH for PBMCs DNA methylation analysis.

Study Population and Recruitment Protocol

All recruitment efforts for the study will be conducted by the research team from the Department of Anesthesiology at CJFH. The team established the NPASI reporting platform website in 2018. We have engaged with anesthesiology departments from multiple tertiary hospital and provided training on the diagnosis and differential diagnosis of POA.

Monthly, the anesthesiologists from these centers will upload clinical data and contact information for suspected POA cases to the NPASI platform. Additionally, suspected patients will also be recruited from the anesthesia consultation clinic at CJFH. After an initial screening to determine eligibility, we will contact the patients to discuss their potential participation in the study. After obtaining consent, we will conduct a detailed medical history review and perform allergen testing.

For the control group, we will select patients from CJFH who have undergone surgery but do not have allergies. These patients will voluntarily participate in the study. The control and case groups will be matched at a ratio of 1:2. To reduce the interference of confounders, demographic data such as gender, age, long‐term residence, and type of anesthesia will be matched between the case and control groups. To explore geographical differences in epigenetic patterns, we will categorize patients based on long‐term residence and aim for a balanced representation of patients from northern and southern regions.

All information about the patients will be kept strictly confidential. To enhance recruitment effectiveness, we have also established an educational website (http://www.periop-anaphylaxis.com.cn/), providing knowledge about POA and information for clinic consultation.

Sample Size and Grouping Method

Sample size calculation is described in the data analysis part. A 2:1 matching ratio will result in 40 cases and 20 controls, with an overall sample size of 60 participants. For subgroup analysis, POA patients will be divided based on different allergens such as NMBAs and antibiotics. Besides, southern and northern groups based on patients' long‐term residence will be analyzed. The Southern group will include South China and East China while the Northern group will include Northeast China, Northwest China, and North China. This categorization will facilitate the analysis of epigenetic differences between the two regions.

Allergen Test Steps and Relevant Examinations for Patients

Initial Contact With Patients

Patients meeting the inclusion criteria for the case group will be contacted by the researcher via telephone or a secure text message. Patients will be required to provide recent blood test results (within the last 6 months), including a complete blood count and coagulation tests, to rule out contraindications for allergen testing. Allergen testing will be scheduled at CJFH 4–6 weeks after the allergic reaction and the researcher will complete the Case Report Form (CRF) during this process.

Case Report Form (CRF) Data Collection

The research team has developed a clinical CRF specifically for POA patients. Two designated personnel will be responsible for double data entry into an Excel spreadsheet, which will be securely stored by the researchers. Information will be collected either at the anesthesia consultation clinic during patient visits or through follow‐ups on the NPASI platform. The information will include, but is not limited to, the following:

Demographic and anthropometric Information: age, gender, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, birthplace, long‐term residence, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, type of surgery, anesthesia method, smoking history, drinking history, allergy history, and medical history of various systems.

Details of perioperative suspected anaphylaxis events: location of the event, clinical symptoms, perioperative medications administration, treatment and rescue medications, patient's surgery status, and patient outcomes.

Peripheral Blood Sample Collection

Before the blood collection, researchers will confirm with patients that they have discontinued any medications that might affect test results. Fresh peripheral blood will be aseptically drawn into EDTA anticoagulant tubes. A total of two tubes will be collected, totaling 20 mL (2 × 10 mL). The samples will be stored at 4°C before conducting the BAT test and isolating PBMCs.

Skin Tests

Morphine, which is recommended in ST guidelines [40] and capable of inducing mast cell degranulation, will be used as a positive control, and normal saline (0.9%) will serve as the negative control for all tests. STs Concentration Sequence [32]

For patients with a history of severe allergic reactions (e.g., anaphylactic shock, severe dyspnea), skin testing will begin at a lower concentration (1/100). For those with milder allergic reactions, testing can start at the highest nonirritating concentration. SPTs will be performed at 1/10 or the highest nonirritating concentrations, with intervals of 20 min between tests. If SPTs yielded negative results, IDTs will be conducted at 1/100, 1/10, and the highest nonirritating concentrations, also at 20‐min intervals. The procedure will be terminated upon obtaining a positive skin test result.

STs Operation Steps [40]

STs include a SPT and IDT, the steps are as follows

SPT

The patient should sit with their arm relaxed and place flat on a table. Mark the forearm (30 mm from the elbow, 50 mm from the wrist) and disinfect the skin with 75% alcohol, or for those allergic to alcohol, use 0.9% saline or 1% chlorhexidine. After the skin has returned to normal color (about 2 min), use a pipette to place one drop of the test solution next to the marked line, starting with the negative control, followed by the drug solution and the positive control. Maintain a 3–5 cm distance between drops. Use a disposable prick needle to puncture the skin vertically through the drop, then discard the needle. After 1 min, use three cotton swabs to wipe away any residual solution in the order of application without mixing the drops. If a strong reaction occurs, immediately wipe away the solution. Closely observe the patient for any adverse reactions, such as severe systemic reactions. Typical alert symptoms may include itching and warmth in the tongue, pharynx, palms, and soles. Record any skin sensations (itching or pain). Observe the test results after 15–20 min. Draw the wheal's outline with a marker, and copy it onto record paper using transparent tape.

Result Interpretation

A wheal diameter of at least 3 mm larger than the negative control (with or without erythema) after 15 min is considered positive. If the patients show strong allergic reactions, the anesthesiologists will take active measures.

IDT

After disinfection of the upper arms, use a disposable 1 mL syringe. Start with low‐concentration drug solutions, injecting 0.02–0.05 mL of a dilution to form a 2–3 mm diameter wheal. Closely observe the patient for adverse reactions, such as severe systemic reactions. Record any skin sensations (itching or pain). Observe test results after 15–20 min, recording the maximum and perpendicular diameters of the wheal and erythema (draw circles with a ballpoint pen and copy onto record paper using transparent tape).

Result Interpretation

An increase in wheal diameter of ≥ 3 mm with or without surrounding erythema at the injection site after 15 min, is considered positive. If the initial IDT test is negative, increase the concentration tenfold after 15–20 min, but not exceeding the maximum concentration. Repeat the procedure as above. For positive results due to patient factors (e.g., scratching causing local swelling), the test should be repeated after ensuring the area is undisturbed.

BAT Procedure

The experimental procedures will follow the protocol from the Flow CAST kit (Bühlmann Laboratories AG, Switzerland), and the results of the BAT will be analyzed by flow cytometry. The recommended drug concentrations used in BAT are listed in the Table 1. The concentrations are primarily determined based on published literature [29]. If no relevant literature was available, the concentrations were set according to the kit laboratory's instructions. For cephalosporin antibiotics, the concentration did not exceed 2 mg/mL. For each tube, basophil activation is induced by adding 100 μL of the stimulation buffer, adding 50 μL of the suspected stimulator (with the stimulation buffer serving as the patient′s background control and FcεRI antibody, fMLP as the stimulus quality control), 50 μL of the patient's whole blood sample, and subsequently, FITC anti‐human CD63 and PE anti‐human CCR3 will be added to stain the cells. The red blood cells are removed through a lysis reaction, and the remaining cells are resuspended in a washing buffer before being analyzed by flow cytometry. To ensure accurate evaluation and standardization, the activation gate should be set to show 2%–2.5% of basophils with spontaneous activation in the background, as required by the kit. BAT will be considered positive if the percentage of activated basophils is 5% or higher and the stimulation index (SI) activated percentage of basophils divided by the percentage of basophils in the negative control group is 2 or higher.

Preservation and Management of Peripheral Blood Samples, Mononuclear Cells, and Biobank Management

Peripheral Blood Sample Preservation

After conducting the BAT, the remaining blood samples will be separated into two parts for preservation. Four milliliters whole blood will be stored in cryotubes, with 1 mL per tube. Eight milliliters of whole blood will be centrifuged (1500g, 20 min) to separate plasma from blood cells. Dispense plasma and blood cells into cryotubes, 1 mL per tube.

PBMCs Isolation Steps

Firstly take 6 mL of whole blood, divide into two tubes with 3 mL each, and add an equal volume of PBS to dilute the blood at 4°C. Then use SepTubeTM 15 mL density gradient centrifugation tubes, and add Ficoll lymphocyte separation solution equal to the blood volume. Slowly layer the diluted blood onto the Ficoll solution and centrifuge (800g, 20 min, RT). After centrifugation, the tube will have four layers from top to bottom: plasma (platelets and cell debris), mononuclear cell white membrane layer, separation solution layer, and red blood cells/granulocytes. Discard the top plasma layer, transfer the white membrane layer to a new centrifuge tube, add 10 mL PBS, and mix, then centrifuge (250g, 10 min, RT). Discard the supernatant and wash twice. Finally, based on the cell count, the cells will be resuspended in the appropriate volume of Bambanker cell freezing solution to achieve each ml contains > 106 cells before being dispensed into cryotubes.

Biobank Management

The whole blood, plasma, blood cells, and PBMC samples will be encoded, labeled, and stored at −80°C in the biobank according to the management protocols of the CJFH Clinical Research Institute. Sample storage and retrieval are managed by specific personnel.

Data Analysis

WGBS Library Preparation and Quality Control

In this study, genomic DNA will be extracted from isolated PBMCs using the chloroform extraction method. After extraction, DNA purity will be assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer, with OD values (A260/A280) ranging between 1.8 and 2.0. DNA integrity was verified by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the total DNA concentration will be measured using a Qubit fluorometer. Upon successful quality control, genomic DNA will be fragmented into 100–300 bp fragments by sonication (Covaris), and the fragments will be purified using the MiniElute PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN).

Enzymatic conversion will be performed using the EpiArt DNA Enzymatic Methylation Kit. Compared to traditional bisulfite conversion methods, this enzymatic approach operates under milder conditions, including low temperatures and neutral buffers, resulting in lower DNA damage and more precise identification of methylation sites [41]. As the accuracy of sequencing depends primarily on enzymatic conversion efficiency, lambda phage DNA will be spiked into the sample DNA, and the methylation rate of the lambda DNA will be calculated to evaluate conversion efficiency during the experiment. A conversion rate ≥ 98% for lambda DNA is required for the enzymatic method [42]. Following conversion, standard library preparation will be performed using the EpiArt DNA Methylation Library Kit on the NovaSeq X Plus platform. This will include DNA denaturation, 3′ adapter ligation, adapter extension, 5′ adapter ligation, library amplification, and three times of magnetic bead purification. The purification steps will use VAHTS DNA clean beads to remove enzymes, adapter remnants, and other contaminants. Library amplification will be conducted with the VAHTS HiFi enzyme system and index primers, with the number of PCR cycles adjusted based on the initial DNA input. The combination of enzymatic conversion and precise size selection throughout the library preparation process helps to reduce technical bias.

Data Alignment and Analysis Workflow

Based on the NovaSeq X Plus sequencing platform, the researchers will perform filtering and quality control on the obtained FASTA files. The filtering criteria includes: removal of reads containing adapter sequences, reads with more than 10% N bases, and low‐quality reads (i.e., reads in which bases with quality scores below Q20 account for more than 50% of the entire reads). In this WGBS study, the amount of sequencing data per sample should not be less than 90G, and the average sequencing reads quality control (QC) should be ≥ 30x accounting for ≥ 80% of bases, and ≥ 20x accounting for more than 95% bases.

The resulting clean reads will then be aligned to the Homo sapiens reference genome (GRCh38, Ensembl Release 113) using BSMAP software (version 2.90) [43] with default parameters. BSMAP achieves fast and accurate alignment of bisulfite‐treated sequencing reads by constructing multiple versions of reference seeds and introducing an asymmetric C/T matching strategy. It is considered one of the classic algorithms for processing WGBS data. Cytosine methylation level analysis will be conducted by categorizing cytosines into three sequence contexts: CG, CHG, and CHH. The proportions of these contexts vary across species. Since the DNA samples will be derived from PBMCs, the methylation level of each cytosine will be represented as a continuous value ranging from 0% to 100%, calculated as the number of methylated reads divided by the total number of effective reads covering that cytosine. The formula for calculating methylation at a single site is: methylation level (%) = 100 × (number of reads covering methylated cytosine/total number of reads covering the site). We will also calculate methylation levels across each chromosome and specific genomic regions, such as 2 kb upstream and downstream of coding regions, as well as various repeat elements, to facilitate downstream analyses.

Differential methylation cytosines (DMCs) and regions (DMRs) will be analyzed using the methylKit (v1.4.1) [44]. Low‐coverage sites will be firstly filtered out, and cytosine sites used in differential analysis will be required to have a minimum sequencing depth of ≥ 4 [45]. Differential methylation analysis will be conducted separately for all C sites, CG, CHG, and CHH contexts, with specific thresholds applied. For CG‐specific differential methylation, an absolute methylation difference ≥ 0.25 and a Q‐value ≤ 0.05 will be required. For all cytosines, an absolute methylation difference ≥ 0.2 and Q‐value ≤ 0.05 will be applied. DMRs will be defined by scanning the genome with 200 bp sliding windows containing at least 5 CG sites per window, and required an absolute methylation difference ≥ 0.25 with a Q‐value ≤ 0.05. All Q‐values will be obtained using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to adjust p‐values for multiple testing, with the overall false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at ≤ 0.05.

To analyze the functional enrichment of genes affected by DMCs and DMRs, Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses will be performed.

Sample Size Calculation and Statistical Analysis of Other Research Data

Based on a literature review, the methylation differences in genes related to Type 2 inflammation (e.g., IL‐4, Toll‐like receptor pathways) range from 0.2 to 0.5 between food allergy and healthy populations [46]. According to expected minimum detectable methylation difference of 0.1, a standard deviation of 0.1, a significance level of 0.01, and a statistical power of 0.8, the calculated required sample size is 20 cases per group. However, to maximize the likelihood of identifying methylation regulatory sites associated with POA, while also taking cost considerations into account, we decide to include 40 individuals in the case group and 20 in the control group. The calculation formula is as follows: n=2*Z1−α2+Z1−β2*σ2δ2

The statistical analysis for this study will be conducted using SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A p < 0.05 will be used as the threshold for determining statistical significance and all tests are two‐sided. Descriptive analyses will be conducted on all variables; categorical variables will be presented using frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables will be detailed using means or medians alongside standard deviations or interquartile ranges (IQR). To assess differences in categorical variables across groups, the χ 2 test and Fisher's exact test will be employed. For numerical variables, following tests for normality, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test will be utilized to identify statistical differences between groups. Additionally, Pearson's correlation coefficient and Spearman's rank correlation coefficient will be used to conduct correlation analyses.

Ethics and Dissemination

This study has obtained ethics approval (NO. 2024‐KY‐168‐1) from the Ethics Committee of CJFH. The research was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400089345). Informed consent will be obtained from patients when they visit the anesthesia consultation clinic. Discussion

This study aims to recruit POA patients to investigate their epigenetic characteristics. Due to the limited number of centers in China capable of conducting both in vivo and in vitro allergen diagnostic tests, we plan to perform allergen testing and collect blood samples at CJFH, making this a single‐center study. However, given the low incidence of severe POA, patient recruitment poses a significant challenge. To address this, our research team will collaborate with principal investigators from multiple tertiary hospital anesthesiology departments and has established the NPASI platform. The NPASI platform currently includes approximately 30 large tertiary hospitals across seven major regions of China, with an annual report of approximately 200 cases. Through this platform, we will conduct a detailed review of medical history and anesthesia records and communicate with principal investigators at each center to assess the likelihood of an allergic reaction. We will then collect clinical data on potential POA patients and prescreen them by phone or message to assess their interest in participating. Currently, we have implemented multiple measures to maximize enrollment rates and have developed contingency plans in case the target number of cases cannot be fully achieved. Optimizing patient recruitment strategies such as posting a recruitment advertisement and extending the enrollment period will be the specific approaches to improve the enrollment rate. As the cases involved in this study, a 1:2 ratio allows for a larger number of cases in the case group, thereby enhancing statistical power. On the other hand, in rare disease studies, increasing the sample size of the case group helps improve the detection of disease‐related epigenetic signals and reduces result instability caused by insufficient samples.

At present, genetic research in the field of allergic reactions mainly focuses on type 2 inflammatory responses [46, 47, 48, 49], specifically Th2 cell‐mediated allergic reactions, as well as asthma, dermatological conditions, and severe adverse drug reactions such as Stevens–Johnson syndrome associated with specific HLA gene mutations [50]. Research on perioperative drug allergies remains relatively unexplored. Previous large‐scale epidemiological studies in China, involving four million surgical cases, revealed a significant finding: the incidence of allergic reactions increases from north to south, with the highest incidence area having three times the rate of the lowest incidence area [8]. This epidemiological survey study indeed focused on suspected allergic reactions without definitive allergen identification. However, all included cases were severe allergic reactions, and anesthesiologists ruled out other potential clinical conditions that could cause significant respiratory or cardiovascular manifestations. Therefore, it is highly likely that these patients experienced anaphylaxis. Moreover, prior allergy‐related survey studies conducted in other countries have documented geographical differences in the prevalence of such reactions. This suggests that geographical and environmental factors may play an important role in the mechanism of allergic reactions. Therefore, we will analyze this issue from an epigenetic perspective, considering how environmental factors may influence genetic epigenetic changes in allergic patients, including DNA methylation and histone modifications.

While this study will utilize whole‐genome bisulfite sequencing to analyze epigenetic changes in POA patients, we acknowledge several potential challenges. The challenges and limitations of subsequent analysis may include complex model analysis, data quality control, and cleaning. We will collaborate with experienced statisticians and data analysis experts to ensure the reliability of our data analysis results.

One of the main difficulties in recruitment is the willingness of patients to participate in the trial. Our research team will communicate extensively with patients, offer appropriate compensation for their time and travel, and provide real‐time contact channels to ensure patients can reach out to researchers promptly if they have any questions or wish to withdraw from the study.

In summary, this epigenetic study has the potential to significantly advance our understanding of POA. By exploring the interplay between epigenetic factors, regional environmental exposures, and POA, we aim to identify the relevant cellular and molecular signaling pathways. This study is expected to provide valuable insights into the future prevention and treatment of POA, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
