Trophoblast derived extracellular vesicles trigger secondary vesicle release from endometrial epithelial cells
Authors: Subhashini Muhandiram, Seyed Omid Reza Mousavi, Suranga Kodithuwakku, Kasun Godakumara, Sergei Kopanchuk, Ago Rinken, Getnet Midekessa, Alireza Fazeli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are emerging mediators of cellular communication, carrying bioactive molecules that can alter the function of recipient cells. Yet, how these signals are recognized and interpreted by recipient cells remains unclear. For communication to be effective, signals must reach the right cells at the right time, suggesting that EVs likely target specific cells, an aspect often overlooked in mechanistic studies.In this study, we used an in vitro embryo implantation model consisting of JAr (trophoblast) and RL95-2 (endometrial) cells to explore targeted communication in a physiologically relevant context. Our previous findings showed that JAr-derived EVs specifically induce rapid secretion of milk fat globule epidermal growth factor-8 (MFGE8) from RL95-2 cells. Using MFGE8 as a functional readout, we further investigated the underlying mechanisms of EV mediated targeted cell communication.We found that RL95-2 responses to JAr EVs depend on clathrin-mediated endocytosis and endosomal acidification. Moreover, Rab27a-dependent secondary vesicle release contributed to increased MFGE8 secretion in endometrial cells. Importantly, optimal EV activity required precise pairing between EVs and recipient cells, indicating that EV-triggered vesicle release is not merely a recycling process. Finally, recipient cell stress disrupted vesicle release and impaired responses to EV signals. By combining this in vitro model with MFGE8 as a rapid functional readout, we highlight a natural mechanism of targeted EV communication.

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a diverse population of nanosized vesicles released by nearly all cell types. Although the term “EVs” is commonly used as a broad reference for all lipid membrane-bound vesicles secreted by cells, they can be classified into different subtypes, such as exosomes, ectosomes, and microvesicles, based on their biogenesis pathways and physico-chemical properties [1]. EVs carry a diverse molecular cargo encapsulated within lipid bilayer membrane, including proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and small metabolites [2]. The molecular cargo of EVs serves as a distinctive fingerprint of their cell of origin [3], which then can be taken up by other cell types, facilitating the transfer of biological information between cells [4, 5]. Hence, EVs are now recognized as crucial mediators of intercellular communication in both physiological and pathological conditions [6].

While some evidence suggests that the recipient cell response to EVs is highly specific and influenced by EV source and dosage [4, 7], other studies indicate that EV uptake can occur across a diverse range of cell types, suggesting that EV communication is largely nonspecific and stochastic [8]. This has sparked an ongoing debate among EV researchers about whether EVs exhibit specificity or tropism toward particular cell types or not [2, 9]. If such specificity exists, it implies that EVs carry some form of molecular “address label” that directs them to their target cells. Identifying the unique instances where EVs participate in targeted cellular communication is crucial for understanding the mechanism of EV tropism. Despite this, many mechanistic studies on EVs have often overlooked the importance of EV donor-recipient cell pairings, potentially missing key insights into EV specificity [10, 11]. Better understanding of EV kinetics are also crucial for understanding their mechanism of action, including the evidence on optimal EV-to-cell ratio required for EV bioactivity, the minimum time needed for EV internalization, whether EV internalization leads to cargo release, and the resulting functional effects. There is also ongoing discussion about how EVs exert their biological activity, particularly whether they need to be internalized or whether simple binding of EVs to the cell surface is sufficient to trigger biological effects. Hence, understanding the relationship between EV uptake and its functional outcomes remains an interest for scientific investigation. While some in vitro models have been developed to study EV uptake and cargo release, these often involve engineered cells or EVs [10]. Such modifications may limit our ability to observe the natural dynamics of EV-mediated communication. Therefore, there is a growing need for innovative and physiologically relevant models to study EV bioactivity without the need for manipulating cells or the EVs.

In this study, we utilized a unique biological model of embryo implantation, where EV-mediated intercellular communication plays a critical functional role, to investigate the mechanisms underlying EV activity. Embryo implantation is a crucial step in pregnancy establishment in mammals. It is a sequential process involving (i) apposition, where the embryo approaches the receptive endometrial epithelial cells (EECs), (ii) attachment, where it adheres to the endometrial lining, and (iii) invasion, where the trophoblast cells penetrate the EEC layer. Embryos are known to release EVs, which can be internalized by EECs, reprogramming them into an implantation-supportive phenotype [12, 13]. Likewise, EEC-derived EVs have the potential to functionally reprogram the embryonic cells [14–16], highlighting the critical role of bidirectional EV-mediated communication in guiding implantation. Previously, we established an in vitro assay to simulate EV mediated embryo maternal cross-talk using trophoblast analogue JAr cells and receptive EEC analogue RL95-2 cells. Using this model, we demonstrated that trophoblast-derived EVs can transfer signals to receptive EECs to alter their gene expression [17] and secretome [18]. Furthermore, we showed that this interaction exhibits functional specificity, reinforcing the relevance of this model for studying targeted intercellular communication [4, 18]. More recently, we demonstrated that trophoblastic EVs can increase MFGE8 protein secretion from EECs in a time and dose dependent manner which could be quantified using simple ELISA. Notably, MFGE8 release can be detected as early as 30 min post-EV treatment, providing a rapid functional readout of trophoblast EV activity [19]. This approach eliminates the need for protein or RNA extraction from recipient cells, streamlining the detection of EV activity. MFGE8, a peripheral protein, is typically secreted within membrane vesicles ranging from 100 to 200 nm in size via unconventional secretion mechanisms. It plays a crucial role in cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions across various tissues and shown specific role in embryo implantation process as well [18, 20, 21]. In the current investigation, we provide mechanistic insights into how trophoblast EVs trigger MFGE8 secretion from EECs. Our findings suggest a novel mechanism by which EV uptake via endocytosis leads to specific functional responses in targeted recipient cells.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Cytochalasin D (C8273) and Bafilomycin A1 (SML1661) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Dynasore (S80470), Chlorpromazine (S574902), Heparin sodium (S1346), Genistein (S1342), Wortmannin (S2758) were purchased from Selleckchem. Nexinhib 20 (CAYM29899-1) was purchased from Cayman Chemical. TNFα (rcyc-htnfa) was purchased from Invivogen.

Cell culture

The human choriocarcinoma cell line (JAr) and human endometrial adenocarcinoma cell line (RL95-2) were used to mimic the trophoblast and EECs in vitro. Both cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (HTB-144™, Teddington, United Kingdom).

JAr cells (HTB-144™, Teddington, UK) were cultured in T75 flasks with RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), 1% Penicillin Streptomycin (P/S) and 1% L-glutamine in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. RL95-2 cells were grown in DMEM F12 medium (DMEM 12–604 F, Lonza, Verviers, Belgium) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco™, 10500064), 1% P/S (Gibco™, 15140122, Bleiswijk, Netherlands), and 5 µg/mL insulin (human recombinant insulin, Gibco™, Invitrogen, Denmark) in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. The media was changed every second day until the cells reached 80% confluency. The immortalized human keratinocyte cell line HaCaT (Cellosaurus, CVCL_0038) was grown in DMEM medium supplemented with 4.5 g/L of glucose, 10% of FBS and 1% P/S in 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Media was changed every second day until the cells reached 80% confluency.

Preparation of EV depleted medium

Preparation of EV depleted medium was performed as described previously [18, 19, 22]. In brief, Amicon® Ultra-15 centrifugal filters with a 100 kDa cut-off (Merk KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) was used to filter FBS by centrifuging 3000 × g for 55 min. The filtrate is considered as EV depleted FBS where nanoparticle concentration was reduced by 90%. EV depleted FBS at 10% concentration was used to supplement all relevant culture media.

EV isolation and characterization

Our lab has a well-established protocol for EV enrichment from cell culture medium using size exclusion chromatography which follows the guidelines of minimal information for studies of extracellular vesicles (MISEV) [23]. EV isolation from JAr and HaCaT cells were performed according to the established protocols [4, 18]. In brief, JAr and HaCaT cells were grown until 80% confluency, cells were washed, and media was replaced with EV depleted medium. After 24 h of incubation, cell culture conditioned medium was collected and EVs were isolated using sequential centrifugation followed by size exclusion chromatography. The size and concentration of the EVs were determined using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). The morphology of the EVs were analysed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and enrichment of EV samples with typical EV markers such as CD9, CD63, CD81 and TSG101 was analysed and reported previously [4, 24, 25]. Milk EVs was isolated from commercially available pasteurised low-fat milk (1.8% Tere joogipiim, TERE AS, Lelle 22, Tallinn 11318, Estonia) and as described previously [26]. In summary, whey liquid from milk was separated by reducing the pH of the milk using glacial acetic acid. Then the milk left in + 4 °C for 1 h to form curds and curds were separated using Whatman grade 4 qualitative filter papers and 0.45 μm bottle top dead-end filters. Filtrates were subjected to tangential flow filtration (TFF) using benchtop TFF system (Centramate, Cytiva, MA, USA), with a 300 kDa molecular weight cutoff polyethersulfone membrane filter (Omega PES membrane, 0.02 m2 surface area, Cytiva, MA, USA) to obtain EV concentrate. The buffer was replaced with Dulbecco’s PBS (Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA). The EV enriched concentrate was centrifuged 3000 × g and the filtrate were stored at -80 °C. The milk EV characterization was performed using NTA, TEM and protein marker analysis. The results of milk EV characterization were reported previously [26]. Algea EVs were enriched from Chlorella Vulgaris microalgae culture conditioned medium. The microalgae conditioned media was collected and filtered through grade 292 filter paper (5–8 μm pore size). The resulting filtrated was further processed using bench top TFF system as described above. The microalgae EV characterization was performed using NTA, TEM and protein marker analysis.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was determined using Resazurin-based cell viability assay as described previously [27, 28]. Cells were grown in 96 well flat bottom plates and incubated in CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Following the desired treatments, cells were washed with PBS (containing Ca2+ and Mg2+) one time. Then the PBS was replaced with 50 µmol/L resazurin solution in PBS. The plates were placed into the multimode reader, and measurement of fluorescence was performed (540 nm and 590 nm).

MFGE8 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Cell culture supernatants were collected from RL95-2 cells seeded in 48 or 96 -well plates and centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min to remove any contaminating cells and debris. The MFGE8 concentration was measured using MFGE8 ELISA according to manufacturer’s instructions (Human MFGE8 Quantikine ELISA Kit, R&D systems) and as described previously [19].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). Statistical differences between two groups were evaluated using t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by either Tukey’s post-hoc test or Dunnett’s test. The Shapiro-Wilk normality test was applied to assess data distribution before conducting group comparisons. Results are expressed as mean ± SD, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Experimental design

Determining the chemical modulators that affect the JAr EV functionality within RL95-2 cells

Six pharmacological inhibitors, each selectively targeting different EV uptake pathways (Table 1) were used to block JAr EV uptake by RL95-2 cells. Inhibitor concentrations were selected based on published literature [34–38]. To determine the optimal non-toxic concentrations, RL95-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates and pre-incubated with varying concentrations of each inhibitor for 30 min in EV-depleted medium. The medium was then replaced with fresh inhibitor-containing medium, and incubation continued for another 30 min. Cell viability was assessed using a resazurin-based assay.

Different chemical modulators related to EV uptake

The highest non-toxic concentrations were selected for further studies. To evaluate whether RL95-2 cells rely on endosomal acidification in response to EVs, they were treated with Bafilomycin A1. To determine the optimal concentration, cells were pre-treated with 50 nM, and 100 nM Bafilomycin A1 for 30 min, followed by incubation in fresh inhibitor-containing medium for an additional 30 min. Similarly, Nexinhib 20 was tested at concentrations of 1 µM, 10 µM, and 100 µM. Cell viability was assessed with resazurin-based assay. DMSO or sterile water was used as vehicle control as appropriately. Non-toxic concentrations of each inhibitor that did not affect MFGE8 secretion were selected for subsequent experiments.

For effect of inhibitor experiments, the RL95-2 cells were seeded in 48 well plates (1.2 × 105 cells/mL) and grown until 95% confluency. The cells were washed once with PBS and pre-treated for 30 min with various EV uptake inhibitors, Bafilomycin A1, or Nexinhib 20 in EV-depleted medium. The inhibitors were used at the following concentrations: Heparin (20 µg/mL), Cytochalasin D (10 µM), Chlorpromazine (20 µM), Wortmannin (1 µM), Dynasore (20 µM), Genistein (50 µM), Bafilomycin A1 (100 nM), and Nexinhib 20 (10 µM). Following pre-treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium supplemented with JAr EVs at a concentration of 10⁹ particles/mL, and incubation continued for another 30 min/4 h in the presence of fresh inhibitors. Sterile water, PBS, or DMSO were used as vehicle controls where appropriate. Cell culture supernatants were collected, and MFGE8 levels were quantified using ELISA. Each experiment included an inhibitor control to confirm that the inhibitor alone had no effect on MFGE8 secretion by RL95-2 cells. All experiments were performed in triplicate or more. Compounds and vehicle controls were diluted in 1:1000 in the cell culture medium before application to the cells.

Determining the specificity of MFGE8 release from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs

To investigate the role of EV donor and recipient cell pairing in EV functionality, RL95-2 cells were cultured in 48-well plates and treated with JAr EVs, HaCaT EVs, milk-derived EVs, or algae-derived EVs at a concentration of 10⁹ particles/mL. Cells were incubated for 0 h (baseline), 30 min, or 24 h. The baseline MFGE8 level (0 hreading) was subtracted from the 30-minute and 24 h readings to account for MFGE8 originating from EV treatment. However, ELISA kit was very specific towards human MFGE8 and didn’t cross react with bovine or plant proteins.

Following incubation, cell culture supernatants were collected, and MFGE8 levels were quantified using ELISA. Cell viability after EV treatment was assessed using a resazurin-based viability assay. While different EV treatments did not affect cell viability at the 30 min time point, a slight reduction in viability was observed at 24 h. Therefore, the 24 h MFGE8 readings were corrected for cell viability variations across different EV treatments. To determine whether the specific increase in MFGE8 secretion in RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs was due to the activation of immune signaling pathways such as NF-κB, RL95-2 cells were treated with TNFα (a known activator of NF-κB in EECs) at concentrations of 10 µM, 50 µM, and 100 µM for 30 min. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Determining the effect of recipient cell stress on the function of EVs

Stress in RL95-2 cells was induced by hypoxia. Hypoxia was induced in RL5-2 cells using CoCl2 as described previously [39]. After treating RL95-2 cells with CoCl2 for 24 h, cells were washed with PBS and media was changed to EV depleted medium with or without JAr EVs (109 particles/mL) and incubated for 30 min. RL95-2 cells without EVs were used as a control. Cell culture conditioned medium was collected and MFGE8 ELISA was performed. Experiment was performed in triplicates.

Using an in vitro embryo implantation model comprising the trophoblast analogue JAr cells and the EEC analogue RL95-2 cells (Fig. 1a), we recently demonstrated that MFGE8 protein secretion from EECs increases within 30 min of JAr EV treatment and this effect persists for up to 24 h [19]. In the current study, by utilizing the MFGE8 readout and the same embryo implantation model, we investigated the mechanism by which EVs reprogram their target recipient cells.

RL95-2 cell response to JAr EV is dependent on clathrin-mediated endocytosis

To determine JAr EV uptake mechanism in to RL95-2 cells, the cells were treated with various EV uptake inhibitors, including cytochalasin D, heparin, chlorpromazine, genistein, wortmannin, and dynasore (Fig. 1b). Figure 1c illustrates the various EV uptake pathways and the mechanisms of action of the chemical inhibitors used to block each pathway. For each inhibitor, highest inhibitor concentrations that had minimum effect on cell viability was selected for treatment. Higher concentration of dynasore and genistein affected the cell viability significantly. Therefore, lower concentrations of these inhibitors were used for EV uptake inhibition (Fig. 1d–i).

Chlorpromazine and dynasore significantly reduced MFGE8 release from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs at concentrations of 10 µM and 20 µM, respectively (Fig. 1j and k). In contrast, heparin, cytochalasin D, and genistein had no significant effect (Fig. 1m–o). Interestingly, wortmannin slightly increased MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs (Fig. 1l). Since chlorpromazine and dynasore target clathrin-mediated endocytosis, these findings suggest that this pathway may play a crucial role in the JAr EV internalization within RL95-2 cells and the subsequent rapid release of MFGE8 protein.

To assess whether prolonged inhibitor treatment with cytochalasin D, genistein or heparin (4 h) influences JAr EV functionality and MFGE8 release, we first evaluated the effects of extended inhibitor exposure on RL95-2 cell viability. The minimum concentrations that did not affect cell viability were selected for further studies (Fig. 2a and b). However, genistein significantly reduced cell viability even at its lowest concentration and was therefore excluded from further experiments (Fig. 2c). Cytochalasin D continued to show no significant impact on JAr EV-induced MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells even after 4 h (Fig. 2d). Interestingly, heparin treatment for prolong period reduced JAr EV-induced MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells (Fig. 2e).

RL95-2 cell response to JAr EVs was dependent on endosomal acidification and resulted in secondary vesicle release

To investigate how JAr EVs induce MFGE8 protein release from RL95-2 cells via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, we examined whether the EVs follow endosomal maturation. Baflomycin A1, a specific inhibitor of vacuolar proton ATPases was used to disrupt this process by preventing endosomal acidification and thereby blocking progression of the endocytic pathway from early to late endosomes (Fig. 3a). Notably, Bafilomycin A1 has shown to affect small EV production from cells [40]. On the other hand, MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells has been associated with vesicle secretion, suggesting that MFGE8 secretion reflects EV release from RL95-2 cells [19]. Therefore, a non-toxic concentration of Bafilomycin A1 that does not affect MFGE8 secretion in resting RL95-2 cells was selected, as shown in Fig. 3b for further studies. Interestingly, inhibition of endo-lysosomal acidification using Bafilomycin A1 at 100 nM concentration significantly abrogated the increased MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs (Fig. 3c).

A key pathway of EV biogenesis from cells involves the fusion of mature endosomes/multivesicular bodies (MVBs) or amphisomes with the plasma membrane, leading to EV release. Rab27a and Rab27b proteins guide docking and fusion of MVBs/amphisomes at the plasma membrane [41, 42]. To investigate this mechanism, we used the Rab27a inhibitor Nexinhib20 (Fig. 3a). A non-toxic concentration of Nexinhib20 that does not affect MFGE8 secretion in resting RL95-2 cells was selected, as shown in Fig. 3d. Notably, treatment with 10 µM Nexinhib20 significantly reduced MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs (Fig. 3e). Unique pairing between EV and recipient cell type was required for EV triggered responses in recipient cells

We previously established that increased MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells was specific to trophoblast derived EVs compared to EVs from non-trophoblastic origin (HEK-293 cell derived EVs) after 24 h of post EV treatment. To get broader idea of EV specificity in terms of EV mediated embryo maternal dialogue at 30 min, we further evaluated the MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells in response to EVs derived from multiple sources (EVs from human HaCaT cell culture, EVs from bovine milk and EVs from microalgae).

Interestingly, only JAr EVs induced significant rise in release of MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells after 30 min of EV treatment (Fig. 4a). However, after 24 h of treatment, both JAr, HaCaT and milk derived EVs induced secretion of MFGE8 from RL95-2 cells (Fig. 4b). Microalgae derived EVs suppressed the MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells. HaCaT and milk EVs also induced the secretion of MFGE8 protein from RL95-2 cells but not up to the extent of JAr EVs. To ensure secretory MFGE8 protein changes seen in RL95-2 cells is not due to the cell proliferation, RL95-2 cell viability was assessed after treatment with JAr, HaCaT, milk and microalgae derived EVs. All EV types except microalgae EVs didn’t affect the RL95-2 cells viability over 24 h incubation period. However, microalgae derived EVs show slight decrease in RL95-2 cell viability (Fig. 4c). The rapid induction of MFGE8 secretion by JAr EVs within 30 min, compared to other EV types, suggests that JAr EVs exhibit a specific functional tropism toward RL95-2 cells. This highlights the importance of a unique EV-recipient cell pairing in mediating EV-triggered responses in recipient cells.

NF-κB pathway is an important immune signalling pathway activated during embryo attachment in vitro. MFGE8 production in EEC is linked to NF-κB signalling. To test whether MFGE8 secretion from EECs in response to trophoblast EVs also under the influence of NF-κB Pathway, we activated NF-κB using known activator TNFα. However, TNF α at 100 µg/ml concentration could not alter the MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells within 30 min (Fig. 5a). Previously, we showed that hypoxia in EEC induced by CoCl2 treatment for 24 h can change the EV composition of RL95-2 cells and can differentially alter the trophoblast cell transcriptome [39]. Oxygen deprivation in the endometrium is known to affect embryo implantation. Therefore, we assessed whether hypoxia in endometrium may control EV mediated embryo maternal cross-talk, with a particular focus on its impact on MFGE8 secretion. NTA analysis demonstrated that hypoxia increased particle release from RL95-2 cells following 24 h of CoCl₂ treatment (Fig. 5b). ELISA-based MFGE8 measurement served as a complementary functional readout to corroborate this finding (Fig. 5c). Therefore, it can be postulated that EV release and MFGE8 secretion may influence the trophoblast cell function to guide implantation. To test whether endometrial cell response to trophoblast EVs persist after inducing hypoxia, we introduced trophoblast EVs in to RL95-2 cells that had undergone hypoxia (CoCl2 treatment for 24 h). Interestingly, hypoxic cells could not increase the MFGE8 secretion to same extent as the non-hypoxic cells (Fig. 5d). Discussion

EVs are increasingly recognized as important mediators of intercellular communication. EV cargo, composed of proteins, lipids and nucleic acids, can alter the recipient cell gene expression and function [43], however the exact mechanisms remain elusive. In the current study, we used MFGE8 protein secretion as a natural readout in an in vitro embryo implantation model to investigate the mechanism of EV-mediated intercellular communication. This in vitro EV activity assay can be conducted in a 48- or 96-well format, allowing the assessment of EV function without manipulating recipient cells, thereby preserving their natural state and minimizing the need for large-scale EV production.

EV uptake by recipient cells enables the transfer of bioactive cargo and is essential for EV-mediated communication. EVs are internalized through multiple endocytic pathways, including clathrin-dependent endocytosis, caveolin-mediated uptake, macropinocytosis, phagocytosis, and lipid raft-mediated internalization [44]. Due to the heterogeneous nature of EVs and their ability to be internalized by various cell types, it is likely that different EV subpopulations enter recipient cells via multiple routes. Interestingly, the link between EV uptake and functionality is not well established. EV internalization may not always be necessary for them to exert biological effects. Some studies suggest that binding of EVs to the recipient cell membrane alone is sufficient to activate intracellular signalling pathways [45], leading to functional changes in recipient cells. Additionally, EVs may fuse with the recipient cell membrane in a manner similar to viral fusion [10]. Although receptor-ligand interactions are believed to facilitate EV fusion and internalization, direct evidence remains limited [46]. Therefore, understanding the precise mechanisms of EV internalization is essential for controlling natural EV uptake and regulating their subsequent functional effects [47]. To further investigate trophoblast EV uptake within EECs, different pharmacological inhibitors of EV uptake were used. Chloropromazine and dynasore, both inhibitors of clathrin mediated endocytosis, significantly reduced the trophoblastic EV activity on EECs. EV internalization by clarthrin mediated endocytosis is caused by invagination of the plasma membrane to contain EVs. Clathrin is a 190 kDa protein interact with group of membrane associated protein called adaptor proteins (e.g., AP1, AP2, and AP3) to form supramolecular lattice that results in invagination of the cell membrane to surround EVs (endosome) [48]. Chlorpromazine, a cationic amphipathic drug, depletes clathrin and serves as an inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis [49]. Similarly, dynasore inhibits dynamin, a protein essential for vesicle fission during clathrin-mediated endocytosis [50]. Growing evidence indicates that clathrin-mediated endocytosis plays a crucial role in EV uptake, enabling their functional effects in both physiological [51] and pathological processes [49, 52]. Interestingly, in a recent study it has been shown that this mechanism has also been observed in fungal EV uptake by plant cells, suggesting it may represent one of the universal pathways for EV uptake [53]. In contrast, genistein an inhibitor of caveolin-mediated endocytosis and cytochalasin D which inhibits EV macropinocytosis/phagocytosis, did not affect the EV bioactivity within 30 min. Notably, wortmannin, a specific inhibitor of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K), slightly enhanced MFGE8 secretion from EECs in response to trophoblastic EVs. Since MFGE8 can induce PI3K expression by binding to integrin receptors [54–56], it is possible that the secreted MFGE8 counteracted the inhibitory effects of wortmannin, leading to an overall increase in its expression. Although heparin did not show any effect 30 min after EV treatment, it exhibited a clear effect after 4 h. This suggests that EV binding or fusion at the recipient cell surface may also influence MFGE8 release from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs. In contrast, cytochalasin D showed no effect even after prolonged incubation. This lack of response could be attributed either to the minimal involvement of macropinocytosis or phagocytosis in EV uptake by RL95-2 cells, or to the short half-life of cytochalasin D which may have reduced its inhibitory action on these pathways [57]. Nevertheless, results from the multiple inhibitor experiments showed that both dynasore and chlorpromazine, which target clathrin-mediated endocytosis, affected MFGE8 protein secretion from EECs in response to trophoblast cells, thereby strengthening the evidence for the involvement of this endocytic pathway in this specific response.

Early endosomes are the arrival stations for EVs internalized through endocytosis. The molecular contents within these endosomes are typically directed toward lysosomes for degradation. However, endosomes can also fuse with other membranous compartments, such as the plasma membrane [13], endoplasmic reticulum [58] or autophagosomes [59], facilitating the exchange or recycling of molecular material inside EVs. Early endosomes gradually mature into late endosomes or MVBs through the V-ATPase mediated acidification [60]. Endosomal acidification can trigger back fusion of EV membrane with endosomal membrane allowing EV cargo release. Therefore, EV cargo release is considered to be dependent on endosomal pH. It is believed that most MVBs fuse with lysosomes for content degradation, while a small fraction of EVs may escape into the cytosol (< 1%) or be fused with plasma membrane to be re-released as secondary vesicles. Few in vitro assays have been developed to track EV cargo fate, with findings suggesting that direct EV cargo release is an inefficient process [10, 11]. To investigate whether endosomal acidification is necessary for EECs to release MFGE8 in response to JAr EVs, we used Bafilomycin A1, a potent V-ATPase inhibitor that prevents endo-lysosomal acidification. Bafilomycin A1 significantly abolished the increased MFGE8 secretion from EECs in response to trophoblast-derived EVs indicating the importance of endosomal acidification in MFGE8 protein secretion.

Trophoblastic EVs increased MFGE8 protein secretion by RL95-2 cells while MFGE8 production within cells remain unchanged [19]. MFGE8 is a cysteine-rich glycoprotein that is known to be secreted in vesicles [20]. This form of protein secretion is synonymous with unconventional protein secretion, which is partly controlled by autophagic pathway [59, 61]. Interestingly, autophagic pathway is increasingly recognized as novel EV biosynthesis pathway [62]. In unconventional protein secretion, cargoes-carrying autophagosomes can fuse with endosomes, MVBs or secretory lysosomes and are directly transported to the plasma membrane for protein release. To test whether MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells in response to JAr EVs was due to MVB/amphisome fusion with plasma membrane, we used Rab27a inhibitor Nexinhib 20. Inhibition of Rab27a significantly reduced MFGE8 secretion, indicating that Rab27a-dependent vesicle exocytosis is required for this process. Because Rab27a regulates the exocytosis of both MVBs and amphisomes, our findings support the involvement of vesicle-mediated secretion but do not distinguish between these two pathways. Furthermore, treatment with Bafilomycin A1, which inhibits endosomal and lysosomal acidification and disrupts autophagic flux, also significantly reduced MFGE8 secretion from endometrial cells [63]. These finding together suggests that autophagy-dependent vesicle trafficking may also contribute to MFGE8 release, supporting the possibility that amphisome-mediated secretion, in addition to direct MVB exocytosis, participates in this process. However, further studies using pathway-specific approaches are required to distinguish the relative contribution of these two secretion mechanisms.

The release of MFGE8 containing secondary vesicles from endometrial cells in response to JAr EVs is more likely driven by redistribution of pre-existing vesicular pools following EV uptake, rather than by EV cargo–induced de novo signaling and protein synthesis. This interpretation is supported by the rapid kinetics of the response, with an increase in MFGE8-positive vesicles detected as early as 30 min after EV exposure. For an EV cargo-mediated mechanism to explain this effect, the internalized cargo would need to induce MFGE8 gene expression, followed by translation, post-translational modification, and secretion processes that are unlikely to occur within such a short time frame. To further investigate the specificity of this response, we treated EECs with EVs derived from different sources. Interestingly, only JAr-derived EVs significantly increased MFGE8 protein secretion from EECs 30 min after treatment, suggesting a potential tropism between trophoblast and endometrial cells. However, the cellular response observed 24 h post-EV treatment showed a different pattern, although trophoblast EVs continued to exhibit the strongest capacity to induce MFGE8 secretion. Given that plant- and bovine-derived EVs do not generate detectable MFGE8 signals in cell culture supernatant when measured by ELISA, and that MFGE8 was quantified at two time points to capture real-time secretion dynamics, potential confounding from exogenous EV-associated MFGE8, including the low levels detected in HaCaT-derived EVs, is effectively minimized. Accordingly, the observed changes in MFGE8 levels are more likely to reflect endometrial cell responses to EV stimulation rather than passive detection of EV-derived MFGE8, supporting a regulated and specific cellular uptake and signaling mechanism. Non-trophoblastic EV interactions with endometrial cells appear to occur more slowly than those of trophoblast-derived EVs. The rapid interaction of trophoblastic EVs with endometrial cells may be facilitated by a strong affinity between EVs and recipient cell membranes, mediated through specific ligand-receptor interactions on the cell surface facilitating their rapid internalization. These may include molecules such as tetraspanins, integrins, extracellular matrix proteins, lectins, proteoglycans and members of the immunoglobulin superfamily, which are known to be enriched on the surfaces of both endometrial and trophoblast cells [64–66]. In particular, the surface glycosylation profile of EVs has been shown to reflect their cell of origin, and different recipient cell types exhibit preferential uptake of EVs with distinct glycosylation patterns [67]. Recent evidence also suggests that the glycosylation profile of trophoblast cells plays a critical role in their functional activity, raising the possibility that specific interactions between trophoblast and endometrial cells may be partly mediated by these glycan-dependent mechanisms. However, further experimental evidence is required to confirm this hypothesis [68]. In contrast, the delayed response of endometrial cells to bovine milk- and human HaCaT cell derived EVs may reflect the absence of specific receptor–ligand interactions that facilitate efficient recognition and uptake. Consequently, these EVs are likely internalized with lower affinity and slower kinetics, resulting in gradual, nonspecific intracellular accumulation over time. Such accumulation may overload the endocytic machinery, induce cellular stress, and subsequently stimulate the release of MFGE8-enriched EVs later [7, 69]. Therefore, the delayed increase in MFGE8 secretion observed with these EVs is more likely represent a nonspecific cellular response rather than the targeted intercellular communication. Nevertheless, the accumulation of microalgal EVs seems to reduce the MFGE8 release from endometrial cells. This further indicates that the observed increase in MFGE8 secretion from endometrial cells in response to EVs is unlikely to be a simple consequence of vesicle recycling. Instead, EV-specific cargo composition may play a key role in determining the downstream cellular response. Overall, these findings indicated that only specific EV types can trigger secondary vesicle release from EECs. Thus, the ability of trophoblast EVs to induce vesicle release appears to be a unique and targeted mechanism, likely supporting bidirectional communication between the embryo and maternal endometrium. This specificity may, in part, be attributed to distinct cargo profiles carried by different EV types [4].

When exploring the underlying mechanisms of MFGE8 secretion from endometrial cells, TNF-α emerges as an important pro-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic cytokine that plays a significant role in regulating the embryo implantation process. TNFα increases MFGE8 production from EECs cells via NF-κB pathway [70]. Therefore, we detected whether MFGE8 secretion from RL95-2 cells also under the influence of TNFα and activation of NF-κB pathway. Importantly, TNFα has been reported to regulate MFGE8 gene expression and increase MFGE8 protein production in EECs [70]. The lack of a detectable effect of TNFα on MFGE8 secretion in our study further supports the notion that the rapid increase in MFGE8 observed following JAr EV treatment is unlikely to result from de novo protein synthesis. Instead, our findings are more consistent with the release of pre-existing MFGE8 through EV-mediated secretory pathways.

To further understand whether microenvironmental changes influence EV-mediated communication between the embryo and the endometrium, we induced hypoxia in endometrial cells. Interestingly, hypoxia-induced cellular stress led to an increased release of vesicles and MFGE8 from EECs. However, when stressed EECs were exposed to trophoblast-derived EVs, the induction of MFGE8 secretion was markedly reduced compared to that in non-stressed cells, suggesting that EV-mediated signalling is impaired under stress conditions. Although CoCl₂ treatment slightly reduced RL95-2 cell viability, apoptotic bodies and cellular debris were removed during the EV isolation procedure. Nevertheless, given the reduction in cell viability, we cannot completely exclude a minor contribution of cell death-associated particles to the observed increase in particle counts. However, several lines of evidence suggest that this increase predominantly reflects enhanced EV secretion rather than nonspecific particle release. Cellular stress is well established to stimulate EV biogenesis while altering EV cargo composition [69]. Hypoxia particularly is known to stimulate EV biogenesis and release through activation of the hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF1A) pathway [39, 71]. In particular, CoCl₂ stabilizes HIF-1α, which has been reported to promote the localization of RAB27A to MVBs. This facilitates MVB docking and fusion with the plasma membrane, thereby enhancing EV secretion [72, 73]. In addition, HIF1A activation has been reported to downregulate ATP6V1A expression, impairing lysosomal degradation. This shifts the fate of MVBs away from lysosomal fusion and toward fusion with the plasma membrane, further promoting EV release. The HIF2α–Rab27b–EV secretion pathway has also been shown to be conserved in primary human endometrial stromal cells and is activated during pregnancy in response to the hypoxic microenvironment [74]. Hypoxia-induced EVs released by endometrial stromal cells have been reported to carry MMP-9, thereby promoting luminal epithelium remodelling to facilitate embryo invasion. Based on these findings, it is plausible that HIF2α, in addition to HIF1α, may also contribute to the regulation of EV biogenesis and secretion in EECs. However, the role of HIF2α in controlling EV release from EECs has not yet been investigated and warrants further study. Consequently, the response of hypoxic endometrial cells to trophoblast-derived EVs may be altered due to a shift of cells toward stress-adaptive EV secretion programs. In addition to impaired EV-mediated signalling under hypoxic conditions, it is possible that hypoxia and trophoblast-derived EVs independently activate overlapping EV secretion pathways. As hypoxia alone already enhances EV secretion, hypoxic RL95-2 cells may have approached their maximal secretory capacity. Consequently, subsequent stimulation with trophoblast-derived EVs may produce only a modest additional increase in MFGE8 secretion compared with non-hypoxic cells, where the basal secretory activity is lower and a greater dynamic response is possible. Hypoxia may also influence EV-cell interactions by inducing post-translational modifications such as of N-linked and O-linked glycosylation of membrane proteins [75]. Importantly, previous studies have shown that hypoxia can modify the glycosylation of membrane proteins, such as integrins [76] and CD133 [77]. As glycosylation of membrane proteins can regulates protein trafficking, stability, receptor assembly, and signalling, these modifications may change the composition and functional properties of the cell surface. Consequently, altered glycosylation of receptors and other membrane proteins could affect EV recognition, binding, and internalization, thereby modifying the cellular response to trophoblast-derived EVs.

In our previous work, we demonstrated that MFGE8 release from EECs in response to trophoblast EVs depends on factors such as EV dose, EV-cell interaction time, and the microenvironment of the donor cells [19, 24]. In the present study, we further reveal that EV type and the stress status of the recipient cells also influence this process. Overall, EV-mediated bidirectional communication appears to be regulated by a complex interplay of factors, including trophoblast cell characteristics, EV cargo composition, the physiological state of endometrial cells, and the integrity of the endocytic pathway. It is noteworthy that intracellular vesicle trafficking involved in protein secretion is linked to various cellular functions, including extracellular matrix remodelling, cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [78–80]. Similarly, altered expression of Rab proteins has been shown to affect the polarity of EECs, contributing to their remodelling and preparation for embryo implantation [74]. Therefore, it can be proposed that EV uptake triggers secondary vesicle release as a cellular mechanism to redistribute proteins and lipids within the plasma membrane.

Future studies should aim to determine whether the functional specificity of EVs is primarily exerted at the level of EV uptake or during the process of secondary vesicle release. Understanding this distinction is crucial for deciphering the mechanisms behind selective intercellular communication. Early endosomes serve as key sites for initial EV cargo sorting and the formation of intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) under the influence of endosomal sorting complex required for transport proteins (ESCRT), which are precursors of secondary vesicles [81, 82]. A deeper investigation into how EV-derived cargo is processed within endosomal compartments, particularly the molecular machinery involved in cargo sorting and ILVs formation, could reveal how secondary vesicles are selectively loaded with target molecules. This knowledge would provide important insight into how the endometrial cells repackage and relay signals back to the trophoblast cells, potentially uncovering new layers of regulation in embryo-maternal communication during implantation. Although the in vitro embryo implantation model used in the current study, comprising JAr and RL95-2 cells, has long been established as a representative model of the pre-implantation microenvironment [18, 83, 84], validating these findings using primary trophoblast and endometrial cells would further strengthen their physiological and translational relevance. Nevertheless, the present study provides important insight into the mechanisms by which EVs influence recipient cells and may support the future development of EV-based diagnostic and therapeutic strategies to improve assisted reproductive technologies as well. It is also important to note that several of the EV uptake inhibitors used in the study are known to exhibit off-target effects, and their specificity in selectively inhibiting particular pathways has been questioned. For example, chlorpromazine primarily targets clathrin-mediated endocytosis, but it may also exert off-target effects on clathrin-independent endocytic pathways. Similarly, dynasore mainly inhibits clathrin-mediated endocytosis, although it can additionally affect caveolin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, and phagocytosis. Genistein has also been reported to partially inhibit caveolin-mediated endocytosis; however, its specificity remains unclear. Furthermore, cytochalasin D interferes with actin polymerization, thereby affecting multiple endocytic pathways simultaneously. This represents a limitation of the present study [30]. To overcome this limitation, knockout of key components involved in EV uptake pathways or overexpression of dominant-negative inhibitors, together with appropriate controls, could be employed in future studies [85].

Conclusion

In this study, we used unmodified EVs and considered the existence of targeted communication between trophoblast-derived EVs and EECs. By utilizing MFGE8 secretion as a functional readout of EV activity, we aimed to elucidate the precise mechanisms by which EVs are processed inside recipient cells. This approach offers a valuable opportunity to gain deeper insight into the selective nature of EV-cell interactions. In this study, we demonstrated the mechanism by which trophoblast EV uptake in EECs leads to the subsequent release of secondary vesicles. Interestingly, this response of recipient cells to EVs appears to be dependent on the cellular origin of EVs, providing strong evidence for the existence of EV tropism. From a clinical perspective, understanding how EECs respond to trophoblast-derived EVs is crucial for exploring potential strategies to enhance implantation success and treat infertility.
