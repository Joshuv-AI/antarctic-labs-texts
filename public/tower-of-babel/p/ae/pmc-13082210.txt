Severe Hypertension and Hematologic Abnormalities in Pseudoxanthoma Elasticum: A Pediatric Case Report
Authors: Hana Flogelova, Dagmar Pospisilova, Lenka Bakaj‐Zbrozkova, Lucie Sochorcova, Jirina Manakova, Eva Kriegova, Pavla Koralkova, Katarina Hlusickova Kapralova, Monika Horvathova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in ABCC6, leading to progressive calcification of elastic fibers. Although PXE typically presents in adolescence with dermatologic or ocular manifestations, early vascular involvement, including pediatric hypertension, is increasingly recognized. We report a 3‐year‐old boy referred for evaluation of persistent erythrocytosis of unclear etiology. Clinical, biochemical, and hematologic assessments revealed intermittent reticulocytosis, normal oxygen saturation, and suppressed/normal erythropoietin levels. Long‐term zinc supplementation initiated in infancy for suspected dermatitis enteropathica preceded the onset of erythrocytosis. At age eight, severe systemic hypertension was diagnosed together with medullary nephrocalcinosis and splenic and pancreatic calcifications. Imaging excluded renal artery stenosis. Whole‐exome sequencing identified compound heterozygous pathogenic ABCC6 variants, establishing the diagnosis of PXE. Ophthalmologic examination revealed peau d'orange. Blood pressure control required combination antihypertensive therapy and was associated with partial normalization of red blood cell (RBC) parameters and a marked increase in plasma renin activity. RBC enzymatic profiling demonstrated metabolic adaptations consistent with oxidative stress. This case illustrates an unusual clinical presentation of PXE in early childhood. We propose that erythrocytosis, potentially promoted by zinc supplementation, may have contributed to early vascular stress and hypertension. Later, renal microvascular calcification and renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system activation may have contributed to the persistence of elevated RBC counts despite low erythropoietin. These observations suggest that PXE may be considered in the differential diagnosis of children with unexplained hypertension, particularly when additional systemic findings are present.

Introduction

Severe and treatment‐resistant hypertension in children is rare and usually secondary to an underlying condition. The younger the child and the more severe the blood pressure increase, the more likely a secondary cause exists. This commonly includes renovascular or renal parenchymal diseases, endocrine disorders, and congenital heart problems like aortic coarctation. While genetic factors can play a role in essential hypertension among adolescents, especially with a family history, monogenic hypertension, such as Liddle syndrome or familial hyperaldosteronism, is rare [1]. Therefore, genetic testing is not routinely performed in most pediatric hypertension cases unless clinical signs indicate a genetic syndrome.

Erythrocytosis is defined by elevated hemoglobin and hematocrit, reflecting either a true increase in red cell mass (absolute erythrocytosis) or reduced plasma volume without increased red cell mass (relative erythrocytosis) [2]. Absolute erythrocytosis may be caused by intrinsic bone marrow disorders such as polycythemia vera or primary familial and congenital erythrocytosis or driven by elevated erythropoietin (EPO) in response to chronic hypoxia, tumors, certain drugs, or rare congenital defects in oxygen‐sensing and hemoglobin oxygen affinity. Relative erythrocytosis is typically due to dehydration or volume contraction. In pediatric patients, distinguishing between these forms is essential but can be difficult, especially when EPO levels are normal and oxygenation remains intact [2].

Pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE; OMIM #264800) is a rare autosomal recessive metabolic disorder of connective tissue calcification, primarily caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in the ABCC6 gene. ABCC6 encodes a transmembrane transporter that facilitates ATP efflux from hepatocytes. ATP is subsequently hydrolyzed by ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) to produce inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi), a potent inhibitor of soft tissue and vascular calcification [3, 4]. Loss of ABCC6 function reduces circulating PPi levels, thereby predisposing to ectopic mineralization (Figure S1) [5, 6].

The clinical manifestations typically include characteristic skin lesions (yellowish papules in flexural areas), ocular findings such as peau d'orange, angioid streaks, and vascular complications related to progressive arterial calcification. PXE is usually diagnosed during adolescence or adulthood; the estimated prevalence ranges from 1 in 25 000 to 1 in 100 000 individuals [7, 8]. While life expectancy in PXE is generally normal, morbidity can be substantial due to extracutaneous complications, particularly vision‐threatening ocular disease and progressive vascular involvement that may lead to cerebrovascular events or myocardial infarction.

PXE shares pathogenic mechanisms with other inherited calcification disorders, most notably generalized arterial calcification of infancy (GACI), a life‐threatening condition that typically appears in the neonatal period with widespread arterial calcification, heart failure, and often early death [9, 10]. GACI results from mutations in ENPP1, although pathogenic ABCC6 variants have also been reported. Survivors of ABCC6‐associated GACI may later develop PXE‐like features, suggesting a shared pathophysiological spectrum [11]. Other related syndromes include arterial calcification caused by CD73 deficiency (encoded by the NT5E gene) and familial tumoral calcinosis caused by defects in fibroblast growth factor‐23 (FGF23) or polypeptide N‐acetylgalactosaminyltransferase 3.

Here, we report a unique case of a boy with biallelic pathogenic ABCC6 variants presenting in early childhood with severe hypertension and unexplained erythrocytosis, preceding any visible skin lesions. This case illustrates that hematologic abnormalities may complicate the clinical course of PXE.

Methods

Clinical and Biochemical Analyses

Clinical history and laboratory data were collected. The patient was referred for additional specialized hematological, cardiological, pulmonological, nephrological, and ophthalmological examinations. Serum EPO levels and additional biochemical parameters in the serum, plasma, and urine were measured by standard methods. FGF23 and erythroferrone serum concentrations were measured with ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions (Biomedica Immunoassays and Intrinsic LifeSciences, respectively).

Genetic Testing

Genomic DNA was used for whole‐exome sequencing and Sanger sequencing. Preparation of the DNA library (50 ng of genomic DNA) and whole exome capture was performed using the Human Core Exome Kit (Twist Bioscience, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions, followed by next‐generation sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina Inc., CA, USA). A minimum depth coverage of 90× was achieved for all samples. After removing reads containing sequencing adapters and low‐quality reads, high‐quality reads were aligned to the human genome reference sequence (hg38) using the Burrows‐Wheeler Aligner (https://hpc.nih.gov/apps/bwa.html.gov). After sorting the reads using Samtools (https://www.htslib.org/), the Genome Analysis Toolkit (version 3.8; https://software.broadinstitute.org/gatk/) software was used to detect PCR duplicates and to call variants. Annotation of called single‐nucleotide polymorphisms and small insertions/deletions was performed using the variant effect predictor (https://ensembl.org/info/docs/tools/vep/index.html). Variants that fulfilled the QScore (minimum threshold of 30) were manually checked using the Integrative genome viewer (https://igv.org) and annotated using clinical databases/tools (COSMIC, ClinVar, Ensemble Variant Effect Predictor tool). Only pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants as assessed by ACMG criteria were reported. The presence of ABCC6 mutations was confirmed by targeted Sanger sequencing using purified PCR products (SEQme company, Dobris, Czech Republic).

Red Cell Analyses

The activity of erythrocyte enzymes involved in the pentose phosphate pathway and oxidative defense was determined according to the methods recommended by the International Committee for Standardization in Hematology [12] as we previously described [13]. The levels of reactive oxygen species in erythrocytes was measured using 0.4 mM 2′,7′‐dichlorofluorescein (Sigma‐Aldrich, Darmstadt, Germany) and flow cytometry (FACS Calibur, BD Bioscience, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA) [14].

Results

Case Presentation

The proband is a male child, born at 32 weeks of gestation (birth weight 1600 g) from the second pregnancy; his older sister is healthy. The neonatal course was uncomplicated, and the mild psychomotor delay resolved with early neurorehabilitation. From three months of age, he was treated with zinc supplementation (20 mg/day) for suspected dermatitis enteropathica and low serum zinc (1.0 µmol/L; normal 10.3–18.1). Within one month, zinc normalized, and skin lesions improved.

At age three, erythrocytosis was identified during routine follow‐up (RBC 6.5 × 101 2/L, hemoglobin 16.3 g/dL, hematocrit 0.45, reticulocytes 109.6 × 109/L). Lip and hand plethora were observed. Serum EPO was decreased (3.7 IU/L; normal 5.4–28.4), while pulse oximetry remained normal. Erythrocytosis with intermittent reticulocytosis and low EPO persisted for over 5 years. Although the parents declined red cell mass isotope testing, the stable hematocrit‐to‐hemoglobin ratio did not suggest hemoconcentration. Erythroid progenitor assays showed normal growth and no hypersensitivity to EPO (Figure S2A), indicating a secondary cause. Serum erythroferrone, a marker of stress erythropoiesis, was undetectable (not shown) [15]. Bone marrow biopsy revealed normal erythropoiesis (30% erythroblasts) with no pathological features. Targeted sequencing of known erythrocytosis‐related genes was negative.

At age eight, an abdominal ultrasound revealed medullary nephrocalcinosis and splenic calcifications (Figure 1). At the same time, severe systemic hypertension was diagnosed: blood pressure (BP) 193/159 mmHg, >95th percentile, whereas earlier pediatric records (ages 3, 5, and 7) showed normal BP. The boy was asymptomatic, with height and BMI at the 25th–29th percentiles. Echocardiography showed left ventricular hypertrophy; aortic coarctation was excluded. Biochemistry excluded hypercortisolism, hyperaldosteronism, and hyperthyroidism (Table 1). Kidney function and electrolytes were normal, plasma renin activity (PRA) was at the upper normal limit, and urinalysis showed mild calciuria and intermittent oxaluria. Proteinuria and albuminuria were absent. MR/CT angiography ruled out renal artery stenosis. Imaging confirmed medullary nephrocalcinosis and splenic and pancreatic calcifications (Figure 1). Ophthalmology revealed hypertensive neuroretinopathy, partially reversible after BP control.

Genetic Analysis and Additional Tests

Given the complex and unexplained phenotypic findings, additional detailed genetic analysis was performed. Whole‐exome sequencing revealed compound heterozygosity for pathogenic ABCC6 variants: c.3421C>T (p.R1141*) and c.2252T>A (p.M751K), establishing the diagnosis of PXE. The variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Figure S2B), but parental segregation analysis was declined. The p.R1141* is the most frequent PXE mutation in Europeans, leading to premature truncation and haploinsufficiency [8, 16]. The p.M751K variant lies within the nucleotide‐binding domain 1 near the ABC signature motif, and is likely to impair ATP binding/hydrolysis [8]. No erythrocytosis‐associated mutations were identified. Additional heterozygous variants in recessive disease genes (Table S1) were unlikely to explain the phenotype. Ophthalmologic follow‐up with fundus autofluorescence revealed bilateral peau d'orange, particularly in the macula.

Because elevated FGF23 is implicated in the early vascular calcification and hypertension in ENPP1‐related GACI [17], we measured serum C‐terminal FGF23 in our patient. Levels were within the normal range (1.8 pg/L; control range 0.2–5.2 pg/L, n = 8), excluding a major role of FGF23 in vascular calcification and hypertension in our patient.

Treatment and Follow‐Up

Antihypertensive therapy began with amlodipine, followed by enalapril (after excluding renovascular stenosis). Later, hydrochlorothiazide and rilmenidine were added, alongside dietary salt/oxalate restriction. BP control was achieved at home (<95th percentile), though 24‐h ABPM at 11 years confirmed persistent systolic hypertension (day 132/62 mmHg vs. 127/81; night 118/54 mmHg vs. 114/66). Hematologic indices initially normalized with BP control, but later RBC counts increased and fluctuated around the upper limit of normal (Table 1 and Figure 2A‐C), together with persistently low EPO (Table 1). A significant rise in PRA during angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibition (above the measurable range) (Table 1) was likely related to treatment rather than disease progression. Aldosterone remained within the normal range (Table 1). The current regimen involves amlodipine, ramipril, nebivolol, and urapidil.

Over a period of 2.5 years, calcifications in the kidney, spleen, and pancreas remained stable. Carotid intima‐media thickness was elevated at 0.644 mm (>97th percentile; mean ± SD: 0.44 ± 0.06 mm for 10–12 years) [18]. Kidney function was preserved. Lipids were mildly elevated, calciuria/oxaluria/phosphaturia intermittently increased, and proteinuria and albuminuria were absent.

Erythrocyte Assays and Idiopathic Erythrocytosis

To investigate the potential pathology of erythrocytes contributing to persistently elevated RBC counts, erythrocyte enzyme assays were performed. Although the absolute enzyme activities were within the normal range, diagnostic ratios of glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase/gluconolactone dehydrogenase, pyruvate kinase/hexokinase, and glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase/hexokinase were increased (Table 2). These ratio‐based analyses are particularly informative in the presence of reticulocytosis, which can mask subtle defects when evaluating absolute enzyme activities [12]. In addition, erythrocytes showed increased reactive oxygen species levels (Table 2). Together, these findings indicate oxidative and metabolic stress with compensatory upregulation of glycolysis and redox balance.

Given the idiopathic erythrocytosis at age 3, we re‐examined zinc therapy. Literature supports zinc as a stimulator of erythropoiesis [19, 20]. Review of records revealed that at baseline, zinc was markedly reduced, while RBC parameters were normal. Zinc supplementation (20 mg/day) normalized its serum levels within one month, with RBC indices within the normal range. During continued therapy, zinc remained within the reference range, but RBC indices gradually rose. After the dose was doubled to 20 mg twice daily, serum zinc exceeded the normal range and coincided with the peak of erythrocytosis (Figure 2A‐C). Zinc absorption from capsules is ∼50%–60% [21], representing nearly tenfold the recommended daily intake for infants and children up to 3 years of age (2–3 mg/day).

Discussion

PXE, caused by pathogenic ABCC6 variants, is classically associated with progressive calcification of elastic fibers in skin, eyes, and vasculature. Hypertension has been reported in some pediatric patients with PXE. However, due to several other clinical and developmental abnormalities reported in these patients [22, 23], the direct association between ABCC6 deficiency and blood pressure regulation has not been proven.

Hypertension in PXE is thought to result from calcification of medium‐sized arteries, including renal vessels, within the tunica media. This process increases stiffness, impairs vasodilation and may elevate systemic vascular resistance and activate the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) [24]. In our patient, plasma renin activity was already at the upper limit of normal at the time of hypertension diagnosis, suggesting early RAAS involvement despite preserved glomerular filtration and absence of renovascular stenosis. The marked rise in renin observed during follow‐up is most likely attributable to chronic ACE inhibitor therapy rather than progressive renal ischemia. Similar vascular dysfunction has been observed in Abcc6−/− mice [25]. Notably, the accompanying erythrocytosis may have exacerbated vascular stress through increased viscosity and impaired microcirculatory flow [26], thereby potentially contributing to the early hypertensive phenotype observed in this child. From a clinical perspective, this case highlights the diagnostic challenges posed by severe hypertension in children when classical manifestations of rare systemic disorders have not yet become apparent.

Erythrocytosis is not considered a defining feature of PXE. In our patient, extensive genetic and functional testing excluded known primary forms and oxygen‐sensing or hypoxia related defects. While an as‐yet unidentified genetic or molecular mechanism cannot be fully excluded, the combined genetic, functional, and clinical data strongly support a secondary, acquired origin of erythrocytosis, further supported by its partial mitigation following BP control.

We propose a two‐phase acquired mechanism contributing to elevated RBC counts in this patient. In early childhood, prolonged zinc supplementation likely increased erythropoietic drive, consistent with prior studies demonstrating stimulatory effects of zinc on erythropoiesis [19, 20]. Later in the disease course, when the zinc treatment was ceased, renal microvascular calcification and functional RAAS activation may have contributed to the persistence of mildly elevated RBC counts despite persistently low EPO levels. Angiotensin II has been shown to directly stimulate erythroid progenitors via AT1 receptors, enhance sensitivity to EPO, and activate hypoxia‐inducible factor signaling even under normoxic conditions [26, 27]. Splenic calcifications observed in our patient may have further contributed by impairing clearance of senescent erythrocytes.

Erythrocyte studies revealed increased glycolytic and antioxidant enzyme activity ratios together with elevated reactive oxygen species. These findings likely reflect compensatory adaptations to systemic oxidative stress and microcirculatory disturbances rather than a primary red cell disorder. Erythrocytes are increasingly recognized as active participants in vascular homeostasis, and alterations in their metabolic and redox state have been described in hypertension and vascular disease [28]. Because hypertension and arterial stiffness represent key manifestations of vascular involvement in PXE, the erythrocyte metabolic adaptations observed in our patient may plausibly reflect secondary responses to chronic vascular stress. Whether such alterations represent a broader feature of ABCC6 deficiency warrants further investigation in larger PXE cohorts.

Currently, no disease‐specific treatment for PXE is available, although several approaches targeting ectopic mineralization pathways (Figure S1) (e.g., bisphosphonates and magnesium supplementation, TNAP inhibitors or recombinant ENPP1) are under investigation [29, 30, 31].

In conclusion, this case illustrates that complex vascular–renal–hematologic interactions can significantly influence the clinical course of PXE. While these findings are limited to a single case, they highlight the potential for systemic interactions to modify the clinical phenotype of the disease. Our observations suggest that ABCC6‐related disease may be considered in the differential diagnosis of children with unexplained, treatment‐resistant hypertension, particularly when multisystemic involvement is suspected, to facilitate earlier diagnosis and comprehensive management in such complex presentations.
