Interrogating perceived relevance and feasibility of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis: A novel model of PrEP acceptability among cisgender women who inject drugs
Authors: Leanne Zhang, Joseph G. Rosen, Lyra Cooper, Praise F. Olatunde, Danielle Pelaez, Susan G. Sherman, Ju Nyeong Park, Jennifer L. Glick
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Uptake of daily oral HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) remains low among cisgender women who inject drugs (WWID) in the U.S., who experience elevated sexual- and injection-mediated HIV exposure risks. While anticipated barriers to PrEP uptake have been explored, further work is needed to situate PrEP acceptability (i.e., PrEP interest and willingness) within the context of WWID’s lives and the HIV risk environment.Methods:We conducted semi-structured, in-depth interviews (N = 27) with cisgender WWID in Baltimore, Maryland, between April–September 2021. Interviews explored women’s daily survival needs, substance use practices, healthcare experiences, HIV risk perception, and PrEP perspectives. Thematic analysis revealed complex dimensions of WWID’s health perspectives and HIV risk conceptualization, from which a Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability and a novel construct of ‘perceived PrEP relevance’ were developed using a grounded theory approach.Results:In our Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability, anticipated PrEP feasibility and perceived PrEP relevance shape women’s interest in and willingness to initiate PrEP. Attending to PrEP-related healthcare tasks (e. g., medical appointments, bloodwork) and managing a daily medication regimen were anticipated as feasible but would likely be de-prioritized relative to women’s competing substance-related and survival needs. Women’s perceptions of both their locus of HIV risk and sufficiency of existing HIV prevention practices determined their perceived PrEP relevance. PrEP was least relevant for women perceiving their prevention practices as sufficient to mitigate self-controlled HIV risk and most relevant for women perceiving their prevention practices as insufficient to mitigate environmentally shaped HIV risk.Conclusions:Our models demonstrate how PrEP acceptability is influenced by WWID’s lived experiences and complex risk conceptualization. Effective multilevel interventions to enhance PrEP engagement should support women’s competing needs, affirm women’s existing prevention strategies, and address the harms of the HIV risk environment.

Introduction

A decade after the approval of daily oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) – a self-managed, biomedical HIV prevention method – PrEP use among cisgender women remains low relative to eligibility. In the United States (U.S.), cisgender women represent nearly 20% of new HIV diagnoses but only 8% of active PrEP users, and PrEP is prescribed to merely 10% of eligible women (AIDSVu, 2022; Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2021; National Center for HIV, Viral Hepatitis, STD and TB Prevention, 2021). This incongruity is even more pronounced in Baltimore City, where women account for 25% of new HIV diagnoses (Center for HIV Surveillance, Epidemiology, and Evaluation, 2021), but only 4% of PrEP-indicated cisgender women are prescribed PrEP (Schumacher et al., 2021).

Cisgender women who inject drugs (WWID) experience elevated HIV risk relative to their male counterparts (Des Jarlais, Feelemyer, Modi, Arasteh, & Hagan, 2012). They are hyper-vulnerabilized to both sexual- and injection-mediated HIV transmission risk via heightened frequencies of violence victimization by romantic and sexual partners (Iversen, Page, Madden, & Maher, 2015; Park et al., 2019), receptive syringe sharing (Iversen et al., 2015; Park et al., 2019; Walters, Reilly, Neaigus, & Braunstein, 2017), transactional sex (Brookmeyer, Haderxhanaj, Hogben, & Leichliter, 2019; Iversen et al., 2015), and challenges negotiating condom use (Decker et al., 2020; Walters et al., 2017). Given this constellation of overlapping HIV exposure risks, PrEP is a promising HIV prevention tool for this population.

Among WWID in the U.S., PrEP awareness remains low but is increasing, and PrEP willingness is high (Roth et al., 2019; Walters et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2019). In Baltimore, 65% of WWID report interest in taking PrEP after learning about it (Sherman et al., 2019). However, translating women’s interest in and willingness to initiate PrEP – termed “PrEP acceptability” (Glick et al., 2020; Guy et al., 2022) – into actual PrEP uptake remains a significant hurdle; few studies (Felsher et al., 2020; Roth et al., 2021) have been able to demonstrate or assess real-world PrEP uptake and continuation among WWID in the U.S. (Baldwin, Light, & Allison, 2021; Glick et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2021).

The importance of considering the structural determinants of HIV in research and intervention design is widely recognized (Kippax, 2008; Shannon, Goldenberg, Deering, & Strathdee, 2014). However, characterizations of the persistent gap between PrEP acceptability and uptake among WWID have largely focused on individual, behavioral, and interpersonal factors (Baldwin et al., 2021; Blankenship, Reinhard, Sherman, & El-Bassel, 2015; Li et al., 2022; Rhodes, Singer, Bourgois, Friedman, & Strathdee, 2005). Low perceived HIV risk (Bazzi et al., 2018; Biello et al., 2018; Rutledge, Madden, Ogbuagu, & Meyer, 2018), concerns about side effects (Biello et al., 2018; Bradley et al., 2019), medication costs (Glick et al., 2020; Tran et al., 2021), daily pill requirements (Shrestha & Copenhaver, 2018; Tran et al., 2021), and barriers to healthcare engagement (e.g., lack of insurance, transportation challenges) (Biello et al., 2018; Bradley et al., 2019; Roth et al., 2018) are oft-cited explanations.

The HIV risk environment framework (Rhodes, 2002; Rhodes et al., 2005) encourages an analytic focus on the social, economic, and political forces that shape the environments where risks are experienced. These physical and social environments may promote risk by constraining agency or differentially exposing individuals to harms. In this paradigm, macro-level factors (e.g., criminalization of drug use and sex work (Rhodes et al., 2012); neighborhood disinvestment through redlining and urban renewal (Brawner et al., 2022; Cooper, Friedman, Tempalski, & Friedman, 2007); patriarchal violence (Shannon et al., 2008)) interact with micro-level factors (e.g., police surveillance and hyper-incarceration (Kerr, Small, & Wood, 2005; Krusi, Kerr, Taylor, Rhodes, & Shannon, 2016), reduced access to services and employment opportunities (Linton et al., 2013), gender-based violence victimization (Bourgois, Prince, & Moss, 2004; Leddy, Weiss, Yam, & Pulerwitz, 2019)) to influence individual behavior, decision-making, and risk of HIV exposure among WWID in Baltimore.

Situating facilitators and barriers of PrEP uptake among WWID within their lived experiences and the broader HIV risk environment can inform more responsive structural interventions for PrEP provision and HIV prevention. In response, we qualitatively explored how the landscape of WWID’s daily needs and priorities, substance use patterns, and conceptualization of HIV risk shape the acceptability of daily oral PrEP.

Methods

The Optimizing PrEP Engagement among Women Living in Baltimore City (OPAL) study was a formative research study that qualitatively explored barriers and facilitators of PrEP engagement among HIV-vulnerabilized cisgender WWID in Baltimore. Findings informed the development and implementation of a PrEP capacity-building intervention delivered to harm reduction workers at local agencies (Cooper et al., 2023; Glick et al., 2023; Rosen, Zhang, Pelaez, Park, & Glick, 2023; Rosen, Glick, et al., 2022; Rosen, Zhang, et al., 2022).

Sampling and recruitment

We conducted semi-structured in-depth interviews (N = 27) with cisgender WWID in Baltimore, Maryland, between April and September 2021. Interviews were conducted both via telephone (April–September; n = 8) and in-person (June–August; n = 19), in response to COVID-19-related research constraints.

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling via 1) flyers distributed by harm reduction organizations in Baltimore, or 2) inperson interception near mobile syringe service program sites. Eligible participants: 1) were aged ≥18 years, 2) identified as a cis-woman, 3) injected any non-prescribed drugs in the past six months, 4) selfreported HIV-negative or unknown serostatus, 5) were English-speaking, and 6) were able to provide verbal informed consent. Callers to the study phone were informed about study procedures and screened for eligibility; eligible participants were interviewed over the phone. Interested individuals intercepted in-person were screened for eligibility, and those eligible were interviewed on the study vehicle or at a semi-private outdoor location.

Data collection

Each 30- to 60-min interview was conducted by one of four trained qualitative interviewers (LZ, JGR, LC, PFO) using a semi-structured interview guide exploring participants’: 1) day-to-day needs and priorities; 2) substance use experiences; 3) health and social service engagement; 4) HIV risk perceptions; and 5) knowledge of, attitudes towards, interest in, and experiences with daily oral PrEP. Interviewers also administered a brief structured survey measuring sociodemographic, substance use, behavioral, and PrEP-related characteristics.

Participant recruitment continued until saturation on PrEP-related domains was achieved (i.e., no new information or novel themes emerged), which was assessed in real time via regular team-based review and weekly interview debriefs during the data collection period. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. Transcriptions were spot-checked against recordings for accuracy. Finalized transcripts were uploaded into Dedoose 9.0.17 (Los Angeles, SocioCultural Research Consultants, LLC) for coding and analysis.

WWID provided verbal informed consent prior to study enrollment. Participants were compensated with a prepaid $50 Visa card. The study was approved by the [blinded for review] Institutional Review Board.

Data analysis

We employed team-based inductive and deductive approaches to synthesize data and identify salient themes (Guest & MacQueen, 2008). Three team members (LZ, JGR, LC) first generated open codes using a subset of four transcripts. Initial codes were collapsed into focused codes and organized by interview guide domains to produce the preliminary coding scheme, which was piloted by four coders (LZ, JGR, LC, PFO) on a second subset of four transcripts. The codebook was then updated to address inconsistencies and reflect newly emerging or refined themes, facilitated by team discussion and analytic memo-writing. The final codebook was applied to each transcript (LZ, LC, PFO), and all coding was reviewed for consistency and codebook fidelity (JGR).

Guided by the principles of grounded theory (Charmaz, 2014), the first author (LZ) further mapped and compared thematic content to generate a conceptual model from the data. Coded text segments relevant to 1) PrEP awareness and knowledge, 2) anticipated or actual barriers and facilitators of PrEP engagement, 3) perceived HIV risk, 4) healthcare engagement, and 5) women’s daily lives were reviewed and constantly compared by the first author to identify conceptual relationships. Emergent findings were further interrogated using analytic memos and continuous comparison against coded text segments within and across transcripts (Ayres, Kavanaugh, & Knafl, 2016). Resultant theories were refined via comparison with existing literature and frameworks and with negative cases within the dataset until they were determined to be satisfactorily comprehensive, explanatory, and representative of the data. Two theoretical contributions, the Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability and the construct of ‘perceived PrEP relevance,’ emerged from this analysis and are presented in the Results.

Results

These results first describe characteristics of the sample and findings on women’s PrEP awareness. We then present and demonstrate the Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability, a theoretical model of PrEP acceptability among WWID with two primary domains: 1) anticipated PrEP feasibility, and 2) ‘perceived PrEP relevance.’ We describe each element of the model in turn, demonstrating how the constructs capture and illustrate the cognitive and behavioral dimensions of PrEP acceptability and their interplay with the HIV risk environment for WWID.

Sample characteristics

Select demographic and behavioral characteristics from the sample of cisgender women (N = 27) are presented in Table 1. The mean age was 39 years (range: 21–60 years), and most women identified as non-Hispanic white (67%), followed by non-Hispanic Black (26%) and American Indian or Alaska Native (15%). Two women identified as Hispanic or Latina (7%). Most women had used opioids (96%) or stimulants (93%) in the past month and had injected drugs in the past month (78%), 71% of whom reported daily injection. Sharing injection equipment was less frequent (30%). Among women who reported being sexually active in the past six months (93%), most reported condomless sex (72%). Many women reported >10 past-month sexual partners (56%), and sex exchange within the past month was common (74%). Many women had received a sexually transmitted infection (STI) diagnosis, both ever (70%) and in the past year (19%).

PrEP awareness was high, but accurate PrEP knowledge was mixed

While most women were aware of PrEP (93%), only one had ever taken it herself. Women’s self-reported PrEP knowledge ranged from simply having heard of PrEP (30%) to knowing a fair amount or a lot about PrEP (19%) (Table 1). Most women described PrEP as something used to prevent HIV, and some women were able to accurately describe PrEP in more detail.

“I just know that [PrEP is] a daily pill that you take when you are involved in risky behavior, like gay men that have anal sex … behaviors that are easier to get HIV from, PrEP can help prevent it.”

Others were less knowledgeable about the purpose of PrEP, with some conflating it with medication for HIV treatment.

“I have a foster sister who’s HIV-positive. I don’t know if she takes PrEP, but she does take one medication every day, and she says she’s undetectable.”

Presenting a psychosocial model of PrEP acceptability

Women’s descriptions of their day-to-day priorities and lived experiences; their anticipated barriers and facilitators of PrEP engagement; and the level, nature, and rationale for their HIV risk perceptions guided development of a new theoretical model to understand PrEP acceptability among WWID: the Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability (Fig. 1).

Overall PrEP acceptability (i.e., interest in or willingness to take PrEP) was shaped by two primary constructs – 1) anticipated PrEP feasibility and 2) perceived PrEP relevance – all embedded within the HIV risk environment (Fig. 1).

Anticipated PrEP feasibility

Anticipated PrEP feasibility describes how logistically achievable women felt it would be to both i) attend to the ancillary healthcare tasks required for PrEP engagement (e.g., scheduling and attending provider visits, pick up prescriptions, completing routine bloodwork), and ii) manage a daily oral medication regimen (e.g., adherence, side effect concerns, medication storage).

PrEP-related health tasks are anticipated to be achievable but may be outcompeted by other needs

Most women reported that the requisite health tasks for PrEP engagement would be achievable. Some women described how these tasks could fit into their existing health-seeking routines – many had other prescriptions, some were already getting routine bloodwork, and some had well-established relationships and regular visits with healthcare providers.

“I get my medication; they deliver it to me. And I go to dialysis three days a week.”

“I have a primary care doctor I go see. I go back there […] every couple months.”

Most women felt that completing bloodwork requirements for PrEP would be feasible, even easy. However, a few cited negative past experiences when phlebotomists had struggled to access their veins, a common challenge for people who inject drugs (Summers, Struve, Wilkes, & Rees, 2017).

“I couldn’t do a blood test; they just couldn’t get my blood. I went to two different places [to do it], and it just wasn’t happening.”

While women largely perceived these ancillary PrEP engagement tasks as feasible, some acknowledged that their competing priorities and the uncertainties of their lives as people who use illicit drugs may preclude them from completing these tasks in actuality. One woman, who described many existing healthcare engagement practices (i.e., having medical assistance, routinely picking up suboxone prescriptions, and regularly seeing her doctor), shared how her substance use-related needs had still superseded her healthcare-seeking intentions in the past:

“I was ill, and I wanted to go to the doctor, but I wasn’t well [experiencing withdrawal]. So I had to get well first [use drugs to alleviate withdrawal], and then by the time I got well, it was too late. They were closed.”

Other women similarly reflected on their daily triaging of needs, in which addressing withdrawal symptoms and attending to survival needs often took precedence over other self-care priorities. When asked about what was important for her to get done in a typical day, one woman responded:

“Food, cigarettes. My drugs. Just mainly those three things. Like I think every day I stress out about how I need to do laundry and never end up getting it done.”

Most women identified “getting well” (i.e., using opioids to alleviate withdrawal symptoms) as their main daily priority, and earning money to purchase drugs – frequently by selling sex – was often a close second. Some women also identified looking for shelter as an important daily task. For many women, managing their substance use and monetary needs occupied the entirety of their day-to-day lives. When asked to describe a typical day, one woman responded:

“Make money. Keep money going. Like, it really sucks because you’re always worried about being sick. So that’s what my day is. It’s just trying to keep from being sick. And making more money to keep going. And that’s it, that’s all I do.

Many women echoed these sentiments, describing how their substance-related needs created “a cycle of the same shit every day” and how “getting high [is] a full-time job.” These characterizations of women’s daily routines depicted just how all-consuming their substance-related needs were – physically, temporally, and psychologically.

Against this backdrop, the actual feasibility of completing PrEP-related health tasks is likely to depend heavily on women’s relative social and economic stability and substance use intensity at a given point in time.

“Now, if I’m really out in the streets getting high, I don’t know if I’ll be making it [to a PrEP provider visit] every three months.”

Maintaining a daily oral medication regimen was largely anticipated to be manageable

The second factor shaping anticipated PrEP feasibility was management of a daily oral medication regimen. While most women reported that daily adherence to oral PrEP “would be easy” and “wouldn’t be hard to remember,” a few anticipated that they wouldn’t take it consistently. As one woman quipped, “I can’t even remember to take my own damn vitamins.” – Participant #21.

Some women expressed a generalized disdain for taking medications, regardless of their indication.

“I just probably wouldn’t want to [take PrEP]. I don’t like taking medications that much.”

Others expressed concerns over potential PrEP side effects, which were sometimes perceived to be significant or severe.

“[PrEP] is nothing I would use or want to use. It seems like the side effects is worse than having [HIV].”

Some women, while not completely deterred by the possibility of side effects, wanted to prioritize getting on medications to address their other health needs ahead of PrEP.

“I think if I get on any medication, [my] first priority is probably going to be mental health because I’m unmedicated.”

“I’m supposed to be getting surgery done on my leg, so I’m just trying to hold out to take another medication.”

Safe storage of PrEP medication was not anticipated to be a challenge for most women, even for women who reported homelessness or housing instability.

“I would keep that [PrEP medication] on me. I wouldn’t leave it. That’s why I’m always carrying bags because there are things that I don’t want to come up missing that I just carry with me.”

Beyond risk perception: the role of perceived PrEP relevance

The women interviewed collectively spoke about HIV risk with a complexity that went beyond describing the magnitude of their risk perception (e.g., feeling at “high”, “low”, or “no” risk) alone. Instead, they explained how their sexual and injection practices, their HIV prevention behaviors, and their social and relational environments all interacted to produce their conceptualization of HIV risk.

Most women did have fears and concerns about HIV and expressed that HIV prevention was a priority. Nearly all described regularly and actively employing HIV prevention methods (i.e., condom use, sterile injection equipment use). These sentiments were intrinsically related and often articulated in the same breath: “I do worry about [HIV], but I try to stay protective, very protective.” – Participant #8.

Yet, women expressed mixed perspectives on the extent to which they felt that HIV risk mitigation was within their own control. Some women’s HIV risk perception centered around their own practices, decisions, and behaviors – for these women, the perceived locus of HIV risk was the self.

“I know what I’m doing, so I’m not worried [about HIV].”

Others, however, expressed a sense that HIV risk was inherently present in their day-to-day lives, and that their prevention practices could never completely mitigate the risk of HIV exposure – for these women, the perceived locus of HIV risk was the external environment.

“All I can do is my best to stay away from all the possible ways to come in contact with [HIV]. And not even just me, just in my surroundings.”

While all women had injected drugs in the past six months, few reported sharing injection equipment; women more commonly attributed their HIV concerns to sexually-mediated HIV exposures than to injection-mediated exposures, particularly in the context of sex work engagement.

“I feel more at risk with the prostitution than with the drugs.”

The interplay between women’s perceptions of i) the magnitude and locus of their HIV risk, and ii) how sufficiently their existing HIV prevention practices could mitigate that risk, together shapes what we have termed their ‘perceived PrEP relevance’ – simply, how relevant women feel that PrEP is for themselves at a given moment in time. This novel construct constitutes the second component of the Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability (Fig. 1), with lower perceived PrEP relevance contributing to lower PrEP acceptability, and higher perceived relevance contributing to higher acceptability.

Presenting a typology of perceived PrEP relevance

Through women’s descriptions of their perceived HIV risk, the risk-reduction strategies they employ, and their level of interest in PrEP, a typology of perceived PrEP relevance emerged with four rough subsets (Fig. 2).

No perceived PrEP relevance: locus of risk is the self and existing practices are sufficient (Fig. 2A)

A few women did not perceive themselves to be at risk for HIV at all; the magnitude of their perceived HIV risk was negligible. For these women, HIV risk was a product of an individual’s own behavior, and they did not see their own injection or sexual practices as risk-inducing. Women in this first subset did not perceive PrEP to be relevant for themselves, even if they recognized its benefits for others.

“[PrEP] works for people that are putting themselves at risk, but I have no use for it […] I’m not sharing needles, not having unprotected sex. I wouldn’t need to take it.”

Low perceived PrEP relevance: locus of risk is the environment and existing practices are sufficient (Fig. 2B)

The remaining women in the sample did perceive themselves to be at some magnitude of HIV risk.

A second subset of women described how HIV risk felt like a ubiquitous aspect of their lives, irrespective of their own behavior. To manage that feeling, they employed HIV prevention practices to the extent they could, but also avoided ruminating too much – either actively or passively – on the possibility of HIV acquisition. For women in this subset, perceived PrEP relevance was low but not negligible. One woman, who was “possibly” interested in taking PrEP herself, described how she reconciled her prevention approach with the HIV risk she perceived as inherent to sex work.

“HIV prevention is very important, especially being a prostitute. But I don’t think that’s one of my priorities … I guess I feel if I’m using a condom, I’m doing it safe, but I put myself at risk all the time usually.”

Another woman, who felt her HIV risk was high because “anybody out here is [at high risk]”, shared where HIV prevention fit into her health priorities:

“It doesn’t. I mean, [HIV] is very important. But you kind of just don’t think about it. I mean, you do think about it. But you need to do what you gotta do to get the stuff you need, and you kind of block it out. Not that you don’t think about it, but you just block it out. Because it sucks thinking about it.”

When asked about her interest in taking PrEP herself, she responded: “Why not?”

Moderate perceived PrEP relevance: locus of risk is the self and existing practices are insufficient (Fig. 2C)

A third subset of women reported a higher magnitude of perceived HIV risk, and attributed that risk to their own imperfect or inconsistent usage of HIV prevention practices. Recognizing these prevention gaps led these women to perceive PrEP as moderately relevant for themselves.

“I’m concerned [about HIV], but I generally use protection, so it’s not always on my mind … I had considered [PrEP] before, but I was kind of on the fence about how much of a need there really is. But I figure if there’s any risk, it would be better to mitigate it.”

Another woman, who had also previously considered taking PrEP, illustrated how she may not always be able to sufficiently employ HIV prevention practices like condom use:

“Guys will say, ‘I’ll give you an extra $20 to not use a condom.’ I’m like, ‘Because HIV is worth $20 to me?’ No! But there’s always times when things happen, any girl that says there isn’t is a liar.

But when you’re sick [experiencing opioid withdrawal], and there’s no dates [sex work clients], and you finally get picked up and oh my god, you don’t have a condom on you, or he doesn’t want to wear one, and you’re not going to get the money, and you’re sitting in your head going, “It’s this or I’m sick,” and you don’t want to deal with the sickness [withdrawal]. So, you do it, and the whole time I’m praying to God, “Please let me be okay.” You know what I mean? It’s scary.”

High perceived PrEP relevance: locus of risk is the environment and existing practices are insufficient (Fig. 2D)

PrEP was perceived as most relevant by a final subset of women who, despite consistently engaging in HIV prevention practices, still felt highly concerned about the magnitude of their HIV risk due to factors outside of their control. Some recognized that condom use was not a foolproof method to prevent HIV. For one woman, this concern led her to perceive PrEP as highly relevant for herself:

“It’s the fact that I’m out here, and I’m risking my life every day. What happens when the condom breaks, and a john [sex work client] does have HIV? I mean, that’s what makes me interested [in PrEP].”

Some women expressed concerns about the actions of other people in the environments they regularly navigate to inject drugs and sell sex. One woman explained that despite using condoms, not sharing injection equipment, and regularly getting tested for HIV, she still felt at high HIV risk “because I’m getting high in the streets with people who have [HIV] … Who’s to say I turn around and somebody switches my tools?”

Fears of this type of injection risk, actively introduced by those around them, were shared by other women. One expressed being “terrified” of HIV because:

“Being out here, shooting up and getting high. I’m sleeping with people I don’t know, or some people not liking me and maybe giving me a dirty needle or something. They do that.”

Perceived PrEP relevance is not static

Perceived PrEP relevance is dynamic and highly subject to change with shifts in women’s perceptions of their HIV risk magnitude and locus or their prevention practice sufficiency. One woman, who described PrEP as irrelevant for herself, acknowledged that she would be open to taking PrEP should her circumstances change.

“If for some reason I started doing things that put myself at risk, yeah I would definitely take it.”

One woman in the sample had previously used PrEP. She described an experience that had shifted her perceived locus of HIV risk from the self to her environment, which motivated her to initiate PrEP. She recounted an experience when she wasn’t able to visit her usual syringe services program for injection equipment and instead purchased a needle from a stranger in the neighborhood:

“I realized when I went home and cooked my stuff up and tried to inject that he had gone to the length of cleaning out his tools and selling used needles to people. I was just astounded, because I could’ve not noticed, and that was scary […] But that was an eye-opener. That made me kind of realize that I was at risk, and […] even though I don’t share needles, it’s still possibly dangerous.”

However, in describing why she ultimately discontinued PrEP, she illustrated that perceived PrEP relevance may also wane as prevention practices change or become perceived as sufficient.

“I just kind of let it go by the wayside. I wasn’t really having unprotected sex like that, so I didn’t really think that [PrEP] was absolutely essential.”

Discussion

This qualitative study explored barriers and facilitators of PrEP engagement among WWID and presents a Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability that is grounded in WWID’s day-to-day lives, health priorities and needs, and lived experiences (Fig. 1). In this model, WWID’s interest in or willingness to take PrEP is influenced by anticipated PrEP feasibility (of both attending to PrEP-related health tasks and daily medication management) and perceived PrEP relevance (determined by perceived magnitude and locus of HIV risk and sufficiency of existing HIV prevention practices to mitigate risk).

Most women interviewed were aware of PrEP and largely anticipated PrEP engagement to be feasible; attending to requisite ancillary PrEP tasks was perceived as doable by the many women who identified their existing healthcare-seeking practices as easy entry points for PrEP engagement. This finding contrasts with previous reports that careseeking and healthcare access barriers (e.g., making and attending appointments, managing costs and insurance, transportation access) constitute primary constraints on PrEP uptake (Biello et al., 2018; Bradley et al., 2019; Roth et al., 2018). Instead, the more salient challenge to PrEP engagement for this population may be in overcoming the dominance that withdrawal management and earning money exert over WWID’s time, resources, and energy (Felsher et al., 2021). Novel interventions that co-locate PrEP with services such as medication for opioid use disorder delivery or heroin-assisted treatment (Sherman, 2019; Tyndall, 2020) may improve PrEP feasibility for WWID (Assoumou et al., 2021; Ni et al., 2021).

Women expressed mixed feelings about taking PrEP as a daily oral pill; most felt it would be feasible, but some expressed concerns about side effects or dislike of oral medication. Novel long-acting PrEP formulations like bimonthly injectable cabotegravir (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2021) and Islatravir implants are highly anticipated to enhance uptake in this population (Biello et al., 2019; Rosen, Park, et al., 2022; Shrestha et al., 2020). Our findings suggest that these longer-acting PrEP formulations could increase anticipated PrEP feasibility for some WWID by addressing oral medication concerns but may not alleviate side effect concerns. Integrating recommendations for side effect management into messaging around all PrEP formulations is needed (NYC Health, n. d.), particularly as PrEP side effects may overlap with or exacerbate opioid withdrawal symptoms (Felsher et al., 2020).

Persistently low PrEP uptake among cisgender women and WWID is commonly attributed in part to low HIV risk perception (Bazzi et al., 2018; Biello et al., 2018; Rutledge et al., 2018; Walters et al., 2021). However, our findings corroborate some previous work (Felsher et al., 2020) demonstrating not only that HIV risk perception is, in fact, high in this population, but also that risk perception alone does not sufficiently explain decision-making around PrEP use. Instead, WWID’s perceptions of both the magnitude and locus of HIV risk and the sufficiency of their existing prevention practices interact to produce their perceived PrEP relevance (Fig. 2). This is lowest among women who perceived their prevention practices as sufficient to counteract their self-located risk and highest among women who perceived their prevention practices as insufficient to counteract environmental risks. This notion of PrEP relevance may help explain the frequent misalignment between women’s self-perceived and clinically indicated risk (Rutledge et al., 2018), prompting the design of PrEP messaging and communication that is responsive to women’s broader risk conceptualization.

Our findings also underscore the dynamism of perceived PrEP relevance and, subsequently, PrEP acceptability. PrEP counseling and messaging that de-emphasizes risk conceptualization at the individual level may improve PrEP relevance and engagement among WWID. Experiences that shift WWID’s perceptions of risk locus or prevention practice sufficiency may also increase their perceived PrEP relevance, creating new opportunities for PrEP engagement. The women interviewed described numerous experiences characterized by violence or restricted agency enacted by other actors in their environment, which increased their perceived PrEP relevance (e.g., coerced condomless sex, inadvertent non-sterile syringe use). Harm reduction, community outreach, or community-based clinical services that engage with women following these experiences are well-positioned to enhance PrEP engagement. Community-based post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) models (O’Byrne, MacPherson, Roy, & Orser, 2016) are valuable interventions for women after these “relevance-promoting” events; expanding such interventions and integrating them with harm reduction services can make biomedical HIV prevention more accessible, and PEP-to-PrEP protocols can be leveraged to transition post-exposure care into continued PrEP engagement (Biello, Mimiaga, Valente, Saxena, & Bazzi, 2021; O’Byrne, Orser, & Vandyk, 2020; Taylor, Walley, & Bazzi, 2019).

The women in the present study collectively described their daily routines navigating a complex and oftentimes violent HIV risk environment. PrEP interventions at the institutional and structural levels that recognize these daily realities and honor women’s existing risk reduction practices will better address structural HIV risk. Improving structural responsiveness of PrEP programs could include smaller-scale service optimizations, like extending clinical service hours or providing broader social supports (i.e., transportation vouchers, childcare, food). Innovative operational approaches could include colocating PrEP services within substance use treatment services (Taylor et al., 2019), syringe service programs (Biello et al., 2021; Roth et al., 2021), or housing programs (Henwood, Rhoades, Redline, Dzubur, & Wenzel, 2019; Kerman et al., 2021); or implementing mobile delivery of clinical and laboratory PrEP services (Bazzi, Shaw, Biello, Vahey, & Brody, 2022; Roth et al., 2018). Distal structural interventions could include drug decriminalization (Bonn et al., 2020), implementing universal basic income programs (Gibson, Hearty, & Craig, 2020; Rubino, 2022), or expanding the Housing First approach (Kerman et al., 2021; Watson, Shuman, Kowalsky, Golembiewski, & Brown, 2017) – an approach to housing programs and policy that prioritizes access to permanent housing as a foundation for unhoused people to address their other needs, rather than conditioning housing access on sobriety, income, service engagement, or the like.

The risk environment framework not only centers such multilevel interventions to support PrEP uptake and mitigate the harms of the risk environment, but also calls for an expansion of the HIV prevention paradigm to create more enabling environments. In such a paradigm, to recognize that WWID’s prioritization of drug purchasing and reliance on survival sex work are barriers to PrEP uptake is to affirm that advocating for safer supply (Bonn et al., 2020; Glegg et al., 2022), expanded access to MOUD (Jones, Compton, Han, Baldwin, & Volkow, 2022), and decriminalization of drugs and sex work (Bonn et al., 2020; Sherman, German, Cheng, Marks, & Bailey-Kloche, 2006; Silberzahn et al., 2021) are all effectively forms of HIV prevention. Consideration of the risk environment demonstrates that PrEP can be a critical risk-reduction tool in the short term, particularly for women with the highest perceived PrEP relevance (e.g., those fearful of HIV exposure through malicious syringe swapping), but other interventions addressing upstream drivers of HIV risk (e.g., overdose prevention sites) can generate more substantial impacts for HIV prevention among vulnerabilized populations. Ultimately, as an individual-level tool to manage structurally determined risk, PrEP can only go so far. HIV prevention strategies must be comprehensive, and comprehensive HIV prevention cannot hinge on PrEP alone.

Limitations

Findings from this study are subject to several limitations. First, participants were recruited via or nearby harm reduction organizations. Thus, the sample may not reflect the perspectives of service-disengaged women, who may have divergent healthcare-seeking, HIV risk perception, and substance use experiences. Second, while women interviewed were offered information about local PrEP services, study procedures did not include the provision of or direct linkage to PrEP services. Thus, the saliency of study findings for actual PrEP uptake and continuation could not be explored. Third, we limited our inquiry to the PrEP options that were accessible to the participants in our sample at the time, which was only daily oral PrEP. Thus, we were not able to assess acceptability or feasibility of alternative oral PrEP regimens or long-acting formulations. Fourth, conducting interviews with monetary incentives among women with significant structural needs may have compelled participants to respond affirmatively to questions about PrEP interest and feasibility. Finally, these qualitative findings are highly specific to the substance use, harm reduction service, and healthcare environments of Baltimore City and may not be transferrable to other contexts. Future research that expands on this study’s contributions should include interrogation of these presented theoretical frameworks in other settings or alongside PrEP provision.

Conclusions

We present a Psychosocial Model of PrEP Acceptability to describe the complex dimensions of WWID’s interest in and willingness to use PrEP. In this model, both the anticipated feasibility of PrEP engagement and perceived PrEP relevance interact with women’s position within the HIV risk environment, which may lead women to de-prioritize PrEP engagement and shapes how women conceptualize their HIV risk. Thus, improving PrEP access entails not only addressing healthcare access barriers (e.g., appointment scheduling, insurance management, transportation), but also alleviating the immediacy of women’s competing substance-related needs to carve out sufficient space in their lives for PrEP engagement. Considering women’s risk perception in conjunction with their existing prevention practices suggests that effective PrEP messaging should go beyond compelling women to “correctly” recognize their HIV risk and instead prompt women to contemplate their own position within their physical and social environments and consider the potential sources of risk therein. Improving PrEP acceptability, supporting PrEP uptake, and enhancing HIV prevention among WWID requires interventions that are responsive to structural dimensions of HIV risk, uplift the prevention strategies women already employ, and are supportive of women’s constellation of health and survival needs.
