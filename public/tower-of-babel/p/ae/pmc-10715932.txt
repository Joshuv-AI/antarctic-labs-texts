Late Outcome of a Left-Atrial-to-Coronary-Sinus Shunt
Authors: Mario D Bassi, Kenza Rahmouni, Alexandros Nantsios, David Messika-Zeitoun, Benjamin Hibbert, Talal Al-Atassi, Vincent Chan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractFrail patients with severe calcific mitral stenosis have few treatment options. We present a novel case successfully treated with a left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt. However, the patient’s symptoms returned, and, at surgery, the shunt was found to be occluded and associated with left atrial and ventricular fibrosis and calcification. (Level of Difficulty: Intermediate.)Key Words: coronary sinus shunt, heart failure, mitral annulus calcification, mitral regurgitation, mitral stenosis, valve fibrosis

History of Presentation

A frail 78-year-old woman received a diagnosis of severe calcific mitral stenosis, severe pulmonary hypertension, and right ventricle dysfunction. She experienced NYHA functional class IV symptoms and had undergone placement of a left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt 4 years prior. Her symptoms improved, but over the preceding 6 months she had experienced a return to class IV dyspnea while receiving maximal medical therapy. She was assessed for surgery and agreed for urgent admission to hospital from home.

Medical History

Her relevant cardiac history included a history of single-vessel coronary artery disease treated with percutaneous coronary intervention of the left anterior descending artery, aortic calcification, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes. Her severe pulmonary hypertension and right ventricle dysfunction were treated with sildenafil and furosemide, despite the limited evidence for phosphodiesterase inhibitor use in patients with type II pulmonary hypertension. She had atrial fibrillation, with a CHADS2 score of 3, and received anticoagulant agents: 81 mg daily of acetylsalicylic acid and a direct oral anticoagulant after her shunt procedure, but her direct oral anticoagulant was discontinued, owing to recurrent severe epistaxis requiring multiple transfusions. Importantly, her preshunt echocardiography in 2019 showed normal right ventricular function with a right ventricle systolic pressure of 52 mm Hg that dropped to 30 mm Hg 1 year after shunt placement.

Her other noncardiac medical history included hypothyroidism, chronic kidney disease, and breast cancer.

Investigations

A CT angiogram of the patient’s chest revealed severe mitral annular calcification (Figure 1A, Video 1) and mural calcification involving the aortic arch and the distal ascending aorta. Preoperative echocardiography showed a mean mitral gradient of 11 mm Hg, Wilkins score of 14, mitral valve area of 1.1 cm2, and extensive annular, leaflet, subvalvular, and commissural fusion. An intraoperative transesophageal echocardiogram confirmed severe mixed mitral valve disease caused by mitral annular calcification, severe pulmonary hypertension, and severe right ventricle dysfunction. The tricuspid valve annulus was 30 mm in the septal-lateral dimension, and there was 1+ tricuspid regurgitation. Right heart catheterization revealed systolic, diastolic, and mean pulmonary artery pressures of 98, 31, and 57 mm Hg, respectively. The pulmonary capillary wedge, left ventricle end-diastolic, and right atrial pressures were 30, 16, and 13 mm Hg, respectively. The patient had a right ventricular systolic pressure of 63 mm Hg and a pulmonary vascular resistance of 758 dynes/s/cm5 based on a cardiac output of 2.1 L/min and systolic blood pressure of 114 mm Hg, confirming the presence of pulmonary hypertension, along with a residual 70% proximal-mid left anterior descending lesion.

Management

Given the extent of annular, leaflet, and commissural fusion, percutaneous valvuloplasty was thought not to be beneficial for the patient. She underwent coronary artery bypass grafting with a left internal mammary artery to left anterior descending coronary artery, tricuspid valve repair with a No. 30 TriAd 2.0 band (Medtronic), left atrial appendage ligation, closure of a patent foramen ovale, and mitral valve replacement with a No. 27 St. Jude Epic Plus Bioprosthesis (Abbott Laboratories). She underwent cardiopulmonary bypass via cannulation of her right axillary artery because of her ascending aortic calcification. Interestingly, the fibrosis and calcification associated with the previously placed left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt (Figures 1B and 2) resulted in a broad region of fibrosis and calcification that extended several centimeters proximally from the annulus in the left atrium and several centimeters into the ventricle involving the papillary muscles. Her bioprosthetic mitral valve was successfully implanted with large felt pledgets placed deep in the ventricle, as previously described by our group.1

Discussion

There are few treatment options for patients with severe calcific mitral stenosis and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF),2,3 For mixed mitral disease, the standard medical therapy recommended by current American Heart Association guidelines is symptom relief with diuretics. If diuretic therapy does not improve symptoms, surgical intervention such as mitral valve replacement may be needed.4

However, these treatments and interventions have limitations, which have driven the investigation of left-to-right shunts that reduce left-sided heart pressures.5

Theoretically, the reduction in left atrial pressure may result in an associated decrease in pulmonary pressure, which may then improve dyspnea and potentially peripheral edema due to augmentation of right ventricle dysfunction.6 As in recent experimental trials, this case demonstrated effectiveness in immediate improvement in dyspnea and functional status of a patient with a left-atrium-to-coronary-sinus shunt.7 Importantly, the clinical benefit of a left-atrium-to-coronary-sinus shunt may be limited in selected patients with a stiff, fibrotic left atrium, wherein left atrial compliance is reduced, and thus adequate decompression with a shunt may not occur.7 This may have developed indolently in this patient, which may have decreased flow across the shunt, which led to shunt closure over time. To our knowledge, mitral valve replacement in a patient with a previously placed left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt has not been previously described. We found that the shunt was associated with a fibrotic reaction that extended into the left atrium and left ventricle, which complicated cardiac surgery. Overall, the time course of left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt closure is also unknown. Indeed, factors that portend the patency of these shunts will be elucidated as their use becomes more widespread.

Regardless of the increased complexity of mitral valve replacement, this case highlights the success and benefits of left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunts in patients with severe calcific mitral stenosis and HFpEF.

Follow-Up

The patient underwent extubation on postoperative day 3 once she was weaned from inhaled nitric oxide. She was otherwise clinically well and was transferred to the ward after 8 days. Her postoperative transthoracic echocardiogram showed a well-functioning mitral bioprosthesis without perivalvular leak and a right ventricle systolic pressure of 68 mm Hg, with borderline reduced right ventricle function at a systolic pressure of 130 mm Hg. Her dyspnea improved, and 2 months after surgery she was in NYHA functional class I.

Conclusions

To our knowledge, this constitutes the first reported case of a left-atrial-to-coronary-sinus shunt that closed late after placement. The fibrosis and calcification associated with this shunt extended several centimeters into the left atrium and left ventricle, which increased the complexity of ultimate surgical mitral valve replacement. Nevertheless, echocardiographic imaging confirmed a well-seated valve without perivalvular leak. This report may have relevance in selected elderly or high-risk patients with HFpEF due to severe calcific mitral valve disease who are considered for a palliative shunt. Further research concerning shunt patency, anatomical considerations, and optimal antithrombotic regimen will shed light on the natural history of these novel devices.
