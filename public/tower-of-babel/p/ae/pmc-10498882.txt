Cost-utility Analysis of Evoke Closed-loop Spinal Cord Stimulation for Chronic Back and Leg Pain
Authors: Rui V. Duarte, Anthony Bentley, Nicole Soliday, Angela Leitner, Ashish Gulve, Peter S. Staats, Dawood Sayed, Steven M. Falowski, Corey W. Hunter, Rod S. Taylor
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Objectives:The effectiveness of Evoke closed-loop spinal cord stimulation (CL-SCS), a novel modality of neurostimulation, has been demonstrated in a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The objective of this cost-utility analysis was to develop a de novo economic model to estimate the cost-effectiveness of Evoke CL-SCS when compared with open-loop SCS (OL-SCS) for the management of chronic back and leg pain.Methods:A decision tree followed by a Markov model was used to estimate the costs and outcomes of Evoke CL-SCS versus OL-SCS over a 15-year time horizon from the UK National Health Service perspective. A “high-responder” health state was included to reflect improved levels of SCS pain reduction recently reported. Results are expressed as incremental cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) was conducted to assess uncertainty in the model inputs.Results:Evoke CL-SCS was estimated to be the dominant treatment strategy at ~5 years postimplant (ie, it generates more QALYs while cost saving compared with OL-SCS). Probabilistic sensitivity analysis showed that Evoke CL-SCS has a 92% likelihood of being cost-effective at a willingness to pay threshold of £20,000/QALY. Results were robust across a wide range of scenario and sensitivity analyses.Discussion:The results indicate a strong economic case for the use of Evoke CL-SCS in the management of chronic back and leg pain with or without prior spinal surgery with dominance observed at ~5 years.

Pain and pain-related conditions are recognized as prominent causes of disability worldwide by the Global Burden of Disease Studies.1–3 Chronic pain has a substantial negative socioeconomic impact. The cost of back pain alone has been estimated at around one fifth the total health care expenditure in the United Kingdom or 1.5% of annual gross domestic product.4 The annual costs associated with the management of chronic pain in the United Kingdom have been estimated to total £11 billion and $100 billion in the United States.5,6

Spinal cord stimulation (SCS) is an accepted surgical intervention for the treatment of refractory chronic pain.7 SCS works by sending small electrical pulses to electrodes in the epidural space that stimulate the spinal cord to modulate pain transmission and relieve pain.8 Randomized controlled trials (RCTs),9,10 systematic reviews,11,12 and economic evaluations11,13,14 have demonstrated the clinical effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of SCS for the management of chronic neuropathic pain.

Previous evaluations have considered neurostimulation devices that provide a constant fixed-output stimulation without the capability of adjusting the dosage to real-time physiological feedback, so-called “open-loop” stimulation. In open-loop SCS (OL-SCS), the intensity delivered to the target tissues and resultant activation constantly changes due to the variability in the distance between the electrode and spinal cord caused by acute physiological functions, such as breathing, heartbeat, and shifting one’s posture. Without the ability to continually measure and adjust stimulation intensity (dosage) on every stimulation pulse, these continual modifications in physiological conditions result in frequent substantive fluctuations in the activation of the cord. Hence pain relief would be expected to vary considerably with OL-SCS. Moreover, none of the commercially available open-loop systems have the ability to measure the spinal cord’s response to stimulation to confirm successful therapy delivery to the intended target neurons involved in the modulation of pain inhibition pathways.15 Consequently, OL-SCS devices are technically incapable of measuring whether stimulation is effectively modulating the inhibition of target pain pathways or consistently delivering a constant level of neural response.

In contrast, the Evoke SCS system can measure the neural response elicited by SCS through Evoked Compound Action Potentials (ECAPs) to provide ECAP-guided programming and deliver physiological closed-loop SCS (Evoke closed-loop spinal cord stimulation [CL-SCS]) by automatically adjusting the stimulation dose in real time to maintain a consistent neural response at a prescribed level despite physiological changes.15 The proposed benefit of this physiological control system is that a more consistent neural response may more effectively drive the pain-inhibitory mechanisms to attain better analgesia.

Traditionally, “satisfactory” pain relief has been defined as a reduction in pain rating of 30 to 50% or greater. However, recent trials with Evoke CL-SCS have reported a substantial proportion of patients reporting higher levels of pain reduction (≥80%).16,17 To date, economic models assessing the cost-effectiveness of interventions for chronic pain have not formally considered these “high-responder” patients.18

The aims of this cost-utility analysis were (1) to develop a de novo economic model to formally reflect the health state of “high (pain) responder” and (2) to estimate the cost-effectiveness of Evoke CL-SCS when compared with OL-SCS for the management of people with chronic back and leg pain with or without prior spinal surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This economic evaluation is reported in line with the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards statement19 and based on NICE reference methods.20

Study Design

The effectiveness of Evoke CL-SCS was evaluated in the Evoke RCT, a multicentre, double-blind, parallel arm trial comparing Evoke CL-SCS to OL-SCS in patients with and without prior spinal surgery.16 The study was conducted in compliance with ethical and regulatory guidelines and was approved by local ethics committees before participant enrollment. The Evoke System (Australia) was used to deliver both CL-SCS and OL-SCS. Patients and investigators were assessed at 3 and 12 months to determine if they were unblinded to treatment assignment.16 All patients were naive to SCS and how paresthesia may differ between Evoke CL-SCS and OL-SCS. Measurement of the neural response elicited by SCS (ie, ECAPs) on every stimulation pulse was performed in both closed-loop and open-loop stimulation modes to confirm activation of the intended target. In open-loop mode, the system delivered fixed-output SCS comparable with that offered by all other currently available SCS devices. However, with Evoke operating as an open-loop system, ECAPs were measured in clinic to inform program settings, offering a unique improvement in programming accuracy to recipients of Evoke open-loop stimulation compared with currently available OL-SCS devices. In contrast, the Evoke System operating in closed-loop mode measured ECAP amplitude, compared the measured ECAP to the target ECAP amplitude, and automatically adjusted the stimulation current in real time to maintain a consistent neural response at a prescribed level. The randomization assignments were generated at the time of the screening trial, after lead placement. The baseline demographics and characteristics were well balanced between the groups.16 The mode of stimulation (ie, closed-loop or open-loop) was enabled during the screening trial phase. Trial participants were blinded to the setting and could not change the device settings from closed-loop to open-loop and vice versa. The trial and implant procedures for both Evoke CL-SCS and OL-SCS were done according to usual practice. Two percutaneous leads were placed in the epidural space over the dorsal column at the thoracic vertebral level associated with pain and connected to the external trial stimulator (during the trial phase) or the stimulator implanted in a subcutaneous pocket (for the permanent implant). The device stimulation parameters were similar between treatment groups with respect to mean frequency (12 mo: 37.7 Hz [10.0 to 70.0] Evoke CL-SCS; 38.8 Hz [10.0 to 80.0] OL-SCS; P=0.76) and mean pulse width (12 mo: 297.8 μs [120.0 to 500.0] Evoke CL-SCS; 287.5 μs [120.0 to 430.0] OL-SCS; P=0.48).

The economic analysis was undertaken from the perspective of the UK National Health Service to estimate the long-term costs and benefits of Evoke CL-SCS when compared with OL-SCS. The economic evaluation considers a population of adult patients (18 to 80 y) with chronic, intractable pain of the back and legs refractory to conservative therapy, with or without previous back surgery. Candidates for SCS had overall back and leg pain scores on the Visual Analog Scale (VAS) of 60 mm or greater (out of 100 mm for worst imaginable pain); Oswestry Disability Index score of 41 to 80; stable pain medications; and no previous experience with SCS.16 A time horizon of 15 years was used to reflect the chronic nature of the condition, and the expected longevity of the devices and to ensure that at least 1 device replacement would take place for both Evoke CL-SCS and OL-SCS within the timeframe. An annual discount rate of 3.5% was applied for both costs and outcomes as per NICE recommendations.20

The Evoke System is a rechargeable device, and the analysis assumes that the open-loop system is also rechargeable. Previous economic evaluations concluded that there is no demonstrable difference in clinical efficacy between rechargeable and non-rechargeable OL-SCS devices but higher overall costs associated with more frequent device replacements for non-rechargeable systems. Device longevity for non-rechargeable devices has been commonly assumed as 4 years in line with previously published literature and economic model assumptions.13 Rechargeable SCS devices typically dominate non-rechargeable devices in cost-effectiveness studies.13,21 As such, this analysis does not consider non-rechargeable OL-SCS devices as a comparator.

Model Structure

A 2-stage model was developed in Microsoft Excel version 16. The decision tree (first stage) reflects the clinical pathway up to 12 months following SCS implantation (Fig. 1A). The Markov model (second stage) is used to explore the clinical pathways over the long term (Fig. 1B). Historically, models evaluating the cost-effectiveness of SCS did not consider a high-responder health state. The model structure has been adapted to include a high-responder health state to better reflect the increased response rate observed with Evoke CL-SCS.

The screening trial was performed with external stimulators attached to implanted percutaneous leads. Costs

The costs used in the model are outlined in Table 2. Costs were sourced from published evidence11,13,25 that has been used to inform NICE appraisals on the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of SCS for the management of chronic neuropathic pain.7,28 Costs considered in the analysis included device list prices, reimplantation, health states, and additional costs. The SCS implantation costs cover the cost of the implantation procedure and all the required consumables, which could include electrodes, leads, implantable pulse generator, remote control, and battery charger. Where required, prices were inflated to the 2020 price year using Personal Social Services Research Unit Pay and Prices Index.27 The devices on which the inflated costs are based on have been superseded by new models with potentially higher costs, therefore, the approach used may be conservative. Because of the small differences, it was conservatively assumed that the costs for the Evoke CL-SCS and OL-SCS were equal for the purposes of the analysis. Because of the similarities in initial device costs, Taylor et al13 were used to provide an estimate for the cost of a replacement closed-loop and open-loop system. For the health state costs, the analysis conservatively assumed that the annual cost of SCS (ie, Evoke CL-SCS or OL-SCS) was the same irrespective of the pain response achieved. The same conservative assumption was made for the costs of CMM (ie, the cost of CMM was the same irrespective of the pain response attained).

Data Analysis

Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses (PSA) were conducted to assess the uncertainty surrounding the model inputs and the sensitivity of the model results to changes in efficacy and cost. One-way sensitivity analyses were performed using realistic minimum and maximum individual model inputs; for all model parameters, the minimum and maximum plausible values for univariate analysis were defined as the lower and upper 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs). All clinical probabilities (eg, SCS trial success, response to treatment) and utilities were varied using a beta distribution, and all costs and device longevity were varied using a gamma distribution, in line with best practice.20,29,30

A cost-utility analysis was conducted, and the results were reported as incremental cost-utility ratio (ICUR). This was done by calculating the ratio of the difference in mean costs and mean change in quality-adjusted life years (QALY).31

Results of univariate sensitivity analyses were depicted as tornado diagrams, demonstrating how changes in individual model inputs between plausible minimum and maximum values influenced the model results. A tornado diagram plots the results of the most influential parameters on the ICUR from a sensitivity analysis exercise; however, it should be noted that all parameters were tested.32 All parameters were simultaneously varied in PSA, and the results of PSAs were depicted on scatter plots on the cost-effectiveness plane, showing the distribution of ICURs generated from 5000 simulations. Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves depicted PSA results and demonstrated the probability of Evoke CL-SCS being cost-effective when compared with OL-SCS over a range of willingness to pay thresholds (WTPs) that a decision-maker may be willing to pay per additional QALY gained.

RESULTS

The base-case analysis shows that Evoke CL-SCS would have a total mean cost of £83,354 per patient over 15 years postimplant compared with £89,228 for OL-SCS. Evoke CL-SCS becomes cost-saving at ~5 years. The higher initial costs for Evoke CL-SCS are due to the higher success rate observed in the screening trial when using Evoke CL-SCS (94%) compared with when using OL-SCS (85.1%). This means that more patients receiving Evoke CL-SCS would incur the initial costs of a full device implantation as they have demonstrated a significant (≥50%) reduction in pain during the screening trial phase.

Evoke CL-SCS is estimated to generate a mean of 7.19 QALYs over 15 years compared with 6.23 QALYs for OL-SCS. Evoke CL-SCS is estimated to generate more QALYs than OL-SCS from the first year. This is due to the higher probability of being a high-responder (55.9%) or responder (27.1%) when receiving Evoke CL-SCS, when compared with OL-SCS (37.3% and 23.7%, respectively). The base-case analysis shows that Evoke CL-SCS is cost-saving and generates more QALYs than OL-SCS (ie, closed-loop is dominant when compared with OL-SCS) (Table 3).

The ICUR for Evoke CL-SCS is below the WTP threshold of £30,000/QALY at year 1 postimplant (Supplementary Material 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CJP/A994). This is due to the higher trial success rate (improved clinical effectiveness) with Evoke CL-SCS, resulting in incremental implants. After this time point, Evoke CL-SCS becomes a cost-effective strategy until year 5 when it becomes the dominant strategy, generating more QALYs while cost-saving when compared with OL-SCS.

Deterministic sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the base-case ICUR to changes in outcomes and costs within the predefined ranges. The results of the deterministic sensitivity analysis comparing Evoke CL-SCS to OL-SCS are presented in Supplementary Material 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CJP/A994. Deterministic sensitivity analysis shows that altering any of the model parameters to the minimum and maximum plausible values does not result in an ICUR above a lower WTP threshold of £20,000 per additional QALY gained for Evoke CL-SCS compared with OL-SCS. The 2 parameters resulting in an increased ICUR for Evoke CL-SCS are due to the limitation of univariate analysis in which only 1 parameter is varied at a time. In these instances, the values for the identified parameter are inconsistent with the other parameters (ie, the cost of CMM alone is lower than the cost of CMM when given in combination with SCS).

The cost-effectiveness acceptability curve for the base-case scenario shows that Evoke CL-SCS has a 92% likelihood of being cost-effective at a WTP threshold of £20,000/QALY when compared with OL-SCS (Supplementary Material 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CJP/A994).

Scenario Analysis—Alternative CMM Costs

With the introduction of the new high-responder health state (≥80% reduction in pain), it can be reasonably hypothesized that the associated costs of CMM for pain relief would be reduced. In the current analysis, it is assumed that the introduction of SCS will reduce the costs of concomitant CMM compared with CMM alone. It is conservatively assumed that there is no difference in high-responders (≥80% reduction in pain), responders (≥50% to 80% reduction in pain), and suboptimal pain relief (<50% reduction in pain). A recent study assessing the cost of chronic pain in Sweden demonstrated that total annual health care costs were 31% lower in patients without pain or mild chronic pain, compared with those with moderate chronic pain, while those with severe chronic pain were 26% more expensive.33

DISCUSSION

Our results indicate that Evoke CL-SCS is cost-saving and provides additional health benefits when compared with OL-SCS for people with chronic back and leg pain with or without prior spinal surgery. Our results were consistent across deterministic and PSA. A scenario analysis demonstrates that Evoke CL-SCS can become the dominant treatment strategy as early as year 2 when considering that high-responders may require lower health care use than patients not obtaining such a response level. This is in line with observations of reduced health care costs in patients without pain or mild chronic pain when compared with those with moderate and severe chronic pain.33 Although explants due to LoE have not been observed for Evoke CL-SCS up to 24 months follow-up,34 we considered a scenario where the explant rate would be the same as for OL-SCS, showing that although the potential cost savings decrease in comparison to the base-case analysis, Evoke CL-SCS remains the dominant treatment strategy. It is likely that OL-SCS would be associated with an explant rate far higher than observed in the Evoke RCT, which utilized ECAP-guided programming, a capability only technically possible with the Evoke SCS device. Greater explant rates for OL-SCS systems would result in additional cost savings and QALY gains with Evoke CL-SCS. Considering an annual explant rate probability for OL-SCS of 8%,35 the estimated cost savings with Evoke CL-SCS would be £14,720 with incremental QALY gains of 1.36, whereas explant rates of 22%,36 would result in further cost savings (£25,032) and QALY gains (1.79).

The current economic evaluation shows Evoke CL-SCS below the £30,000/QALY WTP threshold after the first year, becoming dominant at approximately 5 years. Previous economic evaluations have shown OL-SCS as providing more benefits at a lesser cost for complex regional pain syndrome and persistent spinal pain syndrome than alternative treatments such as physiotherapy37 and CMM,38 respectively. These analyses used a time horizon to account for the remainder of a person’s life; however, the authors reported that SCS was not cost-effective at year 137 or year 2.38 Recently, 2 modes of open-loop (high-frequency and low-frequency SCS) were compared with high-frequency being the dominant strategy considering a 15-year time horizon.25 It is unclear at what time point high-frequency becomes the dominant treatment strategy, hence assumed to be the time horizon of the analysis.

Data from a good quality RCT16 were used to inform the economic evaluation and provide the highest level of evidence of therapy adherence via physiological measurements for a cost-effectiveness analysis of SCS to treat chronic back and leg pain, including persistent spinal pain syndrome and nonsurgical refractory pain. It is plausible that ECAP-guided programming may have produced an overestimate of the true effect of OL-SCS, as other commercially available open-loop systems do not have the ability (like Evoke delivering OL-SCS) to measure the neural response and confirm activation of the intended target while programming in-clinic.15 The observed rates of patients obtaining substantial pain relief using OL-SCS without ECAP measurement are typically lower than those observed in the Evoke RCT. The PROCESS trial observed 22% of patients achieving ≥80% leg pain relief at 6 months (between-group risk difference with CMM: 15%, 99% CI, −3% to −33%).9 Recently, another trial using OL-SCS, including low-frequency, high-frequency, and burst waveforms observed 38% of patients reporting pain relief ≥50%.39 These rates are considerably lower than the corresponding figures in this analysis, where the probability of being a high-responder or responder were 37.3% and 61% of patients, respectively. Replacing the rates of response with OL-SCS observed in the Evoke trial with those from other OL-SCS trials would likely result in more cost savings and increased benefits being achieved with Evoke CL-SCS. Consequently, it is anticipated that our findings are conservative when achievable pain relief with commercially available OL-SCS devices has been shown to be less than observed in the open-loop arm of the Evoke RCT.

Strengths and Weaknesses

We believe this to be the first economic model to formally include the high-responder health state. This is an important contribution, as recent trials have observed an increased proportion of patients achieving high levels of pain relief. The inclusion of this high-responder health state provides the opportunity for future economic evaluations to estimate the cost-effectiveness of chronic pain interventions more accurately. In the absence of robust data on the health care costs of the high-responder health state, we conservatively assumed the costs to be the same as those for a responder or sub-optimal responder. As high-responders are likely to have improved health outcomes and lower costs, we undertook a scenario analysis that demonstrated that we may have underestimated the cost savings of Evoke CL-SCS. In addition, this analysis was based on the Evoke RCT, which includes patients with chronic back and leg pain with and without prior surgery in approximately equal proportions. Consequently, the results presented demonstrate cost-effectiveness regardless of patients’ exposure to prior back surgery. While subgroup analysis could provide cost-effectiveness results according to different pain syndromes, the patient population considered in this study reflects clinical practice more accurately where clinicians would contemplate the use of SCS for mixed pain etiologies.

We adopted several conservative assumptions. These were made in order not to favor Evoke CL-SCS and as such should be considered as a strength of the analysis rather than a weakness. We considered device longevity to be the same for closed-loop and OL-SCS although battery life for Evoke CL-SCS at lower or moderate setting has the potential to be longer than 10 years.23 The Evoke system is a rechargeable device, and previous economic evaluations have consistently found rechargeable SCS to dominate non-rechargeable SCS devices.13,21 Although rechargeable devices have a higher initial cost, this is offset by the longevity of the devices and need for a replacement implantable pulse generator. ECAP-guided programming and closed-loop SCS have the potential to lead to a decrease in the number of reprogramming visits required, which would result in lower costs. A previous study reported that patients required a mean of 1.6 visits per year,40 while with ECAP-guided programming the median number of reprogramming visits observed was 0.3 with CL-SCS and 0 with OL-SCS at 12 months and 0 reprogramming visits for both OL-SCS and CL-SCS at 24 months.34 ECAP-guided programming and the automated programming in CL-SCS could also result in savings to patients and industry due to a decrease in the need for both patients and device representatives to have to attend reprogramming visits.

Although we did not include the costs and health outcomes of SCS complications in our analysis we would note both their very low occurrence and also that there was no difference in the rates of study-related AEs between study arms of the Evoke trial (19.4% closed-loop vs. 16.4% open-loop, rate difference=3.0, 95% CI, −10.0-16.0).16 Furthermore, previous economic evaluations have consistently reported that complications of SCS are not a strong driver of cost-effectiveness results.11,13,25

Although intervention efficacy and safety are usually assumed to be transferable, caution is needed in directly transferring cost-effectiveness results across international health care settings given the considerable variability in the structure of health care systems, differences in the delivery and cost of health care services, and reimbursement mechanisms. Nevertheless, given the observed magnitude of health gain and cost-saving seen in this UK perspective analysis, we believe it is likely that Evoke CL-SCS would also dominate OL-SCS in other health care settings.

Implications for Practice

SCS systems capable of physiological measurement provide an objective therapeutic approach to SCS from which therapy may be confirmed and monitored, providing the transparency that is essential for patients, providers, and payors to weigh the costs and effectiveness of the therapy. Without such objective measurement, one cannot fully interpret outcomes, assess reasons for failure, or adjust therapy other than by trial and error.41 Applying a technological and mechanistic approach to neurostimulation facilitates a deeper understanding that translates into clinical dividends for all stakeholders.42

OL-SCS systems are incapable of continuously measuring and adhering to a prescribed stimulation dose with therapy delivery 99.1% more variable compared with closed-loop at 12 months in the Evoke study (median deviation in neural response from ECAP target prescription [programmed in a sitting position] when lying down was 131.2% with OL-SCS vs. 0.7% with Evoke CL-SCS). With a physiologically responsive closed-loop system that adjusts stimulation dose on every pulse, delivery of accurate and consistent therapy to the prescribed neural response is achieved. Not only was this level of device performance observed through follow-up, it was also confirmed in the screening trial phase with a higher screening trial success rate for Evoke CL-SCS compared with OL-SCS. The screening trial success rate observed with Evoke CL-SCS (94%) is higher than observed in recent RCTs of OL-SCS, including high frequency (86%),43 burst (93%)44, or a combination of paresthesia-inducing, high-frequency and burst (89%).39 Despite the higher initial cost due to the higher screening trial success rate, Evoke CL-SCS remained cost-saving compared with OL-SCS. The higher success rate should be seen as an added advantage of using Evoke CL-SCS and may further question the need for screening trials when using Evoke CL technology.45 A study is currently underway (NCT#04319887) to further examine the clinical utility of these neurophysiological measurements in prognostic and diagnostic applications, such as informing patient selection during the screening phase.

This article highlights the substantial degree of pain relief and associated improvements in quality of life and reduction in costs with Evoke CL-SCS, but the benefits of physiological measurement and control in SCS are also observed through improvements in other secondary outcome measures, notably disability. Higher levels of disability, in addition to pain severity, have been found to be related to higher costs and lower health-related quality of life.46 At baseline, all Evoke RCT participants were classified as severely disabled or crippled on the Oswestry Disability Index (score 41% to 80%). At the 12-month follow-up, the percent of Evoke-CL participants with minimal disability (score 0% to 20%) was almost double for high-responders (47%) compared with responders (29%) and nonresponders (25%). The relationship between disability and costs with Evoke CL-SCS should be investigated further.

Another practice consideration is the reduction in opioid usage in patients receiving Evoke CL-SCS. Notable voluntary reduction or elimination of opioids was seen in 55% of closed-loop compared with 40% of open-loop participants at 12 months. Although the reduction of opioid use is in and of itself a desirable goal, the decrease in opiates may have resulted in decreased health care costs as well, both directly and indirectly (minimizing costs related to complications of opiate therapy). These additional benefits were not captured in this analysis. Given the significant consequences of long-term opioid use and the burden of opioid addiction, opioid-sparing treatments are a focus for policy makers and health systems globally.

CONCLUSIONS

This cost-utility analysis demonstrates that Evoke CL-SCS was dominant, generating more QALYs at a lower cost than OL-SCS for the treatment of chronic back and leg pain with or without prior surgery, based on conservative assumptions. The dominance of Evoke-CL over OL-SCS was observed approximately at year 5 postimplant in the base-case analysis and as early as year 2 in a scenario analysis. The timepoints at which economic dominance was observed are earlier than reported in previous SCS literature, demonstrating the additional clinical value and lower costs associated with Evoke CL-SCS. As a unique closed-loop SCS device, adoption of the Evoke SCS device could therefore offer additional clinical utility to patients with chronic back and leg pain with or without exposure to prior surgery and reduced costs to health systems versus commercially available OL-SCS devices.
