Intraoperative Guidewire Breakage During Open Reduction and Internal Fixation of Pediatric Medial Epicondyle Humerus Fractures: A Single-Institution Review
Authors: Haad A. Arif, Ashleigh N. Pyle, Isabela Lamadrid, Gerald McGwin, Shawn R. Gilbert, Michael J. Conklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile several intraoperative complications of medial epicondyle humerus fracture fixation have been reported, the frequency and implications of guidewire breakage have not yet been described.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted to identify all pediatric patients with a medial epicondyle humerus fracture surgically managed with a cannulated screw. Data collected included patient demographics, guidewire and screw characteristics, tourniquet times, and postoperative complications.ResultsBetween January 2020 and October 2025, 99 cases of operatively managed medial epicondyle humerus fractures were identified within a single level I children's hospital. Six (6.1%) cases were complicated by intraoperative guidewire breakage. Guidewire breakage most commonly occurred when using a 1.25 mm guidewire (n = 5/6, 83.3%) with a 4.0 mm cannulated screw (n = 3/6, 50.0%). Guidewire breakage was associated with significantly longer tourniquet times (P = .01). All guidewire fragments were left in situ with no documented postoperative complications at 3 months follow-up.ConclusionsGuidewire breakage is an underreported complication of medial epicondyle humerus fracture fixation with cannulated screws, warranting increased awareness among the orthopaedic community.Key Concepts(1)Guidewire breakage occurred in 6.1% of patients treated with cannulated screw fixation of a medial epicondyle fracture.(2)All instances of guidewire breakage were using a threaded wire.(3)Limit instances of guidewire repositioning and maintain strict co-axial trajectory during overdrilling.Level of EvidenceIII, Retrospective Cohort Study

Introduction

Medial epicondyle humerus fractures are a common pediatric elbow injury, accounting for 11%–20% of all pediatric elbow fractures [1]. Controversy remains regarding the management of these injuries, with treatment strategy influenced by various factors including patient age, degree of displacement, activity level or sport of preference, entrapment and neurovascular status [[2], [3], [4]]. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) with Kirschner wires (k-wires) or cannulated screws has been demonstrated to convey excellent outcomes regardless of fixation method [3,5,6].

Several intraoperative implant-related complications during ORIF with single cannulated screws have been described, such as screw thread delamination [4,[7], [8], [9]]. Guidewire or k-wire breakage during drilling or screw insertion for various orthopaedic conditions has been previously reported [10]. This complication may lead to concerns about retained metallic fragments, fragment migration, difficulty achieving stable fixation, and the potential for iatrogenic injury [[11], [12], [13]]. Two case report series described guidewire breakage during cannulated screw insertion in pediatric hip surgery [14,15]. To our knowledge, however, no prior study has systematically evaluated the rate of guidewire breakage during medial epicondyle fracture fixation. Understanding the incidence and implications of guidewire breakage during this operation is critical for improving operative efficiency, guiding preoperative informed consent, informing intraoperative decision-making, and potentially influencing the design of pediatric-specific fixation systems.

This study aimed to determine the frequency of intraoperative guidewire breakage during open reduction and internal fixation of pediatric medial epicondyle fractures at a single tertiary pediatric hospital.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study evaluated all medial epicondyle humerus fractures that were surgically treated with ORIF from January 2020 to October 2025 at a tertiary children's hospital. Patients were identified using procedural code 24575. Inclusion criteria were defined as all patients under 18 years of age who underwent cannulated screw fixation of a medial epicondyle humerus fracture. Exclusion criteria included adult patients, patients with alternative fracture management strategies (nonoperative treatment, k-wire fixation, or bone anchor/suture-bridge fixation), revision procedures, or cases with inadequate fluoroscopic imaging.

Data collection

Patient charts and radiographs were reviewed following institutional review board approval. Data collected included patient demographics such as sex, age, race, and body mass index (BMI), tourniquet time, implant characteristics including guidewire size and profile (smooth or threaded), cannulated screw commercial system and size, the use of a washer, and bicortical or unicortical fixation. Operative reports and fluoroscopic imaging were independently reviewed for the occurrence of guidewire breakage. Postoperative complications including wound dehiscence, implant loosening, or refracture were also recorded. The primary outcome was the frequency of intraoperative guidewire breakage during ORIF. Secondary outcomes included the association between guidewire diameter or screw size and guidewire breakage, and operative time differences between cases with and without guidewire breakage.

Surgical technique

All cases were performed by one of six fellowship-trained pediatric orthopaedic surgeons using a standardized posteromedial approach to the elbow. Patients were treated in both prone and supine position, though the prone position is currently preferred at our institution [16]. After reduction and provisional fixation with a guidewire, definitive fixation was achieved using an appropriately sized, partially threaded cannulated screw under fluoroscopic guidance. The use of a washer was left to the surgeon’s discretion. When guidewire fracture occurred, the location (intraosseous versus protruding) and management method (left in situ or removed) were documented.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, 2019) by a trained biostatistician. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all continuous and categorical variables. Continuous variables including patient age, BMI, and tourniquet time, were summarized as means with standard deviations. Between-group comparisons (guidewire breakage vs. no breakage) were performed using independent-samples t-tests. Categorical variables including sex, implant manufacturer, screw size, and guidewire diameter, were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using chi-square test. A two-tailed P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the rarity of guidewire breakage events, the study was not powered for multivariable analysis. Therefore, all findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than inferential.

Results

A total of 99 pediatric patients underwent surgical treatment of a medial epicondyle humerus fracture at our institution during the study period. The cohort was evenly distributed between male (n = 50) and female patients (n = 49) with a mean age of 11.9 ± 0.5 years (range: 5 years–17 years). The average BMI was 22.0 ± 1.1 kg/m2 (range: 13.2–42.6 kg/m2). OrthoPediatrics (Warsaw, IN) was the most commonly used cannulated screw system (n = 90, 90.9%) followed by Depuy Synthes (n = 7, 7.1%, Warsaw, IN) and headless screws from Vilex (n = 2, 2.0%, McMinnville, TN). There was a mean follow-up of 4.8 ± 0.9 months (range: 0.3 months–24.8 months), excluding four cases without any documented postoperative follow-up. Of the 95 cases with documented follow-up, eight (8.4%) had postoperative complications including refracture (n = 3), nonunion (n = 2), wound dehiscence (n = 1), surgical site infection (n = 1), and median nerve palsy (n = 1). Notably, the patient who went on to develop a postoperative median nerve palsy had initially sustained a concomitant elbow fracture-dislocation. There were no reported cases of ulnar nerve palsy. An additional 19 patients underwent elective screw removal.

Discussion

In this retrospective review of pediatric patients undergoing operative management for medial epicondyle humerus fractures, we found that guidewire breakage occurred in 6.1% of patients, primarily during guidewire repositioning or over-drilling, and was associated with significantly longer tourniquet times. Compared with the reported historical rate of orthopaedic instrument breakage of 0.18%–0.36%, this technical complication represents a meaningful disruption to surgical workflow and is associated with longer operative times [10,17,18]. Despite this, none of the retained guidewire fragments in our cohort were associated with infection, migration, or compromise of fixation stability.

Current literature on medial epicondyle humerus fractures emphasizes functional outcomes and postoperative complications including nonunion, need for hardware removal, and ulnar nerve irritation [3,6,19,20]. Within the pediatric population, Robb et al. [14] and Ilizaliturri et al. [15] cite a combined seven instances of guidewire breakage during slipped capital femoral epiphysis fixation, noting concern of hardware migration into the hip joint and intrapelvic cavity. In their review of general fracture care, Schwend et al. [23] report four cases of 1.25 mm guidewire breakage specifically when using a 3.5 mm cannulated screw system. Our findings expand upon this by providing the first quantitative analysis of guidewire breakage in pediatric medial epicondyle fixation, thereby highlighting a complication that may be more common than previously recognized.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the risk of intraoperative guidewire breakage, including reduced guidewire integrity and tensile strength secondary to repeated use, deformation, or contact with dense cortical bone, particularly in the pediatric distal humerus [7,8,24]. Price et al. [17] additionally suggested that the steep drilling angle required during distal humerus fixation increases the likelihood of instrument failure. In our series, all six cases of guidewire failure occurred during either wire repositioning or over-drilling. Repeated reinsertion of the same guidewire, as is often necessary when attempting fracture reduction, appears to exacerbate metal fatigue, thereby heightening the risk of wire fracture [12,13,18,25]. Every instance of breakage during over-drilling was preceded by visible bending of the guidewire, most commonly at points of resistance along the dense cortical bone of the anterior humeral cortex or the region above the olecranon fossa (Fig. 3).Figure 3Deflection of the guidewire on the anterior cortex as easily visualized on a lateral fluoroscopic image intraoperatively.Figure 3

When drilling was continued over a deformed wire, shearing occurred at the site of curvature. To minimize the risk of guidewire breakage, several technical modifications are recommended (Table 4). These include limiting multiple adjustments of the same guidewire, using fluoroscopy judiciously to detect deflection or deformation early, and considering wire diameter and tip design (trocar, diamond, or fluted) as potential factors influencing resistance and fatigue [26]. Avoidance of the olecranon fossa is strongly recommended as it is likely that any retained foreign body here would be symptomatic. The use of the largest possible guidewire may also aid in mitigating some of the breakage risk that comes with using smaller diameter guidewires. The use of threaded versus smooth guidewires is another potential cause of concern. While the total denominator of threaded versus smooth guidewires used over the 99 fractures was not recorded in this study, all six cases of breakage occurred with a threaded guidewire. It is possible that threaded wires may be more prone to breakage as the threading decreases the effective diameter of the guidewire tip. Additionally, threaded guidewires allow for decreased tactile feedback when reaching the far cortex compared with smooth guidewires. Although not noted in the present study, this may allow for inadvertently placing the guidewire across the far cortex or inadvertent advancement of the guidewire when over-drilling, both of which can increase the risk of radial nerve injury as noted by Marcu et al. [27] Finally, the junction between the threaded tip and the nonthreaded shaft of the guidewire may represent a structural weak point that is more susceptible to breakage. While a definite causative relationship cannot be determined from the present data, the use of a threaded versus smooth guidewire merits important consideration during preoperative planning.

All six cases reported in this study involved the OrthoPediatrics cannulated screw system, however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. The predominance of this system in our study (90.9% of total cases) limits the ability to infer a causal relationship and may simply reflect the overrepresentation of OrthoPediatrics in our study cohort. Subtle design and material differences between systems such as guidewire alloy composition, core stiffness, and tip geometry, may influence their mechanical performance under torsional loading [13,28]. For example, the OrthoPediatrics cannulated screw systems used for pediatric fracture fixation utilize stainless steel guidewires [29]. In contrast, titanium or cobalt-chrome alloy guidewires used by Depuy Synthes and Zimmer Biomet (Warsaw, IN) respectively, are reported to provide greater stiffness and torsional resistance than stainless-steel variants [13,30,31]. These manufacturer-level differences may potentially contribute to differing rates of deformation or breakage under comparable operative conditions, but comparative mechanical testing was beyond the scope of this study. Future studies evaluating alloy-specific or manufacturer-specific performance characteristics could help identify system-level design factors contributing to failure.

Although wire breakage was associated with an increased tourniquet time, the absence of short-term postoperative complications in our study suggests that deeply embedded intraosseous fragments can be safely left in situ if they do not interfere with motion (i.e. are not located within the olecranon fossa) or irritate neurovascular structures. This mirrors the findings by Murawski et al. [8] who were able to safely leave delaminated screw threads in situ during medial epicondyle humerus fracture fixation. Preoperative discussion of this potential complication with patient caregivers may help alleviate concern if a fragment is retained.

Limitations

This study is limited by its retrospective design, single-institution setting, and small number of guidewire breakage events, which restrict the statistical power to detect predictive risk factors. Consequently, the results should be interpreted as descriptive observations rather than inferential conclusions. Operative variables such as drill torque, hand pressure, the use of threaded versus smooth-tipped guidewires, and the number of wire reinsertions were not standardized or consistently recorded, preventing assessment of these potentially critical factors. Additionally, the mean follow-up period of 4.8 months in our guidewire breakage cohort may be insufficient to capture late complications such as wire migration or delayed infection. However, we identified no short-term complications and the lack of additional follow-up in these patients may indicate that they remained asymptomatic. Our cohort study represents the first systematic quantification of guidewire failure in a large pediatric elbow cohort. We establish an incidence estimate and offer practical recommendations to potentially mitigate intraoperative hardware failure.

Conclusion

Guidewire breakage during cannulated screw fixation of pediatric medial epicondyle fractures is uncommon but clinically relevant, occurring in 6.1% of cases and prolonging operative time without increasing postoperative complications. Awareness of possible causes and adherence to meticulous guidewire insertion and over-drilling technique may minimize its occurrence. Future multicenter or biomechanical investigations may clarify system-specific failure mechanisms and inform the development of more durable, pediatric-appropriate instrumentation.
