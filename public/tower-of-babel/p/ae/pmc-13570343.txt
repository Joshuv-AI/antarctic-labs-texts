Mannosylated chitosan-PLGA delivers Ganoderma lucidum triterpenes for oral immunoprotection against H9N2 in broilers
Authors: Ruonan Bo, Ya Tao, Hailong Hong, Peiying Hu, Weihua Wu, Jiahao Wu, Jianqiang Tang, Huijun Hu, Dingzhu Li, Mingjiang Liu, Jingui Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The H9N2 subtype of avian influenza virus remains a major threat to poultry production and public health, creating a need for effective vaccines that induce mucosal immunity. Oral immunization is attractive for poultry because it is convenient and suitable for mass application, but its efficacy is often limited by antigen degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and poor intestinal uptake. In this study, we developed a mannosylated chitosan-poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoemulsion loaded with Ganoderma lucidum triterpenes (MPG) as an oral mucosal adjuvant and delivery system, and subsequently incorporated inactivated H9N2 virus to prepare an oral inactivated vaccine. The MPG nanoemulsion showed a mean particle size of 439.7 ± 2.7 nm, with a negative zeta potential, good stability, protection under simulated gastric conditions, rapid release under intestinal conditions, favorable mucoadhesive properties and a promising immunostimulatory activity in vitro. Broiler chickens were orally immunized twice, and immune responses and protective efficacy were evaluated. Compared with the other groups, the MPG-adjuvanted vaccine significantly increased serum hemagglutination inhibition antibody titers and intestinal H9N2-specific secretory immunoglobulin A levels. After homologous challenge, vaccinated chickens showed reduced oropharyngeal viral shedding and milder lung lesions. In jejunal tissue, the MPG-adjuvanted vaccine increased the RNA expression of immunoglobulin A, polymeric immunoglobulin receptor, J-chain, IL-17, TNF-α, and IL-10, suggesting enhanced intestinal antibody transport and local immune responses. These results indicate that MPG improves the oral adjuvant activity of Ganoderma lucidum triterpenes and enhances both systemic and intestinal immune responses to an inactivated H9N2 vaccine. This strategy may provide a practical approach for oral mucosal vaccination against H9N2 in poultry.

Introduction

The H9N2 avian influenza virus (AIV) is a subtype of influenza A virus with a single-stranded, negative-sense RNA genome (Zhang et al., 2023). It belongs to the genus Influenza A virus within the family Orthomyxoviridae, and its surface glycoproteins are hemagglutinin (HA) and neuraminidase (NA) (Lee and Saif, 2009). H9N2 AIV has become enzootic in poultry in many regions and remains a major cause of economic loss because it decreases production performance, causes respiratory disease, and increases susceptibility to secondary infections (Liu et al., 2023a; Zhou et al., 2025). Beyond its veterinary impact, H9N2 also poses a persistent zoonotic risk because it can sporadically infect humans and has repeatedly donated internal genes to other avian influenza subtypes, thereby contributing to the emergence of reassortant viruses with altered host range and transmissibility (Chang et al., 2025). Accordingly, controlling H9N2 circulation in poultry is a core component of both animal health management and broader One Health risk mitigation.

Vaccination is the primary intervention to reduce H9N2-associated disease burden in commercial poultry. In recent years, significant progress has been made in H9N2 vaccine research, primarily involving inactivated vaccines, recombinant protein vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and gene-engineered vaccines (Su et al., 2020). Inactivated vaccines are widely used because of their established safety and manufacturability; however, field experience and recent mechanistic evidence indicate that conventional inactivated vaccines often fail to fully suppress viral replication at the upper respiratory mucosa, thereby allowing continued virus shedding and transmission within vaccinated flocks (Hu et al., 2025). Rapid antigenic drift and lineage turnover further exacerbate this limitation, requiring periodic updates of candidate vaccine viruses to maintain antigenic match with circulating strains (Zheng et al., 2022). These constraints highlight a practical need for mucosal vaccines that better control early infection at mucosal entry sites and more effectively block subsequent viral transmission. Mucosal immunization, particularly via the oral route, offers operational advantages for poultry (needle-free, scalable administration) and immunological advantages through induction of secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) at mucosal surfaces, where sIgA mediates immune exclusion and can intercept pathogens before epithelial invasion (Bemark and Angeletti, 2021; Sinha et al., 2025). Different mucosal immunization routes have distinct advantages and limitations. Intranasal administration directly stimulates respiratory mucosal immunity and local IgA responses but is technically challenging for mass application. Aerosol or spray vaccination provides broader mucosal coverage and can induce balanced systemic and mucosal immunity; however, it is associated with higher antigen loss, dose variability, and sensitivity to environmental conditions. Oral administration is convenient and cost-effective for large-scale poultry vaccination and effectively induces intestinal sIgA as well as systemic immune responses (Mantis et al., 2011). Its main limitations are antigen degradation in the gastrointestinal tract and potential immune tolerance, which can be partly addressed by using mucoadhesive nanocarriers and mannose-functionalized delivery strategies to protect antigens and enhance uptake by antigen-presenting cells (Zhang et al., 2025; Nahar et al., 2022). Consequently, effective mucosal vaccines generally require not only suitable antigens but also mucosal adjuvants that enhance antigen delivery and immune stimulation. Such adjuvants can improve antigen delivery, enhance immunogenicity, promote secretory IgA production, and support cellular immune responses (Tsai et al., 2023).

Safe immunomodulatory compounds derived from natural products are also increasingly being explored as mucosal adjuvants. Ganoderma lucidum triterpenoids (Glts) are lanostane-type tetracyclic triterpenoid compounds from the medicinal mushroom Ganoderma lucidum, with molecular masses typically ranging from 400 to 600 (Liang et al., 2019). Glts exhibit favorable lipophilicity and possess a broad range of biological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antitumor, immunostimulatory, and antiviral effects (Huang et al., 2017). These properties are mechanistically relevant because innate immune activation can influence the magnitude and quality of vaccine-induced responses. However, the poor aqueous solubility of Glts limits their oral administration. In addition, the lack of targeted delivery further restricts their application as oral immunomodulators. Therefore, it is essential to develop an oral delivery strategy that can selectively deliver Glts to intestinal antigen-presenting cells (APCs) and thereby enhance their immunostimulatory effects.

Chitosan is a cationic biopolymer with favorable biocompatibility and mucoadhesive properties, which can increase antigen residence time and facilitate interaction with mucosal tissues. The poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) is a biodegradable polymer extensively used for controlled release and antigen protection in vaccine delivery (Singh et al., 2016). In poultry models, chitosan-coated PLGA-based delivery systems have been shown to prolong drug release and enhance vaccine immunogenicity (Feng et al., 2021; Tao et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2014). Combining chitosan derivatives with PLGA can integrate mucoadhesion, controlled release and structural stability, which is particularly desirable for oral delivery. In addition to passive delivery, active targeting of APCs may further improve immunogenicity. Mannose receptors are highly expressed on macrophages and dendritic cells, and mannosylation of particulate carriers has been shown to enhance receptor-mediated uptake, promote APC maturation, and improve antigen presentation efficiency (Feng et al., 2021; Pei et al., 2021). Mannosylated chitosan-PLGA systems therefore provide a biologically grounded strategy to increase antigen capture and immune priming at mucosal sites.

In this study, Glts were encapsulated in a chitosan-PLGA carrier, and the chitosan surface was further functionalized with mannose to generate a mucosal immunoadjuvant (MPG). Surface mannosylation was designed to enhance APC targeting and uptake via mannose receptor–mediated endocytosis, thereby potentiating the immunostimulatory activity of Glts. Using this platform, an oral vaccine was formulated by loading inactivated H9N2 virus into the MPG delivery system. In vivo experiments were performed to assess vaccine-induced systemic and intestinal mucosal immune responses as well as protective efficacy, whereas complementary in vitro assays were conducted to elucidate the adjuvant-related mechanisms of MPG, including APC activation and antigen uptake. Overall, this work provides a rigorous conceptual framework and experimental evidence to support the development of orally delivered mucosal adjuvants and oral H9N2 vaccines.

Materials and methods

Materials

Mannosylated chitosan was obtained from Shunna Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA, 50:50, MW=0.7∼2Mw) copolymer was purchased from the Shandong Pharmaceutical Research Institute. Ganoderma triterpenoids were obtained from Kunming Yuncao Plant Technology Co., Ltd. Goat Anti-Chicken IgA H&L was obtained from Abcam. The Hifair Ⅲ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR was the product of Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd. SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus was purchased from Novoprotein. Total RNA extraction reagent was purchased from Vazyme Biotechnology Co., Ltd. TMB was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. ELISA plates were purchased from Suzhou cellpro biotechnology Co., Ltd.

RAW264.7 cells were obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences Cell Bank (Shanghai, China). Cells were maintained at 37°C in 5 % CO2. The H9N2 virus (A/chicken/XZ/508/2019) was donated from the Key Laboratory of Jiangsu Preventive Veterinary Medicine, Key Laboratory for Avian Preventive Medicine, Ministry of Education, College of Veterinary Medicine, Yangzhou University, and cultivated in the allantoic cavities of SPF embryonated eggs.

Preparation and characterization of MPG

MPG nanoemulsions were prepared using a double-emulsion solvent evaporation method. Briefly, PLGA and Glts were dissolved in dichloromethane, and a small amount of ultrapure water was added for ultrasonic emulsification to obtain a primary water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion. The primary emulsion was then added to an aqueous mannosylated chitosan solution and sonicated again to form a water-in-oil-in-water (W/O/W) emulsion. The mixture was stirred in a 42°C water bath to evaporate and remove the dichloromethane, and the resulting nanoemulsions were collected for subsequent characterization. Blank nanoemulsions (MP) were prepared using the same procedure without Glts. Mannosylated chitosan nanoemulsions (MCS) were prepared in ultrapure water using the corresponding procedure.

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading content (DLC) of Glts in MPG was determined by a centrifugation method. Due to the poor water solubility of Glts, non-encapsulated Glts precipitated after centrifugation, while encapsulated Glts remained dispersed in the nanoemulsion. Glts content was quantified using the vanillin–glacial acetic acid–perchloric acid colorimetric method. MPG was centrifuged at 6,000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was collected and diluted to a theoretical Glts concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. Aliquots (0.2, 0.4, and 0.6 mL) were analyzed following the standard curve procedure. Absorbance was measured, and the actual Glts concentration was calculated from the standard curve. Glts EE (%) = actual Glts / total Glts × 100%; Glts DLC (%) = actual Glts / total weight of nanoemulsions × 100%.

The particle size and zeta potential of the nanoemulsion were measured using a Malvern particle analyzer (Malvern Instruments, Malvern, UK), while transmission electron microscopy (TEM; Hitachi HT7800, TKY, JPN) was applied to assess the nanoemulsion morphology. In addition, we also evaluated the release efficiency of Ganoderma triterpenes from the MPG in gastrointestinal simulated fluids, as well as its mucosal adhesion.

Preparation of the oral inactivated H9N2 vaccine

H9N2 virus was heat-inactivated at 70°C for 15 min. Samples were collected at 0, 5, 10, and 15 min for TCID₅₀ determination in MDCK cells. Log reduction values (LRV) were calculated as LRV = lg (initial titer) − lg (final titer), with LRV ≥ 4 considered effective. Kinetic parameters, including D₁₀ and t₉₉.₉₉, were calculated using a first-order linear regression. Complete inactivation was confirmed by three blind passages in MDCK cells (TCID₅₀ < 0.1). Viral reactivation was assessed by incubating the inactivated antigen at 37°C for 7 days. HA titers were measured to confirm antigen retention (≥6 log₂). Only samples meeting these criteria were used for vaccine formulation.

The oral inactivated H9N2 vaccine was prepared using a double-emulsion solvent evaporation method. Briefly, the inactivated H9N2 viral allantoic fluid was used as the internal aqueous phase and emulsified with the organic phase containing PLGA and Glts to form a primary water-in-oil emulsion. Mannosylated chitosan was dissolved in the inactivated viral allantoic fluid and added during the second emulsification step to generate the final water-in-oil-in-water emulsion. The same input amount of inactivated H9N2 antigen was used for all vaccine groups to ensure antigen-dose equivalence among MPG-WIV, MP-WIV, MCS + WIV, Glts + WIV, and WIV groups.

Animal immunity

The immunogenicity of the oral whole-inactivated H9N2 vaccine was evaluated in one-day-old male Arbor Acres broiler chickens. The chickens were housed in a conventional barrier animal facility at the College of Veterinary Medicine, Yangzhou University, under standard husbandry conditions, with ad libitum access to water and a commercial broiler starter diet, Zhengda 510 broiler starter feed. A total of 180 one-day-old broiler chickens were randomly allocated to six groups (n = 30 per group), including MPG-WIV, MP-WIV, MCS + WIV, Glts + WIV, WIV and Control group. chickens were orally immunized with 0.3 mL per bird, followed by a booster immunization 10 days later. Hemagglutination inhibition assay

Blood samples (n = 5) were collected from each experimental group of chickens during the 2nd, 3rd, and 4th weeks after the second immunization. The blood samples were incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes to allow clotting, followed by incubation at 4°C for 30 minutes for serum separation. The samples were then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 5 minutes, and the collected serum was stored at -80°C. The serum was analyzed for AIV-specific HI titers in accordance with the standards outlined in the OIE Manual.

Determination of anti-H9N2 specific sIgA

The levels of H9N2 AIV-specific sIgA in the small intestinal lavage fluid were measured using ELISA. In brief, the plate was pre-coated with H9N2 WIV antigens (1:32, 100 μL per well) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The plate was then blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 2 hours at 37°C. Intestinal lavage fluid, diluted 1:2 in 0.1% BSA (100 μL per well), was added and incubated for 2 hours at 37°C. After five washes with PBS or PBS-Tween, 100 μL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-chicken sIgA (1:40,000) was added and incubated for another 2 hours at 37°C. Following incubation, 100 μL of TMB substrate solution was added and incubated for 10 minutes at 37°C. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 μL of 2 M H2SO4, and the optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

The qRT-PCR for cytokines

Cells and jejunum tissue samples were extracted with TRIzol reagent (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and reverse transcribed to cDNA with HiScript II QRT SuperMix (Vazyme). A CFX96 Real-time PCR system was used to perform RT-qPCR with SYBR (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2^ -ΔΔCt method. Virus challenge experiment

Two weeks after the second immunization, chickens in all groups were intranasally challenged with 0.2 mL of H9N2 AIV at a dose of 1 × 107 EID50. Pharyngeal swabs were collected from chickens in each group on d 3, 5, and 7 post-challenge, and viral titers were determined by TCID50 in MDCK cells. Lungs were collected on d 7 post-challenge and examined by haematoxylin and eosin staining (HE).

Expression of cell surface molecule CD80, CD86, and MHC II

Flow cytometry was used to analyze the impact of MPG on the expression of surface molecules in macrophages. The working concentrations of Glts, MP, and MPG used in RAW264.7 cell experiments were determined based on preliminary cytotoxicity testing. RAW264.7 cells were treated with serial concentrations of Glts, MP, or MPG for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed using a CCK-8 assay. Based on these results, 125 μg/mL Glts, 100 μg/mL MP and 100 μg/mL MPG were selected as the final working concentrations for subsequent experiments. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 106 cells/well) were plated in a 6-well cell culture plate and incubated for 12 hours. MPG (100 μg/mL), MP (100 μg/mL), Glts (125 μg/mL), LPS (1 μg/mL) was then added to each well and incubated for an additional 24 hours. Finally, the cells were suspended and incubated with anti-CD80, anti-CD86 and anti-MHC II at 4°C for 30 min and analyzed by the CytExpert flow cytometer.

Cellular uptake

The antigen uptake capacity of the cells was analyzed by flow cytometry and visualized using fluorescence microscopy. In brief, RAW264.7 cells (1 × 106cells/well) were plated in a 6-well cell culture plate and incubated for 12 hours. MPG and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled OVA were then added to each well and incubated for an additional 24 hours. The fluorescence intensity of FITC in the cells of each group was analyzed using fluorescence microscopy. In addition, the cells were collected and the number of FITC-labeled cells was quantified using flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) when multiple groups were compared, and the differences among the groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, while P < 0.01 was considered extremely significant.

Results

Characterization of MPG

Two nanoemulsion formulations, MP and MPG, were prepared and systematically characterized. Transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 1A) showed that both MP and MPG displayed well-defined spherical morphology; moreover, encapsulated Glts were clearly visible within the MPG nanoemulsion. Dynamic light scattering analysis (Fig. 1B) showed mean hydrodynamic diameters of 387.3 ± 8.3 nm for MP and 439.7 ± 2.7 nm for MPG, with narrow size distributions (PDI: 0.18 ± 0.03 and 0.16 ± 0.01, respectively). Both formulations carried a negative surface charge, with zeta potentials of −30.1 ± 1.1 mV (MP) and −34.0 ± 0.6 mV (MPG). The EE and DLC of MPG were 65.15% and 32.58%, respectively, indicating efficient incorporation of Glts into the mannosylated chitosan-PLGA nanoformulation.Fig. 1Characterization of MPG.A: TEM morphology of MP and MPG; B: Particle size distribution of MP and MPG; C: Standard curve for determination of oleanolic acid; D: Drug release curves of MPG in GSF and ISF; E: Mucosal adhesion characteristics of MP and MPG.Fig 1 dummy alt text

In vitro release profiling (Fig. 1D) demonstrated that MPG released 27% of the encapsulated Glts in simulated gastric fluid within 2 h. After transfer to simulated intestinal fluid, a marked burst release was observed, reaching 53% within 1 h, followed by sustained release thereafter.

Mucoadhesive behavior was assessed by monitoring particle-size changes upon incubation with porcine gastric mucin (PGM). Following sufficient ultrasonic dispersion, the PGM suspension (5 g/L) displayed a multimodal distribution, comprising mucin monomers (10 nm), colloidal particles (100 nm), and larger aggregates (490 nm). Incubation with either MP or MPG reduced the abundance of monomeric/colloidal mucin species and disrupted larger aggregates, resulting in the formation of stable mucin–particle complexes with an average size of approximately 500 nm, consistent with efficient mucin interaction and complexation.

MPG activated RAW264.7 cells

Macrophages are crucial antigen-presenting cells that play a key role in T cell activation and the promotion of cellular immune responses. The increased expression levels of cell surface molecules CD80, CD86 and MHC Ⅱ serve as important markers of macrophage activation. In the present study, after 24 h of treatment with LPS or MPG, RAW264.7 cells showed significantly increased expression of CD80, CD86 and MHC Ⅱ compared with the other groups (Fig. 2A). After macrophage activation, cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α are released to promote T cell and B cell immune responses. MPG enhanced macrophage phagocytosis of FITC-OVA

To determine whether MPG enhanced antigen uptake by macrophages, RAW264.7 cells were incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled ovalbumin (FITC-OVA), and cellular uptake was evaluated by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3, fluorescence microscopy revealed weak green fluorescence in the control group, whereas fluorescence intensity increased in all treated groups. Among them, the MP and MPG groups showed markedly stronger intracellular fluorescence signals than the LPS and Glts groups, with the strongest fluorescence observed in the MPG group, indicating enhanced uptake of FITC-OVA by RAW264.7 cells. Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed these findings. The proportions of FITC-OVA-positive cells were 3.46%, 18.95%, 26.55%, 46.52%, and 53.76% in the control, LPS, Glts, MP, and MPG groups, respectively. Compared with the control group, all treatments significantly increased FITC-OVA uptake by RAW264.7 cells. Notably, the MPG group showed the highest proportion of FITC-OVA-positive cells, followed by the MP group, and both were significantly higher than the LPS and Glts groups. Body weight and immune organ indices

Before animal immunization, the inactivated H9N2 antigen was verified for safety and antigen retention. Heat treatment at 70°C for 15 min resulted in a 4.74 lg reduction in viral titer, and no infectious virus was recovered after three consecutive blind passages in MDCK cells, with the final TCID₅₀ < 0.1. No viral reactivation was detected after incubation at 37°C for 7 days, and HA titers remained at 6.25–6.92 log₂, indicating acceptable antigen retention. These results confirmed that the inactivated antigen was suitable for vaccine preparation.

Compared to the control group, the vaccine groups with different adjuvants showed no significant effects on the body weight or immune organ indices of the experimental chickens (Fig. 4B-4E). Oral administration of MPG enhanced serum HI antibody titers and intestinal sIgA responses

To determine whether oral administration of MPG enhances vaccine-induced immune responses, we performed an in vivo immunization study in chickens. At 14, 21, and 28 days after the second immunization, chickens receiving MPG as the adjuvant exhibited significantly higher AIV-specific HI antibody titers than those in all other groups, and the elevated titers were maintained over time (Fig. 5A-5C). Consistently, intestinal H9N2-specific sIgA levels measured at 7, 14, 21, and 28 days after the second immunization were significantly increased in the MPG-adjuvanted group compared with the other groups, reaching a peak at day 21 (Fig. 5D-5G). Pathological changes in the lungs of chickens challenged with H9N2

To evaluate the protective efficacy of the MPG vaccine, all immunized chickens were intranasally challenged with 200 μL of H9N2 AIV at 1 × 107 EID50. All chickens in each group survived throughout the post-challenge observation period, and the survival rate was 100% in all groups. The protective efficacy of the vaccine was mainly evaluated by oropharyngeal viral shedding and lung histopathological changes. The results indicated that the chickens in the control group experienced significant lung pathology on 7 d after infection with the H9N2 virus. The lungs of chickens in the MPG group showed markedly milder lesions than those in the control group (Fig. 6A). Effect of MPG on cytokines in the jejunal tissue of immunized chickens

The immune response, including the production of cytokines, such as TNF- α, IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-10, and IL-17, is generally recognized as an essential factor in the protection against influenza. Studies have also shown that IgA, J-chain, and pIgR can influence the synthesis and secretion of sIgA. In the present study, to investigate the effect of MPG on the intestinal immune response in experimental chickens, we measured the mRNA levels of IgA, pIgR, J-chain, IL-17, TNF-α and IL-10 in jejunal tissue after immunization using qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 7A-7F, chickens immunized with the MPG vaccine showed the highest mRNA expression levels of IgA, pIgR, J-chain, IL-17, TNF-α, and IL-10 among all groups at 21 days after the second immunization. These findings suggest that MPG not only promoted the IgA secretory axis in the intestine but also enhanced local immune activation. Discussion

H9N2 AIV remains a persistent challenge for poultry production, and although inactivated vaccines are widely applied, they may not fully prevent replication in the upper airway or sufficiently reduce transmission and shedding in vaccinated flocks (Cui et al., 2021; Quan et al., 2025). Oral vaccination is attractive for poultry because it is needle-free and amenable to mass administration, but effective oral immunization requires overcoming gastrointestinal degradation and limited mucosal residence time while providing adequate innate stimulation to avoid weak responses or tolerance (Kang et al., 2018; Vela Ramirez et al., 2017). The physicochemical profile of MPG supports feasibility for oral delivery. Particle size and zeta potential are key parameters for assessing the size distribution and stability of nanoemulsions. The mean particle diameter of MP was 387.3±8.3 nm, exhibiting a negative surface charge. Following encapsulation of Glts, the average zeta potential of MPG shifted from –30.1±1.1 mV to –34.0 ± 0.6 mV, and the mean particle diameter increased to 439.7±2.7 nm. Studies have demonstrated that nanoemulsions with absolute zeta potential values greater than 30 mV exhibit superior physical stability and enhanced drug delivery capabilities (Arshad et al., 2024). The PDI is a critical parameter for assessing emulsion uniformity; a PDI below 0.2 indicates a monodisperse system (Kamwilaisak et al., 2022). The PDI values of both MP and MPG were below 0.2, indicating that the nanoemulsions possessed good stability. The dialysis method is one of the techniques employed to assess in vitro drug release. In vitro release studies reveal minimal payload release in simulated gastric fluid, accompanied by a rapid burst release and subsequent sustained release in simulated intestinal fluid. These findings confirm that MPG can protect the encapsulated payload during gastric transit and promote intestinal absorption, which is essential for the rational design of oral vaccine formulations. Additionally, both MP and MPG formed stable complexes with porcine gastric mucin, consistent with the mucoadhesive and complexation behavior often associated with chitosan-based systems and potentially favorable for prolonging mucosal contact time and enhancing uptake opportunities (Gaglio et al., 2023; TM et al., 2018).

IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α are pro-inflammatory cytokines involved in innate immune activation and the initiation of adaptive immune responses. MHC II mediates exogenous antigen presentation to CD4⁺ T cells, whereas CD80 and CD86 provide essential co-stimulatory signals for T-cell activation (Cobb et al., 2004; Yoo et al., 2002). Mannosylated nanoparticles can be recognized by mannose receptors expressed on macrophages and dendritic cells, thereby facilitating receptor-mediated uptake and antigen presentation. Consistent with this mechanism, MPG enhanced FITC-OVA uptake by RAW264.7 cells and increased the expression of CD80, CD86, and MHC II, indicating improved APC activation and antigen-presenting capacity. The increased mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α further suggest that MPG provided immune-stimulatory signals required for downstream adaptive immune activation. After oral delivery, the mucoadhesive properties of MPG may also prolong antigen retention in the intestinal mucosa and increase interaction with gut-associated lymphoid tissue, thereby promoting local mucosal immune responses.

Glts may further contribute to vaccine efficacy mainly through immunomodulatory activity rather than direct antiviral action in the present formulation. Previous studies have reported that triterpenoids from Ganoderma species possess influenza-related antiviral potential, including neuraminidase inhibitory activity, which may interfere with the release of progeny virions and limit viral spread (Zhu et al., 2015). In addition, Glts have been reported to regulate inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, macrophage activation, and immune-related signaling pathways, including NF-κB and MAPK pathways (Dudhgaonkar et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2019; Ahmad, 2018). However, direct inhibition of H9N2 replication by Glts was not evaluated in this study; therefore, their antiviral role should be interpreted cautiously. The immunomodulatory properties of Glts are more directly relevant to the present results. When incorporated into the mannosylated chitosan-PLGA delivery system, Glts may act as natural immunomodulatory adjuvant components that support APC activation and enhance vaccine-induced mucosal and systemic immunity.

H9N2 AIV binds to host cells via its HA protein interacting with mucosal surface receptors; this interaction not only induces the host immune system to produce virus-neutralizing antibodies but also facilitates viral entry and subsequent replication within host cells (Liu et al., 2023a). sIgA serves as the first line of defense protecting mucosal surfaces from pathogenic microorganisms. It blocks antigens and pathogens from binding to epithelial receptors by trapping them within the mucus layer, and it promotes their removal via peristaltic movements and mucociliary activity, thereby facilitating the clearance of these antigens and pathogens (Mantis et al., 2011). Therefore, actively elevating mucosal levels of H9N2-specific sIgA can effectively inhibit the adhesion and invasion of H9N2 AIV into host cells. Conventional inactivated H9N2 avian influenza vaccines are administered primarily via subcutaneous or intramuscular injection, typically require multiple doses to achieve effective protection, and do not induce mucosal immunity (Liu et al., 2023b).

Oral vaccines can stimulate the production of sIgA, thereby activating cytokine mediated intestinal mucosal and systemic immunity, and offer advantages such as ease of administration, high uptake rates, and low cost (Mantis et al., 2011). Polymeric nanomaterials have been widely employed to construct oral delivery carriers for drugs and antigens; they not only effectively protect these agents as they traverse the highly acidic gastric environment thereby reducing the required dosage, but, through specific epitope modifications of the carrier, can also facilitate targeted delivery of drugs and antigens to designated sites (Allahyari and Mohit, 2016; Pridgen et al., 2014; Vela Ramirez et al., 2017). Among the currently explored mucosal delivery materials, mannose, chitosan, and PLGA have received considerable attention. For example, mannose-modified chitosan has been shown to enhance IgA and cellular immune responses after intranasal DNA vaccination in mice, and chitosan-PLGA carriers have also demonstrated the ability to induce cellular, humoral, and mucosal immunity in SPF chickens receiving a Newcastle disease DNA vaccine (Wu et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2014).

In this study, the whole inactivated H9N2 virus was encapsulated within the MPG to prepare an oral H9N2 vaccine. Chickens exhibited significantly higher AIV-specific HI titers after two oral immunizations and markedly increased intestinal antigen-specific sIgA, with a peak response at day 21. Because secretory IgA mediates immune exclusion by blocking pathogen access to epithelial receptors and facilitating clearance from the lumen, increasing sIgA provides a mechanistically plausible route to improved mucosal control of infection and shedding (Sinha et al., 2024). Consistent with this rationale, MPG vaccination reduced oropharyngeal viral titers and alleviated lung pathology after homologous challenge, outcomes that are especially meaningful in poultry systems where reduced shedding directly relates to flock-level transmission risk. Although the present study demonstrated enhanced intestinal mucosal immunity and protection against respiratory challenge, lung viral titration was not performed, and further studies are needed to directly quantify respiratory tissue viral loads and clarify the respiratory mucosal immune correlates induced by oral MPG-adjuvanted vaccination. The jejunal mRNA profiling further supports the activation of secretory IgA biology. Upregulation of IgA, pIgR, and J-chain is coherent with enhanced IgA production and epithelial transport of polymeric IgA into mucosal secretions, given the established role of pIgR and J-chain in secretory antibody transcytosis (Wei and Wang, 2021). Notably, IL-17 should be interpreted in the context of type 17 mucosal immunity rather than as a Th2 cytokine; IL-17/Th17 pathways have been shown to regulate intestinal pIgR expression and IgA secretion, providing a plausible link to the observed IgA axis activation (Cao et al., 2012). The concurrent increase in TNF-α and IL-10 suggests that MPG may promote immune activation while maintaining regulatory balance, which is important for mucosal adjuvants intended for production animals.

It should also be noted that the present challenge experiment was performed using the homologous H9N2 strain. Thus, the protective efficacy observed in this study should be interpreted as homologous protection rather than broad-spectrum or cross-protective immunity. Because H9N2 AIVs show continuous antigenic drift and genetic diversity, future studies should include currently prevalent heterologous strains to determine whether the oral MPG-adjuvanted vaccine can provide broader protection under field-relevant conditions.

Conclusion

In this study, we established a mannosylated chitosan-PLGA nanoemulsion platform loaded with Glts and demonstrated its potential as an oral mucosal adjuvant and delivery system for an inactivated H9N2 vaccine. MPG enhanced innate immune activation by increasing IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α expression, upregulating APC-associated co-stimulatory molecules including MHC II, CD80, and CD86, and promoting antigen uptake. In chickens, oral administration of the MPG-adjuvanted inactivated H9N2 vaccine strengthened systemic antibody responses and intestinal sIgA production, and reduced viral shedding and lung pathology after homologous challenge. Overall, these findings provide a novel strategy for developing new mucosal immune adjuvants for inactivated poultry vaccines.
