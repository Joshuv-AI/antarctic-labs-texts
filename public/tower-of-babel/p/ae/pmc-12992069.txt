A qualitative study exploring the help seeking attitudes and barriers to support for Pasifika young people
Authors: Angelica Toluono, Emily Deans, Jioji Ravulo, Elizabeth Conroy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis paper explores the barriers and enablers that influence Pacific Islander Pasifika young people's help seeking behaviours in South Western Sydney for substance use and mental health problems.Study designThis study employed qualitative methods via 'Talanoa' and Photo Voice methodology.MethodsOur study conducted ‘Talanoa’ sessions with 27 Pasifika young people, incorporating Pacific-Indigenous worldviews and the 'Talanoa' approach alongside an Interpretative Phenomenological Approach (IPA) and using a Photo-Voice method. Open coding methods were used to analyse the data, with multiple data sources used to enhance the validity of interpretation and subsequent coding.ResultsWe discovered that there is a growing concern around mental illness and substance use among Pasifika young people residing in parts of Greater Western Sydney. Findings illustrate how structural disadvantages operating from individual, community, and systemic levels serve as barriers to help seeking leaving Pasifika young people to deal with these wellbeing issues in isolation.ConclusionsThe study found that Pasifika young people often deal with mental health issues and life stress on their own due to layers of cultural stigma, and that alcohol and other drugs are sometimes used to cope with emotional stress. The study also shows that Pasifika young people are proactive in changing the rhetoric to normalise help seeking and advocating for culturally appropriate services which respect Pacific ways of ‘knowing’ and ‘being’.Keywords: Talanoa, Help seeking, Pacific young people, Qualitative, Population health

Introduction

The Pasifika community is a growing ethnic minority group [1] that makes up 1.6% of Australia's population [2] with Pasifika young people aged 15-29 accounting for over 63,957 of the Pasifika community in Australia [3]. Pasifika people and communities include those who are genealogically, spiritually and or culturally connected to the Oceania regions of Micronesia, Melanesia and Polynesia [2]. Alcohol and other drug (AOD) use and mental health problems such as suicidality, depression and anxiety, are rising among Pasifika communities more broadly [4], including among Pasifika young people residing in parts of New South Wales (NSW) [5] and Australia [6]. Pasifika communities have one of the highest suicide mortality rates in the world [7], and individuals with Pasifika heritage in Australia are disproportionately impacted by suicide when compared to other migrant populations [8]. While peer reviewed literature is limited regarding alcohol and other drug harm, some research with Pasifika young people points to the normalisation of drinking to become intoxicated [9] and the need for increased early intervention support for substance use issues [10]. With respect to the diversity and individuality of each culture within the Pasifika community [1], there are shared perspectives on mental health, whereby Pasifika communities perceive mental health to be inseparable from the overall wellbeing of the body, soul and spirit [[11], [12], [13]]. Overall ‘wellness’ among Pasifika cultures, considers physical, mental and spiritual health, family and culture, and context/environment [14]. Traditionally, wellbeing issues are often dealt with ‘in-house’ due to a general disinclination to seek help beyond the family, a strategy to avoid what is (culturally) perceived as a shameful and stigmatised pursuit [12]. Furthermore, a lack of knowledge or awareness around help seeking amongst Pasifika young people has also been reported [6], as well as a scarcity of Pasifika-led research on substance use and mental health problems [13]. Despite the increased acknowledgement of these issues in the research literature, few studies break down the societal structures and cultural elements that contribute to why this remains an ongoing concern within the Pasifika community and how service-providers can learn from Pasifika cultural knowledge and family values to provide appropriate support [11,13]. This study aimed to understand the help seeking behaviours for substance use and other mental health concerns among Pasifika young people to inform the work of community services in Australia to better support the Pasifika community.

Methods

Study design

The research is framed by a Pan-Pacific Talanoa approach which is a Pacific-Indigenous methodology that incorporates a high level of cultural safety [15], alongside an Interpretative Phenomenological Approach (IPA) which seeks to elicit perspectives and experiences to construct shared meaning [16]. Talanoa encompasses “a personal encounter where people story their issues, their realities and aspirations, allows more mo'oni (pure, real, authentic) information to be available for Pacific research than data derived from other research methods” [17]. A Talanoa approach and IPA were most suited because they serve as an interpretivist and social constructivist driven agenda that prioritises the subjective truths regarding Pasifika young people's phenomena with help-seeking for mental health and AOD. Talanoas were facilitated by a young Pasifika researcher from the local community.

Data collection

Eight talanoa sessions were conducted in a range of community services settings, negotiated with participants. After introductions, participants were encouraged to share freely and were not bound by time or a rigid framework [15]. Each session was attended by 2-8 participants. We used an adapted photo voice approach, to allow young people to convey meaning about their experiences through photos, drawing or painting. All Talanoa sessions included a group discussion about participants finished creative pieces (either in the same session or via follow-up Talanoa) [18]. All discussions were conducted in English and were audio recorded for transcription purposes. Participants were asked to respond via creative expression to the following questions:

1)Do you think there are AOD issues among Pasifika youth, and if so, what are they?

Do you think there are AOD issues among Pasifika youth, and if so, what are they?

2)What does someone from the Pasifika community have available to them to use if they had an AOD or mental health concern?

What does someone from the Pasifika community have available to them to use if they had an AOD or mental health concern?

Recruitment and setting

Snow balling strategies [19] were employed to recruit 27 Pasifika young people from Greater Western Sydney. The research team relied on existing cultural networks and community leaders to promote the study. All participants were approached via email with information about the study. Written consent was obtained from the Pasifika young person, and if they were under 18 years, also from their parent/guardian. Participants ranged in age from 15 to 27 years and came from a variety of Pasifika cultural backgrounds, including Samoan, Tongan, Fijian, Fijian Indian, Māori, Niuean, and bicultural European/Pasifika ethnic backgrounds. There was an equal distribution of both female and male participants in the study. This study was approved by the Western Sydney University Human Research Ethics Committee (H14647) and all participants received a $50 voucher as reimbursement for their time.

Data analysis

Data sources included creative expressions, spoken word, and written word (narrative) to paint a more complete picture of participants' perspectives. To begin, transcripts from Talanoa sessions were uploaded to QSR NVivo 12 for digital transcription and Quirkos was used to support data analysis which was conducted by AT and ED. Where interpretations in meaning differed between the two coders, discussions were had with the broader research team until consensus was achieved. An open coding approach [20] was implemented to highlight nuances between participants’ responses, with multiple data sources used to enhance the validity of coding. The process involved identifying any inconsistencies or differences that stood out from the analysis of transcripts.

Results

Three themes were identified in our analysis which will be explored in further detail below.

Theme 1: “Just deal with it on your own” - cultural stigma and the silencing of mental health concerns

The first theme identified in the study related to how mental health concerns and help seeking for Pasifika young peoples is commonly dealt with in isolation from family and community. Mental health appeared to be a taboo concept within the Pasifika community and helped explain why help seeking for mental health problems is very low for Pasifika young peoples. Participants shared their experiences with mental illness or with reference to mental illness experienced by relatives or friends. Without any access to mental health service-providers to receive clinical diagnosis, treatment or therapy, mental health problems such as suicidality, depression, and anxiety were identified as community concerns, as shown by the following quote:

“You isolate yourself [when you’re] mentally unwell … you don’t even want to talk to anyone that much, you just do your own thing in the outside world … coming home and just staying silent … there has been depression, anxiety, all those kind of things” (Male, Samoan, 23 years, Talanoa 1)

The idea that mental health concerns were “difficult to talk about” was a reoccurring topic discussed across all Talanoa sessions. The realities of being a Pasifika young person dealing with mental health concerns entailed a persistent dismissal when openly discussing mental health within the family or community unit, as demonstrated by the following quote:

“That topic in general is very difficult for us to discuss. Speaking about mental health or saying you have a mental health issue is looked down upon in Polynesian communities. It's because of our upbringing … mental health should not be discussed at all or brought up in the family unit … being vulnerable is shown as a sign of weakness. And as a Pacific Islander, we're brought up to be tough, to be strong, to not cry.” (Female, Tongan, 26 years, Talanoa 8)

Common Pasifika cultural views around mental health help seeking for Pasifika young peoples implied “weaknesses” followed by family and community shame, judgement, and guilt. These expectations appeared to be non-gendered, and applicable for both male and female participants. The idea of being told to put up a strong front when dealing with stressful situations and the presence of a “mocking culture” for mental health support were common forms of judgement experienced by participants when discussing emotional concerns with relatives or friends. The shame stemmed from a fear of judgement, and was experienced as a sense of inferiority, inadequacy, or bringing dishonour to family, inhibiting help seeking behaviour and encouraging a reliance on self-management of emotional issues.

“I feel like our culture just expects you to deal with it on your own.” (Female, Samoan, 20 years, Talanoa 5)

At the same time, the deep sense of isolation reflected a strong sense of ‘Mana’ (supernatural force or power) which Pasifika young peoples showed through their consideration of the collective and to protect the wellbeing of their family before considering their own. There is a complex tension here that may serve as a dilemma for Pasifika young people making decisions to seek help beyond the family unit, as illustrated by the following quote:

“Mental health is a sensitive topic to discuss within our community and is not spoken as much about as they should be. ‘Family comes first’ is a moral and value we are taught as a child and the importance of the family name. The thought of a young person reaching out to a service for assistance is less likely to occur as a young person wouldn’t want to be in a position of shaming the family name.” (Female, Samoan, 20 years, Talanoa 2)

Participants across all Talanoa sessions discussed how culture dictated their perceptions towards help seeking from service-providers. The community issue was said to be exacerbated by 1) cultural influence and 2) lack of culturally safe spaces and health literacy, which prolong those barriers and gradually impact wellbeing. Participants expressed that culture played a major role in a Pasifika young person's ability to “speak up” and “reach out” for help (as depicted in Fig. 1).

“Us Islanders … I don't want to talk too much. I don't know what we're going to do. And it's going to be hard for you to help us because we don't like to speak up.” (Female, Unidentifiable, Talanoa 7)

Theme 2: “it's a cry out for help” - the relationship between mental health and AOD use

Pasifika young peoples are not only silently dealing with mental health challenges but also seen to utilise substances as a convenient and immediate source of self-help for life stressors. AOD use was a help seeking behaviour that was preferred over seeking clinical treatment or therapy as illustrated by the following quote:

“Yeah. Drugs and alcohol, even though I did like I knew in the back of my mind, I never wanted to touch any of that stuff. But what was going on around me, family stress, you know, people passing away? It was. Yeah, like a coping mechanism, I would say, and I just didn't know anything else but that.” (Female, Samoan Māori, 24 years, Talanoa 3)

Alcohol, cannabis, Kava, e-cigarettes, cocaine, and tobacco were identified as commonly used substances within the Pasifika community. The prevalence of substance use was attributed to and enhanced by the following influential factors: cultural acceptance and norms around alcohol use, peer pressure, low levels of health literacy, coping with mental illness, and conveniency of access to AOD. During Talanoa sessions, the participants used personal stories to share how AOD use is seen as a form of stress relief for Pasifika young people dealing with varied life stressors or crisis including responsibilities as the oldest child, overworking/burnout, grief and loss of loved ones, history of trauma, family stress, relationship breakdown, or mental illness. Fig. 2 depicts what a ‘family drink up’ can look like, which symbolised social togetherness and escapism. Alcohol use was culturally acceptable, in contrast to seeking help outside of the family, as illustrated by the following quote:

“It’s more accepted for you to drink. I think there's more of like an expectation sometimes. Culturally … that's a lot easier to do rather than having that conversation, right? That's what's leaned upon … something so deeply ingrained.” (Male, Fijian Indian, 24 years, Talanoa 4)

Family drink-up

Alt Text:Fig. 2 (Talanoa 1) captures what a family drink-up can look like. The participant described that while alcohol may be seen as a mechanism to decrease anxiety and “take our minds off whatever we have gone through”, it can also create a barrier when trying to seek help professionally.

Participants shared how AOD use is a cultural norm within Pasifika communities and accepted in the context of traditional ceremonies (specific to their Pasifika background) that allow for the use of Kava or social gatherings among friends and family that permit alcohol use. However, a minority of the participants stated that this does not apply to all Pasifika young peoples as religious views, elders’ opinions, and parental discipline also influenced AOD use.

Theme 3: ‘let's work together’: young people's resilience and solutions

While the cultural norms and expectations may be a challenge for Pasifika young people, participants showed a sense of courage, resilience, strength and hope in collaborating to make a difference in the lives of Pasifika young peoples. Maintaining ‘Vā’ with Pasifika young peoples that create culturally safe spaces to facilitate brave conversations around mental health and AOD issues was a key enabler for help seeking, as demonstrated by the following quote:

“Nah we need a way where we can express ourselves like physically and mentally, but in a safe area, so we won't be judged. So, we can just like not bottle it up. We just release it in like small amounts.” (Male, Unidentifiable, Talanoa 6)

A core help seeking strategy that was discussed across all Talanoa sessions was Pasifika young people's connection to their cultural value of ‘faith,’ (as depicted in Fig. 3). This was not in the context of religiosity but more so with reference to personal spirituality with Christianity being the basis. Using faith as a protective factor, “if I could just say the one word, I think it's identity. I think having God in my life has given me an identity and a purpose to live and see beyond my situation” (Female, Tongan, 20 years, Talanoa 3).

Participants openness to partner with services/practitioners was evident in Talanoa sessions. Participants shared a range of strategies to mitigate the community concerns and encourage help seeking. These included involving cultural mediators, Pasifika-based/out-reach programs within schools and youth centres, Pasifika-led health & wellness fitness groups, establishing specific support groups that promote ‘brotherhood’ or ‘sisterhood’, and which consider inclusivity of family and community (e.g. Pasifika churches/community leaders). Pasifika representation and holding space for ‘Vā’ (trust) in all spaces was seen as the bridge between institutions, community, and individuals. Enthusiasm and passion for change is illustrated in the following quote:

“I'm hoping to do a not-for-profit organisation specifically dedicated to Pacific Islanders because we have the potential. It's just kind of coming together as a community and doing something about it, the more Pacific Islanders that are working together. You know, doctors, lawyers, students, parents, the more that are involved. I feel like the more effective things can be for mental health, for drug and alcohol and all that to be a conversation to be had.” (Female, Tongan, 26 years, Talanoa 8)

Discussion

The study found that Pasifika young people often deal with mental health issues and life stress on their own due to layers of cultural stigma, and that AOD is sometimes used to cope with emotional stress. The study also shows that Pasifika young people are proactive in changing the rhetoric to normalise help seeking and advocating for culturally appropriate services. These findings have important implications for service system responses, which are explored further below.

A communal responsibility between Australian government sectors, services-providers, Pasifika young peoples and their families to co-designing mental health and AOD services around Pasifika young peoples ‘voices’ is necessary in mitigating barriers to help seeking [21]. Studies show that Pasifika voices are significantly underrepresented in the mental health and AOD service-provider spaces [6,13], exacerbated by a lack of trust in western services [22] and shown by the reported rate of suicide in Pasifika communities [8]. It is also evident that national healthcare systems and institutions are dominated by western approaches that are individualised and medicalised [23] and which have traditionally excluded Pasifika voices in service design and delivery, reflected in a lack of policy and practice tailored to CALD populations more generally [21,24]. There is strong evidence that integration of culture in clinical management and intervention improves the quality of care provided to Indigenous/CALD populations [25].

There are two key Pasifika cultural elements including faith (spiritual) and family (social), that services need to be responsive to when engaging within this complex cultural hybrid [13,21]. First, cultural safety advocates that both professionals and institutions work to establish a safe place for individuals, which is sensitive and responsive to their social, political, linguistic, economic, and spiritual concerns. Second, it requires the professionals to reflect on their own cultural identity and their relative power as health professionals [26]. There have been recent developments in implementing cultural safety frameworks within healthcare systems [27], and we argue that the same would need to be applied to Pasifika young people experiencing mental health concerns and substance use. Focusing more on educating professionals around Pasifika worldviews is crucial for effective practice [28]. The findings emphasise the importance of training non-Pacific professionals around Pacific-Indigenous holistic frameworks e.g. Fonofale Model [29], incorporating Pacific values such as maintaining ‘Vā’ (Sacred Space) between Pasifika young peoples and their families [29], and utilising Pacific approaches e.g. Talanoa [30].

While early intervention approaches to mental health and alcohol and other drug issues have been promoted in the literature [9,10], they must be Pacific-driven and framed around collectivist/community-based and empowerment frameworks [31]. There have been some recent developments in establishing Pacific-driven support programs across Australian communities which uphold values of ‘family’ and ‘community’ however, few are youth-specific [6,9]. Participants in this study confirmed the service gap and suggested that support needs to be framed around Pasifika cultural ways of knowing, being, and doing, when engaging with Pasifika youth. This could be achieved through collaboration between service-providers and Pasifika community leaders and young people [6,13]. Connection, rapport, and impact lie in the art of service providers ability to deliver supports with cultural humility and critical service evaluation, being careful not to emphasise essentialism or health-service provider discrimination [32] but finding the balance between professionalism and authentic partnerships with Pasifika young people.

Limitations

Pasifika young people are not a homogenous group, and the perspectives shared in this research study may not reflect the views of other Pasifika young people in Australia or worldwide. Additionally, we had uneven representation across different Pasifika backgrounds, with many participants of Polynesian heritage. This limits our ability to generalise our findings across the Oceania and we advocate for further research facilitated in a culturally safe way.
