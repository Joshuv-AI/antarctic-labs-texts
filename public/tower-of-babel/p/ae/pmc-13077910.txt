Matrix micro/nano-topography drives oncogenic signaling and drug response in a 3D osteosarcoma model
Authors: Mei-Ling Wang, Xu Cai, Feng Lv, Jun Li, Xue-Yu Chen, Meng-Yuan Wang, Ya-Chao Gu, Han-Lin Tao, Gang Liu, Xi-Qiu Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOsteosarcoma (OS) research is constrained by a scarcity of clinical samples and traditional models that inadequately replicate the natural micro/nano-structure of bone. The role of these topological features in sustaining cellular function and influencing drug response remains insufficiently understood in OS. To address these challenges, a composite alginate-hydroxyapatite (AlgHA) cryogel was developed by incorporating bone-derived hydroxyapatite into an alginate-based cryogel matrix, which simultaneously enhanced mechanical stability and replicated the nano-topography of native bone extracellular matrix (ECM). The AlgHA-based OS model accurately reproduced key physiological characteristics, including cell proliferation, migration, and ECM protein remodeling. Notably, the model exhibited constitutive activation of multiple signaling pathways, such as PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and calcium signaling, which may be associated with malignant phenotypes. A comparative analysis of transcriptomic profiles and drug responses between 2D cultures and the AlgHA model has identified key pathways implicated in drug resistance, such as drug metabolism-cytochrome P450 and ATP-binding cassette transporters. Additionally, potential targets including receptor tyrosine kinases and PIK3CA, which are frequently overlooked in 2D cultures, were identified. These findings underscore the utility of the micro/nano-topological AlgHA cryogel as a physiologically relevant model for OS, facilitating mechanistic studies, therapeutic target identification, and drug sensitivity prediction. Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a rare malignant tumor predominantly affecting in children and adolescents, characterized by high invasiveness and metastatic potential [1]. The five-year survival rate for non-metastatic OS has improved to 65%–70% with the advent of neoadjuvant chemotherapy [2]. However, the survival rate for metastatic or chemoresistant OS remains approximately 20%, and this statistic has not improved significant over the past four decades [3, 4]. The rarity and significant heterogeneity of OS, combined with the lack of effective preclinical in vitro and in vivo models for evaluating drug efficacy, present substantial challenges in translating novel therapies into clinical practice.

Previous researches on OS have primarily utilized 2D tissue culture polystyrene (TCPS) models, which inadequately replicate the in vivo tumor environment due to the absence of critical components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [5]. The TME encompasses the microenvironment surrounding tumor cells, including blood vessels, various cell types, signaling molecules, and the extracellular matrix (ECM), etc. Among these, the ECM forms a 3D network that provides essential structural and biochemical support to tumor cellular components [6, 7], thereby sustaining tumor cell biological functions and facilitating signal transduction [8]. OS is characterized by a bone-specific ECM that is distinct from other tissues due to its complex hierarchical structure, which spans from the micro/nano scale to the macro scale. This unique configuration imparts high mechanical strength and distinctive biological properties to the ECM [9]. The micro- and nano-topographical features of the ECM present substantial advantages over singular micro- or nano-scale topographies. Micro-scale structures contribute to the complexity and heterogeneity of the 3D microenvironment, where nano-scale structures enhance protein adsorption and modulate cellular behavior [10]. Incorporating these inclusion of such topographies into models can stimulate ECM protein dynamics, cell activity, nutrient transport, and angiogenesis [11] without external drugs. Bone ECM is highly mineralized, containing 70% minerals and 30% type I collagen [12, 13]. Hydroxyapatite (HAp) is utilized to mimic bone mineralization, creating nanoscale roughness for better cell adhesion [14] and improved bone integration and regeneration [15, 16]. While these physiologically relevant features are well-known in bone biology, their impact on OS cell function and drug response remains incompletely understood.

In recent years, with the development of in vitro models, 3D tumor models have emerged as a promising tool for tumor research due to their excellent physiological relevance [17]. While biomaterials have been extensively used in the fabrication of 3D soft tissue tumor models, their application on OS models for pathology and drug discovery is relatively limited. Currently, two types of 3D in vitro models are employed to mimic the OS microenvironment: (i) scaffold-free models, utilizing agar/agarose [18], microwell chip [19], or liquid overlay technique [20] to promote cell aggregation into tumor-like spheroids, and (ii) scaffold-based models, employing natural materials, such as Matrigel [18, 21], collagen [22, 23] and gelatin [24], or synthetic materials, such as poly(ethylene glycol) diacrylate [25] and poly(L-lactide) [26], to create porous 3D architectures. Although these models demonstrate the advantages of 3D culture over traditional 2D culture in numerous aspects, they often fail to incorporate the critical micro/nano-structural attributes of native bone ECM. This limitation potentially limiting their ability to fully reflect the biological complexity and heterogeneity of the disease. Furthermore, the systematic application of high-throughput sequencing technologies in physiologically relevant OS models to analyze tumor heterogeneity and identify novel therapeutic targets has not been fully realized.

Building on our previous observation that alginate (Alg) cryogels exhibit an interconnected micro-scale network that closely mimics native ECM [27], we developed an Alg-HAp (AlgHA) composite model. This model incorporates HAp nanoparticles to reproduce the micro/nano-structural characteristics of bone ECM (Scheme 1). Both in vitro and in vivo studies verified that the AlgHA-based OS model enhances the physiological relevance of the model from multiple perspectives, including stable structural properties, OS cellular responses, and ECM protein remodeling. When combined with transcriptomics sequencing analysis and drug response assays, the model further demonstrated significant potential in identifying novel therapeutic targets and predicting drug sensitivity. In this study, the AlgHA model was developed to address the dual challenges of OS as a rare disease, in terms of the scarcity of clinical samples and the difficulty of traditional in vitro models in simulating the physiological relevance of OS. This model provides an advanced platform for studying OS pathology and drug response.

Results

Composite cryogels exhibit adjustable and stable micro/nano-structural characteristics

In this study, solution-blending and freeze-drying techniques were integrated to homogeneously distribute HAp particles within Alg hydrogel matrix, fabricating a 3D scaffold possessing a micro/nano-topography. The stable physicochemical properties of the scaffolds serve as a critical foundation for the successful preparation of the in vitro model. Precise characterization and control of these physicochemical properties are essential to ensure the reliability of research outcomes, thereby enhancing the fidelity and functionality of the tumor model. To evaluate the influence of HAp on the stability of the composite cryogels, four models with different HAp concentrations were selected. After freeze-drying, a cylindrical scaffold with a diameter of about 1 cm and a height of about 3 mm was obtained (Fig. 2A). Nano-scale particulate protrusions were observed at pore junctions, indicating the homogeneous dispersion of HAp within the Alg matrix. All cryogels possessed designed macropores and fully interconnected micropores, with pore size and porosity in the Alg/HAp composites demonstrating an inverse relationship to HAp concentration (Table 1). The average pore size of the Alg/0HAp cryogel was 203.57 ± 43.12 μm. Progressive incorporation of HAp significantly reduced the average pore size to 159.00 ± 13.86 μm, 123.00 ± 18.38 μm, and 103.14 ± 9.10 μm, corresponding to increasing HAp concentrations. Meanwhile, the addition of HAp significantly ameliorated the heterogeneity of internal pore distribution, potentially attributable to the mechanical properties of HAp (Fig. 2D). Concurrently, stability of composite cryogels was assessed by comparing morphological and weight changes induced by immersion in Ringer’s solution (Fig. 2F, G). After 7, 14, and 21 days of immersion, the Alg/0HAp cryogel exhibited slight surface irregularities. In contrast, scaffolds containing HAp maintained relatively regular surfaces with minimal macroscopic morphological changes, and their mass remained nearly constant, with a final Δm of less than 1 mg.

The functional groups and chemical composition of four composite cryogels were analyzed by Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FT-IR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD), comparing with the typical peaks of HAp and Alg powders. In the FT-IR spectrum (Fig. 2B), Alg powder exhibited a broad peak in the range of 3000–3600 cm–¹, corresponding to stretching vibrations of O–H. The asymmetric stretching vibration of O–C–O was centered at 1595 cm–¹. The vibration at 1413 cm–¹ originated from C–OH bending vibrations associated with carboxylate groups O–C–O. In the FT-IR spectrum of HAp powder, the strong bands at 560, 600, and 900–1100 cm–¹ corresponded to the stretching modes of the PO₄³– group. The cryogels with HAp exhibited the characteristic peak of Alg and HAp powder in the FT-IR spectrum. In the XRD spectrum (Fig. 2C), the intensity of these characteristic peaks increased with higher HAp concentrations, particularly within the 30°–35° and 45°–50° 2θ ranges. However, no distinct peaks attributable to Alg were observed in the XRD patterns, consistent with its amorphous nature [28]. These spectral changes collectively confirm the successful integration of Alg and HAp powders within the fabricated composite cryogels.

Micro/nano-topography promotes cell adhesion and enhances cell-ECM interactions in AlgHA model

The concentration of HAp critically determines the micro/nano-topography and mechanical strength of scaffolds, which are pivotal in the OS model by directly regulating the maintenance of the cellular micro-niche [29]. Consequently, a comprehensive investigation into the effect of HAp concentration on mechanical properties and cellular behavior within composite cryogels is essential. Representative compressive stress–strain curves were presented in Fig. 2E. And the compressive modulus, calculated as the slope within the linear region (up to 10% strain) of the stress–strain curves, was summarized in Table S1. The addition of 50% (weight ratio of HAp to alginate, WHAp/WAlg) HAp into Alg increased compressive modulus of cryogel from 0.33 ± 0.028 MPa to 0.800 ± 0.067 MPa, and the compressive modulus of the Alg/5HAp and Alg/25HAp cryogels were 0.520 ± 0.063 MPa and 0.638 ± 0.086 MPa, respectively. These results demonstrate that the addition of HAp significantly enhances the compressive modulus of the cryogels. This improvement in the mechanical properties of the Alg scaffold is attributed to the superior intrinsic mechanical strength of HAp.

Subsequent evaluation of OS cellular responses to micro/nano-topography elucidated their reliability in recapitulating tumor growth patterns and highlighted their potential for investigating migratory phenotypes. Live/dead staining (Fig. 3A) revealed spheroidal aggregation of OS cells across all four cryogel-based OS models, with good cell viability (the ratio of live cell fluorescence area to total cellular fluorescence area exceeded 90%, as shown in Fig. S1). The progressive proliferation of the tumor spheroids was reflected by a time-dependent increase in spheroid volume from Day 3 to 9, reflecting the capacity of these models in simulating in vivo tumor proliferation. By Day 9, enhanced spheroid colonization was observed within micro/nano-structured cryogels (Fig. 3C), confirming the model’s appropriateness for studying dynamic tumor growth. Furthermore, quantitative assessment via CCK-8 assay (Fig. 3B) demonstrated non-cytotoxicity concurrent and significantly increased metabolic activity of OS cells in the composite cryogels. It is worth noting that the highest OD value was exhibited in Alg/25HAp cryogel at later time point (Day 9), indicating that optimal conditions for cell growth was established by this model. In contrast, minimal intergroup differences (p > 0.05) were detected at early time point (Day 3), consistent with initial cellular adaptation processes.

Fluorescence imaging elucidated the detailed internal structure within these spheroids, enabling the visualization of nucleus and cytoskeleton through 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) and F-actin staining (Fig. 3D). Spheroids on Alg/0HAp cryogel exhibited edge regularization without inter-spheroid contact or cellular protrusions towards the cryogel fibers. In contrast, at 25% HAp incorporation, the spheroid morphology became irregular with peripheral cells extending outward, showing a distinct trend of outward migration of the OS cells in this model, as evidenced by the higher-magnification images in Fig. S2. Additionally, representative SEM micrographs (Fig. 3E) confirmed essential spatial arrangement of composite cryogels, where their porous characteristics support cellular nutrient metabolism. Tightly arranged OS cells recapitulated cellular organization in vivo, while intricate inter-spheroid cellular networks in Alg/25HAp cryogel verified its ability for TME biomimicry. As evidenced by these results, the micro/nano-topography of cryogel-based OS models is crucial in replicating biological functions and cell-ECM interactions.

AlgHA model drives transcriptional reprogramming to enhance the expression of abnormal signaling pathways and tumor heterogeneity in OS

These findings underscore the importance of the micro/nano-scale structural features of the model in promoting authentic 3D cell organization and cell-ECM interactions, both of which are necessary to support cell survival and function in a 3D environment. Consequently, the Alg/25HAp cryogel, hereafter referred to as AlgHA, was designated as the biomimetic platform with the micro/nano-topography for in-depth mechanism research. To further investigate whether the cryogel-based model more accurately simulates the characteristics of OS, a comprehensive transcriptome analysis was conducted. Using the traditional 2D cell culture method (TCPS model) as a control, RNA was isolated from OS cells cultured in both the AlgHA and TCPS models. RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) was then performed to evaluate differences in gene expression. This analysis identified differentially regulated signaling pathways and representative tumor heterogeneity-associated genes under both conditions. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed distinct transcriptional profiles of HOS cells cultured on TCPS and AlgHA models (Fig. 4A). The intersection and differences between the two genes groups were analyzed by a venn diagram (Fig. 4B), which demonstrated 909 specific genes in AlgHA, 395 in TCPS, and 15,347 shared genes. Further analysis using volcano plots (Fig. 4C) revealed differential gene expression patterns, with 1,099 upregulated differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in TCPS model compared to 2,018 upregulated DEGs in AlgHA model.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis (Fig. 4D) of above DEGs demonstrated that the AlgHA model exhibited significant enrichment in angiogenesis, ECM organization, and cell migration for biological processes (BP); collagen-containing ECM, focal adhesion, and cytoskeleton for cellular components (CC); and calcium ion binding and platelet-derived growth factor receptor binding for molecular functions (MF). These patterns indicated enhanced cell-ECM/cell-cell interactions and migration activation in AlgHA model. The 28 highly expressed DEGs were selected for Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis. The sankey bubble diagram (Fig. 4E) illustrated their enrichment in pathways related to angiogenesis, cell adhesion, regulation of cell proliferation, collagen fibril organization, transcription, regulation of gene expression, positive regulation of migration, and osteoclast differentiation. To further verify the expression of these genes under different culture conditions, quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was used to validate the expression of key OS markers (Fig. 4F), including matrix proteins (OPN, OCN) [30], growth regulators (PIK3CA, SGK1) [24], proto-oncogenes (JUN, FOS) [31], and stem cell transcription factors (SOX2, OCT-4) [32]. Compared to TCPS group, the AlgHA model exhibited higher expression levels of these genes, indicating enhanced maintenance of OS function under this condition.

AlgHA model induces multi-signaling activation, ECM remodeling, and a pro-metastatic phenotype in OS cells

To investigate the potential mechanisms by which the AlgHA model promotes malignant behavior of OS, we conducted KEGG pathway analysis on the aforementioned DEGs within the contexts of environmental information processing and cellular processes. A hierarchical classification network (Fig. 5A) was constructed for the 14 most significantly enriched pathways (p < 0.05), which are collectively implicated in tumor progression and metastasis. Based on their functional roles in cancer, these pathways were categorized into three groups: (i) cell growth and death, (ii) proliferation and survival, and (iii) invasion and metastasis. Among these, the PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and calcium signaling pathways contained the largest number of upregulated genes, with 66, 64, and 56 upregulated genes, respectively. The PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways are known to promote tumor growth [33], whereas the calcium signaling pathway influences focal adhesion, thereby facilitating cell migration [34]. To investigate whether pathway activation was initiated by cells sensing the physical topography, FAK was inhibited (a crucial molecule for transducing external mechanical signals [35]) by Defactinib. In the FAK-inhibited group, several critical downstream factors, including PI3K, AKT, and MAPK, exhibited a marked reduction in activation (Fig. S4). Analysis of the TARGET-OS database corroborated that increased expression levels of PIK3CA, AKT1, MAPK1, STIM1, and ORAI3 were associated with poorer overall survival in OS patients (Fig. S3). These findings suggest that the mechanical cues provided by the AlgHA cryogel are essential for the activation of the PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and calcium signaling pathways.

The interaction between cells and ECM plays a central role in coordinating fundamental processes within the OS microenvironment. Matrix proteins and their downstream signaling cascades directly regulate tumor progression, invasion, and drug resistance. Beyond the impact of calcium signaling on matrix protein expression, the incorporation of micro/nano-topological cues into the material design allows active regulation of cell-ECM interactions to better mimic the native niche. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) based on RNA-Seq data from the two conditions revealed significant enrichment of ECM-related signaling pathways in the AlgHA model (Fig. 5B). Notably, there was an enrichment in pathways related to ECM-receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and cell adhesion molecules. Analysis of matrix protein-associated gene expression within these three pathways identified 91 DEGs (fold change > 1.2), remarkably 81 of which significantly upregulated in the AlgHA model. Compositional mapping of these DEGs revealed divergent expression of core matrisome and matrisome associated between TCPS and AlgHA models (Fig. 5C). In AlgHA, the main matrisome subsets comprised 13 collagens (16% of its DEGs), 19 ECM glycoproteins (23%), 39 ECM-affiliated proteins (48%), and two ECM regulators (2%). In contrast, the TCPS model exhibited only four ECM glycoproteins (44%) and three ECM-affiliated proteins (33%). Subsequently, genes encoding collagens, ECM glycoproteins, and ECM-affiliated proteins were significantly upregulated (Fig. 5D) in the AlgHA model. These ECM molecules bind to cell surface receptors such as integrins, activating focal adhesion and influencing cytoskeletal dynamics to promote cell migration [34].

Recognizing the role of calcium signaling in migration, we examined related gene expression and found upregulation of several key genes (Fig. 5E). Among these, STIM2 and ORAI3, both core components of store-operated calcium entry (SOCE), as well as TRPV1, regulate calcium influx and modulate cell migration by influencing actin-myosin contractility through MYH9 and AFAP1 [36]. This suggests that the AlgHA model may activate a pro-migratory calcium signaling network, as supported by cell migration assays. HOS cells in the AlgHA model exhibited significantly enhanced migration compared to the TCPS group, with more cells crossing the transwell membrane after 72 h (Fig. 5F). Furthermore, an upregulation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP2 and MMP9) was observed in the AlgHA model (Fig. 5G), indicating that cells not only exhibit enhanced migratory capabilities but also possess an increased ability to degrade the extracellular matrix, thereby promoting invasion [37]. Taken together, these results indicate that the AlgHA model appears to partially recapitulate the ECM composition of native OS tissue and may engage signaling cascades associated with tumor progression and metastasis.

Recapitulation of the OS physiological environment within AlgHA cryogel sustains in vivo cell proliferation and induces higher drug resistance compared with 2D culture

Building on the in vitro evidence of significantly enhanced cell proliferation and ECM remodeling in AlgHA model, we proceeded to evaluate whether these advantages were retained in vivo. For this purpose, we subcutaneously implanted the AlgHA model (with pre-cultured OS cells) into nude mice, thereby generating a cell-line-derived xenograft (CDX) model that retained the original micro/nano-topographical cues. In parallel, OS cells from 2D culture were subcutaneously injected as a control. Then, samples were harvested at the rapid growth period for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis. H&E images (Fig. 6A-i) revealed characteristic OS histology in both groups, evidenced by pleomorphic nuclei and multinucleated giant cells [38]. The TCPS group displayed extensive necrotic tumor regions (blue-purple nuclear debris, periphery). In contrast, tumors supported by Alginate-Hyaluronic Acid (AlgHA) exhibited more pronounced cancerous features: cells migrated beyond the original cryogel fibers (red arrows) and produced abundant osteoid matrix (black arrows). Correspondingly, these tumors also showed elevated deposition of key osteoblast-specific matrix protein: Collagen I (COL I, Fig. 6A-ii) and OPN (Fig. 6A-iii). Besides, The positive rate of Ki67 is the ratio of rapidly proliferating tumor cells to the total number of cells, as one of the indicators reflecting the proliferation speed and invasion recurrence of tumor cells [39]. As shown in Fig. 6A-iv, AlgHA-based cells demonstrated robust proliferation, whereas the positive cells were significantly reduced in the tumor-necrotic regions of TCPS group. Quantification (Fig. 6B) revealed a Ki67 index of 56.98% ± 3.62% for AlgHA versus 38.23% ± 2.79% for TCPS (p < 0.01), indicating that the composite cryogel with micro/nano-topography provided ample 3D space for cell proliferation, thereby maintaining the normal proliferation rate of OS cells. These results strongly demonstrate that AlgHA model effectively captures and preserves the core pathological features of OS in vivo.

Collectively, our results indicate that the AlgHA model successfully replicates essential critical tumor-microenvironmental cues both in vitro and in vivo. This model supports OS progression and metastasis and fulfills the requirement for high physiological relevance in drug-screening applications. The therapy resistance of OS could be promoted by complex morphology and abundant matrix, which were accurately recapitulated by the AlgHA model. Consequently, we hypothesized that AlgHA would more faithfully predict in vivo drug responses. To evaluate this hypothesis, we compared the chemosensitivity of HOS cells in TCPS and AlgHA models to paclitaxel and doxorubicin (PTX and DOX, a first-line chemotherapy for OS [40]), and zoledronic acid (ZOL, a third-generation nitrogen-containing bisphosphonate with anti-osteoclastic and anti-tumor activities [41]). As illustrated in Fig. 6C and D, significantly higher survival rates were observed in the AlgHA model across concentrations of PTX and DOX, with corresponding IC50 values of 1.99 ± 0.61 µM and 0.53 ± 0.10 µM in AlgHA model, and 0.39 ± 0.10 µM and 3.10 ± 1.02 µM in TCPS, respectively. Moreover, up to 900 µM, ZOL exerted no suppressive effects on the AlgHA model, whereas viability of TCPS group declined in a concentration-dependent manner, with an IC50 value of 33.22 ± 0.02 µM. Live/dead staining was performed on the cells following a 72-hours ZOL treatment (Fig. 6E). In TCPS, increasing concentrations of ZOL resulted in a progressive reduction of live-cell area and significant increase in the fraction of dead cells (Fig. 6F-i). In contrast, AlgHA-based spheroids exhibited minimal cell death (Fig. 6F-ii). Nevertheless, both the number and size of the spheroids experienced a modest reduction, and the migratory cells at the spheroid margins were diminished (Fig. 6E), which accounts for the slight decline in viability observed at 100 µM ZOL in Fig. 6C.

Beyond the commonly mentioned chemotherapeutic drugs, small-molecule inhibitors targeted genes specifically upregulated in AlgHA model (such as NOTCH, JUN, and FOS, as indicated in Fig. 4E) were evaluated. IMR-1, a novel notch inhibitor that blocks MAML1 recruitment to the notch ternary complex [42], thereby attenuating target-gene transcription, and DAPT, a γ-secretase inhibitor that suppresses NOTCH1 signaling and induces differentiation [43], showed no cytotoxicity up to 10 µM (Fig. S6A and B), indicating that notch inhibition does not constitute a primary cell death pathway under these conditions. Similarly, T-5224, a selective c-Fos/AP-1 inhibitor that specifically blocks c-Fos/c-Jun DNA binding and down-regulates MMP-3 and MMP-13 transcription [44], did not affect viability (Fig. S6C) but significantly impaired migration (Fig. S6D). In the AlgHA model, the inhibitory effect was notably diminished, with 82.07% ± 7.38% of cells migrated T-5224, compared to 61.08% ± 2.7% in TCPS at 80 µM (Fig. S6E). This resistance is likely attributable to the increased activity of MMPs characteristic of the AlgHA microenvironment (Fig. 5G). These findings demonstrate that the physiological conditions simulated by the AlgHA model play a crucial role in promoting the development of a multidrug-resistant phenotype.

Potential metabolic detoxification mechanisms underlie the AlgHA model-induced drug resistance via tumor microenvironment simulation

To further explore the mechanisms underlying the different responses of OS cells to therapeutic drugs in the AlgHA model, key genes associated with drug transport and metabolism were identified. GSEA performed on the differential expression matrix (Fig. 7A) revealed a significant enrichment of three pivotal KEGG pathways: ABC transporters (q = 0.03), metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450 (q = 0.04), and drug metabolism-cytochrome P450 (q = 0.05). These pathways were markedly upregulated in AlgHA model compared to the 2D culture. The cytochrome P450 (CYP) pathway, known for its central role in metabolism of numerous drugs and endogenous compounds, was notably enriched, highlighting the increased physiological relevance of the AlgHA platform for modeling drug responses in OS cells. This transcriptional reprogramming suggests that the coordinate activation of ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters-mediated efflux pumps and phase-I/phase-II metabolic enzymes constitutes a primary molecular axis driving the heightened drug resistance observed in the AlgHA model. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) mapping via the STRING database (Fig. 7B) identified a densely interconnected network in which CYP1A1, CYP1B1, and CYP2C8 exhibited the highest connectivity scores, highlighting their pivotal roles within the CYP-mediated metabolic pathways. Expression levels of key genes (Fig. 7C) were subsequently analyzed: ATP-binding cassette transporters, phase-I CYP enzymes, and phase-II glutathione S-transferases. Notably, CYP-related genes were markedly elevated in the AlgHA model, suggesting enhanced metabolic enzyme functionality. As crucial components of Phase I xenobiotic metabolism, CYP enzymes are pivotal in the bioactivation of numerous drugs and environmental toxins. Specifically, CYP1B1 expression is known to reduce sensitivity to certain anticancer agents, including the PTX [45]. The overexpression of ABCB1 contributes to PTX resistance by actively transporting the drug, thereby diminishing its intracellular concentration [46]. This upregulation aligned with reduced PTX sensitivity in our chemoresistance assays.

To further analyze and elucidate the relationship between the expression of these key genes and patient prognosis, we analyzed the GSE21257 dataset. The Huvos grading system, a key parameter for predicting long-term outcomes, is applied in assessing chemotherapy response [47]. Lower grades indicate a poor response, while higher grades denote a favorable response. Within this dataset, a decrease in CYP1B1 expression was observed with increasing Huvos grades (Fig. 7D). A similar pattern was noted for ABCB1 expression in metastatic OS samples (Fig. 7E), suggesting that both genes may influence chemotherapy efficacy.

Subsequently, the specific inhibitors TMS and WS-917 were employed to selectively inhibit CYP1B1 and ABCB1, respectively. In the AlgHA model, this inhibition markedly increased the sensitivity to PTX, resulting in a reduction of its IC50 value by approximately 95% and 98% (Fig. 7F and G). This observation strongly indicates that the upregulation of CYP450 enzymes and ABC transporters contributes to the chemoresistance observed in the AlgHA model. This finding further validates the model’s utility as a physiologically relevant platform for drug test.

AlgHA model promotes and evaluates the response of anti-tumor drug in vivo

Utilizing this validated platform, we analyzed RNA-Seq data to identify potential therapeutic targets for drug discovery. Within the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, 110 genes were significantly upregulated, and a PPI analysis of 63 of these genes revealed four functional modules: signaling molecules, ECM-associated proteins, integrins (ITGs), and receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs, Fig. S5). The PI3K-Akt pathway promotes tumor cell cycle progression, survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis [48]. Consistent with prior findings, key ECM-receptor interactors demonstrated the highest connectivity within the network. Furthermore, RTKs were identified as central nodes within the signaling network, interacting with a majority of pathway signaling molecules. To substantiate these observations, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated a marked overexpression of RTKs in AlgHA after 7 days (Fig. 8A). Specifically, there was a 4.19 ± 0.09-fold increase in platelet-derived growth factor receptor 1 (PDGFR1), a 1.90 ± 0.20-fold increase in platelet-derived growth factor receptor 2 (PDGFR2), a 2.07 ± 0.14-fold increase in fibroblast growth factor receptor 1 (FGFR1), a 6.12 ± 0.92-fold increase in proto-oncogene c-kit (c-Kit), a 5.10 ± 0.51-fold increase in vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 1 (VEGFR1) and a 15.05 ± 0.80-fold increase in vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2). Importantly, the protein expression of VEGFR2, PDGFRB, and c-Kit were significantly higher in the AlgHA model compared to the TCPS control (Fig. 8B). In a clinical context, the activation patterns of RTKs can serve as potential biomarkers for risk stratification and prediction of chemotherapy response in OS patients [49].

Guided by these insights, we proceeded to evaluate three FDA-approved agents that specifically target genes selectively overexpressed in AlgHA but not in 2D cultures: anlotinib and pazopanib, both small molecule RTKs inhibitors with anti-angiogenic and anti-tumor effects [50], and alpelisib, a selective PIK3CA inhibitor currently used for advanced hormone-receptor-positive and PIK3CA-mutated breast cancer [51]. As anticipated, the AlgHA model exhibited significantly increased sensitivity to these drugs, as evidenced by lower IC₅₀ values of 6.57 ± 0.93 µM for anlotinib, 19.76 ± 4.13 µM for pazopanib, and 33.22 ± 0.02 µM for alpelisib, corresponding to reductions of 53%, 55%, and 57% relative to the TCPS group, respectively (Fig. 8C and D).

The therapeutic effect of pazopanib was further evaluated using a CDX model in mice. H&E images (Fig. 8E) showed that untreated tumors consisted of densely packed tumor cells with regular nuclear shapes and distinct borders, accompanied by osteoid matrix formation. Following pazopanib treatment, the nuclear boundaries in the AlgHA model became indistinct, and the nuclear diameter decreased by approximately 50% compared to the TCPS group (Fig. 8F). As an inhibitor targeting multiple RTKs, pazopanib significantly inhibited subcutaneous angiogenesis around the tumors [52]. In the AlgHA-based mouse model, we observed a more pronounced anti-angiogenic effect along with stronger inhibition of VEGFR2 activation (Fig. 8G and H). Therefore, targeting the activated RTK-PI3K-Akt axis in AlgHA model revealed that RTK and PI3KCA inhibitors demonstrated significantly enhanced cytotoxicity. In contrast, their efficacy is likely underestimated in traditional 2D models, where these pathways are inadequately activated. This highlights the unique value of the AlgHA model for identifying tumor-dependent targets and predicting the efficacy of targeted therapies.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to develop and validate a physiologically relevant OS model using a simplified biomimetic strategy. Tumor heterogeneity in OS is influenced by acellular ECM components, which play a crucial role in shaping tumor progression [53]. Conventional in vitro models, particularly 2D cell culture, often fail to adequately recapitulate this complex acellular ECM and the resulting metastasis-promoting microenvironment. Our objective was to develop a model that more accurately simulates these features, thereby providing a more reliable platform for cancer research and drug response testing.

To overcome the limitations, the well-defined natural polysaccharide Alg was selected as a single polymer component, thereby circumventing the variability typically in biologically derived matrices such as Matrigel [54] or decellularized ECM [55]. The network of Alg cryogels possesses a natural hierarchical porous structure, forming continuous micro-scale channels. The incorporation of HAp in this model not only mimics the inorganic composition of bone matrix [56], but also allows for precise control of aggregate size and morphology as required. Although the incorporation of ceramic typically reduces porosity and pore size of the model [28], the porosity of the composite cryogels was greater than 60%, meeting the prerequisite for OS cell survival [57] (Table 1). Consistent with the inverse relationship between stiffness and porosity [58], the compressive modulus of AlgHA cryogels was around 0.5 MPa, sufficient to maintain structural integrity under both in vitro and in vivo conditions [59]. Thus, the proposed model effectively addresses the limitations of conventional systems while successfully simulating the native OS microenvironment in terms of structural, mechanical, and biochemical characteristics.

Consistent with previous reports [11], our study confirms that hierarchical micro/nano-topography can enhance the regulation of cellular behavior by providing topological cues, such as enhancing cell viability, cytoskeletal remodeling, and migration. The AlgHA cryogel provided a 3D microenvironment containing both organic and inorganic elements to sustain OS cell functions. Specifically, the AlgHA model significantly promoted the proliferation and viability of OS cells even in the absence of exogenous growth factors (Fig. 3A-C). The incorporation of HAp provides nanoscale surface roughness, thereby increasing the physical interface for cell adhesion and facilitating cell spreading and survival [14]. This benefit was further corroborated by the significantly higher proportion of Ki67⁺ cells observed in the AlgHA group in vivo (Fig. 6B). Notably, the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways were markedly activated in cells cultured in the AlgHA cryogel (Fig. 5A-ii). These pathways are recognized for their role in promoting tumor growth by regulating cell cycle progression and apoptosis resistance [33]. The reduced activation of these pathways following FAK inhibition further demonstrated the impact of the hierarchical micro/nano-topography of the AlgHA model on activating these pro-survival pathways.

In addition, micro/nano-topography can indirectly regulate pseudopodia formation, cell migration, and matrix infiltration by influencing the dynamic reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton [60]. This modulation enabled cells to more effectively adapt to the 3D microenvironment and migrate toward the fiber surface (Fig. 3D and E). Such enhanced cell-matrix interactions provide cells with essential mechanical and biochemical signals, further explaining why the AlgHA model can support the long-term functional maintenance of cells. In contrast, cells in HAp-free Alg cryogels displayed restricted intercellular contacts and proliferation because of the limited specific surface area and insufficient adhesion sites of the cryogels, further underscoring the advantages of hierarchical micro/nano-topography in simulating the TME.

The enhanced cellular proliferation and migration observed validate the high physiological relevance of the AlgHA model in recapitulating malignant behaviors of OS. These phenotypic manifestations are largely attributable to the dynamic interactions between tumor cells and the ECM, which are crucial in regulating tumor metastasis [61]. Previous research has demonstrated that 2D cultures significantly limit the ability to mimic the in vivo TME due to their lack of complex ECM spatial structures and mechanical signals [26]. In contrast, the AlgHA model demonstrated significant advantages in supporting ECM protein remodeling and pro-metastatic phenotype, collectively enhancing OS cell migration (Fig. 5).

As an integral component of the TME, the ECM actively modulates neoplastic behaviors through its biochemical composition and physical properties. A key mechanism in these processes is focal adhesion, a multimolecular complex composed of transmembrane integrins and cytoplasmic proteins that connect the ECM to the actin cytoskeleton [62]. This structure promotes cytoskeletal reorganization, enabling cells to detect mechanical cues [63] and subsequently activate downstream signaling pathways [64]. Thus, the micro/nano-topography plays a crucial regulatory role in modulating cell motility and malignant dissemination. Conversely, enhanced calcium signaling promotes cell migration by activating SOCE channels, increasing intracellular calcium levels, and subsequently modulating focal adhesion turnover and actomyosin contractility [34]. These results indicate the AlgHA model promotes the secretion and assembly of an extracellular matrix that closely resembles natural tissue. This process is driven by combined physical and biochemical signals, ultimately recreating a tumor-supportive microenvironment that facilitates cancer progression.

It is noteworthy that although the role of ECM in maintaining tissue homeostasis is well-established, most previous studies have focused on its applications in bone repair and regeneration [65], while overlooking its regulatory role in the malignant phenotypes of OS. To systematically investigate this mechanism, our study innovatively utilized the AlgHA model to investigate transcriptional reprogramming in OS cells and explore tumor heterogeneity (Fig. 4). The AlgHA model activates key tumor-promoting pathways, with the upregulation of PI3K-Akt pathway providing a molecular basis for heightened metabolic activity and sustained proliferation [24, 66]. The maintained expression of bone-specific matrix proteins further reflects functional preservation of OS-like cells [30]. Upregulation of proto-oncogenes and stemness-related transcription factors suggests an enhanced oncogenic potential and a dedifferentiated state [67].

Importantly, the molecular reprogramming induced by the AlgHA model captures and recapitulates clinically adverse prognostic features, as evidenced by the significant correlation between its upregulated gene signatures and shorter overall survival in patient cohorts. These molecular alterations closely align with the observed phenotypic enhancements in proliferation, migration, and ECM remodeling in AlgHA models. This study underscores that the AlgHA model consistently and accurately​ mimics the pathological features of OS, encompassing matrix remodeling, signaling activation, and aggressive behavior. The ability to replicate these key features is expected to enhance its applicability in investigating tumor biology and assessing responses to anti-tumor therapies.

Subsequent drug response tests and investigations into drug resistance mechanisms (as depicted in Figs. 6 and 7) corroborated these assertions. This model exhibited distinct drug-response profiles compared to traditional drug screening models, demonstrating a stronger induction of drug-resistant phenotypes that closely resemble the clinically observed features of multidrug resistance (MDR) [68]. Transcriptomic analyses revealed a significant upregulation of multiple ABC transporters and CP450 enzyme-related molecules in OS cells cultured on this model. Notably, CYP1B1 and ABCB1, key enzyme and transporter involved in the metabolism and efflux of PTX [45, 46], respectively, were significantly upregulated in the AlgHA model. This upregulation aligned well with the observed drug-resistant phenotype. Inhibiting their expression was found to significantly restore sensitivity to PTX in this model. This pattern suggests their potential role in forming a drug-efflux and metabolic microenvironment that mimics the in vivo MDR microenvironment.

Furthermore, our AlgHA model effectively recapitulated the aberrant activation of the RTK signaling network, a hallmark molecular feature associated with clinically aggressive OS [49, 69]. The stable expression of key RTKs contributes to the formation of an upstream signaling hub, whose activation status is associated with the activity of pro-oncogenic signaling pathways such as PI3K-Akt. This physiologically relevant signaling network enables the AlgHA model to more accurately predict in vivo drug responses (Fig. 8). When targeting the active RTK-PI3K-Akt axis within this model, inhibitors against RTKs and PI3KCA exhibited significantly enhanced cytotoxic effects. In contrast, the efficacy of these targeted agents may be underestimated in conventional 2D models, where pathway activation is often insufficient. This finding highlights the unique advantage of the AlgHA model in identifying tumor-specific targets and predicting the efficacy of targeted therapies. On the other hand, certain drugs exhibited ineffectiveness in both models (Fig. S6), indicating the presence of intrinsic resistance mechanisms that are independent of the culture mode. This observation provides valuable insights for exploring universal resistance mechanisms.

Overall, the 3D bone ECM with micro/nano-topography is crucial for preserving signal transduction and drug responses in OS, suggesting that its potential mechanisms of action in OS treatment warrant further investigation. While the AlgHA cryogel offers a promising platform for OS drug discovery, future research should incorporate two critical steps to enhance the robustness of the conclusions. Firstly, employing a pure Alg scaffold without HAp will help isolate the specific effects of HAp and the micro-nano topography. Secondly, the use of conventional 3D tumor spheroids will facilitate the differentiation between unique signals arising from the ECM and the general effects of 3D cell self-aggregation. Meanwhile, to establish a clearer causal relationship between the micro/nano morphology of the model and cellular response, more comprehensive mechanical transduction experiments, such as altering the stiffness of the scaffold, will be further carried out as a continuation of this study. The reliability of this platform for preclinical research needs to be established. This will involve incorporating more ECM components (e.g., COL I, OPN), testing additional OS cell lines, or validating results using patient-derived cells. Additionally, the mechanisms of action of these effective drugs on OS cells require clarification. The role of therapeutic targets should be explored further through combination therapy, and the model’s applicability and reliability warrant validation with more comprehensive in vivo efficacy data.

Conclusion

In summary, a composite AlgHA cryogel was successfully fabricated by incorporating HAp into an Alg cryogel, presenting enhanced micro/nano-topological cues. The AlgHA cryogel-based OS model has been demonstrated to improve physiological relevance through various aspects, including stable structural properties (such as micro/nano-topography, pore sizes ranging from 40 to 300 μm, and long-term structural integrity), cellular behaviors (including phenotype, proliferation, cytoskeletal organization, and migration), ECM protein remodeling, signaling pathway activation, and malignant phenotype. This model offers a high-fidelity platform for investigating cancer mechanisms and evaluating therapies. It naturally replicates an MDR microenvironment, and the integration of its transcriptomic profiles allows for precise drug response predictions. Our results confirm the potential of the AlgHA model for identifying therapeutic targets and predicting drug sensitivity. We propose that it serves as an effective in vitro tool to advance our understanding of OS complexity and inform the development of targeted therapies.

Materials and methods

Preparation of Alg/HAp cryogels

HAp powder was dispersed in 10 mL purified water and subjected to vigorous ultrasonication for 2 h to obtain a homogeneous suspension. Subsequently, 0.2 g sodium Alg powder was added to the suspension, followed by magnetic stirring at room temperature for 6 h to ensure complete dissolution. The resulting mixture was aliquoted into cell culture plates and lyophilized until complete dehydration. The scaffolds were then cross-linked by immersion in 2% (w/v) CaCl₂ solution for 1 h, rinsed 2–3 times with purified water, and stored for subsequent experiments. Four types of composite scaffolds with HAp/Alg mass ratios of 0%, 5%, 25%, and 50% (WHAp/WAlg) were prepared, designated as Alg/0HAp, Alg/5HAp, Alg/25HAp, and Alg/50HAp, respectively. The Alg concentration was maintained constant at 2 wt% in all formulations. Prior to cellular experiments, all scaffolds underwent sterilization by immersion in 75% ethanol for 4 h, followed by PBS rinsing (1–2 times) and subsequent cell seeding.

Characterization of Alg/HAp cryogels

Surface morphology of Alg/HAp cryogels was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, CarlZeiss). Internal microstructures of Alg/HAp cryogels were visualized and photographed under an inverted microscope after fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in molten paraffin. FT-IR was performed on Alg powder, HAp powder, and Alg/HAp Cryogels. Spectra were recorded in the 4000 –500 cm⁻¹ range at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. XRD patterns of HAp powder and Alg/HAp composite scaffolds were acquired using an instrument model with a scanning range of 20°–80° at a scan rate of 10°/min. Alg/HAp Cryogels of identical dimensions were subjected to uniaxial compression testing using a universal testing machine (Shimadzu) equipped with a 5000 N load cell. Specimens were compressed at a constant rate of 0.2 mm/min until 30% strain was achieved. Compressive modulus was calculated as the slope of the initial linear region in the stress–strain curves.

Porosity (Q) was determined using ethanol displacement. A pycnometer filled with ethanol was weighed (M₁). A pre-weighed scaffold (Ms) was immersed in ethanol to evacuate air bubbles, and the total mass (M₂) was recorded. After removing the ethanol-saturated scaffold, the residual mass (M₃) was measured. Porosity was calculated using: Q = [(M₂ − M₃ − Ms) / (M₁ − M₃)] × 100%.

Stability of Alg/HAp cryogels

Initial weights (W0) of scaffolds were recorded prior to random immersion in pH 7.4 Ringer’s solution (dissolving 9.0 g NaCl, 0.425 g KCl, 0.119 g CaCl₂, and 0.1 g NaHCO₃ in 1 L of purified water). Samples were incubated at 37 °C under 5% CO₂, with solution refreshed every 48 h. After 7, 14, and 21 days (W21), scaffolds were lyophilized, photographed, and weighed. Weight loss ratio (R) was calculated as: R = (W0 − W21) / W0 × 100%.

Biocompatibility

HOS cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/mL) were cultured for 3 and 9 days. Cell viability was quantified by measuring absorbance at 450 nm using a CCK-8 kit. The cryogel’s cytotoxicity was assessed using a calcein-AM (2 µmol/L)/ PI (10 µg/mL) dual-fluorescence staining solution. Samples were incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by imaging with an inverted fluorescence microscope.

Cell morphology

HOS cells were seeded at 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and cultured for 9 days. Cells in the rapid growth phase were rinsed 1–2 times with pre-warmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min. Residual fixative was removed by PBS washing. Permeabilization was performed using 0.1% Triton X-100 for 30 min, followed by blocking with 0.1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h. Actin filaments were labeled with fluorescent phalloidin (C2015M, Beyotime) for 1 h, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (G1012-10ML, Servicebio) for 10 min. Cell distribution was visualized using tetrazolium salt (MTT) staining. All specimens were mounted in anti-fade medium and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope equipped with appropriate filter sets.

Animal tumor models

Male Balb/c nude mice (4–5 weeks old, SPF-grade; license number: SYXK (E) 2021-0057; certification: SCXK (E) 2022-0030; ethics approval: 3846) were acclimatized for 1 week and randomly divided into two groups (n = 3). HOS cells were cultured in 2D conditions and resuspended in blank MEM medium at 2 × 10⁷ cells/mL. For subcutaneous inoculation, 100 µL cell suspension (2 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) was injected into the left axilla of each mouse. Alg/HAp cryogels pre-seeded with HOS cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mL, 6-day culture) were surgically implanted. Mice were anesthetized with 0.8% sodium pentobarbital (100–200 µL, intraperitoneal), maintained at 37 °C using a heating pad, and disinfected with ethanol. Scaffolds were subcutaneously embedded in the dorsal region and sutured with sterile surgical thread, followed by iodophor disinfection. At week 4 post-implantation, mice were euthanized.

Drug response analysis

Cytotoxicity testing was performed on Alg/HAp cryogels to assess their suitability for drug screening. For comparative analysis, a conventional 2D culture model was included. DOX and PTX were obtained from Aladdin. ZOL, Pazopanib, Alpelisib, DAPT, IMR and T-5224 were purchased from MCE. Anlotinib was sourced from Selleck. For in vitro drug testing, HOS cells cultured on cryogels for 6 days were treated with gradient drug concentrations for 72 h, followed by cell viability assessment using a CCK-8 assay. Absorbance values at 450 nm wavelength were recorded for each drug concentration in both models. For in vivo pazopanib treatment, mice were treated with 50 mg/kg pazopanib orally daily, at week 3 post-implantation. After 7 days of treatment, tumors were collected and subjected to H&E analysis, and western blot for p-VEGFR2 expression.

Histomorphological evaluation

Tumor tissues were excised and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde. Specimens were dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices using a microtome. Sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through descending ethanol gradients, followed by distilled water rinsing. For histomorphological evaluation, sections were stained with H&E using standardized protocols. For IHC analysis, deparaffinized sections underwent antigen retrieval in 10 nM citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 98 °C for 20 min. After cooling to room temperature and PBS washing, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min. Non-specific binding was minimized by 1-hour incubation with 5% BSA. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (anti-COL I, OPN and Ki67, 1:300, Servicebio). Following PBS washes, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Servicebio) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. After counterstaining with hematoxylin, slides were mounted with neutral resin and imaged using an inverted microscope. Three random fields per tumor section were captured for quantitative analysis.

Western blot

Protein lysates were prepared from four groups of HOS cells: HOS cultured on TCPS, AlgHA cryogel, AlgHA cryogel treated with 5µM Defactinib for 3 days and tissue samples before and after pazopanib treatment. Briefly, cells and tissues were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentration was determined with the BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein were separated by 8%−12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 6% nonfat dry milk for 2.5 h, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies (all from CST): PI3K, p-PI3K, AKT, p-AKT, MAPK, p-MAPK, VEGFR2, p-VEGFR2, PDGFRB, and c-Kit, each diluted at 1:1000 except phospho-AKT (1:2000). GAPDH (1:5000) served as the loading control. Following primary antibody incubation, membranes were incubated in anti-rabbit HRP (1:5000, CST) for 2.5 h at room temperature in the dark. Membranes were then visualized in a Near-infrared imager (Odyssey CLx Imager) and the band intensities were performed by ImageJ software.

RNA-Seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted from HOS cells under different culture conditions using Trizol reagent (1 mL per 1 × 10⁶ cells) with 10-minute incubation on ice. RNA samples were submitted to BGI Genomics (https://biosys.bgi.com) for sequencing. Transcriptomic profiles of MG-63 cells were generated using Illumina-based RNA sequencing. Bioinformatic analysis was performed by Dr. Tom (BGI Genomics) as follows: Hierarchical clustering heatmaps were generated based on normalized gene transcript expression levels across samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified (adjusted p < 0.05, fold change > 2) and subjected to KEGG pathway enrichment analysis (https://www.kegg.jp) after multiple testing correction using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. PPI networks were constructed using the STRING database and analyzed in Cytoscape (v3.9.1) for topological visualization. Functional associations between candidate targets were interrogated through the STRING platform, with evidence thresholds applied to refine biologically relevant interactions.

Inhibitory assay

To investigate specific resistance mechanisms, WS-917 and TMS (both from MCE) were employed. On the 6-day culture, cells were pretreated with 4 µM of either WS-917 or TMS at 37 °C for 4 h. Following inhibitor pretreatment, cells were exposed to various concentrations of PTX for an additional 72 h. Cell viability was again determined using the CCK-8 assay, with absorbance recorded at 450 nm. A control group treated with DMSO was included in this experiment.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

HOS cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/mL and cultured for 6 days. Cells were then treated with gradient concentrations of T-5224 inhibitor for 72 h. For migration assays, cell suspensions (equivalent density) were loaded into the upper chambers of transwell inserts (8 μm pore size, Corning), while complete medium was added to the lower chambers to establish a chemotactic gradient. After 48 h incubation, migrated cells on the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.2% crystal violet for 15 min. Non-migrated cells on the upper chamber were removed using a cotton swab. Five representative fields (upper, lower, left, right, and central regions) per insert were imaged under an inverted microscope. Migrated cell areas were quantified using ImageJ software by threshold-based segmentation.

Gene expression assay

HOS cells subjected to various experimental conditions were harvested and transferred to RNase-free microcentrifuge tubes. Total RNA was isolated using TRIzol reagent (Takara) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For cDNA synthesis, 1 µg of total RNA from each sample underwent reverse transcription using HiScript III Reverse Transcriptase SuperMix (Vazyme) under optimized thermal cycling conditions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed in triplicate using AceQ Universal SYBR Green Master Mix (Vazyme) on a Q1000 Thermal Cycler (LongGene) with the following parameters: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression quantification was calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method, with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) serving as the endogenous reference gene.

Microarray data and data processing

Publicly available gene expression datasets were utilized in this study. The GSE21257 dataset, comprising 53 OS samples with complete clinicopathological information, was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) database. Additionally, the TARGET-OS database, which includes gene expression profiles and corresponding survival data for 88 primary OS samples, was obtained from the NCI TARGET (https://ocg.cancer.gov/programs/target) database. All subsequent data processing and analysis were performed using R software (version 4.0.5). For survival analysis within the TARGET-OS database, samples were dichotomized into high- and low-expression groups based on the median expression value of each gene of interest. Differences in survival outcomes between these groups were then statistically evaluated using the log-rank test.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons (p < 0.05 significance threshold). Dose-response curves were generated in GraphPad Prism (v9.0) using nonlinear regression (variable slope model) to calculate IC50 values. Results are expressed as mean ± SD.
